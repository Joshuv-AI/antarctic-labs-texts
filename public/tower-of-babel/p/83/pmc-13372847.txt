Prevalence and Histologic Features of Multifocal Fibrosing Thyroiditis in the Routine Pathology Practice
Authors: Floriana Jessica Di Paola, Maria Vittoria Altavilla, Giulia Calafato, Thais Maloberti, Federico Chiarucci, João Vale, Antonio De Leo, Dario de Biase, Catarina Eloy, Giovanni Tallini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMultifocal fibrosing thyroiditis (MFT) is a poorly understood condition characterized by multiple fibrotic scar-like lesions with paucicellular core and peripheral follicular cell atypia. Described by Dr. Rosai in 1990 in consultation cases, MFT has received limited attention due to its perceived rarity. To define its prevalence and clinicopathologic features in the routine practice, consecutive thyroid resections were analyzed from the archives of two medical institutions in Italy and Portugal. Among 1,736 resections 27 MFT were identified (1.56%), corresponding to a detection rate of 1.34% MTF cases per year in the study cohort. No significant difference between Italian and Portuguese cases were observed. Twenty-three of 27 (85.2%) patients were female. Age range was 34–69 years (median: 52). Autoimmune thyroiditis affected 7/27 patients (25.9%), with clinical hypothyroidism in four (14.8%). Clinical hyperthyroidism was present in 4/27 patients (14.8%), including 2 cases of hyperfunctioning adenoma, 1 case of toxic multinodular goiter, and 1 case of Graves’ disease. In 2/27 cases (7.4%) thyroid resection was performed due to fibrotic MFT foci mimicking PTC. Malignant thyroid nodules - papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) in all cases - or other benign nodules caused thyroid resection in 10/27 (37.0%) and in 12/27 (44.4%) cases, respectively; Hashimoto thyroiditis (2 cases) and Graves disease (1 case) caused thyroid resection in the remaining cases. Meticulous histologic examination disclosed additional alterations in 25/27 cases (92.6%), often overlapping with each other and with PTC: incidental millimetric PTC in 7/27 cases (25.9%), follicular nodular disease in 17/27 cases (62.9%), chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis/Hashimoto thyroiditis in 14/27 (51.8%). No genetic alteration was identified in any of the 15/27 cases tested. In the routine practice MFT is an uncommon disorder affecting diseased thyroids that simulates PTC. Its cause remains elusive.Keywords: Multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis, Papillary thyroid carcinoma, Thyroid follicular nodular disease, Chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis.

Introduction

Multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis (MFT) is an uncommon poorly understood condition of the thyroid gland characterized by multiple fibrotic scar-like lesions with a paucicellular fibrotic center surrounded by a peripheral area with follicular cell atypia and variable chronic inflammation [1–3]. The entity was first described by Dr. Rosai in the 1990 edition of the Tumors of the Thyroid Gland fascicle from the Armed Forces Institute of Pathology and named Multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis. In Dr. Rosai’s words this thyroid condition was characterized by multiple “microscopic foci of stellate-shaped fibrosis composed of cellular fibroblastic tissue that may entrap a few thyroid follicles in the center. At low magnification, the individual lesions appear similar to the form of papillary carcinoma classically known as occult sclerosing carcinoma … from which it is distinguished by the absence of cytoarchitectural features of papillary carcinoma in the epithelial component”. He remarked: “The etiology and pathogenesis of this process are not known. The appearance is suggestive of a multifocal injury that has led to follicular loss and replacement by a scar-like tissue” [1].

Since its original description, MFT has received very limited attention in the literature. Due to the uncanny overlap of histologic features with small-sized papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) with fibrotic stroma, once referred to as occult sclerosing carcinoma [4], it is likely that several MFT cases have actually been misdiagnosed as carcinoma in the routine practice [3]. Much of what we know about the entity comes from the analysis by Fellegara and Rosai of 55 cases coded as “Multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis” or (less frequently) “multifocal sclerosing thyroiditis” retrieved between January 1989 and December 2009 from the archives of Dr. Rosai consultation practice [2].

Thus, our knowledge comes from cases with unusual features sent out for second opinion and may not reflect MFT actual prevalence in the routine practice, highlighting the need for further investigation into the diagnostic criteria of MFT and its clinical significance. This contemporary multicentric routine surgical pathology series - first and largest of its kind - was assembled from two different institutions to define the reproducibility of MFT diagnosis, its general prevalence and clinical context, and the thyroid pathology in its background.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Features and Histologic Evaluation of Patient Samples

MFT cases were collected from the archives of Maggiore Hospital in Bologna (Italy) and of the Pathology Laboratory of the Instituto de Patologia e Imunologia Molecular da Universidade do Porto (IPATIMUP) in Porto (Portugal). Bologna cases were retrieved as follows: all histology sections from all the 1393 thyroid resections performed from January 2017 to December 2023 were evaluated at the time of diagnosis and marked for potential MFT. Marked cases were subjected to retrospective slide review to confirm the MFT diagnosis. Porto cases were retrieved as follows: digital slides obtained from all histology sections of the 343 thyroid resection specimens performed from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2022 were retrospectively reviewed to diagnose MFT. Digital observation was performed using protocols validated for routine use at the IPATIMUP Pathology Laboratory in Porto accredited by the College of American Pathologists for digital diagnosis. Such protocols allow total concordance with diagnoses obtained after optical slide observation. Both Bologna and Porto specimens were consecutive unselected cases.

Basic epidemiologic and clinical data (age, sex of the patient, clinical presentation and type of surgery) were extracted from the pathology report and patient charts. All histopathology slides were analyzed for the morphologic parameters listed in Table 1. Digital slides shared with Porto’s archive were reviewed one by one on the CaseSlideView platform (InstantViewer 3.1–3DHISTECH, https://www.3dhistech.com/software/instantviewer/). For histologic analysis, the number, size, and spatial distribution of the fibrotic foci (subcapsular vs. parenchymal), the features of the entrapped and surrounding follicles were recorded. Additionally, the presence of benign and malignant lesions in the remainder of the thyroid gland was evaluated. In cases of MFT with concomitant thyroid malignancy, the malignancy was classified according to World Health Organization criteria [5].

Immunohistochemical Analysis

Tissue samples were formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded. Twenty of 27 slides were stained with anti-BRAF p.V600E (VE1) mouse monoclonal primary antibody (#760 − 509, Roche) following the OptiView Amplification Kit (#860 − 099, Roche) and the OptiView DAB IHC Detection Kit (#760 − 700, Roche) protocols using a BenchMark ULTRA automated immunostainer (Ventana Medical Systems-Roche Diagnostics, Switzerland). Immunohistochemistry was preferred for cases with smaller and more irregular MFT foci, while next-generation sequencing was favored for the analysis of larger foci (see below). The tissue samples of 7 cases triaged for sequencing were not investigated by immunohistochemistry because most MFT foci were exhausted during FFPE sectioning for NGS: all of them were BRAF p.V600E negative by NGS (see Results section).

DNA Extraction and Next-generation Sequencing (NGS)

All samples analyzed were extracted from formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) histological blocks after dissection under microscopic guidance to ensure an adequate proportion of lesional cell enrichment for the analysis. Fifteen of 27 cases were analyzed by NGS: in general, the larger MFT foci were triaged for NGS while immunohistochemistry was used for those with smaller and more irregular foci. In 12 cases MFT foci were small and had an irregular configuration making dissection under microscopic guidance to ensure favorable lesional cell enrichment problematic, all of them tested negative after BRAF p.V600E (VE1) immunohistochemistry (see Results section).

Briefly, DNA was extracted from 10 μm-thick sections using the QuickExtract FFPE DNA Extraction Kit (Epicentre, Madison, WI, USA), according to the selection performed by a pathologist on the last Hematoxylin and Eosin (H/E) slide. DNA was quantified using Qubit dsDNA BR Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Mutational hotspots in the most relevant genes altered in thyroid tumors were analyzed in 13 out of 27 specimens using a previously validated laboratory-developed multigene next-generation sequencing (NGS) panel [6]. The panel allows the analysis of the following hot-spot regions of 28 genes for a total of 330 amplicons (21.77 kb, human reference sequence hg19/GRCh37): BRAF (exons 11, 15), KIT (exons 8, 9, 11, 13, 14, 17), CTNNB1 (exons 3, 7, 8), DICER1 (exons 10, 21, 26, 27, 29), DPYD (exons 11, 13, 22, chr1 intronic regions: g.98187018– 98187098, g.98045419– 98045499, g.97915570–97915789), EGFR (exons 18, 19, 20, 21), EIF1AX (exons 1, 2 and chrX intronic regiong.20148634–20148745), GNA11 (exons 4, 5), GNAQ (exons 4,5), GNAS (exons 8, 9), H3F3A (exon 1), HRAS (exons 2– 4), IDH1 (exon 4), IDH2 (exon 4), KRAS (exons 2– 4), MED12 (exons 1, 2), MET (exons 2, 14), NRAS (exons 2–4), PDGFRA (exons 12, 14, 18), PIK3CA (exons 8, 10, 21), PTEN (exon 5), RET (exons 5, 8, 10, 11, 13, 15, 16), RNF43 (exons 2–10), SMAD4 (exons 2–12), TERT (promoter region, Chr5 g.1295141–1295471) and TP53 (exons 2–11), TSHR (exons 1–10), VHL (exons 1– 3). NGS was performed using the Gene Studio S5 Prime Sequencer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Briefly, about 30ng of DNA was used for the amplicon library preparation, which was set up using the AmpliSeq Plus Library Kit 2.0 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Templates were prepared with an Ion Chef Machine and sequenced using an Ion 530 chip. Sequences were analyzed with the Ion Reporter tool (v. 5.18–Thermo Fisher Scientific). The mean amplicon coverage for the 15 sequenced cases was 2349 reads. Only mutations present in at least 5% of the total number of reads analyzed and observed in both strands were considered for mutational calls [7].

Statistical Analysis

To assess the significance of the compared groups, Fisher’s exact test and Chi-square one-proportion test were performed where appropriate. The non-parametric Mann-Whitney test was used for continuous variables. All statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Statistical significance was assessed as P < 0.05.

Ethics Committee

The study followed Institutional Review Board-approved protocols filed at the Bologna medical center (CE: 446/2024/Sper/AOUBo). Ethic committee approval was waived at the Porto medical center because of the retrospective nature of a study focused on benign thyroid pathology. All study data and patient information were managed using anonymous numerical codes and study and experiments conformed to the principles of the World Medical Association Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Microscopic Features of Multifocal Fibrosing Thyroiditis

MFT is characterized by fibrotic scar-like lesions with jagged edges, containing a paucicellular fibrotic center surrounded by a cellular peripheral area with reactive-appearing follicular cell atypia and variable chronic inflammation (Fig. 1). Individual fibrotic foci display a zonal distribution of morphological alterations (Fig. 2).

Multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis with fibrotic scar-like center surrounded by a cellular peripheral area

Zonal distribution of pathologic changes in multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis

The central portion of the lesions is paucicellular and fibrotic (Fig. 3a and e), sometimes with fibroelastotic collagen similar to that seen in radial scar of the breast (Fig. 3e). The central area is also characterized by clusters of small follicles with scant colloid (Fig. 3b and f) occasionally displaying pseudo-infiltrative features (Fig. 3c and g), and scattered vessels sometimes with ectatic lumina and/or prominent walls (Fig. 3d and h).

Zonal distribution of pathologic changes in multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis: central area. Dense paucicellular fibrosis (a) with fibroelastotic collagen similar to that seen in radial scar of the breast (e); clusters of small follicles (b, f); follicular cells with pseudoinfiltrative features (c, g); vessels with prominent walls (d, h). a, b, c, d: lower magnification. e, f, g, h: higher magnification

The peripheral portions show increased number of follicles lined by cells with reactive/regenerative atypia. Atypical features include nuclear enlargement and elongation with nuclear hyperchromasia (Fig. 4a and d) and chromatin clearing with minor irregularities of the nuclear contour (Fig. 4b and e). Nuclear grooves are rare. Within MFT foci there are no nuclear pseudoinclusions or psammoma bodies. Indeed, features diagnostic of PTC, including nuclear pseudoinclusions or psammoma bodies, were restricted to papillary carcinoma when this coexisted with MFT. Some of the follicles in the peripheral area may be elongated, with their main axis perpendicular to the MFT central fibrotic core (Fig. 4a and d). A predominantly chronic inflammatory infiltrate, including lymphocytes and histiocytes, sometimes with scattered multinucleated giant cells or granulomatous reaction, are also typically present at the periphery of the fibrotic core (Fig. 4c and f).

Zonal distribution of pathologic changes in multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis: peripheral area. Increased number of follicles with reactive-appearing cytologic atypia (a, d, b, e) and predominantly chronic inflammation with granulomatous reaction (c, f). a, b, c: lower magnification. d, e, f: higher magnification

All our cases showed one or more foci with these features, that were identified in both conventional histology sections from Bologna cases and in the digital slides from Porto, with 100% concordance in the diagnoses between all pathologists involved in the study, including two (CE and GT) with a special interest in thyroid pathology.

The largest individual fibrotic foci often had a peripheral/subcapsular - as opposed to a central/intraparenchymal - location: 17/27 (63.0%) versus 10/27 (37.0%) (Table 1).

Similar to what reported by Fellegara and Rosai [2], at low magnification we observed two main patterns of fibrosis: stellate (triangular in shape with the base situated parallel to the thyroid surface and one of the apices pointing to the center of the thyroid if the focus had superficial/subcapsular location) and band-like, the latter appearing as irregular foci of capsular thickening when superficial/subcapsular and as thickening of normal interlobular thyroid septa when the location was central/intraparenchymal. Stellate foci of fibrosis were in general the most common. Indeed, in individual MFT cases, the largest fibrotic focus was more often stellate (17/27, 63.0%) (Fig. 5a; Table 1) as opposed to band-like (10/27, 37.0%) (Fig. 5b; Table 1). In one case (BO-1), confluence of fibrotic foci resulted in a cirrhosis-like appearance (Fig. 5c), similar to case 8 of Fellegara and Rosai [2].

Shape of fibrotic foci in multifocal fibrosing thyroiditis. Stellate (star-like) (a), band-like (b), cirrhosis-like (c)

Multiple fibrosclerotic foci were scattered within the thyroid gland in 22/27 MFT cases (81.5%), with a number of foci ranging from 2 to 17 per case (mean number: 4.6; median number: 4), and a mean diameter of 4 mm (range: 1 to 16 mm) (Tables 1 and 2). A single MFT focus was observed in 5/27 MFT cases (18.5%) with a mean diameter of 4.8 mm (range: 2 to 12 mm).

The site of MFT involvement within the thyroid gland was analyzed. Among cases with total thyroidectomy, the large majority had MFT in both lobes (10/17, 58.9%), 4/17 (23.5%) cases had MFT in the left lobe, 0/17 (0/17, 0%) in the right lobe, and 3/17 (17.6%) in the isthmus. Among specimens where only one lobe was resected, there were five cases with MFT in the right lobe, and five cases in the left lobe. Obviously bilaterality in lobectomy specimens cannot be evaluated. Thus, the overall majority of cases were bilateral, a relative minority consisted of cases that are at least in the left lobe (because only a left lobectomy was performed), followed by cases that were at least in the right lobe (because a right lobectomy was performed), and finally followed by cases that were in the isthmus (Table 1). The presence of only 4 left unilateral cases out of 17 total thyroidectomies was not statistically significant (P = 0.467).

Epidemiology of Multifocal Fibrosing Thyroiditis

In our multicentric retrospective study, we identified 27 cases of MFT among 1,736 consecutive and unselected thyroid resections, with an overall prevalence of 1.56% in the entire cohort. Of these, 22 cases were found over a seven-year period among the 1,393 resections retrieved from the Italian archive (1.58%), and 5 cases over a two-year period among the 343 (1.46%) resections from the Portuguese archive. The average yearly rate of MFT detection was 1.40% (range 0-2.80%) in the Italian thyroid resections, and 1.44% (range 1.28–1.60%) in the Portuguese ones. These figures correspond to an average yearly detection rate of 1.34% for the entire cohort.

Clinical Features of Multifocal Fibrosing Thyroiditis

MFT clinicopathologic features are shown in Table 1, with no significant difference between the Italian and Portuguese cases. There was a clear predominance of female patients: 23/27 (85.2%) were female and 4/27 (14.8%) male. However - in spite of a higher proportion of female patients in MFT - the correlation between MFT and patient sex in the 1,736 cohort population was not significant (P = 0.192), reflecting the general higher prevalence of thyroid pathology in the female sex. Age ranged from 34 to 69 years (mean: 54.4; median: 52.0) (Table 1).

Clinically, 7/27 patients (25.9%) were affected by autoimmune thyroiditis with persistently elevated anti-thyroglobulin antibodies in three. Four patients were clinically hypothyroid. Four of 27 patients (14.8%) suffered from hyperthyroidism, due in two cases to hyperfunctioning follicular adenoma, in one case to toxic multinodular goiter, and in one case to Graves disease.

Seventeen of 27 patients (63.0%) underwent total thyroidectomy, 5/27 (18.5%) right thyroid lobectomy, and 5/27 (18.5%) left thyroid lobectomy (Table 1).

In all cases but two, MFT was an incidental finding in thyroid glands resected for the presence of benign or malignant thyroid pathology. In 2/27 cases (7.4%) thyroid resection was performed for fibrotic MFT foci with worrisome ultrasound features and preoperative cytology that was indeterminate in the first case (BETHESDA III, lobectomy; BO-3) and suspicious (BETHESDA V, total thyroidectomy; BO-8) for papillary carcinoma in the second (Table 1). Malignant thyroid nodules - all papillary carcinomas - or other benign nodules were the reason for thyroid resection in 10/27 (37.0%) and in 12/27 (44.4%; Follicular adenoma: 3 thyroid resections; follicular nodular disease: 9 thyroid resections) cases, respectively. In the three remaining cases, resection was due to chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis (2/27 cases, 7.4%) and Graves disease (1/27 cases, 3.7%) (Table 1).

Thyroid Pathology Associated with Multifocal Fibrosing Thyroiditis

Meticulous histologic examination was performed to identify the spectrum of additional alterations present in thyroid specimens with MFT. Seventeen of 27 cases (63.0%) were associated with PTC, 10 cases being the reason for surgery (see above), and additional 7/27 cases (25.9%) being millimetric PTC found incidentally in specimens removed for benign thyroid pathology. Most PTC measured ≤ 10 mm (12/17, 70.6%), while the remaining cases (5/17, 29.4%) ranged from 13 to 25 mm; in both size groups, PTC were frequently multifocal (overall 9/17, 52.9%) (Table 1). The reason for surgery was benign thyroid pathology in the majority of cases (see above), but histologic analysis revealed a variety of benign thyroid alterations in 25/27 cases (92.6%), often overlapping with each other and with PTC (Table 1). Follicular nodular disease (17/27 cases; 62.9%) and chronic lymphocytic thyroiditis, including Hashimoto thyroiditis (14/27 cases; 51.8%), were the predominant benign histologic alterations in the cohort.

Comparison of Clinicopathologic Features with Dr. Rosai Consultation Series

We compared the clinical and pathologic features of our routine series with those of Dr. Rosai consultation series, which included 55 cases [2] (Table 2). The comparison of clinical features is summarized in Table 2a. Our routine cases developed in older patients (mean age: 54.4 versus 47.0; P = 0.006), but there was no statistical difference for sex, reason for surgery and type of surgery between the two studies (Table 2a). Comparison of MFT microscopic features is summarized in Table 2b. In our routine cases MFT tended to involve both lobes less frequently (Bilaterality: 58.8% versus 90.0%; P = 0.052) but the overall histologic features were no different (Table 2b). Comparison of MFT-associated thyroid pathology is summarized in Table 2c. In our routine cases MFT was more frequently associated with PTC (63.0% versus 31.6%; P = 0.022) or with benign follicular nodular disease (63.0% versus 39.4%; P = 0.080), although PTC dimensions and PTC multifocality were comparable between the two cases series (Table 2c).

Mutation status of Multifocal Fibrosing Thyroiditis

No genetic alteration was identified in any of the cases tested (15/27) for molecular analysis with a broad NGS panel designed to identify single nucleotide variants and small insertion/deletions in hot spot regions of genes relevant for thyroid tumorigenesis. Given the histologic similarity of MFT and PTC and their perceived link, an important reason to perform molecular analysis is to rule out BRAF p.V600E [8]. This was done on all cases by NGS and/or BRAF p.V600E immunohistochemistry, and in no case was BRAF p.V600E found: there was no positivity in any of the 20/27 cases, representative sections of which were stained with a BRAF p.V600E specific antibody. Neither BRAF p.V600E nor any other genetic alteration were identified in any of the 15/27 cases tested by NGS (Table 1).

Discussion

To date, very little is known about MFT, an enigmatic disease entity characterized histologically by fibrotic lesions with a paucicellular scar-like center surrounded by a peripheral area with reactive-appearing follicular cell atypia and variable chronic inflammation. Since its original description by Dr. Rosai in 1990 [1], only 65 cases have been reported in five publications [2, 3, 8–10]: two are reports of MFT images [9, 10], one is a case report [3], one is a miniseries of 7 MFT that included cases from the personal consult files of Dr. LiVolsi, devoted to BRAF p.V600E analysis and with very little detail on the features and number of MFT foci [8], one is a very detailed large series of 55 MFT, all from Dr. Rosai’s personal consult files [2]. None of these publications reflects the routine pathology practice.

To diagnose MFT, we used criteria clearly outlined in Fellegara and Rosai series [2] and reviewed by Orsatti et al. [3]. Specifically, we diagnosed fibrotic scar-like lesions displaying zonal distribution of pathologic alterations as MFT: a fibrotic center containing few entrapped small follicles and scattered vessels, and a peripheral portion with increased follicles lined by cells with reactive atypia (nuclear enlargement and elongation, some chromatin clearing with minor irregularities of the nuclear contour) and a predominantly chronic inflammatory infiltrate. Location within the thyroid (peripheral/subcapsular - as opposed to central/ intraparenchymal), pattern of the lesion (stellate as opposed to band-like), and number of lesions were not taken into account to diagnose MFT. These diagnostic criteria were uniformly applied to all our cases, and proved reproducible enough - even in digital archival slides - to allow confident evaluation of data. Indeed, we demonstrate that all the histologic features of MFT can be detected after observation of either optical or digital slides, enabling confident diagnosis with similar prevalence in both settings.

In our series a significant minority of MFT had a single focus. By strictly applying diagnostic criteria we have identified in routine thyroid resections MFT cases with single, not multiple foci. Given the complete lack of knowledge of MFT in the routine practice this may not be too surprising. As a matter of fact, it is the distinctive histology, not the number of foci, that defines the condition. Since the large majority of MFT cases are multifocal even among our routine specimens we confirm that MFT is typically multifocal and thus do not believe any terminology change is warranted.

The primary MFT differential diagnosis is with PTC. As originally noted by Dr. Rosai, it specifically involves those forms of small PTC reported by Klinck and Winship as occult sclerosing carcinoma of the thyroid [4] that are characterized by fibrotic stroma and peripheral/subcapsular location. It is indeed typical for a MFT focus to be indistinguishable at low magnification from PTC with dense sclerotic fibrosis. Only the absence of cytoarchitectural features of papillary carcinoma at higher magnification and the typical finding of other similar lesions scattered in the thyroid point to the correct diagnosis [1, 2]. In MFT where there are pseudoinfiltrative features in the fibrotic core of the lesion and/or marked reactive atypia in its periphery, or in MFT cases with a single focus, the differential diagnosis with PTC may become problematic. Immunohistochemistry for PTC markers such as HBME-1, cytokeratin 19, Galectin-3 (all overexpressed in papillary thyroid carcinoma), and CD56 (lost in conventional PTC), as well as absence of BRAF p.V600E (absent in MFT) may be of great help [3]. PTC with fibromatosis/fasciitis-like stroma should also be mentioned in the differential diagnosis, but it is easily distinguished from MFT because of its clear-cut neoplastic appearance and because of nuclear beta-catenin accumulation in the stromal component of the tumor by immunohistochemistry due to frequent CTNNB1 mutation [3]. The focal nature and zonal distribution of histologic changes tell apart MFT from other non-neoplastic conditions characterized by stromal fibrosis including those associated with IgG4, such as the fibrosing variant of Hashimoto thyroiditis and Riedel thyroiditis [2]. The presence of numerous histiocytes with lack of fibrosis, and the florid inflammation with hemosiderin deposits distinguish MFT from so-called palpation thyroiditis (multifocal granulomatous thyroiditis) and fine-needle aspiration-induced changes, respectively [2]. More difficult may be the distinction from subacute (De Quervain) thyroiditis, which is often multifocal, but lacks zonal histologic alterations, and shows numerous giant cells and foci of acute inflammation. Late fibrotic stage lesions of subacute thyroiditis may be indistinguishable from MFT, that may indeed represent burned-out remnants of subacute thyroiditis [3]. However, in our series only one case (BO-3) had overlapping alterations, with MFT coexisting with subacute thyroiditis. In none of the other cases there was clinical or histologic evidence of subacute thyroiditis.

Histologic alterations in the individual MFT foci were identical to those reported in Fellegara and Rosai consult series [2], with no difference in the diameter of MFT foci and a similar higher prevalence of stellate versus band-like fibrotic foci. Similar to what reported in Fellegara and Rosai consult series [2], involvement of MFT within the thyroid gland was most frequently bilateral, with no specific site predilection. However, in our routine case series the number of MFT foci and the prevalence of bilateral thyroid lobe involvement was lower, likely reflecting the more widespread alterations in the unusual cases reviewed by Dr. Rosai.

We demonstrate that - although uncommon - MFT is not a rare “pathology unicorn”, with an overall prevalence of 1.56% in our 1,736 cases, corresponding to an average yearly detection rate of 1.34% among our consecutive unselected thyroid resection specimens. The prevalence of MFT was similar among Portuguese and Italian cases, but the overall prevalence of 1.56% in our routine series is almost three times the 0.55% estimated from Fellegara and Rosai series. As predicted by Fellegara and Rosai, the prevalence extrapolated from a consult series - that mostly included cases with problematic differential diagnosis - underestimates the real MFT prevalence [2]. This indicates that at least some MFT cases in the routine practice are ignored and unreported as non-specific thyroid parenchymal scarring, or - more worrisomely - potentially misdiagnosed as PTC by pathologists not aware of the entity [2, 3].

As in Fellegara and Rosai consult series there was a large predominance of female patients, although the association between female sex and MFT in our 1,736 case study cohort was not statistically significant. The presence of a thyroid nodule was by far the most common cause for thyroid resection, and malignant nodules were PTC - all but two cases in Fellegara and Rosai series and all cases in ours [2]. Since PTC prevalence increases with age [11, 12], the stronger association between MFT and PTC in this series is compatible with the older age of our patients. The most common reason for thyroid surgery was to rule out malignancy in a thyroid nodule, in the large majority of cases to rule out PTC. Remarkably, in two of our MFT cases, ultrasound features and cytologic atypia in preoperative cytology simulated PTC, and this misinterpretation caused resection of the thyroid. One of these cases (BO-8) has been previously reported [3].

Only limited clinical information is available in Fellegara and Rosai consult series [2]. In this series, almost a third of patients had altered thyroid function, with equal prevalence of hyper- and hypothyroidism, while about a fourth of them had autoimmune thyroid disease. However, considering the high prevalence of clinical alterations in patients undergoing thyroid surgery, a pathogenic association is unlikely.

The cause of MFT remains elusive. Although this condition is almost always an incidental postoperative finding in thyroid glands resected for other reasons, its frequent association with a variety of other histologic alterations that often coexist with each other is intriguing, to the point that MFT may be considered a disorder of diseased thyroids [3]. Particularly intriguing is the association with PTC, that is not only a very common cause for the resection of MFT cases, but that is also found incidentally in MFT cases resected for other benign thyroid conditions. Many of these PTC are millimetric and often multifocal. Indeed, the possibility that MFT, or at least some of its fibrosing lesions, may represent premalignant PTC precursors following a follicular cell atypia-dysplasia-to-carcinoma progression similar to that hypothesized in the setting of Hashimoto thyroiditis [13–15] has been considered [2]. However, no molecular alterations - and in particular no BRAF p.V600E mutations - have been identified in our series and in additional studies [2], including a previously reported series of seven MFT cases all of which were associated with PTC elsewhere in the thyroid gland [8]. Thus, the concept that MFT is a premalignant condition remains unproven.

This study has some limitations. It includes only a relatively small number of cases. Indeed, due to its low prevalence, our number is smaller than that reported in the series of MFT collected from Dr. Rosai personal consult files during a 20-year period [2]. Another limitation is the fact that - although all MFT were tested for BRAF p.V600E with negative results - NGS molecular profiling could not be performed on all of them. In spite of this, no molecular alteration was identified in any of the cases analyzed by NGS.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that using reproducible criteria MFT is found in a small, but not insignificant minority of thyroid specimens in the routine pathology practice, nearly always associated with a variety of other thyroid alterations, particularly - but not exclusively - PTC. MFT is not merely an intriguing, but clinically irrelevant thyroid curiosity. It can be mistaken for PTC not only in surgical specimens by pathologists unaware of the entity, but also preoperatively. Lastly, our multicentric study demonstrates how the use of archived digital slides is an effective tool for collaborative pathology research.
