Interactive effects of drought and high radiation on photosystem II performance and redox homeostasis in soybean (Glycine max L.)
Authors: Vrishali Bhor, Mahesh Kumar, Jagadish Rane, Arti Guhey, Ajay Kumar Singh, Yogeshwar Singh, Shashi Shekhar, Lalit Aher, N P Singh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPhotosystem II (PSII) is extremely sensitive to drought and high light stress, both of which are expected to increase under changing climate conditions. We studied two soybean cultivars, JS335 and Kalitur, under drought stress, high radiation, and their combination. Chlorophyll fluorescence indicated decline in quantum yield with increasing light intensity and duration, particularly beyond 900 µmol photons m⁻²s⁻¹. JS335 showed better recovery from photodamage compared to Kalitur. The combined drought and high radiation treatment reduced chlorophyll content and relative water content with increased malondialdehyde (MDA) and hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) after 15 days. Antioxidant enzymes responded dynamically, with a gradual increase in catalase activity from day 3 and ascorbate peroxidase reaching its maximum on day 9. These results indicate that combined stress enhanced damage to PSII, while greater antioxidant defense and effective Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS) management in JS335 form the basis of its superior tolerance, providing worthwhile characteristics for improving soybeans under stressful conditions.Keywords: Soybean, photosystem II, soil moisture deficit, high radiation, chlorophyll fluorescence, antioxidant systemSubject terms: Biochemistry, Physiology, Plant sciences

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max) is a major global crop that provides protein and edible oil for human health1. Its growth and productivity are significantly impacted by various environmental stresses, with drought being one of the most severe, affecting all stages of plant development2,3. Drought during critical stages of growth can reduce seed quality and cause yield loss of up to 40%4,5. Soybeans are particularly vulnerable to drought during their reproductive phases, which negatively affect photosynthetic efficiency and overall yield6.

Moderate water stress restricts CO₂ fixation, creating energy imbalances that enhance ROS production and alter non-photochemical quenching, ultimately affecting photosynthetic machinery7. When light intensity exceeds the photosynthesis saturation point, high radiation stress damages PSII reaction center proteins via photo-oxidation. Such damages inhibit photosynthesis and compromise plant growth and survival8,9. However, PSII photo protection and repair systems are often insufficient under extended high irradiation9. Plants have evolved sophisticated mechanisms to sense and respond to environmental stressors, thereby minimizing damage and conserving resources. However, plant responses to drought and high radiation are often examined independently, and their combined influence on PSII function in soybean remains poorly characterized.

Although drought and high radiation stress commonly occur simultaneously under field conditions, their combined effects on photosystem II (PSII) are not always predictable from single-stress responses. However, it remains unclear whether combined drought and high radiation impose merely additive effects or generate distinct physiological constraints on PSII efficiency, recovery dynamics, and redox balance. In soybean, information is particularly limited on how genotypic differences in antioxidant regulation contribute to PSII protection under concurrent stress conditions. Addressing this gap, the present study examines PSII photochemistry, oxidative damage, and antioxidant responses under individual and combined drought and high radiation stress, providing new physiological insight into mechanisms underlying combined-stress tolerance. We hypothesize that soybean cultivars exhibiting higher PSII efficiency and antioxidant activity under combined drought and high radiation stress will demonstrate greater stress tolerance. The effects of high radiation intensity and water stress on PSII in soybean plants examined by employing various physiological and biochemical techniques including chlorophyll fluorescence tools. In addition, changes in the level of oxidative stress under water and radiation stress evaluated by measuring reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and its scavenging capacity in the plant.

Materials and methods

Plant material and treatments

Two soybean (Glycine max) varieties, JS335 and Kalitur, were grown in pots under controlled conditions in a walk-in Plant Growth Chamber (Aralab Fitoclima 1200, Aralab, Portugal). The chamber was programmed for a short-day photoperiod (10 h light/14 h dark), temperature of 28 ± 1 °C, and relative humidity of 60 ± 5%. Light intensity was set to 800 ± 25 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ during the light period. Light intensity inside the chamber was monitored using a quantum PAR sensor placed at canopy height and calibrated prior to the experiment. Relative humidity and temperature were recorded continuously using the built-in sensors of the growth chamber. Plastic pots (15 cm diameter) were filled with a standardized growth medium (soil: vermiculite: FYM = 2:1:1). Five seeds were sown per pot and later thinned to one uniform seedling per pot at two weeks after emergence. The experiment was arranged in a completely randomized design (CRD) with three replications per treatment, each replication consisting of one plant.

After four weeks of sowing, leaves of similar phenological stage were carefully excised from the plants by cutting the twigs underwater to avoid xylem cavitation. The cut ends were kept immersed in water and sealed to maintain hydration during handling. Leaves were dark-adapted for 30 min using leaf clips prior to fluorescence measurements. These excised leaves were then exposed to different light intensities (600–1100 µmol photon m⁻² s⁻¹) for 1, 2, 3, and 4 h to study the direct photoinhibitory effects of high radiation under controlled conditions. This excised-leaf approach was used to examine the inherent sensitivity and recovery capacity of Photosystem II under high radiation in a controlled manner. By minimizing whole-plant influences such as water transport and stomatal regulation, this method allowed a direct comparison of PSII responses between the two soybean cultivars. Chlorophyll fluorescence was recorded at 0, 15, 30, 45, and 60 min after exposure using an FMS-2 pulse-modulated fluorometer (Hansatech Instruments, UK). Minimal fluorescence (Fo), maximal fluorescence (Fm), and maximum quantum efficiency of PSII (Fv/Fm) were obtained following established protocols. In the second stage of the experiment, the two cultivar were subjected to drought, high radiation and their combination .

In a second stage, plants were subjected to drought, high radiation, and combined drought radiation stress. Soil field capacity (FC) was determined gravimetrically prior to stress imposition. Pots filled with the growth medium were fully saturated and allowed to drain freely until pot weight stabilized (4–8 h); the stabilized pot weight was considered as 100% pot field capacity (water holding capacity). Drought stress was imposed four weeks after sowing by withholding irrigation allowing soil moisture to decline gradually over 3–4 days until it reached approximately 40–45% field capacity. After attaining this level, soil moisture was maintained at 40–45% field capacity by daily gravimetric monitoring and controlled water addition, while control plants were maintained at 80–85% field capacity. All physiological measurements were performed on intact plants, while fully expanded leaves were sampled separately for biochemical analyses. Measurements and sampling were carried out at defined time points (0, 3, 6, 9, 12, and 15 days after stress imposition) during the stress period. High radiation stress was imposed by exposure to 1100 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ in the growth chamber. For combined stress, plants were simultaneously exposed to drought and high radiation.

Measurements of Chl fluorescence

To ensure complete relaxation of non-photochemical quenching, leaves were dark-adapted using leaf clips for 25–30 min prior to chlorophyll fluorescence measurements. Chlorophyll fluorescence parameters were then measured using a Fluorescence Monitoring System (FMS2, Hansatech Instruments, UK) to assess Photosystem II performance under the different stress treatments.

Chlorophyll contents and leaf water status

Chlorophyll content was measured according to the method of Hiscox and Israelstam, 1979. Fresh leaves were homogenized and extracted with 80% acetone for 24 h at room temperature in a dark. The absorbance of the solution was recorded at 645 and 663 nm using spectrophotometer and then total chlorophyll was estimated after10. Relative water content (RWC) was measured according to the method of11. Fully expanded leaves were collected to estimate RWC and immediately placed in polythene bags in an ice bucket to prevent moisture loss. Upon arrival in the laboratory, the fresh weight was recorded. The leaves were then floated on deionized water in a closed petri dish for 6 h at room temperature to achieve full turgidity. After blotting off surface water, the turgid weight was recorded. The leaves were dried at 65 °C for 24 h until a constant dry weight was obtained. RWC was calculated using the formula Leaf RWC (%) = (FW-DW)/(TW-DW) × 100.

Stomatal conductance and H2O2 content

Leaf stomatal conductance was measured with a porometer (Model SC-1 Leaf Porometer, Decagon devices). Measurements were taken inside the chambers on a minimum of two, fully expanded leaves per individual plant.

The content of H2O2 was determined following12. Approximately 0.5 g of fresh leaf tissues was cut into small pieces and homogenized in an ice bath with 5 ml of 0.1% TCA. After centrifugation at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C, 0.5 ml of supernatant plant extract was added to 0.5 ml of 10mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0) and 1 ml of 1 M KI. The absorbance of the supernatant was recorded at 390 nm. Final concentration of H2O2 was calculated using a standard curve plotted with known concentrations of H2O2.

Determination of leaf proline content

Free proline was extracted from fresh leaves13. The sample crushed in 3% aqueous sulfosalicylic acid. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. Supernatant was collected with acid-ninhydrin and glacial acetic acid in a test tube. It was kept in boiling water bath for 1 h at 95 °C and then reaction was terminated by keeping in ice bath. Toluene added to the mixture and vortex. The absorbance of toluene phase measured at 520 nm against toluene blank. The soluble protein content of the supernatants was determined according to14.

Lipid peroxidation

Leaf oxidative damage to lipids was estimated as the content of total 2-thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reactive substances and expressed as malondialdehyde (MDA) equivalents following15. Fresh leaf tissue (0.5 g) was homogenized in 5 mL of 0.1% trichloroacetic acid (TCA). The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C, and 2 mL of the supernatant was mixed with 2 mL of 0.67% thiobarbituric acid (TBA) in 5% TCA. The reaction mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 30 min, then cooled in an ice bath. After cooling, the mixture was centrifuged again at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The concentration of MDA was calculated spectrophotometrically from different absorbance of the supernatant at 532 and 600 nm.

Antioxidant enzyme assay

One g of leaf samples was frozen in liquid nitrogen followed by grinding in 10 mL extraction buffer (0.1 M phosphate buffer, pH 7.5, containing 0.5 mM EDTA in case of Superoxide dismutase (SOD), Catalase (CAT) and 1 mM ascorbic acid in case of Ascorbate Peroxidase (APX) to prepare the extract. The homogenates were filtered through four layers of cheesecloth and then centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min at 15000 × g. The supernatant was collected and used for the assays of enzymatic activities. All steps in the preparation of the enzyme extract were carried out at 4 °C.

Total SOD activity was estimated by the inhibition of the photochemical reduction of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) by the enzyme. 2 mM riboflavin (0.1 mL) was added in 3 mL of reaction mixture (13.33 mM methionine, 75 µM NBT, 0.1 mM EDTA, 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7.8), 50 mM sodium carbonate, 0.1 mL enzyme extract) and placing the tubes under two 15 W fluorescent lamps for 15 min to start the reaction. The absorbance was recorded at 560 nm, and one unit of enzyme activity was taken as that amount of enzyme, which reduced the absorbance reading to 50% in comparison with tubes lacking enzyme16.

Catalase was assayed by measuring the disappearance of H2O2. 0.5 mL of 75 mM H2O2 was added in 1.5 mL of 0.1 M phosphate buffer (pH 7) and 50 µL of diluted enzyme extract in 3 mL reaction mixture. The absorbance at 240 nm was monitored for 1 min, and the enzyme activity was determined based on the rate of H₂O₂ reduction17.

Ascorbate peroxidase activity was measured by monitoring the decrease in absorbance at 290 nm, corresponding to the oxidation of ascorbic acid. The reaction mixture contained 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.0), 0.5 mM ascorbic acid, 0.1 mM EDTA, 0.1 mM H₂O₂, 0.1 mL enzyme extract, and water to a final volume of 3.0 mL. The reaction was initiated by adding 0.1 mL of H₂O₂. The decrease in absorbance was recorded spectrophotometrically, and enzyme activity was calculated based on the reduction in ascorbic acid, using a standard curve generated from known concentrations of ascorbic acid18.

Statistical analysis

The experiment was conducted in a factorial completely randomized design. Data were analyzed using two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to assess the effects of genotype, treatment, and their interaction over time. When significant effects were detected, mean comparisons were performed using Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post-hoc test at P < 0.05. All data are presented as mean ± standard error (SE). Statistical analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.4.3) and plots were created using the ggplot2 package.

Results

Effects of different intensity radiation stress on PSII photochemistry

Photoinhibition of photosynthesis results in changes in the quantum efficiency of photosynthetic electron transport, which can be estimated by the fluorescence parameters, Fv/Fm, Fo and Fm values. No significant difference was observed in chlorophyll fluorescence at 600 µmol photon m-2s-1 in both the cultivars. At 900 µmol photons m⁻² s⁻¹ (and higher intensities), Kalitur exhibited a more pronounced decline in QY than JS335, indicating greater sensitivity. (Fig. 1). The study revealed that the high radiation treatment affected quantum yield more drastically in both the cultivars. Recovery of PS-II from light intensity was better in JS335 than Kalitur. PSII of JS335 recovered faster from high light stress than Kalitur.

Changes quantum yield, chlorophyll content and RWC under prolonged drought and high radiation stress

No significant changes in maximal Fv/Fm were observed in dark-adapted leaves after 3 and 6 days of drought stress. However, prolonged exposure to high radiation caused damage to the photosynthetic electron transport chain, more severely in Kalitur than JS335 (Fig. 2). Quantum yield (QY) decreased by over 50% under high radiation alone, and drought further amplified this damage by up to 40%.

Total chlorophyll content decreased in both cultivars under individual and combined water deficit and high radiation stresses, with high radiation causing greater reductions than drought alone. A marked decline in chlorophyll was evident after 6 days across all treatments. SPAD values significantly decreased under increased radiation and drought conditions in both cultivars, although drought-stressed plants retained higher chlorophyll levels up to 6 days. Results indicated significant decrease in RWC with increasing days of treatments. Compared to cultivar Kalitur, cultivar JS335 maintained a relatively greater RWC under all stress treatments. Genotypic variation in RWC content under stress was also evident. Under stress, Kalitur was more affected by the decline in RWC than JS335 ( Fig. 3).

Drought and high radiation reduce stomatal conductance

Stomatal conductance declined under water deficit and high radiation stress, with a more pronounced reduction under combined stress conditions. The magnitude of reduction increased with stress duration and differed between the two cultivars.

Proline accumulation under stress conditions

Proline content increased progressively with stress duration in both cultivars, with the highest accumulation under combined stress. Kalitur showed higher proline accumulation than JS335 under all stress treatments (Fig. 4).

Drought and high radiation stress induces ROS accumulation and lipid peroxidation in leaves

Three days of drought slightly increased the MDA concentrations compared with that of the control and increased up to 11–12 folds after 15 days. During high radiation stress MDA content increased even more in that of combined stress. MDA increased under drought, high radiation, and combined stress, with the greatest increase under combined stress. JS335 exhibitedd better tolerance in our findings (Fig. 4). The cultivar JS335 exhibited lower amount of H2O2 levels compared to Kalitur suggesting the less production of ROS in cells. There was a significant difference for production of H2O2 in genotypes and treatments.

Changes in total protein

Protein content in leaves decreased progressively in both the cultivars across the treatment with the most severe reduction under combined stress. This trend indicates accelerated protein degradation under oxidative stress, supporting the role of ROS-induced metabolic changes during combined stress. The smaller protein loss in JS335 is consistent with our hypothesis of its more robust antioxidant machinery and reduced cellular damage.

Antioxidant enzyme assay to determine the ROS production

Higher light intensity and water deficit were correlated with higher enzyme activities, especially in leaves with combined stress after 15 days of treatment. SOD, CAT, and APX activities increased significantly under all stress treatments, with the strongest activation under combined stress (Fig. 5). This enhanced enzymatic response supports our hypothesis that antioxidant systems are upregulated to counter the amplified ROS burden during dual stress. JS335 showed consistently higher and earlier induction of these enzymes, indicating a more efficient antioxidant defense network and explaining its superior PSII protection and stress tolerance relative to Kalitur.

Discussion

Plants are frequently exposed to multiple interacting stress factors, and their physiological and biochemical responses very widely among genotypes. In the present study, drought clearly deepened the adverse effects of high radiation on Photosystem II (PSII), demonstrating that limitations imposed by water deficit magnify damage due to photoinhibition. Under drought, lower CO₂ assimilation limits the effective use of absorbed light energy, resulting in excessive excitation on the photosynthetic electron transport chain. When this condition coincides with high radiation, this promotes photodamage and oxidative stress19.

Chlorophyll fluorescence is a reliable, noninvasive tool for accessing PSII function under stress. Reductions in Fv/Fm under extended high radiation and coupled stress indicate a loss in PSII efficiency, which may indicate structural and functional degradation of PSII reaction centres. Damage of this kind is frequently associated with D1 protein disruption and constraints in PSII repair mechanisms under stress. Drought stress is known to further constrain PSII recovery prolonging photoinhibition when plants are simultaneously exposed to excess light20,21. The differences in PSII efficiency and recovery indicate varying levels of photo inhibitory stress between cultivars. Leaf-level measurements of short-term responses to high radiation suggest that Photosystem II sensitivity and recovery vary by genotype. These inherent differences are consistent with the variation in whole-plant photosynthetic performance observed under combined drought and radiation stress. It help explain why exposure to multiple stresses at the same time can produce outcomes that are worse than what you would expect by simply adding the effects of each stress on its own. Oxidative stress emerged as a central component of plant responses to combined drought and high radiation. When plants are under stress, higher levels of reactive oxygen species (ROS), especially H2O2, suggest that more electrons are leaking from the photosynthetic machinery. Lipid peroxidation caused by ROS can damage membrane structure, such as the thylakoid membranes, which can make PSII less stable and reduce electron transport22,23. The rise in H2O2 accompanied by the drop in PSII performance suggests that oxidative stress is closely associated with impaired photochemical activity12,24,25.

Plants rely on enzymatic antioxidant systems to cope with oxidative stress, and the effectiveness of these responses often varies among genotypes. Tolerant genotypes tend to have higher antioxidant defenses, which can prevent oxidative damage and help photosynthesis stays stable26. Superoxide dismutase is important because it converts superoxide radicals into H₂O₂, and then catalase and ascorbate peroxidase break down the H₂O₂ to reduce oxidative stress23. Stress induced rise in H₂O₂ have been widely observed and generally rely on stress duration and its level27,28. Timely triggering of antioxidant enzymes is often seen as key for stress tolerance because it reduces the degree of oxidative deterioration and its duration29. In this context, the earlier and stronger antioxidant induction observed in JS335 likely contributed to better PSII protection and sustained photosynthetic performance under combined stress.

In this study, JS335 induced antioxidant enzymes earlier and to a greater extent than Kalitur, which suggests that JS335 has a more efficient antioxidant defense system. This enhanced antioxidant regulation was associated with lower oxidative damage and better maintenance of PSII function, explaining the superior stress tolerance of JS335 under combined drought and high radiation stress.

Proline considered as a scavenger of ROS that reduced the damage of oxidative stress induced by drought, high salinity, high or low light, or UV irradiation. Although proline is often considered an important osmoprotectant and ROS scavenger under abiotic stress, its role in stress tolerance appears to be context dependent. In the present study, Kalitur accumulated higher levels of proline than JS335 under stress conditions, yet exhibited greater susceptibility to combined drought and high radiation stress. This implies that proline accumulation mostly indicate stress intensity rather than contributing directly to photoprotection or tolerance. Similar observations have been reported where higher proline levels were associated with stress severity rather than improved tolerance30. In the other hand, the higher tolerance of JS335 was linked to better PSII stability and a stronger antioxidant defense system. This indicates that coordinated physiological and biochemical control is more important than osmolyte accumulation by itself.

Overall, our findings show that the simultaneous occurrence of drought and high radiation places substantially greater physiological and biochemical constraints on soybean plants than either stress alone. Under these conditions, stress tolerance is not governed by a single trait, but instead depends on the coordinated maintenance of photosystem II stability and an effective antioxidant defense system. The better performance of JS335 indicate the importance of efficient regulation of reactive oxygen species and the ability to sustain photochemical activity, which together appear to be key determinants of plant resilience under increasingly complex environmental stress scenarios19,25,29.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates clear morpho-physiological responses of soybean cultivars to abiotic stresses during critical growth stages. Key parameters such as quantum yield, PSII efficiency, total chlorophyll content, and stomatal conductance were found to correlate strongly with both the type of stress experienced and the duration of that stress. Cultivars exhibiting lower reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, reduced lipid peroxidation, and higher antioxidant scavenging capacity tended to perform better under stress conditions. Additionally, the experiment demonstrated that the impacts of combined stress were more pronounced than those of individual stressors. Among the cultivars evaluated, JS335 showed relatively greater tolerance to simultaneous low soil moisture and high radiation stress compared to Kalitur. This improved tolerance was associated with greater PSII stability and a more effective antioxidant defense system, which likely contributed to improved ROS regulation under stress conditions. Overall, the findings highlight the importance of PSII stability and oxidative stress management as key traits that may contribute to improved soybean resilience under adverse environmental conditions.
