Structure‐Controlled Cyclopentadithiophene Derived Non‐Fullerene Acceptors for Efficient Near‐Infrared Organic Photodetectors
Authors: Jong‐Woon Ha, Jae‐Hyun Kim, Byoungwook Park, Honghyun Park, Sung Cheol Yoon, Jaebin Jeong, Seunghun Lee, Jaeil Park, Jun‐Gyu Choi, Seo‐Jin Ko, Seung‐Hoon Lee, Sungjun Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHigh‐performance near‐infrared (NIR) organic photodetectors are still limited by insufficient absorption beyond 900 nm and the concomitant increase in dark current and noise. Here, we present organic photodetectors based on novel low‐bandgap non‐fullerene acceptors (NFAs), OC and SC, designed via a molecular strategy that introduces dithieno[3,2‐b:2′,3′‐d]pyran (DTP) and dithieno[3,2‐b:2′,3′‐d]thiopyran (DTTP) as central cores. The resulting PTB7‐Th:OC organic photodiodes (OPDs) exhibit well‐minimized dark‐current densities (∼10−8 A cm−2) under a reverse bias of −2 V and maintain high spectral responsivities across the short‐NIR region, reaching 0.4 A W−1 around 900 nm and remaining appreciable up to 1000 nm, leading to an excellent shot‐noise‐limited specific detectivity exhibiting 2.34 × 1012 Jones. Furthermore, the practical applicability of PTB7‐Th:OC‐based OPDs is demonstrated in a NIR photoplethysmogram sensor, which delivers stable and clearly resolved signals. Overall, this work highlights DTP/DTTP‐based NFAs as promising materials for high‐performance NIR‐responsive OPDs and provides useful design guidelines for future NIR photodetection applications.Keywords: DTP, DTTP, near infrared, organic photodetector, photoplethysmogram

Introduction

Near‐infrared (NIR) organic photodiodes (OPDs) have recently gained significant attention due to their emerging applications in biomedical sensing, machine vision, optical communications, and environmental monitoring [1, 2, 3, 4]. Compared with silicon‐based photodetectors, OPDs offer several advantages, including solution processability, low‐cost manufacturing, and favorable compatibility with flexible substrates [5, 6, 7]. Recent advances in state‐of‐the‐art non‐fullerene acceptors (NFAs) have enabled precise tuning of the optical and electrochemical properties, allowing the photoresponse of organic semiconductors to be extended toward the deep‐NIR region [8, 9, 10, 11]. Despite such progress in broadening the absorption range (>1.0 eV), the short‐NIR region (850‐1000 nm) remains a distinct and important spectral window that involves different device requirements [12, 13]. This wavelength range is particularly relevant for applications such as photoplethysmography (PPG)‐based physiological monitoring, proximity and gesture sensing, structured‐light depth imaging, and short‐range optical communications [14, 15, 16, 17]. Accordingly, molecular design strategies that can ensure reliable short‐NIR absorption while simultaneously minimizing dark‐current density (J d) and enhancing photocurrent generation remain essential for achieving high‐performance OPDs in this spectral range.

Among the NIR‐absorbing NFAs, Y6 and its derivatives, which comprise fusedcore acceptor–donor–acceptor (A–D–A) framework, have been widely employed owing to their strong absorption coefficient (ε) values (∼105 cm−1) and favorable optoelectronic properties [18, 19]. However, their absorption onset is typically limited to below ∼900 nm, leading to a significant drop in shot‐noise‐limited specific detectivity (D*) at wavelengths approaching 950 nm. In addition to this spectral limitation, the fused‐aromatic donor core of A–D–A framework generally requires long synthetic routes and limited flexibility for structural modifications. By contrast, acceptor–π–donor–π–acceptor (A–π–D–π–A) type NFAs provide a more synthetically accessible framework in which the π‐bridge and donor units can be modularly tuned, allowing not only targeted short‐NIR absorption profile but also systematic control over frontier molecular orbital energies and charge‐transport behavior [20]. The representative A–π–D–π–A–type NFA, CTIC‐4F, exhibits a strong NIR absorption profile and efficient photoconversion, delivering a responsivity (R) of 0.51 A W−1 at 830 nm [21, 22, 23, 24]. Moreover, its π‐bridge framework facilitates a comparatively straightforward synthesis with high overall yield, thereby establishing this design as a highly viable framework for developing next‐generation NIR‐responsive materials. However, despite these advantages, CTIC‐4F still suffers from the high J d (∼10−6 A cm−2) under applied reverse bias at ‐3.0 V. These limitations indicate that further molecular engineering within the A–π–D–π–A framework is necessary to achieve materials capable of sustaining short‐NIR absorption while suppressing J d.

Several heteroatom‐bridged donor units related to cyclopentadithiophene (CPDT), including dithieno[3,2‐b:2′,3′‐d]pyran (DTP) and dithieno[3,2‐b:2',3'‐d]thiopyran (DTTP), have previously been explored in organic semiconductors [25, 26, 27]. DTP and DTTP are considered promising for electron‐donating building blocks to replace CPDT analogues, because their distinct electronic characteristics allow effective modulation of both absorption behavior and frontier molecular orbital energy levels. However, their implementation as central donor units in NFA systems for OPD applications remains largely unexplored. Compared with CPDT, DTP exhibits a comparatively stronger electron‐donating character, which narrows the optical bandgap and induces a more substantial bathochromic shift, while also promoting a more favorable charge‐transport tendency within the A–π–D–π–A framework. On the other hand, DTTP‐based organic semiconducting materials exhibit a less extended absorption profile and more stabilized frontier molecular orbital energy levels, which are consistent with the π‐accepting characteristics associated with the sulfur unit in DTTP. Meanwhile, both DTP/DTTP‐based donor cores can maintain low J d characteristics by reducing charge carrier leakage enabled by their deep HOMO levels. Therefore, these distinct electronic characteristics suggest that heteroatom‐bridged donor units (DTP and DTTP) provide an effective strategy for tuning the optoelectronic properties of NFAs.

In this work, NFAs capable of extending absorption into the short‐NIR region while maintaining low J d were designed and synthesized for NIR‐OPD applications. We incorporated these two donor units, each possessing distinct structural and electronic characteristics, into the A–π–D–π–A framework to investigate how donor‐core substitution governs intra‐charge transport behavior, absorption characteristics, and short‐NIR photodetection performance. As a result, the DTP‐based NFA (OC) exhibits a more extended absorption profile reaching ∼950 nm (energy bandgap (E g) = 1.29 eV), whereas the DTTP‐based NFA (SC) shows a less extended absorption (E g = 1.39 eV) and more deep‐lying frontier molecular orbital energy levels. The resulting OPDs exhibit significantly low J d (∼10−8 A cm−2) and high R (∼0.4 A W−1) under reverse bias of −2 V. Notably, PTB7‐Th:OC maintains a high photodetection performance at 940 nm with D* of 2.3 × 1012 Jones and R of 0.21 A W−1, while PTB7‐Th:SC shows obviously reduced D* and R owing to its absorption limitation (Table S1, Supporting Information). These findings indicate that donor‐core engineering using the DTP and DTTP within A–π–D–π–A framework provides an effective rational molecular design approach for tailoring short‐NIR OPD performance.

Results and Discussion

Systematic synthetic routes for the NFAs are illustrated in Figure 1a, and detailed procedures are provided in the Monomer and NFA syntheses section in Supporting Information. The central donor cores, DTP and DTTP, were coupled with 5‐bromo‐4‐(2‐ethylhexyl)thiophene−2‐carbaldehyde via Stille cross‐coupling to afford compounds (1) (Figure S1) and compound (2) (Figure S2), which subsequently underwent Knoevenagel condensation with 2‐(3‐oxo−2,3‐dihydro‐1H‐inden‐1‐ylidene)malononitrile to yield the final NFAs (SC and OC). The 1H and 13C NMR spectra of each NFA are illustrated in Figures S3 and S4, while MALDI‐TOF spectra of the synthesized NFAs are plotted in Figure S5. The synthesized NFAs exhibited good solubility at room temperature in common chlorinated organic solvents, such as dichloromethane, chloroform, and chlorobenzene. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) were carried out to investigate the thermal properties of the synthesized NFAs. The decomposition temperatures (T d, at 5 wt.% weight loss) were measured to be 306°C and 293°C for SC and OC, which means good thermal stability for the manufacturing of electronic devices (Figure S6a). The corresponding DSC thermograms are shown in Figure S6b,c, SC showed an exothermic transition at 133.2°C (ΔH cc, 23.99 J g−1) due to cold crystallization of the as‐prepared sample, indicating that a possible phase transition from the amorphous phases to ordered phases, followed by strong endothermic melting transition at 193.4°C (ΔH m, 26.64 J g−1) in the first heating scan. By contrast, OC exhibited lower phase transition temperature (T cc = 105.4°C, T m = 183.8°C) and smaller enthalpy changes (ΔH cc = 7.08 J g−1, ΔHm = 27.13 J g−1) than SC. On the other hand, SC and OC exhibited no distinct phase transition during cooling, indicating relatively slow crystallization rates.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations at the B3LYP/6‐31G(d) level were performed to study the optimized geometric structure, frontier molecular orbital energy levels, and electronic distribution. All side‐alkyl chains were replaced by methyl to simplify the calculation. To investigate the unique characteristics of central core units, we prepared the DFT models shown in Figure S7a–c, Supporting Information. A clear difference is observed in the electrostatic potential (ESP) distribution between SC and OC. In particular, the oxygen atom in the DTP core exhibits a relatively positive ESP distribution. In contrast, a slightly negative ESP distribution is observed around the sulfur atom in the DTTP core, indicating the stronger electron‐donating character of oxygen compared with sulfur. Therefore, it means DTP core possesses a stronger electron‐donating character, which can lead to stronger intramolecular charge transfer strength between the central core and terminal units than that of SC. Consistent with the electronic distribution, the calculated frontier molecular orbital energies are shown in Figure S7d. OC possesses the shallowest HOMO energy level (−5.45 eV) and narrowest calculated bandgap (1.83 eV), whereas SC exhibits a deeper HOMO level (‐5.54 eV) owing to the weaker electron‐donating nature of DTTP core.

The optical and electrochemical properties of photoactive materials are provided in Figure 1c,d. A comparison of the optical absorption and electrochemical properties is a crucial parameter from the molecular design concept point of view. The synthesized NFAs exhibited broad absorption profiles. Especially, OC exhibited an obviously broader absorption profile than SC, because DTP featured a stronger electron‐donating character than DTTP, leading to a strong intramolecular charge transfer (ICT) effect between the central unit and the terminal unit. The estimated ε value of OC was much higher than that of SC. Thus, OC would be expected to have a sensitive response at a specific NIR region even in a weak light source. There is an approximately 100 nm red‐shifted absorbance between the solution and thin film states, as shown in Figure 1d. The synthesized NFAs displayed absorption edges of 891 nm for SC and 967 nm for OC, respectively. Optical bandgap (E g opt) values of the NFAs were estimated to be 1.39 eV for SC and 1.28 eV for OC, respectively, from the corresponding absorption edges in the film states. In particular, OC can show a strong absorption profile in the NIR region that shows a very suitable potential for short‐NIR OPD applications.

The inverted OPDs with the architecture indium‐tin‐oxide (ITO)/ZnO/PTB7‐Th:NFA/MoO3/Ag were fabricated to evaluate the photoelectric performance of PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC films (Figure 2a). The optimization of the photoelectric performance of OPDs depending on the donor/acceptor (D/A) blend ratio, thermal annealing condition, and thickness of active layers was carried out considering a trade‐off relationship between J d and photocurrent density (J ph). The optimized current density–voltage (J–V) characteristics of PTB7‐Th:NFA OPDs in both dark and under simulated solar illumination (AM 1.5G) at various intensities (from 100 to 0.1 mW cm−2) are shown in Figure 2b,c and summarized in Table 2. At −2 V biased condition, the PTB7‐Th:OC OPD exhibited slightly higher J ph value of 2.09 × 10−2 A cm−2 at 100 mW cm−2 solar illumination than that of PTB7‐Th:SC (1.78 × 10−2 A cm−2). When the high intensity was gradually reduced to 0.1 mW cm−2, PTB7‐Th:OC still maintained a relatively high J ph value of 2.83 × 10−5 A cm−2 compared with SC‐based OPDs (2.67 × 10−5 A cm−2). These findings are attributed to the high ε and broad absorption edge of OC, whereas SC is limited by its short absorption onset (below 900 nm). The differences in device characteristics are also reflected in their photovoltaic parameters. As shown in Figure S9 and Table S2, the PTB7‐Th:SC device exhibits a higher open‐circuit voltage (V OC) than the PTB7‐Th:OC device owing to the relatively higher energy level of SC compared to OC. In contrast, the broader absorption range of the PTB7‐Th:OC device leads to a higher short‐circuit current density (J SC) compared to that of the PTB7‐Th:SC device. This difference in the absorption onset leads to pronounced disparities in the J ph of PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC devices under 870 and 900 nm LED illumination (Figure S10). Under 870 nm LED illumination, a comparison of J ph at a reverse bias range across varying light intensities reveals that the PTB7‐Th:SC devices consistently exhibit lower J ph values than the PTB7‐Th:OC devices (Figure S10a,b). Similarly, under 900 nm LED illumination, the J ph values of PTB7‐Th:SC devices remain substantially lower across all light intensities, reaching approximately one order of magnitude smaller than those of PTB7‐Th:OC devices (Figure S10c,d). Meanwhile, the J d was measured at reverse bias of −2 V to evaluate the shot noise‐limited characteristics. PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs showed a comparable J d of 1.84 × 10−8 A cm−2 to that of PTB7‐Th:SC OPDs (2.55 × 10−8 A cm−2). These results show that PTB7‐Th:OC device shows superior NIR detection ability rather than PTB7‐Th:SC device, with the advantage of higher J ph at NIR wavelengths and lower J d.

The results showed excellent reproducibility across all key device parameters, confirming the reliability of the obtained data (Figures S11–S14). Consequently, given the comparable J d of the PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs, the superior D* of the PTB7‐Th:OC OPD than that of the PTB7‐Th:SC OPD is presumed to arise from the enhanced R and more efficient NIR absorption characteristics of the PTB7‐Th:OC blend film. Furthermore, PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs exhibit reduced J d values on the order of 10−8 A cm−2 while maintaining R values of approximately 0.4 A W−1, thereby achieving higher D* over 1012 Jones compared with other NFA‐based OPDs (Table S3).

It should be noted that the D* values discussed above were evaluated under the assumption of shot‐noise‐limited operation. To verify the validity of this assumption for the present OPDs, a detailed analysis of the noise components was performed. In OPDs, white noise (i white), which consists of shot noise (i shot) and thermal noise (i thermal), is generally regarded as the dominant contributor to the total noise current [22, 31]. When the shunt resistance (R sh) is sufficiently high, the i thermal component becomes negligible, and under a reverse bias of approximately −2 V, increased charge injection enhances the shot‐noise contribution, resulting in i shot becoming the principal component of the i white [32]. Therefore, to clearly justify the use of shot‐noise‐based D* for evaluating the relative performance of the PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs, the i white level was calculated and subsequently compared with the corresponding i shot component. To estimate the R sh values of PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs, the I d under reverse‐biased conditions was extracted (Figure S15). The measured R sh values of PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs were 33.9 and 48.7 MΩ, respectively, which is consistent with the trend observed in their I d characteristics. While high D* ensures reliable detection of weak optical signals, practical PPG sensing additionally requires a fast temporal response to resolve pulsatile light variations accurately. Therefore, the switching speed and transient photoresponse of the OPDs were systematically investigated. Since the dynamic response is closely related to charge transport and molecular packing in the active layer, the structural characteristics of the PTB7‐Th:NFA blend films were first examined by 2D‐GIWAXS, as shown in Figure 3a. The GIWAXS patterns of the pristine SC and OC films (Figure 3a‐i, ii) exhibit a preference for edge‐on molecular orientation with strong π–π diffraction along with in‐plane direction. Upon blending with PTB7‐Th, both the PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC films (Figure 3a‐iii, iv) display diffraction patterns characteristic of a predominantly face‐on molecular orientation, which is favorable for vertical charge transport. The corresponding one‐dimensional intensity profiles extracted from the 2D‐GIWAXS patterns. Figure 3b shows that the (100) lamellar and (010) π–π stacking peak positions of the two blend films are nearly identical, indicating comparable intermolecular stacking distances. Interestingly, although the blend films predominantly exhibit a face‐on orientation, a noticeable fraction of edge‐on diffraction is also observed. This contribution is more pronounced in the PTB7‐Th:SC blend film, which indicates a higher degree of mixed orientation. To further quantify the degree of the molecular orientation and orientational disorder, the azimuthal intensity distribution of the (010) π–π stacking peak was analyzed (Figure 3c). Specifically, orientations with an azimuthal angle range of 0–20° are assigned to the face‐on fraction, whereas 60–80° correspond to the edge‐on fraction. Based on this analysis, PTB7‐Th:OC blend exhibits a more prominent fraction of the population in the face‐on orientation (50.0%) than PTB7‐Th:SC blend (45.8%). In contrast, PTB7‐Th:SC blend shows a noticeably larger edge‐on fraction (22.0%), indicating a significantly less‐ordered orientation and orientational disorder in the SC‐based blend films. Based on the GIWAXS characterizations, the more‐ordered orientation in PTB7‐Th:OC blend is associated with suppressed trap‐assisted transport, resulting in lower J d and higher J ph. This interpretation is further supported by atomic force microscopy (AFM) analysis; as shown in Figure S16, both blend films exhibit well‐distributed and uniform bulk‐heterojunction (BHJ) morphologies with smooth surfaces characterized by root‐mean‐square roughness values below nanometer‐scale, thereby excluding large‐scale morphological inhomogeneity as the primary origin of the observed differences in dynamic response.

The μ values for the charge carriers in the PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC blends were determined to be 6.39 × 10−5 and 8.22 × 10−5 cm2 V−1s−1, respectively, indicating that the PTB7‐Th:OC blend film possesses more efficient photo‐induced charge transport characteristics than the PTB7‐Th:SC blend film. Moreover, the ‐3 dB cutoff frequency (f ‐3 dB), which represents the photoresponse speed of the OPDs, was measured under NIR illumination at a bias voltage of −2 V (Figure 3f). The PTB7‐Th:OC OPD exhibited an f ‐3 dB of 180.85 kHz, whereas the PTB7‐Th:SC OPD showed a value of 127.56 kHz, confirming that the PTB7‐Th:OC OPD demonstrates a faster NIR photoresponse than its PTB7‐Th:SC OPD. Collectively, the PTB7‐Th:OC OPD exhibits not only superior NIR absorption but also more efficient photo‐induced charge transport characteristics compared to the PTB7‐Th:SC, enabling more effective extraction of photogenerated carriers. These combined advantages are therefore expected to lead to the higher EQE and R observed in the PTB7‐Th:OC OPD, which, in turn, can reasonably account for its enhanced NIR D*.

To evaluate whether these material and device‐level advantages translate into practical performance gains under biologically relevant, low‐intensity NIR conditions, the OPDs were subsequently integrated into a photoplethysmography (PPG) sensing platform. Figure 4a illustrates the schematic configuration of the transmission‐type PPG sensor implemented in this study. The system operates by positioning the light source and the OPD on opposite sides of a finger, where the incident light is partially absorbed and scattered by the skin, blood vessels, and tissues before being captured by the detector to monitor real‐time changes in blood volume [44, 45, 46]. As depicted in Figure 4b, the choice of NIR wavelengths is crucial for effective biometric sensing. At the same time, visible light penetrates less than 1 mm of human tissue, whereas NIR light can reach depths of approximately 2.5 mm, enabling the detection of deep‐tissue blood flow with higher clarity [47, 48]. To verify the practical utility of our devices, we compared the PPG sensing capabilities of PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs (Figure 4c–f). Under NIR illumination, both PPG sensors exhibited the capability to resolve cardiac pulse signals associated with systolic and diastolic phases under sufficiently strong excitation (940 nm, 92 mW cm−2; Figure 4c,d). Notably, the PTB7‐Th:SC device was able to generate discernible pulse waveforms at 940 nm despite its relatively lower NIR detectivity, which can be attributed to the broad emission spectrum of the LED source (Figure S18a). However, clear differences in device performance became evident as the illumination conditions approached more realistic, low‐intensity regimes relevant to deep‐tissue sensing. Under weak NIR excitation at a longer wavelength (1050 nm, 0.212 mW cm−2) using an LED with emission spectrum depicted in Figure S18b, the PTB7‐Th:SC‐based sensor failed to produce a stable and interpretable PPG signal, with the output dominated by noise and temporal fluctuations (Figure 4e). In contrast, the PTB7‐Th:OC‐based sensor consistently maintained high signal fidelity and yielded well‐defined pulse waveforms under identical conditions (Figure 4f). Although the absorption of the PTB7‐Th:OC blend decreases beyond 1000 nm, reliable PPG extraction is still achieved owing to sufficiently high D* of the PTB7‐Th:OC‐based device under low illumination conditions. Specifically, at 1050 nm, the PTB7‐Th:OC device exhibits a D* of 3.25 × 1011 Jones, which enables detection of illumination levels as low as ∼10−4 mW cm−2. In contrast, the PTB7‐Th:SC device shows a significantly lower D* (4.53 × 1010 Jones), and therefore is unable to reliably detect such weak signals [24]. It should be noted that, due to the finite spectral width of the LED emission, the measured PPG signal may include contributions from shorter wavelengths where the device exhibits higher responsivity. Therefore, the present results do not represent strictly monochromatic detection at 1050 nm, but rather demonstrate the capability of the OC‐based OPD to operate under extended NIR illumination conditions. These observations indicate that the enhanced NIR sensitivity of the PTB7‐Th:OC blend is not merely advantageous under extreme conditions, but is critical for robust and reliable biometric monitoring in low‐photon environments, particularly within the extended NIR window where tissue penetration is maximized.

To further evaluate the practical applicability of the PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC‐based PPG sensors, additional experiments were conducted to examine the photocurrent stability of the corresponding OPD devices under mechanical deformation and thermal stress under −2 V biased conditions. Based on the 6–µm scale low bending radius (Figure S19), the mechanical stress stability measurements showed that both PTB7‐Th:OC and PTB7‐Th:SC devices preserved more than 96.4% and 97.9% of their initial photocurrent, respectively, despite compression and relaxation corresponding to 100%–200% strain (Figure S20). Furthermore, the thermal stability measurements demonstrated that, even after approximately 200 min of heating at 85°C in air under a −2 V bias, the PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC OPD devices retained 92.1% and 93.0% of their initial photocurrent, respectively (Figure S21). These results underscore the intrinsic mechanical and environmental resilience of the PTB7‐Th:SC and PTB7‐Th:OC devices and highlight their strong potential for integration into skin‐conformal NIR‐PPG sensing platforms.

Conclusion

High‐performance solution‐processed NIR‐sensitive OPDs were realized using two low‐bandgap A–π–D–π–A type NFAs incorporating DTP and DTTP donor units. The resulting NFAs, OC (DTP‐based) and SC (DTTP‐based), show an extended absorption profile into the NIR region. In particular, OC exhibits a high ε (∼105 M cm−1) and broad NIR absorption extending to 950 nm. Consequently, PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs display a well‐suppressed dark current density (∼10−8 A cm−2) under a reverse bias of −2 V and a high R (0.21 A W−1) at 940 nm, whereas PTB7‐Th:SC devices exhibit a comparatively narrower photoresponse below ∼850 nm due to the larger E g of SC (1.39 eV). The measured D* of PTB7‐Th:OC reaches 4.83 × 1012 and 2.34 × 1012 Jones at 880 and 940 nm, respectively, benefiting from the high ε and μ of OC, and is competitive with the reported solution‐processed NIR OPDs. PPG measurements using PTB7‐Th:OC OPDs deliver continuous, clearly resolved signals under 940 and 1050 nm illumination, demonstrating their practical NIR biosensing capability. These results highlight the promise of the DTP‐based donor‐core strategy for NIR‐responsive NFAs and provide useful guidelines for designing high‐performance NIR OPDs for applications in optical communications, digital healthcare, and environmental monitoring.

Experimental Section

Materials

(5,5‐Dioctyl‐5H‐dithieno[3,2‐b:2′,3′‐d]thiopyran‐2,7‐diyl)bis(trimethylstannane) (DTTP), (5,5‐dioctyl‐5H‐dithieno[3,2‐b:2′,3′‐d]pyran‐2,7‐diyl)bis(trimethylstannane) (DTP), 5‐bromo‐4‐(2‐ethylhexyl)thiophene‐2‐carbaldehyde were synthesized using a previously reported method [26, 27, 49]. All other reagents were purchased from Sigma‐Aldrich and were used as received without further purification. Further synthesis process of OC and SC molecules is demonstrated in the Monomer and NFA syntheses section of the Supporting Information.

Characterization Methods

Spectroscopic and Thermal Characterization

1H and 13C nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectra were measured using a 400 MHz spectrometer (Varian Mercury Plus). Thermal analyses (TGA, DSC) were performed using Q600 (TA Instruments) under an inert N2 atmosphere, with heating/cooling rates of 10°C min−1. Mass spectrometric analysis was carried out using a MALDI‐TOF spectrometer (Voyager‐DE STR), and the results were obtained. The ultraviolet−visible spectra were recorded using a UV–vis spectrophotometer (UV‐1800).

Electrochemical and Computational Analysis

Cyclic voltammetry experiments were performed with an electrochemical analyzer (CH Instruments) in acetonitrile solutions containing 0.1 M tetrabutylammonium tetrafluoroborate (Bu4NBF4) as the supporting electrolyte, with Ag/AgNO3 as the reference electrode, a platinum wire as the counter electrode, and a platinum working electrode. Density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed using the Gaussian 09 W package with the Becke three‐parameter Lee–Yang–Parr (B3LYP) function and the 6–31G(d) basis set to elucidate the highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and the lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energy levels and backbone planarity.

Morphological Characterization

Two‐dimensional grazing‐incidence wide‐angle X‐ray scattering (2D‐GIWAXS) experiments were performed at the 9A beamlines of the Pohang Accelerator Laboratory (PAL), Korea. X‐rays with a wavelength of 1.1 Å (11.08 keV) were used. The incidence angle (0.12°) was chosen to allow for complete penetration of the X‐rays into the copolymer film. The surface morphologies of the copolymer thin films were characterized using Atomic force microscopy (AFM) (Nanoscope, Bruker, Inc.) while operating in tapping mode.

Optoelectronic Characterization

The performance of the organic solar cells (OSC) was confirmed using a XES‐50S2 solar simulator (San‐Ei, Japan) and Keithley 2400 source meter (Tektronix, USA). When measuring current density–voltage (J–V) graphs under AM 1.5G light conditions, the light intensity was calibrated using a BS‐520BK Si standard reference device (BUNKOUKEIKI, Japan). 870 and 900 nm LEDs (Thorlabs, USA) were used in measuring J–V characteristics of OPDs under near‐infrared light intensity variations. The external quantum efficiency (EQE) data were measured using the Spectral QE Measurement System (TNE TECH CO., LTD, Korea). The EQE values were obtained as a function of wavelength in the range of 300–1100 nm using a 300 W Xe lamp as the light source, and the optical power of the lamp in each wavelength was measured using a silicon photodiode in the 300–960 nm wavelength range, and an indium gallium arsenide photodiode in the 960–1100 nm wavelength range.

Electrical and Biosignal Measurements

The frequency response and photo‐CELIV were measured under dark conditions using a DC2200 1‐channel LED Driver and four different types of fiber‐coupled LED – M940F3 at 940 nm light pulse (Thorlabs, USA). When measuring photoplethysmography (PPG) signals, 940 and 1050 nm LEDs (Thorlabs, USA) were located on fingertips. The measured PPG signals from OPDs were amplified by an SR570 current preamplifier and recorded in a data acquisition system (DAQami). All participants for the skin‐conformal biosignal sensing experiments were co‐authors of this manuscript and provided informed consent, who were approved by the Ajou University Institutional Review Board (AjouIRB, 202410‐HB‐003).

Mechanical and Thermal Stability Measurements

To measure mechanical stability, freestanding flexible OPDs were laminated onto a pre‐stretched adhesive elastomer. Gold wirings served as electrical contacts, fabricated by depositing 100 nm patterned Au onto 12.5 µm polyimide films via a shadow mask. Electrical contact was established using conductive adhesive tape (ECATT 9703, 3 M), and the wiring edges were connected with alligator clips. In case of thermal stability, we measured the J ph under 1 Sun condition with device annealing time variation at 85°C. The devices were not encapsulated via any kind of film.

Device Fabrication

Bulk heterojunction OPDs were fabricated with an inverted structure, consisting of indium tin oxide (ITO)/ZnO/active layer/MoO3/Ag. Typically, the ZnO layer (∼30 nm) acting as an electron transporting layer was coated onto ITO (pre‐cleaned with UV‐ozone plasma), as previously reported. The active layer solutions were prepared as follows: blending donor and acceptor of 1:1.5 weight ratio in chlorobenzene (total concentration = 20 mg mL−1 , add chloronaphtalene as an additive with 2% volume ratio in PTB7‐Th:SC solution and no additive in PTB7‐Th:OC solution) by stirring overnight at 60°C for 300 rpm in a glovebox filled with Argon. After drying the active layer, MoO3 (∼5 nm) and Ag (∼100 nm) layers were formed by thermal evaporation under a base pressure of 10−7 Torr. The effective area of the device was 4 mm2. All samples for dark current were analyzed by the probe station, Center for Research Facilities (CRIEF) at Kongju National University.

In the case of flexible devices, the same bulk heterojunction OPD structure was fabricated on flexible polymer substrates. Glass substrates were sequentially cleaned with detergent, deionized water, acetone, and isopropyl alcohol using 5 min ultrasonication per step, followed by nitrogen drying and 10 min oxygen plasma treatment. A fluorinated polymer solution (Novec 1700:7100 = 1:6 vol%) was spin‐coated at 2000 rpm for 60 s, followed by deposition of a 1 µm Parylene‐C film via CVD and thermal annealing at 120°C for 1 h. A 500 nm SU‐8 layer was spin‐coated (5000 rpm, 60 s) for planarization, soft‐baked at 95°C for 2.5 min, UV‐exposed (365 nm, 2 min), and hard‐baked at 150°C for 1 h. A 100 nm ITO layer was then sputtered without substrate heating. The remaining layers (ZnO/active layer/MoO3/Ag) were fabricated identically. Finally, a 1 µm Parylene‐C passivation layer was deposited via CVD.
