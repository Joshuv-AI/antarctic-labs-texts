Radiology of malignant degeneration of pilonidal sinus: Report of a case and review of the literature
Authors: Fatima Saddouki, Fatima Oussi, Insaf Rhalem, Nizar El Bouardi, Amal Akammar, Meryem Haloua, My Youssef Alaoui Lamrani, Meryem Boubbou, Mustapha Maaroufi, Badr Alami
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Pilonidal sinus disease is a frequent and recurrent pathology in young adults, with a male predominance, while malignant transformation of the pilonidal sinus is a rare complication, it occurs in 0.1% of patients, with a poor prognosis. Early surgical removal of the primary lesion remains the best treatment. We report a case of malignant transformation of pilonidal disease into squamous cell carcinoma.

Introduction

Pilonidal disease is a chronic inflammatory disorder typically located in the sacrococcygeal area, but it can also be found in the axilla, umbilicus, and anogenital region [1]. The risk factors associated with this condition are obesity, sedentary lifestyle, family history, and trauma or irritation to the gluteal cleft skin [2]. Untreated pilonidal disease may lead to a purulent discharge and intermittent pain in the chronic phase or malignant transformation.

Malignant transformation is exceptional [3], rarely reported in the literature, and observed mainly in chronic forms (recurrent and untreated primary pilonidal disease). It was found that malignant transformation occurs in around 0.1% of patients, and in 88% of these cases, squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) was the histological type. Image examinations with a CT scan and MRI reveal local and lymph node infiltration of the tumor and help in treatment planification. The gold standard for treatment is radical excision of the neoplasm with tumor-free margins.

The rarity of this pathology has led us to present a case of malignant degeneration of the pilonidal sinus.

Case presentation

A 32-year-old patient, a chronic smoker with an unremarkable surgical history, had noted a discharging pit in the sacrococcygeal pilonidal sinus that had developed into an abscess 15 years previously and drained spontaneously. He is currently admitted with pain and a suppurating intergluteal mass, which has been present for 3 months. The mass was a small ulcer that progressively increased and became infected.

The general examination findings were as follows: Temperature, 37.1°C; heart rate, 89 beats per minute; blood pressure, 100/70 mmHg; and weight, 55 kg.

Clinical examination revealed an infected ulceration over the sacrococcygeal area, with an irregular margin in the form of a choufleur. The ulcer base was indurated and fixed to the underlying structures, and we also noted the presence of 2 perineal collections, 1 fistulized at the level of the intergluteal crease (2 cm) and the other lateralized on the right (4 cm).

A rectal examination showed a normal sphincter tone and no perirectal bleeding, masses, or ulcers. Palpation of the lymph nodes revealed adenopathy approximately 4 cm in size on the left and 2 cm on the right, firm with no inflammatory signs.

Discussion

Pilonidal disease occurs primarily in young adults and it ten times more common in men (affecting 1.1% of the male population) [3]. Its pathophysiology is debated, but the theory of an acquired condition currently prevails.

The most common presentation of pilonidal disease is cellulites and abscess [4]. Malignant transformation into squamous cell carcinoma is extremely rare.

The process of ongoing repair of tissue damage caused by chronic inflammation is implicated in the pathogenesis of this cancer [5], and this is the probable process in our case.

Tumors complicating pilonidal disease are deeply invasive and tend to metastasize, as is the case in our study.

Ulceration, tumor proliferation and inguinal adenopathy are signs suggestive of malignant transformation [6]. Inguinal lymph nodes metastasis is associated with poor prognosis [6], with a median survival of 2 years.

The diagnosis of this disease based on anatomopathological study, made on biopsy of a chronic ulceration or by chance discovery on anatomopathological examination of a pilonidal sinus excision, (in our case, the diagnosis was made by anathomopathological study of a tumor biopsy). Then the histological type of tumour that arises from a pilonidal sinus is often squamous cell carcinoma. Other tumor types reported are basal cell carcinoma, mixed squamous and basal carcinoma and adenocarcinoma and unspecified tumor [7].

MRI is indicated for assessing locoregional tumour extension, although the CT scan must be done to diagnose distant metastases [[8], [9]]. Colonoscopy may be necessary to exclude extension to the rectum. Our patient performed a colonoscopy which did not reveal rectal extension.

Early diagnosis of these cancers is essential, as they appear to have a worse prognosis than primary skin malignancies.

Wide excision with tumor free margins remains the primary treatment for this complication, thus the abdominoperineal resection is required in case of rectal invasion [10].

Resection must be sufficiently wide to reduce the risk of local recurrence, which can be as high as 44%. The high recurrence rate after surgical treatment can be decreased by the addition of radiotherapy, However, In the case of inguinal or distant lymph node metastases, chemotherapy may be useful, as is the case in this study.

Conclusion

Malignant transformation of pilonidal pathology is rare, and the risk of such degeneration increases when pilonidal inflammation is chronic, for this reason, patient with chronic inflammatory disease of the pilonidal region must be closely monitored. Image examinations with CT scan and MRI help in treatment planification, the best treatment is undoubtedly early surgical removal of all pilonidal sinuses.
