A critical review of microfluidic electroporation for therapeutic cell engineering
Authors: Madeline E. Hoyle, Julio P. Arroyo, Rafael V. Davalos, Aniruddh Sarkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYMicrofluidic electroporation (MFEP) is emerging as a scalable, non-viral modality for engineering therapeutic cells. This review synthesizes recent progress in MFEP, outlining the key advantages of MFEP, including continuous processing at high throughputs, single-cell manipulation and monitoring, electrolysis mitigation, and automation for rapid multi-parameter screening and minimized cell manufacturing complexity. We then survey the waveforms, pulses, and buffers used in MFEP. In doing so, we highlight the diversity of parameters and uncover the inconsistent reporting of outcomes, calling for standardized reporting of electric-field dose, yield, and efficiency. We additionally note the need for investigation into the effects of MFEP on the long-term perturbation of cells given the effects of bulk electroporation compared to other transfection methods. Finally, we conclude that MFEP has many advantages for cell engineering yet must continue to adopt greater validation of short- and long-term efficiency and effects on cells to accelerate clinical adoption.

INTRODUCTION

Cell therapies have emerged over the past few decades as a promising treatment option for a wide range of diseases, with thousands of clinical trials completed or underway.1–4 These therapies involve administering live, often modified, cells, in order to treat, cure, or manage a patient’s disease. The recent approval of CAR-T cell therapies, a type of cell therapy to treat cancers, has brought cell therapies even further into the spotlight and increased interest in other engineered cells, such as natural killer cells, stem cells, and other T cells, as therapeutics.5–7 However, as enthusiasm for cell therapies continues to grow, a significant challenge lies in meeting the escalating demand.

To meet the escalating demand, the manufacturing of these cell therapies must be scalable and cost effective. Many recent review articles have covered the limitations of the current manufacturing model,8–11 highlighting key steps that can be improved upon to increase accessibility, one of which is the gene-editing step.12,13 Gene editing is accomplished via the delivery of a cargo, such as small interfering RNA (siRNA), mRNA, DNA plasmids, or nucleases, like Cas9, across the cell membrane.14 Delivery platforms must deliver this cargo with high efficiency while maintaining high post-delivery cell viability. Additionally, the method for delivery should ideally be low cost, simple to use, and be able to edit large amounts of cells quickly, as typical cell-based therapies can require up to or greater than 108 cells for infusion into individual patients.6 While numerous methods to deliver cargo for efficient gene editing across the cell membrane have been developed for cell therapies, they each have their own strengths and limitations in terms of efficiency, viability, and scalability.

There are many different intracellular delivery techniques that have been extensively reviewed.15–17 Viral vector-based methods are one of the most common techniques that have demonstrated high efficiency, often exceeding 90%.16 As a result, they have gained widespread use in cell engineering.16,18 However, significant logistical limitations exist, including incredibly high costs (>$50,000)9 and complex production of the vectors.11,19 Viruses also have functional limitations, such as preexisting viral immunity in patients and the inability to deliver complex and large genetic payloads such as those often involved in CRISPR-Cas9-based genome editing. Additionally, viral delivery can cause off-target effects in the genome, which is a serious concern for safety.18,20,21

Physical methods for intracellular delivery can address many of the concerns presented by viral vectors.15 Electroporation (EP) is one such method that typically applies a high voltage across electrodes usually placed a few millimeters apart, with cells and cargo suspended between them. This generates a transient electric field that raises the transmembrane potential (TMP) of the cells, creating temporary re-sealable pores in the cell membrane for cargo entry.22 Despite the availability of many commercial EP systems, EP has not been adopted as the primary intracellular delivery method of choice for cell therapy manufacturing.15 This is largely due to concerns that EP compromises cell viability, especially in sensitive primary cells;16 alters the long-term cell functionality;6,23,24 and achieves variable transfection efficiency. Furthermore, the integration of these commercial electroporators into cell manufacturing workflows is hindered by challenges such as limited throughput, manual steps, safety concerns presented by high voltages, and inefficient delivery with predefined settings.25

Despite these limitations, conventional EP is still commonly used as an alternative to viral vectors, including in clinical trials (Table 1). This is likely due to conventional EP’s simplicity, low cost, and ability to achieve delivery efficiency and viability well above 50% in many primary cells (Table 2), which can be satisfactory depending on the application.19 However, the broader adoption of EP as a cost-effective, low-toxicity, and scalable alternative to viral vectors hinges on addressing its current shortcomings by increasing efficiency to match viral vectors, maintaining long-term viability and cell function, and improving scalability.

Over the past few decades, micro and nanoscale EP systems have been developed, with features that can overcome many of the barriers presented by other delivery platforms.43–46 Micro- and nano-scale EP systems can be broadly categorized into systems that either statically position cells or flow cells continuously through microchannels.47 Systems that fall into the first category include micro- and nanoelectrode arrays, porous membranes, micropore arrays, nano-straws, nano-fountain probes, and nano-needles.48 These systems have been reviewed in detail recently and will not be the focus of this review.22,48 Instead, we will focus here on continuous flow-through EP schemes, or microfluidic-EP (MFEP), which offer distinct advantages that solve the challenges presented when using conventional bulk EP in large-scale cell manufacturing. They offer high-throughput, high-viability, high-efficiency, controllable delivery at the single-cell level; simplicity; and potential for integration and automation within larger cell-manufacturing workflows.5–7

These advantages, summarized in Figure 1, make MFEP platforms a promising option for meeting the increasing demands for cell therapies. Here, we review MFEP systems used for mammalian cell transfection from the past decade and the techniques they utilize to show the unique advantages of MFEP. We additionally review considerations for the optimization and validation of these systems given the wide parameter space and the need to effectively evaluate MFEP systems for translation beyond research labs. Given recent concerns about the impact of electroporation on long-term cell functionality,24,27,32,49 we also highlight the need for more rigorous investigation into how MFEP affects cell health over time. Addressing these concerns is critical to realizing the full clinical potential of MFEP technologies.

ADVANTAGES OF MFEP FOR EFFICIENT AND SCALABLE CELL THERAPIES

MFEP systems feature unique capabilities that aid in achieving highly efficient cell engineering due to high throughput, single-cell precision, and reduction of electrolysis. Here, we review a range of different MFEP systems and these design features that enable high efficiency and scalability (Table 3).

High throughput

Modern cell therapies demand high throughputs, ideally processing hundreds of millions of cells per hour, as most cell-based therapies currently require ~108–109 cells for infusion into patients.6 Conventional electroporators are limited to transfecting single batches of <107 cells at a time.36 In contrast, MFEP enables continuous transfection by flowing cells through a closed fluidic system, achieving significantly larger processing capabilities. Achieving high throughput, exceeding 107 cells per minute, typically requires EP of dense cell suspensions at high flow rates, typically 0.5–4.5 mL per minute, in large microfluidic channels.6,20,29,48,52,65 To achieve this, many MFEP systems simultaneously expose large numbers of cells to an electric field in a wide microchannel using a parallel electrode setup,6,20,31,36,43,50,66 (Figures 2A and 2B). This configuration decouples the electric field from the dimension of the microchannel parallel to the electrodes to allow for the microchannel to be scaled up to millimeters in width, processing dense cell suspensions at high flow rates without clogging. Another approach to increase throughput involves arranging multiple microchannels parallel to each other within the same system,29 enabling shared electrodes while increasing throughput in proportion to the number of added channels.

Single-cell manipulation

MFEP systems often incorporate designs that feature narrow microchannels that isolate a single cell between very close electrodes during EP. Such electrode arrangements, compared to bulk EP where electrodes are millimeters apart, dramatically reduce the voltage required for achieving the necessary electric field due to the focusing of the electric field only in this small region and the small electrode distance.5,29,53,55,56 This not only reduces costs of expensive power supplies, but many groups have leveraged single-cell isolation to precisely manipulate and monitor EP.

Cells experience the greatest change in TMP at membrane locations oriented perpendicular to the electric field.67 Consequently, cells static in a bulk solution or flowing through a straight microchannel are typically permeabilized nonuniformly, with pores forming primarily at points perpendicular to the electric field (or at the poles of the cell). To overcome this, MFEP systems have been designed to induce single-cell rotation. By flipping the cell, the areas of the membrane at the poles are continuously changing, resulting in multiple areas of the membrane experiencing a large change in the TMP, theoretically increasing the efficiency of cargo delivery by maximizing the permeabilized area of the cell.53,68 This has been accomplished by flipping a cell via hydrodynamic forces or microchannel geometry. For example, Zheng et al. observed increased PI delivery in cells rotated by a pulsing buffer stream compared to non-rotated cells.53 Similarly, Bhattacharjee et al. achieved a 2-fold increase in transfection efficiency by inducing cell rotation through consecutive turns in a microchannel.47 Geng et al. also reported a 2-fold increase in transfection efficiency with rotation induced by Dean vortices in a spiral microchannel,52 although not at the single-cell level of control. These findings highlight that increased permeabilization uniformity across the entire cell membrane, enabled by single-cell manipulation, results in higher delivery efficiency. Furthermore, it has been shown that with rotation, lower electric fields accomplish higher delivery efficiency than what is accomplished without rotation at high electric fields.52 This use of less intense electric fields is especially relevant in the context of minimizing cell stress to maintain cell viability.

Single-cell monitoring

Another significant advantage with single-cell electroporators is their integration with real-time monitoring techniques such as impedance cytometry.54,57,58,69,70 Impedance cytometry has been adopted in MFEP systems, measuring cell size, location, membrane capacitance, and cytoplasmic conductivity to optimize EP parameters, quantify permeabilization in real time, and monitor recovery after EP.58,60,69 Ye et al. demonstrated a system where a single set of electrodes perform both EP and impedance monitoring as a cell flows through a microchannel (Figure 2C). Their findings revealed that increasing EP intensity reduced post-EP impedance, suggesting a method for real-time and label-free EP validation.54 Similarly, Sherba et al. used a similar concept to determine the optimal EP electric field, using the magnitude of the post-EP impedance drops to monitor and optimize EP voltage parameters.55 Burgel et al. employed a dual-electrode system in their design, using one set of electrodes for impedance monitoring and the other for EP.56 By applying multi-frequency measurements post-EP, in the range of 20 kHz to 20 MHz, they monitored cell swelling, membrane conductivity, and nuclear envelope permittivity, providing a detailed electronic quantification of EP recovery. As an alternative to a narrow microchannel that isolates cells by only allowing a single cell to pass at a time, Zhang et al. used a microelectrode array chip to position individual cells via dielectrophoresis (DEP), which causes movement of the cells via a non-uniform electric field, for EP and monitoring of cell health post-EP.57

Additionally, integrating single-cell electroporators with electrical or optical monitoring techniques can allow for precise control of EP. For example, Rudge et al. developed a label-free MFEP that uses low-frequency impedance measurements to selectively electroporate cells based on their size, enabling selective electroporation of lymphocytes within peripheral blood mononuclear cells (Figure 2D).58 Similarly, Pfister et al. achieved selective electroporation based on fluorescent markers detected via fluorescence microscopy (Figure 2E). This approach enabled the selective EP of labeled cells from heterogeneous populations achieving high specificity and sensitivity (92.7% and 94.7%, respectively).59 Such targeted systems could eliminate the need for upstream cell enrichment and sorting steps often required for many cell therapies. This could be particularly advantageous for applications involving rare populations of tumor, stem, or immune cells, as it reduces risks of cell loss from isolation steps as well as reduces costly reagents and equipment.

While single-cell manipulation and monitoring in MFEP systems enable increased efficiency, decrease required voltages, and enable real-time monitoring and control, they present challenges such as low throughput, sensitivity to cell positioning, and complex and expensive circuitry and system setups.4,57,71,72 These limitations, especially throughput, which is often ~104, could hinder scalability, particularly for clinical applications requiring high-throughput processing, which is most cell therapies.

Reduction of electrolysis

Electrolysis occurs due to applied high voltage at the electrodes. This can cause a release of metal ions, localized pH changes, formation of gas bubbles, and joule heating.15,20,59,73,74 These effects can be toxic to cells and represent a significant challenge that EP systems must address through system and experimental design. Bulk EP systems are particularly prone to electrolysis due to the high voltages required to generate the necessary electric field between electrodes placed millimeters apart. In contrast, MFEP systems naturally minimize electrolysis due to smaller electrode distances and geometries that concentrate electric fields in certain regions. This allows lower voltages, below the threshold for redox reactions that cause metal ion release and bubbles, to be used to achieve necessary electric fields, minimizing electrolysis as a whole.74,75

Additionally, MFEP quickly flows cells away from the electrodes or uses protective buffer streams of fluid to minimize exposure of cells to toxins, gasses, and heat accumulated due to electrolysis. This mitigates these adverse effects.60,73,74,76 Buffer sheath flows protect cells by separating the cells fluidically from the electrodes, isolating electrolysis by-products within the buffer stream.77 Lissandrello et al. used a high-conductivity fast-flowing buffer to separate a cell stream from the electrodes. The high conductivity enabled the electric field to be concentrated in the lower conductivity cell stream, removing toxins from the electrodes lining the edge of microchannel with the faster flowing buffer stream (Figure 2A).6 Similarly, Luo et al. avoided applying the electric field directly to a central cell flow stream by delivering the EP voltage to outer parallel buffer streams that connect to the cell stream through microcapillary openings, preventing electrolysis by-products from reaching the cells.61 Huang et al. employed a different approach by using a buffer flow to create a Dean vortex. This method isolated cells in a triple-layer structure within the first vortex and separated them into distinct cells and buffer streams in the second vortex, shielding cells from direct electrode exposure. This resulted in consistently higher viability compared to without the Dean vortex.50 Moreover, buffer/media exchangers, discussed in the next section, move cells in and out of EP buffers rather than isolating the cells within different streams. This approach similarly reduces the exposure of cells to electrolytic toxins and heat. Overall, MFEP systems can incorporate unique microfluidic features to reduce and mitigate adverse electrolysis effects, leading to higher-viability EP.

Automation and integration

Automation and integration reduce reliance on skilled labor, improving repeatability, lowering costs, and increasing overall efficiency in cell engineering.78 These are critical factors since labor constitutes a significant portion of current cell therapy costs.11 EP requires large amounts of optimization for individual cell types, particularly for cell therapies where patient-specific cell heterogeneity may necessitate customized parameters, potentially requiring large amounts of cells and reagents for optimization alone.20,62 MFEP systems aim to automate optimization of EP parameters by determining optimal conditions in real time or by screening multiple EP variables simultaneously.

As previously discussed in the “single-cell manipulation” section, real-time EP optimization has been accomplished at the single-cell level using permeabilization detection via impedance cytometry,55,57 but also by the design of the MFEP system itself. Zu et al. developed a micropillar array flow through system that allows for size specific electric field exposure with larger cells exposed to more numerous micropillar electrodes.36 Alternatively, systems can instead screen multiple EP conditions simultaneously on arrays of cells. For example, Little et al. introduced a droplet microfluidic platform that isolates samples of cells from one another and simultaneously applies five different conditions of EP to these isolated samples (Figure 3A). Here, the electrical parameters are kept constant but the delivery molecule conditions are varied across the five sample groups.62 VanderBurgh et al. also emphasized the importance of automated screening of multiple delivery conditions simultaneously, screening up to nine electrical and eight cargo parameters at a time in a parallelized version of their previous high-throughput microchannel design.20 Garcia et al. similarly developed a system that simultaneously screened different electric field conditions based on the location of a cell in a microchannel.79 Not only do these systems highlight the importance of efficient optimization of EP parameters through automated screening, but also the need for flexibility in MFEP systems to handle variable input volumes and have tunable EP conditions.62,71 Integrating machine learning with MFEP systems, by utilizing real-time or post-EP feedback (e.g., imaging or impedance based), could further enable optimized device design, delivery, and screening of parameters.14,23,80

Additionally, bulk electroporators are unable to transfect the clinically required amount of cells for therapies in one cuvette, requiring multiple batch operations, exposing cells to the risks of contamination and human error.81 In contrast, MFEP systems enable continuous transfection and can be integrated with other microfluidic modules to conduct isolation and processing steps within a closed and automated system.11,20,36 Microfluidics are well established to be able to isolate cells through hydrodynamics,82 droplet formation,29 acoustophoresis, or DEP.41,83 In MFEP, several groups have demonstrated integration with other microfluidic cell isolating modules. For instance, Wei et al. integrated downstream DEP-based sorting to isolate viable electroporated cells from non-viable cells, removing any need for post-transfection enrichment.41 Han et al. coupled MFEP with downstream droplet formation to encapsulate individual cells for sorting and screening cells.29 Additionally, Hsi et al. used ultrasonic waves to direct cells from a media stream into and out of an EP buffer,64 while Lee et al. employed a microstructure-based passive mechanism to direct cells into the EP buffer, Figure 3B.76 Similarly, Ouyang et al. developed a multi-solution inlet to rapidly exchange solutions to control molecule dosage and facilitating the quick removal of EP buffer (Figure 3C).63 These systems highlight the potential for integrating microfluidic features into EP to optimize and simplify the overall cell manufacturing process, minimizing external wash steps that can cause cell loss as well as minimize manual labor steps to drive down costs in manufacturing.8 However, some of these features can require complex microfluidics, electronics, and optimization, which can make scale up more complex.84 Regardless, with the wide variety of microfluidic sorting and cell analysis techniques available, there remains considerable untapped potential for combining these approaches with EP to further enhance system flexibility and operational efficiency within larger manufacturing workflows.84,85

CONSIDERATIONS FOR OPTIMIZING AND VALIDATING MFEP

Many different parameters affect EP efficiency and the interpretation of EP results, resulting from a combination of different mechanisms underlying the formation of aqueous pores in the cell membrane, as well as the molecular transport of the cargo into the cell via electrophoretic forces or diffusion.86–88 Thus, electroporation is dependent on electronic parameters, such as electric field strength, pulse length, pulse shape, and polarity (waveform), as well as experimental factors, such as EP buffer, the cargo delivered and its concentration, cell type, concentration, temperature, osmotic pressure, and interaction of the cargo with the cell membrane.4,89 Table 4 highlights how despite the very diverse parameter space that MFEP systems have used, high delivery efficiencies and viabilities have generally been accomplished across the board. This section explores considerations regarding these parameters. Given the high efficiency and viability achieved by MFEP, further exploration of how these parameters contribute to long-term safety, efficiency, and cell perturbation is needed. This is essential so that as MFEP continues to become more prevalent in the cell therapy landscape, researchers can understand how to most effectively engineer cells for their respective applications.

EP pulse parameters

As introduced above, many factors influence EP efficiency, and while the mechanisms underlying pore formation during electroporation are largely debated,87 the electrical parameters (including pulse length, frequency, number of pulses, magnitude of the electric field, and waveform) are known to play a large role in causing a change in the TMP and thus cell permeabilization.4,89,94–96 Although there is some theoretical guidance and some groups have done screenings for what pulse parameters should be selected, based on electric field strength or pulse time and cell size which TMP is dependent upon,95,97–99 significantly different pulse lengths, shapes, and repetition can achieve similar transfection efficiency and cell viability, as shown by Table 4. This is largely due to the many factors at play during electroporation that compound with the electrical parameters. This leaves which pulse parameters are best somewhat unclear in current literature, and there has been no clear unifying biophysical principal that can be applied across all cell types presented in MFEP. Given that the electric field strengths experienced by cells for reversible EP in MFEP platforms, as seen in Table 4 (1–3 kV/cm), are also in the range for irreversible EP (>1 kV/cm), where the cells are killed, it becomes critical to select the right parameters to avoid irreversible EP but also to gain greater understanding into biophysical mechanisms at work.95,100,101

The electric field strength for EP itself typically is influenced by the size and type of the target cell. Because the change in TMP is proportional to cell radius, smaller cells theoretically necessitate higher electric field strengths to reach the critical TMP for membrane permeabilization. While this threshold to achieve permeabilization varies across cell types, stronger electric fields generally are associated with greater chance of irreversible EP.79,100 However, EP is not solely determined by field strength as the pulse duration and number critically affect permeabilization and viability. Pulse durations may range from nanoseconds to milliseconds in length, polarity of the pulsed field waveform (which can be monopolar [single direction] or bipolar [both positive and negative] in direction) and can be delivered as single or multiple pulses. The optimal combination of these parameters remains an area of investigation, with both bulk and MFEP systems employing a wide range of configurations (Table 4).15,102

Many MFEP systems have employed a single, monopolar direct current (DC) pulse, often exceeding 1 ms in length.31,36,81,95 This is common with or similar to what bulk EP systems apply.95 As seen in Table 4, many MFEP systems have also adopted repeated microsecond-scale pulses, typically ~100 microseconds, with a cumulative exposure of ~0.5–1 ms. Sub-100-microsecond pulses for gene delivery remain largely unexplored by EP in general, let alone MFEP.95 Interestingly, some groups have demonstrated delivery of small (chemotherapeutics) or large (plasmids) cargo using nanosecond length pulses in bulk EP setups.96,103–105 It is also hypothesized that nanosecond pulses may not only permeabilize the cell membrane but could also intracellular membranes, such as the nuclear envelope, although there is little direct evidence of this beyond increased gene expression or transfection efficiency.91,95,104,106 Nevertheless, a significant limitation of nanosecond pulses is that the pulse generators required for them must handle the high voltages required to generate fields >10 kV/cm at that timescale, which with repeated pulsing, are not currently widely accessible.97 EP that combines short microsecond and sub-microsecond pulses with a longer, lower intensity millisecond pulse is another approach.20,49 This approach theoretically aims to initially permeabilize the cell with short intense pulses, subsequently expanding the pores with longer lower electric field-strength pulses.15,23,91 Although this is just a proposed mechanism for pore formation, Chang et al. reported a 2-fold increase in plasmid transfection efficiency using combined nanosecond and millisecond pulses compared to millisecond pulses alone.41

The waveform itself, which refers to the shape and polarity of the pulses, is another crucial factor influencing EP outcomes. While traditional pulses used by bulk and MFEP systems commonly use exponentially decaying or square DC pulses, alternative pulse shapes have been explored.22 Square monopolar pulses are generally more effective for transfection and are simpler to generate than AC pulses.22,29,63,65,92 However, AC and bipolar pulses offer additional advantages such as reducing electrolysis due to charge balance at the electrodes and enhancing permeabilization by exposing more of the cell surface to the electric field.92 AC pulses in the 10–200 kHz range have shown high efficiency63 in MFEP devices. At frequencies below 10 kHz, cargo delivery can still be achieved but has been shown to be accompanied by greater electrolysis.63,66 Although MFEP systems are generally less prone to electrolysis due to their microscale geometry and lower voltage requirements, waveform is still something to consider given that the electric fields are still high in MFEP systems.20,50

Given the wide variation in pulse parameters and the relatively high efficiency and viability achieved across the board, implementing a metric to quantify the amount of electric field a cell is exposed to, such as total electric field dose, would provide a consistent measurement of exposure.6 This could account for the combined effects of electric field strength, number of pulses, and pulse length, allowing for clearer comparison between pulse parameters in MFEP systems.6 Moreover, incorporating testing of these parameters with standardized cell lines, such as widely available Jurkat or HEK293 cell lines, alongside primary cells would eliminate concerns with donor-to-donor variability to facilitate direct comparisons between systems, thereby ultimately aiding in the optimization and understanding of the many electrical parameters used.

Buffer composition

In addition to the EP pulse parameters, experimental factors can also significantly impact EP efficiency and viability, with buffer composition being a critical determinant. MFEP requires cells to be suspended for long periods of time in EP buffers, some of which have recently been suggested to pose toxicity risks with extended exposure.64 Commonly used buffers include PBS, cell culture media (such as DMEM or RPMI), and low conductivity sucrose-based buffers,15 including commercial OPTI-MEM and BTXpress, as seen in Table 4. These buffers all have different salt, sugar, protein, and ion concentrations, each of which can affect cell function and recovery.107 Critically, buffer conductivity sets the background current flowing through the buffer, and thus, the joule heating that the cells experience during EP. Thus, bulk EP systems often use low conductivity buffers to reduce this effect. Salts control the conductivity of the buffer, while sugars and proteins are often included to mitigate osmotic shock.64 Iso-osmotic and hypo-osmolar sucrose or PBS buffers, typically at a pH of ~7.4, are most used, as seen in Table 4. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of buffer composition in optimal EP outcomes, with some groups developing custom buffers tailored to their specific application.26,64,108–110 Notably, additives such as Mg2+, K+, Ca2+, DMSO, and sodium pyruvate have been shown to influence EP efficiency and recovery.107,109,110 Mg2+ has especially been investigated as a potential enzymatic co-factor that can enhance viability due to potentially increased activation of ATPase membrane ion channels but conversely also reduces transfection efficiency potentially due to activation of DNases and RNases that degrade RNA and DNA delivery cargo.107 Additionally, both Mg2+ and Ca2+ additives are believed to form bridges between negative charges of both DNA and the plasma membrane, improving DNA-membrane interactions to facilitate DNA delivery into a cell.108 However, in depth comparisons across multiple cell types regarding additives remain limited. Consequently, buffer composition should be carefully selected and optimized for specific cell types and experimental objectives.

Methods for validation of delivery via EP

EP is typically evaluated using a transfection efficiency metric, which is determined by quantifying fluorescence intensity following delivery of fluorescent dyes or fluorescent protein-expressing plasmids, mRNA, or siRNA into the cells.6,31,36,63,111,112 However, in prior MFEP literature, efficiency has been used as a somewhat loose term, sometimes reported as the ratio of fluorescence-expressing cells out of the total live and dead cell population,36,66,91 while sometimes it is limited to the ratio of fluorescence-expressing cells out of the total live cells.6,20,63,90 Other groups also present the median fluorescence intensity on its own rather than as a percentage to offer deeper insights into the amount of cargo delivered to cells.113 Many intracellular delivery systems typically first show delivery with small dyes like propidium iodide (PI) or dye-conjugated sugars, due to their ease of use and ability to correlate dye size with pore size in the membrane.58,113 Given that most clinically relevant cargo, such as CRISPR-Cas9 RNP or plasmids, are quite large (~140–2,000 kDa), they thus require large pores to enter the cell.62 Thus, it is helpful to deliver molecules of similar masses first to verify delivery without the complexity presented by the translation and expression of the cargo. Additionally, it is critical to understand the electrophoretic and diffusion transport mechanisms that could help or hinder large cargo delivery through the pores.88,114

Methods for validation of cell viability

As the demand for cell therapies increases, more groups have aimed to show that MFEP systems can produce functional primary cells that have been edited with clinically relevant cargo, most recently for CAR-T cells. However, to truly make this point, MFEP needs to show a very high yield, or a high number of viable transfected cells output by the system. To understand and thus maximize yield, systems must quantify the viability of the cells accounting for the number of cells lost by going through the system. This is critical because a system with high efficiency in live cells might still be unsuitable for cell engineering if there are large amounts of cell loss or poor viability.115

MFEP platforms typically quantify cell viability using either flow cytometry36,91 or fluorescence microscopy, generally within 24 h of EP.47,57,59 Immediate post-EP cell viability can be measured with staining methods such as PI,31,52 viability dyes such as 7-AAD,20,71 Calcein-AM,54,58,59 trypan blue,61 and NucBlu,63 but can also be quantified via MTS, MTT, ViaCount, CCK, JC-1, and ethidium homodimer exclusion assays.26,36,47,50,65,91,116 Among these, calcein-AM and phosphatidylinositol (PI) are the most commonly used live dead staining techniques, likely due to their simplicity of use and high sensitivity.4,61

The wide variety of methods for validation pose significant challenges when comparing and generalizing results across different studies. Many validation techniques introduce factors that can affect the interpretation of results, such as cell loss during wash steps for viability assays or for the removal of cells from EP buffer,63,93 both of which could skew efficiency and viability analysis. Additionally, the choice of technique to analyze the validation method, such as flow cytometry or fluorescence microscopy, may also limit the number of cells analyzed, leading to unclear repeatability or statistical interpretability of results reported.

Thus, minimization of wash steps and clear reporting of methodological practices in terms of analysis is essential for meaningful comparisons. This includes explicitly stating flow cytometry gating protocols, the calculations for viability and efficiency, and what staining methods were used. MFEP systems commonly compare results to bulk EP, which is very valuable. However, many studies do not state whether they are using predefined optimized profiles given by the manufacturer of the bulk EP device or if they are matching the electrical and experimental bulk conditions to the conditions of their MFEP system. Without this information, it is difficult to determine whether the comparison is fair and valid, further complicating the interpretation of results.

Additionally, most prior MFEP systems have primarily focused on quantifying delivery efficiency and viability immediately following EP, typically within a 24-h time. However, for applications in cell therapies, MFEP platforms need to be evaluated in a clear, repeatable, and standardized manner that extends beyond immediate transfection efficiency and cell viability. We propose a recommended minimal reporting standard checklist for researchers to use to encourage greater transparency in methods and data presentation (Figure 4). However, comprehensive evaluations should also include the long-term effects of MFEP on cell function, off-target impacts on gene expression, and changes in cell proliferation.14,25,27,117

Need for longer term analysis of cell perturbation

There is a pressing need to expand the evaluation of MFEP outcomes beyond delivery efficiency and immediate cell viability. This need arises from long-standing concerns that EP can have low viability and high cytotoxicity compounded by recent findings driven by the excitement of CAR-T cell therapies, which show that bulk EP can cause changes in gene expression.2,4,22,32,63,118–122 For example, multiple groups have conducted transcriptomics profiling revealing that bulk EP can cause significant changes in gene expression related to T cell activation, metabolism, stress responses, cell signaling, and immune responses.24,25,27,32,115,123 Not only does bulk EP change gene expression, but functionally these groups have also noticed an increase in cytokine production of electroporated T cells, which could be concerning for applications in cell therapies.25,123 It is also important to note that timescale matters for these experiments, as differences seem to be heightened within the first 24 h of EP. This is suggested by the expression of surface markers, such as CD69 (activation-related) and PD-1 (exhaustion-related). These have been shown to vary across different time points within 24 h after bulk EP,25 and that these effects decrease after 24 h as one group showed no significant difference in activation markers CD25 and CD69 after 24 h in T cells,32 and another group showed that gene expression falls back toward what the controls are by 24 h.25

Additionally, some groups have compared yield and functionality between traditional bulk EP and nucleofection, a method of bulk EP which, although proprietary in terms of exact pulse parameters, is believed to apply a combination of short (10–100 microsecond) and long (1–100 ms) pulses.15 Some showed that nucleofection showed lower yields of cells,115 indicating more cells were lysed or killed. This is contrary to what others have shown, which is that nucleofection caused lower changes in relative gene expression or cytokine secretion in T cells than other delivery methods.24,115 Overall, this suggests that the type of pulse could significantly matter for not only yield, but also for preservation of cell functionality, although this has not been systematically investigated.15,24

Other nano-scale or localized single cell EP systems seem to confirm that the type of EP seems to matter, as they have shown that nano and localized single-cell EP induces fewer off-target effects than bulk EP, making them potentially safer and more suitable for cell therapies.24,25,27,49,62 Additionally, other physical methods, such as mechanoporation, also make the claim that they result in less cell perturbation compared to bulk EP. This reduced perturbation has been hypothesized to be linked to decreased electrolysis compared to bulk EP, causing decreased exposure to toxic metal ions, pH changes, and joule heating, which can contribute to mitochondrial stress, apoptosis, and lipid or protein denaturation.24,49 Given this, establishing where MFEP stands in regard to inducing cell perturbation is crucial for future applications in cell therapies as it is critical to minimize off-target effects for patient safety and efficacy of the treatment. Currently, to the best of our knowledge, only one MFEP group has analyzed perturbation with transcriptomics and cytokine analysis.24 They showed that MFEP was less perturbative compared to both bulk and nucleofection EP methods using transcriptomics, suggesting this to be caused by reduced effects of electrolysis in their system.24 Additionally, they showed an increase in interferon γ and TNF-α secretion compared to nucleofection.

Beyond assessing perturbation, investigating the underlying mechanisms that drive the differences between delivery methods is essential, as they are currently only hypothesized.24,32,49 Although mechanoporation, bulk EP, localized EP, and MFEP all rely on physical methods for membrane poration, they seem to elicit vastly different cellular responses. Understanding the mechanisms responsible for these differences would not only provide insight on how to optimize these systems, but also fill gaps in how these delivery mechanisms themselves work, which is currently somewhat limited.124

For future comprehensive assessments of cell functionality in MFEP, analysis techniques such as antibody staining for surface proteins,32 RNA sequencing and transcriptomics,27,32,116 and tracking changes in cell subsets31,49 can provide valuable insights. For T cells specifically, cytokine assays and analysis of activation and exhaustion surface markers (which have not yet been investigated with MFEP) would provide insight into long-term functionality.24,27,123 Furthermore, it is essential to monitor outcomes over extended periods (>1 week) to detect off-target effects and functional changes as well as proliferation kinetics, especially given the timescale-dependent differences discussed earlier in this section. Most of the current analysis into electroporation-based perturbation has focused on T cells, due to rise of CAR-T therapies. However, there are many other types of cells relevant to cell therapies that also should be investigated, such as dendritic, natural killer, and stem cells. For all of these, maintaining long-term cell functionality remains one of the primary challenges.125 Only by thoroughly investigating cell perturbation can the potential of MFEP in cell therapy manufacturing be validated to ensure its efficacy and safety for cell engineering applications in clinical settings.

COMPARISON WITH OTHER MICROFLUIDIC TECHNIQUES

There are several other physical microfluidic methods for intracellular delivery.2,16,120,121,124 Mechanoporation, creating pores via mechanical force, has emerged in recent years as an efficient technique, encompassing approaches such as microneedles, microinjection, and cell squeezing. Microneedle and microinjection techniques that pierce the cell membrane generally achieve high transfection efficiency and viability, but they are substantially limited in scaling up to meet the demands for cell therapies due to low throughputs.118,121,126 Cell squeezing accomplishes permeabilization through mechanical forces of fluid flow.120,127 Recent reviews of this emerging field highlight that cell squeezing is capable of high throughput, low cost, and >90% transfection efficiency and viability, often in difficult to transfect cells (T, hematopoietic stem, natural killer [NK], and B cells) with highly relevant cargos (mRNA, plasmids, Cas9),2,120,121,128 and with minimal cell perturbation.27,123,129 Direct comparisons of mechanoporation performance with bulk EP should continue to take place to assess if there are considerable gains in performance and safety with mechanoporation.25,32,129 The demand for high delivery efficiency and the success of various microfluidic techniques have also led to the development of hybrid electro-mechanoporation.2,120,130 These hybrid systems combine mechanical and electrical forces to achieve minimal cell death and high transfection efficiency but are still in early stages of development. Overall, despite the potential of many microfluidic intracellular delivery platforms, their clinical translation remains limited. To advance these technologies for clinical applications and manufacturing, the field should prioritize cooperation and a culture of transparency. This could streamline the development and optimization of these platforms, accelerating the translation from proof of concept to clinical implementation.

CONCLUSION

MFEP has emerged as a highly efficient and advantageous method for cell engineering. Broadly, many challenges remain in cell manufacturing, associated with stringent cleanroom standards, complex and lengthy release assays, logistical constraints related to transport between facilities, and process development.10,11,131 However, MFEP offers many advantages. Costs and complexity could significantly be reduced due to eliminating the need for manufacturing and use of clinical grade viral vectors. Process complexity, and thus labor costs, can be further lessened with MFEP features such as integration with cell isolation and sorting fluidic modules or the ability to target cell subpopulations using singe-cell monitoring.11 An example of this idealized simplification and role of MFEP in the specific application of rapid CAR-T manufacturing can be seen in Figure 5, although the ideal MFEP device would vary greatly depending on application. Additionally, the small physical footprint of these devices allows for extensive parallelization, ensuring high-throughput processing while maintaining safety in a closed system.78 Collectively, these advantages position MFEP as a pivotal technology for the development of low-cost benchtop devices for cell manufacturing, thereby potentially enabling the decentralized production of cell therapies.9,10,131

Despite these unique advantages, successful clinical translation depends on MFEP demonstrating that the final product of engineered cells is repeatably efficient with clearly defined parameters.10,131 Most critically, high efficiency must be associated with maintained cell viability and long-term stability of cell function before the products be considered for patient use.13 Achieving this will require a shift from iterative parameter optimization to increased and repeated functional and quality control testing of engineered primary cells, with not only in vitro assays but also in animal models, which is not common in MFEP research.13 Furthermore, there is a critical emerging need for standardized testing and demonstration of the effects of parameters using commonly agreed upon metrics, instead of multiple difficult-to-compare bespoke criteria, if MFEP is to become the method of choice for clinical cell manufacturing. Ultimately, MFEP systems must prove their ability to operate with high throughput, maintain high transfection efficiency, and preserve long-term cell functionality without compromising the advantages highlighted in this review.
