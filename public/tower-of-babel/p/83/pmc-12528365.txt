Environmental factors driving antimicrobial resistance in Indian flying foxes (Pteropus medius): one health implications
Authors: Touseef Ahmed, Aitezaz Ahsan, Muhammad Armaghan Shahzad, Hamid Irshad, Abdul Ali, Muhammad Farooq Tahir, Muhammad Usman Zaheer, Tigga Kingston
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWildlife living near human settlements may be disproportionately exposed to environmental AMR. Pteropus medius, a large colonial fruit-eating bat species in South Asia, commonly roosts and forages in human-dominated landscapes, exposing it to anthropogenically shaped resistomes across a wide geographic area. Moreover, seasonal variations in environmental conditions and food resources may influence bat movements and their exposure to AMR. We investigated the prevalence of AMR in P. medius and explored AMR risk at seven different roosting sites in Pakistan, while considering variations in seasons and Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) types as predictors for AMR exposure. We tested fecal samples for resistance to twelve antibiotics and sought blaTEM, blaSHV and blaCTX-M resistance genes using PCR. Moderate to high resistance prevalence was identified in five out of twelve tested antibiotics, with resistance to aztreonam, cefradine and gentamicin exhibiting a significant seasonal effect. Similarly, tested antibiotics and genes were positively influenced by land use at 14.5 km buffer around the roosting sites. Approx. 37% of E. coli isolates were ESBL producers in both seasons and carried blaTEM genes (> 90%). Strong correlations were found in AMR patterns between P. medius and livestock (cattle and poultry). However, when resistance prevalence data were organized by season and province, the strength of these correlations varied considerably, highlighting the need for further research. Introduction

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is a critical and unprecedented 21st-century health challenge, globally contributing to 5 million human deaths annually either directly or indirectly, with projections suggesting this could reach over 10 million per year by 20501–6. Growing evidence suggests that the environment plays an underappreciated but critical role in the development, spread, and transmission of AMR, facilitating the transfer of resistant pathogens between humans, animals, and the environment7,8. The primary pathways for environmental AMR include pharmaceutical and hospital wastewater, which, when combined with agricultural and livestock waste, contribute to the contamination of freshwater sources. Untreated wastewater loaded with antimicrobial residues and resistant bacteria from these sources creates a selection pressure for drug-resistant organisms to survive in the environment, shaping what is referred to as the environmental resistome9,10. This selection pressure not only favors the survival of clinically relevant resistant pathogens but also enhances the exchange of resistance genes among commensal environmental bacteria such as E. coli, which can act as silent reservoirs and facilitators of resistance dissemination across diverse environmental microbial communities11,12.

The WHO report on AMR surveillance highlights global geographical gaps in AMR surveillance and acknowledges limited research on AMR in wildlife1. The AMR Quadripartite Joint Secretariat that includes four global organizations (FAO, UNEP, WHO and WOAH) is tasked with global AMR control response, including environmental and wildlife AMR, under a One Health framework6,7. Wildlife act as a sentinel of environmental AMR and resistome by providing insights into how both are distributed and transmitted across the landscape, as indicated by very low AMR prevalence in wildlife in areas that are distant from human activity13,14. In contrast, wildlife species, including some bats (order Chiroptera) that are adapted to human-modified habitats, showed elevated levels of AMR15–20. Research has also shown that bats carry extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) producer bacteria and resistance genes, suggesting that bats could not only be a key indicator but also a propagator of AMR across ecosystems15,21.

Despite the integral role that some wildlife species play in human-modified landscapes, there is limited understanding of how they shape or influence the environmental resistome22. Specifically, few studies have incorporated spatial land use variables into wildlife AMR research, yet these are likely to be key predictors of AMR dynamics23. The spatial distribution of land uses, such as agriculture and urbanization, can create environments that promote the development and spread of resistant microbes22,24. Wildlife, acting as reservoirs and vectors, may interact with these modified landscapes in ways that influence the persistence and transmission of AMR25. Furthermore, the movement of wildlife across diverse land use types can facilitate resistance gene flow between ecosystems, potentially exacerbating the spread of resistant strains13,23.

Pteropus medius (Indian flying fox) is a large frugivorous bat with a range that spans from Pakistan in the west to Myanmar in the east and Bhutan in the north to the Maldives in the south. Large populations reside in India, Sri Lanka, Nepal, and Bangladesh and, in many cases, migrate seasonally within and among countries26,27. These flying foxes form colonies of hundreds to thousands of individuals, roosting in large, exposed trees that are frequently near human settlements27. As a result, these bats commonly engage in synanthropic behaviors such as consuming leftover fruits28 and thermoregulatory behaviors that rely on soaking their belly and drinking from water resources that are designated for domestic animals29. Together these behaviors increase their vulnerability to environmental AMR. At the same time, colonies generate a rain of ejecta, feces and urine that accumulates under the roost – accessible to any roaming livestock and wildlife, and possibly to people, creating a propagation pathway for AMR in the environment17,30,31. Furthermore, spatiotemporal variation in the population density of P. medius can potentially provide an influx of antimicrobial resistance, contributing toward environmental AMR propagation32,33.

P. medius forage over mixed landscapes, promoting plant gene flow and forest regeneration in fragmented habitats32,34,35. However, these daily movements also expose bats to diverse sources of environmental AMR associated with human-modified landscapes, such as agricultural run-off and contaminated water sources. Long distance seasonal migration further exposes bats to environmental AMR sources but critically facilitates the dissemination of AMR and resistant genes over a large geographic scale, with serious One Health implications. Therefore, we consider P. medius as an effective sentinel mammalian species to assess environmental AMR due to its extensive synanthropic behavior, large colony size and regional significance in Pakistan.

The emergence and dissemination of drug-resistant microbes are steadily increasing and are aggravated by climate change36,37. Studies from around the globe indicate that an increase in average minimum temperatures is linked to higher rates of antibiotic resistance38–40. Extreme temperatures could contribute to antibiotic resistance indirectly by changing the behaviors of people, livestock, and wildlife and the interactions among them and the environment37,41increasing exposure and vulnerability to the environmental resistome33,42,43. For instance, P. medius roosts colonially in exposed large trees, and mitigates heat stress through a series of cooling thermoregulatory behaviors that intensify as temperatures increase29,44. Behaviors used at the highest temperatures rely on evaporative cooling (wrist licking, panting, opportunistic belly soaking in nearby water bodies). Drinking to restore water balance or belly soaking increases bats’ exposure to water resources29 that are often contaminated with antimicrobial residues and resistant bacteria45 (Supplementary Fig. 1).

This cross-sectional study aimed to determine the seasonal prevalence of AMR in E. coli, ESBL producing E. coli and its commonly associated blaCTX-M, blaSHV and blaTEM resistance genes in P. medius. We tested for resistance against twelve antibiotic classes that are widely used to treat human and livestock infections and are expected to have a higher prevalence in the environment. To ascertain the severity of AMR in P. medius, we used the WHO Medically Important Antibiotics (MIA) 2024 classification to classify the twelve antibiotics studied into six different classes. The MIA classification is an AMR risk management tool by minimizing the impact of antimicrobial use coming from non-human sources to humans46.

We expected to see higher seasonal AMR prevalence in summer than in winter due to the higher flow of water in the rivers and streams, which can spread AMR to a wider geographic area. The water flow in summer is governed by seasonal variation in rainfall and the melting of glaciers and ice caps in northern regions of Pakistan47. More importantly, demands for hydration to offset evaporative cooling losses associated with thermoregulation, as well as belly soaking, increase in summer, which may increase exposure of P. medius to the environmental resistome. Owing to general synanthropic behavior, we predict a higher prevalence of AMR in P. medius in human-modified landscapes, particularly those with a higher proportion of human settlement area. In contrast, bats in roosts surrounded by greater natural vegetation and forests are expected to have lower AMR prevalence. Relying on publicly available data on AMR in poultry and cattle that is spatiotemporally consistent with our P. medius AMR data, we performed spatiotemporal correlation of resistance profiles of P. medius with those of cattle and poultry, to ascertain the major source of environmental AMR in P. medius. These comparisons of AMR across seven roosts, two seasons, and key sources of environmental AMR provide valuable insights into the environmental dynamics of AMR pollution affecting P. medius.

Methodology

Study sites

Pteropus medius, commonly known as the Indian flying fox, roosts in large colonies in the Indus River plain, primarily in the central and northern regions of Pakistan. We selected seven roosting sites of P. medius in urban and semi-urban areas of Punjab and Khyber Pakhtunkhwa (KPK) provinces of Pakistan through field surveys (Supplementary Table 1). These roosts were selected because of inter-roost thermal variability, associated Land Use and Land Cover (LULC) distribution, and accessibility. Study sites in Punjab province were in Lahore, Kasur, Sargodha, and Vehari. KPK roosting sites were in Haripur and Khanpur, and one site was in Islamabad, the capital of Pakistan (Fig. 1).

Land Use and Land Cover classification and locations of studied roosting sites of P. medius in Pakistan. ArcGIS Pro (version 3.5, Esri, 2025)48 was used to create this study map.

Seasonal variation in climate

The seven roosting sites were selected in regions that experience seasonal temperature variation. Roosting sites in Punjab province are subject to a subtropical climate with mean daily maximum temperatures averaging 35 °C during summer but mild winters with mean daily maximum temperatures no more than 23 °C. The climate at the study sites in the KPK province and Islamabad is shaped by elevation gradients due to various mountain ranges in northern Pakistan. These sites experience mild summers with a mean daily maximum temperature averaging 33 °C and colder winters with a mean maximum temperature no more than 21 °C. The monsoon season runs in mid-summer (mid-July to mid-August) and brings 70% of total annual rainfall that increases relative humidity and reduces temperature49. Rainfall during winter is generally lower with heavy snow in the northern mountainous regions of Pakistan. This seasonal variation of temperature and rainfall impacts flying foxes movement for foraging and thermoregulation, moreover, it changes river and stream flow, all of which may influence patterns of AMR47.

Sample collection

Fecal samples were collected monthly from June 2021 to August 2021 and December 2021 to February 2022 to represent summer and winter seasons respectively, based on predicted temperature ranges. Fresh fecal samples were collected noninvasively using a polythene sheet (3.6 m x 2.6 m) spread under roosting sites of P. medius50. Collectors observed the roost for 3–4 h (08:00–12:00) on a single morning once each month and collected fecal samples within 10–15 min of defecation of P. medius. This ensured that material was fresh and that there was no contamination of the samples from fecal matter of other species, although it was possible that a sample might include material from two or more flying fox individuals. Fresh samples were distinguished from older fecal samples based on color and consistency. Older fecal samples are darker in color and more compact. It is important to exclude older samples because exposure to air and dust can alter the relative abundance of bacterial taxa51. Samples were taken in a 15 ml sterilized falcon tube and stored in an ice cooler. Samples were brought to the laboratory on the same day in a cold chain and were stored at -20 °C.

Sample processing and antibiotic susceptibility testing of E. coli isolates

We processed 240 guano samples from across the seven roosting sites for E. coli isolation from the summer collections, and 85 samples from the winter collections at six roosting sites (P. medius was absent from the Khanpur site in winter). Sample size and power analyses was estimated using G*Power, based on the expected seasonal variation in E. coli antimicrobial sensitivity52. To achieve a statistical power of 80% (β = 0.20 and α = 0.05) a minimum of 76 isolates per group (winter and summer) were required. We have 77 E.coli isolates from winter and 240 isolates from summer season. A detailed flow diagram illustrating the number of total fecal samples collected, E. coli and ESBL-producing E. coli isolated is presented (Fig. 2).

One gram of each fecal sample collected was inoculated into 10mL buffered peptone water using a sterile technique. We diluted each guano sample in a phosphate buffer saline using a 10-fold serial dilution. We spread this dilution on tryptone bile X-glucuronide agar and MacConkey agar supplemented with ceftriaxone (4 µg/ml) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The samples were not homogenized, and isolates were randomly taken from fresh guanos. The presumptive E. coli colonies were confirmed using IMViC biochemical tests. Isolated E. coli in summer (n = 138/240 samples) and winter (n = 76/85) were tested using Kirby Baur’s disk diffusion method for Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing (AST) to determine resistance profiles against twelve antibiotic classes. Likewise, we tested these isolates for multidrug resistance (MDR), extensive drug resistance (XDR) and pandrug resistance (PDR) using WHONET (v25.8.7) software53. An MDR isolate is one that has acquired resistance to three or more tested antibiotic classes, an XDR isolate is resistant to all tested antibiotic classes except for two or fewer, while PDR isolate is resistant to all tested antibiotic classes54. We followed Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines55. E. coli ATCC 25,922 was used as quality-control, which is normally used to ensure ideal growth and propagation conditions47. Therefore, we used this strain to assess our conditions and tested this strain occasionally along with our samples. The inhibition zone of this strain was always within the expected range so, our AST results are validated56.

Double disk diffusion test

E. coli isolates were tested using the double disk diffusion test to identify ESBL-producing characteristics of isolated E. coli47,57 (Fig. 2e). Cefotaxime (with and without clavulanic acid) and ceftazidime (with and without clavulanic acid) antibiotic disks were placed on Muller Hinton agar (MHA) 30 mm (center to center) apart. If the resulting zone of inhibition in disks containing clavulanic acid exceed 5 mm, the source E. coli was considered an ESBL-producing strain47,57. This test was performed by placing aztreonam (30 µg), ceftriaxone (30 µg), ceftazidime (30 µg), cefixime (30 µg), and cefepime (30 µg) disks on MHA, 30 mm center to center apart from amoxicillin–clavulanic acid (AMC 30) disk.

Double disk synergy test

To confirm the presence of ESBL character, E. coli isolates were further tested using double disk synergy tests to identify synergism among the five tested antibiotic disks (aztreonam, ceftriaxone, ceftazidime, cefixime, cefepime) toward the AMC disk47,57 (Fig. 2e). Typically, this synergism among antibiotics appears as a key-hole shape in media, indicating an increase in inhibition zone. This shape is absent when antibiotics are tested separately, or if there is no synergy among them.

Molecular detection of ESBL-encoding genes

A total of 22 ESBL-producing E. coli isolates from summer and 19 from winter were selected for the detection of associated blaCTX-M, blaSHV and blaTEM genes detection (Fig. 2f). DNA was extracted from each ESBL-producing E. coli isolate, and was transferred to a fresh tube, stored at -20 °C. This extracted DNA was used as a template in PCR reaction using primers47 (Supplementary Table 2).

AMR severity in P. medius using WHO MIA 2024

To understand the severity of AMR P. medius carries, we used the WHO MIA 2024 classification46 which is mainly intended to provide guidance for the responsible use of antimicrobials in non-human sectors that include animals, plants, crops and the environment. These guidelines were developed by the Quadripartite organizations under a One Health framework, to reduce the risk of development and transfer of resistant bacteria from non-human sectors to humans46.

Land use and land cover

We quantified the area of four LULCs (forest cover, crop cover, human settlement and water bodies) at 14.5 km buffer size around each studied roost (Fig. 1). The selection of this buffer distance was based upon a study of P. medius movements, in which GPS-tagged flying foxes travelled an average of 14.5 km each night to access feeding sites32. With the exception of human settlement, these selected LULC factors vary qualitatively and quantitatively across seasons and can shape environmental AMR dynamics and the resistome47. Although, human settlement does not change seasonally but human behaviors and activities vary seasonally, potentially influencing environment exposure and shaping the dynamics of environmental AMR. Similarly, crop cover serves as proxy for livestock and agricultural farming practiced at subsistence level in our study region. Crop rotation and leaving agricultural fields fallow for one season, especially those located away from rivers or water channels, are commonly practiced to maximize yield and productivity in Pakistan58. Freely available Sentinel-2 imagery was used for LULC variables classification. We used Random Forest, a machine learning algorithm, for LULC classification in Google Earth Engine code editor platform59. Winter and summer areas of LULCs (forest cover, crop cover, human settlement and water bodies) were calculated to explore their influence on seasonal variations in AMR and ARGs prevalence, in comparison to human settlement area which is calculated once per roost and its influence on overall AMR and ARGs prevalence is determined.

Comparison of AMR profiles

We compared resistance profiles of five important antibiotic classes in P. medius with cattle and poultry to determine susceptibility of P. medius to acquire and disseminate environmental AMR. Cattle and poultry data were derived from publicly available AMR surveillance pilot data60 generated under the FAO action plan, that advocates a One Health approach in AMR stewardship. These sentinel data aimed to generate a baseline estimate of the resistance prevalence collected from healthy animals. Poultry data were sourced from provincial diagnostic labs and cattle data from slaughterhouse60. The data are spatiotemporally aligned with P. medius AMR data, as the roosting sites of P. medius fall within the catchment areas of reference labs for poultry (Poultry Research Institute (PRI) in Rawalpindi, Punjab, and PRI in Mansehra, KPK) and cattle (Provincial Disease Diagnostic Laboratory in Lahore, Punjab, and Disease Investigation Laboratory in Peshawar, KPK). These reference labs and their catchment areas are located within 300 km² buffer around the roosting sites, covering the period from 2021 to 2022. We combined the resistance prevalence data from these sectors for comparative analysis. In addition, the cattle and poultry pilot survey utilizes the same CLSI breakpoints as in the present study to set thresholds for interpreting AST results55,60.

Statistical analysis

We determined the percentage prevalence of each tested antibiotic class and compared it across summer and winter seasons. Paired t-test analyses were performed to test the difference between summer and winter AMR, although we hypothesized an increase in AMR prevalence in the summer in P. medius as an indicator of seasonal influence due to higher water demand and increased water flow in water channels during summer (Supplementary Table 3). A two-way ANOVA was conducted to assess the variation in AMR prevalence across seven roosts for tested antibiotics. We calculated the proportional area of LULC variables (forest cover, crop cover, human settlement and water bodies) separately for the summer and winter seasons and determined this seasonal influence on the resistance profile of each of the twelve antibiotics using linear regression models. Akaike Information Criterion was used to determine the best-fitting regression models (Supplementary Table 4). Pearson correlation analysis was used to compare seasonal and province-level P. medius AMR profiles with respective seasonal and provincial cattle and poultry resistance profiles (Supplementary Table 5). For provincial level correlation, P. medius roosting sites at Vehari, Sargodha, Kasur and Lahore were combined as Punjab representative sites; while the remaining three roosts were combined as KPK province representative roosting sites. We conducted all statistical analyses in R, and all values are considered significant at P < 0.05.

Results

We isolated E. coli and ESBL-producing E. coli from fecal samples collected during both summer and winter. Overall, we found 46% of our tested isolates were multidrug resistant (MDR), one was extensive drug resistant (XDR) and none were pandrug resistant (PDR) E. coli isolate. We found seasonal variation in resistance to important antibiotics such as cefradine, ampicillin, ceftazidime, azithromycin and aztreonam (Table 1).

ESBL E. coli and resistance genes prevalence

A total of 37% E. coli isolates tested positive for ESBL producing character using the phenotypic technique from each season (Fig. 2e). Furthermore, we tested selected ESBL-producing E. coli for antimicrobial resistance genes (ARGs) and found high levels of blaTEM in E. coli isolates in both winter (100%) and summer (91%). In contrast, the blaCTX-M gene was only found in winter isolates and at much lower levels (16%) (Fig. 2f). No blaSHV gene was detected in any season.

Seasonal influence of land use and land cover on AMR and ARGs

To test the seasonal influence of LULC available at buffer distance of 14.5 km around the roost on resistance profile of E. coli and ARGs (blaCTX-M and blaTEM), we employed multiple linear regression analyses, which revealed that six antibiotics (cefotaxime, tetracycline, aztreonam, imipenem, ciprofloxacin, and azithromycin) in winter and four antibiotics (gentamycin, cefradine, ciprofloxacin and ceftazidime) in summer to be influenced by LULC. Forest cover was found to be positively associated with resistance to aztreonam, cefradine, ceftazidime and blaTEM gene prevalence but a negative influence on resistance to imipenem and gentamicin. Similarly, crop cover affected negatively gentamicin, cefotaxime, imipenem, azithromycin and ciprofloxacin prevalence, while positively influencing aztreonam, ceftazidime, cefradine and blaCTX-M gene prevalence. The effect of water was mixed, with a positive association with ciprofloxacin in summer but a negative influence on the same antibiotic in winter. In addition, water had a negative influence on ceftazidime, azithromycin and positive influence on blaTEM gene prevalence. Settlement areas also influenced AMR differently, with a negative effect on cefotaxime and gentamicin and a positive effect on cefradine, tetracycline and imipenem. Out of all the tested LULC types, only human settlement areas showed no influence on the prevalence of the tested ARGs (Table 4) (Supplementary Table 4).

Comparison of AMR in P. medius with cattle and poultry

To ascertain the risk of environmental AMR exposure coming from livestock to P. medius, we compared the resistance profile of five antibiotic classes in cattle and poultry with P. medius, as these livestock species are direct recipients, and subsequent polluters of the environment with AMR. Pearson correlation analysis revealed that the overall resistance prevalence in P. medius exhibited similar levels of strong correlation with both cattle and poultry, (Fig. 3a). These correlations held true when compared seasonally (Fig. 3b) but not when the data were organized by province, likely because poultry production in Pakistan is heavily concentrated (70%) in the Punjab province62 (Fig. 3c) (Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

We investigated the AMR prevalence in P. medius and explored the species’ exposure risk to environmental AMR, in different seasons and associated LULCs around the roosts in Pakistan. We found resistance in E. coli isolates against all the tested antibiotics, including reserve antibiotics aztreonam and imipenem that are authorized for human use only. Likewise, HPCIA category antimicrobials tested in our study showed higher levels of resistance, causing serious One Health concerns. We found seasonal influence on three antibiotics (gentamicin, cefradine, and aztreonam) showing significant differences between summer and winter resistance prevalence. ESBL-producing E. coli and its associated ARGs (blaCTX-M and blaTEM) were isolated as indicators of the resistome shaped by human activities. Overall, the prevalence of resistance to five selected antibiotics in P. medius was similar to that seen in cattle and poultry. The prevalence of multidrug resistant bacteria, ESBL-producing E. coli, and their associated ARGs in P. medius is an emerging One Health challenge63.

Overall, 46% of our tested isolates were multidrug resistant. This unexpectedly high level of multidrug resistance is similar in percentage to that found in food producing animals and patients with nosocomial infections16,64. The presence of a high level of resistance against aztreonam in P. medius is of particular concern, due to its potential for developing resistance in both human and animal populations. Aztreonam excessive use in agriculture and clinical settings that ultimately seeps into wastewater65 can reach P. medius through drinking and thermoregulatory behaviors. In addition, resistance against imipenem, another member of the Reserve class antibiotic and High Priority Critically Important Antibiotics (HPCIA) (ciprofloxacin, cefotaxime, and cefepime) and Critically Important Antibiotics (CIA) (azithromycin and ceftazidime) is likely caused by selective pressure exerted by the overuse of antibiotics in human and veterinary clinical practice46.

The tested β-lactam antibiotic groups (penicillin, cephalosporins, monobactams, and carbapenems) are broad spectrum antibiotics, considered as a last resort antibiotics against severe infections. We found ampicillin (penicillin) resistance (37%) in E. coli isolates in P. medius which is comparatively higher than bats from Brazil (5%) and Australia (4%) 15,21 but lower than insectivorous bats in Poland in which 66% E. coli isolates had ampicillin resistance16. We also found similar elevated levels of resistance toward members of cephalosporins class, as compared to migratory birds in Pakistan66. The very high levels of resistance against rifampin, a primary antibiotic used in the treatment of tuberculosis36,67 in E. coli isolates in our study are not unexpected, as resistance toward the rifampin is due to an acquired mutation in the rpoB gene that is available widespread in the environment68. It is important to highlight that E. coli is not a clinical target of rifampin, though the mutation may still indicate selective pressure. Similarly, azithromycin is not standard for E. coli resistance testing under CLSI guidelines but we included it because during the early phase of COVID-19 azithromycin was extensively used in humans69,70. Interestingly we found resistance against azithromycin, with a higher level detected during winter (22%) than summer (15%) time.

A wide range of ESBL-producing E. coli have been reported in bats, contingent mainly on the anthropogenic antimicrobial pollution15,20,21,71. Unlike a previous study in Sri Lanka that reported 34% blaCTX-M genes in fruit bats18our study found a lower rate of 16% in a region characterized by greater seasonal fluctuations and more diverse land-use patterns. In contrast, low prevalence and multidrug resistance in bats have been associated with low human population densities in the Republic of Congo, Portugal, Peru and Indonesia20,71. We found P. medius exhibits a notably higher isolation ratio of ESBL-producing E. coli while lower MDR isolates compared to migratory birds studied in Pakistan72 highlighting their potential role as environmental sentinels for monitoring ESBL E. coli in the region. Extended-spectrum beta-lactamases are capable of inactivating third-generation cephalosporins and aztreonam73 offering an explanation for higher levels of resistance against these antibiotics in P. medius.

We found over 92% of tested ESBL-producing E. coli isolates were harboring blaTEM gene in both seasons, suggesting the source of propagation for this resistance gene was not seasonal. Resistance to β-Lactam antibiotics (ampicillin and amoxicillin) was predominantly conferred by blaTEM genes (92.3 %) in fecal samples of Australian flying foxes (Pteropus poliocephalus)15. However, studies in India and Portugal reported much lower percentage prevalence of the blaTEM gene at 14.2% and 21.1%, respectively19,74, possibly because of regional antibiotic usage patterns and prevailing weather and environmental conditions. In contrast, previous studies in Sri Lanka, Portugal and India reported 34%, 58% and 87% blaCTX-M genes in ESBL-producing E.coli from bats, which is much higher than the prevalence found in this study (16%) in Pakistan18,19,74. The low prevalence of blaCTX-M genes in flying foxes in Pakistan requires additional investigation, as over 92% of ESBL-producing E. coli isolates from winter migratory birds’ fecal samples and more than 86% from domestic animals carried the blaCTX-M gene in Pakistan66,75.

The blaTEM and blaCTX-M genes are responsible for the emergence of ESBL-producing E. coli genotypes64,66,71. The blaCTX-M genes are believed to originate from hospital settings66 while the origin of blaTEM genes is mostly animal. High levels of blaCTX-M and blaTEM genes have been isolated in humans, livestock and domesticated animals in Pakistan64,66,75,76 highlighting their widespread environmental prevalence. Their high prevalence in P. medius further supports the role of these frugivorous bats as an underappreciated reservoir of drug-resistant strains. Understanding seasonal dynamics and controlling the spread of resistance genes in the environment is important for effective antibiotic stewardship. These resistance genes can spread through the environment via horizontal gene transmission mechanisms, potentially leading to resistance in other pathogenic bacteria77.

Water bodies can serve as a reservoir for this resistome, facilitating gene flow that may also lead to multidrug-resistant pathogens in these bats, increasing the risk of spreading resistance across national borders to wildlife, livestock, and humans, due to their long-distance flight ability. To date, there have been no reported cases of direct human infection from bat-borne bacteria51but the possibility that synanthropic wildlife species such as P. medius can transmit ESBL E. coli isolates and their associated ARGs to humans remains, and warrants additional exploration.

In this study, the blaSHV gene was not detected, which aligns with previous reports indicating its zero47 to comparatively low prevalence in human and livestock in our study region78–80. The blaSHV gene confers resistance to β-lactam antibiotics, particularly penicillin and early-generation cephalosporins. Comparatively high prevalence of blaCTX-M and blaTEM genes in different species in our study region reflect regional antibiotic usage patterns and the dominance of specific resistant strains that overshadow blaSHV resistance gene47,78–80. These findings highlight the importance of region-specific resistance profiling to better inform antimicrobial stewardship strategies.

We found varying effects of LULC on different antibiotics that could be due to the combination of ecological, environmental, and microbial factors that influence the selection and spread of antibiotic resistance genes in different parts of the ecosystem. Research has shown that deforestation directly contributes to rising AMR due to subsequent use of antimicrobials in the agricultural holdings that are set up in deforested areas. In addition, deforestation increases the contact rate between humans and wildlife, increasing risk of transfer of resistant pathogens through mechanisms such as horizontal gene transfer and create novel opportunities for disease spillover81. Deforestation also introduces new sources of standing water to local environments which are perfect habitats for the number of wildlife including breeding grounds for disease vectors like mosquitoes. These opportunistic organisms can bring an influx of AMR pathogens along with other zoonoses82.

In our study imipenem and gentamicin are negatively affected by forest cover, suggesting a role of deforestation in the propagation of these two antibiotics in the environment. Crop cover positively influences aztreonam, cefradine, ceftazidime, and blaCTX-M gene prevalence, suggest a role of agro-livestock systems in spread of these antibiotics and ARGs in the environment. Settlement areas have positive effects on cefradine, tetracycline and imipenem, suggesting a high level of usage of these antibiotics in clinical settings for treatment of humans or animals near our study sites of P. medius. Water exerts a complex effect on the prevalence of ciprofloxacin resistance in the environment, indicating water channels are the primary pathway for the dissemination of this resistance. Future research on flow patterns of water channels might explain the seasonal variation of ciprofloxacin resistance found in our study.

Water bodies near livestock farms can be significant reservoirs for AMR due to the runoff of antimicrobial agents and farm waste. Our study indicates that P. medius may be acquiring resistance from livestock sources, given the high correlations observed in AMR resistance patterns of bats, cattle and poultry. In the present study, we could not assess the contribution or relationship with waterborne sources of human manufacturing waste to AMR in bats, and this should be a focus of future research. Seasonal and regional correlation between AMR patterns varied considerably between these species, highlighting the need for further research that should focus on testing water samples from nearby roosting sites of P. medius, which are mostly located on banks of rivers, dams and irrigation water channels in our study region. We did not sample water bodies for AMR in this study, but our results suggest that seasonal sampling to better characterize variation in the water-borne resistome would improve our understanding of resistance dynamics in bats. Likewise, testing left-over fruits that are partially destroyed by P. medius28 but still used in human consumption could generate evidence for whether there is transmission of AMR to humans and other animals from bats. These interactions suggest the need for holistic approaches based on One Health principles for managing and mitigating the growing threat of environmental AMR83.

This study provides insights into the environmental AMR pollution to which wildlife, in particular P. medius is exposed, highlighting the need for environmental AMR surveillance as an integrated One Health approach for effective AMR stewardship. Recent assessment of Pakistan’s First National Action Plan (NAP) on AMR found several limitations including exclusion of non-livestock sectors such as wildlife and the environment in assessing and managing risk associated with AMR84. This study addressed the recognized geographic gaps in AMR surveillance, particularly in wildlife from South Asia, as noted in WHO reports1 by evaluating AMR in P. medius. Investigating the route of ESBL-producing E. coli exposure as well as people and livestock screening near studied roosting sites will further improve our understanding of environmental AMR propagation dynamics. Knowledge about the drivers of AMR propagation by P. medius will provide a foundation for ecological countermeasures through evidence-based antimicrobial stewardship.
