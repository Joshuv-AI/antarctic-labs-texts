Alterations of the oral mycobiome in patients with CCA
Authors: Tong Ren, Bohan Jia, Benchen Rao, Haiyu Wang, Shanshuo Liu, Mengzhao Yang, Zecheng Yang, Yarong Ma, Yanqiu Fu, Wenlong Di, Zujiang Yu, Zhigang Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence links oral bacterial microbiota dysbiosis to cholangiocarcinoma (CCA). However, the oral mycobiome in CCA patients remains unexplored. This study aimed to characterize the oral mycobiome in CCA.MethodsSaliva samples were collected from CCA patients and healthy controls (HCs). ITS rRNA sequencing was performed to analyze microbial diversity, community composition, and function prediction. Spearman correlation analysis was conducted to explore the associations between fungi and bacteria, and key clinical indicators.ResultsA total of 132 HC and 72 CCA saliva samples were included. Compared with the HCs, the CCA group exhibited reduced oral mycobiome diversity. The CCA group was enriched with nine genera, including Candida, Komagataella and Ascomycota_gen_Incertae_sedi, while the HC group was enriched with 36 genera, such as Fungi_gen_Incertae_sedis, Bipolaris and Cryptococcus. Background

Cholangiocarcinoma (CCA) is a malignant tumor originating from biliary tract epithelium, accounting for approximately 15% of all primary liver cancers [1, 2]. CCA is characterized by “silent” early clinical manifestation, a challenging anatomical location for access, highly desmoplastic and pluricellular nature [3]. These features collectively restrict the sensitivity of cytological and histopathological diagnostic approaches, resulting in most patients being diagnosed at an advanced stage. Despite the improvement of current therapies against CCA, patients with CCA still exhibit a high mortality rate [1, 2, 4]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to identify novel biomarkers that can enhance the diagnostic accuracy of CCA and improve patient outcomes.

The oral microbiota includes bacteria, fungi, viruses, and phages, and is the second largest microbial system after the gut microbiota [5]. The oral microbiota assumes an important role in the progression of oral diseases [6]. Furthermore, an increasing amount of evidence supports the existence of a connection between the oral microbiota and cancer [7, 8]. Owing to the simplicity and non-invasive characteristic of obtaining saliva samples, its vast potential as a diagnostic marker for diseases has been progressively explored. Flemer et al. ascertained that the oral microbiota could function as a novel non-invasive biomarker for the diagnosis and prediction of colorectal cancer [9]. Kawasaki et al. verified that oral bacteria possessed the potential for diagnosing esophageal cancer at an early stage [10].

Regrettably, owing to the relatively low abundance of fungi, the difficulty in cultivating numerous fungi, and the absence of a distinct fungal genomic database, much of the research has predominantly concentrated on bacteria, while disregarding the importance of fungi [5, 11]. However, studies on the gut microbiome have demonstrated that even a small quantity of fungi can exert a significant influence on the local and peripheral immune responses of the body [12, 13]. Furthermore, research has been found that highly diverse oral fungi play a critical role in oral diseases through interactions with adjacent mucosal tissues and teeth [14]. In fact, oral fungi have been demonstrated to be correlated with a diverse range of human diseases, such as head and neck squamous cell carcinoma [15], gastric cancer [16], and schizophrenia [17]. However, the correlation between oral fungi and CCA has not yet been evaluated.

Therefore, in this study, we prospectively collected 228 saliva samples and ultimately included 204 samples for internal transcribed spacer (ITS) sequencing. During the discovery phase, we characterized the oral mycobiome features of the CCA and constructed a diagnostic model for CCA. Additionally, in the validation phase, we evaluated the potential of oral fungi as a non-invasive biomarker for CCA.

Methods

Study subjects and design

The aim of this study was to characterize oral fungi in patients with CCA. We prospectively collected a total of 228 saliva samples. Following a rigorous predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, 24 samples were excluded from further analysis. Detailed information was provided below: (1) 6 health controls (HCs) with missing clinical information; (2) 8 patients diagnosed with hepatocellular carcinoma; (3) 5 CCA patients with prior antitumor treatment; (4) 2 CCA patients with other organ tumors; (5) 3 CCA patients with diabetes. Therefore, we ultimately included 204 saliva samples for the subsequent analysis, comprising 132 samples from the HC group and 72 samples from the CCA group. The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of Zhengzhou University (2017-XY-002). All patients signed an informed consent to participate in this study before saliva samples were collected.

Saliva sample collection and DNA extraction

Prior to sample collection, all participants were required to fast and abstain from consuming water for at least 2 h. Participants rinsed their mouths with saline solution to remove food debris during sample collection. Subsequently, 2–5 ml of saliva was collected in a 10 ml sterile centrifuge tube. Finally, the saliva samples were transferred to the laboratory refrigerator and stored at -80 °C until sequencing.

Cells were first mechanically disrupted using a bead beater, followed by phenol-chloroform extraction. Next, DNA was further purified using a Quick gel extraction kit (Qiagen, Germany). DNA concentration was measured by NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific), and its molecular size was estimated by agarose gel electrophoresis [18].

PCR amplification

The universal primer ITS3F (5’-GCATCGATGAAGAACGCAGC-3’) and ITS4R (5’-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3’) were employed to amplify the ITS2 region of fungal ITS genes. The PCR reaction mixture was prepared with the following components: 12.5 µL of 2× Phanta Max Buffer, 0.5 µL of 10 mm dNTPs, 0.5 µL of each primer (10 μm), 0.5 µL of Phanta Max Super-Fidelity DNA Polymerase, 5 µL of template DNA, and 5.5 µL of ddH2O. Amplification was performed using a PCR machine (ABI GeneAmp 9700) under the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94 °C for 30 s, annealing at 55 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 30 s; with a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. Agarose gel (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA) was utilized for the separation, extraction and purification of the PCR products, and the fluorescence assay kit (Quant-iT PicoGreen, Invitrogen) was employed to quantify the product as we previously reported [19].

Library construction and sequencing

The purified first-round ITS2 PCR products were used for standard sequencing library construction using the NEBNext® Ultra™ II FS DNA Library Prep Kit (New England Biolabs) according to the official instructions. The process involved end repair and 3’-adenylation of blunt-ended PCR products, followed by ligation of Illumina-compatible Y-shaped adapters, and subsequent limited-cycle indexing PCR to incorporate full dual-index sequences. The resulting libraries were purified using Hieff NGS® DNA Selection Beads (Yeasen) through a two-round purification strategy, with the volume ratio of magnetic beads to DNA being 0.8 and 0.2 respectively. Following quality assessment, single-end sequencing analysis was conducted using the Illumina MiSeq platform (Shanghai Mobio Biomedical Technology, China) [20]. The raw Illumina read data from all specimens were deposited in the European Bioinformatics Institute European Nucleotide Archive database (PRJNA1263160).

Quality control

The raw sequencing data were subjected to a four-step quality control process as follows: (1) strictly filtering sequences containing uncertain bases (N) (maxN = 0); (2) using cutadapt to remove PCR primers and their complementary strands (-n 2, -j 20); (3) truncating low-quality bases based on quality values (truncQ = 2), filtering sequences with more than 2 expected errors (maxEE = 2), removing sequences shorter than 20 bp (minLen = 20), and eliminating PhiX phage contamination (rm.phix = TRUE); (4) using a consensus-based algorithm to remove chimeric sequences (method = “consensus”). Prior to quality control, a total of 9,685,094 raw reads were generated, with an average sequencing depth of 71,213 reads per sample (range: 25,589 − 211,038).

Following the complete quality control process, 5,728,546 high-quality reads were retained, with an average depth of 42,121 reads per sample (range: 9,151–153,429) for amplicon sequence variant (ASV) clustering. Detailed data were provided in Table S1.

ASV clustering and taxonomy annotation

The original sequencing reads were denoised using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2) (version 1.18.0) algorithm to obtain ASVs table. Taxonomic annotation was conducted using the naïve Bayesian classifier (via the assign Taxonomy function), based on the UNITE fungal ITS database (version 10), with a confidence threshold of 0.8. Classification results were summarized across taxonomic levels, and sequences that could not be confidently assigned were labeled as “unclassified”. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.3.1).

Bioinformatics analysis of ITS rRNA sequencing

Rarefaction curves were generated using the “vegan” package (version 2.6-4) in R software (version 4.3.1) to evaluate sequencing depth. Microbial alpha diversity was analyzed using the Shannon index and Simpson index, and community richness was estimated using the ACE index and Chao index. All alpha diversity indices were calculated using the “vegan” package (version 2.6-4) in the R software (version 4.3.1).

For beta diversity analysis, principal component analysis (PCA) based on Euclidean distance was performed using the “ade4” package (version 1.7–22), principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) and non-metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) based on Bray-Curtis distance were conducted using the “vegan” package (version 2.6-4). The fungal composition stacked bar chart was plotted using the “ggplot2” package (version 3.4.4). To facilitate data visualization, the relative abundance values were subjected to log10 transformation prior to plotting.

The linear discriminant analysis (LDA) effect size (LEfSe) (version 1.1) was used to detect taxa with differential abundance among groups. Metabolic pathways of the fungal community were predicted using Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States 2 (PICRUSt2) (version 2.5.2) based on phylogenetic inference, and ecological functional annotations were performed using FUNGuild database according to the ecological guilds of known fungal taxa.

Spearman correlation analysis using the “psych” package (version 2.3.9) was performed to evaluate the associations between oral fungi and both clinical indicators and oral bacteria. The heatmap was generated using the “corrplot” package (version 0.92). Correlations with |rho| > 0.3 and p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Detailed parameter settings for all analyses were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

ASV biomarker identification and probability of disease (POD) construction

The random forest model was constructed to identify ASV biomarkers. All samples were stratified and randomly assigned to training and test sets in a 2:1 ratio. The model was built using 1,000 decision trees, with key hyperparameters (max.depth = 4, min.node.size = 5, mtry = 2) optimized via 5-fold stratified cross-validation to minimize the risk of overfitting. Feature selection was performed through a three-level stratified strategy, including preprocessing screening, ranking of the top 20 candidate features based on permutation importance, and multi-combination cross-validation optimization. The feature combination with the highest area under the curve (AUC) value was ultimately selected as the optimal discriminant biomarker set. The performance of the final model was evaluated on the internal test set, and the AUC was computed to assess predictive accuracy. Detailed modeling procedures and validation protocols were provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Based on our prior definition of the POD index [18], in this study, it represents the ratio between the number of randomly generated decision trees predicting the sample as CCA and that of HC. Finally, we constructed the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) and AUC using the pROC package to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the established model.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed in R software (version 4.3.1). Normality and homogeneity of variance were assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test and Levene’s test, respectively. For continuous variables satisfying both normality and homogeneity of variance assumptions, group comparisons were conducted using the independent samples t-test (Welch’s t-test applied in unequal variances). Non-normally distributed continuous variables were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test (Wilcoxon rank sum test). Categorical variables were analyzed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Specifically, for comparisons of alpha diversity indices (Shannon, Simpson, ACE, and Chao1) were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The differences in beta diversity were assessed using non-parametric multivariate analysis of variance (PERMANOVA) based on Euclidean and Bray-Curtis distance metrics, with statistical significance determined through 999 permutations. The relative abundances of fungal taxa across taxonomic levels and differences of FUNGuild functional groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. To control the false positive rate, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini-Hochberg method via the R function p.adjust to correct the false discovery rate (FDR). Adjusted q-values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Study design and participant information

Following a rigorous pathological diagnosis and exclusion procedure, a total of 204 saliva samples were incorporated into this study, encompassing 132 HCs and 72 CCA samples. We randomly selected two-thirds of the samples as the discovery cohort and one-third as the validation cohort. In the discovery phase, we characterized the oral mycobiome of CCA patients and HCs, determined the critical biomarkers and constructed the CCA classifier. In the validation phase, the diagnostic efficacy of the CCA classifier was validated (Fig. 1).

Moreover, CCA patients demonstrated markedly impaired liver function relative to HCs, as evidenced by significantly increased levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyI transpeptidase (GGT), total bilirubin and direct bilirubin, along with decreased levels of total protein, albumin and platelets (Table 1). The reduced oral fungal microbial diversity in CCA

To evaluate the quality of the sequencing data, rarefaction curve analysis was performed on samples from both the HC and CCA groups within the discovery cohort. The result demonstrated that the rarefaction curves approached a plateau at a sequencing depth of approximately 5,000 reads, indicating that the current sequencing depth sufficiently captured the fungal diversity present in the samples (Fig. 2A), which provided a reliable data foundation for subsequent alpha and beta diversity analyses.

The Shannon index (B) and Simpson index (C) were used to assessed alpha diversity between CCA group and HC group. The Ace index (D) and Chao index (E) illustrated the richness of CCA patients and HCs. (F) The quantity of ASVs observed in the CCA patients and HCs. All alpha diversitymetrics were compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. CCA, Cholangiocarcinoma; HCs, healthy controls; ASVs, Amplicon Sequence Variants

In the alpha diversity analysis, compared with HCs, the Shannon index (p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U test) was decreased in CCA patients, while the Simpson index (p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U test) was increased (Fig. 2B-C, Table S3). Furthermore, the Ace index (p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U test) and Chao index (p < 0.001, Mann-Whitney U test) showed that the richness of the oral mycobiome in the CCA was lower than that in the HC (Fig. 2D-E, Table S3). Likewise, this finding was also confirmed through the observed ASVs (Fig. 2F, Table S3).

Alterations in the structure of the oral mycobiome in CCA

With the aim of exploring the variances in the oral mycobiome between the two groups, we evaluated these differences using PCA based on Euclidean distance, as well as PCoA and NMDS based on the Bray-Curtis distance. The visualization results showed a significant separation in structure of the oral mycobiome between the two groups (Fig. 3A-C). This finding was further supported by PERMANOVA, which confirmed statistically significant differences (Euclidean distance: R² = 0.06, p = 0.001; Bray-Curtis distance: R² = 0.09, p = 0.001) (Figure S1A-S1B). Additionally, the Venn diagram revealed that two groups shared 36 ASVs, it was worth noting that 152 ASVs were exclusively owned by CCA (Fig. 3D). Overall, these findings implied that alterations of oral fungi might be related to the occurrence of CCA.

Furthermore, subgroup analyses were performed according to the subtypes of CCA. No statistically significant differences were observed in alpha diversity indices (Shannon index, Simpson index, ACE index, and Chao index) among the iCCA, pCCA, and dCCA subgroups (Figure S2A-S2D, Table S4). The beta diversity analysis based on PCA, PCoA, and NMDS revealed a high degree of overlap in the spatial distribution of samples across the three groups (Figure S2E-S2G). The PERMANOVA analysis further showed no significant differences in microbial community composition among the subtypes (Figure S2H). These findings indicated that although the overall oral fungal composition in CCA patients differed significantly from that of HCs, the fungal community structure remained relatively conserved across CCA subtypes, with no evident subtype-specific patterns.

Dysbiosis of oral mycobiome in CCA

To identify the composition and changes of oral mycobiome in CCA patients, we plotted separately at each taxonomic level according to the ASV annotations. At the phylum level, Ascomycota, Fungi_phy_Incertae_sedis, and Basidiomycota were the three dominant populations in two groups, together accounting for more than 98% of the total sequences (Fig. 4A, Table S5). At the genus level, the relative abundance of Fungi_gen_Incertae_sedis, Bipolaris, Candida, Komagataella, and Blumeria were the main genera in the two groups. (Fig. 4B, Table S6).

Compared with HC group, phylum Ascomycota (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001) was increased in the CCA group, while the Fungiphy_Incertae_sedis (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), Basidiomycota (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), and Mucoromycota (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001) was decreased (Fig. 4C, Table S7). In addition, we presented the heatmap depicting the differences in ASV between the two groups (Fig. 4E, Table S9). In conclusion, these results suggested that the mycobiome structure in CCA has undergone significant alterations.

Phylogenetic profile of oral mycobiome communities in CCA

To identify oral fungi associated with CCA, we identified key functional fungal taxa based on LEfSe method. The cladogram highlighted the differential fungal biomarkers ranging from the class level to the genus level (Figure S3A). At the genus level, Candida, Komagataella, Yarrowia, and Ascomycota_gen_Incertae_sedis were enriched in CCA, and that 9 genera were enriched in HCs, such as Bipolaris, Fungi_gen_Incertae_sedis, Blumeria, Cryptococcus and Malassezia (Figure S3B, Table S10). These differentially enriched fungi were expected to serve as biomarkers for distinguishing CCA from HCs.

Fungal function prediction for CCA

To investigate potential metabolic alterations associated with CCA, we employed PICRUSt2 to predict functional gene profiles based on MetaCyc pathways. It was discovered that 30 gene pathways were enriched in the CCA group, including 1,3 propanediol biosynthesis (engineered), fatty acid elongation (saturated) and adenine and adenosine salvage Ⅲ. In contrast, 28 gene functions of the HCs were demonstrated to be advantageous, including fatty acid beta oxidation I, methyl ketone biosynthesis and superpathway of GDP-mannose-derived O-antigen building blocks biosynthesis (Figure S4, Table S11). However, it should be noted that the reference database used by PICRUSt2 has limited representation of fungal genomes [21], so these predictions should be interpreted as exploratory hypotheses rather than exact functional profiles.

In order to combine these predictions derived from genomic analysis with the experimentally verified ecological functions, we performed FUNGuild annotation. The results showed that at the trophic mode level, the CCA group exhibited significant enrichment of the Pathotroph-Saprotroph-Symbiotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001). In contrast, the HC group showed higher relative abundances of Pathotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), Saprotroph-Symbiotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q = 0.04), and Symbiotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q = 0.02), suggesting a more functionally diverse fungal community in healthy individuals (Figure S5A, Table S12).

At the functional guild level, the CCA group exhibited significant enrichment of fungal communities including Animal Pathogen-Endophyte-Endosymbiont-Epiphyte-Undefined Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), Plant Pathogen-Wood Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), and Animal Pathogen-Plant Pathogen-Undefined Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q = 0.008). In contrast, the HC group showed marked enrichment of guilds such as Plant Pathogen-Plant Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), Plant Pathogen (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), Animal Pathogen-Undefined Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), Animal Pathogen-Endophyte-Plant Saprotroph-Undefined Saprotroph-Wood Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001), Plant Saprotroph-Wood Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q = 0.009), and Endophyte-Plant Pathogen-Plant Saprotroph (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with Benjamini-Hochberg FDR correction, q < 0.001) (Figure S5B, Table S13). Notably, the enriched fungal taxa in the HC group were predominantly associated with plant-related ecological functions, highlighting a striking contrast to the animal pathogenicity commonly observed in the CCA group.

Correlation analysis of oral mycobiome with clinical parameters and oral bacteria in CCA

We employed the Spearman rank correlation test to systematically evaluate the correlations between differential fungi and key clinical parameters. The results revealed that specific fungal taxa were significantly associated with tumor markers and disease features. Specifically, Aspergillus, Bipolaris, and Hannaella showed negative correlations with tumor size. In contrast, Blumeria, Alternaria, Aspergillus, and Bipolaris were positively correlated with the lesion location. Notably, Bipolaris demonstrated a significant negative correlation with multiple laboratory parameters, including tumor markers (CA125, CA199) and liver function indices (AST and GLO). To further investigate the characteristics of oral fungal-bacterial interactions in CCA patients, we integrated ITS sequencing data from this study with 16 S rRNA sequencing data from our prior research [22]. Through rigorous sample matching based on the unique identifier (SampleID), a matched cohort of 72 CCA patients was established, ensuring that fungal and bacterial data originated from the same individuals. Based on this, a genus-level fungal-bacterial correlation network was constructed. The results demonstrated that the oral microbiota of CCA patients exhibited significant interactional dysregulation. Notably, among the differential bacteria identified in the constructed correlation network, four genera (Streptococcus, Rothia, Lactobacillus, and Actinomyces) were previously identified as exhibiting significant abundance differences in earlier study [22].

Further analysis elucidated the interaction patterns between these bacteria and fungi. Specifically, Streptococcus, Lactobacillus, and Rothia exhibited negative correlations with Komagataella, whereas Lactobacillus showed a positive correlation with Candida. Additionally, we uncovered multiple significant interactions, including a strong positive correlation between Pleurotus and Flexiline, a positive correlation between Bipolaris and Paludibacteraceae_F0058, as well as a positive correlation between Blumeria and Stomatobaculum (Fig. 5B, Table S15). Collectively, these findings highlight a complex interaction network between fungi and bacteria in the oral microbiota of CCA patients, implying that microbial ecological imbalance may play a critical role in the pathogenesis of CCA.

Identification of diagnostic model based on ASV biomarkers

During the discovery phase, the random forest model identified five ASV biomarkers with the highest discriminatory power, including ASV239 (Malassezia_restricta), ASV49 (Fungi_sp), ASV658 (Blumeria_graminis), ASV324 (Blumeria_graminis), and ASV4 (Dothideomycetes_spe_Incertae_sedis) (Figure S6A). The results demonstrated that the POD index in CCA patients was significantly higher than in HCs (Figure S6B, Table S16). Furthermore, the diagnostic model based on ASV biomarkers exhibited outstanding diagnostic potential (AUC = 99.91%, 95% CI: 0.997–1.000) (Figure S6C). In the internal validation cohort, the POD index was similarly elevated in CCA patients (Figure S6D, Table S17). Additionally, the diagnostic performance was further validated (AUC = 98.33%, 95% CI: 0.956–1.000) (Figure S6E). These findings indicated that oral fungi biomarkers hold promise as non-invasive diagnostic tools for CCA. Notably, the diagnostic performance was based on single-center internal validation cohort and its clinical utility and generalizability still need to be further verified through independent multi-center external validation cohorts.

Discussion

The evidence on the role of fungi in tumor formation is steadily accumulating. However, the relationship between oral fungi and CCA remains elusive. Therefore, conducting an in-depth analysis of the roles played by oral fungi and their functional networks in the onset and progression of CCA is highly significant.

In our previous work, we examined the oral bacterial microbiota of CCA and HC through saliva samples, revealing the CCA-associated oral bacterial characteristics [22]. In this study, we have for the first time explored the characteristics of oral mycobiome dysbiosis in CCA and established a diagnostic model for CCA based on oral fungi. We discovered that CCA patients not only presented a decreased oral mycobiome alpha diversity but also underwent alterations in beta diversity. These results indicated that the reduced microbial diversity in CCA represented a state of dysbiosis. However, this result was contrary to the increased alpha diversity observed in the oral bacterial microbiota of CCA [22], which suggest distinct patterns of mycobiome dysbiosis compared to bacterial dysbiosis in CCA. Evidences suggested that an increase in microbial diversity within mucosal organs typically indicated inflammation and disease [23, 24]. However, we discovered that the microbial diversity of CCA was lower, which might be due to the fact that different diseases possessed distinctive microbial alteration characteristics.

We noted significantly different oral mycobiome composition between CCA patients and HCs, similar to previously discovered in oral bacteria. Our investigation revealed that the predominant phyla of the oral mycobiome in both CCA and HC groups were Ascomycota, Fungi_phy_Incertae_sedis, and Basidiomycota, which was similar to the composition of gut fungi [25]. Notably, CCA patients exhibited distinct fungal profile characterized by an enrichment in Ascomycota and reduction in Basidiomycota. The ratio of the Basidiomycota to the Ascomycota of oral cavity was increased in patients with gastric cancer [16], nonetheless, our results were converse. More importantly, published data showed that the ratio of Basidiomycota and Ascomycota was higher in patients with inflammatory bowel disease in flare than that in healthy controls, but returned to normal during remission, suggesting that the imbalance between Basidiomycota and Ascomycota might be inflammation-driven [26].

At the genus level, the relative abundance of Candida was higher in CCA compared with HCs, while the abundance of Cryptococcus and Malassezia were lower. The excessive growth of Candida can modify the microbial community structure, which may result in the occurrence of various oral diseases [27]. Several studies have demonstrated that an elevation in the abundance of Candida heightens the risk of cellular malignant transformation [28]. Indeed, Candida have been detected in high proportions in oral tumors [29, 30], which is in accordance with our research findings. In this situation, it appears that Candida might function as a biomarker in the diagnosis of malignant diseases, which is also affirmed by the results of our subsequent LEfSe analysis. It even remains controversial whether Malassezia are benign commensals or pathogens. A previous study has found that the colonization of M.restricta may increase the severity of colitis, especially in patients carrying the CARD9S12N risk allele [31]. However, the recent metagenomic data indicated that the colonization of Malassezia on the skin varied with age. The increased Malassezia colonization in adults had the potential to suppress the colonization of more pathogenic microorganisms, thereby offering protection to the host [32]. In addition, an analysis of salivary mycobiome in a cohort of oral squamous cell carcinoma patients showed that higher carriage of Malassezia was positively associated with patient prognosis [33]. These results suggested that the interaction between Malassezia and human hosts was complex and malleable.

Functional prediction of fungal biology revealed that the significantly enriched biological pathways in CCA were predominantly associated with energy metabolism, particularly metabolism of amino acid and nucleotide. This finding was consistent with the bacterial functional predictions observed in both the intratumor microbiota and salivary microbiota of CCA patients [22, 34]. Notably, fatty acid and amino acid metabolism have been recognized as critical regulators of cancer metabolism, and their abnormal activation may contribute to tumor progression [35, 36]. Collectively, these results suggest that oral fungi in CCA could potentially participate in disease onset and progression by modulating metabolic reprogramming.

Moreover, we established a correlation network between oral fungi and bacteria, uncovering the intricate interaction patterns of fungal-bacterial associations within the oral microbiota of CCA patients. Under normal physiological conditions, Candida genus serves as non-pathogenic commensal microorganism residing in the gastrointestinal tract, oral cavity, and female reproductive tract, however, certain members of this genus can transition into opportunistic pathogens, leading to mucosal infections in these regions [13]. Human microbiota, particularly Lactobacillus, appear to play a role in preventing Candida from transitioning from a harmless commensal to a pathogenic microorganism [37, 38]. Researches indicate that the potential mechanisms of action of Lactobacillus on Candida involve modulating the immune function of the host’s epithelial barrier and competing for binding sites to inhibit Candida adhesion [39–41]. Our results demonstrated a positive correlation between Candida and Lactobacillus, which contrasted with previous findings. We propose that the following two mechanisms may collectively account for this observation. First, the relatively small sample size might result in inadequate statistical power, thereby compromising the reliability of the correlation analysis. Second, although glucose is the preferred carbon source for Candida, this fungus displays remarkable metabolic flexibility, allowing it to adapt dynamically to available carbon sources. Consequently, the hypoxic and high-lactic acid microenvironment in tumor patients could potentially drive Candida to shift its metabolism toward utilizing lactic acid as an alternative carbon source, fostering a symbiotic relationship with Lactobacillus.

Finally, the oral fungi-based biomarker diagnostic model constructed in this study exhibited exceptional performance in the diagnosis of CCA, achieving AUC values of 99.91% and 98.33% in the discovery and validation phases, respectively. This finding not only confirmed the clinical value of the oral mycobiome as a non-invasive diagnostic tool but also highlighted the convenience of its simple sampling procedure for large-scale population screening. Although further validation of the model’s generalizability across multi-center cohorts remains necessary, the results of this study have already established an innovative and reliable method for the early diagnosis and screening of CCA.

Our study has the following limitations. First, as an observational study, although we have excluded the known key confounding factors, detailed information on oral health status and lifestyle habits was not systematically collected across all participants. These unmeasured variables may introduce potential residual confounding effects. Second, the PICRUSt2 tool employed in this study primarily developed for bacterial 16 S rRNA gene sequences. Consequently, its application to highly variable fungal ITS sequences may fail to accurately capture fungus-specific metabolic pathways. Third, the correlation analysis based on 16 S/ITS sequencing primarily reflected microbial coexistence rather than interaction directionality, necessitating in vitro co-culture experiments to confirm functional interactions. Last, the diagnostic model was validated only within a single-center cohort, and its generalizability across diverse geographic and clinical populations remains to be further established.

Conclusions

This study, for the first time, systematically revealed the characteristic dysregulation patterns of oral mycobiome in patients with CCA and elucidated the complex yet tightly interconnected ecological interactions between oral fungi and bacteria within the oral microbiota. Additionally, the model developed based on oral fungi offers a promising tool for the non-invasive diagnosis of CCA.
