Single-sterilization modified-position robotic-assisted McKeown esophagectomy versus minimally invasive approach after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy for locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Hailiang Cai, Guoqing Liao, Weijie Zhan, Yuhang Zhang, Lintong Yao, Liyu Cai, Weihuan Lin, Shaowei Wu, Jens-C. Rueckert, Luyu Huang, Haiyu Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSurgical resection remains a key component of multimodal therapy for patients with resectable or locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (nCIT). This study compared the safety and efficacy of single-sterilization modified-position robotic-assisted esophagectomy (SM-RAE) with conventional minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE), focusing on perioperative outcomes, composite adverse events, electronic patient-reported outcomes (ePROs), and short-term oncologic results.MethodsThis single-center retrospective study included 137 ESCC patients who underwent McKeown esophagectomy after nCIT between January 2022 and December 2024. Of them, 36 received SM-RAE and 101 received MIE. Baseline characteristics, perioperative indicators, composite adverse events (operative time ≥330 min, intraoperative blood loss ≥400 mL, conversion to open, non-R0 resection, or Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III complications), ePROs (pain, cough, distress, dysphagia, and dyspnea), and short-term survival outcomes were analyzed. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance baseline variables, and comparisons were repeated after matching.ResultsCompared with MIE, SM-RAE resulted in shorter operative time (270 vs. 295 min, P<0.001) and neck drain duration (4 vs. 6 days, P<0.001), a higher yield of recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) lymph nodes (4.5 vs. 2.0, P<0.001), and fewer composite adverse events (16.67% vs. 41.58%, P=0.007). Multivariate analysis confirmed SM-RAE as an independent protective factor for composite adverse events [odds ratio (OR) =0.28, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.11–0.74; P=0.01]. SM-RAE was not an independent predictor of major pathological response (MPR) or recurrence. ePROs showed lower postoperative symptom scores in weeks 1–8 (P<0.001), indicating faster recovery. Findings remained consistent after PSM. No significant differences were observed in R0 resection rate, overall complications, ward cost, pathological complete response (pCR)/MPR rates, recurrence, or short-term survival.ConclusionsSM-RAE is a safe, effective, and feasible surgical option for ESCC patients after nCIT. For selected patients with locally advanced disease, SM-RAE offers a non-inferior alternative to standard MIE, with advantages in perioperative efficiency, RLN lymphadenectomy, and patient-reported recovery.

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study evaluated a refined robotic approach—single-sterilization modified-position robotic-assisted esophagectomy (SM-RAE)—for McKeown esophagectomy after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (nCIT) in locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC). Compared with conventional minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE), SM-RAE shortened operative time, increased recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) lymph node yield, reduced composite adverse events, and improved early postoperative recovery. Importantly, short-term oncologic outcomes—including recurrence, recurrence-free-survival, and overall survival—were comparable between groups.

What is known and what is new?

• Conventional McKeown MIE requires patient repositioning and repeated sterilization, which complicate upper mediastinal exposure and RLN lymphadenectomy—challenges that become more pronounced after nCIT due to treatment-related fibrosis and edema.

• This study introduces SM-RAE, which integrates single-step sterilization, a modified semi-prone position, and the robotic system’s enhanced visualization and stable fine-motion control to improve mediastinal access and streamline the operative workflow. It represents the first structured evaluation of this approach specifically in the post-nCIT setting.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• SM-RAE offers a safe and efficient alternative to standard MIE after nCIT, enhancing surgical workflow and RLN dissection quality without compromising short-term oncologic outcomes. These findings support broader consideration of SM-RAE for selected ESCC patients and highlight the need for multicenter, prospective validation.

Introduction

Esophageal cancer continues to pose a substantial public health challenge worldwide and remains one of the primary causes of cancer-related deaths in China (1,2). For individuals with locally advanced disease or potentially resectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), neoadjuvant therapy followed by surgery constitutes the established standard of care. In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors have shown promising therapeutic potential in esophageal cancer. Several studies have demonstrated that the combination of immunotherapy and chemotherapy yields favorable outcomes in locally advanced disease (3-5). The ESCORT-NEO trial and another phase III randomized controlled trial further confirmed that chemoimmunotherapy significantly improves the major pathological response (MPR) rate and pathological complete response (pCR) (6,7).

Following neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (nCIT), surgical resection remains necessary, yet, the operative difficulty may increase because treatment-related fibrosis and edema can obscure the upper mediastinum and complicate recurrent laryngeal nerve (RLN) lymphadenectomy. Since its introduction in 1992, minimally invasive esophagectomy (MIE) has been adopted widely (8); however, it remains technically limited in precise RLN dissection, particularly in challenging post-nCIT anatomy (9). Robotic-assisted esophagectomy (RAE), with its stable visualization and refined motion control, has shown advantages in dissecting complex regions and retrieving more RLN lymph nodes than MIE (10-12).

In the conventional minimally invasive McKeown approach, intraoperative repositioning and repeated sterilization prolong operative time and may interfere with workflow efficiency (13). Liu et al. (14) described a single-sterilization minimally invasive technique performed in a modified semi-prone position, which eliminated re-sterilization and reduced operative duration while maintaining safety.

Building on this concept, this study developed a single-sterilization modified-position RAE (SM-RAE) to further optimize exposure of the upper mediastinum and streamline the surgical workflow. The present study compares SM-RAE with conventional MIE in patients with locally advanced ESCC following nCIT, evaluating perioperative outcomes, short-term postoperative results, and electronic patient-reported outcomes (ePROs). We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-1-2462/rc).

Methods

Study design and patient enrollment

This retrospective cohort study, conducted at a single institution, enrolled consecutive patients diagnosed with locally advanced ESCC (cT1N1–3M0 or cT2–4aN0–3M0) treated at Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital between January 2022 and December 2024. Because of the retrospective nature and fixed cohort size, no a priori sample size calculation was performed. All enrolled patients underwent McKeown esophagectomy following nCIT. Details of case inclusion and exclusion are summarized in Figure 1.

Patient inclusion and exclusion flowchart. ESCC, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma; MIE, minimally invasive esophagectomy; nCIT, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy; PSM, propensity score matching; SM-RAE, single-sterilization modified-position robotic-assisted esophagectomy.

Exclusion criteria were: (I) procedures other than McKeown esophagectomy; (II) absence of nCIT; (III) non-squamous cell histology; (IV) incomplete clinical information; and (V) palliative-intent surgery.

Preoperative assessment included routine endoscopic biopsy, thoracoabdominal computed tomography (CT), and cardiopulmonary evaluation. All operations were performed by the same lead surgical team. Surgical candidacy was reviewed and confirmed at a multidisciplinary team (MDT) meeting to ensure the absence of contraindications.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. Approval for this study was granted by the Ethics Committee of Guangdong Provincial People’s Hospital (approval No. KY2025-938-01), and all participants provided written informed consent.

nCIT

All patients received at least one cycle of nCIT with a 3-week interval between cycles. The regimen consisted of albumin-bound paclitaxel combined with platinum-based agents, with a programmed death-1 (PD-1) inhibitor (e.g., sintilimab, tislelizumab, camrelizumab, pembrolizumab, nivolumab, or toripalimab). McKeown esophagectomy (either thoracoscopic or robot-assisted) was performed at least four weeks after completion of nCIT.

Standard surgical procedures

SM-RAE technique

After general anesthesia was administered, patients in the robotic group were placed in a modified single-sterilization position. The procedure began with the patient was set in the left lateral decubitus position. With manual adjustment by the surgical team, the torso was rotated to achieve an approximately 45° semi-supine orientation. The sterilized field extended from the upper one-third of the thigh to the mandible, laterally to the anterior margins of both trapezius muscles; on the right thorax from the posterior midline to the left midaxillary line; the left upper limb below the wrist; and the abdominal region between the bilateral midaxillary lines.

Following routine sterilization and draping, the left upper limb was wrapped in a sterile sleeve and positioned laterally with slight pronation. Povidone-iodine was applied to the cervical region, and single-lung ventilation was initiated. A 1-cm incision was made in the 5th intercostal space (ICS) along the right posterior axillary line for placement of an 8-mm da Vinci trocar. Under thoracoscopic guidance, three additional 8-mm robotic trocars were inserted at the following locations: the 3rd ICS along the midaxillary line, the 7th ICS approximately 2 cm lateral to the posterior axillary line, and the 9th ICS along the right scapular line. A 12-mm assistant trocar was placed in the 6th ICS along the anterior axillary line (Figure 2). Carbon dioxide insufflation was maintained at 8 cm H2O.

Trocar placement for the SM-RAE procedure. Three operating ports were positioned at the 7th ICS, 2 cm lateral to the right posterior axillary line; the 9th ICS along the right scapular line; and the 3rd ICS along the right midaxillary line. The camera port was positioned at the 5th ICS along the right posterior axillary line, and an assistant port at the 6th ICS along the right anterior axillary line. ICS, intercostal space; SM-RAE, single-sterilization modified-position robotic-assisted esophagectomy.

The da Vinci system was then docked, and the thoracic phase of the McKeown esophagectomy was performed, including thoracic esophageal mobilization and systematic lymphadenectomy with emphasis on bilateral RLN lymph node dissection. After completion of the thoracic phase, the patient was repositioned to the supine position without the need for re-sterilization, and the abdominal phase was subsequently completed robotically, including gastric mobilization, abdominal lymph node dissection, and gastric conduit formation. The gastric conduit was then delivered to the left cervical field, where a combined hand-sewn and stapled cervical anastomosis was performed.

Conventional MIE

Patients in the MIE group underwent the conventional multi-position McKeown approach. During the thoracic phase, patients were positioned in the left lateral decubitus position. An 11-mm camera port was placed in the 8th ICS along the posterior axillary line, and three working trocars were inserted at the 4th ICS along the mid-axillary line, the 6th ICS along the anterior axillary line, and the 9th ICS along the infrascapular line.

For the abdominal phase, patients were repositioned to the supine position. A 12-mm infra-umbilical port was used as the optical port, and four additional 5 or 12-mm working trocars were placed at the bilateral subcostal regions along the anterior axillary lines, the left costal margin along the mid-axillary line, and the subxiphoid region.

Data collection and definitions

Collected variables included demographic characteristics [age, sex, body max index (BMI), smoking history, and alcohol consumption], operative parameters (operative time, intraoperative blood loss, conversion to open surgery, and R0 resection status), lymph node yield (total lymph nodes and RLN lymph nodes), drainage duration, intensive care unit (ICU) transfer, length of hospital stay, and postoperative complications classified using the Clavien-Dindo system.

Postoperative complications within 3 months after surgery were systematically recorded. According to the Clavien-Dindo classification, grade 0 indicated no complications; grade I included events not requiring pharmacologic treatment; grade II included complications requiring pharmacotherapy without surgical intervention; grade III included complications requiring surgical, endoscopic, or interventional procedures; and grade IV represented life-threatening events such as respiratory or circulatory failure or major postoperative hemorrhage.

A composite adverse outcome was defined as the presence of any of the following: operative time ≥330 min, intraoperative blood loss ≥400 mL, conversion to open surgery, non-R0 resection, or Clavien-Dindo grade ≥III complication. The cutoff for prolonged operative time (≥330 min) was based on the third quartile (Q3) of the operative-time distribution in the overall cohort, approximating 5.5 hours. The blood loss threshold (≥400 mL) was defined according to institutional clinical practice, as it may be associated with hemodynamic instability. This composite indicator was constructed based on previously published studies incorporating clinically meaningful perioperative safety metrics.

Pathological assessment was performed by experienced pathologists according to the 9th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) tumor-node-metastasis classification (TNM) staging system, documenting tumor invasion depth and longitudinal extent.

Follow-up and outcomes

Patients completed weekly ePROs addressing pain, cough, distress, dysphagia, and dyspnea (0–10 scale) during postoperative weeks 1–8. Follow-up occurred every 3 months for 2 years, then every 6 months up to 5 years. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from surgery to death from any cause. Recurrence-free survival (RFS) was defined as the time from surgery to the first recurrence, metastasis, or death.

Statistical analysis

Frequencies and percentages were used to summarize categorical variables, which were compared using Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests. Continuous variables were represented by medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs), and differences between groups were examined with the Mann-Whitney U test.

Linear mixed-effects modeling was used for ePRO assessment, incorporating fixed effects for group, time, and their interaction. Propensity score matching (PSM) was performed using 1:2 nearest-neighbor matching with a 0.2 caliper. Sex, age, BMI, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) score, smoking and alcohol history, tumor location, and clinical stage served as matching covariates. Post-match balance was deemed acceptable when standardized mean difference (SMD) values were under 0.2.

Confounders included in multivariable models were selected based on clinical relevance and previous literature. Effect estimates from multivariable regression analyses were reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). There were no missing data for key variables; therefore, complete-case analysis was performed, and model assumptions were examined and met. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P<0.05.

All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10.0.0 for macOS (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA; www.graphpad.com). PSM was conducted using R version 4.3.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; www.r-project.org) with the MatchIt package (version 4.5.5).

Results

Baseline clinical characteristics

A total of 137 patients with locally advanced ESCC were included (Table 1), of whom 36 underwent SM-RAE (29 males, 80.56%; 7 females, 19.44%) and 101 underwent MIE (81 males, 80.2%; 20 females, 19.8%). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were generally comparable between groups, including age distribution, proportion aged ≥65 years, BMI, comorbidities, and pre-treatment tumor features (length, thickness, and location on CT). Preoperative laboratory parameters and tumor marker profiles showed no significant intergroup differences.

Perioperative outcomes

Perioperative outcomes are summarized in Table S1. Operative time was significantly shorter in the SM-RAE group than in the MIE group [270.00 (260.00–285.25) vs. 295.00 (270.00–335.00) min; P<0.001]. In addition, 30.7% of patients in the MIE group required an operative time ≥330 min, whereas none in the SM-RAE group had an operative time of ≥330 min (P<0.001). Conversion to open surgery occurred in three MIE patients (2.9%)—two for extensive adhesions and one for uncontrolled bleeding. Blood loss was comparable between groups (P=0.57).

R0 resection rates were similar (97.22% for SM-RAE vs. 97.07% for MIE), as were postoperative ICU transfer rates. The total number of lymph nodes dissected, and number of positive nodes showed no significant differences. However, RLN lymph node yield was significantly higher in SM-RAE (median 4.50 vs. 2.00, P<0.001), consistent across right- and left-sided dissections.

Postoperative complications are listed in Table S2. The overall complication rates were comparable between groups (51.5% for MIE vs. 50.0% for SM-RAE, P=0.88, Table S1). Mild to moderate complications (Clavien-Dindo grade I–II) occurred in 30.7% of MIE and 36.1% of SM-RAE patients, while severe complications (grade ≥III) occurred in 20.8% and 13.9%, respectively. Grade IV complications were observed only in the MIE group (2.97%), including postoperative hemorrhage and cardiopulmonary failure (some cases with both). No surgery-related deaths (grade V) occurred in either group.

Short-term postoperative outcomes (Table 2) showed significantly shorter neck drain duration in SM-RAE [4.00 (3.75–5.00) vs. 6.00 (4.00–8.00) days, P<0.001], while chest drainage duration and length of stay were similar. On postoperative day 1, SM-RAE showed higher white blood cell (WBC) count, neutrophil percentage, and serum albumin, but lower hemoglobin. Postoperative day 3 laboratory values did not differ significantly. Total and operative costs were significantly higher in SM-RAE, whereas ward costs were similar (P=0.23).

A composite adverse outcome (operative time ≥330 min, blood loss ≥400 mL, conversion, non-R0 resection, or Clavien-Dindo ≥ III) occurred significantly less often in SM-RAE (16.67% vs. 41.58%, P=0.007, Table S1). Prolonged operative time (22.63% in the overall cohort) represented the largest proportion of the composite adverse outcome and was the primary contributor to the observed difference. The remaining components occurred at lower frequencies: Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III complications (18.98%), non-R0 resection (2.92%), and conversion to open surgery (2.19%). Prolonged operative time accounted for the largest proportion of the composite adverse outcome, indicating that this endpoint is driven mainly by operative duration and may be more sensitive to differences in operative time than to the less frequent components. In both univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses, SM-RAE was associated with a lower likelihood of composite adverse outcomes (OR =0.28, 95% CI: 0.11–0.74, P=0.01, Table S3).

Pathological outcomes

The rates of pCR were 25.0% for SM-RAE and 29.7% for MIE, while the MPR rates were 50.0% and 53.47%, respectively (Table S1). Post-nCIT pathological stages were predominantly stage I–IIIA, with tumor downstaging rates of 58.33% for SM-RAE and 64.36% for MIE.

Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses showed that the surgical approach was not a significant predictor of MPR (OR =0.87, 95% CI: 0.41–1.86, P=0.72, Table S4).

Short-term survival outcomes

At a median follow-up of 23.1 months (IQR, 15.1–32.6 months), 27 recurrences occurred (17 in MIE, 10 in SM-RAE; P=0.16, Table 2). Median OS was 24.0 months (MIE) vs. 22.9 months (SM-RAE) (P=0.87), and median RFS was 20.0 vs. 20.2 months (P=0.56). Kaplan-Meier curves showed no significant differences in OS or RFS (P=0.45 and 0.71, respectively; Figure 3A,3B). Five deaths occurred in SM-RAE (all ESCC-related), compared with 20 in MIE (one due to sepsis).

Multivariate analyses showed that surgical approach did not independently predict recurrence, RFS, or OS. Vascular tumor thrombus was an independent risk factor for recurrence and RFS (recurrence: OR =7.00, 95% CI: 1.24–39.35, P=0.03, Table 3; RFS: OR =13.06, 95% CI: 1.77–96.59, P=0.01, Table 4), while MPR was an independent protective factor for both RFS and OS (RFS: OR =0.32, 95% CI: 0.13–0.83, P=0.02; OS: OR =0.22, 95% CI: 0.08–0.59, P=0.003, Table 4).

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs)

A linear mixed-effects model was used to analyze temporal changes in ePRO scores. The ePROs included five patient-reported symptom dimensions: pain, cough, distress, dysphagia, and dyspnea, each quantified on a 0–10 scale. As shown in Figure 4A-4E, during postoperative weeks 1–8, the SM-RAE group reported significantly lower symptom scores across all five dimensions compared with the MIE group (all P<0.001), indicating a lighter symptom burden. Over time, symptom scores declined in both groups; however, the SM-RAE group demonstrated a steeper decline, reflecting faster symptom relief and overall recovery.

Discussion

This single-center retrospective study evaluated the safety and feasibility of SM-RAE in patients with locally advanced or resectable ESCC after nCIT, incorporating ePROs to assess early postoperative recovery. Compared with MIE, SM-RAE achieved shorter operative time, a higher RLN lymph node yield, shorter neck drainage duration, and a lower incidence of composite adverse outcomes. Multivariate analysis further identified SM-RAE as an independent protective factor for composite adverse outcomes, supporting its clinical feasibility in the post-nCIT setting.

Lymph node metastasis is the principal pathway of spread in ESCC and an established determinant of stage and prognosis (15). Comprehensive lymphadenectomy, particularly along the RLN, is critical for accurate staging and guiding postoperative treatment (16). However, RLN lymphadenectomy remains technically challenging because conventional thoracoscopic instruments provide limited maneuverability and unstable exposure in the upper mediastinum—difficulties further amplified after nCIT due to treatment-related fibrosis, edema, and tissue fragility (17). Although modified techniques such as RLN traction-suspension have been proposed (18), achieving both adequate exposure and fine-motion control remains a major limitation of standard thoracoscopy.

In this study, SM-RAE demonstrated a consistently higher RLN lymph node yield, aligning with previous evidence favoring robotic assistance in complex dissections (19,20). This advantage likely reflects the combined effects of the modified left semiprone position and the robotic system’s enhanced visualization and stable fine-motion control (21,22). The semi-prone angle allows gravitational retraction of the aortic arch and innominate vein, creating a clearer view of the RLN corridor from the arch to the tracheoesophageal groove. Simultaneously, the robotic system offers high-definition three-dimensional visualization and improved wristed instrument control, enabling meticulous perineural dissection with reduced traction. These factors may contribute to both an expanded RLN dissection range and improved overall dissection quality.

Beyond improved exposure, SM-RAE also enhanced operative efficiency. Consistent with previous studies (23), SM-RAE reduced operative time even after PSM adjustment. This improvement stems from eliminating the need for repeated sterilization and repositioning inherent to the conventional multi-position McKeown approach, while also benefiting from the ergonomic and technical features of robotic surgery. Because prolonged operative time has been associated with increased perioperative morbidity and poorer outcomes (24,25). SM-RAE may offer a meaningful workflow advantage in the challenging post-nCIT mediastinum.

Importantly, SM-RAE significantly reduced composite adverse outcomes. This composite endpoint, integrating multiple meaningful perioperative events, provides greater statistical efficiency and reflects overall perioperative risk more comprehensively than single variables alone (26,27). SM-RAE demonstrated clear advantages in lymphadenectomy and workflow efficiency while maintaining comparable short-term oncologic outcomes. These findings align with previous reports showing similar oncologic safety between robotic and thoracoscopic esophagectomy (13). However, given the relatively small sample size, caution is warranted when interpreting long-term oncologic equivalence.

In addition to objective perioperative metrics, this study incorporated ePROs to capture patient-centered recovery. PROs have become increasingly relevant in surgical oncology and have shown utility in robotic procedures across other disciplines (28,29). Traditional metrics such as complication rates cannot fully reflect postoperative symptom burden or functional recovery. Prior work has suggested that robotic esophagectomy may reduce early postoperative discomfort (30) and improve functional outcomes (31). In our study, SM-RAE was associated with lower ePRO symptom scores and faster symptom resolution during the first eight postoperative weeks, indicating a lighter recovery burden. These results emphasize the value of integrating patient-reported measures into postoperative assessment, particularly in minimally invasive and robotic surgery.

SM-RAE is generally associated with higher operative costs compared with conventional minimally invasive or open approaches. However, evidence from prior studies suggests that total hospital costs are often comparable, likely because reductions in postoperative complications and shorter hospital stays offset the increased procedural expenses. For example, the ROBOT trial showed that RAE reduced postoperative complications compared with open esophagectomy without increasing total hospital costs (32), and observational comparisons with conventional MIE reached similar conclusions (33). These findings indicate that the potential clinical benefits of RAE, including improved lymphadenectomy and workflow efficiency, may balance the higher operative costs, formal cost-effectiveness analysis will be conducted in future studies as more RAE cases are accumulated.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although PSM was applied, the retrospective single-center design does not allow firm causal conclusions and limits the generalizability of the findings. In addition, residual confounding related to surgeon experience and learning-curve effects cannot be fully excluded. Therefore, the conclusions of this study primarily reflect short-term outcomes within a single-center, surgeon-specific context; their applicability across different institutions or surgical teams should be interpreted with caution and requires confirmation in future multicenter, prospective studies. Second, the relatively small number of patients in the SM-RAE group reduces statistical power for oncologic endpoints such as recurrence and survival; thus, the absence of statistically significant differences should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence. Third, the median follow-up was relatively short, limiting assessment of long-term outcomes; larger cohorts with longer follow-up are needed to better evaluate recurrence, survival, and late complications. Fourth, while ePROs provide valuable patient-centered information, they are inherently subjective and may be influenced by patient expectations or digital literacy, which may affect data completeness and consistency. Finally, a formal cost-effectiveness analysis is planned in future work as additional RAE cases become available.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that SM-RAE is a safe, feasible, and effective surgical option for patients with locally advanced ESCC following nCIT. SM-RAE improved perioperative efficiency and RLN lymph node dissection while maintaining comparable short-term oncologic outcomes to conventional MIE. For appropriately selected patients, SM-RAE represents a practical alternative to standard minimally invasive approaches for short-term perioperative results.
