Global research trends, knowledge structure, and future directions in melioidosis research: a bibliometric analysis, 1990–2025
Authors: Junming Zhang, Yiru Shi, Yehua Wu, Yuefu Zhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundResearch on melioidosis has expanded steadily over recent decades, but its global research landscape, knowledge structure, collaboration patterns, and thematic evolution remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsWe searched the Web of Science Core Collection for melioidosis-related Article and Review records published from 1990 to 2025. Bibliometrix-biblioshiny, VOSviewer, CiteSpace, Microsoft Excel, and R were used to analyze publication output, collaboration networks, institutional and author contributions, reference co-citation patterns, keyword co-occurrence, and trend topics.ResultsA total of 3973 Article and Review records from 955 sources were included in the final analysis. The annual publication growth rate was 8.42%, and annual output increased from 12 records in 1990 to a peak of 208 records in 2015, with a model-estimated publication peak around 2019.7 (R2 = 0.973). The United States, Thailand, and Australia were the leading contributing countries, while Mahidol University was the most productive institution and CURRIE BJ was the most influential author. Keyword and co-citation analyses showed that melioidosis research was mainly organized around epidemiology and clinical management, virulence and antimicrobial resistance, pathogen identification and typing, immunopathogenesis and vaccine-related studies, and environmental sources and outbreak-related investigations. Recent trend topics increasingly involved antimicrobial resistance, biofilms, outbreak investigation, environmental factors, mortality, and clinical outcomes.ConclusionsMelioidosis research has entered a stage of bibliometric consolidation, characterized by sustained publication output, stable core contributors, and increasingly diversified research themes. The field has shifted from early disease recognition and laboratory detection toward integrated research on clinical management, antimicrobial resistance, pathogen biology, environmental exposure, and outcome-oriented investigation. Strengthening international collaboration and linking molecular, environmental, and clinical data may help advance future melioidosis research and public health practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41182-026-01012-7.

Background

Melioidosis is an important tropical infectious disease caused by Burkholderia pseudomallei and is mainly endemic in Southeast Asia and northern Australia. Its clinical manifestations are highly diverse and may involve multiple organ systems, including the lungs, bloodstream, central nervous system, and bones and joints. Because of its varied presentations and high mortality in severe cases, melioidosis has been referred to as “the great mimicker” [1]. Previous modeling studies estimated that approximately 165,000 cases of melioidosis occur globally each year, resulting in about 89,000 deaths, suggesting that its disease burden may be substantially underestimated [2]. In addition, melioidosis has not received public health attention commensurate with its disease burden and has been proposed as an important neglected tropical disease requiring greater recognition [3, 4].

In recent years, with improvements in laboratory diagnostic capacity, increasing research on environmental and climate-related factors, and the continuing reports of autochthonous cases in areas not traditionally considered endemic, as well as travel-associated cases, the global epidemiological landscape of melioidosis has continued to evolve [5, 6]. Research topics have expanded from early case reports and descriptions of clinical characteristics to a broader range of areas, including epidemiological surveillance, environmental sources, virulence and antimicrobial resistance mechanisms, biofilms, molecular typing, and whole-genome sequencing, reflecting a distinctly multidisciplinary nature [1, 6, 7].

Despite the continuous accumulation of melioidosis-related studies, the existing literature remains thematically dispersed and spans a wide range of topics, and there is still a lack of a systematic overview of the global research landscape, major contributors, knowledge base, and evolving hotspots in this field. Traditional reviews have largely focused on clinical management or basic mechanisms and are therefore less able to reveal, from a macro-level perspective, the overall developmental trajectory and research frontiers of this field [1, 8, 9].

Bibliometric analysis enables the systematic characterization of the knowledge structure, developmental trajectory, and emerging frontiers of a research field through quantitative analysis of research output, collaboration networks, knowledge base, and thematic evolution [10, 11]. Therefore, based on melioidosis-related publications published between 1990 and 2025, the present study visualized global research output, collaboration networks, knowledge base, and hotspot evolution, with the aim of providing a reference for future basic research, clinical prevention and control, and international collaboration. This approach has been widely applied to map scientific productivity trends, research hotspots, and knowledge structures across biomedical and infectious disease-related fields [12].

Methods

Literature search and eligibility criteria

A bibliometric search was conducted in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC) on January 9, 2026. The search strategy was as follows: TS = (melioidosis OR "Whitmore disease" OR “Burkholderia pseudomallei” OR “B. pseudomallei” OR (“Burkholderia” NEAR/3 pseudomallei)) AND PY = 1990–2025. The publication year was restricted to 1990–2025, and only Article and Review records were included. Full records and cited references were exported in plain-text format.

Data cleaning and standardization

The exported WoSCC records were screened before analysis. Records were restricted to Article and Review document types, limited to publication years 1990–2025, and checked for retraction-related records and potential duplicates using WoS accession numbers, DOI, title, publication year, and source title. After screening, 3,973 Article and Review records were retained for bibliometric analysis.

For keyword analysis, a thesaurus file was used to merge spelling variants and synonymous terms and to remove non-informative search terms or generic terms (S1 Table). Institution names were manually standardized when clear spelling or naming variants referred to the same institution, whereas closely related but separately indexed entities were retained as distinct institutions.

Bibliometric and network analysis

Descriptive bibliometric indicators, including annual publication output, annual growth rate, source distribution, author productivity, country-level scientific production, corresponding-author country distribution, citation indicators, and collaboration indicators, were calculated using bibliometrix-biblioshiny in RStudio and Microsoft Excel [8]. Country-level scientific production was interpreted as the frequency of country occurrences in author affiliations. Single-country publications (SCP) and multiple-country publications (MCP) were used to evaluate international collaboration.

Reference co-citation analysis was performed using bibliometrix-biblioshiny [8]. The unit of analysis was cited references, and the network was constructed using the top 50 nodes. Louvain clustering was applied, isolated nodes were removed, the minimum number of edges was set to 2, and the repulsion force was set to 0.5.

Keyword co-occurrence analysis was performed using VOSviewer version 1.6.20 [13]. All keywords, including author keywords and Keywords Plus, were analyzed using full counting. Keywords with at least 50 occurrences were included after thesaurus-based standardization. Trend topic analysis was performed using bibliometrix-biblioshiny [8], and the top 55 high-frequency trend terms were visualized using R.

Lifecycle fitting and institutional disambiguation

Annual publication output was fitted using a lifecycle model in R to describe the historical publication trajectory of melioidosis research. The coefficient of determination (R2) was used to assess the goodness of fit. The fitted curve was interpreted as a descriptive representation of publication dynamics rather than as a precise forecast of future publication output.

Institutional collaboration networks were generated using CiteSpace version 6.3.1 [14]. For the institution-level network, CiteSpace successfully parsed records from 1995 to 2025; therefore, this effective time span was used for visualization. One-year time slices were applied, and the g-index with k = 1 was used for node selection. Institution names were manually checked and standardized when clear spelling or naming variants referred to the same institution, whereas closely related but separately indexed entities were retained as distinct institutions.

Overall trends in publication output and citation impact

MeanTCperYear is an indicator used to assess citation impact and is calculated by dividing the total citations (TC) of a publication by the number of years since its publication. As shown in Fig. 1B, the MeanTCperYear of melioidosis research was relatively low in the early stage of the study period, at 0.79 in 1990. It then showed an overall upward trend, rising to 2.19 in 2000 and 3.20 in 2010, suggesting that the average annual citation impact of publications increased during this period. The MeanTCperYear reached its highest value in 2018 (4.00). Thereafter, it showed a fluctuating downward trend, declining to 2.21 in 2020, 2.22 in 2023, and 1.02 in 2025. However, the decline in MeanTCperYear in recent years should be interpreted with caution, as newly published papers have had a shorter citation window.

Figure 1B also shows that the annual number of Article and Review publications on melioidosis increased markedly from 1990 to 2025. Annual output rose from 12 publications in 1990 to 208 in 2015, representing an approximately 17.3-fold increase. After 2015, annual publication output remained at a relatively high level, with 203 Article and Review records published in 2025. These findings indicate sustained expansion of formal research output in this field over the past three decades. The lifecycle fitting results shown in Fig. 1C indicated that the annual publication trajectory entered a phase of rapid expansion after 2000 and reached a model-estimated peak around 2019.7, with a good descriptive fit to the observed data (R2 = 0.973). However, the fitted curve should be interpreted as a descriptive representation of historical publication dynamics rather than a causal explanation or precise forecast of future output. Taken together, the observed data and fitting results suggest that melioidosis research has shifted from rapid growth toward a relatively stable bibliometric phase, but this should not be equated with full scientific, clinical, or policy maturity of the field.

National publication output and collaboration

Countries were ranked according to country-level scientific production frequency, and the 10 most productive countries or regions were identified and highlighted (Fig. 2A). The United States ranked first, with 2738 country-affiliation occurrences, followed by Thailand (2,236), Australia (1730), the United Kingdom (1359), China (922), India (783), Malaysia (743), Germany (435), Singapore (385), and the Netherlands (318). These results indicate that melioidosis research is concentrated in both classical endemic regions, particularly Thailand, Australia, Malaysia, Singapore, India, and China, and high-income countries with strong research capacity, such as the United States and the United Kingdom. Figure 2B shows the distribution of corresponding authors’ countries and the proportions of single-country publications (SCP) and multiple-country publications (MCP). The United States ranked first with 785 corresponding-author publications, followed by Thailand (510), Australia (443), the United Kingdom (328), India (281), China (265), and Malaysia (256). By contrast, India and China had relatively low MCP proportions, at 9.6% and 9.4%, respectively, suggesting that publications led by these countries were more often conducted within single-country author networks.

Visually prominent collaboration hubs included the United States, the United Kingdom, Australia, and several Asian countries, especially Thailand, India, China, Malaysia, and Singapore. The network indicates that melioidosis research is supported by extensive cross-regional collaboration, particularly between endemic Asia–Pacific regions and high-income countries with strong research infrastructure.

The reference co-citation network in Fig. 4 revealed a clear intellectual structure of melioidosis research. Cheng AC (2005) appeared as the largest and visually central node, with dense co-citation links to several foundational publications, including White, Wiersinga, Currie, and Limmathurotsakul [2, 9, 17, 19, 31]. The network broadly formed three major co-citation clusters. The blue cluster, represented by White, Dance, Chaowagul and Leelarasamee, mainly reflected early clinical recognition, endemic-region experience, and epidemiological understanding of melioidosis [17, 21–23]. The green cluster, including Holden, Stevens, Jones Wiersinga, was mainly related to pathogen biology, genomic plasticity, virulence, and host–pathogen interaction [18, 20, 27, 28]. The red cluster, represented by Cheng, Wiersinga, Currie, Limmathurotsakul, and related modern epidemiological and clinical review literature, reflected classic reviews, global burden estimation, modern clinical epidemiology, and updated management frameworks [2, 9, 19, 31, 33–35]. Overall, the co-citation network suggests that the knowledge base of melioidosis research has evolved from early clinical and epidemiological observations in endemic regions toward pathogen genomics, global burden assessment, and contemporary clinical management.

Keyword network analysis of melioidosis research

Keyword co-occurrence network and major thematic clusters

Keyword co-occurrence analysis showed that melioidosis research has developed a clear multi-thematic structure (Fig. 5, Table 5). In the co-occurrence network, keywords such as “epidemiology,” “identification,” “infection,” “virulence,” “resistance,” and “diagnosis” appeared as prominent nodes with large sizes and extensive connections, indicating that they constitute major conceptual components of the field. High-frequency keywords in each thematic cluster

Based on the network structure and the characteristics of clustered keywords, the high-frequency keywords were broadly categorized into five thematic clusters (Table 5). Cluster 1, centered on “epidemiology,” “diagnosis,” “australia,” “thailand,” “ceftazidime,” “susceptibility,” “sepsis,” “risk-factors,” and “pneumonia,” mainly reflected epidemiology, diagnosis, clinical manifestations, and treatment. Cluster 2, represented by “virulence,” “resistance,” “escherichia-coli,” “pseudomonas-aeruginosa,” “antibiotic-resistance,” “gene-expression,” and “crystal-structure,” was mainly related to virulence mechanisms, antimicrobial resistance, and broader bacterial pathogenesis. Cluster 3, including “identification,” “burkholderia thailandensis,” “mallei,” “glanders,” “iii secretion system,” “burkholderia mallei,” “rapid identification,” “pcr,” and “sequence,” primarily reflected pathogen identification, typing, molecular detection, and studies of related Burkholderia species. Cluster 4, characterized by “infection,” “lipopolysaccharide,” “capsular polysaccharide,” “murine model,” “pathogenesis,” “mice,” “vaccine,” and “protection,” mainly involved immunopathogenesis, experimental models, and vaccine-related studies. Cluster 5, represented by “soil,” “water,” “outbreak,” “northern australia,” “diversity,” and “survival,” mainly reflected environmental sources, outbreak-related investigations, and ecological aspects of melioidosis.

Overall, the keyword co-occurrence network showed that melioidosis research has expanded into a multi-dimensional framework spanning epidemiology and clinical management, virulence and antimicrobial resistance, pathogen identification and typing, immunopathogenesis and vaccine-related research, and environmental exposure and outbreak investigation. These findings suggest that the field has moved beyond early clinical and epidemiological description toward an integrated research structure linking pathogen characteristics, host responses, environmental reservoirs, molecular detection, and clinical outcomes.

Discussion

Overall development trend

The present study showed that melioidosis research has grown steadily from 1990 to 2025 and has entered a stage of bibliometric consolidation, suggesting that the field has evolved from a relatively limited regional infectious disease topic into an active interdisciplinary research area. However, this bibliometric consolidation should not be equated with full scientific, clinical, or public health maturity. Meanwhile, the research focus has expanded from early case descriptions and epidemiological observations to multiple dimensions, including clinical medicine, microbiology, immunology, molecular mechanisms, environmental exposure, and genomic epidemiology [1, 2, 5, 6, 9, 19]. The post-2019 fluctuation in annual publication output may also have been influenced by broader disruptions to global research activity during the COVID-19 pandemic, and therefore should not be interpreted simply as a decline in scientific interest in melioidosis.

Core contributors and collaboration landscape

The analyses of countries, institutions, and authors showed that the United States, Thailand, and Australia were the major contributors to melioidosis research; Mahidol University was the most active institution; and CURRIE BJ was the most representative prolific and highly influential author. These findings indicate that research in this field is characterized by marked regional concentration and centralization. This pattern is related not only to the long-term accumulation of case resources and research foundations in classical endemic regions such as Thailand and Australia, but also to the advantages of the United States in research infrastructure and international collaboration networks, indicating that melioidosis research is driven by both disease burden and scientific capacity [5, 6, 9, 17, 22, 25, 30–32].

Notably, the geographical distribution of research output should be interpreted with caution. Some melioidosis-endemic countries may appear less visible in bibliometric analyses because of limited research infrastructure, lower international indexing coverage, or publication in regional and local journals. In particular, the absence or low visibility of Vietnam in the country-level results should not be interpreted as evidence of low melioidosis relevance, but may reflect database indexing limitations, local publication practices, and differences in international research visibility. This may partly explain the underrepresentation or absence of some endemic settings in country-level analyses. Therefore, the apparent leadership of high-output, low-burden countries should be interpreted as a structural imbalance between disease burden and research visibility, rather than as evidence of greater local clinical or epidemiological relevance. From the perspective of clinical and public health research, high-quality evidence currently still comes mainly from a limited number of core regions and research centers. Future efforts should therefore strengthen multicenter, cross-regional, and standardized studies, particularly in underrepresented endemic regions, to improve the generalizability and comparability of clinical and epidemiological evidence on melioidosis [5, 6, 30–34].

Thematic evolution and research shift

Keyword co-occurrence, clustering, and trend topic analyses showed that melioidosis research has developed several core directions, including epidemiology and clinical management, virulence and antimicrobial resistance, pathogen identification and typing, immunopathogenesis and vaccine-related studies, and environmental sources and outbreak-related investigations. These findings indicate that the field has progressed from early disease recognition and clinical description toward a multilayered research structure linking clinical questions, pathogen biology, host responses, environmental reservoirs, molecular detection, and transmission-related investigation [9, 17, 19, 20].

In terms of temporal evolution, research hotspots have shifted from early infection recognition, diagnosis, serological testing, and basic epidemiology toward virulence factors, secretion systems, antimicrobial resistance, biofilms, host–pathogen interactions, whole-genome sequencing, outbreak investigation, environmental exposure, and complex clinical phenotypes [18, 20, 27–30, 35, 36]. This suggests that melioidosis research is moving from “disease discovery and description” toward a stage of “explaining disease heterogeneity, tracing environmental and transmission-related factors, and optimizing clinical management.” This shift also indicates that future research will increasingly focus on severe disease risk, differences in clinical outcomes, environmental drivers, treatment failure, relapse, and mortality-related predictors [41, 42].

Implications for future clinical practice and research

The clinical significance of the hotspot evolution identified in this study lies in the fact that melioidosis research has shifted from an early focus on disease recognition and basic description toward a more outcome-oriented and mechanism-informed stage. Future clinical research on melioidosis should place greater emphasis on the early identification of high-risk and atypical cases, stratified management of patients with different sites of infection and degrees of severity, and the prediction of treatment failure, relapse, and adverse outcomes [41, 42].

The emergence of molecular epidemiology topics such as whole-genome sequencing suggests that an important future direction will be to integrate pathogen genomic characteristics with transmission patterns, environmental sources, host status, and clinical phenotypes. Such integration may help translate molecular and environmental information into outbreak tracing, exposure assessment, risk stratification, and clinical management [6, 7, 40]. In parallel, the increasing visibility of environmental and outbreak-related topics highlights the need to link clinical surveillance with environmental sampling, climate-related factors, and regional epidemiological monitoring. The appearance of vaccine-related topics in recent trend topics also indicates that melioidosis research is gradually extending from diagnosis and treatment toward prevention. Future work should therefore pay greater attention to the identification of high-risk populations, candidate antigen screening, and the establishment of protective immune evaluation systems [1, 9, 20].

From a policy perspective, melioidosis should be considered both a regionally important neglected tropical disease in endemic tropical areas and an emerging global health concern in the context of climate change, environmental disturbance, travel-associated infections, and expanding recognition outside traditional endemic regions [3, 4, 6, 40]. Key remaining gaps include insufficient evidence from underrepresented endemic regions, limited integration of clinical, environmental, and genomic surveillance data, incomplete understanding of relapse and treatment failure mechanisms, and the lack of translational vaccine evidence.

Strengths and limitations of this study

Based on publications from 1990 to 2025, this study systematically examined melioidosis research from multiple perspectives, including publication trends, collaboration networks, author contributions, knowledge base, keyword clusters, and thematic evolution. This macro-level perspective provides a comprehensive overview of the developmental trajectory and research frontiers of melioidosis research, which is difficult to achieve through traditional narrative reviews.

Nevertheless, this study has several limitations. First, the main bibliometric analyses were based on records indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection. WoSCC was selected because it provides standardized full-record and cited-reference metadata suitable for citation-based analysis, co-citation analysis, and science mapping. Although a supplementary Scopus coverage check was performed (S3 Table), Scopus records were not merged with the WoSCC dataset to avoid inconsistencies in citation counts, document types, author affiliations, keyword fields, and cited-reference formats across databases. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with consideration of this database-coverage context.

Second, relevant studies published in regional or local journals, especially from melioidosis-endemic countries, may be underrepresented if they are not indexed in WoSCC. This may partly explain the relatively low visibility or absence of some endemic countries in the country-level analyses. Third, bibliometric results are influenced by the search strategy, keyword settings, document-type restrictions, and database indexing quality. Although we used a broad search strategy and standardized keywords using a thesaurus file (S1 Table), the omission of some relevant literature remains unavoidable. Finally, residual inconsistencies in author names, institution names, and cited references may have affected productivity, collaboration, and co-citation analyses. Keyword co-occurrence and trend topic analyses can reveal broad thematic shifts, but they cannot replace in-depth systematic reviews or meta-analyses focused on specific clinical, epidemiological, or mechanistic questions.

Conclusion

Based on the above findings, melioidosis research has continued to grow over the past three decades and has developed a relatively clear knowledge base and research network. However, current studies remain mainly focused on epidemiological description, diagnostic recognition, and traditional clinical management, while research on the mechanisms underlying disease heterogeneity and differences in clinical outcomes remains insufficient. With the continuing advancement of whole-genome sequencing, outbreak investigation, and studies on complex clinical phenotypes, future melioidosis research should increasingly move from “discovering and describing the disease” toward “explaining disease heterogeneity and optimizing prevention and control strategies.” Future efforts should strengthen multicenter, cross-regional, and interdisciplinary collaboration, integrating multidimensional information on the pathogen, host, environment, and clinical features, in order to improve early recognition, risk prediction, and precision prevention and control of melioidosis.
