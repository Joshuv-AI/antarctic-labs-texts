In-person or tele-orthopaedic care? A mixed-methods study exploring parents’ perspectives on the advantages and challenges of both approaches care for children with cerebral palsy
Authors: Reza Moulaei, Leila Oryadi Zanjani, Mohammad Hossein Nabian, Khadijeh Moulaei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren with cerebral palsy (CP) often require ongoing orthopaedic care to address musculoskeletal issues and improve mobility. However, access to specialized services can be challenging due to geographic barriers, patient immobility, transportation difficulties, and long wait times. These challenges place a considerable burden on families, particularly on parents who serve as primary caregivers. This study compared parental perspectives on in-person versus tele-orthopaedic care using a quasi-experimental design, involving interviews with 50 parents to explore the advantages and challenges of both methods.MethodsThis quasi-experimental study employed a mixed-methods approach. Fifty parents of children with cerebral palsy were randomly assigned to intervention (tele-orthopaedics care) or control (in-person care) groups for three months. The control group received in-person orthopaedic services, while the intervention group received tele-orthopaedics care during this period. Quantitative data were collected through structured questionnaires and analyzed using SPSS version 23. In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with all 50 parents (25 from each group), and qualitative data were analyzed using content analysis in MAXQDA version 18 to extract key themes and sub-themes.ResultsBased on the questionnaire results, the main advantages of in-person visits were “Reduction of medical errors” (4.5 ± 0.6), “More accurate diagnosis” (4.4 ± 0.5), and “Improved and easier interaction between patient and provider” (4.3 ± 0.6). In contrast, the key advantages s of tele-orthopaedicss were “Cost savings” (4.5 ± 0.6) and “Eliminating geographical barriers” (4.4 ± 0.6). Interview findings revealed that the most frequently cited advantages s of in-person visits were “Comprehensive physical examination” (n = 24), “More accurate and effective treatment” (n = 24), and “Direct and more comfortable interaction with the physician” (n = 24). Key challenges included “Difficulty in transferring and moving the child” (n = 24), “Wasting parents’ time at the hospital” (n = 22), and “High transportation costs to healthcare centers” (n = 20). Moreover, among the 20 identified advantages, the most frequently mentioned were “Reduced travel costs” (n = 14), “Elimination of geographical distances” (n = 9), and “Reduced sense of isolation and improved communication with the care team” (n = 8). The most common challenges included “Lack of access to high-speed internet” (n = 20), “Limited internet access in remote areas” (n = 19), “Lack of digital literacy in using tele-orthopaedics platforms” (n = 18), and “Lack of physical examination” (n = 18).ConclusionIn-person visits and tele-orthopaedicss each have unique advantages s and limitations, and can complement each other in the healthcare system. While in-person care is vital for complex cases, tele-orthopaedicss helps reduce costs and improve access in remote areas. A hybrid model, supported by better internet infrastructure and digital literacy, can enhance orthopaedic care quality.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Keywords: Tele-orthopaedics, Telemedicine, In-person visits, Challenges, Advantages s, Cerebral palsy

Introduction

Musculoskeletal diseases and conditions are highly prevalent and can affect muscles, bones, joints, and tissues such as tendons and ligaments. According to the World Health Organization (WHO), musculoskeletal disorders including joint diseases, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, osteoporosis, spinal disorders, back pain, and major injuries are among 150 conditions impacting millions of people globally [1]. These conditions are not limited to a specific life stage and may occur from childhood to older age. Musculoskeletal issues can also arise suddenly and be short-term (e.g., fractures, sprains, and strains, often accompanied by pain and functional limitations) or long-term, such as chronic low back pain and osteoarthritis [2]. Additionally, WHO has reported that musculoskeletal conditions lead to high healthcare costs and significant work loss. These disabling and painful conditions reduce individuals’ ability to perform daily activities and enjoy life, creating economic burdens for societies and countries. The number of affected individuals is expected to rise significantly in the coming decades [1]. Among these, cerebral palsy-related musculoskeletal impairments have gained global attention due to their increasing prevalence. cerebral palsy (CP) is a general term for movement and posture disorders caused by non-progressive disturbances in the developing fetal or infant brain. Since it stems from diverse risk factors and causes, no single strategy can entirely prevent cerebral palsy. Hence, potential interventions like telemedicine must be considered for the prevention and treatment of these patients. Moreover, many of these individuals face challenges in receiving orthopaedic care due to long distances to healthcare centers, financial difficulties, lack of continuous access to orthopaedic specialists, and the need to travel for care [3].

Given the widespread prevalence of musculoskeletal disorders especially in cerebral palsy patients and the lack of universal orthopaedic service availability, technologies like tele-orthopaedics can be highly beneficial [1]. Tele-orthopaedics refers to the delivery of specialized orthopaedic care through information and communication technologies while the patient and orthopaedic specialist are geographically separated. This approach can significantly reduce travel time, lessen physicians’ workloads, and improve patient satisfaction with postoperative care compared to traditional methods. Furthermore, due to its more efficient consultation methods, tele-orthopaedics may also lower consultation costs from a societal perspective [4]. Despite these advantages, many people still prefer to receive healthcare services through in-person visits for various reasons [5–9]. The need for in-person visits may vary depending on patients’ demographics and comorbidities. For example, Jabbari et al. [10] reported that patients with chronic conditions like hypertension and coronary artery disease, older adults, and non-Hispanic Black patients are less likely to utilize telehealth services. Other contributing factors include distrust in technology for healthcare, low health or digital literacy [11, 12], concerns about poor clinical outcomes [6], misdiagnosis, ethical and legal concerns, patient privacy, confidentiality, and the risk of negligence or malpractice in remote care [13]. Additionally, unequal access to technology significantly influences the choice between tele-orthopaedics and in-person visits [10]. For instance, studies have shown that Black and Hispanic patients are less likely than non-Hispanic Whites to own smartphones or have access to high-speed internet [14]. A survey on telehealth usage within the Kaiser Permanente system also found that older patients (over 75 years), Black, Latino, and Filipino individuals were less likely to own digital devices or use the internet and electronic tools [8].

To the best of our knowledge, the researchers of the present study found that although numerous studies have explored tele-orthopaedics [1, 15, 16], no research has yet compared in-person and tele-orthopaedic treatment approaches specifically for musculoskeletal disorders in cerebral palsy patients. Therefore, the aim of this study is to compare in-person care and tele-orthopaedics for treating cerebral palsy patients from the perspective of their parents.

Study design and methodology

This research employed a mixed-methods approach using a quasi-experimental design, encompassing both quantitative and qualitative components. Participants were randomly assigned to two groups: the intervention group (receiving tele-orthopaedic services) and the control group (receiving in-person orthopaedic services). For the qualitative phase, an interpretive phenomenological analysis (IPA) was conducted to explore the experiences of parents of children with cerebral palsy and orthopaedic specialists. Quantitative data were collected using structured questionnaires and analyzed using SPSS version 23. Qualitative data were gathered through semi-structured interviews and analyzed using MAXQDA version 18, employing a qualitative content analysis approach.

Study population and sampling

The study population comprised all parents of children diagnosed with cerebral palsy who attended the orthopaedic department of Shariati Hospital, affiliated with Tehran University of Medical Sciences. To recruit participants, invitations containing a brief description of the study were distributed via WhatsApp and Telegram to 70 potential participants. Based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 50 eligible parents were selected to participate in the study.

Participants were randomly assigned into two equal groups: 25 individuals in the in-person orthopaedic care group and 25 individuals in the tele-orthopaedic care group. Randomization was conducted using a simple random sampling method to ensure an unbiased allocation process. Specifically, each eligible participant was assigned a unique identification number, and a computerized random number generator (Microsoft Excel) was used to randomly allocate individuals to the respective study groups. This approach aimed to reduce selection bias and enhance the internal validity of the study.

Intervention Group: Participants in the intervention group received orthopaedic care remotely via social media platforms such as WhatsApp and skype. The tele-orthopaedic services included an initial clinical assessment, specialized consultations, prescription of therapeutic exercises, and follow-up on the treatment process. These services were delivered over a period of three months.

Data collection

Data for this study were collected using a mixed-methods approach, incorporating both quantitative and qualitative components. In the quantitative phase, a structured questionnaire previously developed and psychometrically validated by Moulaei et al. [17], was employed. The validity and reliability of the instrument had been confirmed using Cronbach’s alpha and other psychometric indicators. The questionnaire consisted of four sections: the first included demographic information (4 items); the second addressed details such as disease type, duration of illness, and prior experience with telemedicine (4 items); and the third comprised 34 items focused on the perceived advantages of telemedicine compared to in-person care, and vice versa. These items were rated on a five-point Likert scale ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree.”

For parents whose children received in-person treatment, the questionnaires were distributed and collected in person. For those who had used tele-orthopaedic services, the questionnaire was designed in electronic format and sent via WhatsApp and Telegram.

In the qualitative phase, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 50 parents of children with cerebral palsy 25 from the in-person care group and 25 from the tele-orthopaedic group to explore their experiences, challenges, and perceived advantages s of each treatment modality. The interviews were conducted either in-person or virtually via WhatsApp and Skype. The qualitative data were analyzed using MAXQDA version 18, following a conventional content analysis approach.

Data analysis

Quantitative data were analyzed using SPSS version 23. Descriptive statistical methods, including frequencies and percentages, were employed to summarize the demographic characteristics. To examine significant differences between demographic variables and the two intervention groups (tele-orthopaedics vs. in-person visits), the Chi-square test was utilized.

For qualitative data analysis, all interviews with parents of children with cerebral palsy were first transcribed verbatim by the research team. The transcripts were then imported into MAXQDA version 20 for systematic analysis. Each response was carefully reviewed to identify key semantic units through conventional content analysis. Two researchers independently coded all transcripts to extract these semantic units. Subsequently, the codes were reviewed and grouped into subcategories and main categories based on semantic similarity. To enhance the rigor and validity of the qualitative analysis, the codes, categories, and original transcripts were reviewed by a medical informatics expert experienced in qualitative research and familiar with the design and implementation of telemedicine and tele-orthopaedic systems.

Ethical considerations

For this study included obtaining approval from the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences (approval code: IR.TUMS.SHARIATI.REC.1402.031). All patient data were used anonymously, ensuring that no identifying information was disclosed. Additionally, written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the study to guarantee voluntary participation. All methods were performed in accordance with the guidelines and regulations of the Ethics Committee of Tehran University of Medical Sciences.

Advantages of in-person visits

More accurate physical examination

Participants believed that in-person visits allowed for more thorough and accurate physical assessments. Physicians could directly observe and palpate the patient and utilize advanced diagnostic tools, reducing the likelihood of clinical errors and enhancing decision-making accuracy.

During in-person appointments, the physician can better assess the patient’s condition through touch and direct observation. (Participant 13)

I feel the doctor makes a more accurate diagnosis during in-person visits, with less chance of error. (Participant 9)

More accurate diagnosis

One of the most significant advantages s of in-person consultations was the improvement in diagnostic accuracy. In-person settings enabled physicians to obtain more detailed medical histories, observe symptoms directly, and perform physical examinations, leading to fewer misdiagnoses and faster diagnostic processes.

When I visit the doctor in person, I can explain my child’s symptoms more clearly, and the doctor understands better. (Participant 11)

Some conditions can only be identified through direct observation by the physician, which is possible only with in-person visits. (Participant 7)

Improved treatment quality

In-person consultations facilitated more precise and effective treatment. Physicians could choose the most appropriate therapeutic strategies, provide comprehensive information, and establish greater trust with patients, positively influencing treatment adherence.

During in-person visits, the doctor can better monitor the treatment process, and I feel more reassured. (Participant 18)

Seeing the doctor face-to-face makes me take the treatment more seriously and follow the instructions more carefully. (Participant 20)

More effective communication with physicians

Parents emphasized that in-person visits allowed for closer and more effective communication with doctors. They felt their concerns were more thoroughly heard during face-to-face interactions.

I feel better when I talk to the doctor in person. I can express my concerns more clearly, and the doctor listens more attentively. (Participant 5)

During in-person sessions, the doctor spends more time with us, and we can ask all our questions. (Participant 19)

Face-to-face interaction makes me feel that the doctor truly cares about my child’s condition. (Participant 24)

We can express our emotions more effectively during in-person visits, which is harder online. (Participant 13)

In-person visits allow the doctor to better understand our concerns. (Participant 1)

Greater accuracy in clinical evaluation

In-person assessments provided physicians with the opportunity to conduct hands-on evaluations, which parents found more effective than virtual assessments.

The doctor needs to assess my child’s limb movements, which can only be done in person. (Participant 17)

Sometimes, the doctor needs to feel the muscle spasticity, which is not possible in an online visit. (Participant 18)

With in-person examination, the doctor can better judge whether my child is improving. (Participant 4)

Access to diagnostic tests and support services

In-person visits enabled access to laboratories, imaging services, and rehabilitation centers advantages s not feasible through remote care.

After an in-person exam, the doctor may order lab tests or imaging, which can only be done at the clinic. (Participant 9)

Hospitals provide comprehensive services like labs and rehab centers, which cannot be accessed remotely. (Participant 12)

Increased parental confidence and sense of security

Parents reported that in-person visits alleviated anxiety and boosted their confidence in the physician’s diagnosis and treatment plan.

When the doctor examines my child in person, I feel reassured that nothing important is being missed. (Participant 16)

I feel safer during in-person consultations, knowing the doctor is examining my child thoroughly. (Participant 13)

Opportunity for hands-on training and education

In-person interactions provided parents the chance to receive practical training in caregiving techniques and physical therapy exercises.

During in-person visits, the doctor shows me how to perform specific therapies for my daughter’s legs. (Participant 16)

We learned how to do certain physical therapy exercises only during in-person sessions, not online. (Participant 12)

High costs of in-person visits

Parents of children with cerebral palsy reported that the costs associated with transportation, medical treatment, and other related expenses were among the main problems of in-person visits to healthcare centers.

Every time we go to the hospital, the taxi and transportation costs are very high, especially since we live in another city. (Participant 10)

Continuous therapy sessions require a lot of expenses, which we sometimes cannot afford. (Participant 12)

The accommodation costs in the city are extremely burdensome for us who come from distant areas. (Participant 12)

Difficulty accessing specialized physicians

Parents stated that getting an appointment with a specialist is very difficult and sometimes takes months before they can take their child for an in-person visit.

There are no pediatric orthopaedic specialists in our city, and we have to travel to other cities. (Participant 19)

Sometimes, we have to wait for hours just for a five-minute visit. (Participant 14)

Transportation and mobility challenges

One of the main challenges reported by parents was the difficulty in transporting children with mobility limitations to healthcare centers.

Moving my son, who has severe cerebral palsy, is extremely difficult. Every time we go to the hospital, we need the help of several people. (Participant 11)

Public transportation is not suitable for children with disabilities, and we are forced to take expensive taxis. (Participant 9)

For every therapy session, we must bring a wheelchair and a companion, which is very exhausting. (Participant 24)

Time-consuming nature of in-person visits

In-person visits require a great deal of time, including waiting at the doctor’s office, undergoing tests, and attending therapy sessions.

Sometimes we have to wait for hours in the hospital for our turn, which is very difficult for my child. (Participant 18)

We leave early in the morning and return home at night, just for a short visit. (Participant 22)

The time spent commuting and waiting could have been used to provide better care for our child. (Participant 4)

Stress and fatigue in parents and patients

Parents stated that the process of in-person visits is stressful and exhausting for both themselves and their children.

Every time we go for an in-person visit, we’re anxious about whether the doctor will spend enough time with us. (Participant 8)

My child cannot sit still for long, which makes the entire day of visits very difficult for us. (Participant 21)

After a long day at the hospital, both my child and I are too exhausted to do anything else. (Participant 6)

Advantages of tele-orthopaedics for parents of children with cerebral palsy

A total of 20 advantages of tele-orthopaedics for parents of children with cerebral palsy were identified. The most frequently mentioned advantages included:” Reduced travel costs” (n = 14), “Elimination of geographical distances” (n = 9), and “Reduced sense of isolation and improved communication with the care team” (n = 8).

Reduced need for in-person visits

Using tele-orthopaedics decreases the need for parents to physically attend healthcare centers. This not only saves travel costs but also reduces the stress associated with transporting children with cerebral palsy.

Each time we need to visit the medical center, it takes several hours. But now the doctor can evaluate my child’s condition remotely. (Participant 9)

Hospital visits were very difficult for us, but now we can get consultations without stress. (Participant 23)

Improved quality of medical services

Remote access to specialists increases diagnostic accuracy and reduces medical errors. Doctors can make better decisions using the child’s stored medical history.

The doctor can review foot images before the visit, leading to faster and more accurate diagnoses. (Participant 22)

Sometimes doctors forgot my child’s past information, but now everything is recorded and accessible. (Participant 7)

Treatment management and follow-up

Parents can better manage and monitor their child’s treatment without needing frequent visits.

I can ask about appropriate corrective exercises and get answers immediately. (Participant 11)

Now we don’t have to go to the hospital for every small change. The doctor guides us remotely. (Participant 17)

Reduced treatment costs

Fewer in-person visits lead to savings in transportation and other associated expenses.

We used to spend a lot on travel to see the doctor, but now we do most things from home. (Participant 24)

Easier access to specialists

Tele-orthopaedics enables access to more specialists, avoiding limitations of local healthcare facilities.

Previously, we could only see one doctor, but now we consult with various specialists.

If the main doctor is unavailable, we can consult another. (Participant 19)

Enhanced communication and interaction

Parents can communicate more freely with doctors, sharing concerns more effectively.

The doctor answers our questions more easily now, without the rush of in-person visits. (Participant 21)

I feel like my child’s doctor spends more time explaining things. (Participant 13)

Better management of medical information

Storing information electronically facilitates easier and more accurate access to medical history.

I no longer worry about losing my child’s medical records—they’re all stored in the system. (Participant 14)

The doctor quickly reviews past data, which leads to better treatment. (Participant 12)

Increased interaction and psychological support

Tele-orthopaedics supports not only physical care but also emotional well-being. Direct and ongoing contact with doctors provides a sense of support and reassurance.

Whenever my child needs treatment, I can talk to the doctor and feel we’re not alone. (Participant 16).

Doctors are always supportive and address our concerns remotely. (Participant 7)

Easier access to multiple opinions

Parents can consult multiple specialists, which improves decision-making, especially in complex conditions like cerebral palsy.

More than one doctor can assess my child’s condition, which leads to better care. (Participant 10)

Quick consultations with different doctors make our decisions easier. (Participant 9)

Easier management of treatment plans

Tele-orthopaedics enables parents to adjust and follow treatment plans promptly.

Whenever my child shows new symptoms, we can quickly contact the doctor. (Participant 21)

The doctor can regularly review and adjust my child’s treatment. (Participant 25)

Improved access to educational resources

Parents can access online resources to guide care practices at home.

Doctors send videos that help me better perform daily therapy tasks. (Participant 6)

I use the educational materials sent by the doctor—they’re very helpful. (Participant 4)

Greater access to emergency services

Tele-orthopaedics provides timely medical advice in emergencies, especially when accessing a clinic is difficult.

One night, my child had severe pain, and the doctor was able to help remotely. (Participant 19)

In emergencies, we can quickly talk to a doctor and take necessary action. (Participant 1)

Support for making treatment decisions

Parents can receive advice on innovative therapies and make more informed decisions.

The doctor introduced us to new treatments that could help our child. (Participant 2)

With online consultations, I chose the best treatment for my child. (Participant 1)

Challenges of tele-orthopaedics for parents of children with cerebral palsy in accessing healthcare centers

Technological challenges

Tele-orthopaedics presents numerous technological difficulties for parents of children with cerebral palsy. One of the biggest challenges is internet accessibility. In some regions, parents do not have access to high-speed internet, which can disrupt the online treatment process. Additionally, technical issues such as connection failures or the inability to use online treatment platforms are common. In some cases, parents struggle with new technologies, preventing them from effectively utilizing telemedicine services.

Sometimes the internet is so weak that we can’t easily connect with the doctor online. (Participant 9)

The platforms we use are too complicated, and we can’t use them properly. (Participant 13)

Connection loss during an online treatment session prevents us from continuing the treatment. (Participant 11)

There’s no proper training for using online treatment technologies, and we have to learn everything by trial and error. (Participant 18)

Socio-cultural challenges

Traditional attitudes and a lack of trust in online treatment are among the key challenges. Many parents, due to cultural and social beliefs, are skeptical about tele-orthopaedics. They often feel that online treatment cannot replace in-person care. Concerns about insufficient evaluation and the inability of online doctors to diagnose complex conditions contribute to their reluctance to trust this approach.

We never trusted online treatment and prefer to attend in person. (Participant 23)

How can an online doctor diagnose our child’s condition as accurately as one who sees them in person? (Participant 22)

Sometimes other parents avoid online treatment because of their traditions and beliefs. (Participant 15)

The fact that the online doctor cannot physically examine the child is a big issue for many of us. (Participant 11)

Medical challenges

A major challenge in telemedicine is the inability to conduct a thorough physical examination. Online physicians cannot examine the patient directly, which may lead to diagnostic difficulties or problems with treatment follow-up. Additionally, the need for specific medical equipment for remote care often cannot be met by parents, limiting access to treatment.

The online doctor cannot examine the child, and this is a major problem for us. (Participant 14)

We can’t afford the specific medical equipment required for telemedicine. (Participant 9)

Telemedicine is not very effective for conditions that require physical examination. (Participant 3)

We don’t have access to the special equipment online doctors require. (Participant 25)

Security and legal challenges

Tele-orthopaedic care may come with security and legal concerns. Many parents are worried about the privacy of their and their child’s medical information. Fears about data breaches and unauthorized access to medical records deter them from using these services. Moreover, in some countries, the lack of clear legal frameworks for telemedicine poses challenges for both parents and healthcare providers—especially where telehealth lacks proper legal infrastructure.

We worry about our child’s medical information being accessed by unauthorized individuals. (Participant 11)

Online treatment doesn’t seem as safe as in-person care. (Participant 25)

When online healthcare systems are hacked, our information is at risk. (Participant 9)

Economic challenges

One of the main issues with telemedicine is its high cost. Parents must pay for tele-orthopaedic services, including platform subscriptions and necessary medical devices. Often, these costs are too high to afford. Additionally, effective use of telemedicine requires high-speed internet, which adds further expenses. These financial burdens can discourage parents from using telemedicine services.

The cost of online treatment is very high for us, and we can’t always afford it. (Participant 15)

We need specific devices for online care, and they are very expensive. (Participant 9)

The cost of remote treatment is more than we can pay. (Participant 5)

High-speed internet costs also affect the overall expense and are a problem for us. (Participant 8)

Educational and awareness challenges

Lack of training for using telemedicine services is another significant issue. Many parents have no initial training in how to use online platforms, leading to confusion and underuse of these services. Moreover, a lack of awareness about the benefits and procedures of telemedicine may hinder its acceptance among parents.

We received no training on how to use these online services and had to figure it out on our own. (Participant 11)

We had no guidance from doctors on how to use the telemedicine platform. (Participant 12)

When we encountered problems, there was no one to guide us properly. (Participant 3)

We didn’t know at all how to use these services for treatment. (Participant 2)

Management and coordination challenges

Coordination problems between doctors and specialists are another challenge parents may face. In telemedicine, coordination between healthcare providers in different locations may be lacking, leading to treatment errors or incomplete information sharing. Additionally, scheduling suitable appointment times can be difficult for parents, especially when time zone differences are involved.

Sometimes communication between different doctors is poor, and our child might receive incorrect treatment. (Participant 9)

Communication between online doctors is not effective, and some important information is lost. (Participant 9)

Online doctors often don’t have complete access to our child’s medical records. (Participant 1)

Discussion

This study examined and compared parents’ views on in-person versus tele-orthopaedic care for children with cerebral palsy, focusing on their preferences, perceived benefits, and associated challenges. Based on the questionnaire results, the main advantages of in-person visits were “Reduction of medical errors,” “More accurate diagnosis,” and “Improved and easier interaction between patient and provider.” In contrast, the key advantages of tele-orthopaedics were “Cost savings” and “Eliminating geographical barriers.” Interview findings also highlighted that the most frequently cited benefits of in-person visits included “Comprehensive physical examination,” “More accurate and effective treatment,” and “Direct and more comfortable interaction with the physician.” Reported challenges of in-person visits involved “Difficulty in transferring and moving the child,” “Wasting parents’ time at the hospital,” and “High transportation costs to healthcare centers.” Furthermore, among the identified advantages of tele-orthopaedics, common benefits were “Reduced travel costs,” “Elimination of geographical distances,” and “Reduced sense of isolation and improved communication with the care team.” The most frequently reported challenges were “Lack of access to high-speed internet,” “Limited internet access in remote areas,” “Lack of digital literacy in using tele-orthopaedic platforms,” and “Lack of physical examination.” These findings are discussed below:

Advantages of in-person visits

According to the questionnaire results, the main advantages of in-person visits were “Reduction of medical errors,” “More accurate diagnosis,” and “Improved and easier interaction between patient and provider.” Interview data further highlighted “Comprehensive physical examination,” “More accurate and effective treatment,” and “Direct and more comfortable interaction with the physician.” In-person care provides opportunities for comprehensive physical assessments, which are critical for children with cerebral palsy (CP), who often require detailed neuromuscular evaluations. Direct interaction allows clinicians to assess spasticity, joint mobility, and motor function more accurately, contributing to better diagnostic precision and treatment planning [18]. Moreover, physical presence fosters better communication between the healthcare provider and the patient’s family, which is associated with improved trust and shared decision-making [18]. Studies have consistently shown that hands-on clinical evaluation significantly reduces diagnostic uncertainty and medical errors, particularly in complex pediatric conditions [19].

In-person visits allow for detailed physical examinations that are essential in managing CP, a condition characterized by motor dysfunction requiring nuanced neuromuscular evaluation. Some of study show that clinical tools such as the Hammersmith Infant Neurological Examination and General Movements Assessment rely on physical presence for accurate application, leading to earlier and more precise diagnoses. Early and accurate diagnosis is crucial for initiating timely interventions that improve long-term functional outcomes in CP patients [20]. Furthermore, in-person care enhances patient-provider communication by allowing real-time interaction and interpretation of non-verbal cues, which fosters trust and collaboration between families and clinicians. This improved interaction supports shared decision-making a critical component in pediatric chronic care management ensuring that treatment plans align with the values and preferences of the child’s family [21, 22]. Physical presence also facilitates more effective treatment adjustments based on direct observation of motor function and physical responses [18].

Moreover, multiple studies confirm that in-person clinical evaluations reduce the likelihood of medical errors compared to remote assessments, which may lack comprehensive physical data and subtle clinical signs [23]. Such accuracy is particularly vital in children with CP, whose complex presentations necessitate precise clinical judgment. In summary, while tele-orthopaedics offers convenience and accessibility, in-person visits remain indispensable for thorough assessments, diagnostic accuracy, effective communication, and minimizing medical errors in CP care. An integrated approach combining both modalities may best serve this population’s diverse needs.

Challenges of in-person visits

Interview participants commonly reported “Difficulty in transferring and moving the child,” “Wasting parents’ time at the hospital,” and “High transportation costs to healthcare centers.”

Children with CP often have mobility impairments, making transportation to healthcare facilities physically and logistically challenging. Families may require specialized vehicles, equipment, and significant time to prepare for each visit. These burdens are amplified in rural or underserved areas [19]. Moreover, the time spent in transportation and waiting areas can disrupt caregivers’ routines and employment, leading to increased caregiver stress and lower healthcare satisfaction [24]. Previous research shows that the indirect costs (e.g., time off work, transportation) of in-person visits can be substantial and may limit access to timely care for many families [17].

Mobility challenges in children with CP present substantial barriers to accessing in-person care. The physical effort and logistical planning needed to transport children who often require assistive devices or support personnel represent significant hurdles for families [25]. This is particularly problematic for those living in rural or resource-limited settings where transport options are scarce or unavailable [19]. These obstacles can lead to missed appointments, delayed care, and potentially poorer health outcomes. Additionally, caregiver burden is another critical aspect linked with in-person visits. The combination of travel time, hospital waiting times, and disruptions to daily schedules can contribute to stress and fatigue among parents or guardians. This strain has been associated with decreased satisfaction with healthcare services and can negatively impact the caregiver’s quality of life [26]. Time away from work or other responsibilities due to clinic visits also adds a financial and social burden, potentially worsening health disparities for lower-income families [17].

Economic costs are not limited to direct transportation expenses. Indirect costs such as lost income and additional childcare for siblings further compound the burden of accessing in-person care. These financial pressures can lead to delays or avoidance of necessary visits, negatively impacting the management and outcomes for children with CP. So, the physical, temporal, and financial challenges associated with in-person visits highlight the need for alternative or complementary models of care, especially for families facing geographic or socioeconomic barriers.

Advantages of tele-orthopaedic

In the questionnaire, tele-orthopaedic care was primarily valued for “Cost savings” and “Eliminating geographical barriers.” Interviews supported these benefits, identifying “Reduced travel costs,” “Elimination of geographical distances,” and “Reduced sense of isolation and improved communication with the care team.” Tele-orthopaedic services can reduce financial and logistical burdens for families, especially those living in remote or underserved regions. The elimination of travel time and cost enables more frequent and consistent follow-up, which is crucial for managing CP [27]. Additionally, virtual care platforms can enhance access to specialized care and improve continuity by allowing families to engage with providers without the need for long-distance travel. Telehealth has also been shown to reduce caregiver burden and foster a stronger sense of connection to care teams, despite the physical distance [28].

Some studies have indicated that tele-orthopaedic care effectively reduces barriers related to geography and cost, making specialist services more accessible to families in remote or underserved areas [29]. Research has found that the elimination of travel requirements not only decreases financial strain but also promotes more frequent and timely follow-up appointments, which are vital for optimal management of children with CP. Moreover, several studies have emphasized that telehealth platforms facilitate improved access to multidisciplinary care teams, which is often limited in rural settings [17, 30]. This enhanced accessibility supports better care coordination and individualized treatment planning.

Additionally, evidence suggests that tele-orthopaedic care can reduce caregiver burden by minimizing the need for transportation and lengthy clinic visits. This reduction in logistical challenges has been associated with decreased caregiver stress and improved satisfaction, as virtual visits allow for more flexible scheduling and less disruption to daily life [31]. Some research has also highlighted that regular virtual communication can lessen caregivers’ feelings of isolation and foster stronger connections with healthcare providers [32]. Taken together, these findings suggest that tele-orthopaedic care is a valuable adjunct to traditional in-person visits, especially for families confronting transportation, financial, or geographic barriers.

Importantly, tele-orthopaedic care demonstrated particular value during the COVID-19 pandemic, when access to in-person services was significantly restricted. During this period, telehealth enabled continuity of care, reduced the risk of infection transmission, and maintained regular monitoring of children with CP despite lockdowns and healthcare disruptions [33]. Furthermore, while in-person visits are generally associated with greater diagnostic accuracy particularly for neurological examination tele-orthopaedic care offers complementary advantages in terms of accessibility, cost-efficiency, and patient engagement [33, 34]. This highlights the potential of a hybrid care model, where telehealth and face-to-face visits are integrated to optimize both clinical accuracy and service accessibility. Taken together, these findings suggest that tele-orthopaedic care is a valuable adjunct to traditional in-person visits, especially for families confronting transportation, financial, or geographic barriers, and is most effective when used alongside in-person assessments as part of a balanced, hybrid care approach.

Challenges of tele-orthopaedic

The most frequently cited challenges in both the interviews and questionnaire included “Lack of access to high-speed internet,” “Limited internet access in remote areas,” “Lack of digital literacy in using tele-orthopaedic platforms,” and “Lack of physical examination.” While telehealth offers convenience, it is limited by the inability to perform physical examinations, which are vital for assessing musculoskeletal conditions. Without direct observation and hands-on assessment, subtle clinical signs may be missed, leading to diagnostic inaccuracies [28].

Moreover, digital inequality poses a significant barrier. Many families lack access to reliable internet or the necessary technical skills to effectively use telemedicine platforms, especially in low-resource settings [20]. These limitations can result in poor engagement, reduced satisfaction, and disparities in healthcare access. Some studies [35, 36] have highlighted that despite the convenience of telehealth, the absence of physical examination remains a critical limitation, particularly in musculoskeletal and orthopaedic assessments. The inability to perform hands-on evaluations can result in missed subtle clinical signs, potentially compromising diagnostic accuracy and treatment efficacy. This challenge is especially significant for children with cerebral palsy, whose complex neuromuscular presentations often require detailed physical assessments. Furthermore, research has found that digital inequality is a substantial barrier to effective tele-orthopaedic care. Many families, particularly those in rural or low-resource settings, lack consistent access to high-speed internet and the digital literacy necessary to navigate telemedicine platforms successfully [37, 38]. This digital divide can lead to lower engagement rates, reduced satisfaction with care, and exacerbation of existing healthcare disparities. Several studies emphasize that addressing these technological and educational gaps is essential for ensuring equitable telehealth access and maximizing its benefits [17, 39]. Without targeted interventions, tele-orthopaedic services may inadvertently exclude vulnerable populations, undermining the goal of accessible and patient-centered care.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size of 50 parents may limit the generalizability of the findings to the broader population of children with cerebral palsy. Future studies should aim to include larger and more diverse samples to enhance external validity. Second, the study duration of three months might not capture long-term outcomes and sustainability of both tele-orthopaedic and in-person care approaches. Longer follow-up periods are recommended to assess lasting effects and patient adherence over time. Additionally, data collection relied on self-reported questionnaires and interviews, which may be subject to response and recall biases. Incorporating objective clinical measures and triangulating data sources could improve data accuracy. Finally, since the study was conducted in a specific healthcare setting, the results may not be applicable to other contexts or regions with different resources or infrastructures. Conducting multi-center studies across diverse settings would help validate and adapt findings to various healthcare environments.

Conclusion

The present study explored and compared parents’ perspectives on in-person and tele-orthopaedic for children with cerebral palsy, aiming to identify their preferences, perceived advantages, and challenges. This study compared parents’ perspectives on in-person and tele-orthopaedic for children with cerebral palsy, highlighting their distinct advantages and challenges. In-person visits are crucial for complex cases due to thorough physical exams, fewer medical errors, and direct patient-provider interaction. Tele-orthopaedic care offers cost savings, eliminates geographical barriers, and saves time, benefiting those with limited mobility or in remote areas. A hybrid model combining both approaches is recommended to optimize care. Improving internet access and digital literacy can enhance tele-orthopaedics’ effectiveness. Despite its benefits, tele-orthopaedic care faces challenges such as limited physical exams, technical issues, and reduced patient-provider rapport. Integrating both methods can promote equitable, high-quality orthopaedic care.

This study may serve as a valuable resource for health policymakers and planners in designing effective strategies to enhance tele-orthopaedic services, especially for chronic conditions like cerebral palsy. The results highlight the need to improve digital infrastructure, enhance digital health literacy among parents and healthcare providers, and develop standardized protocols for remote patient assessment and follow-up. Additionally, offering supplementary services such as educational videos and online consultations could help compensate for the lack of physical examination and improve the overall quality of care.
