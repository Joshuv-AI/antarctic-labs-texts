Prevalence, patterns, and factors associated with early childhood cariogenic diet exposure in Kenya
Authors: Judy Gichuki, Donnie Mategula, Rachel Gichuki, Peter Jackson Muriuki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEarly childhood exposure to cariogenic foods and drinks is a key modifiable risk factor for early childhood caries, yet national-level surveillance of dietary risk in children under two years remains limited in Kenya. This study assessed the prevalence, patterns, and factors associated with cariogenic diet exposure among young children in Kenya.We analyzed nationally representative data from the 2022 Kenya Demographic and Health Survey. A total of 7,459 children aged below two years were included in the analysis. Cariogenic diet exposure was defined as consumption of any sugar-containing foods or drinks in the previous day or night. Weighted prevalence estimates were calculated overall and by age group. Bivariate analyses assessed associations with child, maternal, household, and feeding characteristics. Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted using a hierarchical stepwise approach to identify factors associated with exposure.Overall, 21% of children under two years were exposed to a cariogenic diet. Exposure increased markedly with age, from 3.3% among infants < 6 months to 20.2% among those aged 6 to 11 months and 31.2% among children aged 12 to 23 months. The most consumed cariogenic items were sweetened tea or coffee, sweetened milk, and fried or sugary snacks. In the fully adjusted model, older child age was the strongest predictor of cariogenic diet exposure (12–23 months vs. < 6 months: OR = 27.96; 95% CI: 19.44–40.23). Compared with no maternal education, primary (OR = 1.81; 95% CI 1.30–2.53) and secondary (OR = 1.49; 95% CI 1.01–2.20) education were associated with higher odds of exposure. Children in the richest wealth bracket had two times higher odds of exposure compared with those in the poorest bracket (OR = 2.00; 95% CI 1.26–3.18), while continued breastfeeding was protective (OR = 0.57; 95% CI: 0.43–0.75).One in five Kenyan children under two years old is exposed to a cariogenic diet. Early introduction of sugar-containing foods and drinks highlights missed opportunities for prevention during complementary feeding. Integrating oral-health promotion and sugar-avoidance messaging into existing maternal, child health, and nutrition services could strengthen early childhood caries prevention and support Kenya’s national oral health goals. In addition, regulating the marketing and availability of sugar-rich foods can help create healthier food environments for young children.Keywords: Cariogenic, Diet, Childhood, Exposure, Sugar, Kenya

Introduction

A cariogenic diet, defined by the ability of foods and drinks to promote dental caries under conditions conducive to disease development, plays a central role in the onset and progression of dental caries [1]. Dental caries is a chronic, preventable disease characterized by localized demineralization of tooth structure, forming carious lesions [2]. These lesions result from repeated acid attacks in environments where pathological demineralization factors outweigh protective remineralization mechanisms, leading to progressive enamel and dentin breakdown [2].

Early childhood caries (ECC) is a common, yet preventable condition characterized by the presence of one or more decayed, missing, or filled tooth surfaces, due to caries, in any primary tooth of a child under six years of age [3]. Globally, ECC affects nearly half of all children, with an estimated prevalence of 49% [4]. In Africa, prevalence varies widely between countries and between urban and rural settings. A systematic review examining the prevalence of ECC in African countries between January 2000 and March 2025 reported an overall prevalence of 37.9% (95% CI:31.2–44.6). Kenya recorded the highest prevalence in the analysis, with 65.8% (95% CI:58.0–72.8) in males and 61.9% (95% CI:49.6–74.2) in females, alongside the highest mean decayed, missing, filled teeth index of 3.94 (95% CI:2.72–5.16) [4]. The 2015 Kenya National Oral Health Survey reported that nearly half (46.3%) of children aged five years included in the study had dental caries [5]. ECC can cause pain and infection, limit eating, drinking, and communication, and negatively affect physical growth, cognitive development, school readiness, self-esteem, and overall quality of life [3, 6]. In the 2015 Kenya National Oral Health Survey, oral health conditions among five-year-old children were linked to pain, functional limitations, social discomfort, and missed school, demonstrating a meaningful impact on the quality of life at a young age [5]. More than a third of the 5-year-old children surveyed had difficulty in chewing (39%), 18% were dissatisfied with the appearance of their teeth, while dental problems led to missed school for 12% of the five-year-olds, highlighting the functional and social burden of poor oral health among young children in Kenya [5].

Social and behavioral risk factors for ECC include frequent between-meal intake of sugar-containing foods or drinks by the child and regular use of bottles or non-spill cups with sugary liquids [7]. Diets high in sugars, especially sucrose, promote bacterial acid production, lower oral pH, and accelerate demineralization [2]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of cohort studies assessing nutritional and dietary factors associated with the incidence of ECC found that the strongest associations with ECC risk were feeding practices and sugar intake. The meta-analysis found that children who consumed sweet foods more than 5 times a day had 3.2 times higher odds of increased ECC risk (OR 3.24, 95% CI 0.59, 4.03) [8]. Frequent consumption of sugar-rich foods and beverages creates a cariogenic environment that promotes the development and progression of dental caries. Evidence from longitudinal studies shows that caries risk established in early life extends into later childhood, with early feeding practices and sugar exposure being associated with caries in the permanent dentition [9]. Effective management requires addressing this underlying disease process, rather than focusing solely on the restorative treatment of lesions.

Early childhood feeding practices shape children’s dietary preferences and contribute to the development of diet-related diseases, including ECC. Infant feeding practices, including whether a child is exclusively breastfed or formula fed, as well as the timing and type of complementary feeding, influence the risk for early exposure to sugary foods during infancy [10]. Previous studies show that breastfed children are more likely to be introduced to complementary foods closer to the recommended age compared with formula fed children [11]. Early exposure to sugary foods is also shaped by broader social and household factors, including culture, lifestyle, parental education, and household economic status, which influence when and how parents introduce sugary foods [12]. Previous evidence identifies key risk factors for early sugar introduction, including lower maternal education, younger maternal age, and shorter breast-feeding duration [13].

The Kenya National Oral Health Policy for 2022–2030 prioritizes integrated, preventive community interventions using a common risk factor approach, targeting shared behaviors such as high sugar intake and poor nutrition that contribute to dental caries, obesity, diabetes, and other non-communicable diseases [14]. This integrated strategy can strengthen preventive care and improve overall population health. A key focus of the policy is the promotion of a healthy diet across the life course, with specific emphasis on decreasing the consumption of foods and drinks high in free sugars [14].

The first 1000 days, from conception to a child’s second birthday, form a critical window that shapes lifelong well-being [15]. Adequate nutrition and health care during this period influence brain development, immunity, growth, and the establishment of dietary behaviors [15]. Early childhood therefore represents a critical period for establishing dietary patterns linked to oral health. Routine oral health surveillance among children under two years remains limited at national level. Population level monitoring of early sugar consumption is important for tracking the implementation of infant nutrition policies and can assist in informing targeted prevention strategies. Strengthening the use of existing population data to monitor cariogenic diet risk factors provides a practical and cost-effective approach to inform prevention strategies, guide policy implementation, and track progress toward national oral health goals.

The Kenya Demographic Health Survey (KDHS) is a nationally representative household survey designed to generate comprehensive data for monitoring and evaluating key indicators related to population dynamics, health status, and nutrition outcomes [16]. Leveraging the detailed infant feeding data captured in the KDHS offers an opportunity to integrate oral health surveillance within existing national data systems. Methods

Setting

Kenya is an East African country with a projected population of 53,330,978 [17]. It is an ethnically diverse country, with over 40 communities spread across urban and rural areas. The landscape ranges from coastal plains and central highlands to the Great Rift Valley and arid northern regions.

Data

This study analyzed data from the 2022 KDHS. The KDHS uses a standardized methodology and provides nationally representative household data [16]. The authors obtained access to the de-identified dataset on June 12, 2023. All personal and household information was anonymized, ensuring that respondents, households, and communities could not be identified. The 2022 KDHS sample consisted of households drawn from the Kenya Household Master Sample Frame (K-HMSF). A two-stage stratified sampling design was employed to select households. Each of the 47 counties in Kenya was stratified into rural and urban strata, resulting in 92 strata, as Nairobi and Mombasa counties are entirely urban. In the first stage, 1692 clusters were independently selected across sampling strata using the Equal Probability Systematic Sampling Method (EPSSM). In the second stage, all households within each selected cluster were listed, with the resultant household list serving as the sampling frame. From this list, 25 households were then selected from each cluster. In clusters with fewer than 25 households, all households in that cluster were included, resulting in a total sample of 42,022 households. Interviews were carried out exclusively in the selected households and clusters, and no substitutions of the pre-selected units were permitted during data collection [16].

Variables

Outcome variable

The outcome variable was a child’s cariogenic diet exposure, coded as a binary variable indicating whether the child consumed any cariogenic foods or drinks in the previous day or night (yes/no).

Outcome data were collected in the child health and nutrition section of the KDHS women’s questionnaire for children aged 0–23 months living with the respondent. Respondents reported the liquids and foods that their youngest child consumed during the previous day or night, including snacks, small meals, and main meals, whether at home or elsewhere, excluding ingredients used only in small quantities for flavor.

The selection of cariogenic foods and drinks to be included in the analysis was guided by previous literature that developed cariogenicity indices for different foods and drinks [1, 18]. Cariogenic drinks included sweetened or flavored milk, sweetened infant formula, fruit juice, fruit-flavored drinks, sodas, malt drinks, sports or energy drinks, and sweetened tea, coffee, or herbal beverages. Cariogenic foods included candies, pastries, cakes, biscuits, ice cream, popsicles, chips, crisps, puffs, or fried dough.

Predictor variables

The predictor variables included child, maternal, household, and feeding-related characteristics, selected based on prior evidence linking demographic and socio-behavioral factors to early childhood diets [19, 20]. Child related characteristic included the child’s age and sex and were included because dietary diversity, snack consumption, and exposure to sugar-containing foods may vary with age and sex. Maternal characteristics included the mother’s age, education, marital status, ethnicity, and religion and were included as they can be associated with infant feeding decisions, timing of complementary feeding, and caregiver attitude and knowledge related to diet. Household characteristics included the wealth quintile and residence (urban or rural). Feeding-related variables included the breastfeeding status and the care givers receipt of counselling on how or what to feed their child in the last 6 months from a healthcare provider or community health worker. These were included as early feeding practices and receipt of guidance may influence the introduction of complementary foods and exposure to cariogenic foods and drinks among infants.

Statistical analysis

All the analyses were done using R [21]. Descriptive analyses were used to estimate weighted prevalence of cariogenic diet exposure. Children were grouped into age bands of below 6 months, 6–11 months, and 12–23 months to align with recommended feeding practices, including exclusive breastfeeding in the first six months, the introduction of complementary foods from six months, and increasing dietary diversity and frequency in the second year of life. Weighted prevalence estimates and 95% confidence intervals for cariogenic diet exposure were computed. Responses on cariogenic diet exposure coded as ‘don’t know’ were treated as missing and excluded from the primary analyses. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by alternatively classifying ‘don’t know’ responses as non-exposure and exposure to assess the robustness of prevalence estimates. Missing data were excluded from the analysis after confirming that the pattern of missingness was consistent with data missing completely at random (MCAR) using Little’s MCAR test. Bivariate analysis assessed associations between cariogenic diet exposure and key predictor variables using Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests.

Multivariable logistic regression models were fitted using a binomial family with a logit link function to estimate odds ratios and 95% confidence intervals for factors associated with cariogenic diet exposure. The outcome variable was binary, indicating whether a child had consumed any cariogenic food or drink in the previous day or night. Survey weights were applied to account for the KDHS sampling design.

Results

The analysis included 7,459 children aged 23 months and below. Children aged 12–23 months formed the largest share of the sample at 46.7% (n = 3,485), followed by those aged 6–11 months at 27.2% (n = 2026), while infants younger than six months accounted for 26.1% (n = 1948).

Prevalence of early childhood cariogenic diet exposure

Early childhood exposure to cariogenic diets was observed among 1562 of the 7459 children included in the analysis, corresponding to an overall prevalence of 21%. Exposure to cariogenic diets increased with age. Among children under the age of six months, only 65 of 1948 (3.3%) had exposure to a cariogenic diet. This rose to 20.2% and 31.2% for children aged 6 to 11 and 12 to 23 months, respectively (Table 1).

Maternal education was associated with cariogenic diet exposure (< 0.001). Children whose mothers had no formal education had the lowest level of exposure (15.9%), while higher levels of exposure were observed among children of mothers with primary (22.3%), secondary (21.5%), and higher education (23.9%).

Maternal ethnicity was also associated with exposure (p < 0.001). Higher exposure level were observed among children of Meru (28.4%), Kisii (26.3%), Luhya (26.0%), Luo (24.5%), and Kikuyu (24.4%) mothers. Differences were also observed by maternal religion (p = 0.027), with higher level of exposure among children of Evangelical (23.4%) and Protestant (22.0%) mothers, and lower levels of exposure among children of Muslim (18.5%), and Traditionalists (13.5%) mothers.

Children living in urban areas had a slightly higher level of exposure to a cariogenic diet (22.3%) compared with those living in rural areas (20.3%). Exposure also varied across wealth groups, with the lowest level of exposure among children in the poorest households (17%), while the highest level of exposure was in children from the richest households (23.4%).

Among the 3835 respondents who responded to the counselling question, exposure was 44.2% among children whose mothers had received feeding counselling compared with 39.8% among those whose mothers had not received counselling.

Breastfeeding status was also associated with exposure (p < 0.001). The level of exposure was 32.2% among children who had ever been breastfed but were no longer breastfeeding, 26.1% among children who had never been breastfed, and 18.9% among children who were still breastfeeding.

Variables associated with cariogenic diet exposure in the full model included the child’s age, the mother’s age, the mother’s education, marital status, religion, wealth quintile, and breast-feeding status. Compared to children below six months, children aged 6–11 months had 11.26 times higher odds of exposure to a cariogenic diet (OR = 11.26; 95%CI 7.68–16.51), while those aged 12–23 months had 28 times higher odds of exposure to a cariogenic diet (OR = 27.96; 95%CI 19.44–40.23). Compared to mother’s aged 15–19 years, women aged 25–29 had 34% lower odds of their children being exposed to a cariogenic diet (OR = 0.66; 95% CI 0.44–0.99), while those aged 35–39 had 45% lower odds (OR = 0.55; 95% CI 0.35–0.86). For maternal education, compared to children whose mothers had no education, those whose mothers had a primary education had 1.81 times higher odds of exposure to a cariogenic diet (OR = 1.81; 95%CI 1.30–2.53), while those whose mothers had a secondary education had 1.49 times higher odds of exposure (OR = 1.49; 95% CI 1.01–2.20). For marital status, children of separated mothers had 1.72 times higher odds of exposure than children of women never in a union (OR = 1.72; 95% CI 1.03–2.90). Compared to the poorest wealth quintile, children in the richer and richest quintiles had approximately two times higher odds of exposure to a cariogenic diet. Children who were still breastfeeding had 43% lower odds of exposure compared to those previously breastfed but not currently breastfeeding (OR = 0.57; 95% CI 0.43–0.75).

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses showed minimal impact of alternative exposure classification assumptions. Reclassifying “don’t know” responses as exposed resulted in a negligible change in exposure prevalence (20.9%, 1,562/7,459 in the primary analysis vs. 21.1%, 1,570/7,459), while treating “don’t know” responses as not exposed produced identical estimates.

Discussion

Prevention focused oral health approaches offer a more cost-effective and equitable opportunity for better oral health outcomes. In Kenya, however, preventive oral health interventions remain weak and are poorly integrated within the primary health system [23]. Feeding practices among young children are critical, as exposure to sugary foods and drinks in infancy is associated with dental caries [24]. This study examined the prevalence of cariogenic diet exposure among children under two years in Kenya and assessed how child, maternal, and household characteristics were associated with this exposure to inform early prevention strategies.

The findings show that one in five children under two years of age in Kenya had been introduced to cariogenic drinks or foods, with exposure increasing with age. Older infants were more likely to be exposed than those under six months, consistent with evidence showing rising sugar intake after the introduction of complementary feeding [25]. Of concern is the high prevalence of exposure among children from six months to one year of age, at 20.2%. Exposure at this early age has important implications for oral health. A cohort study examining the association between the timing of sugar introduction in the infant’s diet and ECC found that the highest prevalence of caries experience (43.3%) was among children introduced to sugar before 12 months of age [25]. Similarly, analyses of longer-term outcomes showed that children consuming sugar-sweetened beverages at 12 months had a higher risk of earlier caries onset (p = 0.006), while consumption of sweets at 12 months of age was associated with a shorter caries-free period beyond three years of age (p = 0.00034) [24].

Various health organizations, including the International Association of Pediatric Dentists (IAPD) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), as well as dietary policies recommend that children under two years should not consume foods or beverages with added sugars [26–28]. Our findings underscore the need to strengthen parental and caregiver awareness of added sugar as a risk factor for early childhood caries [12, 13].

Sweetened tea or coffee accounted for a sizeable portion of the cariogenic diet exposures, highlighting how common societal and cultural feeding practices may drive early sugar intake. Sugary drinks that are also acidic, such as sugared tea, coffee, lemonade, and soda, present a higher cariogenic risk due to their combined sugar content and increased potential for enamel demineralization [7]. The findings underscore the importance of providing clear guidance during the complementary feeding period to reduce early exposure to cariogenic diets and lower future oral health risks. Identifying practices contributing to early cariogenic diet exposure can provide an entry point for addressing broader dietary patterns associated with free sugar intake in young children.

Among the children exposed to a cariogenic diet, nearly half had exposure to more than one cariogenic food or beverage, with a fifth exposed to three or more items. A cohort study that evaluated associations between patterns of sugar consumption in early childhood and permanent dentition caries at 6 years found that each one-item increase in sweet food consumption at six months of age was significantly associated with permanent molar caries [9]. Our findings suggest that cumulative exposure to multiple sugary foods and drinks may be an important contributor to elevated caries risk later in childhood, reinforcing the need to address overall dietary patterns.

In contrast with previous studies [13], children of mothers with higher education and those from wealthier households had higher odds of exposure to cariogenic diets. Mothers with higher education may be more likely to be engaged in out-of-home employment, which can limit breastfeeding and other recommended feeding practices. In such situations, care may shift to alternate caregivers, including siblings, grandparents, nannies or neighbours, who may have less guidance on appropriate feeding. Previous evidence shows that early cessation of breastfeeding is common when mothers return to work due to workplace constraints, limited lactation support, and low experience with milk expression [29]. Higher cariogenic diet exposure among wealthier families also suggests that higher purchasing power may contribute to increased access to sugary and commercially marketed foods and beverages. From a policy perspective, these findings highlight the need for oral health and nutrition interventions that address dietary risks across socioeconomic groups.

The study findings highlight the need for multisectoral interventions that address early exposure to cariogenic diets. Policies and programs aimed at preventing childhood caries and other sugar related non-communicable diseases should prioritize clear guidance on sugar avoidance during complementary feeding and strengthen regulations on the marketing of sugary foods and beverages targeting young children [25]. Interventions are also needed to address the ongoing nutritional transition in low and middle income countries characterized by shifts in dietary patterns due to the widespread availability and consumption of ultra-processed foods that impacts the health of young children [30]. The Kenya Nutrient Profile Model (KNPM), released in 2025, provides a framework to guide the development and implementation of regulatory measures [31]. These regulations include front-of-pack labelling, fiscal policies such as a sugar tax, and restrictions on the marketing of unhealthy foods to children [31]. Establishment and enforcement of such regulations can help consumers in identifying foods high in sugar and support them to make informed food choices.

In the bivariate analysis, counselling was associated with cariogenic diet exposure, but this association was lost after adjustment in the regression model, suggesting that the crude association was confounded by other factors. In addition, the content, frequency, and quality of counselling were not captured, limiting its interpretation. Among respondents with available data, only a fifth reported having received counselling in the previous six months on how or what to feed their child. The finding points to important gaps in the delivery of preventive nutrition guidance and underscores the need to strengthen caregiver nutrition counselling during the antenatal period and in the child’s first 1000 days, as feeding practices are being established. Within the health system, enhancing nutritional education, early screening, and preventive oral care is key to the prevention and early identification of caries risk. Strengthening childcare and school-based programs, including oral health education, also offers opportunities to establish good oral hygiene and dietary practices early in life.

In line with Kenya’s oral health guidelines, integrating oral health promotion into existing maternal, child health, and nutrition programs offers an opportunity to address shared risk factors through a unified approach [14]. Incorporating oral health dietary guidance within the primary care settings can mitigate the longstanding oral health delivery challenges, including inadequate access to oral health services and shortages in the oral health workforce, that affect oral health outcomes [14]. Healthcare providers’ capacity, however, is central to the adoption of oral health promotion, including continuous training on oral health and dissemination of oral health guidelines [32]. Evidence from a mentorship-based oral health training program for nurses showed that training improved nurses’ confidence and supported the delivery of oral health promotion services, contributing to better overall service provision [33].

The development of ECC is shaped by other factors not captured in this study including oral hygiene practices, genetic predisposition, tooth anatomy, enamel and dentin characteristics, fluoride exposure, and saliva flow [2]. Nevertheless, the findings offer important insights into the prevalence and distribution of cariogenic diet exposure in early childhood, identifying dietary patterns that are relevant for prevention efforts.

Another limitation was the dietary measures used in the study. Cariogenicity depends on the type of food or drink and its patterns of consumption including the frequency, quantity, timing, and feeding context such as consumption between meals or at bedtime [1]. The KDHS questionnaire, however, did not capture the frequency and patterns of intake of cariogenic foods and drinks. Despite this, the analysis identifies commonly consumed cariogenic foods and drinks among young children, which can inform targeted oral health and nutrition promotion messaging.

This was a cross-sectional study measuring exposure at one time only and may not reflect longer-term feeding patterns, it does, however, provide a valuable snapshot of early childhood exposure to cariogenic diets, supporting the identification of targeted interventions. Longitudinal cohort studies would assist to better understand potential temporal associations between early feeding practices and ECC onset. The full model explained 23.3% of the variation in early childhood exposure to cariogenic diets. A substantial proportion of variation remained unexplained, reflecting the complexity of feeding behaviors. Unmeasured influences such as parental knowledge, cultural norms, local food environments, social, and intraindividual factors that shape eating behaviors such as peer influences, and individual preferences are also likely to play an important role in infant dietary patterns [12].

The regression analysis used logistic models because the outcome was binary and the aim was to identify factors associated with cariogenic diet exposure after adjustment for child, maternal, household, and feeding-related characteristics. However, because cariogenic diet exposure was relatively common in this study, the reported odds ratios may be larger than corresponding prevalence ratios and should therefore be interpreted as measures of association with the odds of exposure, rather than as direct estimates of relative prevalence. Future analyses may consider approaches such as modified Poisson regression with robust variance to estimate prevalence ratios directly and improve comparability with studies reporting prevalence-based measures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, one fifth of children aged six to eleven months and nearly one third of those aged 12 to 23 months were exposed to a cariogenic diet. Sweetened tea or coffee, sweetened milk, and snacks were the most reported cariogenic items. Exposure increased with child age, while higher household wealth and maternal education were also associated with higher odds of exposure. Despite these patterns, only a fifth of caregivers reported receiving recent counselling on child feeding. These findings highlight the need to strengthen caregiver awareness and knowledge through consistent nutrition and oral health counselling delivered by health providers during antenatal care and throughout the first year of the child’s life, when feeding practices are being established. In addition, interventions that address social and commercial determinants of diet and oral health are needed, including regulation of the marketing and availability of sugar-rich foods and beverages, as well as policies that support healthier food environments for young children.
