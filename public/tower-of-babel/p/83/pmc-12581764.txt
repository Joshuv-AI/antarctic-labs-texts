Outreach to Outcomes: Advancing HIV Care in Priority Populations
Authors: Lesley Simon, Rachel Deerr, Tabitha Washington, Tara Walker, Stan Pogroszewski, Dean Beals
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe US HIV epidemic disproportionately affects historically marginalised groups, particularly Black individuals in the Southern states. The national “Ending the HIV Epidemic” (EHE) initiative was established in 2019 to reduce HIV infections in priority populations. Systemic barriers such as social stigma, economic hardship, and limited healthcare access contribute to higher HIV incidence and worse outcomes, requiring a multidimensional response beyond clinician education alone. To address this, we developed and implemented five community-engaged programmes in priority jurisdictions, leveraging culturally safe and familiar community venues, such as Black-owned barbershops and Black faith-based organisations. Our programmes used a multidimensional educational strategy: first, for clinicians, focusing on critical gaps such as HIV and PrEP disparities, cultural humility, and rapid ART initiation; second, for trusted community partners, including barbers, peer counsellors, and clergy; and third, for clients, providing educational materials and fostering conversations in multiple languages. A central and vital component of the initiative was providing onsite health education, client incentives, and screenings for HIV alongside other conditions (e.g., hypertension and diabetes) at community events, with an approach designed to mitigate stigma and access barriers by bringing services directly where people live and gather. Across five programmes, 804 HIV screenings were conducted. Over 1,650 clinicians were educated, with demonstrated improvements in knowledge, competence, and confidence regarding HIV and PrEP disparities, cultural humility, and HIV management. The investment of partners (e.g., clergy and barbers) was instrumental in recruiting and educating communities, bridging persistent gaps, and addressing systemic mistrust towards the medical establishment. In conclusion, programmes that incorporate client, clinician, and trusted partner education along with client incentives are valuable strategies to promote HIV testing and treatment in priority populations in priority jurisdictions.KEYWORDS: Healthcare disparities, HIV, barber, community, testing, pre-exposure prophylaxis, clergy

Introduction

The HIV epidemic disproportionately affects historically marginalised groups in the United States [1,2]. Compared to White individuals, Black individuals, specifically black men who have sex with men; people who have been incarcerated; and those experiencing food insecurity and unstable housing have significantly higher rates of HIV and worse outcomes across the entire care continuum [3–5]. In 2019, the US national “Ending the HIV Epidemic” (EHE) plan [6] set a goal to reduce new HIV diagnoses by 90% by 2030. The strategy focuses on early diagnosis, rapid initiation of antiretroviral therapy (ART), and prevention of new transmissions – including HIV pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). The plan identified priority jurisdictions – areas accounting for over half of recent HIV diagnoses nationwide – where focused efforts could have the greatest impact, and while these jurisdictions are spread across the US, they are predominantly located in southern states [6]. Evidence suggests that strategies to mitigate HIV disproportionately benefit White individuals [6]. For example, as of 2024 in Florida, Black individuals represented approximately 41% of new HIV diagnoses but only 23% of PrEP users. In contrast, White individuals accounted for 22% of new diagnoses yet made up 48% of PrEP users [7].

Disparities in HIV prevention and treatment are deeply rooted in systemic barriers and social determinants of health [8]. Contributing factors to higher incidence and worse outcomes in historically marginalised groups include social stigma, housing insecurity, economic hardship, health literacy and barriers to access to healthcare [9–11]. Clinicians face challenges as well, including limited knowledge on how to approach conversations on HIV prevention and treatment with cultural humility, and uncertainty about whom to screen and how to manage HIV effectively. Clinician education alone is insufficient to impact population health. Gaps in care are multidimensional and, therefore, interventions that involve numerous disciplines are required to ameliorate those gaps [12]. Community-engaged approaches – globally recognised as effective – [13] offer promising strategies to address these challenges from the continuing medical education (CME) front. Augmenting clinician education with community education and engagement may overcome barriers to change, change practice, and improve health for people in historically marginalised communities [12].

Culturally safe and familiar community venues (e.g., Black-owned barbershops in Black neighbourhoods, Black faith-based organisations) have proven to be effective in delivering health education, screenings, and linkage to care [14,15]. For example, multidimensional models engaging healthcare providers and Black-owned barbershops and barbers have successfully reduced hypertension in Black individuals [16]. Similarly, clergy in southern states – aware of their influence in uniting communities – have expressed a willingness to serve as partners in addressing the HIV epidemic [15].

Building on these precedents, we developed five community-engaged programmes that integrate HIV services with broader health screenings, bringing care to where people live and gather. Our programmes were designed to mitigate systemic barriers that have traditionally prevented clients from accessing HIV prevention, testing, and treatment services.

Methods

With independent medical education grants from Gilead Sciences, we developed and implemented five multidimensional connected community-engaged education and screening programmes in four priority jurisdictions. All programmes targeted historically marginalised communities, which included individuals belonging to racial and ethnic minority groups, formerly incarcerated individuals, people experiencing food insecurity, and members of the LGBTQ community. We built relationships with local stakeholders and engaged trusted community partners, including barbers, clergy, and counsellors. “Fade Out HIV” was implemented in Los Angeles, CA, and Philadelphia, PA, in collaboration with local Black-owned barbershops. In New Orleans, LA, we partnered with four local Black Baptist churches to host health fairs as part of our “Saving Souls, Healing Bodies” activity. In Fort Lauderdale, FL, two programmes were launched: “Rebuilding Lives: Improving Care in HIV”, in partnership with a re-entry agency, where peer re-entry counsellors assist formerly incarcerated individuals; and “Feeding Souls, Protecting Bodies”, in collaboration with a progressive queer church and food pantry that actively engages the LGBTQ community.

Accredited CME and Establishment of Client-Clinician Referral Network

Clinician education activities were developed collaboratively by DKBmed and expert local faculty and implemented through a combination of live programmes and webinars with question and answer sessions, followed by enduring webcasts. The curriculum was designed to address critical gaps in care by focusing on topics such as the impact of HIV on priority populations, strategies for initiating sexual health conversations, the importance of cultural humility, HIV screening and prevention (including PrEP), and HIV treatment, particularly rapid ART initiation. Knowledge, competence, and confidence were assessed through pre- and post-activity evaluations. Knowledge was measured by the percentage of correct answers to relevant questions (e.g., “How often should people using PrEP be tested for HIV?”) before and after the programme. Competence and confidence were assessed using self-rated questions where clinicians responded on a scale, indicating their perceived ability and assurance in specific practice areas (e.g., “I assess my patients using PrEP for STIs every three months”/“How confident are you in your ability to determine which of your patients have increased risk of HIV based on sexual history?”), both before and after the education. Learners were advised of the activity’s associated partner education and community screening events, and learners who completed the education were invited to participate in that programme’s specific referral network; if interested, relevant information (office location and insurance accepted) was collected. All CME/CE activities adhered to professional accreditation guidelines.

Education for Community Partners and Clients

For four of the five programmes, the local expert faculty for the clinician educational activity also prepared and educated the community partners for that particular programme. Education for community partners was designed to empower them as community health advocates and educators. The community partner education was delivered at barber conventions and barbers at Black-owned barbershops for Fade Out in Los Angeles and Philadelphia, clergy education was delivered through a live presentation during the Freedmen Missionary Baptist General Association meeting for Saving Souls, and peer counsellors were educated at a local re-entry agency for Rebuilding Lives. For the latter, a local expert faculty with expertise in community-based screening for HIV delivered education to peer counsellors.

All community partners – barbers, clergy, and re-entry counsellors – received customised training specific to their community’s population (e.g., Black men for the barber programmes and justice-involved individuals for the peer counsellor programme). The community partners learned about HIV prevalence in their community, the importance of HIV testing, use of nonstigmatizing language, and initiating health discussions. Materials developed or collated by us were provided in multiple languages (English, Spanish, and Creole) to support the community partners and their clients and congregants. The educated community partners were encouraged by faculty to engage with their clients or congregants and distribute materials when appropriate.

Screening for HIV and Other Health Conditions at Community Events

To mitigate access barriers to HIV testing (a central component of our programmes), screening was carried out through onsite health screenings at barbershops, health fairs in churches and in the community, and farmers’ markets. To reduce stigma, HIV testing was offered alongside screenings for other common health issues such as hypertension and diabetes. Test results were not collected, as testing for HIV itself was the goal of the screening events. Comment cards were provided to clients attending screenings. Local healthcare providers and testing organisations conducted the screenings. To boost participation, some events provided incentives, such as coupons for free haircuts for screened barber clients, while others incorporated additional activities and opportunities for socialisation. Referral coordinators were available onsite to help patients schedule follow-up appointments and connect them with appropriate clinicians.

Results

Community Clinician Education

Key characteristics of the clinician education activities are presented in Table 1. Knowledge and practice gaps addressed by the education and educational outcomes (pre- and post-activity knowledge, competence and confidence) are shown in Table 2. In total, over 1,650 clinicians were educated across the five programmes. We observed improvements in knowledge in every area tested: HIV and PrEP disparities, cultural humility, PrEP monitoring and eligibility, HIV screening, and rapid ART. Notably, the largest improvement in knowledge (60%) was observed for the gap related to HIV and PrEP disparities in one of the Florida programmes (Rebuilding Lives) focusing on the formerly incarcerated population. The smallest improvement was observed for the gap in rapid ART in Los Angeles (2%), though this trend was not consistent across all programmes.

Community Health Screenings, Partners, and Client Engagement

Table 3 summarises the key characteristics of the five community-engaged programmes and the number of HIV screenings conducted at community screening events. In total, 804 HIV screenings were conducted across the five programmes. Community events and social media outreach received significant engagement from both partners and clients. The programme in Los Angeles relied on a partnership with a local medical centre for testing; its Philadelphia counterpart conducted screenings at barbershop events. The New Orleans programme collaborated with a local clergy association to educate 715 clergy overall and conduct screenings at church-based health fairs. The programmes in Florida collectively educated 298 community attendees and performed screenings across their events. Attendees completing comment cards expressed positive feedback. Attendees described screening events as “very helpful” and “informative”. Comments included “Learned a lot about blood sugar and HIV”; “first time testing, very happy”; and “we need this in the community”, and expressed appreciation for having questions answered and tests completed. Many felt these events were “a great idea for the community” and expressed a desire for more events.

Discussion

Our unique approach to community-engaged CME improved the knowledge, competence, and confidence of 1,650 participating clinicians, with the potential to impact an estimated 34,653 patients with HIV. These clinician-based education efforts can be maximally effective by connecting with the communities they serve. Working with engaged and respected partners is essential for bridging the persistent gaps in HIV testing, prevention, and treatment, particularly in communities affected by systemic mistrust towards the medical establishment [16]. The investment of partners, such as the Black Barber Influencer with a personal interest in HIV, was instrumental in recruiting and educating other barbers. Peer counsellors from re-entry agencies were trained to become trusted sources of healthcare information for justice-involved individuals, effectively discussing HIV and other infectious diseases. After conducting informal follow-up interviews, we observed deep engagement among counsellors in the “Rebuilding Lives” programme, which reaffirmed the value of highly committed partners. Incentives and client education also encourage testing.

Bringing screening directly to people in community settings can overcome reluctance to screen in traditional healthcare settings. This co-located service delivery model, which involved setting up barbers to provide haircuts adjacent to rapid HIV testing stations, was vital for overcoming logistical obstacles and increasing testing rates. The “Saving Souls” programme hosted health fairs at churches to provide on-site screenings, while “Feeding Souls” partnered with community organisations – including a progressive LGBTQ church – to offer health screenings and a local farmers market to help reduce access barriers for individuals experiencing food insecurity.

Screening for other health conditions, such as hypertension and diabetes, alongside HIV testing, helps reduce stigma and may increase overall engagement. Building upon the historical success of hypertension interventions in barbershops, these screening events integrate HIV testing into broader health discussions. This approach normalises testing and mitigates the stigma often experienced by Black men who have sex with men, offering a less stigmatising entry point to care. Health fairs offered a range of screenings including HIV, A1C, blood pressure, and hepatitis C, promoting comprehensive health engagement rather than a singular focus on HIV.

Finally, the programmes underscored that partners need ongoing education and support on how to have conversations about HIV to help normalise these conversations in nonclinical settings. Challenges arose from barbers’ reluctance to discuss HIV due to time constraints and comfort levels. To address this, programmes offered Instagram Live or Zoom sessions that provided guidance and support to overcome any lingering barriers on how to initiate the conversations.

Conclusion

Programmes that incorporate client, clinician, and trusted partner education along with client incentives are valuable strategies to promote HIV testing and treatment of priority populations in priority jurisdictions and potentially change practice, leading to durable effects.
