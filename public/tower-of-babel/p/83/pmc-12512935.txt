Background

Hepatic hydrothorax (HH), defined as pleural effusion associated with liver cirrhosis and portal hypertension in the absence of cardiopulmonary disease, is a complication that affects between 5% and 16% of patients with advanced liver disease [1–3]. The condition is thought to arise primarily from the movement of ascitic fluid into the pleural cavity through microscopic diaphragmatic defects, facilitated by negative intrathoracic pressure and high abdominal pressure due to ascites [4, 5]. Although hepatic hydrothorax is typically associated with substantial ascites, reports of pleural effusion without clinically detectable ascites have been documented and may complicate timely diagnosis [6, 7].

Secondary infections, particularly following invasive procedures like pleural catheter placement, complicate HH management. These infections, involving pathogens such as Gram-negative bacilli, Enterococcus faecium and Candida parapsilosis, require tailored antimicrobial strategies, especially when in vitro sensitivity tests do not align with clinical outcomes, often due to challenges in drug penetration into the pleural space [1, 4, 5]. The management of HH with such infections becomes complex, particularly in immunocompromised patients, necessitating a multidisciplinary approach and considerations for advanced therapeutic interventions.

Case Presentation

A 49-year-old Chinese male of Han ethnicity was diagnosed with hepatitis B cirrhosis and splenomegaly in April 2020 after detecting thrombocytopenia. He tested positive for HBsAg, HBeAb, and HBcAb with undetectable HBV-DNA (< 0.5 × 10^3 U/ml), starting entecavir (0.5 mg daily) in May 2020. In 2021 and 2022, he had two episodes of melena, with endoscopy confirming esophageal/gastric varices and portal hypertensive gastropathy.

On April 24, 2024, he developed dyspnea; ultrasound showed a large right-sided pleural effusion without ascites (Fig. 1). He was managed with sodium restriction and diuretics, torasemide 5 mg, and spironolactone 40 mg orally per day. In mid-July, the patient’s shortness of breath persisted without relief, worsened after activity, and was accompanied by bilateral lower limb edema. The patient continued oral diuretic treatment.

Ultrasound on April 24, 2024, showing large right-sided pleural effusion without ascites

On August 21, 2024, he was admitted for evaluation. Clinical assessment confirmed advanced cirrhosis (Child-Pugh class B); non-hepatic causes of effusion were excluded. On August 27, due to severe dyspnea and large pleural effusion refractory to diuretics, right-sided thoracentesis with catheter placement was performed to relieve respiratory distress, despite guideline cautions, as a temporary measure pending further management. Figure 2-a shows the ultrasound findings before thoracentesis, demonstrating a large effusion on the right side. Significant pleural drainage was noted, with early cultures negative. Sequential pleural fluid analyses are summarized in Table 1. Treatment options, including repeated thoracentesis, TIPS, pleurodesis, and liver transplantation, were discussed, but the patient declined surgical interventions due to financial constraints, procedural risks, and limited family support, favoring continued thoracentesis for symptom control.

Sequential pleural fluid examination results

On September 10, 2024, pleural fluid showed elevated white blood cell counts, with cultures growing Pseudomonas luteola, consistent with secondary infection following catheter placement. As depicted in Fig. 2-b, the pleural effusion was evident during this time. Cefotaxime was given for one week. On September 18, cultures cleared Pseudomonas luteola but grew Enterococcus faecium (sensitive to vancomycin and linezolid). Septated effusion prompted repeat thoracentesis; antibiotics were deferred pending further results.

On October 7, 2024, cultures grew Enterococcus faecium and Candida parapsilosis, prompting vancomycin and voriconazole. Persistent septated effusion required catheter replacement on October 14. Figure 2-c highlights the septated pleural effusion observed on October 14. On October 16, cultures still grew Candida parapsilosis with pleural fluid white blood cell counts at 1788 × 10^6/L, indicating voriconazole failure, so caspofungin was started. By October 22, pleural fluid white blood cell counts dropped to 780 × 10^6/L with negative cultures, confirming caspofungin’s efficacy. On October 25, 2024, pleural fluid culture turned negative, and the patient’s condition gradually improved, leading to discontinuation of caspofungin. By October 28, the patient improved clinically, and caspofungin was discontinued. Figure 2-d demonstrates the significant reduction in pleural effusion after successful treatment. Due to the recurrent nature of hepatic hydrothorax, we held further discussions with the patient and family at discharge, encouraging reconsideration of TIPS, pleurodesis, or liver transplantation as potential treatment options.

Discussion and conclusions

Hepatic Hydrothorax Without Ascites: Mechanisms Beyond Diaphragmatic Defects. Hepatic hydrothorax (HH) is traditionally explained by the transdiaphragmatic movement of ascitic fluid into the pleural cavity through small defects in the diaphragm, particularly on the right side. However, cases like this one, where HH occurs without detectable ascites, suggest alternative mechanisms. Research suggests that in such cases, elevated portal pressures may increase pleural capillary permeability, allowing fluid to migrate directly into the pleural space [8, 9]. Additionally, lymphatic dysfunction, particularly impaired lymphatic drainage at the diaphragm, may also contribute by limiting pleural fluid reabsorption, causing fluid accumulation in the pleural space. This highlights the need for a broader understanding of HH pathophysiology beyond diaphragmatic defects [10, 11].

Following catheter placement, this case developed a nosocomial pleural infection involving Pseudomonas luteola, Enterococcus, and Candida parapsilosis. Cirrhotic patients are immunocompromised, increasing susceptibility to opportunistic infections during invasive procedures like thoracentesis. These pathogens, detected post-catheterization, highlight infection risks in catheterized hepatic hydrothorax patients. The immunocompromised state of cirrhosis, combined with prolonged hospital stays and invasive procedures, predisposes patients to opportunistic infections from these pathogens [12, 13]. For patients declining transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt due to financial constraints or procedural risks, catheter drainage remains common, requiring strict aseptic measures to reduce infections [14].

Antibiotic management in catheter-dependent hepatic hydrothorax (HH) requires addressing pathogen-specific challenges. In this case, voriconazole failed to clear Candida parapsilosis despite in vitro sensitivity. Discrepancies between in vitro sensitivity and clinical efficacy may be due to insufficient drug penetration into the pleural cavity or the formation of fungal biofilms, which can reduce drug susceptibility in vivo [9, 11]. Switching to caspofungin, which has better biofilm activity and distribution properties, successfully eradicated the infection, emphasizing the importance of selecting antifungal agents with activity against biofilm-forming organisms in persistent fungal infections [14]. In patients with hepatic dysfunction, such as those with moderate liver impairment, dose escalation risks adverse effects like hepatotoxicity, complicating treatment. In lean patients, where voriconazole’s distribution volume shows minimal variation, both serum and pleural fluid concentrations are expected to remain stable, suggesting other factors may drive treatment failure. The inability to monitor voriconazole plasma or pleural effusion concentrations further hinders optimal dosing, necessitating a switch to alternative agents with broader efficacy.

For medically refractory hepatic hydrothorax (HH), treatment options include repeated thoracentesis, transjugular intrahepatic portosystemic shunt (TIPS), pleurodesis, diaphragmatic repair, or liver transplantation. TIPS reduces portal pressure and fluid accumulation but risks hepatic encephalopathy [8, 11] Pleuroperitoneal shunts, enabling drainage to the peritoneal cavity, suit patients unsuitable for TIPS [13]. However, some patients, like this case, decline surgery due to procedural risks, costs, or limited family support, opting for pleural catheter drainage. This approach carries risks of infection, protein and electrolyte depletion, renal dysfunction, and bleeding [15, 16], requiring strict aseptic measures and nutritional support. Temporary catheter use relieved dyspnea but increased risks, necessitating risk-benefit assessment. Patient refusal of TIPS reflects common barriers, suggesting strategies like infection prevention and nutritional monitoring. This single-case study limits generalizability, and catheter safety requires further research.
