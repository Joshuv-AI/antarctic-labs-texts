Broadband solar energy harvesting and near-perfect thermal emission on a unified stepped-high concentric dual-ring metamaterial
Authors: Qiuqun Liang, Yuanqing Gu, Shanguang Wu, Junxue Chen, Xuandong Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of advanced solar energy harvesting systems requires metamaterial absorbers that overcome the limitations of conventional noble-metal designs, particularly their insufficient thermal stability and significant optical losses. This study introduces a stepped-height concentric dual-ring metamaterial absorber (SHCDR-MA) based on titanium nitride (TiN), which achieves an average absorptivity of 99.27% across an ultra-broadband spectrum from 150 to 3000 nm. The structure employs a vertically graded dual-ring configuration, atop a silicon dioxide (SiO2) dielectric spacer and a TiN substrate, enabling multi-resonance coupling and impedance matching. Absorption originates from the synergistic interplay of several mechanisms: a vertical impedance gradient created by the concentric ring height difference, coherent transition from TiN interband transitions and localized surface plasmon resonances at shorter wavelengths to propagating surface plasmon resonances and dielectric-guided modes at longer wavelengths, and efficient energy dissipation through magnetic hotspots and Poynting vector convergence. The absorber exhibits excellent angular independence (up to 60°) and maintains near-ideal, temperature-invariant blackbody-like emission across a wide temperature range (373–1500 K). By leveraging the refractory properties of TiN, this design provides a feasible strategy for advanced applications in solar energy harvesting and high-temperature solar thermophotovoltaics.

Introduction

The global energy landscape remains predominantly dependent on finite fossil fuel reserves, with their unsustainable consumption precipitating multidimensional crises encompassing environmental degradation, economic instability, and societal challenges. In this context, solar energy emerges as a transformative renewable alternative, offering theoretically inexhaustible potential, zero-emission operation, and universal geographical accessibility1–4. Harnessing this resource through advanced photothermal technologies presents a viable pathway to address impending energy shortages. Central to these systems are high-performance solar absorbers, where metamaterial-based designs enable unprecedented spectral control from deep-ultraviolet to mid-infrared wavelengths4–8.

While the potential of solar energy is immense, its efficient harvesting demands precise control over light-matter interactions at nanoscale dimensions. This control is fundamentally governed by plasmonic phenomena – the coordinated charge oscillations that occur when photons couple with conductive materials9–12. Plasmonic phenomena arise from coordinated oscillations of free electrons in metallic systems when excited by external electromagnetic radiation9–12. Nanoscale metallic configurations can concentrate optical energy beyond conventional diffraction boundaries, producing amplified local electric fields with precisely tunable resonant frequencies across the visible and near-infrared spectrum. These engineered field enhancements enable transformative technologies ranging from ultra-sensitive molecular detection and precision biomedicine to renewable energy systems, where plasmon-mediated hot electron generation significantly boosts solar conversion efficiency13,14. Current implementations predominantly utilize precious metals such as gold and silver, which present substantial integration challenges with semiconductor manufacturing processes owing to their poor thermal resilience, chemical reactivity, and problematic thin-film deposition characteristics15,16. Additional performance constraints stem from inherent optical absorption losses caused by interband electronic transitions within these materials17.

Consequently, the development of innovative plasmonic metamaterial configurations for solar energy harvesting systems is critically needed, as conventional noble metal nanostructures face fundamental limitations including complementary metal-oxide-semiconductor (CMOS) fabrication incompatibility, inadequate thermal/chemical robustness under operational conditions, and substantial optical losses particularly in visible wavelengths.

Recent research has increasingly focused on alternative plasmonic materials that combine favorable optical properties with enhanced stability. Among these, refractory transition metal nitrides, particularly titanium nitride (TiN), have shown significant promise18–24. TiN exhibits optical characteristics comparable to noble metals while offering superior thermal resilience (with a melting point up to 2930 °C) and chemical inertness18–24. Its compatibility with standard semiconductor fabrication processes makes it a viable substitute for gold in high-temperature plasmonic applications. TiN also demonstrates optimal impedance matching with air and significant optical attenuation in the visible spectrum, making it highly effective for photonic energy capture and protective coatings in extreme environments18–24.

Several studies have explored TiN-based absorbers, yet limitations remain in achieving full-spectrum performance. Yang et al.18 demonstrated a TiN/SiO2 cylinder-array metasurface absorber achieving 94% solar absorption across 300–2500 nm through synergistic surface plasmon and Fabry-Pérot resonances, though limited to visible-NIR wavelengths. Zeng et al.19 developed a refractory TiN/Al2O3 metasurface absorber achieving 91.5% solar absorption across 300–2500 nm via synergistic plasmonic-FP resonances, though constrained to visible-NIR wavelengths and requiring multilayer fabrication. Feng et al.20 developed a TiN-based monolithic absorber achieving 91.3% average absorption across 300–2500 nm through coupled plasmonic-guided mode resonances, demonstrating wide-angle (60°) and polarization-insensitive operation for solar thermophotovoltaic applications. Sun et al.21 demonstrated a TiN ring-array metamaterial absorber achieving 95.8% energy absorption ratio under AM1.5 solar spectrum (300–2500 nm) with wide-angle stability, though its inability to control mid-IR thermal emission motivates the development of novel architectures. Gao et al.22 designed a SiO2/TiN four-layer absorber achieving 99.52% average visible-light absorption (400–800 nm) through integrated plasmonic resonances, yet remains fundamentally limited to the visible spectrum and lacks thermal emission functionality essential for full-spectrum solar utilization. Liu et al.23 demonstrated a Ti-TiN-Si3N4-based metamaterial absorber achieving an average absorption of 98.9% across 280–3800 nm through synergistic plasmonic and cavity resonances. Li et al.24 proposed a wide-angle broadband solar absorber with a horizontal multi-cylinder structure based on MXene material, achieving high absorption efficiency in a wide wavelength range, but it didn’t explore the ultra-broadband absorption from deep-UV to mid-IR and the related high-efficiency thermal emission performance. While these studies have significantly advanced broadband absorption within specific spectral ranges, their limited coverage of deep-UV and mid-IR wavelengths, coupled with insufficient thermal emission control, highlights critical gaps in current metamaterial absorber designs.

Recent advances highlight that employing spectrally overlapping multiple resonances is a key strategy to overcome the narrowband limitation of single modes and achieve ultra-broadband, high-efficiency absorption. This principle is validated across diverse platforms: from multi-resonant Mie arrays for solar cells25 and flexible absorbers utilizing synergistic cavity-mode overlap26, to bio-inspired structures employing cascaded resonances for microwave absorption27. The concept extends beyond absorption, aiding in the design of structural colors via controlled Fabry-Pérot modes28 and supported by theoretical frameworks like quality-factor-weighted mode density engineering29. Furthermore, the deliberate engineering of cavity mode overlap itself is a proven route to enhance optical performance, as seen in efficient structural30 and transmissive colors31. Building upon these established principles of multi-resonance coupling and engineered mode overlap, we propose a novel stepped-height concentric dual-ring metamaterial architecture.

To overcome these limitations, we introduce a novel stepped-height concentric dual-ring metamaterial absorber (SHCDR-MA) that synergistically integrates multi-resonance coupling and impedance-matching structural engineering. Its design features concentric dual rings with precisely controlled height differences, forming a vertically graded architecture that enhances broadband electromagnetic energy capture. The SHCDR-MA achieves ultra-broadband near-perfect absorption (150–3000 nm) through synergistic excitation of multiple plasmonic resonances, while simultaneously enabling high-efficiency thermal emission across an ultra-wide temperature range (500–2000 K). This dual functionality is enabled by the stepped-height concentric dual-ring geometry combined with refractory materials such as TiN, ensuring exceptional thermal stability and durability under extreme operational conditions.

Structural design and physical modeling

The proposed stepped-height concentric dual-ring metamaterial absorber (SHCDR-MA) employs a vertically stratified architecture with discrete stepped-height configurations to achieve multi-physical field synergy. The structural hierarchy comprises: (1) a TiN substrate (thickness t0) serving dual functions as a broadband electromagnetic reflector and thermal dissipation layer; (2) a SiO2 dielectric spacer (thickness t1) engineered for simultaneous phase matching and Fabry-Pérot resonance modulation; and (3) a top metasurface featuring concentric TiN annular resonators with vertically graded heights – the inner annulus (radii R1/R2) elevates to height t3, while the outer annulus (radii R3/R4) maintains reduced height t2 (t3 > t2). This deliberate height discontinuity induces cascaded plasmonic hot spots through synergistic inter-ring near-field coupling and intra-ring vertical mode hybridization, enabling enhanced broadband absorption through spatial overlap of multiple resonance modes. Figure 1a illustrates the three-dimensional schematic of the proposed SHCDR-MA, with Fig. 1b and c presenting cross-sectional views in the x-y and x-z planes, respectively, demonstrating a unit cell with lattice period P. The selection of titanium nitride (TiN) as the metallic constituent is motivated by three critical merits: (1) CMOS-compatible chemical stability, (2) tunable plasmonic responses across visible to near-infrared regimes, and (3) superior thermal management capability owing to its high thermal conductivity. Silicon dioxide (SiO2)32 is chosen as the dielectric spacer due to its ultralow optical loss, precise thickness control during deposition, and thermal expansion coefficient matching with TiN. Full-wave electromagnetic simulations were performed using finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) method33. The temperature-independent complex permittivity data for TiN and SiO2 were sourced from Palik’s handbook (3rd edition, Chap. 12)34 and were used for the simulations across the entire temperature range studied. The numerical simulations implement periodic boundary conditions along the x-y plane while utilizing perfectly matched layers (PML) for z-axis wave absorption. The optimized structural parameters include a TiN substrate (t0 = 100 nm), SiO2 dielectric spacer (t1 = 60 nm), concentric dual-TiN rings (outer ring: t2 = 290 nm, radii R1 = 130 nm and R2= 145 nm; inner ring: t3 = 470 nm, radii R3 = 60 nm and R4 = 80 nm), and a lattice period (P = 380 nm), which collectively enable multi-mode resonance coupling and impedance matching.

Although this article primarily focuses on numerical simulation studies, it is necessary to briefly outline the standard fabrication process to clarify the manufacturability of the designed structure. The process begins on a cleaned silicon or quartz substrate. First, a 100 nm thick TiN base layer is deposited via magnetron sputtering. Subsequently, a SiO2 dielectric layer is grown using plasma-enhanced chemical vapor deposition (PECVD), followed by the deposition of a 470 nm thick top TiN film via Magnetron Sputtering42. Next, the first electron beam lithography (EBL) and etching step is performed: after spin-coating photoresist, the outer ring area (the annular region between R3 and R4) is exposed and developed using a mask. Reactive ion etching (RIE) is then employed to precisely remove 180 nm of TiN, leaving a remaining thickness of 290 nm in this area to create the step height difference. This is followed by the second EBL and etching step: photoresist is reapplied, and the structural isolation areas—comprising (1) the inter-unit isolation trench (from R4 to the period P boundary), (2) the gap between the inner and outer rings (the annular region from R2 to R3), and (3) the central cylinder of the inner ring (from the center to R1)—are exposed and developed via a mask. RIE is used again to completely etch through the TiN in this region until the underlying SiO2 dielectric layer surface is exposed, ultimately forming an array of independent concentric dual-ring structures with step-height features. Owing to its near-unity solar absorption performance and scalable nanofabrication approach, the engineered metamaterial system exhibits versatile deployment potential in ultraviolet radiation barriers, solar-thermal conversion units, broadband photodetection arrays, and spectrally selective thermal emitters.

The above process outlines a standard fabrication route. In a practical implementation, however, steps such as EBL and RIE may introduce imperfections such as sidewall roughness, dimensional variations, or inter-layer misalignment. Nevertheless, the design exhibits inherent resilience to such variations. This resilience stems from its operating principle: the ultra-broadband near-perfect absorption is achieved not through a single, narrow resonance, but through the synergistic spectral overlap of multiple excitation pathways (e.g., interband transitions, localized and propagating plasmon resonances, and guided-mode couplings, as detailed in Section “Mechanisms for the spectrally-selective characteristics”). This multi-resonant nature means that minor geometric perturbations are more likely to induce slight spectral shifts in individual resonances rather than their complete suppression. Consequently, the overall, spectrally integrated absorption efficiency remains high under typical fabrication tolerances. This inherent resilience of broadband metamaterial absorbers is further supported by the parametric sensitivity analysis conducted in Section “Considerations on localized heating and structural stability”, which shows maintained high performance over a range of geometric parameters, and is consistent with experimental demonstrations of similar TiN-based nanostructures reporting good agreement between simulated and measured optical responses.

The SHCDR-MA achieves near-perfect absorption spanning the deep-UV to mid-IR (0.15–3 μm) through self-adaptive dual-resonance impedance matching, as validated by the stabilized effective impedance (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${Z_{eff}}$$\end{document}) characteristics in Fig. 3a. The real component (Re(Z), red curve) remains at 1.0 ± 0.1 throughout the operational spectrum, while the imaginary component (Im (Z), blue curve) oscillates minimally within ± 0.2. This dual stability ensures near-unity impedance matching with free space (Z0 = 1 Ω), effectively suppressing Fresnel reflection to < 1.5%. Plasmonic metamaterials fundamentally exhibit metallic-dielectric heterojunctions manifesting sign-inversion phenomena in the real permittivity component, transitioning abruptly from negative to positive dielectric response across the interface. The real and imaginary components of TiN’s permittivity, depicted in Fig. 3b, characterize polarization response and electromagnetic energy dissipation mechanisms, respectively. TiN’s dielectric characteristics dominate below 500 nm, while metallic behavior prevails above ~ 500 nm16,17, enabling surface plasmon excitation through negative permittivity. The imaginary component of complex permittivity quantifies photon attenuation capacity, with enhanced values directly correlating with heightened absorption efficiency. In the visible regime, electronic interband excitation processes dominate dissipation, while collective electron oscillations described by Drude formalism mediate energy loss in lower-frequency regimes. The concentric dual-ring geometry compensates for TiN’s material dispersion by generating complementary magnetic and electric resonances that collectively maintain Re(Z) ≈ 1. Concurrently, the high imaginary permittivity drives dual energy dissipation pathways: Ohmic losses via electron-phonon scattering dominate above 495 nm, while interband transitions with photon-induced carrier multiplication prevail below 495 nm. This wavelength-adaptive synergy achieves > 98% absorptance over 0.15–3 μm, demonstrating exceptional environmental stability due to TiN’s refractory properties and the stress-buffered stepped-height design.

Thermal radiative performance analysis

The proposed SHCDR-MA exhibits exceptional thermal emission characteristics that closely match ideal blackbody radiation across an ultra-broad wavelength range, evaluated through numerical simulation. It is important to note that these simulations employed the temperature-independent complex dielectric function of TiN, sourced from Palik’s handbook, a common practice for the initial theoretical assessment of refractory metamaterial designs. Consequently, the reported high emissivity values, particularly at elevated temperatures, represent the inherent optical potential or an upper-bound performance estimate of the electromagnetic design. This is because, in practice, the optical damping (imaginary part of the permittivity) of TiN increases with temperature due to enhanced electron-phonon scattering, which would likely reduce the peak emissivity values to some extent if accounted for.

The analysis focuses on a stable and practical temperature range from near-ambient to high temperatures relevant for solar-thermal applications. As demonstrated in Fig. 4, starting at 373 K (Fig. 4a), the absorber achieves a high total emissivity (εtotal) of 98.57%. Its thermal emission spectrum is already concentrated in the infrared region, showing significant spectral overlap with the ideal blackbody profile. At 500 K (Fig. 4b), the total emissivity increases to 98.89%, and the spectrum begins to broaden. As the temperature rises to 1000 K (Fig. 4c), the spectrum expands dramatically toward shorter wavelengths, developing strong emission peaks in the visible and near-infrared range while maintaining an outstanding emissivity of 99.42%.

At 1500 K (Fig. 4d), the performance reaches a peak emissivity of 99.5%, with the emission curve exhibiting near-perfect spectral overlap with blackbody radiation. Notably, the fundamental broadband and high-efficiency trend is rooted in the multi-resonant, robust nature of the metamaterial design, which relies on spectral overlap of multiple modes rather than a single sharp resonance. This design philosophy offers inherent resilience against performance degradation that might arise from increased material damping at high temperatures. Furthermore, 1500 K represents a practical upper operating limit for the current TiN/SiO2-based material stack, considering the thermal stability of the components, particularly the SiO2 dielectric spacer. To realize operation approaching or exceeding 2000 K, future material optimization would necessitate replacing SiO2 with an ultra-high-temperature dielectric (e.g., HfO2, AlN) within the same structural framework.

These results collectively affirm that the SHCDR-MA, based on its electromagnetic design, functions as a highly stable and temperature-invariant quasi-ideal blackbody across a broad and practical operational range (from ~ 373 K to 1500 K), highlighting its great potential for applications in high-precision radiation thermometry and high-temperature solar-thermal energy conversion. For accurate performance prediction at a specific high operating temperature, future work should integrate experimentally measured or theoretically validated temperature-dependent optical constants of TiN into the model.

Mechanisms for the spectrally-selective characteristics

A comprehensive examination of the underlying physical principles governing the proposed architecture was conducted, focusing on the mechanisms that amplify absorption through detailed electromagnetic field analysis. Figure 5 systematically elucidates the spectral migration of electromagnetic energy confinement in SHCDR-MA through coupled Poynting vector trajectories and magnetic field (|H|2) distributions, at the wavelengths of five absorption peaks: (a) 345 nm, (b) 485 nm, (c) 730 nm, (d) 1800 nm, and (e) 2400 nm. The selection of these spectral points enables a systematic evaluation of absorption trends as the wavelength increases, offering valuable insights into the absorber’s performance across a broad spectral range. This methodology aligns with our objective to explore the intrinsic absorption properties of the structure under study. Each subplot meticulously maps the spatial configuration of the magnetic field within the x-z plane, revealing its behavior at varying spectral intervals. The structure was subjected to normally incident light, with the electric field vector oriented along the x-axis, indicative of a TM polarization mode. The magnetic field distributions were depicted using contour plots, augmented by black arrows that signify the Poynting vector, thereby providing a clear visualization of energy flow dynamics and field distribution patterns.

At the shorter wavelength of 345 nm and 485 nm (Fig. 5a,b), most of energy fluxes dominantly penetrate the TiN inner ring cavity, as evidenced by the dense convergence of poynting vectors toward the central void region (black arrows). The remaining energy distributes through inter-ring gaps and inter-unit nanogaps, and is ultimately absorbed by the sidewalls of both concentric rings. Meanwhile, magnetic hotspots concentrate on upper segments of dual TiN rings, confirming ultraviolet-short wavelength visible absorption is dominated by TiN interband transitions.

When increasing to 730 nm (Fig. 5c), partial energy persists in accessing the inner ring cavity, while major energy fluxes redirect toward the inter-ring gaps, ultimately absorbed by inner ring sidewalls (arrow density analysis confirms this). The distribution of the normalized magnetic field intensity (|H|2) splits into three enhanced zones: crown region of inner cavity, upper outer-wall of inner ring, and SiO2 dielectric layers between adjacent units, indicating visible absorption via hybrid cavity-edge modes.

A critical transition occurs at an intermediate wavelength of 1800 nm (Fig. 5d) where energy flow completely terminates inner ring cavity penetration, contrasting sharply with 485 nm behavior. Instead, two distinct absorption pathways are observed: part of flux impinges on the inner ring’s outer wall (lower segment absorption) and part of flux bypasses the outer ring, passes through the bottom SiO2 dielectric, and ultimately absorbed by inner ring’s terminal edge. The |H|2 distribution also changes accordingly—becoming concentrated within the outer side of the inner ring and the SiO2-filled cavity base. Dielectric-loaded surface waveguide effects, alongside the excitation of magnetic resonance within the SiO2 layer, enhance the mid-infrared absorption, demonstrating efficient infrared energy funneling.

At the 2400 nm absorption peak (Fig. 5e), energy flows follow three key paths: along the outer edges of both metallic rings; through the bottom SiO2 layer; and ultimately absorbed at the inner ring’s edge. The magnetic energy (|H|2) is strongly concentrated within the SiO2 layer, confirming two absorption mechanisms: a substrate-coupled Fabry-Pérot resonance and a magnetic resonance activated within the SiO2 dielectric layer. As wavelengths increase from 385 nm to 2400 nm, the absorption mechanism shifts gradually: it starts with TiN interband transitions at short wavelengths and transitions to dielectric-guided, cavity-enhanced modes with strong magnetic activity at long wavelengths at long wavelengths. This smooth transition across wavelengths enables broadband absorption through carefully designed impedance matching gradients in the structure.

To provide rigorous, vector-field evidence beyond qualitative field plots and to clearly identify the resonant mechanisms, we conducted a complementary analysis of the in-plane displacement current and out-of-plane magnetic field distributions in the XY-plane at the key absorption wavelengths of 730, 1800, and 2400 nm, as shown in Fig. 6. This analysis reveals the spectral evolution of localized magnetic dipole activity.

At 730 nm (Fig. 6a), within a plane intersecting the upper section of the nanostructure (z = 345 nm), the displacement current density exhibits symmetric, counter-rotating vortex-like patterns confined within both the inner and outer TiN rings. The out-of-plane magnetic field component, Hz shows corresponding enhancement. The spatial correlation between the vortex centers and regions of strong Hz field gradients visually confirms the Jd → Hz source-field relationship, signifying an excited magnetic dipole character. Synthesized with the dominant energy flow and field profiles in the XZ-plane (Fig. 5c)—characteristic of hybridized localized surface plasmon resonances (LSPR)—this indicates that the resonant mode at 730 nm possesses a discernible magnetic dipole oscillation accompanying the primary plasmonic response.

At 1800 nm (Fig. 6b), shifting to a plane near the base of the structure (z = 65 nm), the dominant electromagnetic activity transitions. The displacement current forms a pronounced vortex pattern primarily localized within the outer TiN ring. The Hz field distribution shows a strong, co-located extremum. This clear “current-loop-enclosing-magnetic-hotspot” topology provides definitive evidence for a localized magnetic dipole resonance strongly associated with the outer ring at this wavelength.

At 2400 nm (Fig. 6c), within the same base plane (z = 65 nm), the locus of magnetic activity shifts inward. A dominant, symmetric displacement current vortex is now established within the inner TiN ring, with its center again locked to the sign-reversal region of the Hz field gradient. This provides unambiguous vector-field evidence for a prominent magnetic dipole resonance localized at the inner ring.

Combined with the vertical magnetic energy confinement observed in XZ-plane views (consistent with substrate-coupled Fabry–Pérot (FP) cavity modes), this XY-plane vector analysis elucidates the in-plane oscillatory character of these vertical cavity modes. The absorption mechanism thus evolves spectrally: from a synergy of LSPR with a secondary magnetic dipole character at 730 nm, to FP cavity modes dominated by strong magnetic dipole resonances localized successively in the outer (1800 nm) and inner (2400 nm) rings. This analysis quantitatively establishes the role of wavelength-selective, localized magnetic dipole resonances in achieving ultra-broadband absorption.

The spatial evolution of normalized electric field intensity (|E|2) distributions across the SHCDR-MA structure under varying illumination wavelengths reveals distinct absorption mechanisms and synergistic coupling effects. At the absorption peaks of 345 nm and 485 nm illumination (Fig. 8a,b), weak field enhancement occurs due to TiN’s inherent optical properties below its plasma frequency (λ < 500 nm) [15–17], where the real part of its dielectric function remains positive. Transitioning to 730 nm (Fig. 8c), pronounced confinement of the electric field is observed in the upper and middle regions of the inner ring, while the exterior surfaces of both rings exhibit a moderate enhancement in field intensity—a characteristic signature of localized surface plasmon resonance (LSPR) activation, enabled by TiN’s negative permittivity regime.

The wavelength-dependent LSPR behavior exhibits enhanced coupling at 1800 nm (Fig. 8d), where complete coverage of the inner ring’s outer wall coincides with supplementary field concentration along the upper interior of the outer ring. This configuration facilitates progressive plasmonic hybridization. The magnetic field distribution extends across adjacent unit cells, indicating the emergence of propagating surface plasmon resonance (PSPR) [51–53]. At 2400 nm (Fig. 8e), the absorption mechanism undergoes a fundamental transition as PSPR becomes the dominant phenomenon. Intense field confinement is localized within inter-unit nanogaps along the outer ring periphery, and these PSPR-induced hotspots propagate across adjacent unit cells via long-range plasmonic coupling [51–53]. This spatial extension of electromagnetic hotspots enables collective resonance behavior across the metasurface.

This spectral progression from LSPR to PSPR dominance is associated with absorption bandwidth expansion, facilitated by three synergistic mechanisms: (1) optimized impedance matching through multi-scale geometric design, (2) intrinsic ohmic losses from TiN’s interband transitions and electron-phonon scattering [15–17], and (3) coherent superposition of magnetic resonances with both LSPR and PSPR modes. The resulting broadband absorption enhancement originates from electromagnetic energy being funneled into the concentric ring cavities, where cascaded plasmonic resonances efficiently convert incident photons into thermal energy via multiple reflections and interference. This energy is then dissipated through thermal channels, achieving enhanced broadband absorption.

Considerations on localized heating and structural stability

The strong electromagnetic field localization within the TiN nanostructures, particularly at the edges and gaps of the concentric rings, leads to enhanced local power dissipation (hotspots). A relevant consideration is the potential impact of this localized plasmonic heating on the nanoscale structural stability under high-temperature operation. The proposed design is inherently robust for two primary reasons. First, the active material, TiN, is a refractory ceramic with an exceptionally high melting point (~ 2940 °C) and excellent chemical stability, making it fundamentally resistant to shape deformation or degradation at the operating temperatures envisaged for solar-thermophotovoltaic applications (typically up to 1500–000 K). Second, the continuous TiN substrate provides an effective lateral heat conduction path, mitigating extreme local temperature gradients. The maintained near-ideal optical and thermal emission performance at a uniform temperature of 1500 K, as shown in Fig. 4, serves as a consistent indicator of the structural integrity of the design in the simulated thermal state. While a detailed thermomechanical analysis is beyond the scope of this work, the combination of refractory material selection and validated high-temperature performance strongly supports the operational stability of the SHCDR-MA.

Thickness-dependent absorption modulation in dielectric spacer layer

Figure 9b clearly demonstrates systematic variations in both peak positions and intensities as the SiO2 insulating layer thickness increases from 40 nm to 90 nm. The most prominent feature is the progressive red-shift exhibited by all five absorption peaks with increasing thickness. In the UV-visible region (150–750 nm), thinner SiO2 layers (40–60 nm) maintain higher absorption rates, while thicknesses ≥ 70 nm develop distinct absorption dips whose depths intensify with thickness. Notably, the first three absorption peaks show minimal intensity variations (< 0.3%), whereas the fourth and fifth peaks in the long-wave infrared region display initial enhancement followed by attenuation. Within the transitional 0.7–1.25 μm band, a characteristic absorption dip emerges with its absorption rate monotonically increasing across the 40–90 nm thickness range. Particularly intriguing is the non-monotonic behavior observed in the 1250–2400 nm band: absorption initially improves from 40 to 60 nm but deteriorates from 60 to 90 nm, only to resume its increasing trend beyond 2400 nm. These phenomena correlate strongly with the enhanced magnetic field concentration in the bottom SiO2 layer observed in Fig. 5. Comprehensive analysis reveals that the 60 nm SiO2 thickness achieves optimal broadband absorption (0.15–3 μm) while preserving high UV-visible performance, whereas either thinner or thicker layers lead to spectral-selective degradation.

Thickness-dependent absorption modulation via metallic grating structures

Figure 9c distinctly demonstrates the modulation effects of TiN outer ring thickness (t2) on absorption characteristics in SHCDR-MA structure. Across the UV-visible spectrum (150–750 nm), the positions and intensities of the first three absorption peaks remain remarkably stable regardless of thickness variations, indicating superior thickness insensitivity. The characteristic absorption dip near 600 nm exhibits pronounced thickness dependence, showing an initial increase in absorption rate followed by a subsequent decrease as t2 increases from 230 nm to 350 nm. In the infrared region (> 750 nm), more complex nonlinear responses emerge: both the fourth/fifth absorption peaks and their transitional zones show enhanced absorption when t2 ranges 230–290 nm, but drop 1–3% at 310–350 nm thicknesses, revealing that excessively thick TiN rings may reduce infrared absorption. Notably, the fifth peak undergoes about 400 nm red-shift with increasing thickness, which expands long-wave bandwidth at the cost of reduced overall absorption intensity. Comprehensive analysis identifies 290 nm as the optimal thickness that achieves the best balance - maintaining stable UV-visible absorption while delivering > 98% high-absorption platforms in key infrared bands (1.5–2.8 μm), providing definitive guidance for broadband absorber optimization.

Figure 9d reveals that the first, second, fourth, and fifth absorption peaks maintain highly stable positions and intensities across all thickness variations. As the TiN inner ring thickness (t3) increases, the third peak exhibits a significant redshift (~ 200 nm) while retaining its peak intensity. The spectral region between the second and third peaks shows progressive absorption reduction with increasing t3, developing a distinct valley with minimum absorption reaching 0.97. Conversely, the region between the third and fourth peaks demonstrates gradual absorption enhancement with thickness. Beyond 1.4 μm wavelength, absorption rates remain unaffected by t3 variations, demonstrating exceptional thickness insensitivity in long-wavelength infrared regions. This observation aligns with prior findings confirming the dominant role of lower concentric double-ring structures in governing long-wavelength absorption behavior. When the thickness is 470 nm, the system achieves optimal performance.

Furthermore, an advanced solar energy harvester should exhibit omnidirectional performance stability and polarization-insensitive behavior, ensuring maximum photon collection efficiency throughout the entire solar spectral range. For real-world implementation, solar irradiance exhibits random spatial distribution and non-normalized incidence on absorber surfaces. Consequently, maintaining consistent photon capture efficiency across varying illumination angles becomes essential for optimizing solar energy collection. The angular-dependent optical performance of our designed absorber is systematically evaluated through spectral analysis at oblique incidence up to 80° for both TM polarized and TE polarized light, as presented in Fig. 11a, b. Specifically, the device achieves a solar absorption efficiency of 96.1% for TM polarization and 93% for TE polarization even at a large incidence angle of 60°. This quantitatively confirms the exceptional suitability of our design for real-world solar applications where sunlight is rarely at normal incidence.

This robust angular tolerance originates from the synergistic interplay of three key design features. First, the perfectly concentric and rotationally symmetric geometry ensures polarization independence and invariance to the azimuthal angle of incidence. Second, the broadband absorption is governed by hybridized, vertically-confined resonant modes (e.g., gap plasmons and dielectric-guided modes), which couple effectively to the normal component of the incident field and exhibit broad spectral overlap. This multi-resonant nature compensates for the gradual reduction in coupling strength at any single wavelength as the angle increases. Third, the stepped-height profile creates a graded effective index, promoting broad-angle impedance matching and suppressing reflection at oblique incidences. This exceptional angular tolerance stems from the unique cavity resonance effects generated within the nanostructured cavities, enabling stable performance under diverse solar illumination conditions.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we have numerically demonstrated a SHCDR-MA that achieves an average absorptivity of 99.27% across an ultra-broadband spectrum from 150 to 3000 nm. The exceptional performance stems from the synergistic interplay of multiple physically tailored mechanisms. The height difference between concentric rings establishes a vertical impedance gradient, enabling smooth spectral coupling, while the absorption mechanism transitions coherently from TiN interband transitions at shorter wavelengths to localized and propagating surface plasmon resonances combined with dielectric-guided modes at visible and infrared wavelengths. This multi-resonance synergy, along with efficient energy funneling and dissipation via magnetic hotspots and Poynting vector convergence within the ring cavities and dielectric layer, ensures continuous broadband absorption. Furthermore, the absorber exhibits excellent angular independence (up to 60°) and near-ideal, temperature-invariant blackbody-like emission (εtotal > 98.57% from 373 to1500 K). By leveraging multi-resonance coupling and impedance matching engineering, our TiN-based SHCDR-MA presents a feasible and effective strategy for advanced solar energy harvesting and high-temperature solar thermophotovoltaic applications.
