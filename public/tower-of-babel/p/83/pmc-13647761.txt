Exploring the association between women’s empowerment and household dietary diversity in rural Armenia: a cross-sectional analysis
Authors: Narges Ebadi, Davod Ahmadi, Elise Carbonneau
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWomen’s empowerment plays a critical role in improving household dietary diversity. Empowered women have greater control over resources, information, and decision-making regarding nutrition, leading to healthier dietary practices for their families. This study investigates the association between women’s empowerment and household dietary diversity in rural Vayots Dzor, Armenia.MethodsUsing Oxfam database, a cross-sectional analysis utilized data from 350 women residing in the Vayots Dzor region. Women’s empowerment was assessed across multiple dimensions: social capital, economic empowerment, information and communication technology access, decision-making power, attitudes towards gender roles and leadership, and public event participation. Household dietary diversity was measured using an adapted Household Dietary Diversity Score, which encompasses six key food groups. Descriptive statistics and binomial regression models were employed to analyze the data and identify relationships between variables.ResultsFindings from the regression analyses showed a positive association between women’s empowerment dimensions and household dietary diversity. The adjusted regression model indicated that women with low social capital (OR = 0.409; 95% CI = 0.235–0.713) had low household dietary diversity score. Additionally, shared decision-making within families increased the likelihood of high diversity (OR = 2.481; 95% CI = 1.143–5.384).ConclusionsThis study underscores the need for multifaceted approaches to women’s empowerment to support improved household dietary outcomes. While causal inference is limited by the cross-sectional design, the findings suggest that policies integrating women’s participation in household decision-making with broader social and economic support mechanisms may help enhance dietary diversity in rural settings. Further research is needed to evaluate context-specific empowerment interventions and their pathways to improving food security and gender equality.Keywords: Women’s empowerment, Household dietary diversity, Gender roles, Armenia

Introduction

Women’s empowerment is increasingly recognized as a cornerstone in efforts to improve household dietary diversity and overall nutrition [1–3]. Empowerment, in this context, refers to the process by which women’s inherent rights to make strategic life choices are affirmed, often overcoming previous limitations on their autonomy [4]. This recognition of their decision-making power and control over their own lives is crucial not only for individual well-being [5] but also for the health [6] and nutrition of their families [7].

Women’s empowerment, encompassing resources, agency, and achievements [8], influences household dietary diversity and food security through several pathways [9, 10]. The Resources–Agency–Achievement framework provides a useful analytical lens. Access to economic resources, such as participation in income-generating activities and control over financial resources, can enhance women’s ability to purchase a wider variety of nutritious foods and reduce dependence on staple-based diets. Women’s agency, particularly involvement in household decision-making, shapes food purchasing decisions, meal composition, and intra-household food allocation, often prioritizing dietary quality and the nutritional needs of household members. In addition, social capital and access to information, including through information and communication technologies (ICT), can improve nutrition knowledge, market awareness, and food-related practices, further supporting more diverse diets. Collectively, these resources strengthen women’s agency and contribute to improved household dietary diversity and food security [11–20].

According to the FAO [21], household dietary diversity (HDD) is defined as a qualitative measure of food consumption that reflects household access to a variety of foods and serves as a proxy for the nutrient adequacy of the diet of individuals. The HDD captures the variety of food groups consumed by a household over a specified period, typically 24 h, and is used to gauge the economic ability of the household to access diverse foods. This variety is a key indicator of not just food security, but also nutrition and overall health [22, 23]. Based on Eat-Lancet commission report, healthy diets have an optimal caloric intake and consist largely of a diversity of plant-based foods, low amounts of animal source foods, contain unsaturated rather than saturated fats, and limited amounts of refined grains, highly processed foods and added sugars [24].

The link between women’s empowerment and dietary diversity arises from the critical role women play in household food security, which includes food production, procurement, preparation, and distribution. Research shows that empowered women tend to have more control over household resources, better access to information and services, and a stronger role in decision-making, resulting in more diverse diets and improved nutrition for all family members [25–28]. In Armenia, gender-based inequalities in agriculture pose significant barriers to women’s empowerment, restricting their access to land, credit, and agricultural inputs. Nevertheless, enhancing women’s empowerment remains essential for improving household food security and nutrition, as evidence indicates that equitable access to resources and decision-making power significantly improves household health and well-being [29].

Building on this broader empowerment–nutrition link, decision-making within the household emerges as a key mechanism through which women influence dietary outcomes. Studies show that households where women control food purchases, meal planning, and resource allocation tend to have more diverse and nutritious diets [30, 31]. For example, in a study by Cunningham et al., found a positive correlation between women’s decision-making and dietary diversity in sub-Saharan Africa [32]. Women involved in household decisions are more likely to include a wider range of food groups, enhancing diet diversity. Positive attitudes towards gender equality and leadership further empower women [33]. Women who advocate for equality are more likely to support their own and their children’s nutritional needs, resulting in a more varied diet [34, 35]. Women’s participation in community meetings and agricultural training provides valuable knowledge and support for improving household dietary diversity [36–40]. This engagement enables informed food choices.

However, the relationship between decision-making power and dietary choices differs across regions. In Western contexts, women frequently shoulder the primary responsibility for selecting, purchasing, and preparing food, often alongside full-time employment. This dual responsibility highlights the persistence of traditional gender roles, which, while less explicitly stated, continue to shape expectations about food-related activities. Research shows that women often justify their disproportionate role by emphasizing the need to safeguard family health, maintain high standards, or avoid family conflict by “just doing it themselves” [41]. This unequal distribution of caregiving tasks reinforces gendered expectations and adds to the psychological and emotional workload on women, contributing to stress and mental strain [42]. The continued impact of these roles suggests a need for more equitable caregiving distribution to reduce the burden on women and promote better well-being.

Empowering women is crucial for securing a nutritious future for families. Research links women’s control over resources and decision-making with a greater variety of healthy foods consumed [43, 44]. Women’s multifaceted role in food security involves acquiring, preparing, and distributing food [45]. Empowerment enhances their access to information on healthy eating and strengthens their decision-making role [46], leading to better health outcomes [47]. Education also plays a key role; educated mothers are more likely to adopt healthy practices and seek medical care [48]. Additionally, empowering women helps protect them from domestic violence, which can negatively impact nutritional status [11]. Empowered women are better equipped to make informed choices that improve family health and well-being.

While existing research, based on the studies mentioned above, consistently shows positive associations between women’s empowerment and dietary diversity, empirical evidence remains heavily concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, with limited attention to post-Soviet and middle-income rural contexts. This study addresses this gap by examining the empowerment–dietary diversity relationship in rural Vayots Dzor, Armenia. It contributes to the literature by providing rare evidence from a setting like Vayots Dzor by applying a multidimensional empowerment framework to identify which specific empowerment dimensions are most strongly associated with dietary diversity; and by highlighting the importance of shared household decision-making, offering nuanced insights into intra-household power dynamics beyond individual autonomy. Addressing gender inequalities is crucial for advancing food security and sustainable development in Armenia [49].

Methodology

Study region

Vayots Dzor is a rural region in southern Armenia. The region’s economy is heavily reliant on subsistence farming, with most of the population engaged in agricultural activities, particularly in horticulture and crop production. Socio-economic conditions for entire population are modest, with limited access to markets, financial resources, and economic opportunities. Geographic isolation further hinders economic development, as many communities are distant from main roads and larger economic centers, making it difficult for farmers to sell their produce and access services. Despite the region’s agricultural potential, these factors contribute to persistent economic difficulties for many households [50].

Data sources

This study utilizes data from a prior intervention conducted among women’s cooperatives in rural Vayots Dzor, Armenia, accessible through the UK Data Service. The dataset, compiled by Oxfam, provides detailed insights into women’s empowerment, encompassing various dimensions, household characteristics, and dietary diversity. This makes it well-suited for examining the natural relationships among these factors within this population. The household survey included 107 women who participated in the project and 350 women from a comparison group. While the project concluded in 2013, the evaluation was conducted in November 2015, two years after its completion [50]. Propensity score matching was conducted as part of the original intervention evaluation to construct a comparison group that closely resembled the intervention group in baseline characteristics. The present study uses only the matched comparison group to examine the natural association between women’s empowerment and household dietary diversity, independent of program exposure. No treatment effects are estimated. Trained enumerators administered the questionnaire on behalf of Oxfam UK ensuring data integrity and ethical participant engagement.

Sample size

Because the data were collected in 2015, the findings may not reflect more recent political and economic changes in Armenia; however, the study provides valuable baseline evidence on empowerment–nutrition pathways in a post-Soviet rural context. The analysis is based solely on the matched comparison group (n = 350), which was constructed as part of the original intervention evaluation using propensity score matching to resemble intervention villages in terms of socio-economic characteristics, climate, and agricultural production. The present study intentionally restricts the analysis to this non-intervention group in order to examine the natural association between women’s empowerment and household dietary diversity without the influence of program exposure. Prior to analysis, the data were carefully screened for quality. No missing values or influential outliers were detected. Outcome variable (s).

To assess household dietary diversity within the studied households in rural Vayots-Dzor this research employed the adapted Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS). Due to limitations in the number of unique food items captured during data collection, the standard HDDS, which utilizes 10 food groups as outlined by the Food and Agriculture Organization [21, 51], was adapted to a 6-item scale focusing on the sixth key food groups (eaten) in the last 7 days: cereals (rice, buckwheat), vegetables (tomato, cucumber), meat (pork, beef, chicken), eggs (analyzed separately for a more detailed protein source analysis), pulses (lentils) and sugar. The adapted HDDS still provides a valuable assessment for exploring the associations between women’s empowerment and dietary patterns within this specific context.

The categorization of the dietary diversity scores into two ranges—0 to 3 and 4 to 6—was implemented to effectively distinguish between households with lower and higher levels of dietary diversity. The lower range (0 to 3) captures households with fewer food groups, potentially indicating limited access to a variety of foods, while the higher range (4 to 6) reflects households with greater dietary diversity, suggesting better access to a diverse array of foods. This distinction is crucial for understanding how varying levels of dietary diversity are associated with indicators of women’s empowerment, such as decision-making power and economic opportunities. By simplifying the data into these categories, the study enhances the ability to detect meaningful differences and assess whether improvements in dietary diversity correlate with increased empowerment. This approach aligns with the research objectives and provides a practical framework for interpreting how access to diverse food groups impacts women’s well-being in rural Vayots Dzor.

Controlling factors

In addition to key independent variables, a range of socio-demographic factors were included in the multivariable models to control for potential confounding. The selection of these characteristics was carefully guided by existing theory and prior research. These factors were selected based on prior literature. While no formal directed acyclic graph (DAG) was constructed, the selection was theory-informed and grounded in existing empirical studies to improve model specification and interpretability.

Household assets

To assess access to essential amenities within the household, the study employed a survey question about ownership of six key items. Participants indicated whether they owned washing machines, refrigerators, gas boilers, gas stoves along with transportation options like cars and ownership of private land. These responses were then combined to create a new variable range from 0 to 6. It is categorized as “Low” (3 or fewer items) or “High” (4 or more items) asset ownership. Households classified as having a “Low” ownership were given a score of 0, while those classified as “High” scored 1.

Livestock

To assess livestock ownership, the study employed a straightforward approach. Participants were asked if they owned any cattle, sheep, goats, poultry, or donkeys, which are commonly owned animal in Armenia. Each animal type received a binary code: “0” for no ownership and “1” for owning at least one. By summing these binary scores, the researchers created a single index reflecting the overall variety of livestock owned by each participant. This score was then categorized into three levels: low (0–1), moderate (2), and high (3–5) ownership.

Household size

Originally measured by a variable ranging from 1 to more than 9, was recoded into a new variable with three categories. This recoding aimed to simplify the data and potentially highlight specific household composition patterns. Households with 1 to 3 members were assigned a score of 2, households with 4 to 6 members were assigned a score of 1, and households with 7 or more members were assigned a score of 0.

The type of toilet facility in the household was assessed using the question, “Where is your main toilet located in the house today?” with response options including “inside” and “outside.”

In addition to household-related factors, women’s marital status and education levels were included in the descriptive analyses. Marital status was assessed using the question, “What is your marital status?” with response options including Married, Cohabitant/Common Law, Single, and Widowed/Divorced. Education levels were evaluated through the question, “What is the highest grade you completed?” with options such as Middle School (5–9 grades), High School (10–12 grades), Initial Professional Education, and Intermediate Professional Education.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS version 24. This study utilizes a multi-pronged approach to data analysis, employing descriptive statistics, bi-variate analyses, and binomial regression models. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize and understand the distribution of all key variables in the study. This provides a foundational understanding of the data for the dependent variable (household dietary diversity score), independent variable (women’s empowerment dimensions), and the control variables (household size, household assets, etc.). Frequencies were calculated to explore the distribution of categorical variables, such as the prevalence of different levels of women’s empowerment or dietary diversity within the sample. To examine the specific effect of women’s empowerment on dietary diversity while adjusting the potential confounding factors, two Binary logistic regression models will be employed. The first model was unadjusted and assessed the relationship between each individual women’s empowerment dimension and dietary diversity. The second model was adjusted, controlling for socio-economic factors such as household size, household assets, and livestock, to examine the association between women’s empowerment dimensions and dietary diversity independent of these potential confounders.

Ethics and privacy

In adherence to ethical data collection practices, all information that could directly identify participants, such as names and phone numbers, was removed from the dataset. Potentially identifying variables were either recoded, like household size being capped at nine, or excluded entirely, such as religion or specific events recalled. We also took particular care to protect the privacy of respondents by removing any data concerning experiences with or opinions on gender-based violence, acknowledging the sensitive nature of this topic. Through these anonymization techniques, we ensured the confidentiality of participants while maintaining the integrity of the data for meaningful analysis.

The adjusted model (Table 6) identified three key factors significantly associated with HDD. Women with no social capital (OR = 0.409; 95% CI = 0.235–0.713) were significantly more likely to experience low HDD. Notably, the analysis also revealed significant associations between women’s decision-making power and HDD. Compared to women with sole decision-making power (the reference group), women with no decision-making power (OR = 2.253; 95% CI = 1.056–4.809) and those with joint decision-making power (OR = 2.481; 95% CI = 1.143–5.384) were more likely to have higher HDD. The stronger odds ratio observed for the joint decision-making power group suggests that shared power may have a more positive relationship with dietary diversity than either sole or no decision-making power. Additionally, living in a larger household (7 or more members) was associated with higher HDD (OR = 2.649; 95% CI = 1.102–6.369).

Discussion

The primary objective of this study was to examine the relationship between women’s empowerment (WE) and household dietary diversity (HDD) in in rural Vayots Dzor, Armenia.

The results indicate that most households consumed foods from 4 to 6 food groups in the past week, corresponding to a higher household dietary diversity score. These results align with broader trends of improving food security, particularly in rural areas of Armenia, as highlighted by prior study [52]. This research highlights significant advancements in agricultural production and food self-sufficiency in Armenia between 2017 and 2021, which have enhanced the availability of locally produced food and reduced poverty in rural communities. In contrast to findings by Kumar and Gautam in India and Nupo et al. in Nigeria, which reported limited diets in rural areas, these results underscore the pivotal role of targeted agricultural and economic policies in driving food security improvements and addressing persistent gaps in dietary diversity [53, 54].

In the present study, social and economic factors like social capital and economic empowerment are all linked to a more diverse diet. This investigation takes a distinct approach by directly examining the differential association of social capital and economic empowerment on dietary diversity within Armenian communities. This study reinforces the established link between economic empowerment and dietary diversity, aligning with previous research by Bidisha et al. in Bangladesh and Mbiro & Ndlovu in Zimbabwe [55, 56]. Economic empowerment was not significant in the adjusted models. However, a significant association was observed between borrowing from informal sources, such as friends, and the variety of foods consumed by households, underscoring the direct impact of informal borrowing on dietary diversity. This finding highlights the critical role informal borrowing plays in mitigating food insecurity, even after accounting for other factors in the adjusted analysis. Additionally, in some cases, these borrowed funds are directed toward agricultural inputs, which may contribute to improving long-term food security [57].

This study found no significant association between ICT ownership and HDD among women in Armenian households, likely due to contextual factors. While ICT has the potential to enhance food security and dietary outcomes by providing agricultural knowledge, market information, and health advice, its impact in rural Armenia is limited by barriers such as low internet access, digital illiteracy, and inadequate infrastructure [58, 59]. Additionally, the influence of ICT on dietary diversity may be mediated by socioeconomic status, cultural norms, and access to diverse foods. Research suggests that ICT’s effects on food systems depend on broader socio-economic improvements, which might explain the absence of a direct relationship in this study [60].

Results emphasize the role of both limited decision-making power and shared decision-making within households in achieving dietary diversity. Shared decision-making aligns with findings by Ogutu et al., in India which suggest that when women have greater influence in household decisions, whether independently or jointly, it enhances the Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS) [61]. Similarly, Bai et al. [62]. Demostatrated that joint decision making on food purchases has a more significant positive impact on dietary diversity. In Malawi, households that shared decision-making over agricultural income also reported higher dietary diversity [63].

This study found that women with no or sole decision-making power sometimes had greater dietary diversity compared to those with sole authority. This counterintuitive finding may be influenced by broader household or community dynamics rather than individual empowerment alone. In rural contexts, sole decision-making authority does not necessarily indicate empowerment; it may instead reflect female-headed households or the absence of a male earner, circumstances often associated with economic vulnerability and limited access to diverse foods [64, 65]. Conversely, joint decision-making may capture resource pooling and cooperative household strategies, which can better support dietary diversity. Previous research in rural Nigeria, for instance, suggests that seasonal food availability, local agricultural practices, and household norms can overshadow individual agency in shaping dietary outcomes. In some cases, women with limited decision-making power may still benefit from shared household resources or community-level food systems that ensure access to diverse foods, highlighting the importance of structural and environmental factors in determining dietary diversity [19].

According to the findings, households with more persons reported having high HDD. In rural West Bengal, India, Sarkar found that larger households (more than eight members) reported higher dietary diversity compared to smaller ones [66]. However, other studies, like Anarfi and Ahiadeke in Ghana, suggest that increasing household size can lead to more mouths to feed and higher food expenditure, potentially reducing the quality, quantity, and variety of food consumed [67]. This difference in findings highlights the need to consider contextual factors. Resource availability, economic status, and cultural practices might influence the relationship between household size and dietary diversity across different settings. Future research could explore these factors and how household composition (age distribution, dependency ratios) and measurement methods might affect the observed associations.

The lack of a significant association between livestock ownership and household dietary diversity may stem from several factors. Livestock might be viewed primarily as financial assets rather than sources of food, with their products sold instead of consumed in the time of financial needs [68, 69]. Or, cultural or dietary preferences may reduce the consumption of livestock products, even when owned [70]. Gender dynamics play a role, as limited decision-making power of women might hinder the effective use of livestock for improving diets [70].

The findings from this study directly align with several Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) [71], particularly SDG 2 (Zero Hunger) and SDG 5 (Gender Equality). SDG 2 aims to end hunger and ensure access to safe, nutritious, and sufficient food for all, while SDG 5 focuses on achieving gender equality and empowering all women and girls. This study highlights the critical role of women’s empowerment in improving household dietary diversity, a key indicator of food security. By showing that increased social capital and shared decision-making power among women are associated with more diverse and nutritious diets, the study underscores how empowering women can contribute to reducing hunger and malnutrition. Furthermore, the research reinforces the importance of integrating gender equality into nutrition and food security interventions, aligning with the broader goals of promoting social and economic inclusivity under the SDGs. These findings suggest that targeted efforts to empower women in rural, underserved communities can significantly advance progress toward achieving both SDG 2 and SDG 5 [72].

Strengths of the study

One of the key strengths of this study is its focus on a rural, underserved population in Vayots Dzor, Armenia, providing valuable insights into the relationship between women’s empowerment and household dietary diversity in a unique socio-economic and cultural context. The use of a comprehensive women’s empowerment framework, which includes dimensions such as social capital, economic empowerment, and decision-making power, allows for a more nuanced understanding of how different aspects of empowerment influence dietary outcomes. This careful methodological approach strengthens the validity of the study’s conclusions.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the use of an adapted Household Dietary Diversity Score (HDDS), which reduces the original 12 food groups to a 6-point scale and omits categories such as cheese, oils/fats, and fish/seafood, may underestimate true dietary diversity, particularly among households that rely on these food groups. Second, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, as the analysis captures associations at a single point in time rather than dynamic relationships over the life course. Third, the reliance on self-reported data may introduce recall or social desirability bias in reporting both food consumption and empowerment dimensions. Although the analysis controls for several socio-economic characteristics, unobserved factors—such as cultural norms, intra-household food allocation practices, or localized food availability—may also influence the observed associations. Fourth, the findings are not generalizable to women who are not in cooperatives. Finally, the sample is based on a matched comparison group originally constructed for an intervention evaluation. While this design enhances internal consistency by reducing heterogeneity across rural contexts, it limits statistical generalizability to all rural women in Vayots Dzor. The findings should therefore be interpreted as analytically generalizable, offering insights into empowerment–nutrition pathways within comparable rural and post-Soviet settings rather than population-wide estimates. Future research using representative and longitudinal data, as well as more comprehensive dietary diversity measures, would strengthen causal interpretation and external validity.

Future research

Future research should build on these findings by employing longitudinal designs to assess the long-term effects of women’s empowerment on household dietary diversity and food security. This approach would help clarify the directionality of the relationship and capture dynamic changes over time. Additionally, exploring specific empowerment interventions, such as access to credit or education, and their effectiveness in diverse cultural settings would provide more targeted policy recommendations for improving nutrition outcomes. Expanding the dietary diversity score to include a broader range of food groups, such as dairy, oils, and seafood, would allow for a more accurate and comprehensive assessment of household dietary patterns, further enhancing the relevance of future studies.

Conclusion

This study offers valuable insights into the relationship between women’s empowerment and household dietary diversity in rural Vayots Dzor, Armenia. The findings highlight the critical influence of social capital and shared decision-making in enhancing dietary outcomes. The results also revealed that women with no decision-making power exhibited better dietary diversity than those with sole decision-making power. The study also contributes to the growing body of evidence that empowering women is crucial for enhancing household food security, especially in rural and underserved populations. These findings have implications for policymakers, suggesting that interventions aimed at increasing women’s access to resources, decision-making, and leadership roles may lead to improved nutrition outcomes. Future research should aim to address the current study’s limitations by incorporating longitudinal data and a more comprehensive dietary diversity index, as well as investigating specific empowerment interventions tailored to different socio-cultural settings. Overall, this research highlights the critical need to integrate women’s empowerment strategies into public health and nutrition policies to improve food security and health outcomes in rural communities.
