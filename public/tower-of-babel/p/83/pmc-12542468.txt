Mental health disorders and determinants among adolescents living with HIV: a first national estimation in Togo (The MIND-HIV study), 2025
Authors: Sonia Kanekatoua, Arnold Junior Sadio, Fifonsi Adjidossi Gbeasor-Komlanvi, Saliou Salifou, Latifou Assikpa, Aboubakar Issa, Zakillatou Adam, Komlan Mawuko Gbetogbe, Damega Wenkourama, Charfoundine Affo, Adjévi Awoussi, Eddy Yehouenou, René Aklesso Kpeguie, Jean Marie Atsou Alley, Vincent Palokinam Pitche, Didier Koumavi Ekouevi, Simliwa Kolou Dassa, Anoumou Claver Dagnra

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDepression and anxiety are leading mental health disorders (MHD) among adolescents. Adolescents living with HIV (ALHIV) face distinct challenges such as stigma, social isolation, and psychosocial adversity, which compound their vulnerability to MHD. Despite its significance, data on MHD among ALHIV in Togo remains limited. This study aimed to provide the first national estimate of MHD prevalence and associated factors among ALHIV in Togo.MethodsA national cross-sectional study was conducted from February to March 2025 across the six health regions of Togo. A stratified multistage random sampling approach was used to recruit ALHIV aged 10–19 years from 25 health facilities. Data collection involved validated psychosocial tools, including the PHQ-9 A for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety, and ULS-8 for loneliness. For prevalence estimation, depression was dichotomized into “Yes” (PHQ-9 score ≥ 10) and “No” (PHQ score < 10). For inferential analysis, depression was categorized into three levels: absent, moderate, and severe. Ordinal and binary logistic regression models were used to identify factors associated with depression and MHD (“combined depression and anxiety”).ResultsA total of 542 ALHIV (48.5% females) were included (median age: 15 years). The prevalence of depression among ALHIV was 24.5* with moderate depression observed in 19.9% and severe depression in 4.6%. Anxiety was less prominent, with 0.4% (95%CI: 0.04–1.34%) reporting moderate to severe symptoms. ALHIV experiencing loneliness (aOR: 1.13; 95%CI: 1.06–1.20), stigma (aOR: 4.21; 95%CI: 1.79–9.88), and having coexisting chronic diseases (aOR: 4.09; 95%CI: 1.50-11.16) were more likely to be depressed. Conversely, ALHIV from households earning more than twice the minimum interprofessional wage were significantly less likely to suffer from depression (aOR: 0.25; 95%CI: 0.07–0.91). The same associations were observed for MHD.ConclusionThe study highlights a substantial mental health burden among ALHIV in Togo, with depression associated with loneliness, stigma, and comorbidities. Findings emphasize the integration of routine mental health screening into HIV care and tailoring stigma-reduction and economic-support strategies. Background

Depression is a major global public health issue, affecting more than 280 million people, according to the latest estimates by the World Health Organization (WHO) published in 2023 [1]. Adolescents aged 10 to 19 are particularly impacted. Globally, one in seven individuals in this age group is affected by mental health disorders, accounting for 15% of the overall disease burden in this group [2]. Depression, anxiety, and behavioral disorders rank among the leading causes of illness and disability in adolescents [2]. Recent data highlight that depression is now one of the top causes of years lived with disability among individuals aged 10 to 19 worldwide [2]. Epidemiological studies reveal that adolescents living with chronic diseases had a 2 to 3 times higher risk of developing depressive disorders compared to their healthy peers [3, 4]. This vulnerability is significantly increased when adolescents face additional challenges, such as managing a chronic and stigmatized condition like HIV infection [5].

In 2023, an estimated 1.5 million adolescents (aged 10–19) were living with HIV, including 140,000 new infections [6]. Sub-Saharan Africa, which accounts for more than 80% of adolescents living with HIV (ALHIV) [7], remains a region with insufficiently described mental health challenges among this population, despite extensive documentation available in a few countries. The scarcity of comprehensive data and their uneven availability across countries significantly limit access to adequate care [8]. To eventually establish complete regional estimates, it is crucial for each individual country to generate robust national data, rather than relying solely on limited sub-population or care center-specific data. Studies estimating the prevalence of depression among ALHIV reveal considerable variability, with reported rates generally ranging from 11.4% to 45.8% [9–11].

In a systematic review of studies on ALHIV (aged 10–19), published between 2008 and 2019, the prevalence of depression ranged from 14% to 26%, with girls appearing to be more affected than boys [12]. For instance, in a study conducted in Zambia, 69% of symptomatic teenagers were female [12]. These rates often surpass those observed in other studies focusing on the general adolescent population of the sub-Saharan region, which are estimated to range between 8% and 30% [13, 14]. Beyond the stigma experienced, other factors frequently associated with depression among adolescents living with HIV have been reported in the literature, such as a lack of social support and pronounced feelings of isolation [15, 16]. Additionally, the female gender has also been identified as a significant vulnerability factor for depression [17].

In Togo, where an estimated 6,000 adolescents aged 10 to 19 were living with HIV as of 2024 [18], there are no national prevalence data on depression and its associated factors within this population. Although this aspect is mentioned in the national HIV care guidelines, certain structural challenges raise concerns about its effective and efficient implementation. In Togo, there is no formal, organized network of mental health professionals to support the care of ALHIV; individual facilities attempt to secure these services, but the primary challenge remains a marked shortage of such specialists nationwide. For instance, the country has only 0.05 psychiatrists per 100,000 inhabitants, and 92% of healthcare facilities lack staff trained in adolescent mental health [19]. Within such a context, it becomes imperative to better understand the actual scope and specific determinants of depression among ALHIV in the country. This deeper understanding would enable the planning and development of locally tailored interventions aimed at significantly improving the holistic care of these particularly vulnerable youth. The aim of this study was to provide, for the first time, a national estimate of mental health disorders and their associated factors among ALHIV in Togo.

Methods

Study design and setting

A national cross-sectional survey (The MIND-HIV study) with descriptive and analytical aims was conducted from February 14 to March 14, 2025, across all six health regions of Togo. Togo is a West African country with a population currently estimated at 9,000,000 inhabitants [20], including 2,110,436 adolescents aged 10 to 19 [21]. According to UNAIDS estimates for 2023, the national HIV prevalence in the general population (15–45 years) was 1.6% [22].

Study population and sampling

The target population for the survey consisted of ALHIV in Togo. Included in the study were all individuals living with HIV who met the following criteria: (i) aged 10 to 19 years; (ii) had lived in Togo permanently during the year before the study; (iii) were being treated and monitored at one of the sites selected for the study; and (iv) had provided consent to participate, or whose parent/guardian had given consent (for minors under 18 years of age with their assent).

For the sample size calculation, a single population proportion formula with correction for finite populations was used [23]. This correction accounts for the known and relatively small size of the population from which the sample is drawn, allowing an accurate calculation of the required sample size. Based on the literature, the prevalence of depression among ALHIV in low- and middle-income countries, particularly in Africa, is estimated to be around 25% [24]. Considering the number of ALHIV aged 10 to 19 in Togo, estimated at 6,000, with a 95% confidence level, a 5% margin of error, introducing a design effect of 1.5, and accounting for 20% non-responses or unusable data (due to the sensitive nature of the studied question), a minimum of 505 adolescents living with HIV needed to be included.

A multi-stage stratified random sampling method was employed. Stratification was based on the type of health facility (public or private). The first stage involved randomly selecting the districts to be surveyed within each health region, while the second stage focused on selecting the health facilities to be surveyed. The final stage consisted of randomly selecting ALHIV. In total, 25 care sites were selected. The number of participants to be included per region and per health facility was calculated based on the relative weight of the region or health facility within the total population of adolescents under follow-up. The selection of participants at each site was conducted through simple random sampling using databases provided by Togo’s national HIV/AIDS, viral hepatitis, and sexually transmitted infections control program.

Data collection

A questionnaire was developed and validated by a multidisciplinary committee consisting of representatives from associations, psychiatrists, psychologists, epidemiologists, and leaders of the HIV program. The finalized questionnaire covered items such as: (i) Sociodemographic characteristics and medical history (age, sex, education level, psychiatric history, etc.); (ii) mental health assessment scales (PHQ-9 A for depression, GAD-7 for anxiety); (iii) isolation/loneliness (ULS-8 scale). Once approved, the questionnaire was implemented on a digital platform (KoboCollect). The surveys were conducted by healthcare professionals of the national HIV/AIDS control program, all holding at least a bachelor’s degree and having prior experience in the care of people living with HIV. The questionnaires were administered in French, and local languages. For the latter, the committee ensured consensus on the translation of each item, guaranteeing uniform understanding across all translated questions.

Operational definitions

Depression

To document depression, PHQ-9 (PHQ-9 A for under-17s) was used [25, 26]. Based on the total score across items, participants were classified as follows: (i) 0–4: minimal depression; (ii) 5–9: mild depression; (iii) 10–14: moderate depression; (iv) 15–19: moderately severe depression; and (v) 20–27: severe depression. For the overall estimation of depression prevalence, participants scoring ≥ 10 were classified as “Depression: Yes,” while those scoring below this threshold were categorized as “Depression: No.” Regarding the dependent variable, which consisted of depression categorized into three ordinal levels: (i) “Absence of depression” included the recoded modalities of minimal and mild depression; (ii) “Moderate depression” remained unchanged; (iii) “Severe depression” encompassed the recoded modalities of moderately severe and severe depression.

Anxiety

To assess anxiety, the GAD-7 scale was used [27, 28]. Scores ranged from 0 to 21, with thresholds indicating mild anxiety (5–9), moderate anxiety (10–14), and severe anxiety (15–21). Furthermore, numerous studies on adolescent anxiety rely on the GAD-7 scale due to its validation for use within this specific age group [29].

Alcohol consumption

To document alcohol consumption, the Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test - Consumption (AUDIT-C) questionnaire was used [30]. The total score ranges from 0 to 12. While cutoff thresholds exist for this score, for practical purposes, we retained it as a quantitative variable. The higher the score, the greater the level of alcohol consumption.

Loneliness

The University of California Los Angeles (UCLA) Loneliness scale (ULS-8 scale) was used to assess perceived loneliness. This scale includes questions that measure three dimensions of loneliness: relational ties, social ties, and perceived isolation. The score ranges from 8 to 32, where, the higher the score the greater the level of perceived loneliness [31, 32].

The assessment of stigma took adolescents’ experiences of stigma in the past and at the time of the survey into account.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses were performed. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and proportions, while quantitative variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges. The prevalence of depression was estimated along with its 95% confidence interval.

An ordinal logistic regression model was performed to identify factors associated with depression (categorized into three levels: absent, moderate, severe). The explanatory variables included in the model were selected based on scientific rationale and evidence from the literature. The proportional odds assumption was verified. Another model, a binary logistic regression, was computed to identify factors associated with mental health disorders overall (depression or anxiety). For this model, a composite binary variable “Mental health disorder” was created. It was assigned a value of “1” if adolescents exhibited moderate to severe depression or moderate to severe anxiety, and “0” otherwise. For all statistical tests performed, the significance threshold was set at 5%. All analyses were conducted using R software, version 4.3.2.

Ethical considerations

The present study received approval from the Bioethics and Health Research Committee of the Ministry of Health (Approval No. 047CBRS, December 5, 2024). Each participant was invited to sign an informed consent form. For participants under the age of 18, in addition to their assent, consent from a legal guardian was required. All participants identified with warning signs (notably suicidal ideation or a history of suicide attempts) received a session of attentive listening and were referred to a psychologist either onsite or near the inclusion site.

Several factors were significantly associated with a higher likelihood of experiencing depression. Adolescents with a secondary or higher education level were more likely to experience depression compared to those with a primary education (aOR = 1.85; 95% CI: 1.05–3.25). Loneliness was also a factor increasing the likelihood of experiencing depression; the greater the loneliness, the more likely the adolescent was to be depressed (aOR = 1.13; 95% CI: 1.06–1.20). The presence of other chronic diseases in the adolescents’ medical history substantially increased this likelihood, making them more likely to experience depression compared to those without such conditions (aOR = 4.09; 95% CI: 1.50–11.16.50.16). Furthermore, reporting stigmatization was strongly associated with being more likely to experience depression (aOR = 4.21; 95% CI: 1.79–9.88).

Conversely, certain factors were associated with a reduced likelihood of experiencing depression. Household income proved to be a significant protective factor. Compared to adolescents whose household income was less than 1 SMI (minimum interprofessional salary), those with an income between 1 and 2 SMI were less likely to experience depression (aOR = 0.49; 95% CI: 0.25–0.94). This protective effect was even more pronounced for adolescents with a household income greater than 2 SMI (aOR = 0.25; 95% CI: 0.07–0.91). In addition, male ALHIV also seemed to show a tendency to be less exposed to depression than their female counterparts. (aOR = 0.65; 95% CI: 0.43–1.00.43.00). The same factors were associated with the presence of mental health disorders (depression or anxiety combined) (see Additional file 1).

Discussion

This study presents the first national-level estimation of mental health disorder prevalence and associated factors among ALHIV in Togo, revealing that one in four ALHIV were affected by depression. Anxiety prevalence was notably lower (0.4%), with no severe cases. Key risk factors identified for depression included loneliness, stigma, and coexisting chronic diseases. Conversely, being male and belonging to a higher-income household emerged as significant protective factors.

The estimated depression prevalence of 24.5% aligns with prior sub-Saharan African research, where rates among ALHIV range from 11.4% to 45.8% [9–11], and pooled regional analyses indicate approximately 26% [24]. This substantiates observations that ALHIV face a significantly higher risk of depression compared to the general population (8%−30%) [13, 14]. Although direct comparisons with the general Togolese adolescent population are impeded by a lack of local studies and variations in assessment tools (e.g., PHQ-9 vs. DSM criteria), indirect comparisons can be drawn from regional estimates. For instance, data from UNICEF and WHO estimate that approximately one in seven adolescents (14%) in the general population suffers from depression [33]. The 24.5% prevalence of moderate to severe depression found among adolescents living with HIV in Togo is markedly higher than this regional estimate, highlighting their increased vulnerability. This finding urgently necessitates strengthening existing and developing new national prevention strategies. Such an approach must be inclusive, integrating national mental health programs rather than solely relying on HIV control initiatives.

The anxiety prevalence (0.4%) observed in this study is markedly lower than global rates for sub-Saharan African adolescents (29%) [34]. This discrepancy may stem from cultural differences in symptom expression or interpretation. While both depression and anxiety are common in ALHIV, depression appears to be more frequent, potentially because individuals managing HIV and social discrimination channel psychological distress into depressive patterns [35, 36]. Another reason for the lower prevalence of anxiety could be underreporting due to social norms, the insensitivity of the GAD-7 as a measurement tool in this specific cultural context, or other methodological constraints. This warrants further qualitative investigation to better understand how anxiety is expressed and perceived among this population. In any case, even if depression may be more prevalent, both depression and anxiety are significant concerns for ALHIV and should be addressed. Addressing these mental problems necessarily means understanding their contextual determinants.

Being male emerged as a protective factor against depression among Togolese ALHIV, consistent with global epidemiological data showing higher depression rates in females during adolescence [37]. Biological, psychological, and sociocultural factors contribute to this disparity [37]. Gender roles, particularly socialization for strength and emotional control in boys in Sub-Saharan Africa [38], may buffer depressive symptoms, while girls may be more attuned to interpersonal stressors like stigma and relational conflicts linked to depression in ALHIV. However, this finding may be influenced by underreporting. Socio-cultural norms in the region often socialize boys to suppress emotional expression, which could lead to a lower reporting of depressive symptoms compared to their female counterparts, thus masking the true prevalence of depression among male adolescents [39]. This highlights the need for gender-sensitive mental health interventions, with targeted programs for female ALHIV addressing stigma, loneliness, and relational stressors.

A key finding was the contrast between high self-reported psychosocial support and the significant role of loneliness in predicting depression among ALHIV, with stigma also being a major determinant. Despite over 80% reporting support, loneliness and stigma significantly impacted depression, suggesting that perceived support may not translate into meaningful emotional connection. Structured programs often focus on informational or instrumental aid, potentially neglecting emotional and relational aspects, leading to persistent isolation, especially with social stigma or secrecy around HIV status. This aligns with literature on HIV and mental health in sub-Saharan Africa, where stigma perpetuates feelings of exclusion [40]. Fear of rejection in relationships due to HIV status can also drive loneliness and impact mental health, crucial during adolescence when social connections are vital [41]. The pressure to maintain secrecy about their diagnosis, compounded by internalized stigma, may inhibit ALHIV from forming or sustaining close, trusting relationships, further exacerbating their feelings of loneliness.

To effectively mitigate loneliness, stigma, and their impact on depression, psychosocial support must address emotional and relational dimensions. Interventions should foster authentic connections and belonging, going beyond formal counseling. Group therapy sessions addressing stigma and loneliness, led by ALHIV peers (as implemented in some Togolese facilities), could be more impactful than traditional one-on-one models [42]. A national situational analysis is needed to identify areas for implementing and strengthening this approach. Integrating stigma reduction into HIV care, empowering adolescents to discuss challenges in safe environments, and involving families, schools, and community leaders are also critical [43]. Finally, socio-behavioral surveys are needed to better understand the complex relationship between psychosocial support and loneliness among ALHIV. Adolescents’ perceptions of the type, quality, and consistency of support they receive could help design more effective, context-specific interventions.

Coexisting chronic diseases such as asthma, blindness or heart failure, even when affecting only 3.3% of ALHIV, were linked to higher depression risks, likely due to cumulative stress and vulnerability. This aligns with data indicating multimorbidity elevates mental health risks [44, 45]. Therefore, prevention of chronic diseases (e.g., respiratory issues, blindness) and integrated care addressing both physical and mental health are essential for this population.

Conversely, ALHIV from higher-income households (above the minimum interprofessional salary) showed decreased depression risks. The well-documented link between poverty and mental health imbalance [46, 47] suggests that poverty restricts access to vital resources like nutritious food, transportation, and regular care for ALHIV. Economic empowerment programs and support for ALHIV and their families can yield significant mental health benefits. Existing food or financial support initiatives at some PLHIV care sites in Togo, though facing irregular availability due to financial challenges, need to be reassessed and reinforced, particularly concerning financial empowerment for adolescents and their families.

This study’s findings have several implications for advancing mental health care among ALHIV in Togo. First, prioritize mental health screenings in standard HIV care using validated tools like the PHQ-9 A for early identification and appropriate support. Second, combating stigma through community leaders and anti-stigma messaging in health system training is crucial. Third, address economic disparities with cash support, vocational training, or supervised financial empowerment opportunities for ALHIV and their caregivers. Finally, multi-level actions are needed to address loneliness, including reassessing and strengthening existing peer support groups, mentorship programs, and community recreational spaces, and intensifying training for healthcare workers in sensitive communication strategies.

The originality of this study lies in the fact that it is the first national survey of mental health among ALHIV in Togo. These initial data lay the foundations for a genuine national mental health program or strategy for ALHIV. The use of random sampling on a national scale and the use of validated tools, such as the PHQ-9 A, GAD-7, and ULS-8 scales, are its strengths.

Nevertheless, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, social desirability bias may have led to underreporting of highly stigmatized behaviors or experiences, such as suicidal ideation, loneliness, or perceived stigma. Adolescents may have provided responses they perceived as socially acceptable, resulting in underestimation of these social parameters. Second, challenges related to delays in data collection stemming from the suspension of U.S. funding impacted the study timeline and engagement of health professionals at selected sites. This extended survey period could have impacted the investigators’ motivation, and indeed, a relaxation of procedures over time cannot be ruled out. However, considering the results obtained and the level of missing data, we believe this did not have a significant impact. Finally, the data on chronic comorbidities were self-reported and not clinically confirmed, which could affect the reliability of these findings and potentially under- or overestimate their prevalence and association with depression.

Conclusion

This study provides critical insights into the double burden of HIV and depression among adolescents in Togo, revealing high prevalence rates comparable to the broader sub-Saharan region. By unraveling key psychosocial and clinical determinants, such as loneliness, stigma, and chronic diseases alongside protective factors like economic security, it offers a roadmap for tailoring interventions to address mental health needs holistically. These data provide a solid basis for improving adolescent-centered HIV care in resource-limited settings such as Togo. In addition, the study highlighted the need for socio-behavioral studies to document the complex links between psychosocial support perceived by ALHIV and the subjective perception of loneliness and stigma, in order to effectively prevent depression in these adolescents.
