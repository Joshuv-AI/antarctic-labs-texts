Introduction

The clitoris is a highly complex female organ responsible for female sexual pleasure, analogous to the male penis [1]. Clitoromegaly, or abnormal enlargement of the clitoris, is commonly associated with disorders of sexual development and hormonal imbalances [2]. However, non-hormonal causes, such as epidermal clitoral cysts, are exceedingly rare, particularly in pediatric patients [2]. When clitoral cysts are identified, most cases are associated with trauma or mutilation [3]. Nevertheless, spontaneous occurrences and a single case of Lichen Sclerosus have also been identified in the literature as potential causes of clitoral cysts [[4], [5], [6]]. Regardless of etiology, finding a cyst in this area is highly uncommon and requires thorough clinical assessment, especially when other complex medical conditions may be involved.

In this context, we describe a child with precursor B-cell Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) who presented with a spontaneous epidermal clitoral hood cyst. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a clitoral cyst in a patient with ALL and only the twelfth known case of a spontaneous clitoral cyst in the literature. This case was managed in accordance with current surgical standards favoring individualized excision of symptomatic or high-risk epidermoid cysts, particularly in immunocompromised hosts, as supported in the existing surgical literature [7,8]. By sharing, we hope to enhance knowledge about clitoral cysts in the pediatric population, especially when other underlying conditions or malignancies may be present. This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [9].

Case presentation

A 4-year-old Caucasian female with previously diagnosed ALL was admitted inpatient for scheduled clinical management following the St. Jude treatment plan of high-dose methotrexate chemotherapy. She was under the care of a foster mother at the time of presentation. Her prior medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no known surgical interventions or prior hospitalizations aside from chemotherapy administration. Her current medications included methotrexate and supportive care agents (e.g., antiemetics), and there were no known drug allergies. There was no significant family history of endocrine or dermatologic disorders. The patient lived in a smoke-free household and was up to date on vaccinations. Review of systems was unremarkable, with no history of recent fever, weight loss, rashes, or systemic complaints.

Methotrexate, which is excreted in the urine, is known to cause mucositis and chemical burns to the perineum [10,11]. Though potty-trained, this child had a history of not notifying family or nurses if she had urinated in the bed. Accordingly, a Foley catheter was inserted to prevent perineal and buttock burns during the administration and clearance of high-dose methotrexate. During the urinary catheter insertion, the oncology team noted a white mass near the urethra (Fig. 1). On further examination, a clitoral hood mass was noted. The mass was mobile, non-tender, and well-circumscribed with a thick white secretion. There was no associated bleeding or additional discharge. Notably, there was no virilization or clinical signs of hyperandrogenism. The key diagnostic challenge was distinguishing between benign and malignant causes of the mass in an immunocompromised patient. Given the risks of leukemic infiltration or opportunistic infection, a prompt decision was made to excise rather than delay care for additional imaging or biopsy. Consent was received, and the patient underwent a complete surgical excision of the clitoral mass under general anesthesia at a Level I trauma center by a pediatric urologist with extensive specialized training, including dual fellowships in Pediatric Urology and Pediatric Surgery.

Preoperative optimization included review of active medications, confirmation of nil per os (NPO) status, and pre-anesthesia clearance. No changes were made to the planned intervention, and no additional lifestyle or psychological interventions were indicated. The patient was brought to the OR under pediatric urology care, positioned supine, and prepared with chlorhexidine skin antiseptic prior to cyst excision. Examination under anesthesia confirmed the pre-op diagnosis (Fig. 2). A circumferential incision was made around the cyst at the midline of the clitoris to avoid injury to the clitoral hood nerves. The dissection was superficial to prevent injury to the erectile bodies of the clitoris. Complete resection was performed, and the clitoral hood was approximated using fine absorbable sutures in an interrupted manner (Fig. 3).Fig. 2Intraoperative image of the clitoral cyst showing a mobile, non-tender, well-circumscribed mass with a thick white secretion, immediately prior to surgical excision.Fig. 2Fig. 3Post-operative image demonstrating resolution of the cyst.Fig. 3

Histopathological review confirmed a 1.5 × 1.5 cm structure lined with squamous epithelium consistent with an epidermal cyst (Fig. 4). The cyst contained only keratin debris, with no additional contents (Figs. 5, 6). There were no histopathologic features suggestive of malignancy or leukemic infiltration, and no prognostic concern for recurrence based on complete excision and benign pathology. Her recovery was uneventful (Clavien-Dindo Grade 0), and she demonstrated full adherence to postoperative instructions with no complications or functional concerns. Discussion

The differential diagnosis for clitoral masses in the pediatric population covers a wide array of causes, including both benign and malignant conditions. Hormonal imbalances and sexual development disorders, notably endocrinopathies, masculinizing tumors, and exposure to androgens, are the primary hormonal causes of clitoromegaly [12]. Non-hormonal causes of clitoromegaly include Neurofibromatosis [13], Epidermal cysts, and various syndromes such as Congenital Generalized Lipodystrophy [14], Turner Syndrome [15], and Fraser Syndrome [16].

Differentiating between hormonal and non-hormonal causes of clitoromegaly is crucial for guiding management, as hormonal etiologies may require endocrine evaluation, including serum androgens, DHEA-S, 17 hydroxyprogesterone, and an ACTH stimulation test when CAH is suspected [17]. In contrast, non-hormonal causes may warrant imaging studies (e.g., pelvic ultrasound, CT) or genetic testing to assess for structural anomalies or syndromic conditions [18].

While the differential diagnosis of clitoral masses includes both hormonal and non-hormonal etiologies, epidermal clitoral cysts represent a rare non-hormonal cause. These cysts may develop congenitally due to displaced epithelial tissue during embryogenesis or acquired postnatally following trauma, including surgical intervention or female genital mutilation [19,20]. The underlying pathophysiology is due to epidermal tissue being trapped within the dermal or subcutaneous layers, leading to cystic inclusion and abnormal keratinization over time [21].

Treatment of clitoral cysts typically involves surgical removal, which has shown positive results in the literature for cysts exceeding 2 cm or those causing pain, infection, or functional impairment [22]. However, the timing and method may differ based on cyst size, location, and the presence of associated symptoms. Smaller cysts (<1.5 cm) that remain asymptomatic may be managed conservatively with observation and regular follow-ups, while cysts >2 cm are generally recommended for surgical excision due to the risk of discomfort, adhesions, and secondary complications [23]. Cysts exceeding 3 cm have been associated with interference in daily activities such as walking and sitting, further supporting the need for timely surgical intervention [24]. However, management decisions should be individualized, particularly in patients with complex medical conditions where diagnostic uncertainty, immunosuppression, or risk of treatment delays must be considered.

Although smaller clitoral cysts (<1.5 cm) are often managed conservatively, surgical excision was warranted in this case due to several key factors. First, secondary infection and diagnostic uncertainty posed a risk, especially in a patient undergoing high-dose chemotherapy for ALL. Given that infections remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in pediatric oncology patients, timely intervention is crucial to prevent complications in the immunocompromised individual [7]. Furthermore, while no large studies specifically evaluate epidermoid clitoral cysts in immunocompromised pediatric patients, established guidelines for oncology and transplant medicine support early intervention for soft tissue lesions in immunosuppressed individuals, critical to preventing secondary infections, minimizing treatment delays, and ensuring optimal oncologic care [8, 25]. This case highlights that size alone should not dictate management decisions, as clinical factors such as underlying immunosuppression, risk of misdiagnosis, and treatment implications must be carefully weighed.

Conclusion

This case is notable as the first reported instance of an epidermal clitoral cyst in a pediatric patient with ALL and one of the few documented cases of spontaneous non-hormonal clitoral cysts in children. It highlights the value of timely interdisciplinary communication, enabling early identification, appropriate surgical management, and avoidance of oncologic treatment delays. Given the potential for misdiagnosis, secondary infections, and treatment delays, a multidisciplinary approach, integrating oncology, pediatric urology, pathology, and endocrinology, is critical for accurate diagnosis and appropriate intervention. However, this case is limited by the lack of preoperative imaging and endocrine evaluation, which were deferred due to the patient's immunocompromised status and elevated infection risk. Additionally, although the patient has shown no recurrence to date, long-term follow-up is still ongoing, and late complications or recurrence cannot be entirely excluded.

By sharing this case, we aim to enhance awareness and guide clinicians in recognizing and managing similar cases. Further research into long-term outcomes and recurrence rates of epidermal cysts in both immunocompromised and immunocompetent pediatric patients may help refine treatment guidelines and optimize patient care. However, this case reinforces that early recognition and surgical intervention in high-risk patients can prevent complications, improve outcomes, and avoid unnecessary delays in care.
