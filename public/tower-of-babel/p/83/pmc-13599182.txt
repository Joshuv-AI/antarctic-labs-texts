Influence of a head-turned position on landing movement patterns: a crossover trial
Authors: Kim Hébert-Losier, Blake Anderson, Ivana Hanzlíková
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn sports where the ball is played overhead, anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are mostly sustained during landing with athletes looking elsewhere, often with their heads turned. The purpose of this study was to examine the effect of a head-turned position on landing movement patterns and jump heights using the Landing Error Scoring System (LESS), as a means of altering sensory input to enhance sport specificity and relevance to ACL injury screening.MethodsThirty-five healthy adults (22 males, 13 females) participated in this within-participants crossover trial, completing three double-leg jump landings assessed under two randomised conditions allocated sequentially: control and head-turned LESS. Overall LESS scores, proportion categorised at risk (≥ 5 errors), occurrence of specific landing errors, and jump heights were compared between conditions.ResultsLESS scores were higher in the head-turned condition than control (6.8 ± 1.5 versus 6.4 ± 1.4 errors, P = 0.027) and jump heights lower (32.7 ± 10.2 versus 34.8 ± 10.3 cm, P = 0.002), both with small effects (Cohen’s d = 0.27 [0.03, 0.51]; 0.20 [0.07, 0.34]). Under the head-turned condition, participants more often landed with lateral trunk flexion (20% vs. 54%, P = 0.002) and wide stance (23% vs. 46%, P = 0.002). Odds of being classified at risk did not differ significantly (P = 0.625, OR = 3.00 [0.31, 28.80]).ConclusionPerforming the LESS with a head-turned position to alter sensory input reduced jump height and increased LESS errors, but did not meaningfully alter injury risk categorisation. Future research should determine whether a head-turned condition enhances the predictive validity of the LESS for ACL injuries and use in injury prevention and rehabilitation.Trial registrationAustralian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12625001299493), 21/11/2025. Background

Anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) injuries are one of the most common and severe sport-related knee injuries [1], with injury incidence rates increasing over the last 20 years [2]. Sport-related ACL injuries often stem from non-contact mechanisms. For example, landing accounts for 57–82% of ACL injuries in sports involving overhead play, such as netball, volleyball, and badminton [3]. Non-contact ACL injuries are considered largely preventable under specific conditions, particularly through the implementation of exercise-based [4] training programmes incorporating plyometrics [5] and neuromuscular [6] components. Evidence from a meta-analysis of meta-analyses indicates that ACL injury prevention programmes are associated with an approximately 50% reduction in overall ACL injury risk across athletes, and up to an 67% reduction in non-contact ACL injuries in female athletes [7]. Movement screening is therefore commonly used to identify athletes at greater injury risk and those most likely to benefit from targeted injury prevention interventions [8].

The Landing Error Scoring System (LESS) is a screening task used to assess landing patterns in relation to ACL injury mechanisms [9]. The LESS involves performing a double-leg jump-landing (DLJL) task from a 30 cm box, with the benefits of requiring minimal time, space, and equipment, making it practically useful in team sports involving multiple players. The LESS has shown predictive value in 11-year-old to 18-year-old footballers, with athletes scoring ≥ 5 errors having 10.7 times greater odds of sustaining a non-contact ACL injury [10]. Similarly, the odds of sustaining a lower-limb musculoskeletal injury was 16 times greater in entry-level male military personnel (aged 18–25 years) scoring > 5 errors [11]. The LESS has, however, been criticised for lacking predictive value for ACL injury in student athletes [12], with the DLJL task overall lacking sport specificity towards ACL and other knee injury mechanisms [13, 14]. In netball where non-contact ACL injuries are common [15], players sustaining such injuries were observed to have their head turned away from their injured side at the time of injury, such as when catching the ball or looking for teammates [16]. Similarly, video analysis of ACL injuries in basketball identified players looking at the ball, rim, or opponent in all but four of 39 injuries [17]. Head position and where athletes are looking are potentially important, but overlooked, factors in ACL injury risk screening.

From a biomechanical perspective, athletes focusing on heading a soccer ball during DLJL [18] or stop-jump [19] tasks were more likely to demonstrate biomechanics consistent with ACL injury mechanisms compared to the same tasks without balls. These biomechanical patterns linked with ACL injuries included decreased knee flexion angle and displacement [20], lateral trunk flexion [21], and increased vertical ground reaction forces [22]. Although these studies primarily relied on dual-task and ball tracking paradigms, visual feedback was also inherently altered from control conditions. Visual feedback is deemed important for motor control [23], with visual feedback reliance increasing post-ACL reconstruction [24]. Performing the LESS task with the head turned alters the visual feedback loop, as individuals are not looking at the landing area. Furthermore, head orientation is a key contributor to postural control during sport-related movements. Changes in head position relative to the trunk modify cervical proprioceptive, alter vestibular input, and interact with visual information to regulate postural muscle activity and whole-body coordination during dynamic tasks [25, 26]. Inspired by real-life sporting situations, we posit that performing a head-turned LESS alters sensory input, thereby increasing sport specificity and relevance to ACL injury mechanisms. We recognise that such alterations may compromise sporting performance, as reflected by reduced jump height [18].

We examined the effect of a head-turned position on landing movement patterns and jump heights of the LESS task as a means of altering sensory input and increasing sporting relevance of the task to ACL injury screening. Specifically, we compared LESS scores, risk categorisation based on a 5-error threshold, occurrence of specific LESS errors, and jump heights between a control and head-turned LESS condition. We hypothesised that adding head rotation would increase overall LESS scores, proportion of individuals categorised at high risk, and occurrences of specific LESS errors associated with knee flexion (item 1) and lateral trunk flexion [6] at initial contact, and decrease jump heights versus the control LESS.

Methods

Experimental approach

This study used a crossover experimental design with repeated measures. Sample size calculations were performed a priori using G*Power 3.1.9.7. A standard two-tailed hypothesis with 80% power (β = 0.20), 5% significance level (α = 0.05), and moderate effect size difference (Cohen d = 0.50) between conditions for paired data indicated that 34 participants were required. This effect size difference would align with a 1-error LESS difference defining a clinically meaningful change [9, 27], and a 2-error standard deviation of the difference in paired means, which is reasonable based on published data [28]. The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines [29] were followed in study reporting. The trial was retrospectively registered in the Australian New Zealand Clinical Trials Registry (ACTRN12625001299493) on 21st November 2025 (https://anzctr.org.au/ACTRN12625001299493.aspx). There were no changes to the planned trial methods or outcomes from the date of ethical approval, which was granted by the Health, Engineering, Computing, and Science Division Human Research Ethics Sub-Committee of the University of Waikato ((HREC[HECS]2023#43) prior to data collection, with the protocol adhering to the Declaration of Helsinki. Sport science studies [30], such as those assessing the effect of a change in condition on biomechanics with no longitudinal follow-up, are typically not preregistered partially due to a general sense that such acute interventions do not represent a medical clinical intervention.

Participants

Thirty-five participants (22 males, 13 females, aged 18.1–27.2 years) were recruited and tested in a movement laboratory in Hamilton, New Zealand, within two weeks in May 2024 from a convenience sample of healthy university students (Table 1). Most participants (83%) participated in sport, of which over two-thirds (69%) were court or field sports (e.g., rugby, netball, hockey). Participants were free of pain, injury, or medical conditions that may have affected their movement or landing mechanics. Participants with injuries to their lower-extremity, back, or pelvis regions within the last three months were excluded. A history of injury or surgery was not an exclusion criterion, as injury-risk screening is clinically relevant for previously injured athletes. Moreover, the within-participant study design minimises the influence of individual differences in neuromuscular control strategies. Nonetheless, none of the participants disclosed sustaining a prior ACL injury. Participants signed an informed consent form prior to participating that explained the study aims, risks (e.g., chance of injury due to physical activity participation), and benefits (i.e., individualised reports). Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants.

Procedures

Following informed consent, participants completed a baseline questionnaire that included self-reported ethnicity and the International Physical Activity Questionnaire–Short Form (IPAQ–SF) to categorise their daily physical activity levels as high, moderate, or low [31]. A stadiometer (seca model 0123) and digital scale (seca model ESE813) were used to record body height and mass of participants barefoot.

The LESS is evaluated using two-dimensional video recordings obtained from frontal and sagittal views of a DLJL task. A 17-item scoring sheet is used to assess specific movement errors associated with ACL injury mechanisms, including stiff landings marked by reduced knee and hip flexion, excessive knee valgus defined by medial positioning of the patella relative to the midfoot, and the presence of lateral trunk flexion. The tool demonstrates good to excellent intra-rater, inter-rater, and inter-session reliability, and the validity of individual LESS items related to key ACL injury risk factors has been reported as moderate to excellent in the literature [14]. The LESS testing and scoring followed the original protocols [9], except for the head-turned condition incorporating a visual target. For testing, participants jumped horizontally with both legs from a 30-cm high box to 50% of their body height marked on the floor using tape, before landing and jumping vertically as high as possible in one motion. In the head-turned condition, participants were asked to maintain eye contact with an ‘X’ positioned on the wall 2 m above and 4 m to the right of the DLJL landing distance, resulting in a 65–90° head-turned position during the task. Tests were invalid and repeated if participants did not land at the correct distance; jumped one foot at a time; lost balance; or did not maintain eye contact with the ‘X’ in the head-turned condition, as visually determined by the two research team members present during data collection. Prior to completing a given LESS condition, participants performed one-to-three familiarisation trials. Three valid LESS tests were recorded per condition, with no participant completing more than five trials per condition. No feedback on performance was given unless the task was being performed incorrectly to avoid influencing results [32]. Between trials, participants rested in quiet stance for one minute and rested five minutes between conditions. Condition order was allocated using a systematic alternating sequence to ensure counterbalancing, with the first participant starting in the control condition (determined via a coin toss), the subsequent in the experimental condition, and onward based on enrolment, resulting in 18 and 17 participants starting with the control and head-turned conditions, respectively. Participants completed the familiarisation and three valid experimental trials for one condition before the other. Participants used their own footwear during testing. All sessions were supervised by a registered physiotherapist (KHL) and sport scientist (BA). The blinding of participants and those administering the test was not possible due to the nature of the assessment. No adverse events occurred during data collection or were reported following study completion.

Two cameras with a focal length of 8.8–73.3 mm (35-mm equivalent focal length of 24–200 mm) captured the DLJL trials at 120 frames per second (Sony RX10 II, Sony Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). One camera was positioned in front of participants to capture frontal plane movement, and the other to the right to capture sagittal plane movement and visual gaze. Cameras were placed 3.5 m away from the landing distance and mounted on tripods with a lens-to-ground distance of 1.3 m. Videos were analysed using Kinovea (version 0.9.5, https://www.kinovea.org/). A single rater (BA) with > 3 years of experience analysing human movement conducted all LESS ratings after receiving two training sessions from expert raters (IH, KHL) who had completed > 500 ratings each. The rater completed > 20 ratings before analysing the current dataset. Trials were presented at random for rating, with the rater blinded to the randomisation sequence and LESS scores of individuals from the other experimental condition, but not to the study aims. Due to the nature of the task, it was not possible to blind the rater to the condition being rated.

Statistical analysis

For analysis, the average of participants’ three trials was used in line with the original LESS protocol [9] and most studies using the LESS [34]. Differences between conditions in overall LESS errors and jump heights were analysed using mean differences with 95% confidence intervals, two tailed paired t-tests, and Cohen’s d effect sizes for paired samples using the average variance. Effect sizes were categorised as small, medium, and large when Cohen d reached 0.20, 0.50, and 0.80, and trivial when < 0.20. Furthermore, the number of individuals that exhibited a meaningful change in LESS score (1 error or more [9, 27]) between conditions were counted.

Mcnemar’s test and odds ratio (OR) were used when comparing individual LESS errors and risk categorisations (high risk ≥ 5 errors) between conditions. When contrasting the occurrence of specific LESS errors, for each participant, an error was considered present when present in two of the three trials for items 1–15. For items 16 and 17, an error was considered present when the ‘average’ rating was present in two of three trials or when the ‘poor/stiff’ rating was present in one of three trials [9, 35]. An OR > 1.00 indicates a greater proportion of errors or of at-risk participants in the head-turned than control condition.

Significance level was set at P ≤ 0.05 for all analyses, except for when analysing individual LESS errors where Bonferroni correction was applied, accounting for the 17 multiple comparisons (P ≤ 0.003). Analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel for Microsoft 365 MSO (v.2109, Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA, USA), VassaStats for Statistical Computation (http://vassarstats.net/), and Real Statistics Resource Pack for Excel (https://real-statistics.com/). There were no missing LESS data or loss to follow-up, with all participants completing both conditions. No harm occurred to participants during the trials or was reported in the following two months.

Results

Group mean LESS scores were significantly higher in the head-turned (range: 3.7–9.7 errors) than control (range: 3.3–9.7 errors) condition, with the mean difference of 0.4 errors being small (Cohen’s d = 0.27 [0.03, 0.51], Table 2). Eighteen participants had a meaningful change of 1 error or more in LESS score between conditions, with 13 having a meaningful increase and 5 having a meaningful decrease in the head-turned compared to the control condition.

Discussion

We examined the effect of a head-turned condition on LESS performance, with the aim of altering sensory input to better reflect sport-specific conditions and enhance ecological relevance. As hypothesised, mean LESS scores were significantly greater in the head-turned than control condition. However, the corresponding small increase of 0.4 errors was below the meaningful change threshold of ≥ 1 error [9, 27]. Therefore, the clinical meaningfulness of this change is debatable, especially given the non-significant difference in group-level risk categorisation. Also agreeing with our hypothesis, average jump height was 2.1 cm lower in the head-turned condition. This compromised jump performance is deemed clinically important as it exceeds the typical 2 cm measurement error for this measure [36, 37]. At an individual level, 13 of 35 participants (37.1%) had a meaningful increase in LESS errors under the head-turned condition, five (14.3%) a meaningful decrease, and 17 (48.6%) no meaningful change. Although our data indicate no meaningful group-level impact of head turning on LESS outcomes, those individuals with worse head-turned LESS scores may represent individuals more visually reliant and predisposed to injury in multitasking environments.

Interpretation of our findings should consider that most participants were already categorised as high risk (82.9%) in the control condition based on the conventional 5-error LESS threshold [10]. Larger between-condition differences may have emerged in a cohort with lower baseline LESS scores. In the present study, mean control LESS scores were 6.4 ± 1.4 errors, which is slightly higher than the weighted mean of 5.2 ± 1.7 reported in a systematic review of nine LESS studies [14]. Only one study [38] in that review reported higher mean scores; however, this observation was in a younger cohort (15.2 ± 1.2 years) compared with the present sample (20.3 ± 1.8 years). Importantly, a study with comparable participant characteristics, including age, anthropometrics, and physical activity level (IPAQ–SF), reported similar mean LESS scores (6.2 ± 1.5) and a comparable proportion of participants classified as high risk (77.5%). The baseline risk profile of our sample is thus consistent with comparable populations reported in the literature [35].

Incorporating a head-turned aspect into the LESS task was informed by ACL injury research identifying head turning as a potential risk factor [16]. Video analyses of elite netballers show ACL injuries frequently occur with the head turned away from the injured knee [16], and is often accompanied by trunk perturbations or lateral flexion [39]. Accordingly, the head-turned condition aligns with known mechanisms [24, 25] and was expected to influence LESS performance by altering visual input and cervical proprioceptive and vestibular signals, thereby affecting trunk orientation and movement control, which may lead to increased errors and a greater proportion of participants categorised as at risk. The lack of meaningful differences between conditions aligns with prior studies examining LESS variations, such as with-or-without footwear [35] or an overhead goal [40]. Collectively, these findings highlight the limited sensitivity of LESS to protocol modifications, especially where many individuals are already classified as high risk under the standard 5-error threshold. Greater differentiation may have emerged if a single-leg jump, rather than a double-leg jump, had been employed. Indeed, a preliminary modified LESS template incorporating a single-leg jump with rotation has been proposed to increase biomechanical challenge [41]. The LESS may be constrained by a ceiling effect and its checklist approach, whereby added task difficulty does not yield further errors once these are already present. For example, item 6 is scored as an error for any degree of lateral trunk flexion at initial contact, meaning that increases in lateral flexion between conditions cannot be identified if the error is already present under the control condition. Thus, the LESS identifies whether a given movement pattern is present or absent, but it does not quantify its magnitude—an aspect more directly related to injury risk. For example, increasing lateral trunk flexion has been shown to be positively associated with higher peak knee abduction moments [42]. Once these moments exceed the capacity of the ACL to withstand loading, an injury ensues.

Participants jumped on average 2.1 cm lower in the head-turned than the control condition, despite being asked to jump as high as possible. Assuming maximal effort was applied, this reduction may reflect uncertainty and reduced visual information of the landing area, leading to compromised initial landing and reduced jump force production. This interpretation aligns with studies showing greater jump height under full-vision than no-vision [43]. Effective movement depends on perceiving and responding to environmental constraints [44]. Incorporating an ecological constraints approach into injury screening and prevention research enhances generalisability of findings to broader sporting contexts [45]. While reductionist methods permit biomechanical precision, they overlook adaptability and natural human movement variability [45]. Combining 3D motion with force plates during landing could provide more precise verification of movement differences between protocol variants than LESS scoring via video analysis. Such methods would improve the accuracy of LESS scoring [10, 46], allow assessment of joint moments and other biomechanical factors linked with ACL injury [47], and help identify the mechanism influencing jump height, but reduce practicality and transferability to clinical settings.

At the individual level, participants who scored higher in the head-turned condition may have been more reliant on or sensitive to changes in sensory input (predominantly visual), potentially placing them at greater sport-related injury risk. Visual reliance is known to increase in double- and single-leg balance tasks, as well as hopping tasks, following ACL reconstruction [24]. To our knowledge, no research has examined whether visual reliance itself is a risk factor for ACL injury. Incorporating visual constraints into dynamic tasks like the LESS may therefore provide a valuable stimulus in rehabilitation, preventive neuromuscular training, and return-to-play preparation Ultimately, prospective studies are needed to determine whether the head-turned LESS offers greater predictive value than the standard LESS for injury due to its increased visual constraint.

Previous research has shown that reduced vision can result in stiffer landings characterized by greater vertical ground reaction forces and reduced hip and knee flexion [48]. In contrast, we found no significant differences in LESS items related to stiff landing technique, including item 16 (joint displacement) and item 17 (overall impression). Overall, item-specific LESS errors were largely unchanged, with significant differences limited to item 6 (lateral trunk flexion at initial contact) and item 7 (stance width at initial contact, Table 3). Lateral trunk flexion is a common movement in sport that has been linked to increased ACL loading and injury risk [20, 49], as it shifts the centre of mass and places greater load on one leg [39, 50]. Landing with an overly wide base of support, often used to assist balance, has also been identified as a common ACL injury scenario [16]. Notably, the increase in these errors in the head-turned condition was not accompanied by changes in other LESS items. This last observation suggests participants may have compensated in ways undetected by the LESS (e.g., greater unilateral loading) or the presence of a ceiling effect of the tool, as noted previously.

This study has limitations. Most notably, gaze behaviour was not reliably measured during the head-turned condition. Although participants were instructed to look at the ‘X’ on the wall, which elicited a head-turned position, participants may still have directed their gaze toward other environmental cues to assist in their DLJL performance. Future studies could monitor gaze behaviour using eye-tracking technology or incorporate a visual task (e.g., letter array) to improve ecological validity and reliability. As noted earlier, a large proportion of participants were already classified as high risk and exhibited a relatively high number of landing errors in the control condition. This distribution may have resulted in a ceiling effect, potentially reducing the sensitivity of the LESS to detect additional differences between the control and head-turned conditions. Consequently, the magnitude of condition-related effects may have been underestimated. A further limitation is that, although the head-turned LESS introduces a novel alteration in sensory and postural input, its predictive validity for ACL injury has not yet been established. As such, conclusions regarding its clinical or screening utility should be interpreted with caution. Prospective studies are required to determine whether this modified approach improves the identification of athletes at increased risk of ACL injury.

Conclusions

Adding a head-turned component to the LESS increased task difficulty, as shown by reduced jump heights, but did not meaningfully alter overall scores or risk categorisation. Some errors were more prevalent, and a subset of individuals showed meaningful increases in LESS scores, indicating potential value of a head-turned LESS for injury risk screening and rehabilitation, particularly in individuals with high visual reliance or heightened sensitivity to altered proprioceptive and vestibular input. However, its predictive utility for ACL injury remains unproven, and further research is needed before the head-turned LESS can be recommended for clinical use in injury risk screening, prevention, and rehabilitation.
