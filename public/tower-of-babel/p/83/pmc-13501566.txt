Effectiveness analysis of a multi-track teaching model based on OBE concept in pediatric ophthalmology clinical internship
Authors: Yang Wang, Qing Liu, Ning Ke, Xinke Chen, Xuan Zhang, Xin Li, Xiaohui Zhu, Lin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo explore the application effect of a multi-track teaching strategy integrating Outcome-Based Education (OBE) concept with Case-Based Learning (CBL), Problem-Based Learning (PBL), Team-Based Learning (TBL), and multi-station teaching methods in pediatric ophthalmology clinical internship courses.MethodsA total of 212 fifth-year pediatric undergraduate students who completed ophthalmology clinical internships from April to May 2025 were selected. They were divided into a research group (107 students, odd-numbered groups) and a control group (105 students, even-numbered groups) according to internship groups (15–16 students per group). The research group adopted a multi-track teaching model based on OBE concept, while the control group followed a traditional clinical internship teaching model. The pre-class, in-class, and post-class teaching evaluations and course satisfaction of the two groups were compared. SPSS 20.0 was used for statistical analysis. Count data were expressed as n (%), analyzed by χ² test; measurement data were expressed as (x̄ ± s), analyzed by independent samples t-test.ResultsThe post-class case analysis score of the research group was significantly higher than that of the control group (45.10 ± 1.36 vs. 41.50 ± 1.35, P < 0.05). The in-class evaluation scores of the research group in operational skills, case analysis, doctor-patient communication, and teamwork ability were significantly higher than those of the control group (19.34 ± 0.57 vs. 16.97 ± 0.37, 19.53 ± 0.87 vs. 17.95 ± 0.69, 19.26 ± 0.65 vs. 17.92 ± 0.78, 19.42 ± 0.68 vs. 17.58 ± 0.65, P < 0.001). In the teaching feedback, the research group’s scores in learning motivation (4.85 ± 0.13 vs. 4.21 ± 0.18), key concept comprehension (4.72 ± 0.21 vs. 4.21 ± 0.18), integration of theory and clinical practice (4.87 ± 0.18 vs. 4.28 ± 0.15), and formation of clinical diagnostic thinking (4.84 ± 0.12 vs. 4.27 ± 0.23) were significantly higher than those of the control group (P < 0.001). The overall evaluation of the internship course by the research group was significantly higher than that of the control group (4.82 ± 0.24 vs. 4.32 ± 0.18, P < 0.001).ConclusionThe OBE concept-based multi-track teaching model was associated with improved clinical reasoning, practical skills, and self-directed learning among pediatric ophthalmology interns, suggesting potential value for clinical teaching quality.Keywords: OBE concept, Case-based teaching, Problem-based learning, Team-based learning, Ophthalmic teaching

Introduction

With the upgrading of global medical education standards (such as the “Global Standards for Quality Assurance of Medical Education” released by the World Federation for Medical Education (WFME) in 2021), modern medical education is transitioning from “knowledge transmission” to “competency development,” emphasizing that medical students need to possess core competencies such as clinical thinking, practical skills, and doctor-patient communication [1]. As an important branch of medical education, pediatric ophthalmology clinical teaching not only needs to impart professional knowledge but also cultivate medical students’ clinical competence to respond to the particularities of pediatric patients (e.g., communication difficulties, high disease concealment). However, traditional pediatric ophthalmology clinical internships often adopt a “teacher-centered” model, using a single teaching method of “theoretical lectures + case demonstration,” which leads to disconnection between knowledge and practice, insufficient student initiative, and weak clinical thinking cultivation [2–4].This model is difficult to meet the needs of modern medical education for “patient-centered” doctor training, and it is urgent to introduce Outcome-Based Education (OBE) concept, which promotes teaching model innovation through clear competency objectives, diversified teaching methods, and continuous evaluation feedback [5, 6].

OBE concept is “student-centered, competency-oriented,” emphasizing that teaching activities designed to meet learning objectives promote the comprehensive development of students’ abilities. Tony Succar pointed out that the OBE model, through the cycle of “clear learning objectives → design teaching activities → continuous evaluation feedback,” can effectively solve the problems of “knowledge fragmentation” and “insufficient competency cultivation” in traditional teaching, especially suitable for disciplines requiring high practicality (such as pediatric ophthalmology) [7, 8]. In medical education, OBE has been widely applied in disciplines such as internal medicine and surgery, and has been proven to effectively improve students’ clinical practical ability and autonomous learning ability [9]. Multi-track teaching models (such as Case-Based Learning (CBL), Problem-Based Learning (PBL), Team-Based Learning (TBL), and multi-station teaching) serve as specific implementation paths for OBE. By integrating the advantages of different teaching methods, they form a closed loop of “theory-practice-collaboration,” compensating for the shortcomings of traditional teaching [10]. For example, CBL guides students to analyze problems through real cases, PBL stimulates active learning through problem-driven approaches [11], TBL cultivates team communication ability through group collaboration [12], and multi-station teaching strengthens practical skills through simulated clinical scenarios [13]. The synergistic effect of these methods is highly consistent with the core logic of OBE “competency-oriented,” providing new ideas for pediatric ophthalmology clinical teaching.

Based on this, this study aims to explore the application effect of a multi-track teaching model based on OBE concept in pediatric ophthalmology clinical internships, evaluate its advantages in case analysis, practical skills, doctor-patient communication, and teamwork by comparing with traditional teaching models, in order to provide empirical evidence for the reform of pediatric ophthalmology clinical teaching.

Materials and methods

General information

This study was a single-center, non-randomized, quasi-experimental interventional study conducted in strict accordance with the “Declaration of Helsinki”. The study was sourced from an educational reform project (Chongqing Medical University Children’s Science College Educational Reform Research Project, project number: EY202308) and are exempt from Ethics Committee of the Children’s Hospital of Chongqing Medical University. All participants signed informed consent forms. From April to May 2025, 212 fifth-year pediatric undergraduate students who completed ophthalmology clinical internships were selected. All participants had completed a 4-week ophthalmology basic theory course before the internship and had not received ophthalmology clinical skill training. Inclusion criteria: (1) graduating undergraduate students who had completed relevant theoretical courses; (2) completed all tests and questionnaires. Exclusion criteria: (1) latecomers, early leavers, or absentees; (2) those who disagreed to participate in the study. Grouping: According to the group numbers assigned by the University Academic Affairs Office, the internship groups (each consisting of 15–16 members) are divided into a research group (odd-numbered groups) and a control group (even-numbered groups). A total of 212 students were finally included, with 107 in the research group (42 males, 65 females, mean age 22.22 ± 0.80 years) and 105 in the control group (40 males, 65 females, mean age 22.12 ± 0.81 years). There was no statistically significant difference in general data between the two groups (P > 0.05), as shown in Table 1.

Methods

According to the latest teaching syllabus requirements of Chongqing Medical University Children’s Science College, pediatric ophthalmology has 32 class hours, including 12 internship hours, divided into 4 sessions, 3 class hours each. The four courses were “Conjunctival Diseases, Eyelid Diseases, and Lacrimal Apparatus Diseases,” “Glaucoma, Corneal Diseases, and Ocular Trauma,” “Vitreoretinal Diseases, Uveitis, and Retinal Diseases,” and “Optometry, Extraocular Muscle Diseases, and Amblyopia.” According to the course objectives and content requirements, the traditional clinical internship model and the OBE concept-based multi-track teaching model were adopted for classroom design. Each internship course was led by 1 associate professor and 2 excellent young teachers for collective lesson preparation to ensure the quality of the internship course. Both groups were taught by the same 3 instructors, with each instructor leading one group (15–16 students) per session.Instructors were involved in evaluating student performance (e.g., operational skills).

Control group

Traditional clinical internship model: Teaching was led by the supervising teacher, strictly following the teaching syllabus, and carefully selecting typical cases. Bedside teaching was the main method. The supervising teacher inquired about the patient’s medical history and conducted physical examinations, guiding students to make preliminary diagnoses and differential diagnoses and formulate corresponding treatment plans. At the same time, the supervising teacher demonstrated and guided students in basic operational skill training. Taking “Conjunctival Diseases, Eyelid Diseases, and Lacrimal Apparatus Diseases” clinical internship as an example, the supervising teacher carefully selected conjunctival diseases, eyelid diseases, and lacrimal apparatus diseases as typical cases. Bedside teaching was the main method. The supervising teacher inquired about the patient’s medical history, detailed the onset time, course of development, past medical history, etc. During physical examinations, the supervising teacher carefully examined the patient’s ocular signs, such as conjunctival hyperemia type, eyelid shape, tear film break-up time, etc. When guiding students to make preliminary diagnoses and differential diagnoses, the supervising teacher combined the medical history and examination results to gradually analyze the characteristics of different diseases. For example, for conjunctivitis, the teacher guided students to diagnose based on conjunctival hyperemia type, secretory characteristics, etc., and distinguish it from other possible causes of conjunctival hyperemia; for eyelid diseases, the teacher allowed students to observe eyelid deformities, positions, etc., and judge whether it was infectious or non-infectious eyelid disease, and differentiate it from other eyelid diseases; for lacrimal apparatus diseases, the teacher diagnosed based on tear film break-up time, tear duct obstruction, and other symptoms, and guided students to determine the location and severity of the obstruction, and gave corresponding treatment plans, including drug therapy, surgery, etc., and explained the principles and basis of treatment in detail. Finally, the supervising teacher systematically explained the basic theoretical knowledge of conjunctival diseases, eyelid diseases, and lacrimal apparatus diseases, covering common etiologies, pathogenesis, diagnostic criteria, and treatment methods, helping students review and consolidate relevant knowledge. At the same time, the supervising teacher demonstrated and guided students in basic operational skill training, such as slit lamp examination, tear duct irrigation, eyelid examination, etc., and explained the significance of each clinical examination in detail, allowing students to understand how to obtain key information through these examinations and how to make diagnoses and treatments based on examination results.

Research group

Based on the OBE concept-based multi-track teaching model, this group adopted a multi-track teaching model based on OBE (Outcomes-Based Education) concept, as shown in Fig. 1. According to the teaching syllabus requirements, teaching objectives were set, covering knowledge objectives (knowledge integration ability), ability objectives (clinical core ability, autonomous learning), and emotional objectives (doctor-patient communication, teamwork), aiming to comprehensively improve students’ clinical diagnosis and treatment comprehensive ability. Taking “Conjunctival Diseases, Eyelid Diseases, and Lacrimal Apparatus Diseases” clinical internship as an example, the multi-track teaching model of this group integrated CBL (Case-Based Learning), PBL (Problem-Based Learning), TBL (Team-Based Learning), and multi-station teaching methods [14, 15]. (1) PBL + CBL: Jointly guided by the teacher through specific cases of conjunctivitis, students were guided to analyze and solve problems, requiring students to be able to diagnose pediatric conjunctivitis clinically. PBL focused on setting problems at key nodes to stimulate students’ learning interest and initiative, cultivate autonomous learning ability. CBL guided students to think and analyze, helping them establish a clinical thinking framework. Through thinking guidance, students’ comprehensive ability to evaluate knowledge was enhanced, and at the same time, through problem promotion, students’ theoretical knowledge was consolidated and integrated. For example, “What are bacterial conjunctivitis, viral conjunctivitis, and allergic conjunctivitis?” “Explain the common signs of conjunctivitis,” “Understand the differences in signs of different types of conjunctivitis,” and be able to differentiate “conjunctivitis” of different types in clinical practice to make diagnoses and differential diagnoses. Based on clinical symptoms and examination results, analyze the characteristics of “conjunctivitis” common signs, make correct diagnoses for clinical cases. (2) Group collaborative learning + multi-station teaching methods: During the pediatric ophthalmology clinical internship process, multiple different stations were set up. Each station represented a specific skill operation. Station tasks included: skill training and examination result interpretation (slit lamp examination, fluorescein sodium staining, tear duct irrigation, tear film break-up time examination, tear secretion test). Within the specified time, students completed each station’s tasks in groups. Each skill operation was demonstrated and guided by the supervising teacher, and students completed the interpretation of examination results through mutual assistance and operation training in the group. Through operation evaluation, students’ skill operations and group collaboration abilities were evaluated. (3) CBL + TBL: In the real clinical diagnosis and treatment scene of pediatric ophthalmology, students deeply experienced learning doctors’ doctor-patient communication skills during clinical consultations. Taking the internship group as a unit, the group made comprehensive decisions on the treatment of outpatients, and each student participated in the discussion. The teacher scored each student’s discussion situation to evaluate students’ comprehensive application ability to connect theory with practice.

Teaching effect evaluation

Learning outcome evaluation

Combined with the multi-evaluation thinking of TBL teaching method, the learning outcomes of the internship were evaluated before, during, and after class [11]. Pre-class: Completed online, testing the theoretical knowledge of the internship content: 5 questions, 20 points each, total 100 points, used to understand students’ mastery of theoretical knowledge before the internship. In-class: Teachers employ standardized scoring scales to evaluate students operational skills, case analysis, doctor-patient communication, and clinical comprehensive ability, 20 points each, total 100 points. The group collaboration evaluation could refer to the small group test form of TBL teaching method, and evaluate students’ teamwork and communication ability by the completion of group tasks such as case analysis. Post-class: Completed online, testing the internship content, including 5 theoretical questions, 5 points each, total 50 points, and 2 case analysis questions, 25 points each, total 50 points.

Teaching feedback

Through questionnaire surveys to evaluate learning outcomes and course satisfaction, after the internship ended, through online anonymous methods, an improved ECO questionnaire was used to complete teaching feedback and satisfaction questionnaires [16]. The questionnaire included 10 evaluation items: learning motivation, course content mastery, knowledge point memory, key concept comprehension, integration of theory and clinical practice, formation of clinical diagnostic thinking, learning pressure, pre-class workload, post-class workload, and overall course satisfaction. A 5-point Likert-type scale was used for evaluation: strongly disagree = 1 point, disagree = 2 points, uncertain = 3 points, agree = 4 points, strongly agree = 5 points.

Statistical methods

SPSS 20.0 statistical software was used for data statistical analysis. Count data were expressed as n (%), analyzed by χ² test; measurement data were expressed as (x̄ ± s), analyzed by independent samples t-test. P < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences.

Discussion

In traditional ophthalmology clinical internship teaching, theoretical knowledge is often the main focus, with clinical internship content centered around theoretical knowledge points. Students conduct clinical internships based on the knowledge points explained by the supervising teacher. This “teacher-centered” internship process is not conducive to the cultivation of students’ clinical thinking, practical skills, and autonomous learning ability [3, 4]. Theoretical knowledge and clinical practice cannot be effectively closely integrated, and students find it difficult to apply the knowledge they have learned to solve actual clinical problems. With the teacher’s lecture as the main method, there is a lack of interactivity and participation, which is not conducive to stimulating students’ learning interest and initiative [17, 18]. Based on the OBE concept-based multi-track teaching model, teaching emphasizes “student-centered” and reverse design, with the core connotation of “student-centered” [19, 20]. In this study, according to the OBE concept, in ophthalmology clinical internships, teaching objectives were decomposed into: knowledge objectives (knowledge integration ability), ability objectives (clinical core ability, autonomous learning), and emotional objectives (doctor-patient communication, teamwork). Through the combination of multiple teaching methods, from PBL + CBL (cognitive level) → TBL + multi-station (skill level) → TBL + real case CBL (application level), students’ learning initiative was effectively stimulated.The teaching implementation process was based on learning outcomes to organize diversified teaching activities, focusing on students’ acquired abilities and outcomes, emphasizing students’ subjectivity, and guiding students to actively participate in and acquire knowledge.

The results of the present study indicate that students in the research group achieved higher scores than those in the control group in doctor-patient communication, teamwork, and self-reported learning motivation(P < 0.001). These observations suggest that the OBE-based multi-track teaching model may be associated with improvements in these outcomes. However, given that all assessments relied on subjective measures and the study employed a non-randomized design, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than causal effects. These results are broadly consistent with previous literature. Fang Wang et al. [21] and Qiang Zhao et al. [22] reported that the integration of CBL and PBL was associated with enhanced clinical thinking and problem-solving abilities in internal medicine education, whereas Hua Zhang et al. [23] observed that TBL was associated with improved teamwork and communication skills. The pedagogical mechanisms underlying these associations may involve the combined use of PBL and CBL to stimulate learning interest through case-based guidance and problem-driven inquiry, while TBL may foster collaborative skills via structured group interaction. These components may enable students to better integrate theoretical knowledge with clinical practice and develop clinical reasoning patterns. Furthermore, this model may be associated with the cultivation of autonomous learning and problem-solving abilities that are beneficial for long-term career development, a perspective consistent with Li Ming et al. [24], who argued that integrated teaching methods offer distinct advantages in clinical education. However, although this study employed an alternating numbering system (odd/even groups) rather than random assignment, no significant statistical differences were observed between the two groups regarding baseline variables such as age, gender, or pre-class theoretical knowledge test scores, thereby further reducing the risk of selection bias and its impact on internal validity. Accordingly, the present findings should be regarded as preliminary evidence supporting the exploratory effectiveness of this teaching model. Future research should employ randomized controlled designs, systematically assess and statistically control for potential confounders—such as learning motivation, self-directed learning ability, and teaching model acceptance—using validated standardized scales at baseline, and incorporate quasi-experimental approaches, including propensity score matching or instrumental variable methods, to more rigorously isolate the independent effect of the teaching model.

In terms of case analysis ability and operative skills scores, the research group scored higher than the control group (P < 0.001). This observation suggests that the OBE-based multi-track teaching model may be associated with the development of clinical thinking patterns and practical operative abilities. This potential association may be explained by the model’s emphasis on integrating practical operations with theoretical knowledge. Through the combined use of PBL and CBL, students are guided to solve practical problems, which may deepen their understanding of operative skills. Additionally, TBL combined with multi-station teaching enables students to receive systematic clinical skills training within limited timeframes, facilitate peer learning and exchange, and enable students to acquire knowledge and experience from diverse perspectives. This finding is consistent with Li Sun et al. [13], who reported that multi-station teaching significantly improves students’ operative skills (P < 0.001), and extends prior work by highlighting the potential importance of group collaboration (TBL) in skill learning—a view shared by Liu Qiang et al. [25], who noted that “group collaboration can promote the consolidation and application of skills.” However, all scores for operative skills, case analysis, doctor-patient communication, and teamwork were assessed by the same teaching team that delivered the intervention, without the use of standardized objective instruments. Consequently, these results may be susceptible to observer bias and expectation effects arising from teachers’ subjective judgments. Although the observed associations are promising, verification through more rigorously designed studies employing independent blinded assessors and validated objective instruments is warranted to confirm the true efficacy of this teaching model.

Statistical analysis revealed no significant differences in theoretical knowledge between the two student groups (P > 0.05). No notable disparities were observed in curriculum content mastery or knowledge retention, indicating that traditional teaching methodologies still demonstrate certain advantages in knowledge delivery.Systematic instruction of theoretical concepts ensures comprehensive understanding of foundational knowledge. As theoretical foundations serve as the basis for medical students ‘subsequent clinical practice, conventional teaching approaches excel in knowledge transmission and memorization, providing students with solid theoretical grounding. In educational reform, complementary strengths can be leveraged while optimizing and integrating teaching methods within traditional frameworks. The study’s key contribution lies in its innovative integration of diverse teaching approaches guided by the Outcomes-Based Education (OBE) philosophy, emphasizing holistic student development (including clinical thinking, practical skills, and teamwork competencies).

The CBL + TBL teaching methodology may enable students to engage in physician-patient communication and teamwork within real or simulated clinical settings. Through collaborative task completion, students may not only master patient communication skills but also develop teamwork competencies, enhancing team cohesion and operational efficiency. Physician-patient communication and collaborative skills remain key objectives in modern medical education. This instructional approach may facilitate students’ adaptation to clinical environments and improve healthcare service quality. Real-world outpatient interactions may allow students to apply acquired knowledge and skills in authentic clinical scenarios, while clinical practice application application may be cultivated through patient diagnosis plans and comprehensive decision-making processes [26, 27].

The research group’s overall satisfaction with the internship course was significantly higher than that of the control group (P < 0.001), suggesting that the multi-track teaching model may be more welcomed by students.It may improve students’ satisfaction with the course.The student-centered teaching model may stimulate students’ learning initiative and initiative.The multi-track teaching model, through the organic combination of multiple teaching methods, enriches the teaching content and forms, meeting the diversified learning needs of students, thereby potentially improving their satisfaction with the course.In addition, there were no significant differences in learning pressure, pre-class workload, and post-class workload between the two groups of students, indicating that the multi-track teaching model did not appear to increase students’ learning burden, but may have improved learning effectiveness through reasonable arrangement of teaching content and forms, allowing students to improve learning effectiveness without increasing learning burden.

Limitations and Future Directions: (1) The sample size was relatively small (n = 212) and limited to a single medical school, which may affect the generalizability of the study findings; (2) The absence of randomization in this study could introduce selection bias, but no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups regarding baseline characteristics (gender, age) or pre-test scores (P > 0.05), thereby further reducing the risk of selection bias and its impact on internal validity; (3) The involvement of teaching faculty in assessing student performance may have introduced observer bias, but all experimental groups received standardized teaching quality delivered by the same faculty team, with evaluations conducted using standardized scales, ensuring consistency in instructional conditions across groups. This approach mitigated teacher assignment bias and observer bias; (4) The assessment tools employed (pre-test, post-test, and teacher evaluation scales) were self-developed and lacked systematic validity and reliability validation, necessitating cautious interpretation of results to avoid misleading conclusions due to overgeneralization. For future research, we aim to employ a randomized controlled design, independent blinded evaluators, and assessment tools validated for reliability and validity to further validate the efficacy of this teaching model in ophthalmology education. Additionally, we anticipate expanding the sample size and conducting multicenter trials to further confirm the effectiveness and applicability of this teaching model.

Conclusion

The results of this study indicate that the OBE concept-based multi-track teaching model was associated with enhanced performance in clinical reasoning, practical skills, and self-directed learning among students during pediatric ophthalmology clinical internship, suggesting its potential value for improving ophthalmology clinical teaching quality. However, given the non-randomized group allocation, potential confounding factors, unvalidated and partially subjective assessment tools, and absence of blinding, these associations should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory findings rather than established causal effects. Therefore, further research is needed to confirm the true efficacy of this model through rigorously designed randomized controlled trials that employ independent blinded assessors and validated objective instruments.
