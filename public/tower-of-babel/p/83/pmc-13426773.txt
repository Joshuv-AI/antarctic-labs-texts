Soil nitrogen oxide emissions and their impacts on air quality over the United States
Authors: Grant A Parajuli, Lina Luo, Golam Sarwar, Daniel S Cohan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoil emissions of nitric oxide (NO) and nitrous acid (HONO) contribute to ozone, which is becoming more sensitive to non-fossil fuel nitrogen oxides (NOx = NO, NO2) as fossil fuel emissions decline. Soil emissions of NO and/or HONO (SNO/HONO) arise from complex biogeochemical processes and are intensified by nitrogen additions from fertilizers. Despite this, most air quality models use simple parameterizations of these processes to quantify soil NO emissions. In this study, we compare the Yienger & Levy (YL) and the Berkeley-Dalhousie Soil NOx Parameterization (BDSNP) soil NO models. These models are compared with the mechanistic Fertilizer Emissions Scenario Tool for CMAQ (FEST-C*) that simulates agricultural soil NO and HONO emissions. We quantify the impacts of each emission estimate on ozone and particulate matter with the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model over the United States. We compare modeled nitrogen dioxide (NO2) concentrations with observations at rural monitoring sites and modeled NO2 vertical column densities with satellite measurements. Agricultural SNO/HONO from FEST-C* were double that of YL but two-thirds that of BDSNP. FEST-C* improved CMAQ's agreement with some rural NO2 observations. Satellite NO2 columns showed FEST-C* having intermediate correlation and bias with the lowest bias over agricultural areas. We also examine how each SNO/HONO estimate affects ozone pollution in several cities near agricultural areas. Higher SNO/HONO increased the number of days exceeding ozone regulatory thresholds in some cities. This study highlights needs for field SNO/HONO observations, better fertilizer use estimates, and mitigating the impact of agricultural emissions on ambient pollution.Keywords: Ozone, Soil NO and HONO emissions, Model evaluation, Agroecosystem modeling

Introduction

Tropospheric nitrogen oxides (NOx = NO, NO2) and nitrous acid (HONO) are precursors for ozone (O3) and contribute to particulate matter (PM) by forming nitrate (NO3-). Ozone and PM are known to cause significant health impacts (Donzelli and Suarez-Varela, 2024; Sharma et al., 2024) and are subject to ambient air quality regulations. Soils are a globally significant source of NO, contributing ~15–20% of global emissions (Hudman et al., 2012; Steinkamp and Lawrence, 2011; Vinken et al., 2014; Weng et al., 2020). As fossil fuel NOx emissions have declined since the early 2000s (EPA, 2025), U.S. ozone concentrations have become increasingly sensitive to other sources of NOx, including soil emissions (Geddes et al., 2022; Huber et al., 2024). Hudman et al., (2012) estimated that fertilizer applications are responsible for 17% of global soil NO emissions. Several U.S. regions with intense agricultural activity, such as the San Joaquin Valley in California (Lieb et al., 2024; Lieb and Faloona, 2025; Zhu et al., 2023) and the Corn Belt in the Midwest (Griffis et al., 2019; Huber et al., 2024), have substantial ozone impacts from soil NO emissions. Characterizing soil NO emissions is thus important to inform ozone and PM reduction strategies.

Several studies have documented that satellite-observed NO2 columns have not declined as quickly as expected based on modeled reductions in U.S. fossil fuel NOx emissions (Qu et al., 2021; Silvern et al., 2019). Different sources such as lightning, aircraft emissions, and the photolysis of particulate NO3 may be slowing the decline of NO2 (East et al., 2022; Shah et al., 2023). Soil NO can also contribute to modeled vertical NO2 profiles, especially in areas with intense agricultural activity (Feng et al., 2025; Huber et al., 2020; Vinken et al., 2014).

The magnitude and timing of U.S. soil NO emissions remain uncertain. For instance, estimates of spatially averaged California cropland NO emissions vary widely (Lieb et al., 2024) between 0.48 kg N ha−1 yr−1 (Guo et al., 2020) and 19.8 kg N ha−1 yr−1 (Almaraz et al., 2018). Instantaneous measured NO fluxes in the Imperial Valley were as high as 284 kg N ha−1 yr−1 (Oikawa et al., 2015). Vinken et al. (2014) found that the Hudman soil emissions scheme caused their model to over-estimate satellite-observed NO2 over the U.S. Midwest. However, their study did not provide an explanation for this overestimation. Some studies have scaled back model estimates of soil NO emissions based on the Vinken et al. findings (Silvern et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). Variations in fertilizer application amounts and timing during the growing season can strongly affect emissions seasonality and pollution impacts (Luo et al., 2025). Soil NO models often make simplifying assumptions to distribute fertilizer applications within the growing season (Cao et al., 2020; Hudman et al., 2012; Ren et al., 2025).

Soil NO emissions are caused mainly by microbially driven nitrogen cycling. One pathway is through nitrification, by which NH4+ is oxidized to NO2- and then NO3- (Luo et al., 2025; Venterea and Rolston, 2000). Denitrification can anaerobically reduce NO3- in the soil to N2 (Izaurralde et al., 2017). Rates of nitrification and denitrification and associated emissions of NO and N2O to the atmosphere vary with environmental conditions (Firestone and Davidson, 1989). Intermediate NO2- in the soil from nitrification may form HONO, which can be emitted directly or further form and emit NO (Oswald et al., 2013; Venterea and Rolston, 2000). HONO may also form during denitrification in moist soils (Wu et al., 2019). The ratio between soil HONO and NO emissions depends on soil conditions, with some studies reporting that HONO emissions can be comparable to or even greater than NO under certain conditions and negligible in others (Oswald et al., 2013; Wang et al., 2023). Tropospheric HONO rapidly photolyzes to NO and OH in the troposphere and thus contributes to ozone formation (Sha et al., 2024).

Several modeling schemes have attempted to parameterize soil NO and/or HONO emissions (SNO/HONO) based on meteorology, land use, fertilizer applications, N deposition, canopy reduction, and pulsing mechanisms. The two most widely used soil NO schemes in U.S. air quality modeling are the Yienger and Levy (YL) parametrization (Yienger and Levy, 1995) and the Berkeley-Dalhousie Soil NOx Parameterization (BDSNP) (Hudman et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2021). Both schemes have been integrated as online options in full-scale chemical transport models, which simulate photochemical and meteorological processes to estimate how concentrations of various atmospheric pollutants vary with time. Although the original YL and BDSNP parameterizations neglected HONO emissions, some modelers have added HONO to BDSNP (Sha et al., 2024) or developed their own parameterizations that include HONO (Ren et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2022).

Other models represent soil nitrogen cycling and emissions mechanistically, tracking the evolution of soil properties and nutrient content as agricultural activities and natural processes occur. Several agroecosystem models have been used to model cropland emissions, including DAYCENT (Parton et al., 1998), DNDC (Li et al., 2000), APSIM (Keating et al., 2003), and several others. In contrast to parametric models, mechanistic models can simulate nitrogen cycling in a more detailed way, considering losses to the atmosphere, water, crop uptake, and nitrogen retained in soils. One example, the Fertilizer Emissions Scenario Tool for CMAQ (FEST-C) models U.S. crop-specific soil nitrogen and carbon pools using the Environmental Policy Integrated Climate (EPIC) agricultural model for up to 42 different crop types (Cooter et al., 2012; Ran et al., 2019) and was designed to interface with the Community Multiscale Air Quality (CMAQ) model (U.S. EPA Office of Research and Development, 2024). Luo et al. (2022) modified FEST-C to simulate NO emissions from both nitrification and denitrification and HONO emissions from nitrification (Fig. 1) and used it to estimate agricultural soil emissions of NO, HONO, NH3, and N2O in the United States. This modified FEST-C model is known as FEST-C*. FEST-C and FEST-C* have also been extended to China (Fu et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2025).

Diagram of the FEST-C* modeling framework and its integration with CMAQ in this study, including selected soil factors. Adapted from Luo et al. (2022).

In this study, we compare SNO/HONO estimates from two parametric models, YL and BDSNP, with mechanistic estimates from FEST-C* over the continental United States. We apply each set of SNO/HONO estimates in CMAQ to examine associated impacts on ozone, PM, and NO2. The simulations are evaluated using ground-level observations of NO2 and satellite observations of NO2 columns. We also assess the contributions of SNO/HONO to ozone and PM.

Methods

Model configuration

We applied the CMAQ model version 5.4 over a domain that includes the continental United States with 12 km × 12km resolution for a simulation period of April 1 through September 30, 2017. Input meteorology, initial and boundary conditions, and emissions from all sources other than soils were taken from the EPA's Air QUAlity TimE Series (EQUATES) project (Foley et al., 2023). The chemical mechanism was Carbon Bond 6 release 5, with particulate NO3 photolysis included as described in section 2.4. We used CMAQ's M3dry module to simulate dry deposition and NH3 bi-directional fluxes in a manner consistent with EPIC (Pleim et al., 2019). Input EPIC files containing soil NH3 pools and properties for NH3 bi-directional flux were from our own simulations for 2017 using the FEST-C* model with the same configuration as Luo et al. (2022) including fertilizer upscaling to match 2017 fertilizer usage data (Falcone, 2021). Biogenic VOC emissions were simulated with the Model of Emissions of Gases and Aerosols from Nature (MEGAN) version 3.2 implemented in-line in CMAQ (Kang et al., 2024). Soil NO emissions estimates were unique to each modeling scenario as described in section 2.2. We also include modifications from CMAQ version 5.5 (U.S. EPA Office of Research and Development, 2024) for N deposition and dry period calculation in MEGAN BDSNP.

Soil NOx models

The YL and BDSNP soil NO schemes implemented in CMAQ in-line MEGAN version 3.2 were each used to model soil NO emissions in separate scenarios (Table 1). Time-independent MEGAN input land cover and other environmental conditions were downloaded from 2018 EPA modeling inputs based on EQUATES (U.S. EPA, 2023). To obtain BDSNP fertilizer input data representative of 2017, we use fertilizer data simulated by FEST-C*, similar to Rasool et al. (2016). Gridded crop fraction data for FEST-C* in NLCD40 MODIS categories were from Luo et al. (2022), generated using 2011 NLCD and MODIS satellite data. MEGAN input cropland fraction was adjusted to match the land fraction used in FEST-C*, with a corresponding reduction in other land use categories to preserve the ratios between them while not exceeding 100% total land use. By default, the BDSNP scheme in MEGAN simulates emissions only to the dominant land use category in each cell based on 24 soil biome categories (Steinkamp and Lawrence, 2011). We modified this scheme to account for the individual land fractions of each cell using the mapping between NLCD40 MODIS categories and soil biome categories from Rasool et al. (2016) to translate the land fractions from FEST-C* into the categories used in BDSNP. The agricultural and non-agricultural emissions in each modeling scheme were separated based on fractional land use category, where croplands, cultivated crops, and cropland/vegetation mosaic categories in the NLCD40 MODIS scheme were considered agriculturally managed. We separated agricultural emissions in each modeling scheme into regions using the Farm Resource Regions defined by the United States Department of Agriculture (Heimlich, 2000).

FEST-C* was used to generate a mechanistic representation of agricultural SNO/HONO (Table 1). HONO emissions were calculated as a function of soil NO emissions from nitrification based on a maximum ratio of HONO to NO (0.429) and an adjustment based on soil water content (Luo et al., 2022; Rasool et al., 2019). FEST-C* estimates of SNO/HONO were weighted according to each crop and summed. The same canopy reduction factor implemented in BDSNP was applied to FEST-C* SNO/HONO (Wang et al., 1998). Since FEST-C* is only capable of simulating U.S. agricultural SNO/HONO, non-agricultural and non-U.S. agricultural NO emissions were taken from the BDSNP scenario. Daily FEST-C* SNO/HONO were partitioned into hourly data by using the hourly NO emissions profile from BDSNP for each cell. In cells where BDSNP did not simulate any soil NO emissions, FEST-C* SNO/HONO were divided equally over the day.

Model evaluations with observational data

Model estimates of surface hourly NO2 concentrations are compared with observational data from the U.S. EPA Air Quality System (AQS; https://www.epa.gov/aqs, last access August 20, 2025) and the Clean Air Status and Trends Network (CASTNET; https://www.epa.gov/castnet, last access August 20, 2025). Site data were matched in time between model and observations with the Atmospheric Model Evaluation Tool (Appel et al., 2011) version 1.5. All CASTNET sites are rural, and for the AQS network we limited our NO2 analysis to monitors in rural or agricultural areas (Harkey and Holloway, 2024).

We also compare model estimates of NO2 vertical column density (VCD) with observations from the Ozone Monitoring Instrument (OMI), using data from the standard product version 4 retrieval (Lamsal et al., 2021). OMI data were regridded to the CMAQ grid using the CMAQ Satellite Processor (https://github.com/barronh/cmaqsatproc) with an area-weighted average approach (Sarwar et al., 2025). Results

Spatial and temporal emissions comparisons

The three modeling schemes differed substantially in the timing and magnitude of agricultural SNO/HONO. Fig. 2 shows the spatial pattern of simulated U.S. agricultural SNO/HONO averaged over the simulation period. BDSNP mean emissions were 3 times higher than YL, while FEST-C* mean emissions were 2 times as high as YL (Fig. 2). All models simulated emissions to be highest in the Heartland, the region containing the most cropland (Fig. 3). BDSNP simulated higher emissions than other schemes in the Heartland, Fruitful Rim, Mississippi Portal, and Prairie Gateway regions, which are the regions that have the largest amounts of cropland and fertilizer applications. FEST-C* simulated more emissions than other schemes for the Northern Great Plains (Fig. 3), the region with the most wheat crops (Heimlich, 2000).

Maps of modeled agricultural SNO/HONO by each modeling scheme, averaged over April-September, 2017.

Histogram of regional average agricultural NOx emissions, summed over all timesteps. Regions are defined by USDA farm resource regions (Heimlich, 2000).

A time series of emissions rates shows that BDSNP and FEST-C* exhibited similar variability and seasonal patterns whereas YL simulated lower and less temporally variable emission rates (Fig. 4). In the spring and early summer, BDSNP simulated higher emissions peaks, while FEST-C* simulated slightly higher peaks in mid-May and in late summer. Emissions were similar between BDSNP and FEST-C* from mid-August through September. Both BDSNP and FEST-C* simulated spikes in emissions in mid-May and mid-June, with BDSNP showing an especially large peak on June 14, 2017 (Fig. 4). Closer examination of the mid-June event shows that it occurred mainly in Midwest states amid substantial fertilizer applications when rain followed an extended dry period (Fig. S1). Rain following a dry period is known to increase pulsing in soils by activating soil microbes (Hudman et al., 2012; Rasool et al., 2016; Yienger and Levy, 1995).

Daily timeseries of U.S. agricultural SNO/HONO averaged over all model grid cells with non-zero cropland fraction.

April-September SNO/HONO estimates are compared to the 2017 EPA National Emissions Inventory (NEI) for all soil NO emissions (Fig. 5), distributed by month based on each month's proportion of annual emissions (U.S. EPAO, 2017). The molar weight of NO2 was used to convert modeled emissions to compare with NEI biogenic NO emissions. MEGAN YL emissions were 0.85 times that of the NEI from April through September. On the other hand, BDSNP and FEST-C* total emissions were, respectively, 2.7 and 2.4 times the level of the NEI emissions. In both schemes, the contribution from BDSNP non-agricultural emissions was higher than simulated agricultural emissions (Fig. 5).

One reason for YĽs lower soil NO emissions magnitude (Fig. 5) and variability (Fig. 4) could be the scheme's treatment of fertilizer applications. In the model, the growing season is latitude-dependent, comprising May through October over most U.S. agricultural regions. Agricultural soil NO emissions are assumed to be at their strongest during the first month of the growing season and then linearly decay to zero over the rest of the growing season. After the growing season, croplands are treated similarly to grasslands. This fertilizer parameter is unaffected by any estimate of the magnitude or spatial variation of fertilizer use, leading to emissions uniformity and much lower overall emissions. Our emissions estimates using YL over California cropland are less than half those reported by Guo et al., (2020) using the DNDC model, which itself was the lowest estimate compiled from the literature by Lieb et al., (2024); Kang et al., (2024) also found low emissions estimates over California using the YL scheme in MEGANv3.2.

The BDSNP scheme yielded the highest estimates of agricultural soil NO emissions in all regions except the Northern Great Plains (Figs. 2 and 3). Emissions were initially stronger in BDSNP than FEST-C* in April, with some differently timed peaks in May and an exceptionally high emissions pulse on June 14th (Fig. 4, S1). This pulsing event in the Heartland resulted from concurrent fertilizer applications, high temperatures, and a drought for the two weeks prior to a large precipitation event (Fig. S1). Emissions then remained relatively close in spatial average magnitude over the rest of the simulation. A major reason for similar seasonality between FEST-C* and BDSNP is the use of FEST-C* simulated fertilizer applications in the BDSNP scenario. Previous simulations using the original BDSNP input fertilizer applications (Fig. S2) had strong increases in soil NO emissions persisting throughout the summer and early fall (Fig. S3), which could result in substantial MDA8 ozone impacts in summer, when ozone is particularly sensitive to NOx emissions.

SNO/HONO estimates in the FEST-C* scenario were closer to BDSNP than to YL. FEST-C* simulated higher emissions than BDSNP in the Northern Crescent, Eastern Uplands, and Northern Great Plains (Fig. 3). FEST-C* simulated smaller magnitudes than BDSNP in notable agricultural regions like the Heartland, Prairie Gateway, and Fruitful Rim. Despite these differences in overall simulated magnitudes, the relative ratios between regions in FEST-C* and BDSNP were similar (Fig. 3) showing that the modeling schemes similarly represented regions with intensive agriculture. The notable exceptions are the Northern Great Plains and Mississippi Portal, where FEST-C* simulated the highest and lowest emissions out of all three modeling schemes, respectively (Fig. 3). Since FEST-C* mechanistically simulates several processes that are more simplistically parametrized in BDSNP and YL (Fig. 1), it exhibits more spatiotemporal variability (Figs. 2 and 4), especially in agricultural regions (Fig. 3). For example, the mid-June pulsing event is significantly lower in FEST-C* than BDSNP (Fig. S1A and B) despite sharing fertilizer application input data and similar methods for pulsing (Luo et al., 2022). This could be the result of explicitly modeling the nitrogen in the soil from fertilizer applications and nitrification and denitrification rates instead of assuming a certain fraction of fertilizer applications is emitted after application. Additionally, pulsing in FEST-C* decays daily (Luo et al., 2022), while the in-line BDSNP calculates the pulsing factor hourly (Hudman et al., 2012), resulting in a higher peak pulsing factor immediately after soil re-wetting. While pulsing in YL depends on precipitation amount, BDSNP and FEST-C* instead consider changes in soil moisture to cross a wet-dry threshold (Rasool et al., 2019).

Evaluation of rural NO2

Since SNO/HONO is just one of many factors that affects model predictions of ambient ozone and NO2 concentrations, the apparent performance of the SNO/HONO schemes can depend strongly on other aspects of the photochemical model that affect ozone and NO2 predictions. In particular, neglecting pNO3 photolysis may lead some models to underpredict ozone and NO2 by omitting the return of gas-phase NOx from the particulate state. Our inclusion of pNO3 photolysis substantially reduced model underestimation of MDA8 ozone overall but resulted in small overestimates at some sites (Fig. S4B). Those overestimates persisted even in sensitivity cases with the removal of all SNO/HONO (Fig. S4A).

Higher SNO/HONO in the BDSNP and FEST-C* scenarios generally led to higher modeled estimates of NO2, with increases within 1 ppb. Rural NO2 monitoring site comparisons showed that the FEST-C* scenario improved bias in a slight majority of the sites when compared with YL (Fig. 6D) in California, the Northern Great Plains, and the central Heartland with worsened bias in the southeast (Fig. 6C). Mixed performance was seen in the central Western U.S. FEST-C* improved bias in a slight majority of sites over BDSNP (Fig. 6B), including the Northern Great Plains and Texas, with some worsened bias in California (Fig. 6A).

April-September mean NO2 bias difference maps and histograms of model/obs pairs for (A,B) FEST-C* and BDSNP and (C,D) FEST-C* and YL. Warm colors represent locations where the former scheme is more biased than the latter, with cool colors representing lower bias.

Rural NO2 bias comparisons showed that FEST-C* outperformed BDSNP over the Northern Great Plains and Texas (Fig. 6A and B). This suggests that FEST-C* may be averting some overestimates of soil NO emissions by BDSNP (Fig. S3). Vinken et al., (2014) found that BDSNP overestimated Midwestern summer soil NO emissions by 50%, which could be driven by default fertilizer applications peaking in the summer (Fig. S2). That has led some studies to apply a reduction factor to BDSNP estimates of soil NO emissions (Silvern et al., 2019; Wang et al., 2021). Some of the overestimates of NO emissions by BDSNP in previous studies may have resulted from overestimates of fertilizer use. Even after we adjusted BDSNP to match the lower fertilizer application rates from FEST-C*, its emission estimates remained higher than FEST-C* and YL.

FEST-C* showed rural NO2 bias improvements over YL in regions spatially co-located with agricultural activities, such as in the Northern Crescent, Heartland, and California (Fig. 6C and D). It is important to note that FEST-C* and YL comparisons include not only the impact of switching agricultural SNO/HONO schemes, but also the impact of BDSNP non-agricultural emissions over YL non-agricultural emissions (Table 1). Comparisons with the 2017 NEI demonstrate that BDSNP non-agricultural emissions dominate over agricultural emissions in both the FEST-C* and BDSNP scenarios (Fig. 5). These high non-agricultural emissions may be due to the high emissions factors for evergreen needleleaf forests and grasslands, representing the two highest non-agricultural emissions factors (Steinkamp and Lawrence, 2011). Thus, while the FEST-C* scenario does show worse bias in the west than YL, some decreased performance is likely due to large non-agricultural soil NO emissions from the BDSNP scheme that were used in this scenario.

Evaluation with OMI satellite data

Modeled NO2 VCDs exhibited differences with OMI VCDs over the continental U.S. in all modeling scenarios on seasonal scales, while all modeling scenarios simulated smaller NO2 columns than OMI (Fig. 7). Higher NO emissions in BDSNP led to the lowest bias and error of any models but the weakest correlation, while the lowest NO emissions in YL had the highest bias and error but the strongest correlation. FEST-C* had intermediate performance for correlation but lower bias and error than the YL scenario. The spatial distribution of VCD differences averaged over the simulation period is shown for each modeling scheme in Fig. 8. The YL scenario underestimated NO2 VCDs in most regions with slight overestimates in the northwestern U.S. (Fig. 8A). The higher SNO/HONO in BDSNP and FEST-C* broadened this overestimated area and increased its magnitude (Fig. 8B and C). BDSNP also overestimated NO2 VCDs over the Midwest during the mid-June pulsing event (Fig. S1).

All three modeling schemes displayed overall underestimates when compared with OMI NO2 VCDs, consistent with previous comparisons of CMAQ results with satellite data (East et al., 2022; Sarwar et al., 2025; Silvern et al., 2019). One caveat to these previous results and the results in this study is the selection of only comparing non-cloudy NO2 VCDs. A recent study found that surface NO2 measurements and model biases may be larger on cloudy days than on clear-sky days (Goldberg et al., 2025). While including pNO3 photolysis could improve the discrepancy in model biases, further work is needed to identify the differences.

BDSNP simulations had the lowest normalized mean bias and error of the modeling scenarios but also the lowest correlation coefficient when seasonal averages were compared with OMI VCDs (Fig. 7B). The exceptionally large mid-June pulsing event in the Midwest (Fig. S1A) resulted in emissions considerably higher than any other SNO/HONO modeling scheme, deviating strongly from OMI VCDs in June (Fig. S6) and increasing the full season Midwestern average bias (Fig. 8B). While some of this higher peak may be due to using FEST-C* fertilizer use data with BDSNP, the original BDSNP fertilizer use had persistently high emissions throughout the summer (Fig. S3), which could exacerbate comparisons with OMI. The reduced overall correlation with OMI signals that some cells may be simulated too high, thus reducing normalized mean bias but also decreasing correlation.

YL emissions had the best correlation with OMI VCDs but also the highest normalized mean bias and error (Fig. 7A) over most of the simulation domain (Fig. 8A). Cells associated with agricultural areas in the Heartland and Prairie Gateway regions appear to be slightly low biased in the YL scheme. The reduction of positive bias in the northwest U.S. compared to the BDSNP and FEST-C* scenarios improved correlation in the YL scenario (Figs. 7 and 8). California NO2 VCDs are also underestimated in the YL scenario, supporting that California SNO/HONO may be underestimated in this scenario. This modeling scenario demonstrates that low SNO/HONO estimates can contribute to negative biases when compared with OMI in agricultural regions.

The FEST-C* modeling scenario was intermediate between the two other modeling schemes in terms of both normalized mean bias/error and correlation, showing improved correlation but worse bias than BDSNP and improved bias but worse correlation than YL (Figs. 7 and 8). The large estimates of non-agricultural soil emissions by BDSNP, especially over evergreen needleleaf forests (Fig. S7), also play a role. Note that our FEST-C* runs combine FEST-C* agricultural emissions estimates with BDSNP estimates for non-agricultural soils. The northwestern U.S. is a region not dominated by cropland (Fig. S5) where FEST-C* and BDSNP have worse bias than YL (Fig. 8). FEST-C* and BDSNP show similar performance in this region, which could mean that BDSNP non-agricultural NO emissions (Fig. S7) are driving this difference in performance with YL. The FEST-C* scenario performs better than any modeling scheme in the Heartland region, showing slight low bias instead of a high bias in BDSNP (Fig. 8B and C) or larger low bias in YL (Fig. 8A–C). This is a region of intense agricultural activity and emissions in both modeling schemes and is thus important in model evaluation (Fig. 2).

Impacts on MDA8 ozone and PM2.5

Modeled daily maximum 8-h average (MDA8) ozone in each modeling scheme in the 3×3 cells surrounding the central latitude and longitude of selected U.S. cities are shown in Fig. 9, following a similar approach as Huber et al., (2024). Fresno, CA is located in the San Joaquin Valley, an area with intense agricultural activities known to be impacted by SNO/HONO (Lieb et al., 2025). Denver, CO is a city with close proximity to agricultural soils (Fig. S5). While Louisville, KY is not immediately adjacent to cropland, it could be affected by ozone transport from upwind cropland (Huber et al., 2024). All of these cities are in areas with 2010 populations over 1 million that are classified by the U.S. EPA as non-attainment areas, with design values above the 70-ppb ozone standard as of February 4th, 2026 (U.S. EPA, 2024a, 2026). The choice of SNO/HONO modeling scheme can result in substantial variation in the number of MDA8 ozone days violating the 70-ppb standard. FEST-C* showed small decreases in the number of MDA8 ozone days in each city when comparing with BDSNP, which highlights the impact of changing agricultural SNO/HONO. High BDSNP non-agricultural soil NO emissions also had strong effects on ozone, more than doubling the amount of MDA8 ozone days for Fresno, CA and Denver, CO when comparing with the no SNO/HONO scenario (Table 2). Comparing these results with the cases with only non-agricultural soil NO or no SNO/HONO emissions also identifies the urban ozone pollution impacts of non-agricultural soil NO emissions and SNO/HONO, respectively (Table 2).

Temporal average fine PM (PM2.5) concentrations demonstrate that the FEST-C* scenario and BDSNP simulated slightly higher PM2.5 concentrations than YL in most regions (Fig. S8). The maximum increase was 0.5 μg/m3 around the San Joaquin Valley, with smaller increases in the Pacific Northwest, Upper Midwest, and Southeastern United States. The strongest effects occurred in regions that may be in non-attainment of the tightened 9.0 μg/m3 NAAQS standard based on 2020–2022 monitoring data (U.S. EPA, 2024a; 2024b), including the San Joaquin Valley. Thus, while overall effects are minor, SNO/HONO could contribute to PM2.5 concentrations in an area that could exceed the 9.0 μg/m3 NAAQS standard.

Conclusion

Limitations in the model and in the availability of observational data make it impossible to definitively conclude which scheme is giving the most accurate estimates of SNO/HONO. Nevertheless, our results help illuminate differences among the schemes. The mechanistic FEST-C* model produced agricultural SNO/HONO estimates that were intermediate between the low estimates of YL and the high estimates of BDSNP. BDSNP and FEST-C* exhibited more temporal variation in emissions than YL, with a particularly strong pulsing event in BDSNP. Site bias comparisons showed mixed performance, with FEST-C* showing improvements in some areas. Comparisons with OMI NO2 VCDs showed that FEST-C* and BDSNP had strong impacts on modeled NO2 VCD biases in both agricultural and non-agricultural areas. FEST-C* showed the lowest NO2 VCD bias in the central U.S., but also higher biases than YL in other areas and a worse correlation. On the other hand, the YL scenario had the strongest correlation yet a substantial low bias when compared with NO2 VCDs. Choosing a different SNO/HONO modeling scheme could increase the number of MDA8 ozone days modeled in several cities affected by agricultural SNO/HONO emissions. PM2.5 differences were small but were in areas that may be in non-attainment. The inclusion of pNO3 photolysis also substantially reduced modeled low biases for MDA8 ozone and NO2 VCDs, which enabled a more accurate comparison of agricultural SNO/HONO but resulted in positive biases for MDA8 ozone in the eastern U.S. even in the absence of any SNO/HONO. Future work should attempt to constrain agricultural SNO/HONO using field measurements, including the relative magnitude of U.S. NO and HONO emissions and ideally their timing. The timing and magnitude of fertilizer applications is a significant and uncertain factor in agricultural SNO/HONO models. Input fertilizer use strongly impacts emissions magnitude and timing, which could affect estimates of peak ozone pollution. Studies modeling SNO/HONO should also clearly identify agricultural and non-agricultural sources to inform studies on pollution mitigation.
