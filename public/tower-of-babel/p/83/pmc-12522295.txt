Computational pathology approach for assessment of prognosis and immunotherapy response in pan-gastrointestinal cancer
Authors: Taojun Zhang, Zepang Sun, Zhe Li, M. Usman Ahmad, Md Tauhidul Islam, Fan Yang, Xianqi Yang, Lin Wu, Jingjing Xie, Wanying Feng, Yan Chen, Alyssa A. Guo, Guoxin Li, Wei Wang, Jiang Yu, Zhenhui Li, Yuming Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCurrent cancer staging methods cannot accurately predict survival outcomes and therapeutic benefits in cancer patients. Digital pathomics, a rapidly evolving field, holds significant potential to revolutionize disease evaluation.MethodsThis international multicenter study included 2463 patients with pan-gastrointestinal cancer from twelve cohorts of seven cancer centers, of whom 1653 patients were diagnosed with gastric cancer (GC). We proposed a deep learning pathomics signature (DLPS) by integrating information on three scales from the whole slide images (WSIs) of H&E-stained tissue, including pathomic features related to the nucleus, microenvironment, and single-cell spatial distribution. Next, we assessed the predictive accuracy of the DLPS for prognosis, chemotherapy response, and immunotherapy response.ResultsOur findings revealed that the DLPS was significantly associated with overall survival in gastric cancer (GC) and pan-gastrointestinal cancers, exhibiting good accuracy with area under the curve values ranging from 0.723 to 0.840 across GC cohorts. Multivariable Cox regression analyses indicated that the DLPS is an independent prognostic factor. The nomogram that integrated the DLPS and TNM stage showed improved performance in predicting cancer prognosis compared to that with TNM stage alone (P < 0.05). Importantly, GC patients with low-DLPS (but not those with high-DLPS) exhibited substantial benefits from adjuvant chemotherapy (P < 0.05). Furthermore, the objective response rate to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy was significantly higher in the low-DLPS group (29.6%) compared to the high-DLPS group (8.3%, P < 0.05).ConclusionsThe DLPS has the potential to enhance prognosis assessment and identify patients who are likely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy and immunotherapy in pan-gastrointestinal cancers, as well as in other solid tumors.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-06181-3.

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) is one of the most common malignancies and the third leading cause of cancer-related deaths worldwide [1, 2]. Currently, the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) staging system is the most commonly used system for prognostic prediction and treatment decision-making [3, 4]. Chemotherapy is one of the basic treatments for GC, and the current standard of adjuvant chemotherapy is fluorouracil and platinum-based [5]. However, substantial variability in clinical outcomes and treatment responses is observed even among patients with identical TNM stages and similar therapeutic regimens, indicating the limitations of the current staging system [6]. Meanwhile, immunotherapy for programmed death 1 (PD-1) and programmed death ligand-1 (PD-L1) has emerged as a transformative approach in oncology [7], though its efficacy in GC is highly heterogeneous, often leading to suboptimal outcomes and high costs [8]. Therefore, identifying patients with high sensitivity to chemotherapy and immunotherapy could significantly improve survival rates and quality of life by enabling more tailored treatment strategies.

Traditional biomarkers such as carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen (CA) 19–9 have been proposed to provide additional assessment of prognosis and treatment response in various cancers [9–11]. However, their clinical utility is limited due to low sensitivity and specificity [12]. Advances in technology have facilitated the exploration of novel tumor markers, including genomic sequencing [13], circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) analysis [14], and immunohistochemistry (IHC) PD-L1 staining [15]. Although these methods deepen our understanding of GC heterogeneity, their widespread clinical adoption is hindered by high costs, time demands, and technical complexity [15].

The tumor microenvironment (TME) has been increasingly recognized as a critical driver of cancer progression and therapy resistance [16, 17]. The TME is a complex system composed of tumor cells, lymphocytes, myelocytes, stromal cells, and non-cellular components, which are associated with tumor proliferation, metastasis, angiogenesis, and immune modulation [18–22]. Moreover, interactions between tumor cells and the TME are increasingly being recognized as key regulators of chemotherapy and immunotherapy responses [23–25]. Thus, elucidating the TME’s contribution to tumor heterogeneity offers valuable insights into prognostic assessment and therapeutic optimization. Interestingly, a large number of studies have highlighted that digital hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining slides provide a rich repository of multidimensional TME information [26], which can be transformed into mineable features for tumor evaluation.

H&E-stained slides are routinely used by pathologists for diagnosing, staging, and evaluating treatment responses in GC [27, 28]. These slides are widely accessible and cost-effective. However, manual assessment by pathologists is labor-intensive, tedious, and time-consuming [29]. The advent of whole-slide imaging (WSI) technology has enabled the digitalization of H&E slides, offering a scalable and economical solution for pathological analysis [4, 24, 30]. Recent advances in pathomics have facilitated the extraction of high-throughput data from annotated tumor images using deep learning algorithms, providing a robust framework for tumor evaluation [31]. Pathomics features derived from WSIs capture critical information about tumor cells, the microenvironment, and cell spatial distribution, demonstrating strong predictive performance for clinical outcomes across various cancers [4, 32]. Therefore, we hypothesized that deep learning pathomics models based on histopathological images have the potential to predict prognosis and therapeutic benefits in cancer patients. To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has investigated the correlation between deep learning pathomics features specifically related to the TME and prognostic predictions in GC.

Herein, we aimed to develop and validate a deep learning pathomics signature (DLPS) derived from pathological images to predict survival in patients with pan-gastrointestinal cancers. Furthermore, we investigated its association with chemotherapy and immunotherapy responses. To enhance the interpretability of the model, we analyzed multi-omics data, including genomics, transcriptomics, microRNA, and proteomics, and employed the Shapley value strategy to provide biological insights and importance ranking.

Methods

Study design and participants

This multicenter observational study included 12 independent cohorts from seven cancer centers, comprising 2463 patients with gastrointestinal cancers. The study included retrospective GC cohorts for the development and validation of the DLPS, a multi-omics GC validation cohort to provide biological interpretability, and three gastrointestinal cancer (esophageal, colorectal, and pancreatic cancers) cohorts to evaluate the model’s clinical generalization and applicability. The study adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines, ensuring methodological rigor [33]. The training cohort (381 patients) and internal validation cohort (179 patients) were consecutively treated at the Sun Yat-sen University Cancer Center (SYSUCC, Guangzhou, China) from January 2015 to March 2017 and from April 2017 to June 2019, respectively. The external validation cohort one (234 patients) was recruited from the Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University (SMU, Guangzhou, China) between January 2015 and April 2017. The external validation cohort two (266 patients) was consecutively enrolled from the Yunnan Cancer Hospital (YNCH, Kunming, China) from March 2011 to September 2017. The external validation cohort three (140 patients) was consecutively enrolled from the First Affiliated Hospital of Kunming Medical College (KMU, Kunming, China) from January 2014 to August 2016. The immunotherapy cohort one (69 patients) was recruited from the Nanfang Hospital of Southern Medical University (SMU, Guangzhou, China) between January 2020 and March 2022. The immunotherapy cohort two (17 patients) was recruited from the Guangdong Provincial Hospital of Chinese Medicine (GPHCH, Guangzhou, China) between April 2020 and April 2022. The immunotherapy cohort three (16 patients) was recruited from the Peking University Cancer Hospital & Institute (PUCH, Beijing, China) between January 2017 and April 2019. The multi-omics cohort (TCGA-STAD) of 351 patients was collected from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database (2023). Additionally, three gastrointestinal cancer datasets from the TCGA database were collected, including an esophageal cancer cohort (TCGA-ESCA) with 123 patients, a colorectal cancer cohort (TCGA-CRAD) with 517 patients, and a pancreatic cancer cohort (TCGA-PAAD) with 170 patients. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Supplementary Methods, with the patient workflow illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 2.

The baseline information including age, sex, tumor location, size, Lauren type, TNM stage, tumor differentiation, carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA), cancer antigen 19-9 (CA19-9), and chemotherapy history, were extracted from medical records. TNM staging was reclassified according to the 8th edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) Cancer Staging Manual, AJCC/International Union Against Cancer [34]. Adjuvant chemotherapy was fluorouracil-based. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the initiation of anti-PD-1 immunotherapy to disease progression or death due to any cause. Overall survival (OS) was calculated from surgery or anti-PD-1 immunotherapy initiation to death or last follow-up.

H&E image acquisition, image processing, and feature extraction

H&E staining of formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tumor samples was performed as described previously [35]. Subsequently, H&E staining slides were scanned using the Aperio ScanScope Scanner platform (Leica Biosystems) with the ×20 objective, and images were digitized as SVS format files. Two gastrointestinal pathologists reviewed all WSIs to ensure sufficient image quality. For each WSI, automated segmentation of tissue was conducted using the public CLAM repository [36], and two pathologists annotated regions of interest (ROIs) using ImageScope software. Features from ROIs were extracted using ResNet50, chosen for its ability to effectively capture intricate visual patterns and characteristics present in complex medical imagery [37]. HoverNet, a state-of-the-art instance segmentation model adept at delineating individual cell boundaries [38, 39]. The combination enabled cell-level feature extraction, yielding 525 pathomics nucleus features, 1024 pathomics microenvironment features, and 1024 single-cell spatial distribution features. Features were standardized via Z-score normalization for subsequent analysis.

Briefly, 1024 pathomics tumor microenvironment (pTME) features were extracted from image patches (256 × 256 pixels) using a ResNet50 model pretrained on ImageNet [36, 40]. After segmenting and classifying cells in the ROI (8192 × 8192 pixels) as tumor cells, lymphocytes, stromal cells, non-neoplastic epithelial cells, or other cells using a HoverNet model, 1024 single-cell spatial distribution (pSPA) features were captured using ResNet50 [38]. Additionally, following preprocessing with a HoverNet model, 525 pathomics tumor nucleus (pNUC) features were extracted from each ROI image using the CellProfiler platform [41] (version 2.2.1, https://cellprofiler.org/). Details regarding image processing and feature extraction are provided in the Supplementary Methods.

Construction of the deep learning pathomics signature

We constructed a pathomics nucleus model to predict prognosis by utilizing a linear combination of selected image features weighted by their respective coefficients. Due to the large number of variables involved, we employed the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) method to select the most informative predictive features from the training cohort. To ensure reliable results, the dataset was resampled, and the parameters of the algorithm were determined by estimating the expected generalization error using tenfold cross-validation.

This procedure was similarly applied to the pTME and pSPA feature sets, generating prediction signatures that were aggregated into a new feature set for developing the DLPS. A survival regression model was employed to construct the DLPS, weighting each feature by its coefficient. The optimal DLPS cutoff value was determined using the survivalROC R package within the training cohort. Further details on model construction and patient stratification are available in Supplementary Methods.

Prognostic value of the DLPS

The DLPS’s prognostic value was assessed in all patients and across subgroups stratified by clinicopathological factors. The accuracy of the DLPS in predicting OS was further assessed using time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, with comparisons made via the area under the curve (AUC). To assess generalizability of the DLPS, we also evaluated its prognostic value in pan-gastrointestinal cancers, including pancreatic, esophageal, and colorectal cancers.

Establishment of the pathomics nomogram

We conducted a univariate cox regression analysis to delineate the relationship between clinical characteristics and prognosis. Multivariable cox regression analysis was then applied to identify independent predictors of prognosis. Based on these results, we developed a nomogram that integrated the DLPS with the independent predictors identified from the multivariable cox regression.

A nomogram integrating the DLPS with these independent predictors was constructed. The nomogram’s performance was evaluated using metrics such as the concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The C-index quantified the nomogram’s discriminative power, calibration curves assessed alignment between predicted and actual survival probabilities, and DCA evaluated the clinical utility of the nomograms. These metrics collectively demonstrated the nomogram’s potential as a practical tool for individualized prognostic assessment in GC patients, enhancing the clinical applicability of the DLPS.

Association of the DLPS with chemotherapy benefit

We explored the association between the DLPS and response to adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with stage II and III GC. Among the 1200 patients included in this study, we identified 898 patients with stage II-III GC who had available information regarding their therapy.

To minimize potential selection bias and confounding effects, we implemented a matching strategy to ensure balanced groups. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted using a 1:1 nearest matching between patients receiving chemotherapy and those not. The following variables were matched: age, sex, differentiation status, tumor location, depth of invasion (T), lymph node metastasis (N), and TNM stage.

Association of the DLPS with immunotherapy response

For the subset of 102 patients receiving immunotherapy, the DLPS’s predictive value for anti-PD-1 response was assessed. Responses were categorized as objective response (OR), stable disease (SD), or progressive disease (PD) according to RECIST v1.1 criteria [42], with evaluations performed every 4 weeks. Additionally, PFS and OS outcomes were analyzed in relation to the DLPS.

SHAP interpretation

The SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) strategy is a game-theoretic technique that enhances model interpretability by quantifying the contribution of each feature to the final output [43]. To address the challenge of black-box predictions, we employed SHAP to gain insights into our model’s decision-making processes. SHAP values, calculated by the explainer model, quantify the impact of each input feature on the predicted output and rank their importance. Using Python’s SHAP package, we evaluated the role of DLPS and clinicopathological characteristics in prognostic prediction.

Multi-omics analysis

This study utilized the Cancer Genome Atlas Stomach Adenocarcinoma (TCGA-STAD) dataset to investigate the relationship between multi-omics characteristics and our model. The multi-omics data consisted of publicly available processed genomic data, level 3 normalized RNAseq data, level 3 microRNA data, level 3 reverse phase protein array (RPPA) data, and H&E slides.

We conducted a comparative analysis of DLPS classification with the existing molecular subtypes, including the Asian Cancer Research Group (ACRG) and TCGA subtypes, to assess patient prognostic stratification. Differential expression analyses of mRNA, miRNA, and proteomic profiles identified key molecules (fold-change ≥ 2 or 1.5, P < 0.05) associated with DLPS. GSEA software was used for pathway analysis, using default parameters and an adjusted P-value threshold of < 0.05 for significance.

Statistical analysis

Statistical comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, while categorical variables were analyzed using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test. Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were performed to calculate hazard ratios (HR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Analyses were carried out using R software (version 4.3.1), SPSS software (version 25.0), and Python software (version 3.9.13), with detailed algorithms provided in the Supplementary Methods. A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics

The overall study design is shown in Fig. 1. We analyzed 1200 GC patients with available pathological images, complete follow-up, and chemotherapy data to evaluate the DLPS’s prognostic and chemotherapy benefit predictive capabilities. Furthermore, patients treated with anti-PD-1 immunotherapy (n = 102) were used to investigate the association between the DLPS and immunotherapy response. To assess the generalizability of the DLPS as a prognostic indicator, the model was tested on three independent cohorts (n = 810) of gastrointestinal cancer patients, including those with pancreatic, esophageal, and colorectal cancers. Additionally, genomic characteristics associated with DLPS were decoded by analyzing 351 patients with both pathological images and multi-omics data.

These patients had a mean age of 59.83 years [standard deviation (SD) of 12.1], with 67.8% being male. The 3-year overall survival rate among these patients was 64.5%, and the majority (78.7%, 1220/1551) were diagnosed with stage II or III disease. The clinicopathological characteristics and treatment regimens were generally similar across different centers, with some variations observed in tumor size, differentiation status, tumor location, and TNM stage (Supplementary Table 1). Construction of the DLPS

Feature selection identified three sets of features that exhibited significant associations with prognosis: 13 pathomics nucleus features, 24 pathomics microenvironment features, and eight single-cell spatial distribution features. These feature sets were utilized to train three single-scale prediction models, namely the pNUC, pTME, and pSPA models, using the LASSO Cox regression method (Supplementary Fig. 3). The predictive signatures from these models were integrated to develop the DLPS. Detailed calculation formulas for pNUC, pTME, pSPA, and DLPS are described in the Supplementary Methods. The median distribution (interquartile range) of the DLPS did not exhibit statistically significant differences among the SYSUCC [− 0.083 (− 0.689–0.513)], SMU [− 0.173 (− 0.714–0.277)], YNCH [− 0.075 (− 0.707–0.557)], KMU [0.057 (− 0.635–0.727)] and TCGA [− 0.014 (− 0.675–0.544)] cohorts (P > 0.05). Based on an optimal cutoff determined using the survivalROC package, patients were stratified into low-DLPS (DLPS < − 0.0875) and high-DLPS (DLPS ≥ − 0.0875) groups (Supplementary Fig. 4).

DLPS is associated with prognosis

We evaluated the prognostic value of the DLPS in various cohorts. Patients with relatively lower DLPS achieved a favorable prognosis in the training and validation cohorts. In the training cohort, the 5-year OS rate was 69.5% for the low-DLPS group compared to 19.3% for the high-DLPS group (Supplementary Fig. 5). To evaluate the generalizability of DLPS, we applied it to three gastrointestinal cancer datasets—pancreatic, esophageal, and colorectal cancers. DLPS maintained its statistically significant prognostic value in patients with the aforementioned gastrointestinal cancers (Fig. 2B).

Development and validation of a pathomics nomogram for prognosis

Univariate Cox regression analysis identified DLPS, TNM stage, CA19-9, tumor location, tumor size, and Lauren type as significant factors associated with OS in the training cohort (Supplementary Tables 8–9). Subsequently, through backward stepwise multivariate Cox regression analysis, we identified the DLPS and TNM stage as independent predictors of OS (Table 2). Based on these findings, we developed a pathomics nomogram incorporating DLPS and TNM stage (Fig. 3A). The purpose of this nomogram is to provide clinicians with a quantitative tool for predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS probabilities of GC patients.

The nomogram’s performance was validated through calibration plots, which showed excellent agreement with ideal predictions in both the training and validation cohorts (Fig. 3B; Supplementary Table 4). DCA further demonstrated the nomogram’s superior net benefit compared to DLPS and clinical factors across a range of threshold probabilities (Supplementary Fig. 9). Notably, the nomogram achieved a C-index of 0.690–0.820, significantly outperforming both DLPS (0.667–0.757) and TNM stage (0.579–0.714; Supplementary Table 10).

DLPS predicts benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy

We further assessed the predictive value of the DLPS regarding the benefit of adjuvant chemotherapy in patients with stages II and III disease. PSM was used to balance clinical characteristics between chemotherapy and non-chemotherapy groups. After achieving comparable characteristics, survival outcomes were analyzed based on DLPS status, demonstrating the predictive utility of DLPS in guiding chemotherapy decisions.

For patients in the low-DLPS group, adjuvant chemotherapy was associated with improved overall survival for patients with stage II and III disease (SYSUCC cohort: HR 0.308 [95% CI 0.131–0.724], P = 0.007; external validation cohorts: HR 0.272 [95% CI 0.089–0.835], P = 0.023; Fig. 4A–C). Conversely, adjuvant chemotherapy showed no survival benefit for patients in the high-DLPS group with stage II or III disease. Similar findings were observed in analyses of all stage II–III patients without PSM (Supplementary Fig. 10). These results indicate that the DLPS model effectively predicts which patients benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy, enabling more precise stratification and treatment selection. DLPS predicts response to immunotherapy

We further investigated the relationship between DLPS and immunotherapy response in three independent cohorts from different centers. Notably, patients in the low-DLPS group (29.6%) in total immunotherapy cohorts demonstrated a significantly higher objective response rate than those in the high-DLPS group (8.3%, P < 0.05), which was also confirmed in the independent cohorts (Fig. 4D–F). Furthermore, following stratification according to treatment lines (including the first, second, and third lines), similar results were obtained (Supplementary Fig. 11). Additionally, Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed significant associations between DLPS and both PFS and OS (Fig. 4G). These findings suggest that the DLPS model identifies patients unlikely to respond to immunotherapy, guiding the development of combination treatment strategies for these individuals.

We further analyzed the transcriptional profiles to identify dysregulated genes associated with the DLPS (Fig. 6C). We identified 131 differentially expressed genes, most of which were correlated with tumor-regulation and immune modulation, including PRSS27, CCL23, and DHRS2. We also identified 42 microRNAs (Fig. 6D) and 15 proteins (Fig. 6E) that were differentially expressed between the low and high-DLPS groups. The dysregulated proteins and microRNAs identified in our analysis play important roles in tumor progression, including BRAF, IRS1, MS4A1, RAF1, and has-mir-518d. Furthermore, pathway analyses at the transcriptomic level (Fig. 6C) showed that high-DLPS was positively correlated with multiple tumor-promoting pathways, immunosuppressive pathways, and metabolism-related pathways, such as the KRAS signaling pathway, IL-6/JAK/STAT3 pathway, epithelial-mesenchymal transition pathway, and nicotinate metabolism (Fig. 6C).

Discussion

The risk stratification and management of GC patients based solely on TNM staging is inadequate [44]. Patients with the same TNM stage and similar treatment regimens often exhibit significant variability in clinical outcomes, chemotherapy efficacy, and immunotherapy responses [3, 7, 45]. This underscores the urgent need for a novel, precise prognostic tool and individualized decision-making system. In this study, we established the DLPS from digital pathological images, which demonstrated superior performance in predicting prognosis and therapeutic benefits in patients with GC. We also found a statistically significant prognostic value of the DLPS in pan-gastrointestinal cancers. By integrating DLPS with TNM staging, we constructed and validated a nomogram that significantly improved prognostic accuracy. Furthermore, the DLPS effectively identified patients likely to benefit from chemotherapy and immunotherapy. We also provided the biology and performance-based interpretability of the prediction model using multi-omics data and Shapley value analysis. These results indicate that the DLPS is a reliable tool to provide information regarding prognosis and therapeutic responses in GC, with potential applicability to pan-gastrointestinal cancers and other solid tumors.

Adjuvant chemotherapy is commonly recommended for GC patients undergoing radical surgery due to its demonstrated improvement in oncological outcomes compared to surgery alone [46]. However, substantial variability in chemotherapy responses persists, even among patients with the same TNM stage and similar regimens. Identifying patients most likely to benefit from chemotherapy is therefore critical. Our DLPS model successfully stratifies patients based on chemotherapy benefit, with low-DLPS patients deriving significant advantages, while high-DLPS patients do not. Similarly, immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have revolutionized GC treatment [2], but accurately identifying responders remains challenging [15, 47]. Traditional methods, such as PD-L1 staining, are costly and time-intensive. By contrast, our DLPS model, derived from H&E-stained slides, offers a cost-effective alternative for identifying patients likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Multi-omics analysis revealed that low DLPS is associated with tumor cell autophagic death and changes in the immune tumor microenvironment, further highlighting DLPS as a reliable guide for personalized treatment strategies.

The growing body of evidence supports the feasibility of using pathomics for tumor evaluation. Jiang et al. developed a pathomics model for colon cancer prognosis and chemotherapy benefit prediction, demonstrating strong performance in estimating 2-, 3-, and 5-year DFS and OS while identifying patients who could benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy [48]. Similarly, Zhang et al. employed deep-learning analysis of histopathological images to improve prognostic predictions and therapeutic response evaluations in small-cell lung cancer [49], demonstrating their model’s ability to provide comprehensive prognostic information beyond TNM staging and molecular subtyping. In GC, significant advancements have also been achieved. Chen et al. constructed a machine-learning model leveraging quantitative pathomics features extracted by CellProfiler to predict GC prognosis and chemotherapy benefit [4]. Xiao et al. integrated preoperative contrast enhanced computerized tomography and pathomics features into a machine-learning model for recurrence-free survival prediction in gastrointestinal stromal tumors, achieving a high C-index of 0.917 [50]. However, the number and dimensions of the pathomics features used in these studies were limited. Our study extracted multi-scale pathomics features from the WSIs, including tumor microenvironment, tumor nucleus, and single-cell spatial distribution features. It can better characterize tumor heterogeneity and conduct the task of predicting prognosis and treatment response. Additionally, the DLPS model was validated using multicenter datasets and pan-gastrointestinal cancers, reducing overfitting risks and improving its clinical generalizability.

Despite these promising findings, this study had several limitations. Firstly, this was a retrospective study, which needs validation in a prospective study. Survival data were limited by patients who were lost to follow-up. Secondly, although high-quality biopsy slide images were used to train and validate the DLPS, these histopathological images were collected from various scanners with differing parameters, potentially introducing bias. Thirdly, multi-omics findings from a cohort only, further validation is needed to confirm the association between multi-omics characterizations and the pathomics signature. Additionally, the DLPS was specifically developed to predict responses to fluorouracil-based adjuvant chemotherapy and PD-1 immunotherapy in GC. Thus, its application to other therapeutic regimens or cancer types should be approached with caution.

The clinical integration of artificial intelligence (AI) in medicine faces significant challenges related to transparency and reproducibility [51]. The TRIPOD statement for machine learning (2019) and the SPIRIT-AI and CONSORT-AI guidelines (2020) outline best practices for protocols and reporting to address these issues [52, 53]. Following these guidelines, a multicenter, prospective validation study is essential. If validated, the DLPS could be integrated as a plugin within hospital medical systems. This integration would enable clinicians to seamlessly incorporate the technology into clinical workflows, offering an automated tool to quantify each patient’s survival prognosis and treatment response based on their pathological images. As a decision-support system, the DLPS could aid physicians in developing personalized treatment plans.

The development of the DLPS marks the beginning of an exciting direction in precision oncology. Our team has initiated the collection of prospective data to further validate the DLPS's clinical utility. If confirmed, the DLPS could serve as a valuable tool for improving prognostic accuracy and guiding personalized anti-cancer therapies in clinical practice. Furthermore, we are advancing machine learning and deep learning methods to analyze multimodal data, including RNA sequencing, radiographic imaging, and histopathological images. Our long-term goal is to integrate these diverse datasets with the DLPS to enhance its predictive capabilities and expand its clinical applications.

Conclusions

The DLPS model, derived from digital pathological images, was designed to predict the prognosis of patients with GC, which enabled the identification of individuals likely to benefit from adjuvant chemotherapy or immunotherapy. The model has the potential to assist in risk stratification and individualized treatment guidance for GC, which may be extended to pan-gastrointestinal cancers and other solid tumors. Our ultimate aim is to evolve the DLPS into a robust clinical decision-support system capable of predicting and guiding therapeutic regimens.
