Effect of repetition of rTMS at different frequencies on the efficacy of swallowing disorders after stroke: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Meixia Sun, Kang Chen, Yanwen He, Yukun Zhang, Yue Zhuo, He Zhuang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:To systematically evaluate the curative effect of repeated transcranial magnetic stimulation at different frequencies on swallowing disorders after stroke.Methods:A search was conducted for randomized controlled trials of repeated transcranial magnetic stimulation for stroke patients in CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, ProQuest, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science databases until December 2022. The 2 researchers strictly screened and checked the included documents, extracted relevant data, assessed the risk quality of the literature using the Cochrane manual, and conducted a network meta-analysis of the data using State16.0.Results:Eighteen studies included 680 participants. The results of the reticular meta-analysis showed that in the leakage-aspiration scale (PAS) indicators, 1 Hz, 3 Hz, 5 Hz, and 10 Hz were all better treatment effects compared with the control group, and there was a statistically significant difference (P < .05). In the standard swallowing function assessment (SSA) index, 3 Hz, 5 Hz, and 10 Hz compared with the control group were statistically significant (P < .05); there was no difference between 1 Hz and the control group (P > .05). The cumulative probability ranking results showed that the intervention effect of 3 Hz was the best in the PAS index, much greater than that of other frequencies, and the intervention effects of 10 Hz and 5 Hz were similar. For the SSA index, the intervention effect was optimal at 10 Hz, followed by 5 Hz. Note that the treatment effect of 1 Hz ranked last, even lower than that of the control group. The results of the 5 Hz treatment site grouping analysis showed that the affected side was > bilateral > healthy in PAS and > bilateral > healthy in SSA.Conclusion subsections:Based on the current study, the optimal frequency and site selection results of the 2 evaluation indicators are not uniform, but from the combination of the 2 evaluation indicators, the treatment effect of 10H is good, and the effect of bilateral stimulation for the selection of stimulation sites is good. The above conclusions need to be verified in high-quality studies.

1. Introduction

As a common cerebrovascular disease, stroke is one of the main causes of disability in China, which seriously threatens the safety of Chinese people.[1] Swallowing disorder is one of the main complications of stroke, with an occurrence rate of 46% to 57%.[2] Dysphagia primarily manifests as salivation, drinking cough, and prolonged eating time. Patients often have malnutrition, electrolyte disorders, aspiration, aspiration pneumonia, and other complications, which seriously affect the health and quality of life of patients, increase the burden on family and society, and even endanger the life safety of patients.[3] Therefore, the rehabilitation of swallowing dysfunction after stroke remains an urgent issue.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, rTMS) is a noninvasive and unpainful method for neurostimulation therapy. An electromagnetic coil is applied to the patient’s scalp to generate a magnetic field, thus changing the excitability of the cortex at the stimulation site and the transsynaptic excitability at the distant site. It is a safe and effective treatment method for central nervous system diseases and is applied in the treatment of swallowing disorders after stroke.[4] It is generally accepted that rTMS exerts either excitatory or inhibitory effects on the stimulated cortex. For example, low-frequency rTMS (1 Hz) is inhibitory, whereas high-frequency rTMS (3 Hz, usually 5 or 10 Hz) is excitatory.[5,6] In recent years, several meta-analyses have analyzed the efficacy of rTMS; however, there has been no systematic and effective comparison of rTMS at different frequencies. In this study, a meta-analysis of rTMS for different frequencies of swallowing disorders after stroke was performed to provide support for future clinical treatment options.

2. Material and methods

In the CNKI, Wanfang, VIP, ProQuest, PubMed, Cochrane Library, Scopus, and Web of Science databases, the search language was Chinese and English, and the relevant randomized controlled trial (RCT) studies were used to build the database until September 2022. The Chinese search terms included repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation, rTMS, cerebrovascular accident, cerebral hemorrhage, cerebral stroke and stroke, swallowing disorder, dysphagia, swallowing, etc; The English search terms were stroke, apoplexy, cerebral hemorrhage, dysphagia, swallowing disorders, deglutition, transcranial magnetic stimulation, rTMS, random, and randomized controlled trial; and finally, a manual search of gray literature was supplemented to ensure a comprehensive search.

2.1. Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria:

Clinical studies that met the following criteria were included:

(1) Subjects: all patients met stroke-related diagnostic criteria and showed imaging evidence of relevant pathology on head CT or MRI; patients were identified with dysphagia; no participants had swallowing disorders due to other diseases; onset for 6 months. (2) Interventions: the trial arm was treated with rTMS in the control group. (3) Outcome measures: ① leakage-aspiration scale (PAS) and ② standard swallowing function assessment (standard swallowing function assessment, SSA). (4) Study type: All studies were RCT with an impact factor of 1.

Exclusion criteria were as follows:

(1) repeatedly published literature with incorrect data, (2) rTMS intervention, (3) cases of stroke swallowing disorder combined with other organic diseases, (4) animal experiments, review, and experience summary, (5) inability to find the full text of the experiment, (6) unreasonable experimental design without comparable baseline data between groups, (7) plagiarism, and (8) no selection of outcome measures.

2.2. Literature screening

Literature screening by 2 researchers using Endnote X17. First, the initially screened articles were deleted and repeated, and then, the abstracts and titles were read and further screened according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria. Finally, we left the standard articles to read the full text and determine the final included articles through the above steps. Data on the first author, publication year, country of publication, sample size, age, intervention measures, intervention frequency, intervention duration, and outcome measures were collected in Excel. In addition, if data were missing from published articles, the corresponding author of the original study was contacted for the required information.

2.3. Methodological quality assessment of the included studies

Risk bias was evaluated according to Cochrane volume, and the methodological quality of the included literature was evaluated by 2 reviewers. The results were exchanged at the end of the evaluation. These differences were discussed and evaluated by a third reviewer.

2.4. Statistical methods

Net meta-analysis and figure drawing of the included data were performed using Stata16.0. Weighted mean differences and 95% credible intervals were used as effect sizes. First, the network evidence map for direct comparison between the interventions and the consistency of the closed loop were evaluated by the ring inconsistency test, and the results of the mesh meta-analysis were presented by pairwise comparison of forest maps. The cumulative ranking probability map is drawn according to the cumulative area value under the curve (the surface under the cumulative ranking curve, SUCRA), and the best stimulation frequency is determined according to the area under the curve. Subgroup analysis was performed according to different stimulation sites, and the quality of the stimulation sites was judged according to the cumulative probability map. Finally, comparison-corrected funnel plots were used to test the possibility of publication bias and small-sample effects.

3.4.4. Cumulative ranking probability results.

In PAS and SSA, the lower the score, the better the intervention effect, and in the study result, the larger the area under the cumulative curve of the intervention, the better the intervention results. The results showed that the 3 Hz intervention was the best in PAS, far more than the other frequencies, and the 10 Hz and 5 Hz intervention were similar, with SUCRA of 50.9% and 49%, respectively. In the SSA index, the intervention effect of 10 Hz was optimal, with 67.7% in SUCRA, followed by 5 Hz and 62.7% in SUCRA. Notably, the treatment effect of 1 Hz was less than that of the control group (Fig. 5).

A cumulative ranking probability plot.

3.4.5. Treatment effects at different stimulation sites.

Due to the different stimulation sites, it also affects the efficacy of rTMS in the treatment of swallowing disorders after stroke. To determine the treatment effect of rTMS with different stimulation sites, we selected the 5-Hz stimulation frequency with the most included studies and conducted subgroup analysis according to 3 different stimulation sites: the affected side, the healthy side, and the bilateral side. Among them, 3 studies were selected for stimulation on the affected side, 7 studies for stimulation on the healthy side, and 2 studies on bilateral stimulation. The results showed that for PAS, rTMS had the best treatment effect on the affected side, the bilateral treatment effect ranked second, and finally the treatment effect on the healthy side. For SSA, rTMS had the best treatment effect, followed by the affected side and finally the healthy side. The details are presented in Figure 6.

Subgroup analysis of the cumulative probability ranking results.

3.4.6. Publication bias.

As shown in Figure 7, although the indicators of the included studies are roughly symmetrical on the funnel chart, some points of the 2 research indicators are scattered at the bottom and outside. The results suggest that there is some publication bias and a small sample size effect in this study, indicating that the research results should be treated cautiously.

3.5. Adverse reactions

Repeated transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) has few adverse effects and is highly safe for the treatment of swallowing disorders after stroke. None of the trials included in this study experienced serious adverse effects. In the Chen 2020 study, one patient experienced mild pain under magnetic stimulation and had no problems measuring heart rate and blood pressure.

4. Discussion

The incidence of swallowing disorders after stroke is high, and patients show dysphagia, dyspepsia, tongue muscle weakness, and drooling, which seriously affect their quality of life. As a noninvasive treatment, rTMS is widely used in clinical.[25] The rTMS through the magnetic field induces synaptic plasticity in the cerebral cortex, increases the activation of the swallowing center, and regulates its excitability, thus maintaining the balance of the bilateral hemisphere of the excitability, promoting the recovery of swallowing function[26]; its therapeutic effect is beyond doubt, but rTMS for different swallowing disorders after stroke brain injury and degree, attack period after the treatment site, stimulation intensity, stimulation time, and treatment cycle is still not a unified treatment standard. In this study, a systematic evaluation of the treatment effects of different frequencies was conducted to determine the treatment effects of different treatment frequencies to provide a basis for clinical treatment.

PAS is a widely used poststroke dysphagia evaluation scale that conducts semiquantitative assessment of penetration-aspiration degree[27]; thus, this study used PAS to evaluate the therapeutic effect of rTMS with different frequencies on swallowing function in patients. The results showed that the treatment effect of rTMS regardless of the frequency was better than that of conventional treatment, fully affirming the therapeutic advantage of rTMS in swallowing dysfunction in stroke. The results of several frequency comparisons showed the greatest dominance of the 3 Hz treatment, with the remaining 10 Hz > 5 Hz > 1 Hz.

SSA is a commonly used method to evaluate swallowing function with good reliability and validity.[28] Therefore, SSA was included as another evaluation index in this study. The results showed that the treatment effect of 1 Hz was less than that of the conventional treatment, but the other frequencies of rTMS were better than the conventional treatment, followed by 10 Hz > 5 Hz > 3 Hz. Notably, in both PAS and SSA, the therapeutic effect of 1 HzrTMS was ranked after the other frequencies. Generally, low-frequency rTMS (1 Hz) inhibits cerebral cortical excitability, whereas high-frequency rTMS (>1 Hz) induces cerebral cortical excitability.[5] Strengthening or weakening the compensatory function of the central nervous system can promote cerebral cortex reconstruction and improve body function. High-frequency rTMS can enhance the compensatory effect of healthy lateral brain tissue to improve the swallowing function of patients. Low-frequency rTMS mainly depends on the residual cortical functional reorganization of the affected hemisphere on the affected side, while the limited neural reorganization ability of residual neurons in the affected side (the improvement of swallowing function in patients is not ideal) results in the recovery of swallowing function after stroke mainly through the compensatory function of the healthy hemisphere.[22] This may be the reason for the insignificant effect of the 1 HzrTMS treatment. However, compared with low-frequency rTMS, the risk of high-frequency rTMS-induced epilepsy is much higher,[18,29] which should be measured in clinical treatment. In addition, for the PAS index, the 3 Hz treatment effect was the best, with SUCRA as high as 96.1%, far exceeding the other frequencies. However, for the SSA index, 10 Hz performed the best, so from the perspective of optimal selection, the frequency selection of the 2 evaluation indexes could not be unified. This may be related to the selection of treatment sites in the individual studies. The current study shows that high-frequency rTMS on cerebral infarction rat bilateral central helps to shorten the conduction time and cortical latency, making the rat bilateral brain balance.[30] However, most studies believe that high-frequency rTMS only on swallowing disorder patients damaged cortical function, can improve its excitability, and form long-range synergistic effects.[31] Foreign scholars also believe that the use of high-frequency rTM. Some foreign scholars also believe that using high-frequency rTMS to stimulate the healthy side of patients with swallowing disorders is also conducive to the recovery of swallowing function of.[32] The treatment mechanism of this stimulation protocol is the interactive hemispheric inhibition theory, in which low-frequency rTMS is used to inhibit a healthy hemisphere. As a result, the inhibitory effect of the corpus callosum in the healthy hemisphere on the affected hemisphere is weakened, which can accelerate the plastic change of the residual neurons in the affected hemisphere and the distal area, and ultimately improve swallowing function.[18]

We chose a stimulation frequency of 5 Hz according to 3 different stimulation sites, including the affected side, healthy side, and bilateral side. The results showed that for PAS indicators, the treatment effect on the patient side was the best, and from the results of SSA, the treatment effect on both sides was much greater than that on the other 2 sites. Based on the results of these 2 indicators. Bilateral stimulation had a positive effect on improving the 2 evaluation indicators. High-frequency rTMS stimulation can improve the excitability of the damaged contralateral pharyngeal motor cortex, enhance the reorganization of nerve connections in the healthy hemisphere, and accelerate the improvement of swallowing function. Simultaneous stimulation of the bilateral swallowing cortex can improve poststroke swallowing function.[33] Based on the results of these 2 indicators. Bilateral stimulation had a positive effect on improving the 2 evaluation indicators. It is worth noting that the treatment effect of both evaluation indices was the worst on the healthy side, which was even slightly lower for SSA than in the control group. However, multiple selective clinical stimulations are administered to the healthy side. The site of stimulation and efficacy were different. It is necessary to further study whether the different treatment effects are caused by different treatment sites to explore the best treatment plan. However, the results may be biased because the course of the disease and intervention duration cannot be unified.

Considering the treatment effect of the 2 indicators, the treatment effect of 10 Hz rTMS was good. Previous studies have also shown that high-frequency rTMS (5 Hz) can improve local blood perfusion in the brain and increase the remodeling of cerebral cortical function.[34] However, few articles used 10 HzrTMS to treat dysphagia after stroke.

This study had some limitations. Although we included 18 studies and 680 participants, the RCT number and sample size were not sufficient to support a completely reliable conclusion. In particular, the RCT included in the 2 evaluation indicators were insufficient, and the sampling error of a small sample size may threaten the validity of the meta-analysis. The studies included in the analysis may also draw inaccurate conclusions because of insufficient trial process. For example, 11 concealed trials and 12 blinded trials were excluded. Thus, there is a possibility of a risk shift.

None of the RCTs included in this study had serious adverse effects, and only one study reported a mild discomfort response without requiring treatment. This indicates that rTMS for swallowing after stroke can be widely used in clinical practice. Therefore, the optimal frequencies of the 2 indicators used in this study should be considered. In addition, different treatment cycles, disease progression, and individualized differences also affect research results. In the future, large-sample research evidence and more rigorous RCT are still needed for data support to optimize the clinical model in practice and better guide rTMS in the treatment of poststroke swallowing disorders.
