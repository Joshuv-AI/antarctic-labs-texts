Initial systolic blood pressure, antihypertensive treatment timing and outcomes in acute ischemic stroke: a subgroup analysis of CATIS-2 trial
Authors: Wei Zhao, Yufei Wei, Yuesong Pan, Mengxing Wang, Xuewei Xie, Aili Wang, Zilin Zhao, Dacheng Liu, Ximing Nie, Xin Liu, Lina Zheng, Chongke Zhong, Yonghong Zhang, Liping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective:Whether initial systolic blood pressure (SBP) influences blood pressure management in acute ischemic stroke (AIS) and its association with outcome remains inconclusive. We conducted a subgroup analysis of CATIS-2 trial (China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke II) to assess the impact of early vs. delayed antihypertensive treatment in AIS, stratified by baseline SBP.Methods:CATIS-2 was a multicenter, randomized trial enrolling AIS patients within 24–48 h after onset, presenting with SBP 140–219 mmHg and without reperfusion therapy. Participants were randomized to receive immediate antihypertensive treatment or delayed treatment initiated on day 8. The primary outcome was functional dependency or death (modified Rankin Scale score ≥3) at 90-day. Three prespecified baseline SBP subgroups (<160, 160–179, and ≥180 mm Hg) were analyzed.Results:4802 patients (mean age 63.7 years, 65.0% male) were included, with baseline SBP < 160 mmHg (n = 2233), 160–179 mmHg (n = 1849), and ≥180 mmHg (n = 720). Among patients with baseline SBP 160–179 mmHg, early antihypertensive treatment was associated with increased risk of primary outcome compared to delayed treatment [13.3% vs. 10.4%; odds ratio (OR) 1.32, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.00–1.76]. This finding was not observed in the baseline SBP < 160 mmHg subgroup (OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.84–1.50) or ≥180 mmHg subgroup (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.66–1.46). No significant differences were found in stroke recurrence or major vascular events between treatment strategies across SBP subgroups.Conclusion:Early antihypertensive treatment may increase the risk of functional dependency or death in AIS patients with moderately elevated baseline SBP. These findings provide valuable insights into clinical practice regarding individualized blood pressure management in AIS. Further research is warranted.Registration:ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03479554)

INTRODUCTION

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) remains a leading cause of mortality and disability worldwide [1], with early blood pressure (BP) management recognized as a critical component of AIS treatment. The benefits of antihypertensive therapy in the secondary prevention of ischemic stroke are well established [2]. However, the optimal timing for initiating antihypertensive treatment during the acute phase remains uncertain and is an area of ongoing research. Previous studies comparing early antihypertensive intervention with no BP-lowering therapy in the acute phase of AIS have largely yielded neutral results [3–5].

Elevated systolic blood pressure (SBP) is observed in approximately 73–81.6% of AIS patients during the acute phase [6–8]. Prior researches have demonstrated a J- or U-shaped association between baseline SBP levels and adverse clinical outcomes, including death or severe disability [7,8], suggesting that both high and low SBP may be detrimental to AIS prognosis. Notably, SBP tends to decline spontaneously within the first week following stroke onset [9]. Theoretically, this transient BP elevation may serve as a compensatory mechanism to preserve perfusion in the ischemic penumbra, supporting the rationale for delaying antihypertensive intervention in the acute phase. However, excessively high BP may also increase the risk of cerebral edema and hemorrhagic transformation, underscoring the need to establish appropriate treatment thresholds.

The optimal BP target and timing of antihypertensive therapy remain unresolved. The Enhanced Control of Hypertension and Thrombolysis Stroke (ENCHANTED) trial reported that intensive BP lowering (SBP 130–140 mmHg) within 6 h of AIS onset did not confer superior outcomes compared with standard BP management (SBP < 180 mmHg), irrespective of baseline SBP stratification at ≤166 mmHg or >166 mmHg [10]. A subgroup analysis of the China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke (CATIS) demonstrated that early antihypertensive treatment did not improve primary functional outcomes but was associated with reduced 14-day mortality in patients with baseline SBP ≥180 mmHg [11]. These findings highlight the ongoing debate regarding whether antihypertensive strategies should be individualized based on baseline SBP heterogeneity.

The China Antihypertensive Trial in Acute Ischemic Stroke II (CATIS-2) was a multicenter, randomized clinical trial comparing early vs. delayed antihypertensive treatment on clinical outcomes in AIS patients [12]. In this study, we performed a subgroup analysis of CATIS-2 to evaluate the effects of early vs. delayed antihypertensive therapy across different baseline SBP strata, to assess whether the clinical outcomes were influenced by baseline SBP level in early vs. delayed treatment.

METHODS

Study design and participants

CATIS-2 is a nationwide, multicenter, randomized controlled trial conducted across 106 hospitals in China from June 2018 to July 2022, which aimed to test whether early antihypertensive treatment starting within the first 24–48 h of AIS onset would reduce the risk of dependency or death [modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≥3] at 90 days, compared with delayed antihypertensive treatment starting on day 8. The details of rationale, design, and overall findings of CATIS-2 have been published [12,13]. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03479554).

Briefly, patients (age of ≥40 years) meeting the following criteria were recruited: 24–48 h post-onset, baseline SBP 140–219 mmHg, AIS diagnosed by imaging examinations. The exclusion criteria included baseline SBP ≥220 mmHg or diastolic BP ≥120 mmHg, receiving intravenous thrombolytic or endovascular thrombectomy, hemorrhagic stroke, severe stroke [National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) score ≥21], deep coma, mRS score ≥3, severe heart failure, atrial fibrillation, acute myocardial infarction, unstable angina, or aortic dissection. Participants were randomized (1:1) to receive early antihypertensive treatment (immediate BP-lowering after randomization, aimed to achieve a SBP reduction of 10–20% from baseline within the first 24 h, and achieve mean BP <140/90 mmHg within 7 days) or delayed treatment (7-day without antihypertensive medications, and restart antihypertensive treatment on day 8, aimed at achieving mean BP <140/90 mmHg within 2 weeks after randomization). Three baseline SBP subgroups (<160, 160–179, ≥180 mmHg) were prespecified. This current study was the prespecified SBP subgroup analysis of CATIS-2 data.

This trial received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Beijing Tiantan Hospital (China) and all participating Chinese centers, as well as the Tulane University Institutional Review Board (USA). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legally authorized representatives.

Data collection

Demographic and medical history data were collected at enrollment. Trained neurologists assessed stroke severity using NIHSS score (ranging from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating more severe neurological deficit) and mRS score (ranging from 0 to 6, with 0 indicating no symptoms, 5 indicating severe disability, and 6 indicating death).

BP measurements

BP was measured by trained nurses using an automated device (Omron HBP-1300) with patients in the supine position, according to the guidelines of American Heart Association [14]. BP measurements were taken three times consecutively at 30-s intervals at enrollment, every 3 h for the first 24 h after randomization, every 8 h from day 2 to day 14 (or until hospital discharge), and during scheduled follow-up visits at 21 and 90 days. Baseline SBP was defined as the mean SBP value from the enrollment measurements.

Outcome assessment

The primary outcome of the current study was a composite outcome of functional dependency or death (mRS ≥3) at day 90 after randomization. Secondary outcomes comprised ordinal mRS score, recurrent stroke, major vascular disease events (including non-fatal stroke, non-fatal myocardial infarction, vascular deaths, coronary revascularization, and hospitalized angina or heart failure) at 90-day follow-up visit, as well as the combination of functional dependency or death (mRS ≥3), ordinal mRS score at 14-day/hospital discharge. Outcome assessments were performed by trained researchers on site through follow-up visits. All clinical outcomes were reviewed by Outcome Adjudication Committee, with the involved study staff blinded to the randomization result.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed according to the intention-to-treat principle. Patients who withdrew consent were excluded. Participants were divided into three subgroups according to the baseline SBP: < 160 mmHg, 160–179 mmHg, ≥180 mmHg. Baseline characteristics were presented as mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR) for continuous data, and numbers (percentages) for categorical variables. General linear model was used to test the differences in the distribution of NIHSS scores between the two treatment groups. The proportions of the primary outcome and secondary outcomes between the early and delayed treatment groups were compared using a χ2 test. Odds ratio (OR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) for clinical outcomes associated with early vs. delayed antihypertensive treatment were estimated using logistic regression models. We used ordinal logistic regression to estimate the effect of early antihypertensive therapy on the full range of mRS scores distribution. Heterogeneity of treatment effects across baseline SBP subgroups was evaluated by adding an interaction term between treatment assignment and baseline SBP into the logistic regression models. Sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the stability of model by adjusting for potential confounding variables, including age, sex, time from onset to randomization, baseline NIHSS score, baseline BP, history of hypertension, diabetes and stroke. Statistical significance was defined by two-sided P < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA).

RESULTS

Among the 4,810 AIS patients enrolled in the CATIS-2 trial, eight participants withdrew informed consent during study. Finally, 4802 cases were enrolled for baseline SBP subgroup analysis, with 2408 cases in early antihypertensive treatment group and 2394 in delayed group. The participants were stratified by baseline SBP into three subgroups: <160 mmHg (n = 2233, 46.5%): 1107 cases in early group vs. 1126 in delayed group; 160–179 mmHg (n = 1849, 38.5%): 941 cases receiving early intervention vs. 908 under delayed strategy; ≥180 mmHg (n = 720, 15.0%): 360 cases per treatment strategy. Nineteen patients were lost to follow-up at 90-day (Fig. 1).

Baseline data

In the whole enrolled patients, the mean age was 63.7 years, and 65.0% were male. History of hypertension was present in 79.7% of patients. The mean time from symptom onset to randomization was 36.2 h, and median NIHSS score was 3 (IQR 2–5). In the three predefined baseline SBP subgroups (<160, 160–179, and ≥180 mmHg), mean baseline SBP at enrollment was 150.0 mmHg, 168.3 mmHg, and 188.5 mmHg, respectively. Patients in higher baseline SBP subgroups tended to be older (mean age: 63.2 vs. 64.2 vs. 64.4 years), have lower male proportion (67.6% vs. 63.6% vs. 60.7%), and have higher baseline NIHSS scores (median [IQR]: 3 [1–5] vs. 3 [2–5] vs. 3 [2–5]) (Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content). Baseline characteristics of patients were similar between the early and delayed antihypertensive treatment groups in the three baseline SBP levels (Table 1).

Use of antihypertensive medications

As this study focused on investigating the timing of BP reduction rather than the efficacy of specific antihypertensive medications, no specific requirements were imposed regarding the choice of antihypertensive medications. Primary and secondary outcomes

At the 90-day follow-up, early antihypertensive treatment was associated with a higher risk of functional dependency or death compared with delayed treatment in the baseline SBP 160–179 mmHg subgroup (13.3% vs. 10.4%; OR 1.32, 95% CI 1.00–1.76). No statistically significant differences in primary outcome were observed between the two antihypertensive strategies in the baseline SBP < 160 mmHg subgroup (OR 1.12, 95% CI 0.84–1.50) or the baseline SBP ≥180 mmHg subgroup (OR 0.98, 95% CI 0.66–1.46). For the secondary outcomes, the subgroup analyses stratified by baseline SBP levels revealed no significant differences of early vs. delayed treatment in dependency or death at 14 days, or in mRS score distributions at 14 and 90 days. No significant interactions were observed (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

As a prespecified subgroup analysis of the CATIS-2 trial, this study is the first designed to compare the effects of early vs. delayed antihypertensive treatment on clinical outcomes in acute ischemic stroke patients stratified by baseline systolic blood pressure. We identified a statistically non-linear association between baseline SBP and the treatment effect. Specifically, early blood pressure lowering initiated within 24–48 h of onset appeared to be potentially detrimental in patients with baseline SBP 160–179 mmHg compared with delayed treatment started until day 8, whereas no significant differences were observed between the two treatments in patients with lower (<160 mmHg) or higher (≥180 mmHg) baseline SBP. These findings underscore the importance of individualizing BP management in AIS based on initial SBP levels, and suggest that a one-size-fits-all approach to early antihypertensive treatment may not be appropriate.

Substantial uncertainty remains regarding the optimal timing and BP threshold for initiating antihypertensive therapy after acute ischemic stroke. Previous clinical trials have reported neutral results [3–5]. The Efficacy of Nitric Oxide in Stroke (ENOS) [5] trial found that BP lowering did not improve functional outcome. Prespecified subgroup analyses across baseline SBP (≤160, 160–180, 180–200, and >200 mmHg) showed no significant differences or clear trends in the effect of antihypertensive treatment on outcomes [5]. However, it included both ischemic and hemorrhagic stroke patients without separately analyzing the ischemic stroke cohort. The ENCHANTED trial demonstrated that early intensive BP lowering (SBP 130–140 mmHg) did not improve clinical outcome vs. standard BP management (SBP < 180 mmHg), regardless of baseline SBP levels (≤166 mmHg or >166 mmHg) [10]. Nevertheless, this finding was specific to patients treated with intravenous thrombolysis. It is noteworthy that our study, which focused on AIS patients who did not undergo reperfusion therapy, provides some evidence supporting the necessity for an individualized approach to acute BP management.

Prior observational studies have demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between baseline SBP and unfavorable outcomes of disability or death after AIS [8,15]. The optimal SBP range associated with relatively better outcomes was reported as 140–179 mmHg in the International Stroke Trial and 150–169 mmHg in a Japanese study [8,15]. Theoretically, relatively moderate BP elevation as a compensatory response during the acute phase of AIS may be beneficial by improving perfusion in ischemic penumbra [16–18], implying potential harm from antihypertensive therapy. Our results provide some support for this theory, indicating that the optimal BP range in the natural course of the disease may also represent a window of therapeutic sensitivity for antihypertensive intervention. The finding of the association between early antihypertensive treatment and worse outcomes in patients with baseline SBP 160–179 mmHg may be physiologically plausible. Within this range, the intervention-induced SBP reduction (12% in the early group vs. a spontaneous 6.6% decrease in the delayed group within the first 24 h) could compromise this compensatory mechanism mentioned above, thereby leading to adverse outcomes.

By contrast, among patients with baseline SBP below 160 mmHg, no significant differences in clinical outcomes were observed between the two treatments. This neutral finding may be explained by the magnitude of SBP reduction. Previous studies have reported that the efficacy of antihypertensive therapy is associated with the magnitude of SBP reduction [19]. In our participants with baseline SBP < 160 mmHg, the SBP reduction during the first 24 h was relatively small (early group: 5.9% vs. delayed group: 1.3%), which may limit the possibility to detect significant differences in treatment effects between groups. Furthermore, for this subgroup with only mildly elevated baseline SBP (mean, 150 mmHg), the relatively minor treatment-induced BP decrease itself may no longer be the dominant factor influencing prognosis.

On the other hand, severely elevated BP during the acute phase of AIS may adversely affect clinical outcomes by exacerbating cerebral edema or increasing the risk of hemorrhagic transformation [17,18,20]. Therefore, moderate BP reduction may confer benefits in these patients [21,22]. Guidelines from European Stroke Organisation and American Heart Association/American Stroke Association recommend 15% BP reduction in AIS patients within the first 24 h if BP ≥220/120 mmHg [23,24]. But no clear guidelines specify the optimal timing or the BP threshold for initiating antihypertensive treatment in patients with BP < 220/120 mmHg and without reperfusion therapy. Previous CATIS subgroup analysis indicated that early BP lowering might reduce 14-day mortality in patients with baseline SBP ≥180 mmHg, compared with discontinuing all antihypertensive medications [11]. However, in this CATIS-2 subgroup analysis, we did not demonstrate significant benefits from early antihypertensive treatment in patients with baseline SBP 180–219 mmHg. This finding may be attributed to the characteristics of the study population, which consisted predominantly of patients with mild to moderate stroke (median NIHSS score 3, IQR 2–5). The relatively mild symptoms usually suggest relatively smaller infarct volume, thereby diminishing the potential value of BP reduction in preventing hemorrhagic transformation or severe cerebral edema in this population. Future clinical trials are needed to test the efficacy and safety of BP control in patients with severely elevated BP during the acute stage of ischemic stroke.

This study contributes to individualized BP management during the first week after ischemic stroke onset based on baseline SBP levels. The results indicate that early antihypertensive treatment did not confer clinical benefit among patients with baseline SBP 140–179 mmHg. Specifically, in patients with baseline SBP < 160 mmHg, BP lowering showed no significant impact on clinical outcomes, suggesting that antihypertensive treatment may not be urgent. For those with baseline SBP 160–179 mmHg, early BP reduction may have potential detrimental effects; therefore, a cautious and delayed antihypertensive strategy might be preferable. In patients with baseline SBP ≥180 mmHg, early BP lowering appeared at least safe, without increased risk, and moderate BP reduction could be considered based on clinical indications such as target organ protection. Despite the recent focus on BP management following reperfusion therapy for AIS, data from Europe indicate that only a minority of AIS patients actually receive such treatment, with an average of 17% undergoing intravenous thrombolysis and 7% receiving endovascular treatment annually [25]. Optimal acute-phase BP management for the majority of AIS patients without reperfusion treatment remains undefined, underscoring the clinical relevance of our study. Although these findings are exploratory and should be interpreted with caution, they have important implications for clinical practice.

This study has some limitations. First, the predominance of mild to moderate stroke severity in our participants may limit the generalizability of our findings to severe stroke patients. Second, patients scheduled to undergo reperfusion therapy were excluded due to the distinct requirements for BP management in this population [23,24]. Finally, as the exploration characteristic of post hoc analysis, the findings were cautiously interpreted.

CONCLUSIONS

Early antihypertensive treatment started within 24–48 h of disease onset may increase the risk of 90-day functional dependency or death in AIS patients with moderately elevated baseline SBP, compared to delayed treatment until day 8. Although the findings should be cautiously interpreted, they still provide valuable insights into clinical practice regarding individualized blood pressure management in the acute phase of ischemic stroke based on baseline SBP levels. Further research is warranted in the future.
