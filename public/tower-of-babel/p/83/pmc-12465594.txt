Restrictive fluid management with early de-escalation versus usual care in critically ill patients (reduce trial): a feasibility randomized controlled trial
Authors: Thummaporn Naorungroj, Ukrit Prajantasen, Tewa Sanla-ead, Tanuwong Viarasilpa, Surat Tongyoo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOptimal fluid management in critically ill patients varies across different phases of care. This study examined the feasibility of adding a restrictive fluid strategy with early de-escalation to standard care for patients with circulatory shock.MethodsWe performed a single-center, feasibility, randomized controlled trial, involving patients with shock who required fluid resuscitation and were admitted in the medical intensive care unit. After initial fluid resuscitation, patients were randomly assigned (1:1) to either a restrictive fluid strategy or usual care. The restrictive group targeted a near-zero fluid balance over 3 days by limiting fluid intake and using diuretics or mechanical fluid removal when needed. The primary outcome was cumulative fluid balance at day 3. The secondary outcomes were the lengths of stay in the intensive care unit and the hospital, the mechanical ventilation duration, acute kidney injury, renal replacement therapy, and mortality.ResultsWe enrolled 100 patients, assigning 50 to the restrictive strategy and 50 to usual care. By day 3, the restrictive group showed a lower cumulative fluid balance than usual care (‒2353 mL vs. 793 mL, p < 0.001). This trend continued to day 7 (‒3032 mL vs. 1125 mL, p < 0.001). The restrictive group also had shorter stays in the intensive care unit and the hospital (7 vs. 10 days, p = 0.006; 16 vs. 22 days, p = 0.02). There were no statistically significant differences in hospital or 30-day mortality rates between the groups (18% vs. 38%, p = 0.05; 12% vs. 30%, p = 0.05, respectively). Similarly, no significant differences were observed in the incidence of acute kidney injury or the use of renal replacement therapy.ConclusionsA restrictive fluid strategy with early de-escalation and de-resuscitation is feasible and may reduce fluid accumulation and showed a signal for reduced hospital stay without increasing adverse events in critically ill patients following acute fluid resuscitation.Trial registrationTCTR20220719002 (The trial has been reviewed and approved by TCTR committee on July 16th, 2022).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13054-025-05624-z.Keywords: Circulatory shock; Fluid balance; Mortality; Restrictive fluid management; Fluid de-escalation, de-resuscitation

Background

Acute circulatory shock is a common condition in critically ill patients and is associated with high mortality [1, 2]. Although fluid resuscitation with crystalloids and colloids remains pivotal, fluid overload can lead to complications such as pulmonary edema [3]. Several studies have demonstrated that positive fluid accumulation correlates with higher mortality and worse outcomes in critically ill patients [4, 5]. Conversely, conservative fluid therapy aiming for zero fluid balance in the intensive care unit (ICU) can effectively limit fluid accumulation [6, 7]. However, the optimal fluid management strategy after initial resuscitation in circulatory shock remains controversial.

The Conservative versus Liberal Approach to Fluid Therapy of Septic Shock in Intensive Care (CLASSIC) trial found that restrictive fluid resuscitation after initial management of septic shock neither reduced 90-day mortality nor increased adverse events [6]. Similarly, the Crystalloid Liberal or Vasopressors Early Resuscitation in Sepsis (CLOVERS) trial demonstrated that a vasopressor-first strategy to limit fluid administration in sepsis-induced hypotension did not lower 90-day mortality, nor did it increase serious adverse events [8]. In contrast, a restrictive fluid regimen in abdominal surgery was linked to a higher risk of acute kidney injury (AKI) [9]. Although resuscitation is critical, it accounts for only 1 source of total fluid input in critically ill patients [10]. Maintenance fluids and fluid creep also contribute to positive fluid balance and have been associated with worse outcomes [11]. Fluid creep refers to the inadvertent administration of intravenous fluids through diluents for medications, flush solutions, and small-volume infusions that contribute to overall positive fluid balance [12]. A de-resuscitation approach can reduce mechanical ventilation duration [7]; however, consensus on its indications, timing, and implementation remains elusive.

Fluid management strategies beyond initial resuscitation often require distinct approaches. Fluid de-escalation refers to the strategy of reducing ongoing fluid administration by minimizing maintenance fluids, parenteral nutrition, and medication diluents, with the goal of preventing further fluid accumulation. In contrast, de-resuscitation specifically involves the active removal of accumulated fluid from the patient, typically through diuretics or renal replacement therapy, to reverse fluid overload and improve organ function. While both aim to mitigate the harms of fluid accumulation, de-resuscitation is specifically targeted at reversing existing positive fluid balance [12].

In light of these uncertainties, we conducted a single-center, feasibility randomized controlled trial comparing a protocol-based restrictive fluid strategy combined fluid de-escalation and de-resuscitation with usual care in patients with circulatory shock after initial resuscitation. Our primary objective was to assess the feasibility and impact of this intervention on fluid balance in ICU patients.

Methods

Study design and population

This was a randomized, single-center, unblinded feasibility study conducted at 2 medical ICUs at Siriraj Hospital. The study protocol, developed by the investigators, was approved by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board (approval number SI 277/2022) on March 31st, 2022 and registered with the Thai Clinical Trials Registry (20220719002) on July 16th, 2022; details of the trial protocol have been published previously [13]. Adult patients (> 18 years old) who were critically ill, required fluid resuscitation or vasopressors, and had been admitted to the ICU for at least 12 h after initial stabilization were eligible. Patients with hemorrhagic or hypovolemic shock, active ongoing bleeding, severe AKI stage 3, or an urgent indication for renal replacement therapy (RRT) and in palliative care were excluded.

Circulatory shock was defined clinically by persistent hypotension (e.g., Mean Arterial Pressure < 65 mmHg) requiring vasopressor support, or evidence of tissue hypoperfusion (e.g., elevated lactate levels, decreased urine output, altered mental status). Initial stabilization was determined clinically by the attending intensivist, typically indicated by resolution of active shock signs and achieving hemodynamic stability (e.g., Mean Arterial Pressure > 65 mmHg, improved peripheral perfusion, trends in lactate clearance and urine output).

Intervention

The intervention and fluid removal protocol have been described elsewhere [13]. Briefly, eligible patients were randomized (1:1) using sequentially numbered, sealed envelopes containing permuted blocks of 4. The trial was unblinded to investigators, attending staff, and patients. Study follow-up extended until ICU discharge or a maximum of 7 days. This follow-up duration applied uniformly to both the restrictive fluid strategy (intervention) group and the usual care (control) group.

Patients assigned to the restrictive fluid strategy group followed a daily fluid restriction protocol aimed at a neutral fluid balance over the first 72 h after randomization (as detailed in Fig. S4: Intervention Protocol). This approach limited fluid intake from medications, nutrition, parenteral fluids, and blood transfusions —such as by using the lowest possible diluent volumes for medications and the highest feasible concentration of enteral nutrition. Fluid boluses were given only when clinically indicated based on assessment of fluid responsiveness. Fluid responsiveness was assessed using a combination of dynamic parameters (e.g., pulse pressure variation, stroke volume variation), inferior vena cava parameters (e.g., collapsibility, distensibility), or formal fluid challenges depend on attending physician. Due to the feasibility nature of this trial, granular data on the specific types and frequency of fluid responsiveness tests performed were not systematically collected; clinical decisions were made by the treating physician based on available bedside monitoring. Diuretics (mainly furosemide) were used to promote diuresis or if predicted intake exceeded output by about 500 mL per day. The initial furosemide dose ranged from 10 mg to a maximum of 1 g per day, with an infusion rate not exceeding 4 mg/min. The control group received standard ICU fluid management at the discretion of the attending physician. Mechanical fluid removal via RRT was considered under specific conditions (as detailed in Fig. S5: Fluid Removal Protocol). RRT was initiated if fluid intake was consistently greater than output by approximately 1000 mL per day for 48 h despite maximal diuretic titration, or immediately if the patient met any pre-defined urgent indications for RRT.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was cumulative fluid balance over the first 72 h after randomization. The secondary outcomes were major adverse kidney events by day 30. These encompassed 30-day mortality; AKI on days 3, 7, and 30; and RRT requirements on days 3, 7, and 30. Other secondary outcomes were vasopressor-free days at day 30, ventilator‒free days at day 30, Duration of MV, ICU and hospital lengths of stay, and ICU and hospital mortality.

Sample size calculation

Based on data from the FINNAKI study [4], we hypothesized that reducing fluid balance by 1.2 L might alter patient outcomes. We assumed a standard deviation (SD) of 2 L. The study power was set at 80% (type II error of 0.20) and the significance level at 5% (type I error of 0.05). Using these parameters, 50 patients per group would be required.

Data collection

We collected patient demographics, primary diagnoses, vital signs, and illness severity scores, as well as ICU and hospital outcomes. Information on fluid balance, intravenous fluid administration, vasopressor use and dosage, mechanical ventilation, and RRT was also recorded. Biochemical, hematological, and blood gas analysis parameters were documented, alongside mortality data, the incidence of AKI, and the number of patients requiring RRT. All other variables were obtained from routine measurements in electronic medical records or existing hospital databases.

Statistical analysis

We performed statistical analyses on the intention-to-treat population, which included all randomized participants who consented to data usage. The final conclusions were based on this intention-to-treat analysis. A sensitivity analysis was also conducted on a per-protocol population, defined as the intention-to-treat population excluding individuals with protocol violations or ICU stays shorter than 72 h post-randomization.

The primary outcome—cumulative fluid balance—was analyzed with independent t-tests for normally distributed data or the Mann‒Whitney U test for nonnormal data. For patients who died prior to 72 h, their cumulative fluid balance was calculated up to the time of death. The secondary outcomes, including mortality and AKI incidence, were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, and the independent t-test or Mann‒Whitney U test for continuous variables. For continuous outcomes such as ICU length of stay and mechanical ventilation duration, the actual duration until the patient’s death was used for those who died during the follow-up period. Ventilator-free days and vasopressor-free days are defined as the number of days a patient is alive and free from mechanical ventilation or vasopressor support, respectively, within the first 30 days after randomization [14]. To evaluate the effect of the intervention on fluid balance over time (daily and cumulative fluid balance trends) and to assess for a group-by-time interaction, we utilized a linear mixed model. This model included group, time, and their interaction as fixed effects, and patient as a random effect, allowing for analysis of repeated measures data. A Kaplan-Meier curve and Cox-proportional Hazard model were used to analyze 30-day survival. A p value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R software, version 4.0.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patients

Between April 2022 and December 2024, we screened 322 critically ill patients across 2 medical ICUs at Siriraj Hospital. Of these, 222 patients were excluded, primarily because of severe AKI, an imminent need for acute RRT, or end-stage renal disease (Fig. 1). The remaining 100 patients were enrolled and included in the final analysis, with 50 randomly assigned to the restrictive fluid strategy (intervention) group and 50 to the usual care (control) group.

Primary outcome

All randomized patients remained in the ICU at 72 h. The restrictive fluid strategy group had a significantly lower cumulative fluid balance at 72 h than the usual care group (‒2353.50 mL [‒4132.50 to ‒879.50] vs. 792.50 mL [112.50 to 1548.75], p < 0.001; Table 2). Sources of fluid administration during the first 3 days after randomization are shown in Table S2. The total volumes of maintenance fluids and intravenous fluids for medications and fluid creep were significantly higher in the usual care group than in the intervention group. Only 4 patients in this study received fluid resuscitation after randomization; all of whom were in the usual care group. No significant differences were observed in the amount of fluid resuscitation, blood component use, or oral fluid intake between the two groups. Urine output was higher in the intervention group (Table S3 and Fig. S2).

Secondary outcomes

On day 7, the intervention group showed a significantly lower cumulative fluid balance than the usual care group (‒3031.50 mL [‒5020.50 to ‒1522.75] vs. 1125.00 mL [‒1944.00 to 3432.50], p < 0.001; Table 2; Fig. 2). Daily fluid balance was significantly lower in the intervention group over the first 4 days and did not differ from day 5 to day 7 (Table S1 and Fig. S1). The incidence of AKI and RRT use did not differ between the groups on days 3 or 7 (Table 2). However, the intervention group had a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation (6.00 days [4.00‒9.50] vs. 10.00 days [6.00‒17.00], p = 0.02) and shorter ICU and hospital stays (16.00 days [9.50‒23.50] vs. 22.00 days [13.00‒32.00], p = 0.02) compared to the control group. Ventilator-free days (28.00 days [25.25-29.00] vs. 22.00 days [0.00–29.00], p = 0.01) and vasopressor-free days through day 30 (29.00 days [26.00–29.00] vs. 25.50 days [0.00–29.00], p = 0.01) were significantly longer in the intervention group. Hospital mortality (18% vs. 38%, p = 0.05) and 30-day mortality (12% vs. 30%, p = 0.05) did not differ significantly between groups (Table 2). However, the 30-day mortality rate was significantly lower in the intervention group (hazard ratio: 0.36; 95% confidence interval: 0.14‒0.94; p = 0.04; Fig. S3).

Discussion

Key findings

This pilot study assessed the efficacy of a restrictive fluid strategy aimed at achieving a neutral to negative fluid balance within the first 72 h in critically ill patients with shock after initial fluid resuscitation and maintaining adequate tissue perfusion for at least 12 h, most of whom had sepsis. The findings demonstrated that a protocol-based intervention targeting zero fluid balance in ICU patients after initial fluid resuscitation was feasible. Its effects persisted until day 7 after randomization. The approach involved restricting fluid intake, discontinuing unnecessary fluids, minimizing parenteral nutrition and blood transfusions, and administering diuretics to increase urine output. Our exploratory analysis of secondary outcomes suggested potential clinical signals included a reduced duration of mechanical ventilation, more ventilator-free and vasopressor-free days, shorter overall length of stay, and lower mortality. No statistically significant differences in the pre-specified serious adverse events attributable to the fluid strategy were reported, suggesting the intervention was well-tolerated compared to usual care. The recruitment rate was 3 patients per month, and no protocol violations occurred.

Relationship with previous studies

The results of this study are consistent with those of prior research confirming the feasibility and safety of restrictive fluid management in critically ill patients with circulatory shock. In the CLASSIC trial, a restrictive intravenous fluid strategy in patients with septic shock did not alter 90-day mortality or serious adverse events [6]. Similarly, the CLOVERS trial compared 2 fluid management approaches in sepsis-induced hypotension (“vasopressors-first” versus “fluid-first”) and found no significant differences in 90-day mortality or serious adverse events [8]. However, these trials largely focused on early fluid therapy phases, including resuscitation, optimization, and stabilization.

A retrospective study in a Belgian tertiary mixed ICU population showed that maintenance fluids and fluid creep (fluids used as carriers for medications or electrolytes) contributed more to positive fluid balance than did fluid resuscitation [15]. The REVERSE-AKI study also demonstrated that a restrictive fluid management strategy in critically ill patients with AKI, aimed at a negative or neutral balance by the day after randomization, reduced cumulative fluid balance at 72 h without increasing adverse events [16]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of careful fluid management throughout all critical illness phases, including de-escalation.

Unlike the above studies, our trial implemented a fluid management protocol after initial resuscitation, once hemodynamics was stable. Both groups received approximately 4.5 L of fluid prior to randomization. Then, this protocol optimized fluid administration, minimized inadvertent fluid, and employed active de-resuscitation using diuretics and RRT if needed. We specifically targeted a neutral to negative fluid balance after initial fluid resuscitation, which should be tailored to each patient’s baseline characteristics, comorbidities, illness severity, prior fluid administration, and diagnosis [17]. At randomization, participants had received an average intravenous fluid volume exceeding 30 mL/kg, aligning with the CLASSIC and CLOVERS trials and with Surviving Sepsis Guidelines for adequate initial fluid resuscitation [18]. The total fluid balance during our study was higher than in some prior trials. A retrospective analysis by Murphy et al. suggested that administering more than 20 mL/kg of intravenous fluids and achieving a central venous pressure higher than 8 mmHg might reduce hospital mortality [19]. This approach should be followed by conservative late fluid management, aiming for at least 2 consecutive days of neutral or negative fluid balance in the first week after sepsis onset.

Our study reached a neutral or negative fluid balance despite similar diuretic doses between groups. Adequate initial fluid resuscitation may enhance early tissue perfusion [20], while subsequent fluid restriction likely prevents renal congestion and improves kidney function [21]. Consequently, diuretic requirements to increase urine output may be lower.

We observed potential clinical benefits associated with a restrictive fluid strategy, including a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation, more ventilator-free days and vasopressor-free days and reduced ICU and hospital stays. A secondary analysis of the CLOVER trial showed that in patient with chronic kidney disease (CKD) and sepsis-induced hypotension, an early restrictive fluid management was associated with longer ventilator-free and vasopressor-free days [22]. Similarly, Previous comparisons of liberal and conservative fluid strategies in acute lung injury patients showed that conservative fluid management improved ventilator-free days, organ-failure‒free days, and ICU-free days [7]. One possible mechanism is that minimizing fluid overload may enhance vasopressor responsiveness and preserve organ function [23–25]. However, a subgroup analysis in the RADAR-2 study found that active de-resuscitation within 24–48 h of ICU admission could increase mortality risk in mechanically ventilated patients with sepsis [26]. These findings highlight the need to individualize active de-resuscitation.

In our study, the rate of AKI and other serious adverse events was similar between groups, aligning with other conservative fluid management trials. However, in the RELIEF study of patients undergoing major abdominal surgery, a restrictive fluid approach was associated with a higher incidence of AKI [9]. This discrepancy underscores the heterogeneity of patient populations and suggests that fluid strategies must be tailored to the clinical context.

Clinical implications

This study suggests that a protocol-based fluid management strategy, implemented after initial hemodynamic stabilization in critically ill patients with septic shock, can feasibly achieve a neutral or negative fluid balance. Furthermore, our findings suggest that this approach may potentially improve outcomes, such as shortening mechanical ventilation duration, increasing ventilator-free and vasopressor-free days and trending toward reducing mortality, without increasing the observed incidence of pre-specified serious adverse events. Moreover, our data indicate that total fluid administration in our ICU was generally higher than in other trials, which may contribute to worse outcomes. This volume level, however, represents a modifiable factor that clinicians can optimize to enhance patient outcomes. These findings warrant confirmation in larger, adequately powered studies.

Strengths and limitations

Our randomized controlled trial evaluated the effectiveness of a protocol-based restrictive fluid strategy in a high-risk cohort of critically ill shock resuscitated patients, most with septic shock. A key strength of this approach is the structured integration of both fluid de-escalation and active de-resuscitation strategies, implemented early in the post-resuscitation phase after hemodynamic stabilization. This addresses an important knowledge gap in fluid management by focusing on a phase in which protocolized fluid stewardship is not yet well established. The intervention was both feasible and reproducible in the medical ICU setting. We relied on basic bedside monitoring to identify and manage circulatory shock, randomizing patients only after stable hemodynamics were achieved. No protocol violations occurred.

Several limitations warrant mention. First, the unblinded design could have introduced bias, particularly given the discretion of fluid management in the control group and the potential for variability in approaches to fluid responsiveness assessment. Additionally, the pre-defined numeric cut-ff for “completed resuscitation” prior to randomization was determined by the attending physician based on clinical judgment. However, participants had received an average intravenous fluid volume exceeding 30 mL/kg prior to randomization, with a mean arterial blood pressure > 65 mmHg and a median lactate level ≤ 2 mmol/L in both groups, suggesting adequate fluid resuscitation. This approach also reflects real-world variability in fluid practices and was a pragmatic choice for a feasibility trial comparing a protocol-based strategy to usual care. Second, as a single-center study with a relatively small sample size, the results may carry an elevated risk of type I error. Still, our findings align with prior research, underscoring the importance of considering all phases of fluid therapy in critical illness. Third, the control group’s fluid balance was relatively high compared with other studies, which could have magnified the apparent benefits of the restrictive approach. Nonetheless, the overall fluid volume was lower than in landmark resuscitation trials published in the past decade [27–29]. Fourth, baseline characteristics were not fully balanced, as malignancy was more common in the control group (42% vs. 20%, p = 0.03). Since malignancy is a strong predictor of mortality and morbidity in the ICU, we acknowledge that patients with malignancy often have a higher burden of illness, may be more susceptible to complications, and can have different trajectories of fluid management and outcomes. This imbalance is a significant potential confounder that could partially or fully explain the observed differences in outcomes such as hospital mortality, 30-day mortality, and lengths of stay. Additionally, baseline CVP was higher in the control group (median 12.00 mmHg [9.00–14.00]) compared to the intervention group (median 9.00 mmHg [7.00-12.25], p = 0.03). This difference in CVP could suggest a greater intrinsic fluid burden or tendency towards fluid accumulation in the control group, potentially confounding the interpretation of outcomes related to renal and other organ recovery. Given the feasibility nature of the study and its limited statistical power to detect differences in clinical endpoints, these findings should be interpreted with caution, acknowledging the potential influence of baseline imbalances.

Conclusions

A protocol-based restrictive fluid strategy, combined with early fluid de-escalation and de-resuscitation after the acute resuscitation phase, is feasible and can reduce fluid accumulation. This approach was associated with a signal for shorter ICU and hospital stays, more ventilator-free and vasopressor-free days and a trend towards lower 30-day mortality, without an observed increase in pre-specified adverse events. These findings suggest the potential for clinical benefits that require validation in larger, adequately powered randomized controlled trials.
