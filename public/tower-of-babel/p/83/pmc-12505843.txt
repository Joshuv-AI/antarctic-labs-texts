Trend of malaria test positivity rate among pregnant women, Savannah Region, Ghana, 2018–2022
Authors: Wadeyir Jonathan Abesig, Joseph Alphonsus Chantiwuni Nindow, Annungma C Bagonluri, Adjei Kwabena Sarfo, Gyesi Razak Issahaku, Magdalene Akos Odikro, George Akowuah, Delia Akosua Bandoh, Ernest Kenu, Chrysantus Kubio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria in pregnancy (MiP) remains a serious public health problem due to its adverse effects on maternal health, pregnancy outcomes, and the cost of healthcare delivery. Significant successes have been achieved in controlling malaria globally. However, little is known about the progress made in controlling malaria in pregnancy in the Savannah Region. We described the trend of the MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah Region of Ghana from 2018 to 2022.MethodsThe study employed a retrospective, descriptive, cross-sectional study using data from 2018 to 2022. Data on malaria in pregnancy in the Savannah Region was extracted from the DHIMS 2 database and transferred into Microsoft Excel version 19. Descriptive statistical analyses were done. Results were summarised into frequencies and percentages and presented in tables, graphs, and maps.ResultsOverall, the MiP testing rate was 90.8% (46,734/51,479) and 54.5% (95% CI: 54.0–55.0) of pregnant women tested positive. The majority, 68.0%, of MiP cases in the region were mothers aged 20–34 years. However, the MiP test positivity rate was highest among mothers aged below 20 years, 59.3% (95% CI: 58.1–60.6). Bole District had the highest MiP test positivity rate, 70.1% (95% CI: 69.3–70.8) and accounted for 40.5% (10,315/25,463) of all MiP cases. There was a decline in the MiP test positivity rate from 2018 to 2022 from 60.4% (95% CI: 59.3–61.5) to 51.6% (95% CI: 50.7–52.6).ConclusionThe MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah Region is high, with disparities among Districts. However, there is an apparent decline over the study period. There is a need to strengthen existing malaria prevention interventions in pregnancy, such as the use of SP for IPTp and ITNs to prevent negative maternal and fetal birth outcomes. Public health education programmes could improve the uptake and utilisation of ITNs. Further research should be conducted to unravel the reasons for the disproportionately high MiP test positivity rate in the Bole District to enable the implementation of preventive measures.Keywords: Malaria in pregnancy, Trend, Savannah region, Ghana

Background

Malaria in pregnant women remains a serious public health problem due to its adverse effects on maternal and pregnancy outcomes [1, 2]. Adverse effects of malaria infection in pregnancy (MiP) include stillbirth, preterm birth, congenital malaria, maternal anaemia, low-birth weight, and maternal and perinatal mortality [3–5]. MiP is responsible for about 0.5% − 23% of maternal deaths globally, with significant regional disparities [6]. MiP also imposes financial burdens on pregnant women, their families, and the healthcare system [4, 7]. Africa bears the highest burden of malaria, accounting for 95% of global cases and 96% of malaria-related deaths [5]. In 2021, 11.6 million pregnant women were exposed to malaria in Africa, with 4.6 million in the West Africa sub-region [8].

There have been some significant successes achieved in the global control of malaria. However, progress in the African region is slow [9, 10]. Despite preventive measures such as free distribution of long-lasting insecticide-treated nets (LLINs) and intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine-pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP), malaria remains a significant burden among pregnant women in Ghana. The prevalence of malaria in pregnancy was found to be 11.0% among pregnant women receiving antenatal care at the Akatsi South District Hospital [11]. In four districts of Ghana’s Bono-East Region, the prevalence was 20.4% [12], and in the northern region of Ghana, 9.5% [13]. A study comparing malaria rapid diagnostic tests (mRDTs) and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) for malaria diagnosis among pregnant women in northern Ghana found prevalence rates of 14.1% and 13.4%, respectively [14].

Previous research conducted in northern Ghana estimated malaria prevalence [7, 15]. However, MiP test positivity in the Savannah Region is not well documented. Therefore, there is a need to investigate malaria during pregnancy in the Savannah Region of Ghana. Findings from this study will help bridge the knowledge gap in the trend of MiP in the Savannah Region and inform the development of practical intervention strategies to prevent the disease. We described the trend of MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah Region of Ghana from 2018 to 2022.

Methods

Study setting

The study was conducted in the Savannah Region, one of the 16 administrative regions of Ghana. The region was carved out from the Northern Region in 2019. Located in the northwestern part of Ghana, the region shares borders with Upper West to the north, Bono and Bono East Regions to the south and to the east by North East and Northern Regions. It also shares international boundaries with Côte d’Ivoire and Burkina Faso to the west. It is the largest region in the country by land area and spans 35,862 km2 of land. This covers about 15% of the land area of Ghana. The region has seven Municipal/District Assemblies with an estimated population of 653,266 [16]. The Savannah Region has a total of 205 Health facilities, consisting of five District Hospitals, three Polyclinics, 26 Health Centres, 158 Community-based Health Planning and Services (CHPS) Compounds and 13 clinics and maternity homes [17]. The vegetation is predominantly grassland, with clusters of drought-resistant trees such as baobabs and acacias. There are mainly two seasons, the dry (December – April) and the wet (July – November) seasons. These seasons are generally associated with the incidence of malaria, with the prevalence of MiP ranging from 59.7% in the rainy season and 41.3% in the dry season [18].

The main activities of the population are farming, hunting, and gari processing. There are also illegal small-scale mining activities (Galamsey) in some districts, such as the Dollar power in the Bole district. The region is home to the Mole National Park, the oldest and largest wildlife park in Ghana, which predisposes the region to vector-borne diseases such as malaria. It also has many rivers, notably the White and Black Volta, which overflow their banks and make some communities inaccessible (overseas areas) during the rainy seasons [17]. These factors may affect healthcare delivery in the region.

Operational definitions

Suspected MiP case

Any pregnant woman WHO presents with a fever or history of fever in the last 48 h and lives in malaria-endemic areas, or has a history of travel within the last 30 days to a malaria-endemic area [19].

Tested MiP case

A suspected case of malaria in a pregnant woman subjected to a malaria Rapid Diagnostic Test (mRDT) or microscopy test [19].

MiP positive case

A suspected MiP case in which malaria parasites have been verified by microscopy or malaria Rapid Diagnostic Test (mRDT) [19].

MiP positivity rate

Number of MiP-positive cases confirmed by microscopy or mRDT divided by the total suspected MiP cases tested, multiplied by 100.

MiP not-tested but treated

A suspected case of malaria in a pregnant woman in which microscopy or RDT is not done, but the woman is put on artemisinin combination therapy (ACT).

MiP tested negative but treated

A suspected case of MiP in a woman who tested negative for malaria parasites by microscopy or mRDT, but the woman is put on ACT.

Data collection and processing

Secondary data was analysed to describe MiP in the Savannah Region of Ghana from 2018 to 2022. The data was abstracted from the District Health Information Management System-2 (DHIMS-2) and transferred into Microsoft Excel version 19. DHIMS 2 is an integrated internet-based electronic database that aggregates health facility-based data on health services provided nationwide for Ghana [20]. Variables abstracted included district, age, suspected malaria, suspected malaria tested, tested positive, not tested but treated, and tested negative but treated for pregnant women.

When the data was extracted from the DHIMS 2 into Microsoft Excel, it was cleaned and checked for completeness and inconsistencies. All columns were inspected to ensure they were all in the correct data format. The data elements that described the same variable were rearranged to contiguous columns for easy identification and verification, as well as for easy aggregation. Generally, due to the robust supervision of DHIMS 2 by its management team on data completeness and consistency upon entry by health information officers, the dataset was found to be comprehensive and accurate, with no instances of missing data or inconsistencies.

Data analysis

Descriptive statistical analyses, which included frequencies and percentages, were used to summarise the variables, including age, suspected malaria, suspected malaria tested, tested positive, not tested but treated, tested negative but treated, and district. Maps were drawn using Geographic Information System (QGIS) software with shapefiles from DHIMS 2 to show MiP test positivity rates and proportions of MiP-positive cases by district. The results were presented in tables, graphs and maps.

Results

Characteristics of pregnant women

For the study period, the overall MiP testing rate was 90.8% (46,734/51,479) and the average positivity rate was 54.5% (95% CI: 54.0–55.0) in the Savannah Region (Table 1). The majority of MiP cases were in the 20–34-year age group, accounting for 68.0% (16,293/25,463), while women within the 10–19-year age group had the lowest proportion of MiP cases, at 14.3% (3,736/25,463). Pregnant women less than 20 years had the highest positivity rate, 59.3% (95% CI: 58.1–60.6), while those aged 35 years and above had the lowest positivity rate, 48.7% (95% CI: 47.6–49.9) (Table 2).

Discussion

The study sought to describe the trends of the MiP test positivity rate of the Savannah Region of Ghana from 2018 to 2022. Overall, the MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah Region during the study period was high, 54.5%, compared to the national estimate of 34.3% [21]. This calls for health authorities in the Savannah Region to review their existing MiP interventions and strategies to improve the MiP situation in the region.

The MiP test positivity rate generally declined from 60.4 to 51.6% over the study period. This decline is in line with global and national trends, though there were disparities among the three ecological zones in Ghana [21]. The national MiP test positivity rate declined from 54.0 to 34.3% between 2014 and 2021 [22]. The Savannah ecological belt experienced the least decline, at 43%, compared with 53% and 74% in the forest and coastal ecological belts, respectively [21].

The declining trend in the MiP test positivity rate nationally and in the Savannah Region might indicate the possibility of some positive interventional effects on MiP, such as the use of sulphadoxine-pyrimethamine (SP) for intermittent preventive treatment (IPTp) and the use of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs). This effect, however, is moderate in the Savannah Region compared to that observed nationally. This moderate decline in the MiP cases in the study area may be due to the region being a high-malaria prevalent area with marked seasonal variation, and hence, periconceptional infection might be a key factor accounting for the slow decline in MiP cases in the region [18, 21, 23]. Additionally, the region’s seasonal malaria transmission pattern and the hot weather in the Savannah belt may discourage consistent bed net utilisation [22, 24]. This suggests that reducing malaria among pregnant women in the Savannah Region requires more than just the use of SP and ITNs, and calls for in-depth analysis of these interventions as well as new approaches by the health authorities and all other stakeholders. A chemoprevention similar to the seasonal malaria chemoprevention done for children under 5 years could be considered in women of reproductive age in the Savannah region as a starting point.

There was a surge in the MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah Region in 2020. This increased trend in malaria cases was observed globally in 2020 and is attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic which began in Wuhan, China, in 2019. The WHO in 2021 stated that there were about 14 million more malaria cases and 69,000 more deaths in 2020 compared to those in 2019, attributable to disruptions of service provision in malaria prevention, diagnosis, and treatment during the pandemic [8]. According to Hussein et al., the pandemic disrupted malaria prevention interventions in pregnancy, such as the distribution of insecticide-treated nets (ITNs), indoor residual spraying, as well as malaria chemoprevention during the lockdown, and therefore called for an urgent need for rapid and effective responses to avoid malaria outbreaks [25]. In Sub-Saharan Africa, Heuschen and his group reported that many researchers provided potential explanations for the effects of COVID‑19 on the malaria burden; these ranged from relevant diagnostic and clinical aspects to reduced access to health care services, impaired availability of curative and preventive commodities and medications, and effects on malaria prevention campaigns [26]. Evidence from Ghana showed a reduction in the proportion of pregnant women WHO received IPTp between 2019 and 2020, attributed to the effects of COVID-19 [19]. In the northern region of Ghana, it was found that when the lockdown was implemented, pregnant women experienced reduced access to intermittent preventive malaria treatment and insecticide-treated nets, resulting in a subsequent higher malaria morbidity in 2020 [27].

All the age groups of pregnant women were infected by malaria, with the majority within the 20–34 years age group. This agrees with the findings of many studies of malaria among pregnant women, which found the majority of MiP cases to be between 25 and 34 years [12, 28, 29]. This observation may be because women at this age are at their prime for reproduction and represent the majority of pregnant women. This study, however, revealed that pregnant women aged < 20 years were more susceptible to malaria in pregnancy as they presented with the highest MiP test positivity rate. This agrees with previous observations by other researchers in Ghana [21]. It was observed that adolescent girls in sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) are more prone to experience clinical malaria episodes during pregnancy and have peripheral malaria and placental infection at delivery than adult women [30]. Young pregnant women and women of low gravidity are said to lack immunity to plasmodium-infected erythrocytes that sequester in the placenta and thus are susceptible to placental malaria (PM), leading to severe adverse outcomes [21, 22, 31]. Preventing MiP in this category of pregnant women will not only reduce the burden of MiP but also prevent pre-eclampsia, as it is linked to placental malaria [32]. A more effective IPTp drug, such as dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine (IPTp-DHA-PPQ), could be considered in this category of pregnant women, as it was associated with a lower incidence of malaria infection during pregnancy and at delivery compared to IPTp-SP [33].

Further analysis of the distribution of MiP by district shows that the districts in the Savannah Region with high MiP test positivity rates are mainly three. These Districts are Bole, Sawla-Tuna-Kalba, and North Gonja. These Districts remain at risk and pose challenges for the fight against malaria among pregnant women in the region. The Bole District alone accounted for over two-fifths of the MiP burden in the region over the study period. Bole and the Sawla-Tuna-Kalba are districts that share boundaries with the Ivory Coast and the Burkina Faso, and pregnant women from nearby communities often cross into these districts to access healthcare when they are ill. This could account for the increased MiP in these districts.

Additionally, Bole District hosts illegal small-scale mining sites, popularly known as galamsey in Ghanaian local parlance, with many people from other parts of the country coming into the district with their families. The Sawla-Tuna-Kalba and North Gonja districts are home to the Mole National Park, a rich wildlife reserve. This wildlife could also serve as a reservoir for malaria parasites and a vector for their transmission. Furthermore, the Black and White Volta rivers and their tributaries also pass through the Sawla-Tuna-Kalba Districts and may serve as breeding sites for mosquitoes. The high MiP test positivity rate and malaria incidence correlate poorly, and these districts with high MiP test positivity rates may not have a high incidence of malaria in pregnancy [34, 35]. A study in the Democratic Republic of Congo, which evaluated trends in reported malaria cases between 2005 and 2014, attributed the sharp increase in confirmed cases after 2010 to the introduction and scale-up of mRDTs rather than an actual increase in malaria incidence [36].

Strengths and limitations

The study has several strengths, including being the first to examine the MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah Region. The findings could thus serve as the basis for other future studies. Furthermore, the findings are based on actual data captured in the DHIMS 2, which is retrospectively analysed but not based on the perception, opinion, and recall ability of respondents. This eliminated the potential effect of recall bias. Malaria test positivity is not affected by RDT stock-out or interruptions in microscopy services because the denominator does not include suspected cases that are not tested, especially since the average testing rate was above 90% [37] The data sample in the analysis was large (census of all MiP-positive cases over the study period in the DHIMS 2), and hence the findings are representative of the region.

Despite these strengths, this study has some limitations. The MiP test positivity rate correlates poorly with malaria incidence rate; hence, the Savannah Region cannot be said to have a high MiP incidence rate solely because the positivity rate is high and vice versa. Again, the MiP test positivity rate does capture pregnant women WHO are asymptomatic of malaria but have parasites. Again, the study relied on secondary data in the DHIMS 2. Therefore, the accuracy and quality of the data captured in the DHIMS 2 cannot be fully accounted for. Thus, any data capture errors could not have been addressed adequately. DHIMS 2 data is aggregated, and analysis at the individual level is not possible.

Conclusion

The MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah region is high, with a notable decline over the study period. However, the decline is slower than the national trends observed. Disparities exist among the districts in the region, with three of them being the most affected. Also, pregnant women aged < 20 years have a disproportionately high MiP test positivity rate in the Savannah region. The Savannah Regional Health Directorate should intensify awareness creation and education of pregnant women and their families (community leaders and members) about malaria in pregnancy and its negative effects on maternal health and pregnancy outcomes. We recommend that the Regional Health Directorate to undertake a thorough review of malaria prevention practices in Districts with high MiP test positivity rates. This assessment will inform targeted interventions and enhance the effectiveness of malaria elimination efforts. Further research should be conducted to identify the reasons for the disproportionately high MiP test positivity rate in the Bole District, enabling the implementation of targeted and practical measures.
