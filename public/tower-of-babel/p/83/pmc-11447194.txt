Efficacy and safety of two Antivenoms in the treatment of eastern copperhead (Agkistrodon contortrix) envenomations in Southeast Texas
Authors: Spencer Greene, Alexander Teshon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThere are two Food and Drug Administration–approved antivenoms for the treatment of North American pitviper envenomations: Crotalidae polyvalent immune fab (ovine) (CroFab, henceforth FabAV) and Crotalidae immune F(ab’)2 (equine) (Anavip, henceforth Fab2AV). This study compared the effectiveness and safety of the two products in eastern copperhead envenomations.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed eastern copperhead bites on which our toxicology service consulted between January 1, 2021 and November 1, 2023. Collected data included patient demographics, bite location, clinical features, antivenom use, response to treatment, and adverse reactions to antivenom.ResultsThere were 134 patients with confirmed copperhead envenomations. We administered antivenom to 89 patients, of which 36 (40%) were female. The median age was 42 years old (range: 2–89 years). Fifty‐nine patients received Fab2AV and 30 were treated with FabAV. Initial control was achieved in 30 (100%) patients treated with FabAV. In the Fab2AV group, 53 (89.8%) achieved control; six patients with acute adverse reactions declined further treatment despite persistent symptoms. The median FabAV dose required for initial control was six vials (range: 4–6). A median dose of 10 vials (range: 10–30) was used in the Fab2AV group. Repeat doses of Fab2AV were required in 15 (25.4%) cases. There were no acute adverse reactions to FabAV. Acute adverse reactions were seen in seven (11.9%) of patients treated with Fab2AV.ConclusionFewer patients treated with FabAV required repeat antivenom dosing to attain initial control following eastern copperhead envenomation. A higher percentage of patients treated with Fab2AV developed acute adverse reactions.Keywords: Agkistrodon, antivenom, copperhead, envenomation, snakebite

INTRODUCTION

Background

The majority of snake envenomations evaluated in the emergency department (ED) in the United States are attributed to two native copperhead species. 1 , 2 The eastern copperhead (Agkistrodon contortrix), which is broadly distributed throughout the Southeast, Midwest, and Atlantic coast, is responsible for most of these bites. 3 The broad‐banded copperhead (Agkistrodon laticinctus) has a more limited habitat extending from Central Texas to Southern Kansas. There are currently two antivenoms that are approved by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) for the treatment of copperhead and other North American pitviper envenomations in adult and pediatric patients. 4 , 5 Crotalidae polyvalent immune fab (ovine) (CroFab, BTG International Inc., henceforth FabAV) has been available since 2000. Crotalidae immune F(ab’)2 (equine) (Anavip, Rare Disease Therapeutics Inc., henceforth Fab2AV) was introduced to US markets in 2018 and was originally indicated only for rattlesnake envenomations. It received approval for all North American pitviper envenomations in 2021.

Importance

Most studies comparing the two antivenoms have focused on their use in rattlesnake envenomations. 6 , 7 Proponents of Fab2AV argue that it performs better in minimizing hematologic recurrence, yet copperhead envenomations rarely cause hematologic laboratory abnormalities. 8 , 9 , 10 Systemic toxicity is also relatively infrequent. Clinical features are typically limited to local tissue injury, which has led several authors to question whether antivenom is necessary for copperhead envenomations. 11 , 12 , 13 However, a randomized clinical trial of FabAV for mild to moderate copperhead envenomation found clinically significant symptomatic improvement at 2 weeks in the treatment group compared to the placebo group. 14 There are no similar studies evaluating the efficacy of Fab2AV in copperhead envenomation. A recent case series described multiple patients with copperhead envenomations who required FabAV after they failed to improve with Fab2AV. 15 It is possible that the structural or compositional differences between the two antivenoms make one more appropriate for copperhead envenomations than the other, which may have relevance when determining how hospitals stock antivenom.

Goals of this investigation

The purpose of the study is to assess the effectiveness and incidence of acute adverse reactions of two antivenoms in the treatment of copperhead envenomations.

METHODS

Study design and setting

This is a retrospective review of all copperhead envenomation patients treated with antivenom at our suburban community teaching hospital in Southeast Texas between January 2021 and November 2023. Financial support for the study was provided by BTG International, Inc., for which the lead author serves as a paid speaker and consultant. The study was determined to be exempt or excluded from institutional review board oversight in accordance with current regulations and institutional policy.

The Bottom Line

There are two antivenoms approved by the Food and Drug Administration for the treatment of North American pitviper envenomations: Crotalidae polyvalent immune Fab (ovine), also known as CroFab, and Crotalidae immune F(ab’)2 (equine), also known as Anavip. In our study, initial control was achieved in all patients treated with a single dose of CroFab (range: 4–6 vials.) Control was achieved in 89.8% of patients treated with Anavip, although repeating dosing was required in 25.4% of patients (range: 10–30 vials). Acute adverse reactions were seen in 11.9% of patients treated with Anavip and no patients treated with CroFab.

Selection of participants

We reviewed the charts of all suspected snakebite patients who were seen in our hospital during the study period. It is hospital policy to consult our medical toxicologist for every potential snakebite, and it is the toxicologist who directs the care of these patients in the ED and upon admission. No patients were admitted to the intensive care unit. The subjects were identified by the patient log maintained by the toxicologist. Patients were included in the study if the snake responsible for the bite could be definitively identified as a copperhead. The toxicologist, who also consults for the zoo and moderates an on‐demand snake identification service, relied on multiple phenotypical traits to identify snakes (Figure 1). Unless the snake could be definitively identified as a copperhead by the toxicologist, the case was excluded, even if the patient was confident of the snake identity. Patients with bites from other snakes and those who did not receive antivenom were also excluded.

Interventions

Patients were treated with either FabAV or Fab2AV. Our hospital system switched from FabAV to Fab2AV in June 2022. Neither the treating physicians nor the patients were blinded to which antivenom they received. Individual treatment decisions, including whether to administer antivenom and if additional doses were necessary, were made by the toxicologist using the Unified Treatment Algorithm for the Management of Crotaline Snakebite in the United States and the American Academy of Emergency Medicine guidelines for crotalid envenomations. 16 , 17

Measurements

All patients had serial blood testing for platelet count, fibrinogen, and prothrombin time. A baseline basic metabolic profile was also obtained but only repeated if there were clinically significant abnormalities. The frequency and duration of laboratory testing was decided by the treating toxicologist. Serial examinations of the affected limb were performed every 15 min until the initial dose of antivenom was started. We relied on the leading edge of tenderness and swelling as recommended in a widely cited snakebite algorithm. 16 We chose not to use circumferential measurements of the affected limb because we have noticed significant inter‐rater and intra‐rater variability among our colleagues with this technique. We defined local progression as proximal migration of both swelling and tenderness. The frequency of reassessment was at the discretion of the treating physician but occurred within 1 h of completing the initial antivenom dose and at least every 4 h afterward.

Outcomes

The two primary outcomes measured in the study were the number of antivenom vials needed to attain initial control and the incidence of acute adverse reactions. Our indications for antivenom included any of the following: (1) progressive local findings (eg, swelling and tenderness that cross a major joint), (2) systemic signs and symptoms (eg, dyspnea, hypotension, or recurrent vomiting or diarrhea), and/or (3) clinically significant hematologic laboratory abnormalities. We defined dyspnea as the subjective experience of difficulty breathing and/or the appearance of someone having difficulty breathing, for example, accessory muscle use and bronchospasm. We arbitrarily chose a systolic blood pressure less than 90 mm Hg as the cutoff for hypotension. We defined thrombocytopenia as a platelet count <150 K/µL and hypofibrinogenemia as a fibrinogen count <150 mg/dL. Initial control was defined as a resolution of systemic toxicity, cessation of progression of local findings, and improvement in any hematologic toxicity. Adverse reactions were categorized as pruritus, urticaria, hypotension, and/or dyspnea.

Secondary outcomes included the need for repeat doses of antivenom and treatments required for any adverse reactions.

Data analysis

The number of vials used were reported as median and interquartile range (IQR). Baseline patient characteristics and incidence of acute adverse reactions were reported as frequencies and percentages. Data were analyzed using chi‐square test (graphpad.com).

RESULTS

Characteristics of study subjects

There were 226 snakebite patients, including 134 (59.3%) whose bites were due to eastern copperheads (Figure 2). We administered antivenom to 89 (66.4%) subjects, of which 36 (40%) were female. The median patient age was 42 years old (range: 2–89 years). Every patient who received antivenom had progressive local swelling and tenderness. Antivenom was recommended in an additional 24 (17.9%) cases. Following a discussion of the potential risks, benefits, and cost of antivenom, these patients declined antivenom for financial reasons. A hospital financial counselor was not available to discuss payment options.

Efficacy

Fab2AV was administered to 59 (66.3%) patients. FabAV was used in 30 (33.7%) cases. No patients received both antivenoms. Control was achieved in 30 (100%) FabAV patients and 53 (89.8%) Fab2AV patients, p = 0.639. A median dose of six vials (range: 4–6, IQR 4–6) was required in the FabAV group. No FabAV patients required repeat doses. A median dose of 10 vials (range: 10–30, IQR 10–20) was required for patients treated with Fab2AV, and 15 (25.4%) subjects required repeat doses.

The FabAV package insert recommends maintenance dosing of two vials every 6 h for three doses after initial control is attained to prevent recurrence. During the first few months of the study period, the treating physician ordered maintenance FabAV dosing as recommended. Once it became apparent that the additional doses were unnecessary because there was no recurrence, the toxicologist decreased the number of maintenance doses until he stopped ordering it completely. Overall, a median of two vials (range: 2–6) were administered for maintenance in 16 (53.3%) subjects.

Adverse events

There were no adverse reactions observed in the FabAV group. Acute adverse cutaneous reactions were seen in seven (11.9%) patients treated with Fab2AV, and one (1.7%) patient also had anaphylaxis characterized by dyspnea and hypotension, p = 0.059. Six patients were treated with H1 and H2 antagonists. One subject also required epinephrine (Table 2). No treatment beyond antivenom discontinuation was needed in the remaining case. No patients with acute adverse reactions had been previously bitten by a snake or had received any previous antivenoms.

LIMITATIONS

There are several limitations to this study. The small sample sizes in both groups limit the ability to draw generalizable conclusions and may explain the lack of statistically significant differences. The two groups were slightly dissimilar at baseline; the Fab2AV group had a wider age range and a higher percentage of female patients, while the FabAV group had more systemic toxicity and a higher percentage of patients with lower extremity bites. Follow‐up information was unavailable for most subjects, so long‐term outcomes could not be assessed.

The toxicologist was not blinded to the choice of antivenom. The antivenoms were used at different time periods; it is possible that the treating physician altered his approach to treating envenomations over time. These results may not be generalizable to all copperhead envenomations; only eastern copperhead envenomations in Southeast Texas were included in this study. Lastly, this study only compared the number of vials needed to obtain control and the incidence of acute adverse reactions. It does not consider the cost‐effectiveness of one product over the other or of management of these envenomations without antivenom.

DISCUSSION

Whether copperhead envenomations warrant treatment with antivenom remains controversial. Several authors have argued that because most local findings eventually resolve and significant systemic toxicity is uncommon, the potential risks and cost outweigh the benefits. However, other studies have found that a significant number of copperhead bites that go untreated or undertreated can have prolonged disability. 18 , 19 A randomized clinical trial of FabAV use in mild and moderate copperhead envenomations found that treatment resulted in shorter time to complete recovery. 14 Furthermore, the proportion of patients requiring opioids was smaller in the FabAV treatment group compared to controls at all time points. 20 Total opioid use was also lower in the treatment group.

Because of these conflicting data, and because the snake identity is often unknown, current snakebite guidelines recommend that treatment decisions should be based on clinical and laboratory features rather than the snake species. 16 , 17

The risk of adverse reactions to antivenom may be overestimated by many clinicians. Previous studies have estimated the incidence of acute adverse reactions to FabAV to be between 0% and 8%. 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 In the largest study, only 19 (1.4%) of 1340 adult and pediatric subjects developed reactions, and in no cases did the antivenom infusion need to be discontinued. 22 Although we are unaware of any studies that were designed specifically to measure the incidence of acute adverse reactions in Fab2AV use, 11.6% of patients developed rash and 46.5% of patients experienced pruritus in one study. 6

There are structural differences between the two antivenoms that may explain our results. Fab2AV is a large molecule with an elimination half‐life of approximately 133 h, while FabAV is smaller and has a half‐life estimated to be between 12 and 23 h. 4 , 5 Although there are no studies that have specifically addressed this, we suspect the purported benefit of Fab2AV in preventing hematologic recurrence is likely due to its persistence in the circulation. However, we speculate that the larger size may also limit its ability to penetrate tissue. The smaller FabAV may enter tissue more easily. This could explain why it controlled local tissue damage better in our study.

In summary, we have a low threshold to treat copperhead envenomations with antivenom. Benefits include faster recovery and less need for opioids. Risks of going untreated include prolonged, and possibly permanent, disability. In this small study, we found that patients with eastern copperhead envenomations who were treated with FabAV required fewer vials of antivenom when compared to Fab2AV. No FabAV patient required repeat dosing. Acute adverse reactions were not observed in the FabAV group but were seen in some patients treated with Fab2AV. A randomized clinical trial comparing the two antivenoms may better quantify their respective safety and effectiveness in the treatment of copperhead envenomations.
