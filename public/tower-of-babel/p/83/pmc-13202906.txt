Acute inferior STEMI in a unique setting: case report on anomalous circumflex and multivessel coronary artery disease
Authors: Hesham Salah Eldin Taha, Mirna Mamdouh Shaker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe majority of coronary artery anomalies are harmless and asymptomatic. However, in rare cases, they have been linked to myocardial ischemia and sudden cardiac death. One of the most frequently occurring coronary anomalies is the aberrant origin of the circumflex coronary artery (Cx) from the right coronary sinus.Case presentationWe present a case of a 59-year-old man who suffered an inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. His coronary angiography revealed severe multivessel coronary artery disease (CAD) and anomalous origin of the Cx. The case presented a diagnostic challenge regarding whether the right coronary artery (RCA) or the anomalous Cx was the primary culprit. This uncertainty was further compounded by the fact that established algorithms for differentiating the culprit artery may not be directly applicable to anomalous coronary arteries. Primary percutaneous coronary angiography (PCI) was performed on the RCA. PCI to the anomalous Cx was challenging due to difficulty in coronary engagement and wiring. This was complicated by extensive dissection, which was managed through re-wiring and the placement of multiple stents. The left anterior descending artery stenting was uneventful. The patient had a favorable final angiographic and clinical outcome and has been asymptomatic for one year.ConclusionsThis case demonstrates the successful diagnosis and management of ACS in the context of multi-vessel CAD and an anomalous circumflex coronary artery. It highlights significant diagnostic challenges stemming from the lack of specific algorithms for such cases and the considerable management complexities faced during PCI. These insights underscore the critical need for tailored approaches and updated guidelines to effectively address similar intricate scenarios.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05979-3.

Introduction

Congenital coronary artery anomalies (CCAA) refer to abnormalities in the origin, course, or structure of these arteries. They occur secondary to changes that take place during the third week of fetal development [1–3]. The incidence of these anomalies varies, ranging from 0.2% to 5.6% [4–6]. In coronary angiographic studies, the incidence of anomalous coronary arteries with origin in the wrong sinus and single vessel coronary arteries falls between 0.28% and 1.74% [7]. One of the most common CCAA is the circumflex coronary artery (Cx) arising from the right coronary sinus, which can be categorized into three types based on its origin and its relationship with the right coronary artery (RCA) [8–10].

The majority of these anomalies are asymptomatic and have no hemodynamic or prognostic implications [7]. It is uncommon for patients with CCAA to present with an ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI), with a reported incidence of 0.4% [11]. However, while most CCAAs are benign, in some case reports, they have led to sudden death, myocardial infarction (MI), and angina pectoris, even in the absence of atherosclerotic lesions [12]. Furthermore, they are the second most common cause of sudden cardiac death in young athletes [4, 5].

In this case report, we present a patient with an anomalous circumflex coronary artery (Cx) originating from the proximal RCA. The patient presented with an inferior STEMI and multi-vessel CAD, including significant lesions in both the RCA and the anomalous Cx.

Interventions for anomalous coronary arteries in the setting of multi-vessel disease and acute coronary syndrome (ACS) are uncommon. This report examines the applicability of current algorithms for identifying the culprit artery in such scenarios. It also underscores the gap in acute management guidance within existing guidelines, which primarily address elective cases.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old gentleman with a history of dyslipidemia and smoking presented to the emergency department with severe compressing retrosternal and epigastric pain, radiating to both arms, of 1-h duration. His family history was irrelevant, and he had no history of previous cardiac events. On examination, his blood pressure (BP) differed significantly between the arms, measuring 150/90 mmHg in the left arm and 95/60 mmHg in the right arm. Additional findings included a weaker pulse in the right arm, normal jugular venous pressure and waveform, an S4 sound heard over the apex on auscultation, and scattered expiratory wheezes throughout the chest. His body mass index (BMI) was 26 kg/m2.

The unequal blood pressure and pulse in the upper limbs initially raised concerns about aortic dissection. However, further history clarified that this was due to prior trauma to the right arm. After a minor rotation of the arm, the right arm's BP measurement aligned closely with that of the left arm.

His ECG revealed sinus bradycardia with ST-segment elevation in the inferior leads, more prominent in lead III compared to lead II. Additionally, there was significant reciprocal ST-segment depression and T-wave inversion in leads I and aVL. Emergency coronary angiography was performed, revealing no Cx arising from the left main artery or the left coronary sinus. This absence was accompanied by the lack of arterial inflow to a substantial area of the posterior lateral left ventricle, known as the “avascular sign” or “sign of non-perfused myocardium” (Fig. 3). A significant stenosis was identified in the middle segment of the left anterior descending artery (LAD). Further right coronary arteriography revealed severe stenosis in both the proximal and distal segments of the RCA. Subsequent non-selective injection into the right coronary cusp demonstrated that the Cx originated from the proximal segment of the RCA (Fig. 4). Discussion and conclusions

The Cx anomaly, although typically regarded as benign, can lead to myocardial ischemia and has been associated with rare cases of sudden death, MI, and angina pectoris, even in the absence of atherosclerotic lesions [12].

An anomalous origin of Cx from the right coronary sinus is the most common congenital variant, with a prevalence of 0.18–0.67% [8]. It can be classified into 3 types: type A, where the Cx arises separately from the right coronary sinus, type B, where it arises from a common origin with the RCA and type C, where it arises from the proximal segment of the RCA [9, 10].

To the best of our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of a patient with an anomalous Cx originating from the proximal RCA, presenting with an inferior STEMI and exhibiting significant lesions in both the Cx and RCA.

Interventions for anomalous coronary arteries remain relatively uncommon in the literature, particularly when encountered alongside multi-vessel disease in the setting of acute coronary syndrome (ACS). This case seeks to bridge this gap by evaluating the applicability of existing algorithms for identifying the culprit artery in such complex scenarios. Moreover, current guidelines for managing anomalous coronary arteries predominantly focus on elective cases, emphasizing CABG or conservative approaches, while offering limited clarity for acute settings.

Most CCAAs are asymptomatic [4], often being identified incidentally during diagnostic evaluations for CAD [2]. Dursunoğlu et al. described a case involving a 57-year-old man who presented with an inferior STEMI and a history of hypertension (HTN), diabetes mellitus (DM), dyslipidemia, and prior primary PCI (PPCI) for an occluded RCA during an acute MI. Coronary angiography showed a total occlusion of the proximal RCA stent, along with a previously undetected anomalous Cx originating independently from the right coronary sinus, which was free of disease [14]. Similarly, Bhattad et al. reported the case of a 58-year-old man presenting with a cocaine-induced inferior STEMI. PPCI was performed for an occluded proximal RCA using one DES, and an anomalous Cx originating from the right coronary sinus was incidentally detected on angiography [15].

Congenital anomalies of the Cx may be implicated in the development of ischemic events even in the absence of significant coronary lesions [16]. Mahdhaoui et al. documented the case of a 21-year-old man who experienced a posterolateral MI and was treated with thrombolytic therapy. Angiography revealed an anomalous origin of the Cx, yet no evidence of stenosis was present [17].

Several anatomical factors are associated with a higher risk of ischemia in CCAA. These include a narrow slit-like deformation of the coronary ostium, an acute angle take-off, and kinking of the coronary artery as it exits from the aorta. Additionally, hypoplasia and/or stenosis of an intramural segment and an interarterial course can contribute to the increased risk of ischemia [18].

Moreover, the aberrant course of the artery itself may contribute to the development of atherosclerotic lesions. Yamamoto et al. [19] reported a case involving a 65-year-old gentleman with a history of HTN, DM and smoking, who presented with an infero-posterior STEMI. Emergency coronary angiography revealed an obstruction in the proximal segment of the Cx, which originated from the right coronary sinus and followed a retroaortic course. Pre-PCI intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) demonstrated atherosclerotic changes within the anomalous vessel, including plaque rupture and thrombus formation at the site of the culprit lesion [19].

Some studies suggest that the presence of atherosclerotic disease in an anomalous Cx does not necessarily correlate with disease in other coronary arteries, implying that the anomalous Cx might be more vulnerable to atherosclerosis. West et al. noted that 73% of participants in their study exhibited significant atherosclerosis in the anomalous Cx, compared to only 47% in other major coronary vessels [20]. Conversely, other studies challenge the notion of increased susceptibility to atherosclerosis in the anomalous Cx [21, 22]. For instance, Mohsen et al. found that seven out of 18patients with an anomalous Cx had obstructive atherosclerotic disease in the anomalous artery, but significant disease was also present in the LAD and RCA [8].

Failure to identify an aberrant Cx during cardiac catheterization is a common issue, often arising from misinterpretations or assumptions about occlusion. This can delay timely interventions in cases of ACS or result in a misdiagnosis of MI with normal coronary arteries (MINOCA) [23]. Additionally, undiagnosed anomalies can lead to serious complications during valvular procedures, such as transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) or mitral valve surgery, where accidental damage to the aberrant artery may occur [24]. Thus, recognizing and accurately diagnosing these anomalies beforehand is crucial for ensuring safe and effective procedural planning.

Identifying CCAA using conventional coronary angiography can be particularly challenging, especially in emergencies [11]. Two specific angiographic signs have proven effective in pinpointing an anomalous Cx before selective demonstration. The first is the "aortic root sign", observed in a right anterior oblique view during left ventriculography, where the artery is seen coursing behind the aortic root. The second is the "sign of non-perfused myocardium," indicated by the absence of arterial flow to a significant area of the posterior lateral left ventricle during selective injections of the left main coronary artery (LM) [25].

Additionally, Triantafyllis et.al proposed an angiographic algorithm to identify an aberrant Cx. Their findings suggest that a LM length exceeding 17.7 mm in a cranial view increases the likelihood of an aberrant Cx by 5.3 times. For LM lengths below this threshold, assessing the bifurcation angle in a caudal view is advised; a LM length over 13.9 mm paired with an acute bifurcation angle of 62.9° or more raises suspicion for the anomaly [23]. In our case, the length of the left main coronary artery to the first branch was 17.2 mm in cranial view and 14.6 mm in caudal view, with an acute bifurcation angle measuring 60.9°. These measurements align with the diagnosis of an aberrant Cx. While applying this algorithm could have been advantageous during the intervention, it was not feasible due to the emergency nature of the situation.

Selective engagement of the ostium of an anomalous vessel can be quite challenging. Limited support from the guiding catheter often hinders the ability to cross the culprit lesion with a guidewire or to successfully deliver a balloon and/or stent. In the presented case, engaging the anomalous Cx was particularly difficult and necessitated wiring of the RCA to provide additional support. Factors such as the challenging engagement, the angulated course of the artery, and the potential influence of using a polymeric coronary guidewire likely contributed to the occurrence of coronary dissection. This complication was effectively managed through rewiring and the deployment of three DES. Notably, it has been previously reported that angioplasty of anomalous coronary arteries carries an increased risk of dissection [26].

To address such challenges, several strategies have been proposed to facilitate PCI in cases of CCAA. These include employing extra support guidewires, utilizing the buddy-guidewire technique, selecting larger-diameter guide catheters, deploying anchoring wires or balloons either distal or proximal to the lesion, and using catheter extension devices, such as the "mother-and-child" catheter technique [11, 19, 27]. Furthermore, while CABG is indeed a crucial consideration in managing complex coronary cases, especially CCAA, our case was primarily influenced by the acute nature of the STEMI presentation. The patient’s immediate clinical needs during an ACS were best managed with primary PCI. The other arteries were readily accessible for PCI, and their management yielded a good outcome. CABG was discussed as an option but was not prioritized due to the acute setting of the STEMI and the feasibility of achieving revascularization via PCI.

Equally important, follow-up coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) or invasive angiography should be considered in patients with a stented aberrant Cx, as the artery may be under compression, which increases the risk of long-term stent failure.

Finally, CCAA are rarely seen in regular clinical practice, and the presentation of an anomalous coronary artery during an ACS event is even less common. However, they often pose significant challenges in diagnosis and PCI due to their unique coronary anatomy. While various aspects of the anomalous Cx remain subjects of debate in the literature, awareness of this anomaly is crucial, as a lack of knowledge can result in diagnostic mistakes and may adversely impact treatment outcomes.

In conclusion, this case provides valuable insights into the practical challenges and clinical decision-making processes involved in addressing such unique presentations. We believe it underscores the need for further studies and discussions to refine guideline recommendations and improve strategies for addressing ACS in similar cases.
