Unsupervised clustering based on a graph attention network reveals high-risk subgroups among ICU dementia patients for enhanced mortality prediction
Authors: Chuanxiong Li, Lei Zhang, Xiaoyu Zhang, Tong Sun, Qianwen Ruan, Meihua Qiu, Lei Pan, Binyang Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDementia presents a significant global health burden, with an increasing prevalence and considerable economic implications. In the intensive care unit (ICU), the number of dementia patients has steadily increased, highlighting the need for more accurate risk stratification. Traditional methods of patient classification often assume homogeneity, limiting their ability to identify subgroups at varying levels of mortality risk. This study explored the potential of graph attention networks (GATs) in addressing this gap, aiming to better characterize high-risk dementia subgroups in the ICU and improve clinical outcomes.MethodsThis study utilized anonymized patient data from the publicly available MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III datasets. Dementia patients were identified using ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes, with exclusions based on specific admission criteria, resulting in the inclusion of 7904 patients. A comprehensive set of demographics, comorbidities, ICU severity scores, and laboratory parameters was selected. Three unsupervised clustering methods were employed: a graph autoencoder (GAE), a graph attention autoencoder (GAT), and K-means combined with UMAP-based dimensionality reduction. The optimal number of clusters (k = 3) was determined by maximizing the silhouette scores and Calinski–Harabasz indices. The GAT method quantitatively outperformed both the GAE and K-means methods, achieving superior cluster separation (MIMIC-IV: highest silhouette score: 0.090; Calinski–Harabasz: 345.501; Davies–Bouldin: 2.30; MIMIC-III: highest silhouette score: 0.210; Calinski–Harabasz: 380.620; Davies–Bouldin: 1.850). Three distinct subgroups were identified: Cluster 1 represented the highest-risk phenotype, characterized by advanced age, coagulopathy, anemia, and organ dysfunction, with a strikingly elevated mortality risk (HR = 21.58, p < 0.001). Compared with the unstratified cohort, the machine learning models trained on the MIMIC-IV and validated on the MIMIC-III cohort showed improved predictive performance (AUC up to 0.85 within clusters). SHAP analysis revealed cluster-specific drivers, including a prolonged length of stay, renal impairment, and inflammatory markers. Visualization of the subgroups was achieved via sunburst plots, radar charts, and boxplots.ResultsThis study included 7904 patients, who were divided into two groups: the nonoccurrence group (n = 5,038) and the occurrence group (n = 2,866). Baseline characteristics, including demographic, clinical, and laboratory factors, significantly differed between the groups. Unsupervised clustering methods identified distinct patient subgroups, with the GAT algorithm outperforming K-means and GCN in clustering both cohorts. Cox regression revealed that the GAT identified high-risk groups, with mortality significantly higher in Cluster 1 (HR = 21.58, P < 0.001). Cluster analysis revealed that Cluster 1 had the highest mortality risk and was characterized by advanced age, severe renal dysfunction, and poor clinical outcomes. To validate the superiority of the GAT-based unsupervised clustering approach, we applied various machine learning algorithms, which demonstrated enhanced predictive performance across the identified subgroups. The feature distributions and interrelationships were visualized using radar plots, boxplots, and SHAP analysis. SHAP further revealed distinct risk profiles: Cluster 0 was associated with an increased risk of thrombotic events and acute kidney injury; Cluster 1 reflected a high metabolic stress state indicative of early systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) or sepsis; and Cluster 2 was primarily marked by severe renal failure.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the effectiveness of GAT-based clustering for identifying high-risk subgroups among dementia patients in the ICU. By providing more granular risk stratification, these models can support clinical decision-making and improve patient management. These findings underscore the potential of advanced machine learning techniques in enhancing ICU care for dementia patients, with future research focusing on temporal validation and integration into clinical workflows to optimize outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03336-2.Keywords: Dementia, Intensive care unit, Mortality prediction, Graph neural network (GNN), Graph attention network (GAT), Unsupervised clustering

Introduction

Dementia represents a major global health challenge, with high mortality and substantial variation in patient outcomes [1]. According to the Global Burden of Disease Study, from 1990 to 2016, the number of individuals with Alzheimer’s disease and other dementias increased by 117%, with a higher prevalence in women than in men [2]. Additionally, a study on the economic costs of dementia from 11 European countries revealed that, with an aging population, the health care economic burden of dementia is steadily increasing [3]. This burden not only puts pressure on public finances, but also results in rising economic pressure on individuals. Furthermore, as the aging population increases, the number of elderly patients in intensive care units (ICUs) has steadily increased [4]. A key limitation of existing research lies in its assumption of homogeneity among patients, despite the considerable heterogeneity observed in clinical trajectories, comorbidities, and responses to interventions. Accurate identification of high-mortality-risk dementia populations in the ICU is critically important for improving clinical decision-making, optimizing the allocation of limited medical resources, and enabling timely, personalized interventions that may improve patient outcomes.

Traditional analytical approaches—such as logistic regression, Cox proportional hazards models, or unsupervised clustering based on demographic and clinical variables—often treat the patient population as a uniform group [5–7]. These methods are limited in their ability to identify complex substructures or extreme-risk subgroups within the dementia population. Current studies primarily use unsupervised clustering to distinguish dementia populations from cognitively normal or selected control groups [8, 9]. However, research focusing on stratifying individuals with a major challenge is to move beyond traditional variable-based models and identify hidden subgroups within the dementia population that show distinct mortality risks. By accurately recognizing the highest-risk individuals, it may be possible to construct subgroup-specific prediction models that achieve better performance than models trained on the entire population do. For instance, clinical predictors can be used to construct models with XGBoost to predict dementia risk and stratify dementia patients on the basis of their risk [10]. However, traditional machine learning methods are limited in handling patient heterogeneity, whereas GNNs are more effective at identifying distinct subgroups within the dementia population.

Graph neural networks (GNNs) [11], particularly graph attention networks (GATs) [12], offer a promising avenue for addressing this problem. These models are well suited for representing and analyzing complex relational data, such as patient interaction networks, disease co-occurrence graphs, or drug coprescription networks. By incorporating relational information that traditional models overlook, GNNs can potentially reveal hidden structures associated with mortality risk. In this work, we explored the use of GATs to identify and characterize high-risk subgroups among dementia patients in the ICU, aiming to improve the accuracy and granularity of mortality risk prediction by identifying the patients with critical illnesses.

Methods

Data resource

Data source and ethical considerations: Patient data for this secondary analysis came from the anonymized MIMIC-IV [13] and MIMIC-III [14] datasets. Robust deidentification safeguards were applied to all sensitive identifiers in this publicly available resource. Under applicable research governance policies (specifically recognizing HIPAA-compliant deidentified data use), this research qualified for an exemption from fresh ethics committee review and the need to obtain informed consent. The researcher Binyang Wang, who accessed this database, completed the required ethical training and obtained database certification (ID: 13488206).

Patients selection

We identified dementia patients using ICD-9 and ICD-10 diagnostic codes, considering only the first ICU admission for patients with multiple hospitalizations. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) first admission to both the hospital and ICU; (2) length of stay in the hospital and ICU less than 24 h; (3) age greater than 18 but less than 85 years; and (4) lack of essential demographic information. We extracted data from two datasets, with 4551 (MIMIC-IV) and 3353 (MIMIC-III) patients.

Variable selection

This study incorporated several categories of variables, including demographics (age, race, sex, length of stay in the hospital, and mortality), comorbidities (preexisting medical conditions classified using standard criteria, such as cerebrovascular disease, chronic pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure, diabetes with or without chronic complications, malignant cancers, metastatic solid tumors, severe and mild liver disease, myocardial infarction, paraplegia, peptic ulcer disease, peripheral vascular disease, renal disease, and rheumatic disease), ICU severity indices (validated scores recorded at ICU admission, including the Charlson comorbidity index, Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), Oxford Acute Severity Of Illness Score (OASIS), Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II)), and laboratory parameters (values measured at or close to ICU admission, including coagulation parameters such as international normalized ratio (INR), partial thromboplastin time (PTT), and prothrombin time (PT)), chemistry parameters (such as anion gap, bicarbonate, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), calcium, chloride, creatinine, glucose, potassium, and sodium), and hematology parameters (such as hematocrit, hemoglobin, and mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), platelet count, red blood cell count (RBC), red cell distribution width (RDW), and white blood cell count (WBC)).

Feature selection and engineering

First, the Gini importance of all the features was calculated using the random forest algorithm to evaluate their contribution to the prediction outcome. The variance inflation factor (VIF) was subsequently computed for each feature to assess multicollinearity. The results indicated that the vast majority of the features had VIF values less than 10, suggesting that there were no severe multicollinearity issues in the data. Features with a VIF exceeding 10 were removed after confirmation by clinical experts within the team. The above procedures were applied to both datasets, and the clinical features ultimately selected for unsupervised clustering were those common to both datasets after VIF calculation and removal.

Algorithms and performance evaluation

To comprehensively explore the potential structure of the data and identify different patient subgroups, this study employed three complementary unsupervised clustering analysis methods: topology-preserving clustering based on a graph autoencoder, adaptive clustering based on a graph attention autoencoder, and direct clustering combining classical K-means with nonlinear dimensionality reduction. The specific processes and parameter configurations for each method are as follows.

A comprehensive analysis was performed to identify the optimal cluster solution (k). We computed the silhouette score and Calinski–Harabasz index for k values ranging from 2 to 15 for all the algorithms.

Method 1: topology-preserving clustering based on a graph autoencoder

First, all the raw features were Z score standardized and transformed into a distribution with a mean of zero and a standard deviation of one to eliminate the influence of dimensional differences on the subsequent distance calculations. In the standardized feature space, the k-nearest neighbors algorithm was applied to calculate the Euclidean distances between samples. On the basis of this measure, bidirectional connections were established between each sample and its 10 nearest neighbors, generating a symmetric adjacency matrix for the sample relationship graph, which was then converted into the edge index tensor format required by the graph neural network.

On the basis of the above topology, a graph autoencoder (GAE) model was constructed. The encoder used a two-layer graph convolutional network architecture (GCNConv): the input feature dimension was expanded to twice the target embedding dimension (default is 16 dimensions), and a ReLU activation function was applied to introduce nonlinearity; the feature dimension was then compressed to the target embedding dimension (default is 16 dimensions), and the final low-dimensional node embedding representation was output. The model reconstructed the adjacency matrix via an inner product decoder, with the optimization objective being to minimize the reconstruction loss (typically using cross-entropy or binary cross-entropy). The training process used the Adam optimizer with a learning rate set to 0.001, and the model was trained for 500 iterations.

Node embeddings were extracted, and K-means clustering was performed using the Lloyd algorithm to optimize cluster center positions, assigning cluster labels to all samples. The embeddings learned by the GAE were input into the UMAP algorithm for dimensionality reduction to a two-dimensional space. A custom color map was used to distinguish the clusters, with the cluster centers marked and point differentiation enhanced using edge enhancement techniques, resulting in a publication-quality visual image. This method innovatively integrates modeling of sample graph structures and feature space partitioning, achieving topology-preserving feature decomposition of medical data in an end-to-end manner.

Method 2: adaptive clustering based on a graph attention autoencoder

All the features were Z score standardized, and principal component analysis (PCA) was applied for feature space compression, with a cumulative variance explanation rate set to 95%, significantly reducing the computational complexity of the subsequent steps. In the reduced feature space, the cosine distance (as opposed to the Euclidean distance) was used to optimize the k-nearest neighbors algorithm to construct a sample relationship network. After a sparse adjacency matrix was generated, it was converted into an efficient coordinate-formatted edge index tensor.

The graph attention autoencoder model consists of the following: encoder: a single-layer graph attention mechanism (GATConv) was used, with two attention heads for multiperspective feature aggregation, and an ELU activation function was applied to enhance the model’s nonlinearity. Decoder: the adjacency matrix was reconstructed via the inner product of node embeddings. The model was trained using the Adam optimizer with a learning rate of 0.005 and an early stopping mechanism (patience set to 20 epochs) to improve training efficiency and prevent overfitting.

Method 3: direct clustering combining classical K-means with nonlinear dimensionality reduction

The original dataset was directly loaded, retaining all 39 features as analysis dimensions without feature selection or dimensionality reduction to maximize the retention of original information. All feature columns were Z score standardized to eliminate the influence of dimensionality and provide a foundation for distance calculations.

In the standardized feature space, the classical K-means algorithm was applied to perform clustering. The algorithm iteratively optimized the cluster center positions using the Lloyd algorithm until convergence, ultimately assigning cluster labels to each sample and directly adding them to the original dataset, forming a new dataset with labels. The original 39-dimensional features were directly compressed to a two-dimensional space using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP), and the clustering results were visualized using a custom ternary color map.

The silhouette score (1), Calinski–Harabasz index (2), and Davies–Bouldin index (3) were used for multidimensional evaluation within a preset range of cluster numbers (k = 2–15) to determine the optimal clustering number intrinsic to the data. On the basis of the determined optimal k value, the K-means clustering algorithm was executed to assign cluster labels to the samples, and the aforementioned three evaluation metrics were used to quantify clustering quality. Node embeddings were visualized by reducing them to two dimensions using UMAP, and cluster-level feature heatmaps were generated to reveal key discriminative raw features distinguishing different subgroups.

Training process: For training, we selected the optimizer (Adam), initial learning rate (0.01) with a decay schedule (reduce-on-plateau), batch size (256), training epoch (1000), and early stopping criteria (patience = 20 epochs based on validation loss). We described our strategy and the search ranges for key hyperparameters (e.g., learning rate: from 1e-4 to 1e-2; hidden dimensions: from 32, 64 to 128).

Machine learning algorithms

For machine learning (ML) models, the scikit-learn Python library and XGBoost packages were used to create models and tune the hyperparameters. We used three ML models. We used the MIMIC-IV as the training cohort and the MIMIC-III as the validation cohort. These were used to construct prediction models. The predictive performance of the five candidate models was comprehensively evaluated using ROC curves. Following model selection on the basis of these criteria, we employed SHAP values to provide both local interpretability and global feature importance rankings.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics for cohort baseline characteristics were generated per outcome group. Categorical variables are displayed as counts and percentages. Continuous variables are reported as either means ± SDs (if a normal distribution was confirmed) or medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs). Group differences for continuous metrics were assessed using either parametric tests (ANOVA or t test) or nonparametric alternatives (Kruskal‒Wallis or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests), determined by normality testing. Associations for categorical variables were evaluated via Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests.

Missing data were handled prior to model development. Features with a missing rate greater than 30% were excluded from the subsequent analysis. For features retained in the analysis, missing values were imputed using linear interpolation for sequential measurements. For nontime series or baseline characteristics, missing continuous variables were imputed with the mean, and categorical variables were imputed with the mode.

This study systematically implemented three complementary unsupervised clustering methods to subdivide patient data into subgroups. Survival analysis was then used to assess the differences in clinical prognosis between the subgroups. Multivariate log-rank tests were applied to verify the differences in survival distributions across subgroups, and Kaplan‒Meier curves were plotted to visualize survival probability changes, with log-rank test p values (< 0.001) and sample sizes for each cluster annotated. Cox regression analysis was used to evaluate the risk of mortality for different clusters.

We simultaneously used boxplots to visualize and compare the selected cluster features with those of other clusters. In this process, we employed a feature-type adaptive test to identify significant variables. Numeric features were classified as continuous variables and subjected to ANOVA (α = 0.05), whereas the remaining features were categorized as categorical variables and tested using Chi-square tests (α = 0.05). The top 9 significant features were integrated in ascending order of p value and visualized. All figures are annotated with p values and significance levels (***: P < 0.001, **: P < 0.01, *: P < 0.05).

Results

Baseline information

This study included 7,904 patients, who were divided into two groups on the basis of whether the target outcome occurred (Table 1): the nonoccurrence group (n = 5,038) and the occurrence group (n = 2,866). Comparisons of baseline characteristics revealed significant differences between the two groups in terms of demographic, clinical, and laboratory indicators (P < 0.05). Compared with the nonoccurrence group, the occurrence group was significantly older (69.05 ± 13.40 years vs. 59.69 ± 16.56 years, P < 0.001), with a lower proportion of men (57.29% vs. 60.04%, P = 0.017). In terms of disease severity, the occurrence group had significantly higher SOFA scores (3 [1–5] vs. 2 [1–4]), SAPSII scores (41 [33–51] vs. 32 [24–41]), and OASIS scores (35 [30–41] vs. 32 [27–38]) (P < 0.001), while the Glasgow Coma Scale score was significantly lower (13 [0–15] vs. 14 [0–15], P < 0.001). Coagulation function indicators (INR: 1.20 [1.10–1.50] vs. 1.20 [1.10–1.30]; PT/PTT was significantly prolonged) and inflammatory markers (WBC: 9.8 [7.3–13.6] × 10⁹/L vs. 9.5 [7.2–12.7] × 10⁹/L; P = 0.004) were generally abnormal in the occurrence group. Renal function impairment was more pronounced (creatinine: 0.95 [0.70–1.50] mg/dL vs. 0.85 [0.70–1.15] mg/dL; urea nitrogen: 21 [14–35] mg/dL vs. 15 [11–23] mg/dL; P < 0.001) and was accompanied by metabolic disturbances (anion gap: 13.5 [12.0–16.0] vs. 13.0 [11.5–15.0]; P < 0.001). Additionally, hemoglobin levels (10.23 [9.00–11.85] g/dL vs. 10.95 [9.60–12.70] g/dL, P < 0.001) and urine output (1299 [800–2079] mL vs. 1770 [1141–2610] mL, P < 0.001) were significantly lower in the occurrence group, with an extended hospital stay (10.86 [6.24–19.05] days vs. 9.69 [5.83–16.70] days, P < 0.001).

Baseline information of survival and non-survival patients in MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III cohorts

Different datasets baseline information

On the basis of the baseline data shown in Supplementary Table 2, this study found that in the MIMIC-IV (2974 in the survival group/1577 in the nonsurvival group) and MIMIC-III (2064 in the survival group/1289 in the nonsurvival group) cohorts, the patients in the nonsurvival group were significantly older (median age of MIMIC-IV: 70.0 years vs. 62.0 years; MIMIC-III: 67.9 years vs. 56.3 years, p < 0.001), the length of hospital stay was longer (median MIMIC-IV: 12.1 days vs. 10.5 days; MIMIC-III 9.6 days vs. 8.4 days, p < 0.001), and there were systemic clinical differences between the two groups; moreover, the nonsurvival group had greater coagulation dysfunction (median INR 1.25 vs. 1.20; PTT 31.3 s vs. 29.6 s), poorer organ function (SOFA score 4 points vs. 3 points; SAPSII 42 points vs 34 points), lower blood pressure (average BP 59 mmHg vs. 61 mmHg), and more severe renal function impairment (creatinine 1.0 mg/dL vs. 0.8 mg/dL, urine output reduced by 30%). In addition, significant differences were also observed for electrolytes, blood cell indicators and inflammatory parameters (p < 0.001). It is worth noting that the proportion of white people in the MIMIC-III nonsurvival group was as high as 84.9%, while the racial distribution in the MIMIC-IV cohort was more balanced.

Quantifying model unsupervised clustering performance

We further characterized the effects of the different unsupervised clustering methods by visualizing patients and their representations using uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) (Fig. 1). To visualize the distribution of clusters, we utilized UMAP. The following parameters were used: n_neighbors = 15, min_dist = 0.1, n_components = 2, and metric = ’Euclidean.’

UMAP visualization provided an initial impression of cluster separation: K-means showed ambiguous classification results in both cohorts (Fig. 1A and C), whereas the UMAP of the GCN exhibited improved cluster separation. The UMAP of the GAT suggested the most distinct cluster distribution among the methods. These visual patterns are consistent with, and supported by, the quantitative differences in cluster balance shown in the subsequent analysis of sample distribution.

We used bar charts to quantitatively display the differences in sample distribution across different cohorts under the three unsupervised clustering algorithms. In the MIMIC-IV (Fig. 1B) cohort, both the K-means and GCN algorithms yielded extreme classification results in Cluster 0 (with more than 80% of the samples), whereas the GAT algorithm yielded more balanced classification results across clusters. However, in the other cohort (Fig. 1D), K-means showed a more uniform distribution, whereas the GCN and GAT clustering algorithms exhibited more concentrated distributions. The quantitative assessment of cluster balance provided by this sample distribution analysis substantiates the separation quality observed in the UMAP projections.

Clustering comparison for three algorithms

The choice of k = 3 was confirmed through a data-driven approach, as it simultaneously maximized the silhouette score [15] and Calinski–Harabasz index [16, 17] when a range of cluster solutions were evaluated (k = 2–15; see Supplementary Figure S1).

The clustering performance of all three algorithms (K-means, GCN, and GAT) across the five evaluation metrics is quantitatively compared in Fig. 2. The upper heatmap (Fig. 2A) presents the results from the MIMIC-IV cohort, while the lower heatmap (Fig. 2B) shows the corresponding results from the MIMIC-III cohort.

The unsupervised cluster method scores (silhouette, Davies–Bouldin, Calinski–Harabasz, completeness and homogeneity scores) in K-means, GCN and GAT algorithm in MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III cohorts. A The performance score in MIMIC-IV; B the performance score in MIMIC-III

Across both cohorts, a consistent pattern emerged: the GAT method achieved the highest scores on key internal clustering metrics—silhouette scores, Calinski–Harabasz index, and Davies–Bouldin index—demonstrating its superior ability to form compact and well-separated clusters. Specifically, on the MIMIC-IV, the GAT attained a silhouette score of 0.090, a Calinski–Harabasz index of 345.501, and a Davies–Bouldin index of 2.30. On MIMIC-III, it achieved a silhouette score of 0.210, a Calinski–Harabasz index of 380.620, and a Davies–Bouldin index of 1.850. This clear geometric separation of clusters is central to our unsupervised learning objective, as it establishes a foundational step toward identifying distinct and clinically actionable risk strata.

However, a deliberate trade-off is observed. GAT underperformed on the external validation metrics of completeness and homogeneity, which measure the alignment between the discovered clusters and preexisting clinical labels. We prioritized the internal clustering metrics for method selection because our core study objective was to discover novel, separable patient subgroups on the basis of data-driven geometry rather than to recapitulate existing classifications. The high separation quality achieved by the GAT, as quantitatively demonstrated by its leading scores on the first three metrics, makes it the preferred method for our goal of revealing potentially new and distinct risk strata. The lower scores on the label-based metrics suggest that the clusters identified by the GAT may represent novel phenotypes that do not perfectly align with current clinical conventions, a finding that warrants further investigation into their clinical meaning and utility.

Cox regression and KM curve analysis

As shown in Fig. 3, the analysis using the KM curve revealed that Cluster 2 was clearly defined and associated with high mortality in the MIMIC-IV cohort (log-rank P < 0.005). However, K-means was ineffective in distinguishing a high-mortality subgroup within the MIMIC-III cohort (P = 0.02). The GCN algorithm successfully identified a high-mortality subgroup in both datasets (P < 0.001). Similarly, the GAT algorithm effectively differentiated high-mortality subgroups in both cohorts. Notably, the GAT algorithm achieved better separation of the low-mortality group, particularly visible as a reduced overlap area in the graphical representation. This enhanced separation by GAT was consistent in both MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III.

We also performed Cox regression to compare the mortality risk associated with the classification results of different algorithms in Table 2. In the MIMIC-IV dataset, the K-means algorithm identified two high-risk clusters (Cluster 1, HR = 1.284; Cluster 2, HR = 2.112; both P < 0.001); in the GCN algorithm, Cluster 0 exhibited a sharp increase in mortality risk (HR = 12.620; P < 0.001), and Cluster 1 also showed a significant increase (HR = 1.946; P < 0.001). The GAT algorithm further revealed extreme risk stratification, with Cluster 1 having an HR of 21.58 (P < 0.001), making it the highest-risk group in this dataset. In the MIMIC-III dataset, Cluster 1 in K-means showed a mild but significant increase in risk (HR = 1.209, P = 0.023), whereas Cluster 2 was not statistically significant (P = 0.09). The GCN and GAT algorithms highlighted even more extreme risk heterogeneity—Cluster 2 in GCN had a dramatic increase in risk, reaching an HR of 33.45 (P < 0.001), and Cluster 2 in GAT exhibited an astonishingly high HR index. The GAT algorithm identified a high-risk subgroup. However, an extremely wide confidence interval (134.0–767.7) indicated that the estimation was unstable but still suggested a high-risk subgroup for Cluster 2.

Cluster baseline information

We ultimately chose the GAT to identify three distinct patient subgroups (Cluster 0, n = 3029; Cluster 1, n = 1929; and Cluster 2, n = 2946) through cluster analysis in MIMIC-IV (Table 3). Patients in Cluster 1 were the oldest (median 67.876 years), had the highest proportion of women (41.420%), and had the longest hospital stay (12.633 days). It presented the worst coagulation function (INR 1.30, PT 14.20 s), the lowest average blood pressure (67.185 mmHg), the highest heart rate (102.00 beats per minute), and the highest respiratory rate (26.00 beats per minute). It was accompanied by severe anemia (hemoglobin 10.00 g/dL) and the highest disease score (SOFA score of 3 points and SAPSII of 43 points), and the final mortality rate was as high as 84.863%. In contrast, patients in Cluster 0 were the youngest (57.632 years old), were mainly men (61.836%), had the shortest hospital stay (7.858 days), had the most stable vital signs and laboratory indicators, and had the lowest mortality rate (3.698%). The various indicators of patients in Cluster 2 were between those of the two other clusters, with an age of 65.546 years and a mortality rate of 37.916%. In addition, except for the blood sodium level (P = 0.894), there were statistically significant differences in the remaining 34 indicators (including white blood cell count P = 0.032) among the three groups (P < 0.05).

The output of the model for Cluster 0 (Fig. 6A, D) is primarily driven by features associated with a prolonged hospital stay (length of stay), advanced age (age), and markers of coagulation dysfunction (prolonged PTT) and impaired renal function (decreased urine output). In conclusion, this study highlights the potential of unsupervised clustering methods, especially graph-based models such as GAT, in enhancing patient risk stratification in the ICU. By accurately identifying distinct patient subgroups with varying mortality risks, our approach has the potential to improve clinical decision-making and outcomes. These findings suggest that integrating advanced machine learning techniques into standard ICU practices could lead to more precise prognostication and better targeted treatments for dementia patients in critical condition. Future research should focus on validating these results in larger cohorts and exploring how these methods can be incorporated into real-time clinical workflows to further optimize patient care.

Discussion

The results of this study provide valuable insights into the clinical characteristics and mortality risk of dementia patients in the intensive care unit (ICU). By applying unsupervised clustering techniques, we identified three distinct patient subgroups with markedly different clinical outcomes. Critically, our GAT model revealed a high-risk phenotype (Cluster 1 in MIMIC-IV) characterized by a specific and clinically salient pattern: the most pronounced decrease is characterized by low mean arterial pressure, severe coagulation disorders, anemia and organ failure. This hemodynamic profile is a classic sign of compensatory shock [33], often seen in sepsis or severe hypovolemia, and aligns perfectly with the observed extreme mortality rate of 84.9% in this group.

Baseline information revealed that patients with all-cause mortality were significantly older and had higher disease severity scores (SOFA, SAPSII, and OASIS) and more pronounced abnormalities in key clinical parameters, such as coagulation function, renal function, and inflammatory markers. A study on COVID-19 dementia patients revealed that dementia patients commonly have a higher incidence of renal failure and cerebrovascular disease [18]. In another study on sepsis and dementia, we similarly found that compared with nondementia patients, dementia patients had more severe disease severity scores, higher creatinine levels, and greater levels of coagulation-related markers such as platelets and hemoglobin [19].

The application of unsupervised clustering methods, including K-means, GCN, and GAT, has provided interesting insights into the utility of graph-based models in patient subgroup identification [20–22]. In our study, although K-means clustering showed some success in the MIMIC-III cohort, it struggled to achieve clear classification in the MIMIC-IV cohort. We propose that the superior performance of the GAT model stems from its unique ability to capture complex, nonlinear relationships that reflect underlying pathophysiology. Crucially, the model revealed a high-risk cluster characterized by a synergistic combination of hemodynamic and hematological dysfunction; specifically, a comprehensive decrease in mean arterial pressure was accompanied by a compensatory increase in heart rate, which was concurrent with significant coagulopathy. This pattern is not a mere statistical association but is highly indicative of a specific and life-threatening clinical condition recognized by intensivists: distributive shock [36], most commonly resulting from severe sepsis. The co-occurrence of hypotension [34] and coagulopathy [35] aligns perfectly with the established clinical understanding of sepsis-induced organ dysfunction, where systemic inflammation leads to vasodilation, myocardial depression, and activation of the coagulation cascade.

Previous studies have shown that the GAT is highly effective at subtyping various diseases [23, 24]. In cancer treatment, owing to the heterogeneity of treatment responses, researchers have proposed a GNN-based graph-encoded mixture survival model to distinguish subtypes of advanced non-small cell lung cancer, optimizing treatment choices for patients [25]. In psychiatric disorders, which involve multiple symptoms and treatment responses, GNN classification of patients’ neuroimaging data has enabled more accurate classification of clinical subtypes, leading to improved diagnostic accuracy [26]. Another study, which also utilized the MIMIC-IV and MIMIC-III cohorts, applied a GNN-based network to predict early mortality risk in heart failure patients. Their results also indicated that compared with other models, GNN-based models can better identify patient characteristics and provide superior predictive performance [27]. Owing to the high-dimensional, heterogeneous, and dynamic nature of the data [40], many traditional machine learning methods struggle to accurately capture the complex relationships between features. In contrast, graph neural network (GNN)-based approaches are particularly adept at encoding high-dimensional data into low-dimensional spaces [41] while preserving topology and node properties. By incorporating attention mechanisms, GNNs can more effectively capture the importance of adjacent nodes [42]. Specifically, in the GAT model, the attention mechanism computes a weighted aggregation of features from a patient’s neighbors in the graph. This means that the model does not treat all connections equally; instead, it learns to “pay more attention” to connections where specific comorbidity relationships are highly predictive of the outcome. For instance, when a heart failure management model is constructed, the GAT can analyze the complex interactions between heart failure and factors such as dyslipidemia, blood pressure, and age, thereby quantifying the extent to which these factors affect management outcomes [41]. This dynamic, weighted inference captures the complex interplay of conditions that define a high-risk clinical phenotype, which is a significant advantage over methods that aggregate information uniformly.

In dementia-related research, studies have shown that graph attention networks are more effective at classifying Alzheimer’s disease patients using electronic health records [28]. Our study extends this by not just classifying but also revealing a novel, high-acuity phenotype within the ICU dementia population. We identified a high-risk mortality cluster, where our study revealed that older age, severe anemia, renal insufficiency, and multiorgan failure are associated with extremely high mortality rates in the dementia population. Moreover, SHAP analysis further enhanced our understanding of the key clinical features driving patient prognosis. In Cluster 1, WBC count, glucose level, and mean arterial pressure were the three clinical features with the most substantial influence on mortality. These parameters are readily obtainable at admission, highlighting their potential utility in early risk stratification for adverse outcomes. Furthermore, this particular combination of features—elevated WBC, dysregulated glucose, and low arterial pressure—strongly suggests a state of high metabolic stress and systemic inflammation [37–39]. The hospitalization rate and advanced age were found to be important factors in Cluster 0. A previous study revealed that dementia patients often experience longer durations of hospital stays, which is positively correlated with mortality [29]. Many patient evaluation tools that have been linked to mortality, such as the SAPSII and SOFA scores, have been confirmed in multiple studies [30, 31]. Age and blood-related indicators are significant clinical markers of mortality across different clusters. The MIND-China Study from China revealed that RDW tends to have a J-shaped relationship with Alzheimer’s disease [32].

The identification of these phenotypes, particularly the high-risk shock-like phenotype, has direct clinical implications. First, it enables early risk stratification. Clinicians could use this model upon ICU admission to identify dementia patients at extreme risk who may benefit from more aggressive, personalized monitoring and treatment. Second, our findings generate a novel hypothesis: dysfunction of hemodynamic compensation might be a critical determinant of outcomes in critically ill dementia patients. Future research should focus on validating these phenotypes in external cohorts and determining whether phenotype-targeted interventions can improve survival.

However, this study has several limitations inherent to retrospective analyses of large clinical databases. First, the sensitivity and specificity of dementia diagnostic codes are limited, potentially leading to misclassification bias that may dilute intergroup differences and lead to underestimation of true risk stratification. Future studies incorporating neuroimaging or detailed cognitive assessments could improve cohort accuracy. Second, despite extensive feature engineering, the database may not fully capture all the factors influencing dementia prognosis in the ICU. Third, treatment decisions and care intensity are complex and influenced by factors beyond available clinical variables. Fourth, although the GAT model shows strong discriminatory performance, further temporal and geographic validation is needed to confirm its generalizability and robustness before clinical application. Finally, our focus on all-cause mortality warrants expansion to specific causes of death (e.g., cardiovascular events, infections) and functional/cognitive outcomes post-ICU to better understand the impact of critical illness on dementia patients. Although our model demonstrated strong performance on a temporal validation cohort from the same health care system (MIMIC-IV), true temporal validation across diverse and independent health systems is necessary to fully establish generalizability and remains a critical next step.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the potential of unsupervised clustering methods, especially graph-based models such as GAT, in enhancing patient risk stratification in the ICU. By accurately identifying distinct patient subgroups with varying mortality risks, our approach has the potential to improve clinical decision-making and outcomes. These findings suggest that integrating advanced machine learning techniques into standard ICU practices could lead to more precise prognostication and better targeted treatments for dementia patients in critical condition. Future research should focus on validating these results in larger cohorts and exploring how these methods can be incorporated into real-time clinical workflows to further optimize patient care.
