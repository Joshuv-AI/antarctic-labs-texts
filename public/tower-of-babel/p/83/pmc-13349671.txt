ABSTRACT

Introduction: Biomphalaria straminea (B. straminea) is an intermediate host of Schistosoma mansoni (S. mansoni), and its distribution in Shenzhen threatens public health. This study aimed to investigate the distribution patterns and ecological habitat characteristics of B. straminea to inform public health prevention and control strategies.

Methods: Sampling was conducted across major natural water systems (rivers and reservoirs) and vegetable-planting areas in Shenzhen. Species were identified using morphological and molecular methods. S. mansoni infection and water quality were analyzed to compare infested and snail-free habitats.

Results: No live B. straminea were detected in major rivers or reservoirs. Instead, stable populations were established in agricultural areas. Infested habitats had heavy organic deposition and significantly higher Ca2+, HCO3-, and PO43- levels than snail-free areas (P<0.05). Molecular testing of 64 snail samples confirmed the absence of S. mansoni infection.

Conclusion: This survey revealed an apparent redistribution of B. straminea in Shenzhen, where the snail is apparently absent from urban rivers but has colonized polluted agricultural waters. Although the samples tested negative, dense colonization of this competent host in agricultural landscapes with frequent human-water contact enables potential local transmission if the pathogen is introduced. Future monitoring and control efforts should prioritize agricultural ecosystems.

Schistosoma mansoni (S. mansoni) is a global public health threat, with 54 million annual infections worldwide. Its transmission range is determined by the dispersal of its intermediate host, Biomphalaria straminea (B. straminea)(1–2). In China, while Schistosoma japonicum is under control, increased exchange and trade with schistosomiasis-endemic regions under the Belt and Road Initiative have made imported S. mansoni control increasingly critical (3). Multiple imported cases were reported in China, predominantly among travelers and workers returning from S. mansoni endemic regions (4), indicating a persistent cross-border introduction risk. Systematic surveillance and early warning for imported S. mansoni remain insufficient in China, and control capacity needs further improvement. As a major port city, Shenzhen faces introduction risks from frequent cross-border personnel, trade, and ballast water (5).

Since 1981, B. straminea has spread to Dongguan, Huizhou, and other Pearl River Delta cities (2). Systematic historical surveillance has documented the widespread distribution of the snail in Shenzhen's major urban rivers and reservoir flood channels during 2012–2013, with multiple high-density breeding populations detected in these lotic river habitats (6–7). Previous studies mainly focused on its distribution and dispersal, with limited analysis of its adaptive mechanisms, environmental drivers, and niche dynamics across habitats. In recent years, river restoration and channel-hardening projects in Shenzhen have drastically altered its original habitats. However, its response to these changes and the colonization of new habitats remain unclear. Therefore, this study aimed to assess these ecological changes to inform public health strategies.

This study was conducted from August to December 2023 in Shenzhen, a coastal city in southern China. Shenzhen has a subtropical monsoon climate (annual mean temperature about 23 °C, abundant rainfall) and extensive river networks. Surveys covered diverse freshwater habitats in Shenzhen, including major rivers, reservoirs, and agricultural sites. Sampling points were established at the upper, middle, and lower reaches of waterways to ensure geographical representation.

Targeting stagnant waters and aquatic vegetation, we searched within 20–50 m of each site. Snails were collected using dip nets and hand-picking and then transported to the laboratory in ventilated containers with moist gauze. For molecular analysis, 20 mg of soft tissue was dissected and preserved in absolute ethanol at −80 °C. Surface water samples were collected from 12 sites using portable samplers for water quality analysis (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://weekly.chinacdc.cn/), with the sampling basis following the Technical Specifications for Environmental Quality Monitoring of Surface Water (HJ 91.2-2022). Statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.1, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Differences between snail-infested and snail-free habitats were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. The significance level was set at α=0.05, and P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

All the collected specimens were black morphotypes, measuring 4–12 mm in diameter and 1–3 mm in height. The shells were dextral and discoid, with four rapidly expanding whorls and a typical horseshoe-shaped aperture. The flattened dorsal, deeply concave apex, and prominent umbilicus matched the taxonomic description of B. straminea.

Field surveys showed significant differences in B. straminea distribution across Shenzhen’s main water systems. No live B. straminea were found in Shenzhen's main rivers or the Shenzhen Reservoir flood channel, with only a few empty shells discovered at some sites. In contrast, the snail was prolifically breeding in the vegetable-planting areas of Longgang and Pingshan, forming stable communities (Table 1).

The environmental characteristics differed significantly between snail-infested and snail-free habitats (Table 2). In snail-free rivers and reservoirs, water was odorless, clear, or slightly turbid, with minimal humus and debris. However, snail-present vegetable areas had stagnant, shallow, turbid, and malodorous water, with heavy humus deposits and dense vegetation covering the water surface.

Water quality analysis showed that snail-infested waters had high hardness and eutrophication (Table 3). Specifically, Ca2+, Mg2+, HCO3-, and PO43- concentrations were significantly higher in snail-infested areas (P<0.05). Mean Ca2+ reached 132.23 mg/L in snail-infested areas, compared with 31.38 mg/L in snail-free areas. Similarly, HCO3- and PO43- differed markedly, with snail-infested values being significantly higher. Moreover, PO43- in snail-infested areas severely exceeded the surface water standard (total phosphorus ≤0.1 mg/L), indicating heavy organic pollution. In contrast, pH, Na+, Cl-, DO, and NH3−N showed no significant differences (P>0.05). These results confirm that B. straminea prefers calcium-rich, eutrophic, and polluted stagnant waters. All the 64 samples tested negative for S. mansoni by PCR. Molecular evidence confirmed that none of the B. straminea samples harbored S. mansoni.

DISCUSSION

Historical surveys consistently recorded widespread B. straminea populations in Shenzhen’s urban rivers and reservoir flood channels (6–7). In contrast, our survey found no live snails in these habitats. Compared with historical data, this cross-sectional survey suggests an indicative spatial distribution shift of B. straminea in Shenzhen. Their apparent absence from urban rivers aligns with riverbed hardening and restoration that eliminated the organic-rich substrates essential for their survival (8). In addition, we observed that B. straminea has invaded agricultural irrigation systems with stagnant, calcium-rich water. This environment favors shell calcification and population establishment (9) and increases the likelihood of schistosomes being close to agricultural areas with frequent human activities.

Although this study adopted a cross-sectional design and lacked data from neighboring cities, it demonstrated that environmental changes have reshaped the distribution of B. straminea. Notably, as a key international hub, Shenzhen has frequent cross-border exchanges with S. mansoni endemic areas (10), which may elevate the probability of pathogen introduction. Although no infected snails were found and no epidemiological linkage exists between imported cases and local agricultural settings, if S. mansoni is introduced into these agricultural landscapes, the co-occurrence of the pathogen, a competent intermediate host, and human water contact could form a biologically plausible transmission cycle. The current situation of "snails without schistosomes" provides a valuable opportunity for disrupting potential transmission chains. Prevention and control efforts should focus on vector control, pathogen surveillance, and protection of high-risk populations.

Implications for public health arising from the apparent redistribution of B. straminea into agricultural ecosystems necessitate a strategic adjustment of schistosomiasis control in Shenzhen. Prevention efforts should focus on the irrigation networks; for example, dredging sediment and controlling Ca2+ and PO43- levels to reduce eutrophication can disrupt snail breeding habitats and reduce reliance on chemical molluscicides. Routine malacological surveys should be linked with active stool screening for high-risk groups, including agricultural workers and laborers returning from endemic areas, to enable timely response through the existing reporting mechanisms. In addition, health education targeting farming communities and a joint prevention framework involving Shenzhen, Dongguan, and Huizhou are essential for bridging surveillance gaps and reducing the risk of a localized S. mansoni outbreak across shared watersheds.
