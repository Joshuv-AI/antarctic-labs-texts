Optimizing pediatric ITP therapy: a real-world study on plasma concentration-guided eltrombopag dosing
Authors: Jia Li, Bo Liu, Nan Wang, Shuyue Dong, Jingyao Ma, Xiaoling Wang, Peng Guo, Runhui Wu, Xiaoling Cheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEltrombopag (ELT) is a second-line therapy for pediatric immune thrombocytopenia (ITP), but inter-individual variability in metabolism leads to wide plasma concentration differences at the same dose, affecting efficacy and adverse drug reactions (ADRs). To validate the feasibility of individualized drug regimens based on blood drug concentration guidance in pediatric persistent/chronic ITP (P/CITP) and to provide a new method of individualized treatment with eltrombopag for pediatric P/CITP. This prospective, observational cohort study enrolled 70 children with refractory persistent/chronic ITP (P/CITP) to evaluate the feasibility and value of plasma concentration–guided individualized dosing. Patients were assigned to a conventional group (dose titration by platelet counts and bleeding events) or an individualized group (dose adjustments additionally guided by ELT concentrations), with 6-month follow-up. Although no statistical significance was observed, the individualized group achieved a higher overall response rate (82.9% vs. 71.4%) and complete response rate (65.7% vs. 48.6%), with more stable platelet counts, fewer bleeding events, reduced requirements for concomitant/rescue therapy, and a lower incidence of ADRs (17.1% vs. 37.1%). Similarly, though not statistically significant, the individualized group had lower direct medical costs and improved cost-effectiveness. These findings suggest that plasma concentration-guided individualized ELT therapy may enhance therapeutic efficacy, improve safety profiles, and reduce medical costs, which represents a meaningful step toward precision medicine in the field of pediatric hematology.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00277-026-06875-8.Keywords: Eltrombopag, Immune thrombocytopenia, Individualized treatment, Pediatrics

Introduction

Immune thrombocytopenia (ITP) is an acquired autoimmune disease manifesting as decreased platelet counts and hemorrhagic manifestations [1]. While many pediatric cases resolve spontaneously, approximately one-third progress to persistent/chronic ITP (P/CITP), requiring prolonged pharmacologic management to maintain hemostasis and quality of life [1].

Current therapeutic options for pediatric ITP include first-line intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIG) and glucocorticoids, as well as second-line agents like Thrombopoietin receptor agonists (TPO-RAs) and splenectomy (reserved for refractory cases) (Table 1). IVIG enables rapid platelet elevation but is costly with transient efficacy and infusion-related reactions; long-term glucocorticoid use carries risks of growth retardation and osteoporosis in children. Rituximab, a monoclonal antibody targeting CD20, may induce durable remission but is associated with infusion reactions and long-term immunodeficiency risks. And splenectomy is limited by lifelong infection risks. TPO-RAs have emerged as the best treatment to elevate and maintain the PLT count for children with P/CITP. Romiplostim (subcutaneous TPO-RA) requires parenteral administration, which may compromise adherence [2, 3].

Eltrombopag (ELT), an oral thrombopoietin receptor agonist (TPO-RA), stimulates megakaryocyte proliferation and platelet production via the JAK2/STAT5 signaling pathway [4]. The efficacy and safety of treating ITP with ELT has also been proven in international multicentre clinical studies and post-marketing studies in children [5–10]. It is endorsed as a second-line treatment in pediatric ITP guidelines [11, 12]. However, pediatric use is complicated by age-related variability in drug absorption, metabolism, and clearance, resulting in heterogeneous plasma concentrations at equivalent doses. Both under-and overexposure can compromise therapeutic outcomes or increase the likelihood of ADRs.

Therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) offers a strategy for individualized dose optimization based on measured plasma concentrations. In our previous study, it was confirmed that blood concentration-based guidance of ELT dose adjustment in children with ITP increased the response rate from 53.8% (21/39) to 82.1% (32/39) [13], but there is a lack of direct cohort comparative studies of the individualized application approach versus the conventional treatment approach.

To this end, we conducted a prospective, observational, real-world cohort study to compare the clinical efficacy, safety, and economic benefits of an individualized dosing approach based on blood concentration guidance with those of a traditional treatment approach that clinically adjusts the dosing regimen based on platelet counts alone, and to provide a reference basis for individualized dosing guidance for clinical ELT in children with ITP.

Though prior studies suggest potential benefits [14], robust comparative data on conventional versus TDM-guided dosing remain scarce, particularly in pediatric populations. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical benefits of plasma-guided individualized ELT dosing in comparison to conventional platelet-based dose titration in children with P/CITP.

Methods

Ethical approval

This study was performed in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Approval was granted by the local Ethics Committee of Beijing Children’s Hospital (approval number: [2024]-Y-098-D) and was registered for clinical trials (registration number: ChiCTR2400086099). Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians included in the study.

Study patients

This study is a prospective, observational cohort investigation designed to evaluate and compare the clinical effectiveness, safety profile, and cost-effectiveness of an individualized ELT dosing strategy guided by plasma-concentration monitoring, versus standard dosing practices, in the management of pediatric ITP.

Inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of P/CITP per Chinese pediatric ITP guidelines [15]; (2) age < 18 years; (3) fixed-dose ELT treatment ≥ 2 weeks before plasma trough concentration testing at our institution.

Exclusion criteria: (1) suspected or confirmed other causes of thrombocytopenia; (2) history or suspicion of arterial/venous thrombosis or thrombophilia; (3) baseline hepatic/renal dysfunction (ALT, AST, ALP, etc. >1.5×upper limit of normal [ULN] ; total bilirubin, blood creatinine, etc. >1.2× ULN); (4) prior TPO-RA or rhTPO use within 30 days before enrollment.

Treatment and follow-up

All patients were given oral eltrombopag tablets, 25 mg per tablet. The initial dosage of ELT was 1.5 mg/kg per day for children aged 5 years or younger, 37.5 mg per day for children older than 5 years and with a weight < 27 kg, 50 mg per day for children older than 5 years and with a weight ≥ 27 kg. [16]

In the conventional group, dosing was adjusted according to platelet response and bleeding events, with 20% increments or reductions based on the initial dose and a maximum of 75 mg/day. In the individualized group, adjustments also incorporated drug concentration monitoring in addition to conventional criteria (Table 1). ELT was discontinued if platelet counts exceeded 400 × 109/L, with blood counts monitored every 3–5 days, and could be restarted if < 150 × 109/L. Each adjustment was maintained for at least 2 weeks to evaluate platelet response.

For pediatric patients in the conventional group initiating treatment or undergoing dose adjustment, blood counts were monitored weekly. In the individualized group, once a response or complete response was achieved, monitoring could be reduced to one monthly. ELT concentrations were measured biweekly during the initiation phase or dose adjustment period, with sampling conducted at a fixed daily dose and time; measurements were repeated two weeks following any dose escalation or the occurrence of ADR. Blood count monitoring followed the same schedule as in the conventional group. Liver function tests were performed two weeks after the first dose and after any dose escalation.

Measurement of eltrombopag plasma concentrations

ELT concentrations were measured after at least 2 weeks of stable dosing. On sampling days, patients had blood drawn at 7 a.m. before medication under researcher supervision to minimize timing variability. Forearm venous blood was collected into K3-EDTA tubes, processed immediately, and prepared by protein precipitation. Eltrombopag-13C4 served as the internal standard, and calibration curves were established with the reference standard. Concentrations were determined using high-performance liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (AB Sciex Triple Quad 4500 MD) with sodium hexanesulfonate-Sinopharm and acetonitrile-TEDIA as mobile phases under gradient elution. Stock solution (1000 µg/mL) was prepared by dissolving 10.204 mg eltrombopag in DMSO and stored at-80℃. The assay demonstrated good linearity (0.1–25 µg/mL), a quantification limit of 0.1 µg/mL, intra-day and inter-day precision < 10%, recovery 90–100%, and matrix effect 85–110% [17]. Data were analyzed with Analyst® 1.6.3 software.

Data collection and Evaluation of outcomes

Data collection comprised baseline and follow-up information. Baseline data included age, sex, weight, time of diagnosis, time of first ELT administration, platelet count, thrombopoietin and megakaryocyte levels, ICR bleeding score, prior ITP therapies, concomitant medications, and dosage. Follow-up lasted six months and covered three domains: effectiveness (platelet counts, ICR bleeding scores, ELT dose adjustments), safety (liver function tests, adverse events), and economy (direct medical costs).

Statistical analysis

Our research uses R Studio and SPSS 24.0 for data analysis. Figures were drawn by GraphPad Prism 9.0. For data description, Mean and Standard Deviations (SD), or Median and Interquartile Range (IQR) were used for continuous variables; counts (percentages) were used for categorical variables. Group differences were analyzed by independent two-tailed t-test or Mann–Whitney U test for two continuous variables, one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis H-test for multiple continuous variables, and the chi-squared test for categorical variables. Statistically significant was defined as p-value < 0.05.

Results

Baseline characteristics

The patients were enrolled from October 2023 to June 2024, and the principle of informed and voluntary enrollment was applied to the children who met the criteria for the study’s nativity in the specialized outpatient clinic of the Department of Hematology, Beijing Children Hospital. For the children who were willing to participate in the study, a tripartite decision was made by the doctor, the pharmacist, the children, and their families for the enrollment group, and the individualized treatment group was matched with the conventional treatment group for the enrollment. A total of 72 pediatric patients were enrolled in the study, with 36 allocated to each group. The patients were observed over a period extending from October 2023 to December 2024. One child in each of the individualized treatment groups and the conventional treatment group withdrew from the study due to the inability to undergo the frequent visits, with a loss-of-attendance rate of approximately 2.8% (1/36). Eventually, 35 children were included in each of the two groups, totaling 70 children, and all patients completed a six-month follow-up. The enrollment flowchart and the baseline information of the patients are shown in Fig. 1; Table 2. There was no statistical difference in baseline information between the individualized and conventional treatment groups.

Effectiveness

The individualized medication strategy for pediatric ITP was guided by the reference range of ELT serum trough concentration (1.299–3.539 mg/L) established in our team’s prior exploratory study [19]. For the individualized treatment group in this research, the measured serum trough concentration of ELT was 2.33 ± 0.23 mg/L, which fell within the pre-determined reference interval and verified the rationality of the personalized dosing regimen.

The Overall response (OR) and Complete response (CR) rates were slightly higher in the individualized treatment group than in the conventional treatment group. The OR rates were 82.9% (29/35) in the individualized treatment group and 71.4% (25/35) in the conventional treatment group, p = 0.255. In the individualized treatment group, 65.7% (23/35) of children achieved CR and 17.1% (6/35) achieved Partial response (PR). In the conventional treatment group, 48.6% (17/35) of children achieved CR and 22.9% (8/35) achieved PR, p = 0.344.

The baseline median platelet counts did not differ significantly between groups. During follow-up, median counts in the individualized treatment group at weeks 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 were 127.00, 119.00, 112.00, 114.50, 126.50, and 129.00 × 109/L, respectively, compared with 87.00, 93.00, 103.50, 103.00, 108.00, and 115.50 × 109/L in the conventional treatment group. A significant difference was observed at week 4 (p = 0.040), whereas differences at later time points (weeks 8–24) were not significant. Changes in platelet counts are shown in Fig. 2.

The proportion of children who developed a PLT < 20 × 109/L was 22.9% (8/35) and 37.1% (13/35) in the individualized and conventional treatment groups, respectively, p = 0.192. The proportion of patients with PLT > 400 × 109/L was 17.1% (6/35) and 14.3% (5/35) in the two groups, respectively, p = 0.743.

At baseline, the incidence of bleeding events was 17.1% (6/35) and 14.3% (5/35) in the individualized and conventional treatment groups, respectively, p = 0.743. The two groups reported two and three Grade 1 bleeding events and four and two Grade 2 bleeding events. During the six-month follow-up period, bleeding events were reported in a total of 17.1% (6/35) of patients in the individualized treatment group and 25.7% (9/35) in the conventional treatment group, p = 0.328. No serious bleeding events of Grade 3 or higher were reported in either group. The occurrence of bleeding events during the follow-up period is shown in Fig. 3.

Safety

The occurrence of adverse events (AEs) during the follow-up period is detailed in Table 3. During the follow-up period, ELT was generally well tolerated, with an incidence of AEs of 17.1% (6/35) and 37.1% (13/35) in the individualized versus conventional treatment groups, respectively, p = 0.060. In the individualized treatment group, severe thrombocythemia (PLT > 900 × 109/L) [20] occurred in 8.6% (3/35) of children, upper respiratory tract infection in 5.7% (2/35), and diarrhea in 2.9% (1/35). In the conventional treatment group, severe thrombocythemia occurred in 11.4% (4/35) of the children, upper respiratory tract infection in 14.3% (5/35), diarrhea in 5.7% (2/35), elevated ALT in 2.9% (1/35), and elevated TBIL in 2.9% (1/35).

All AEs were CTCAE Grade 1 or 2 and resolved or disappeared after ELT discontinuation or dose reduction. No serious AEs such as thromboembolism, myelofibrosis, or cataract were observed during follow-up.

Economic Evaluation

This study mainly compares the total expenses incurred by patients during the follow-up period. The total direct medical costs during the follow-up period were 27,855.00 (22,880.50, 40,518.60) yuan and 29,936.00 (16,882.05, 46,579.58) yuan for the individualized treatment group and the conventional treatment group, respectively, with p = 0.834. Although there was no significant difference in total treatment costs between the two groups during the follow-up period, the individualized treatment group had lower overall costs than the conventional treatment group.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the clinical value of an individualized dosing study based on plasma concentration-guided ELT for the treatment of children with P/CITP by conducting a prospective, observational, real-world cohort study. The results of the study suggest that the effectiveness of ELT therapy can be improved, AEs can be reduced, and it is more cost-effective through plasma concentration monitoring.

The individualized group showed higher response and CR rates than the conventional group. In the conventional group, the ELT response rate was 71.4% (25/35), consistent with the previously reported 69.2%–76.7% efficacy range in Chinese children with P/CITP [16, 21, 22]. With plasma concentration guidance, the individualized group achieved an 82.9% (29/35) response rate and a CR rate of 65.7% (23/35), compared with 48.6% (17/35) in the conventional group. After accounting for treatment changes, the overall response rate rose to 91.4% (32/35) in both groups, while the CR rate remained higher in the individualized group (74.3% [26/35], 60.6% [20/35]).

Platelet counts during the follow-up period were higher in the individualized treatment group than in the conventional treatment group. Although there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups in baseline PLT, the baseline PLT in the conventional treatment group was slightly higher than that in the individualized treatment group. At week 4, the PLT of the individualized treatment group was statistically higher than that of the conventional treatment group, and this difference was maintained until the end of the follow-up. There were fewer PLT extremes (PLT < 20 × 109/L or PLT > 400 × 109/L) in the individualized treatment group during the follow-up period, and blood concentration monitoring allowed for a more stable PLT.

The individualized group showed fewer bleeding events, concomitant therapies, and salvage treatments than the conventional group. No Grade ≥ 3 bleeding occurred in either group, and bleeding episodes decreased over time, with more rapid and sustained control in the individualized group. Concomitant therapy use declined in both groups, but in the individualized group it fell from 28.6% (10/35) at baseline to 2.9% (1/35) by week 16 and remained stable through follow-up, demonstrating superiority over the conventional group. These findings suggest better platelet control in the individualized group.

Dose adjustments were more frequent, numerous, and of greater magnitude in the individualized group, while fewer patients required switching to other therapies due to ELT inefficacy compared with the conventional group. Reported initial response rates of ELT in pediatric P/CITP range from 69.2% to 76.7% [16, 21, 22], but decline with prolonged concomitant therapy, with sustained response rates of 36%–56.3% [17,18]. Among children treated for over three years, 24.5%–29.2% tapered ELT after achieving stable platelet counts, and 17.5%–24.2% maintained response 12 months post-discontinuation [22, 23]. Accordingly, the latest Chinese expert consensus recommends individualized maintenance therapy with TPO-RAs. In this study, 71.4% (25/35) of children in the individualized group underwent dose reduction, averaging 1.92 ± 1.04 per patient, significantly more than in the conventional group. These findings indicate that plasma concentration–guided dosing can optimize ELT reduction and discontinuation while maintaining favorable response rates.

In terms of safety, no serious AEs of CTCAE Grade 3 or above occurred in both groups, and all AEs were resolved by drug reduction or discontinuation. The number of patients with AEs in the individualized treatment group was lower than that in the conventional treatment group, and the number of drug-dose-related AEs (PLT elevation, liver function abnormalities, etc.) was lower than that in the conventional treatment group. The results suggest that the individualized guidance method based on blood concentration can prevent AEs associated with ELT therapy to a certain extent and improve the safety of drug therapy. Compared with the 2023 Study [24] (56 patients) and 2020 Study [25] (71 patients), our study (35 patients) is a prospective cohort study and has a superior safety profile, with no reports of severe adverse events such as venous thrombosis, oral aphthous ulcers, or allergic urticaria—events that occurred at 1%-2% in the other two studies. Our study only documented three mild, low-incidence adverse events (thrombocytosis 8.6%, diarrhea 2.9%, upper respiratory tract infection 5.7%), with thrombocytosis showing consistent low rates (6%-8.6%) across all three studies, while common events like headache (7%-11% in other studies) were not reported (Table 4). However, the short follow-up period of our study may lead to missed delayed adverse events or underreported reactions, and the small sample size limits the generalizability of its safety conclusions.

In terms of economy, the economy of the individualized treatment group was superior to that of the conventional treatment group. The individualized treatment group was superior to the conventional treatment group in terms of clinical efficacy and AE control, and the total direct medical cost during the follow-up period was lower than that of the conventional treatment group, mainly because the overall platelet maintenance of the children in the individualized treatment group was good, and the proportion of patients with a drug reduction period was high. So, individualized treatment is the preferred solution.

Early use and long-term maintenance of TPO-RAs benefit ITP patients by reducing bleeding events, hospitalizations, and treatment costs, and by improving health-related quality of life (HRQOL) [26–28]. Current economic evaluations mainly compare costs and outcomes of different TPO-RA formulations (ELT versus romiplostim), with no pharmacoeconomic analyses of individualized therapy or pediatric ITP. As the first such evaluation in children, our study shows that plasma concentration–guided individualized therapy can improve ELT efficiency, lower adverse events to some extent, and does not increase direct healthcare costs, supporting its clinical application and promotion.

However, the limitations of this study include a small sample size, a short follow-up period, and the lack of relevant evaluations of HRQOL for patients and their parents. Future studies with extended 12–24-month follow-up, expanded sample sizes and collected evaluations of HRQOL, are therefore imperative to validate the individualized dosing regimen’s long-term efficacy, safety and economic evaluation for pediatric ITP clinical practice.

Conclusion

This study compared plasma concentration–guided individualized treatment with conventional therapy in terms of effectiveness, safety, and economy. Results showed that individualized treatment achieved higher OR and CR rates, more stable platelet counts, fewer bleeding events, concomitant and salvage treatments, and a lower incidence of AEs. In addition, it proved more economical than conventional treatment. Although individualized ELT treatment was superior in many parameters, no statistical difference was found.

In conclusion, the study of individualized application of ELT in pediatric P/CITP patients based on plasma concentration guidance has good clinical value.
