Substandard and falsified medicine recalls in the legitimate supply chain: a systematic review of evidence
Authors: Izabella Fryze, Bernard D Naughton
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo compare international substandard and falsified (SF) medicine recall trends from published research papers based on governmental databases, summarise the extent of the problem in the legitimate supply chain, and identify ways to manage the issue.DesignSystematic review of published academic evidence.Data sourcesDrug recall data in published literature, obtained from official international government regulator databases in the USA, the UK, Canada, Sri Lanka, Zambia, Portugal, Nepal, Saudi Arabia, Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Cuba, Colombia, Mexico, Bolivia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Panama, Peru and Venezuela.Eligibility criteriaA search for literature published between 2010 and 2024 was conducted using PubMed, MEDLINE and Embase. Included studies examined recall data of substandard and/or falsified medicines obtained through official government regulator websites.Data extraction and synthesisData were extracted using Excel files and synthesised using a thematic analysis approach.Results13 research papers containing original data were included. Recall data were obtained from official regulatory databases in 23 different countries. Substandard medicines had significantly higher recall rates than falsified products, while parenteral drugs and tablets were the most recalled formulation types. The leading reasons for defective medicines were contamination, out-of-specification results, stability and packaging issues. India was identified as a common source of SF medicines in Zambia, Sri Lanka, Brazil and Nepal. Frequent recalls of anti-infective drugs were observed in countries with equatorial, tropical and subtropical climates, while high-income countries like Canada, Saudi Arabia and the UK faced issues with defective antihypertensive drugs. Interestingly, medicines affected by nitrosamines’ contamination were recalled in all regions examined in 2018, but in other recall cases, there were disparities among recall action.ConclusionsThere appeared to be similar international recall practices for some products like nitrosamines and not for others like rosiglitazone across the same time frame, which raises questions concerning international drug safety disparities. The requirement to align and globally strengthen regulatory frameworks was of emerging importance. Cooperation between regulatory authorities to create a harmonised approach to reporting medicine recalls and standardising the data included in a recall notification is proposed to facilitate a more accurate comparison of international trends surrounding recalled SF medicines.

Introduction

Substandard medicines are described as poor-quality products which do not meet their specifications and/or standards. Meanwhile, falsified medicines are deliberately imitating the identity, contents and source of a legitimate product.1 According to the WHO, 1 in 10 medicines in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are substandard or falsified (SF).2 However, high-income countries are also at risk. SF medicines often contain incorrect amounts of the active ingredient and can be contaminated with impurities, and in some cases, the active ingredient stated on the packaging is absent.3 Administration of these medicines can lead to fatal consequences with regulators playing a key role in prevention.2

In recent years, falsified medicines have grown in popularity among criminals in high-income countries due to increasing demand. In June 2024, the WHO issued a medical product alert for falsified Ozempic.4 This example saw the product enter both middle-income and high-income countries through legitimate supply chains. Ozempic contains the active ingredient semaglutide which is used to treat type 2 diabetes but has recently been in short supply due to the off-label use for weight loss. This shortage of an expensive product was seen as an opportunity for criminals to profit from falsified versions.4

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) reported a trend of fraudulent ‘lifestyle’ medicines commonly circulating in wealthier countries. This contrasts with lower-income countries which face a problem with falsified medicines for life-threatening conditions like HIV and malaria.1 Regulatory authorities have developed stricter laws and regulations to tackle this problem; an example is the Falsified Medicines Directive (2011/62/EU) (FMD) which was fully implemented in the European Union (EU) in February 2019.57 The focus of medicine regulators is to safeguard public health, and through new regulations, inspections and initiating product recalls, they are a key stakeholder in the fight to prevent substandard medicines from reaching the patients.

The recent case of contaminated paediatric cough syrups sold over the counter in Gambia, Uzbekistan and Indonesia illustrates the seriousness of the issue surrounding the presence of substandard medicines.8 The syrups contained toxic levels of diethylene glycol and ethylene glycol which resulted in over 300 deaths of young children.8 For transparency and public awareness, most countries publish recall data on government websites, which usually includes information about why the recall occurred, name of affected product and the batch numbers.9

The growing presence of SF medicines is a global problem which places many lives at risk daily.2 Several studies have assessed the prevalence of SF medicines in LMICs by combining medicine quality sampling study results, but a study comparing SF medicines across multiple countries based on the legitimate supply chain and regulator data remains unexplored.10 11 Almuzaini et al conducted one of the first reviews of recall data from regulator databases in the UK to determine common reasons for recalls in high-income countries.12 Researchers in other regions have done the same,91316 which has provided an opportunity to amalgamate data and develop an international picture of medicine quality in the legitimate supply chain.

The aim of this study is to explore medicine recalls recorded in pharmaceutical regulatory databases from existing research to better understand international medicine quality trends. We also ask how do recalled SF medicines in one legitimate supply chain compare to trends in other countries, and what are the cross-border disparities.

Methods

The study protocol was published on PROSPERO (CRD42024552059). A PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) flow diagram was developed to illustrate the search and review process.

Eligibility criteria, data sources and search strategy

Three databases were used to perform a literature search for this systematic review: Embase, PubMed and MEDLINE. Search terms related to “substandard”, “falsified”, “recall”, “medicine” and “prevalence” were used. The terminology and search results can be found in Table 1 of online supplemental 1. Search results were filtered to include articles published between 2010 and 2024.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Screening of papers followed the studies’ inclusion criteria. Studies were considered eligible if they were available in English and discussed recalls surrounding SF medicines, and recall data were obtained from official regulatory websites. Articles which were not in English, focused on sampling studies, did not discuss recalls and did not obtain data from official government websites were excluded. Only studies with full text available were included. This systematic review does not focus on falsified and/or substandard medicines obtained through illegitimate websites.

Screening and selection process

Results from each research database were exported and uploaded to Covidence to conduct title and abstract screening, followed by full-text review by both authors. Both steps were performed independently by two reviewers, and duplicates were automatically removed. Abstracts were screened against pre-determined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Studies which obtained two ‘yes’ votes were transferred for full-text screening. Reviews which received two ‘no’ votes were excluded, with those which received one ‘maybe’ vote being discussed to resolve conflict.

Data extraction and analysis

Data extracted from full-text studies included the following: general information about the publication, source of data, geographic study location, applicable regulatory authority, time period covered, type of medicine recall (substandard/falsified), number of recalls in study period, year with most recalls, urgent recall classification, reasons for recall, therapeutic class, formulation type and country of medicine origin. The extracted data for each country were then compared with determine trends. Direct quantitative comparisons were not possible in all cases due to varying classifications by different regulatory authorities and missing data in some papers. From available data, patterns were identified and discussed under separate headings in the results section. PowerPoint was used to prepare graphs for data visualisation.

Assessment of bias

A risk of bias assessment of included studies was performed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). The methodological quality of each paper was reviewed on an individual basis, based on the type of research conducted. Using MMAT allows for critical appraisal of five different research categories, consisting of five questions each. The five categories are as follows: qualitative, randomised controlled, quantitative non-randomised, quantitative descriptive and mixed methods.

Patient and public involvement

None

Results

The initial database search yielded 3367 studies for screening, of which 1382 were removed as duplicates. Individual searches of the three databases produced the following results: Embase (n=1823), PubMed (n=810) and MEDLINE (n=734). The total number of records sourced from databases which proceeded to screening was 1985 after duplicate removal. The abstract and title of all 1985 papers were screened, leading to exclusion of 1972 articles based on the study inclusion and exclusion criteria. 13 articles underwent full-text screening, and 7 articles were excluded after full-text screening as they did not analyse a recall database (n=6) or the full text was unavailable (n=1). Six articles were extracted for review. An additional nine articles were identified via other methods, such as websites (n=1) and included study bibliography searching (n=8). All nine reports were screened, with two papers excluded as both studies did not analyse a recall database. Therefore, seven were included via other methods which were added to the original six articles. A total of 13 articles were included in this study and are presented in figure 1. A full list of included studies can be found in Table 5 of online supplemental 1.

PRISMA 2020 flow diagram of included studies.

Assessment of bias

13 papers were assessed for risk of bias using the MMAT. Three out of five categories were applicable to the included papers: qualitative (n=1), quantitative descriptive (n=11) and mixed method (n=1) studies. For a study to be included, a score of >3 must be achieved. Overall, no studies achieved a score of <3, meaning all 13 papers were included. Scores ranged from 4 to 5 and results are illustrated in Table 2 of online supplemental 1.

Geographical distribution

23 countries across five continents were discussed in the 13 included papers. The regions included were North America (n=9), South America (n=8), Africa (n=1), Europe (n=2) and Asia (n=3). These regions included the USA, the UK, Canada, Sri Lanka, Zambia, Portugal, Nepal, Saudi Arabia, Argentina, Brazil, Chile, Cuba, Colombia, Mexico, Bolivia, Costa Rica, Ecuador, El Salvador, Guatemala, Honduras, Panama, Peru and Venezuela. Two of the identified papers covered recall data published by the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in the USA. Similarly, three different articles discussed recall data published by ANVISA (National Health Surveillance Agency) in Brazil, and two papers discussed recalls in the UK published by the Medicines and Healthcare Products Regulatory Agency (MHRA). Other regulatory authorities are only discussed in individual studies.

Falsified product recalls

The included articles discussed recall data between 2000 and 2021. Each paper covered recalls during different time periods, for example, AlQuadeib et al discuss recalls in Saudi Arabia between 2010 and 2019, while Chabalenge et al examine recalls in Zambia between 2018 and 2021.13 14 Across the 13 studies, only 7 cover recalls which included both substandard and falsified medicines as seen in figure 2. Nepal experienced the highest number of falsified medicine recalls of the seven studies, with 11% of recalled medicines between 2010 and 2020 being falsified.15 In the UK, 5% of recalls between 2012 and 2020 included falsified medicines as reported by Naughton and Akgul.9 Almuzaini et al reported a similar figure of 4% in the UK between 2001 and 2011.9 12 The number of substandard medicine recalls was significantly higher in all seven papers when compared with recalls of falsified medicines.

Substandard versus falsified medicine recalls in seven studies.

The remaining papers discuss substandard medicines only. There were common learnings in most articles which allowed for comparison of trends, such as commonly recalled formulations, therapeutic classes, defect types and countries of origin. The class of recall or alert and year with most recalls were also identified in some articles (online supplemental 2).

Formulation types

Oral dosage forms and parenteral preparations were commonly reported across the different countries. More specifically, tablet forms accounted for most recall data. Solutions and topical preparations were also among commonly recalled formulations for many countries. In Saudi Arabia, Canada, Portugal and Nepal, tablets accounted for the highest number of recalled medicines, while Zambia reported that oral solid dosage forms accounted for 53% of recalls between 2018 and 2021. In the USA, tablet forms were associated with a higher rate of a formulation-based recall. Parenteral formulations were frequently withdrawn in Canada, Portugal, Sri Lanka, Brazil and the UK. Recall data from two different time periods in Brazil were discussed and discovered the percentage of tablet recalls remained similar between 2007 and 2018. The number of recalled parenteral products was higher in Brazil between 2007 and 2017, than during the period of 2010–2018. Therapeutic classification

Recalls of medicines for cardiovascular health such as antihypertensives and anti-infective drugs like antibiotics, antivirals and antimicrobials were regularly observed in the included articles. Anti-infective drugs were commonly reported in Nepal, Saudi Arabia, Zambia, Sri Lanka, the UK, Canada and Latin America. Cardiovascular medicines were frequently recalled in Canada, the UK, Zambia and Saudi Arabia. Interestingly, antiseptic and disinfectant products were the most recalled product type in Zambia.

Defect type

Contamination issues and packaging defects were the most common reasons for product recalls. Other frequent issues include delivery and stability defects, as well as out-of-specification (OOS) results for physical characteristics, laboratory assays and drug contents. Contamination issues were common in articles discussing recalls in Brazil, Canada, the UK, the USA, Sri Lanka, Zambia, Saudi Arabia and Nepal. Recalls due to packaging problems were commonly reported in Nepal, the UK, the USA, Sri Lanka, Portugal, Canada and Latin America.

The three most frequently reported defect categories across the included studies are illustrated in figure 4 and these include contamination, packaging and OOS. These categories were further subcategorised to reflect the specific issue which led to a recall. Other problems such as delivery defects leading to product leakage, faulty device, damage and/or loose seal were one of the leading issues faced by the UK in both papers reporting the period of 2001–2020. Stability problems were common in Sri Lanka, Brazil and Canada. Country of medicine origin

Some articles reported the country of origin for recalled medicines. India was mentioned in 5 of 13 papers and was determined to be the leading source for recalled products in Sri Lanka, Zambia and Nepal.14 15 18 India was also noted as a common supplier of substandard and/or falsified medicines in Brazil and Saudi Arabia.13 19 Approximately 70% of recalled substandard medicines in Sri Lanka originated from India, while only 11.8% were sourced from local companies.18 In Zambia, 38.6% of medicines were manufactured in India and 25.3% locally.14 Between 2007 and 2017, 76.1% of recalled medicines in Brazil were of local origin with 14.7% arriving from India.19

Discussion

Overview

This study demonstrated that tablet and parenteral formulations were the most frequently recalled dosage forms. Contamination, OOS, stability and packaging issues emerged as the most common defect types and substandard medicine recalls were more frequent than falsified medicine recalls. Additionally, some articles discussed product origin which identified India as a common source of substandard products in Sri Lanka, Nepal, Brazil and Zambia.

Formulation types: tablets and parenterals

It is concerning to discover that poor-quality parenteral drugs are commonly reported. Parenteral medicines do not pass the gastrointestinal tract in the same way oral solid medicines would. Therefore, any deviation from specified quality parameters can create a risk to patient health.20

In 2023, tablets were reported as the dominant contributor to the pharmaceutical market, making up 27% of the total market share.21 It would be expected that tablets would be the leading recalled formulation type internationally as they are the most common dosage form, but in the UK, Brazil and Sri Lanka, parenteral drugs were more frequently SF. As tablets are cost effective and easy to manufacture and administer and they are prescribed for various indications, they have high production volumes. In contrast, parenteral formulations are more complex to produce due to contamination and instability risks, which may explain increased recall rates.22

The economic and environmental climate in Sri Lanka may also play a factor in this finding. According to the World Bank Group, Sri Lanka is classified as a lower-middle income country which supplies medicines free of cost to the public sector.23 This cost-efficient programme may result in financial pressure on both the healthcare system and manufacturers, resulting in insufficient resources and poorer-quality medicines. In addition, parenteral drugs are often in liquid form which creates an attractive environment for microbial growth and stability problems to occur. Contamination accounted for 36.2% of substandard medicine recalls in Sri Lanka, followed by 25.2% due to stability defects.18 The climate in Sri Lanka may also cause challenges upon storage as it is one of the hottest countries in the world, with temperatures averaging at 27–28°C.24 Unfortunately, this can result in stability issues if appropriate storage and distribution controls are not implemented and followed. As parenteral medicines are often temperature sensitive, they are susceptible to degradation in hot and humid climates. Brazil also reported a significant amount of parenteral drug recalls, accounting for 52% of defective medicines between 2007 and 2017.19 Similarly to Sri Lanka, Brazil’s equatorial, tropical and subtropical climates create the perfect environment for degradation and contamination of parenteral products, which may be responsible for these elevated recall rates.25

In contrast, the UK is classed as a high-income country with many available resources within the pharmaceutical industry and milder environmental conditions yet faces a similar problem with parenteral medicines. Despite this, contamination was also the leading cause of recalls in the UK which highlights the global scale of this issue and calls for stricter measures to ensure drug quality. The UK may be able to detect more quality issues than Sri Lanka, owing to a more developed regulatory framework and industry resources. It is difficult to accurately compare the reasons for recalls in these countries due to different economic, environmental and regulatory contexts. However, it is expected that the high number of parenteral drug recalls is more likely due to the criticality of their route of administration, as even minor defects could cause severe health consequences. This criticality may be leading to companies and regulators being more likely to initiate a recall due to the gravity of the public health risk to health from substandard parenteral medicines.

Recall and Indian medicine imports

Medicine importation appeared as an important issue among the papers examined. Sharma et al reported that approximately 70% of recalled medicines in Sri Lanka were imported from India.18 Similarly, Nepal, Brazil, Zambia and Saudi Arabia noted high numbers of recalled drugs originating from India. This influx of defective medicines could be due to climate, location, border control issues and the receiving countries’ economic status. Sri Lanka, Zambia and Nepal are classed by the World Bank as lower-middle income countries, while Brazil is recognised as an upper-middle income country.26 Based on these economies, it may be attractive to source cheaper medicines to improve affordability and availability to patients. India is a major supplier of low-cost generic medicines to international markets but faces challenges with meeting consistent standards of quality. Imported products must be stored and transported under strictly controlled temperature conditions to prevent degradation from India’s hot climate. As 25.2% of recalls in Sri Lanka resulted from stability issues, the potential could also be poor distribution practices.18 India accounted for 48% of the total active pharmaceutical ingredient drug master files globally in 2021.27 These high production volumes likely correlate with more substandard medicines being manufactured, with India facing continuous criticism over poor-quality drugs.28 29 The Central Drugs Standard Control Organisation is India’s regulatory body, which has been known for its inconsistent operations and recent reports of bribery.30 The high volume of production, uneven enforcement, poor resources and regulatory gaps paint a picture of a context which may encourage the manufacture and entry of substandard or falsified products into legitimate supply chains. India has a history of distributing falsified medicines with many seizures of illegitimate products like antibiotics being continuously reported by the media and the government.31 32 Regulatory frameworks in LMICs may not be as developed as in Europe or the USA, with a limited capacity to inspect and monitor imported drugs. Nepal shares a border with India; therefore, distribution of such products is made more convenient by the availability of several transportation options and the need to cross a single border. The island of Sri Lanka is also conveniently located close to India. Interestingly, Saudi Arabia which is a high-income country also reported some defective medicines arriving from India. Saudi Arabia and India have historically acted as trading partners with the relationship continuously growing; therefore, the import of medicines may also be due to bilateral trade relations.33

Recalls due to contamination

A very common finding was the high number of recalled medicines due to contamination. Naughton et al discuss data obtained from the MHRA website, where an urgent recall of valsartan was reported in 2018 due to potential contamination with N-nitroso-dimethylamine which can cause genotoxicity and has carcinogenic potential.9 Valsartan is used to treat high blood pressure and due to prescribing demand, production volumes of antihypertensive medicines are high.34 Based on this, the 2018 recall of valsartan products affected many countries around the world.35 Saudi Arabia, the USA and Zambia all successfully recorded this recall on their official regulator websites in 2018, proving consistency of reported data across three different continents.13 14 16 Contamination is a common issue across all 5 continents discussed in this review, irrespective of the country’s economic classification. The ability to identify and recall contaminated products highlights the importance of compliance with current good manufacturing practice guidelines, adequate quality control measures and the need for stricter adherence by companies worldwide.

Packaging and labelling recalls

Packaging and labelling issues have led to many recalls throughout the included studies, particularly in the UK between 2001 and 2011 where Almuzaini et al reported 25% of recalls resulting from packaging defects.12 In 2008, Actavis Inc. recalled 14 lots of fentanyl patches due to a packaging issue which exposed users to the strong and addictive opioid. The patient and others handling the product were in danger as contact with the drug could lead to overdose and fatal consequences.36 Upon assessment of defective medicines in Portugal between 2000 and 2015, Machado Reis et al discovered that primary packaging defects were the second most common reason for substandard product recalls, with secondary packaging defects in third place.37 Although secondary packaging does not directly contact the drug, it plays an important role as it provides additional protection and contains labels and inserts with valuable product information. In the study of substandard medicines in Brazil by Fedalto et al, damaged blisters, missing contents, printing errors, lack of product information and other packaging problems made up over half of reported defects between 2007 and 2017.19 For packaging recalls to be reduced, it could be beneficial for manufacturers to introduce more advanced technologies for packaging line checks. Regulators have published guidance documents to encourage companies to implement modern techniques such as process analytical technologies which can enhance quality assurance by reducing human error and increasing efficiency.38 For example, vision-based or thermal systems have been developed for blister and seal integrity testing. These novel methods may be beneficial to prevent future packaging withdrawals as seen with fentanyl patches in 2008.36 39

Most recalled class: anti-infective and antihypertensive medicines

The most recalled class of drugs identified in this study was anti-infective and antihypertensive products. Anti-infective medicines were the most frequently recalled therapeutic class in Latin America, Sri Lanka and Nepal. Zambia also struggled with poor-quality anti-infective medicines between 2018 and 2021, as antibiotics accounted for 16.9% of recalls. Due to climate and status of socioeconomic development, some diseases and therefore treatments may be more common in these countries when compared with Europe, the USA and Canada. Hot and humid weather conditions create ideal circumstances for climate-sensitive infections such as vector-borne diseases to survive and spread.40 Countries with a poorer socioeconomic status also face several challenges such as higher risk of disease due to insufficient healthcare resources.41 In 2023, the United Nations published a list of 45 least developed countries, which included Zambia and Nepal.42 Socioeconomic struggles of certain Latin American countries and Sri Lanka have also been recognised in recent reports, putting them in a vulnerable position against fighting infectious diseases.43 44 These factors naturally encourage a higher occurrence of substandard and falsified anti-infective medicines in continents like South America, Africa and Asia. For example, dengue fever is a viral illness which spreads through mosquitos in tropical and subtropical climates like South America, Sri Lanka and Nepal, leading to high demand for antiviral medicines.45 Other common viral diseases in these regions like malaria and yellow fever may also increase demand for these drugs. Substandard and falsified antibiotics are a consistent issue in developing countries, often due to the prevalence of diseases like typhoid fever and cholera which can spread due to inadequate water sanitation systems. This is rarely an issue in developed regions such as Europe where access to clean water systems is widespread.46 Also, Zambia saw most recalls resulting from antiseptic and disinfectant products attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020.14 The demand for hand sanitisers grew rapidly which opened a window for SF products to enter the Zambian market, with many items containing significantly lower amounts of alcohol than advertised and harmful impurities such as methanol, benzene and acetaldehyde.14 Administration of contaminated, sub-potent or super-potent products can contribute to microbial resistance and lead to treatment failure and serious adverse reactions.

Antihypertensive medicines were a commonly reported therapeutic class in Canada, Saudi Arabia, Zambia and the UK. In high-income countries like Canada, the UK and Saudi Arabia, the demand for antihypertensive medication would be higher than anti-infectives due to lower exposure to tropical and subtropical diseases. Hypertension is prevalent in these regions, creating a target population for falsified and legitimate products to treat this condition. It is estimated that 1.28 billion adults between the ages of 30 and 79 around the world suffer from hypertension.47 Based on this high demand, many companies manufacture large volumes of antihypertensive medicines, which also increases the chance of quality defects. Zambia reported a parallel issue with antibiotics and antihypertensive medicines, as each therapeutic class accounted for 16.9% of recalls during the studied period.14 In recent years, LMICs have experienced an increased prevalence of hypertension, creating an additional burden to an already existing struggle with infectious diseases.48

Substandard and falsified medicine recall rates

Most countries experienced growth in the number of recalls throughout the period covered. The Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA) reported only 9 drug recalls in 2010, which increased to 52 in 2018.13 A similar situation occurred in Nepal, where the number of recalls rose from 1 in 2010 to 123 in 2018.15 Certain countries experienced significantly higher rates of recalls than others. For example, only 72 recalls were reported in Nepal between 2010 and 2020, while the USA experienced 10 676 drug recalls during the period of 2012–2019.15 16 The difference between the numbers of recalls in these countries is substantial and is likely to be due to the population sizes and therefore product demand. Nepal is less populated with approximately 30 million occupants in 2022, in comparison to 338 million in the USA during the same year.49 50 Certain countries may also experience higher recall rates due to more stringent regulatory frameworks and tighter controls on medicine quality.

It is difficult to determine if the reason for the continuous growth in medicine recalls is due to an increase in substandard and falsified medicines on the market, or if the ability of regulators to detect these products has improved throughout the years leading to higher detection rates. Regulatory authorities in countries like Saudi Arabia and Nepal may not have been as developed as the FDA and MHRA in earlier years, which would explain their trends. For example, the SFDA was established in 2003 in comparison to the US FDA which formed in 1906.51 52 This naturally provides regulators like the FDA with more extensive experience. Brazil’s regulatory agency is known as an established body, which corresponds to the findings of Oliveira et al who reported a consistent number of recalls between 2010 and 2018.17 Similarly, the Portuguese National Health Authority experienced a steady rate of recalls between 2005 and 2015.37

The importance of context for longitudinal recall studies

Assessing international longitudinal data concerning medicine quality or recalls raises some interesting challenges. There is a risk that researchers may not be comparing like with like when combining and comparing data, a challenge which has been discussed in the academic literature previously.10 53 54 Although the value of the data outweighs the perceived limitations of the approach and in the absence of perfect data, we still need this information from medicine sampling and medicine recall studies to support policy decisions. Academics and Non Governmental Organisations (NGOs) have produced longitudinal systematic reviews based on medicine sampling and medicine recall studies.11 55 We suggest that future studies like these should record local social, criminal, environmental, political, economic and regulatory maturity factors which are unique to the region where data are collected to support the comparison of data from similar contexts, building on the McManus and Naughton framework.10 In the absence of a more refined data collection approach, longitudinal data presented in this and other articles are still the best available evidence to support regional policy decisions.

Recall consistency and inconsistency

Differences in recall protocols could also be a contributor to the number of recalls in each country. Although the withdrawal of valsartan was successfully conducted in Zambia, the USA and Saudi Arabia, inconsistencies in recall procedures still exist as each country has its own regulatory agency and therefore unique protocols. The varying recall approaches can lead to defective drugs being removed from the market in one country and not another.56 In 2010, rosiglitazone used to treat type 2 diabetes was removed from the EU due to concerns regarding cardiovascular safety, while the USA implemented prescribing and dispensing restrictions.56 57 Further comparison of recall procedures could provide valuable insight concerning the number of recalls in each country and may explain why higher recall rates occur and how they might correlate with different withdrawal protocols.

Limitations and future work

Naughton and Akgul acknowledge the limitations to determining the exact prevalence of SF medicines due to missing information on recall databases, such as batch sizes and the specific number of medicine doses recalled.9 Implementing a standardised approach to reporting medicine recalls could be considered to accurately estimate the true extent of this issue. More rigorous methodologies to reporting recalls would be beneficial to manage the misalignments between regulatory approaches in different countries. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development launched the global recalls portal in 2012 to create a single space for governments to publish information surrounding recalls, which includes healthcare products.58 Currently, only 47 countries participate in this programme, and involvement of more countries could make this system more comprehensive. An international standardised template for all regulators to record medicine recalls would be beneficial, creating more consistent and comparable data. This solution may take several years to navigate and the cooperation between governments would be required, but it would allow researchers and policy makers to truly understand the complexities and context of international SF medicine rates. Another limitation of the work would be that the study was carried out by two researchers, IF and BN. A third researcher would have added additional rigour to the study but was outside of the financial scope of the study.

Call to action

As mentioned in previous studies, this is a valuable but heterogenous dataset and we call on researchers in future studies to record economic, social and environmental contextual factors when gathering recall data. We call on regulators to share not only the type and batch number of medicines which are being recalled but also the batch size to allow stakeholders to fully appreciate the volume of medicines affected by the recall. This would provide transparency and help stakeholders to understand the true volume of SF medicines in circulation.

Regulatory authorities in LMICs could consider following the EMA’s footsteps by implementing regulations like the Falsified Medicines Directive to protect the public. However, this approach may be difficult to implement as LMICs face additional challenges concerning modern technology and internet.59 If a harmonised approach to reporting medicine recalls globally became available, an opportunity for more research would emerge to identify and compare the prevalence of substandard and falsified medicines internationally.

Conclusion

Substandard and falsified medicines remain a challenge worldwide, regardless of location and economic status. This systematic review identified common trends such as the predominance of recalls for tablets and parenteral formulations, due to common defect types like contamination, OOS results, stability and packaging issues. Improvements to supply chain management and good manufacturing practices are required to mitigate these risks. Substandard medicine recalls are more frequent in comparison to falsified products, which demonstrates compliance and quality control issues as an area of concern. India was identified as a common source of defective medicines in Zambia, Nepal, Brazil and Sri Lanka, highlighting the need for stricter monitoring of supply chains and better regulatory harmonisation. Strengthening cooperation between regulatory authorities could reduce the risks associated with substandard and falsified medicines. This could be supported by creating a global system for international regulators to upload recall information in a standardised manner to make data comparison more accurate and valuable. In the meantime, more research is encouraged to analyse recent recall data to better understand trends in LMICs.
