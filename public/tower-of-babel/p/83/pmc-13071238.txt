Real‐world predictors of progression to type 2 diabetes among adults with prediabetes
Authors: Tyler Dunn, Riddhi Doshi, Victoria Divino, Justin Chen, Michael Radin, Dajun Tian, Lin Xie, Carolina Solis‐Herrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsAssess predictors of progression to type 2 diabetes (T2D) among patients with prediabetes.Materials and methodsThis case–control study assessed adults with prediabetes using US claims and EMR‐linked data from January 1, 2016, through January 31, 2024. T2D cases were patients with prediabetes who progressed to T2D (index: date of first T2D diagnosis); controls had prediabetes but did not progress to T2D. Control index dates were randomly assigned from cases. Demographic and clinical characteristics in the year before progression to T2D were assessed to identify progression predictors.ResultsThe study included 39 281 T2D cases and 58 751 controls. Data on race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, Asian‐Other) were available for 62% of the study sample. Demographic characteristics for cases and controls were similar in the overall population, by sex, and by race/ethnicity. During the 1‐year baseline, T2D cases had a higher prevalence of obesity, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) than controls. Comorbidity patterns varied by sex and race/ethnicity. Compared with patients aged 18–34 years, patients aged 35–44 (OR, 1.37), 45–54 (OR, 1.49), and 55–64 (OR, 1.33) had higher odds of progression. Obesity (OR, 1.45), CVD (OR, 1.64), OSA (OR, 1.36), metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH; OR, 1.37), and neuropathy (OR, 1.19) were associated with T2D progression. Black (OR, 1.27) and Hispanic (OR, 1.21) patients had higher odds of progression than White patients (all p < 0.0001).ConclusionsAmong patients with prediabetes, age 35–64 years, obesity, CVD, OSA, MASH, and neuropathy in the year before progression are associated with progression to T2D.Plain Language SummaryWhat is the context and purpose of this research study? This research study looked at why some adults with prediabetes were more likely than others to develop type 2 diabetes (T2D). The researchers wanted to know if factors like age, sex, race, history of health conditions (such as obesity, heart disease, and sleep disorders), and abnormal lab tests (like elevated blood sugar levels) could predict who will get T2D. What was done? The researchers conducted a study using a US database of insurance and medical records from 2016 to 2024. The researchers compared two groups: 39 281 adults with prediabetes who developed T2D and 58 751 adults with prediabetes who did not develop T2D. The researchers looked for patterns in these groups and used statistics to identify risk factors. What were the main results? The study found that middle‐aged adults (35–64 years old) and those with health problems like obesity, heart disease, sleep apnea, neuropathy, and liver disease were more likely to get T2D. These patterns were similar across patients of different races and genders. However, Black and Hispanic patients had a higher risk of T2D compared to White patients. There was no difference in the risk of developing T2D between patients with Asian or Other ethnicities and White patients. What is the originality and relevance of this study? This study is important because it identifies factors that increase the risk of developing T2D in people with prediabetes. It suggests that age, certain health conditions, and race can affect this risk. These findings can help doctors create better treatment and prevention plans for high‐risk patients.

INTRODUCTION

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) is a public health problem in the United States (US).1, 2 In 2021, 14.7% of the adult population had T2D, 3 and the prevalence is expected to increase during the next two decades. 4 Further, it is estimated that more than one in three adults and one in five adolescents have prediabetes, 5 which increases the risk of T2D. Each year, 5%–10% of people with prediabetes develop T2D, and approximately 70% of adults with prediabetes will develop T2D in their lifetime.6, 7 Prediabetes not only increases the risk of progression to T2D; it is also linked to other health conditions, such as obesity, liver disease, peripheral neuropathy, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), and eye disease, as well as early mortality.8, 9, 10

Given the burden of diabetes on population and individual health, research has been conducted to better characterise contributing factors to progression from prediabetes to T2D. A study of 77 000 adults with prediabetes in the United States found that the risk of progression to T2D over 2 years increased with higher baseline glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) and body mass index (BMI). 11 A study of 26 007 adults with prediabetes in Denmark found that 11.8% progressed to T2D over 2.7 years. Higher HbA1c and BMI, as well as treatment with antihypertensive medication and previous pancreatic disease, were associated with increased risk of progression. 12 In China, a study of 12 009 older adults with prediabetes found that the average annual progression rate to diabetes was 14.2%. In that population, predictors of progression included fasting plasma glucose (FPG), triglycerides, waist circumference, total cholesterol, BMI, and alanine transaminase. 13

While the prevalence of prediabetes is increasing across the population, progression to T2D is not equally distributed among demographic subpopulations. Studies show the incidence of progression varies on a range of factors, including age, sex, ethnicity, and socioeconomic status. For example, the aforementioned US study revealed that younger age and female sex were associated with a greater risk of progression, 11 and other research suggests specific age‐ and sex‐linked health conditions, such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), increase the risk of progression among women. 14 Furthermore, even after accounting for age and sex, the risk of T2D differs by socioeconomic characteristics (e.g., education) and race/ethnicity.2, 12, 15

Prevention research, such as the Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP), demonstrates that progression to T2D can be prevented or delayed with diet modifications, physical activity, and other lifestyle changes. However, because lifestyle changes may not be sufficient for some patients, pharmacologic treatment with agents such as biguanides (e.g., metformin), thiazolidinediones (e.g., pioglitazone), or glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1 RAs; e.g., semaglutide, tirzepatide) may also be used to prevent progression.16, 17, 18, 19 Given patient variation in time to progression and response to treatment, there is a need to better understand real‐world, patient‐level factors that influence progression to T2D, especially during the year before progression. Most prior research on patient‐level factors and progression to T2D has focused on limited sets of factors (e.g., selected demographic, clinical, or laboratory factors but not broad groups of these), and the literature also points to a need for studies of predictors of progression to T2D in population subgroups. 20 Therefore, we conducted a real‐world, case–control study to identify predictors of progression to T2D among patients with prediabetes. We evaluated a broad range of demographic and clinical predictors of progression to T2D in the year before progression and compared patients who did or did not progress in the overall population, among women, among patients with obesity, among patients with laboratory data, and by race/ethnicity.

METHODS

This case–control study included patients with prediabetes and compared predictors of progression to T2D between patients with an incident T2D diagnosis (T2D cases) and patients without a T2D diagnosis (controls) (Figure S1, Supporting Information). Predictors were assessed in the 1 year before progression to T2D for cases and in the same 1‐year comparable period for controls who did not progress to T2D. The study used the IQVIA PharMetrics® Plus adjudicated claims database linked to the IQVIA Ambulatory Electronic Medical Records (AEMR) from January 1, 2016, through January 31, 2024. A subset of T2D cases and controls was also linked to the IQVIA Consumer Attributes (Cx) database, which provided patient race and ethnicity data.

Patients with linkage between PharMetrics Plus and AEMR during the study period were eligible for inclusion. T2D cases were patients with prediabetes with incident T2D, defined by either ≥1 confirmatory medical claim with an International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) code for T2D in the PharMetrics Plus database or laboratory result in AEMR indicating T2D (HbA1c ≥6.5%, FPG ≥126 mg/dL, or 2‐h plasma glucose [PG] ≥200 mg/dL) from January 1, 2017, through January 31, 2024. Controls were patients with prediabetes without either an ICD‐10‐CM code for T2D in PharMetrics Plus or laboratory results in AEMR indicating a T2D diagnosis during the study period. The index date for T2D cases was the date of first T2D diagnosis. Each control patient was assigned a proxy index date drawn from the case group's index dates. This method ensured that the yearly distribution of control patients closely matched that of the cases for each index year. Additionally, T2D cases and controls must have had continuous enrolment in medical and pharmacy benefits in PharMetrics Plus 1 year before the index date (1‐year baseline period), age ≥18 years at the index date, and prediabetes in the 1‐year baseline period (defined based on any of the following four criteria: ICD‐10‐CM diagnosis code for prediabetes, FPG value of ≥100 to <126 mg/dL, 2‐h PG during 75‐g oral glucose tolerance test value of ≥140 to <200 mg/dL, or HbA1c ≥5.7% to <6.5%). 21 The final sample of T2D cases and controls included identification of subgroups by sex and race/ethnicity. See Figure S2 for additional details on inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Patient demographic characteristics, assessed at the index date, included age, sex, geographic region, payer type (commercial, Medicaid, Medicare Advantage, self‐insured, other), type of health insurance plan (consumer‐directed health plan; health maintenance organisation; indemnity, point‐of‐service, preferred provider organisation, other), and race/ethnicity. See Table S1 for brief descriptions of payer and health insurance plan types. Race/ethnic groups included White, Black, Hispanic, and Asian‐Other. Patients with Asian or Other race/ethnicity were aggregated due to small sample sizes (Figure S2). A binary variable denoting the index month before or after March 2020 was recorded because the study period overlapped with the COVID‐19 pandemic. Clinical characteristics, assessed over the 1‐year baseline period prior to the index date, included the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), comorbidities of interest (cerebrovascular disease, CKD [excluding end‐stage kidney disease], chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, chronic pain/fibromyalgia, liver/gallbladder disease, myocardial infarction, neoplasms, neuropathy, obesity, osteoarthritis [OA], overweight, rheumatologic disease, sleep disorders, smoking, and thyroid disease), obesity‐related complications (anxiety, asthma, atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [ASCVD], CVD, depression, dyslipidaemia, gastroesophageal reflux disease [GERD], hypertension, musculoskeletal pain, metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis [MASH]/metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease [MAFLD], obstructive sleep apnea [OSA], knee or hip OA, urinary incontinence [UI; among females only]), and use of antidiabetic medications (ADM; metformin, any other oral ADM, and any GLP‐1 RA indicated for T2D or obesity).

Laboratory values and BMI weight class from the AEMR database were assessed during the 1‐year baseline for the value nearest 180 days before the index date. Laboratory values included HbA1c, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol, total cholesterol, and triglycerides. BMI (kg/m2) was categorised as underweight (<18.5), normal weight (18.5 to <25.0), overweight (≥25.0 to <30.0), obesity class I (≥30.0 to <35.0), obesity class II (≥35.0 to <40.0), or obesity class III (≥40.0).

Descriptive statistics were used to analyse baseline characteristics for T2D cases and controls overall and among subgroups by sex and race/ethnicity. Differences in baseline characteristics that were ≥5% between T2D cases and controls were considered notable. Multivariable logistic regression was used to assess baseline characteristics as predictors of progression to T2D. Five logistic regression models were estimated, including one for the overall study population and four for subpopulations derived from the main study population, based on data availability and interest: women, patients with obesity, patients with race/ethnicity data available in Cx, and patients with laboratory data in AEMR (HbA1c, BMI, SBP, HDL, total cholesterol, triglycerides; DBP data were available in AEMR but not included in the regression model due to collinearity with SBP). Covariates included in the logistic regression models were selected based on clinical relevance to T2D, descriptive findings, and collinearity assessment. Correlations among demographic and clinical variables were assessed during model development. In cases of high collinearity (phi coefficient >0.80), a decision was made to retain one variable and not the other. For example, payer type and plan type were highly collinear; payer type was retained in the model while plan type was excluded. Adjusted odds ratios (OR) for each predictor in the logistic regression models were reported with corresponding 95% confidence intervals. An OR >1 indicates the predictor is associated with increased odds of progression to T2D, whereas an OR <1 indicates the predictor is associated with lower odds of progression to T2D, with an OR equal to 1 indicating no association. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). This study was exempt from institutional review board approval.

RESULTS

This study included 98 032 adults with prediabetes, including 39 281 who progressed to T2D (T2D cases) and 58 751 who did not progress to T2D (controls) (Table 1). T2D cases and controls were similar in the distribution of most demographic characteristics at baseline, including mean age (55.8 vs. 56.8 years, respectively) and the percentage who were female (53.1% vs. 50.8%, respectively). There were, however, notable differences (≥5%) between T2D cases and controls in the proportion of patients located in the US South and those with self‐insurance or a preferred provider organisation (PPO) health plan.

Baseline demographic characteristics between T2D cases and controls remained generally similar in subgroup analyses by sex and race/ethnicity. There were a few notable differences (≥5%). Among men (n = 47 316), a lower proportion of T2D cases had commercial health insurance. Among women (n = 50 716), a higher proportion of T2D cases were aged 45–54 years or had a PPO health plan (Table 1).

In each race/ethnicity subgroup, T2D cases and controls were similar in age and sex distributions. However, there were a few notable differences (≥5%). Among White patients, a higher proportion of T2D cases had a PPO health plan (70.8% vs. 65.5%). Among Black patients, a higher proportion of T2D cases were aged 45–54 years (30.9% vs. 25.2%) and had a PPO health plan (65.9% vs. 59.4%). Among Hispanic patients, a higher proportion of T2D cases lived in the Midwest (18.1% vs. 9.9%) and self‐insured (24.6% vs. 17.6%), but a lower proportion had commercial insurance (68.8% vs. 75.7%) or lived in the South (42.9% vs. 47.9%) Among Asian‐Other patients, a higher proportion of T2D cases lived in the South (29.7% vs. 19.7%) and were self‐insured (18.7% vs. 11.6%), but a lower proportion were aged 65–74 years (14.8% vs. 20.1%), had commercial insurance (78.0% vs. 84.8%), or lived in the West (47.6% vs. 63.5%).

In the overall study population, T2D cases had a higher comorbidity burden than controls (mean CCI, 1.1 vs. 0.8; Table 1). A notably higher proportion (≥5% difference) of T2D cases had obesity (47.1% vs. 31.9%), CVD (72.9% vs. 60.4%), hypertension (67.7% vs. 54.0%), rheumatologic disease (47.5% vs. 41.0%), sleep disorders (28.2% vs. 19.5%), and OSA (20.7% vs. 12.4%).

The pattern of baseline clinical characteristics between T2D cases and controls remained generally similar in subgroup analyses by sex and race/ethnicity. The mean CCI score was higher for T2D cases among men (1.0 vs. 0.7) and women (1.2 vs. 0.9) (Table 1). Among men, a higher proportion of T2D cases (≥5% difference) had ASCVD. Among women, a higher proportion of T2D cases had chronic pain/fibromyalgia, neuropathy, OA, and GERD. Within each race/ethnicity subgroup, there were some differences in the prevalence of comorbidities between T2D cases and controls. Among White, Black, and Hispanic patients, a higher proportion of controls had a CCI score of 0; no difference was observed among Asian‐Other patients. Among White patients, a higher proportion of T2D cases had obesity (47.2% vs. 32.2%), rheumatologic disease (47.9% vs. 41.5%), and sleep disorders (29.9% vs. 20.5%). Among Black patients, a higher proportion of T2D cases had chronic pain/fibromyalgia (18.7% vs. 13.6%), neuropathy (18.1% vs. 12.6%), and GERD (24.5% vs. 18.9%). Among Hispanic patients, a higher proportion of T2D cases had obesity (48.0% vs. 32.9%), OA (22.5% vs. 16.9%), rheumatologic disease (45.0% vs. 38.5%), and sleep disorders (23.3% vs. 15.9%). Among Asian‐Other patients, fewer differences were observed, except that a higher proportion of T2D cases had obesity (29.7% vs. 15.9%) and sleep disorders (20.2% vs. 14.8%).

Patients aged 35–44, 45–54, and 55–64 years had higher odds of progressing to T2D (OR range, 1.33–1.49), compared with those aged 18–34 years. Sex was not associated with progression. Patients insured through Medicare Advantage or Medicaid had higher odds of progression (OR, 1.06 [1.01–1.13]), compared with all other types of payers. Patients with a CCI score of 3, 4, or ≥5 had higher odds of progression (OR range, 1.30–1.48), compared with patients with a CCI score of 0. Several comorbidities were associated with progression, including obesity (OR, 1.45 [1.41–1.45]), CVD (OR, 1.64 [1.60–1.70]), OSA (OR, 1.36 [1.30–1.41]), and MASH/MAFLD (OR, 1.37 [1.29–1.45]). Use of metformin (OR, 9.29 [8.88–9.72]), any other oral ADM (OR, 6.79 [5.71–8.08]), and any GLP‐1 RA indicated for T2D or obesity (OR, 1.79 [1.64–1.96]) was associated with progression (Figure 1).

Across the subgroups (Figures 2, 3, 4 and S3), higher odds of progression to T2D were observed for patients aged >35 to <65 years and with greater comorbidity burden. Use of ADM was associated with progression in the subgroups. Among women, higher odds of progression were observed among age groups 35–44, 45–54, and 55–64 (OR range, 1.26–1.47), whereas patients aged ≥75 had lower odds of progression (OR, 0.77 [0.67–0.89]). Obesity (OR, 1.39 [1.33–1.45]), CVD (OR, 1.68 [1.60–1.75]), OSA (OR, 1.40 [1.32–1.49]), MASH/MAFLD (OR, 1.45 [1.33–1.57]), and neuropathy (OR, 1.25 [1.18–1.33]) were associated with progression (Figure 2).

Among patients with laboratory and weight data available in the AEMR database (n = 11 407), higher baseline HbA1c values were associated with progression to T2D (HbA1c ≥5.7% to <6.0%; OR, 2.17 [1.83–2.57]; HbA1c ≥6.0% to <6.5%; OR, 10.78 [9.13–12.72]). Compared with patients with a BMI <25.0 kg/m2, patients in obesity class I, class II, or class III had higher odds of progression (OR range, 1.68–2.69). In addition, higher triglyceride levels were associated with higher odds of progression (OR range, 1.20–1.57). Higher HDL cholesterol was associated with lower odds of progression (OR, 0.79 [0.67–0.94]) (Figure 3).

Among patients diagnosed with obesity at baseline (n = 37 268), higher odds of progression to T2D were observed for patients aged 35–44, 45–54, and 55–64 years (OR range, 1.24–1.38), but not for patients aged 65–74 years. Conversely, patients aged ≥75 years had lower odds of progression (OR, 0.70 [0.59–0.84]). A CCI score of 3, 4, or ≥5 and the presence of CVD, OSA, MASH/MAFLD, and ASCVD were each associated with progression. Use of metformin (OR, 6.64 [6.23–7.08]), any other oral ADM (OR, 7.16 [5.39–9.51]), or any GLP‐1 RA (OR, 1.54 [1.39–1.71]) was associated with progression (Figure 4).

Among the 62% of patients with race/ethnicity data available in the Cx database (n = 60 905), Black patients (OR, 1.27 [1.19–1.36]), and Hispanic patients (OR, 1.21 [1.12–1.31]) had higher odds of progression to T2D than White patients. Patients with Asian‐Other race/ethnicity did not have higher odds of progression (OR, 0.94 [0.86–1.02]) than White patients (Figure S3).

DISCUSSION

This real‐world case–control study characterised a patient population with prediabetes and identified demographic and clinical predictors—assessed in the 1‐year period before T2D diagnosis—that were associated with progression to T2D. In the overall study population, middle‐aged patients and those with a higher comorbidity burden had higher odds of progression. Specific comorbidities, including obesity, CVD, OSA, MASH/MAFLD, and neuropathy, were also associated with progression in the overall population as well as among women, patients with obesity, patients with available laboratory, and patients with available race/ethnicity data. The odds of progression were higher among patients with elevated HbA1c levels, elevated triglyceride levels, higher BMI classification, or Black or Hispanic race/ethnicity.

Age consistently predicted progression in the overall study population and subgroups defined by sex and race/ethnicity. Specifically, adults aged 35–44, 45–54, and 55–64 years had higher odds of progression than adults aged 18–34 years.12, 22, 23 However, older adults (aged ≥65 years) did not have higher odds of progression, suggesting the risk of T2D is not linear across adult age groups. Other studies have also found increased risk of progression among middle‐aged adults. Progression to T2D reflects an interplay between age and other risk factors, such as lifestyle, genetic predisposition, weight, comorbidities, and/or metabolic changes over the life course. 24 The lower odds of progression observed among older adult populations could also be attributable to survival bias or the natural history of disease at older ages, such as slower disease progression with later onset.23, 25, 26 In contrast, the rising incidence of T2D among younger adults (<40 years) may be linked to increasing obesity prevalence in the population and faster disease progression with earlier age of onset. 27

The prevalence of T2D remains high among both men and women, 28 yet men and women may have different risk profiles for T2D. Women may also have a broader risk factor array for T2D. Sex‐specific risks and clinical features pertinent to women include hormonal fluctuations related to pregnancy/reproduction and metabolic changes over the life course, as well as specific comorbidities.29, 30 Among women in our study, a higher proportion of T2D cases than controls had chronic pain, neuropathy, OA, and GERD. Other research suggests the risk of progression to T2D in women could also be attributable to sex‐specific comorbidities, such as PCOS, a common endocrine disorder. While it is uncertain if PCOS is an independent risk factor for T2D, 14 research indicates the odds of T2D are 2.87 times higher in women with PCOS than in women without PCOS. 31 Advancing our understanding of sex‐linked comorbidities that increase the risk of progression to T2D could improve clinical care among patients with prediabetes. Early identification of comorbidities, such as PCOS, can also inform care planning and treatment. 32

The risk of progression to T2D may also vary by race/ethnicity. Our study found higher odds of progression among Black and Hispanic patients than White patients. These findings are consistent with other research showing that diabetes is unequally distributed among race/ethnic subgroups in the population.2, 33 Data from National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey cycles show that non–Hispanic Black individuals have the highest prevalence of diabetes among all ethnic groups, 2 and other research has found that Asian, Black, and Hispanic individuals have a higher risk of prediabetes than White individuals. 34 In our study, Asian‐Other patients did not appear more likely than White patients to progress to T2D. However, the combined grouping of patients identified as Asian or Other, due to small sample sizes, makes the interpretation of this finding challenging. Variation in diabetes onset and prevalence across race/ethnic groups may be attributable to population differences in various factors, such as age structures, lifestyle factors, biological variability in gene expression and metabolic function, cardiometabolic comorbidities, and health care practices.22, 35, 36, 37, 38 In our study at baseline, there were some notable differences (≥5% difference) between race/ethnic groups in the prevalence of comorbidities, although specific comorbidities—obesity, CVD, OSA, and MASH/MAFLD—were consistently associated with progression to T2D in the overall population and subgroups. Our study's findings are consistent with prior research demonstrating an association between these comorbidities and T2D.39, 40, 41, 42 Clinical care guidelines recognise the role of comorbidities, in addition to patient demographic characteristics, preferences, health status, and prognosis, in T2D care planning and disease management. The American Diabetes Association Standards of Care, for example, emphasise monitoring patients with prediabetes for progression based on a comprehensive individualised risk assessment, informed by clinical information as well as demographic, laboratory, and medication information, among other components. 43 The results from our study identify numerous salient factors that consistently correlate with progression to T2D and should be considered in individualised risk assessment and the development or refinement of risk stratification tools or algorithms.

ADM, such as metformin, has been shown to delay or prevent progression from prediabetes to T2D.44, 45 Although baseline use of metformin, other oral ADM, and GLP‐1 RA were predictors of progression to T2D in our study, these associations may be a function of the retrospective study design, and we cannot rule out the possibility of protopathic bias. Patients with prediabetes who are prescribed ADM may have been considered at higher risk for T2D. Previous prospective, randomised clinical trials among individuals at high risk for T2D showed that lifestyle intervention and metformin reduce the risk of progression to T2D by 58% and 31%, respectively, compared with placebo. 46 GLP‐1 RAs, such as semaglutide and tirzepatide, also reduce progression from prediabetes to T2D, 47 and among patients with T2D, GLP‐1 RAs effectively lower HbA1c and FPG levels. 18

This study's strengths included using a large nationally representative source of administrative claims linked to electronic medical records to examine the association between predictors and progression to T2D. The integrated dataset enabled assessment of demographic, clinical, and laboratory predictors among patients in real‐world settings. This study assessed associations between the predictors and the outcome; however, causality cannot be established. We did not explore interaction effects between predictors in regression modelling; therefore, this study does not provide information about potential joint effects between predictors. Furthermore, the study datasets did not include information on all potential predictors, such as behavioural, lifestyle, and socioeconomic factors, that can influence the persistence and progression of prediabetes. 48 These limitations are inherent to administrative claims data and case–control study design. Although the study included patients from a wide range of demographic and payer groups, the sample comprised primarily insured patients; therefore, generalisability of the findings to other populations (e.g., the uninsured) may be limited.

CONCLUSIONS

It is crucial to identify patients with prediabetes who have a high risk of progression to T2D so that appropriate preventive actions can be taken. This study identified demographic, clinical, and laboratory factors associated with progression to T2D and contributed important information about the patient risk profile in the year before progression. Specifically, this study suggests that middle age, Black or Hispanic race/ethnicity, overall comorbidity burden, and specific comorbidities—obesity, CVD, OSA, MASH, and neuropathy—are associated with progression to T2D. The study findings can inform treatment planning and decision‐making to mitigate progression in patients with prediabetes.
