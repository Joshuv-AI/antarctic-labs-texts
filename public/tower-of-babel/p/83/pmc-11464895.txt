Importance estimate of features via analysis of their weight and gradient profile
Authors: Ho Tung Jeremy Chan, Eduardo Veas
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Understanding what is important and redundant within data can improve the modelling process of neural networks by reducing unnecessary model complexity, training time and memory storage. This information is however not always priorly available nor trivial to obtain from neural networks. There are existing feature selection methods which utilise the internal working of a neural network for selection, however further analysis and interpretation of the input features’ significance is often limiting. We propose an approach that offers an extension that estimates the significance of features by analysing the gradient descent of a pairwise layer within a model. The changes that occur with the weights and gradients throughout training provide a profile that can be used to better understand the importance hierarchy between the features for ranking and feature selection. Additionally, this method is transferable to existing fully or partially trained models, which is beneficial for understanding existing or active models. The proposed approach is demonstrated empirically with a study which uses benchmark datasets from libraries such as MNIST and scikit-feat, as well as a simulated dataset and an applied real world dataset. This is verified with the ground truth where available, and if not, via a comparison of fundamental feature selection methods, which includes existing statistical based and embedded neural network based feature selection methods through the methodology of Reduce and Retrain.

Introduction

Redundancy in dataset is a common issue that is especially severe with large dataset. Identifying and removing redundancy is beneficial to the processing, development and understanding of the dataset. Feature selection and feature transformation are two common techniques to do so.

In the domain of dimension reduction, feature selection and feature transformation are different approaches. Feature selection refers to techniques that select a subset of the dimensions and discard the rest. This retains semantic meaning within the chosen data while avoiding the loss of information from the transformation of dimensions1. Feature transformation focuses on transforming the data into a lower dimension with minimal loss to the significance present within the data. Common feature transformation includes, PCA2, t-SNE3, etc.

Deep learning algorithms use computational units (neurons) to learn hierarchical representations of the data in the form of a neural network (NN) model. A powerful property of NNs is that they can learn meaningful representations (features) without manual feature engineering4. The caveat is that the blackbox nature of NN makes it difficult to examine or explain the relationship between the input and the output. However, the identification of important features within the input can provide informative details that could be useful in understanding the dataset and improving the functioning model. For example, in an extremely high-dimensional problem, knowing what features are important can reduce the computational complexity in training. This also helps to develop a better understanding about the basis of the problem from the model prediction.

In this work, we propose an extension that can estimate the relative importance of features. Additionally, this extension is suitable, applicable, and generalisable to NNs. We accomplish this by extending different functioning models of different use cases, which can be any type of NN with any type of architecture, via embedding a layer of weights in between the input and the first layer of the model. When the extended model is trained, its internal feature selection can be reflected upon the embedded layer of weights through back propagation. This information is analysed in different forms to determine the relevance between the features of the dataset: Importance Estimate via Weight Profiles and Importance Estimate via Gradient Profiles. The analyses utilise the hierarchy principle from statistics5–8 which is also present in NNs. We confirm any estimated significance from the analyses with the ground truth when it is available. Alternatively, the estimated significance and redundancy can also be verified by the Reduce and Retrain9,10 methodology. This is where systematically decreasing subsets of selected input channels are retrained with the original architecture, such that the quality of the selection can be measured by performance retention and compared with other selections’ performance retention.

We demonstrate the proposed approach by several case studies concerning different datasets and different NN architecture. They include a binary classification simulated dataset from section “Simulated dataset”, 3 binary text recognition benchmark datasets from section “E2: scikit-feature”, a multi-class handwritten text dataset from section “E3: MNIST”, and a multi-class image recognition dataset from section “E4: Dry bean dataset”. We compared the proposed approach with a variety of existing methods, such as random selection, statistical methods, and existing embedding based deep learning methods. We used the ground truth for verification when possible, we visualised identified feature importance when appropriate, and we employed the Reduce and Retrain methodology for additional verification with both the Balanced Error Rate10 and the F1-score as well. Lastly, we investigated the feasibility of applying the proposed approach to partially trained models and compared performances in section “Attachable mechanism: partial training”.

This paper can be summerised by its sections, section “Introduction” introduces the problem and why it is important. Section “Background” presents relevant background knowledge in relation to the proposed approach. Section “Related work” presents the more directly related work that the proposed approach utilised during development. Section “Approach: pairwise importance estimate extension” details the proposed approach along with methods of analysis and verification. Section “Experiments” details the experimental setups and details of the experiments. Section “Results” presents empirical results obtained from the experiments and details the analyses that were performed from the results. Section “Discussion” proceed to answer the Research Questions within this work, and dives deeper into the strengths and limitations of the proposed approach. And this paper is concluded by Section “Conclusion”, which summarise the proposed approach, along with the achievements and contribution it made.

Feature selection and feature importance

Feature selection is a process which aims to select a smaller subset of features, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D*$$\end{document}, from the complete set of features, D, to perform the task at hand. There are 3 main types of feature selection methods: Filter14, Wrapper15, and Embedded16. Each follows a different principal to select a subset of features that could produce the best performance.

The proposed method from this work follows the principal of embedded methods, which are methods that adapt the subset of features for selection as the model trains. A notable difference is that the proposed method does not utilise feature sparsity within hierarchy constraints17,18 to arrive at the subset of features for selection, although the use of feature sparsity to determine the subset of features for selection tends to be the norm for deep learning feature selection methods.

The core of the proposed method seeks to determine the importance of each feature. Related work

Knowing what features are important and which are redundant is a major challenge, because this information is usually not known a priori21,22. Therefore we tend to use all features irrespective of the complexity23. Neural Networks learn direct mapping from raw input to model prediction, and their ability to process large amounts of data allows them to deal with such redundancy. This is attributed by NNs’ ability to conduct feature selection internally, as features are processed by the intricacies throughout its hidden units. The issue being the limiting comprehension of the weighting of features with respect to the output of the model24. This is what the proposed method of this work aims to achieve, to identify the features that are being selected internally and to estimate the importance of such features.

There are different ways to utilise the internal mechanism of NN to perform feature selection. There exists embedded methods which perform the feature selection at the input level. They work by embedding weights after the input level, such that each of these weight correspond to one of the input. This is reinforced by the hierarchy principal from statistic5–8. When the NN is trained, the weights are regularised based on some constraint, such as Elastic Net regularisation, which then allows for a subset of the input features to be automatically selected, hence utilising the internal training mechanism and regularisation to perform feature selection. Examples include. Deep Feature Selection1, LassoNet17, Sparse Centroid Encoder25, and variations of NNs with L1 regularisation that utilise the sparsity in features specifically to perform feature selection as the model trains. In such cases, features become ’inactive’18 within the NN. Although this is desired in embedded methods of feature selection, the binary nature of the selection leads to an incomplete understanding of feature importance amongst the features. Additionally, since prior knowledge or expert opinion regarding the data is not always available, the binary nature of the selection not only cannot provide information regarding the relativity of importance amongst the features, it also makes it difficult to be used with verification approaches of feature importance due to the limited information available. We aim to identify feature importance without relying on prior knowledge or expert opinion. The proposed approach from this work allows for a more complete understanding by quantifying relative feature importance via the different analyses from the proposed approach, which can be additionally used for feature selection.

There also exists methods which utilise embedding of multiple NNs in order to perform feature selection. For example, in an existing work of Gui et al.26, it is shown that a detachable Attention based mechanism27 is able to select features through an attention network between the input and a secondary fully connected network. This attention network correlates the input with the loss function between the output of the secondary network, and the supervised target to provide each feature a probability of being selected. In another attention based work of Wang et al.28, self attention is used to connect words to better capture global sentence semantics for text. Even though it is used within the context of text semantics, the principle of correlating input (words) with embedded mechanisms still stands. In Huang et al.10, a neural network which performs threshold activation is embedded between the input and a secondary fully connected network to perform feature selection. Even in recent works of Khan et al.29, Guo et al.30, and in the work of Wojtas and Chen31, NN of different architectures, such as variations of CNN and Inception network, are used as essentially feature extractor before feeding the extracted feature, in another words: selected features, towards a secondary fully connected network. The caveat of NN layering is the additional complexity that it introduces, especially concerning large network with many parameters. In order for the embedding of NN layering to work as intended, this would first require computational cost and computational time to fine tune the parameters of the respective NNs. This is not guaranteed to work and is subject to many different factors32. Upon the completion of fine tuning, if it is successful, it would require additional computation cost and additional computational time to run the layered NNs. In the proposed approach of this work, instead of utilising complex layering of NNs which would incur additional computational cost and additional computation time along with the demand of fine tuning effort in order for the layered NNs to work. We propose an extension, which could be embedded into NN that would capture the information necessary during the forward and backwards propagation of training. This incurs minimal computational changes and minimal architectural changes to the underlying functioning NN. This would also not require fine tuning of parameters. Specifically, the embedded extension would create a statistical profile33 for each feature via information collected from training. This is similar to Tonekaboni et al.’s34 instance wise feature importance, which quantified shifts in predictive distribution over time. Another embedded based NN method is shown in Liu et al.35 where weight changes during training are used for importance estimate. This reinforce that information collected during training can be used for analysis. In our case, the information that are collected can be either gradient changes of each feature during training or the corresponding weights of each feature at the end of training. Standard procedures such as Area under Curve36, Standard Deviation37 and sensitivity of change also known as Rate of Change38, are used to analyse the collected information from each epoch of training. Moreover, layered NNs10,26,29 have shown that the principle of transfer learning39 can work in tandem with NNs. This suggests that it is possible for the proposed extension to be applied to untrained models as well as partially trained models, which makes the method more generalisable and applicable towards many use cases.

Approach: Pairwise Importance Estimate Extension

Pairwise Importance Estimate Extension is an extension that is attachable to NNs, which can offer different analyses of importance estimate via the information that it can collect during training. The extension aims to estimate the importance of data channels with respect to the objective function of a NN. In this approach, we are looking to accrue the relative importance of each input feature by monitoring a representation of a NN’s internal mechanism. Namely, we aim to answer the following with our approach:

RQ1 Which data channels are significant or redundant?

RQ2 What portion of redundant features can be omitted without affecting the model detrimentally?

RQ3 Is it possible to apply the proposed method towards a model that is partially trained?

Roadmap

We intend the proposed method to act as a trainable extension that can be attached or detached from functioning NN models. Following the detachable novelty, this extension should not affect or have a minimal impact upon the integrity of any functioning model. It should serve to provide the means to observe the internal workings of the model in order to compile information that reflect upon the relative importance of input features for the model. This is achievable via extracting information during backpropagation, which results in a fingerprint importance attribution based solely on the data used in training. This allows the extension to maintain the integrity of any functioning model, which is different to existing methods that inadvertently obscure the information that the NN learn with additional complexity of NN layers or sparsity. This is useful in many cases as this introduce minimal complexity to the model, which is less likely to interfere with the parameters that were already fine tuned.

The outline of the principles behind the proposed approach is as follows: The prerequisite is an existing architecture, which is specific to the use case, that can perform its task accurately, or a model that is partially trained for its task. This is followed by extending the model such that it becomes capable of producing some importance estimate. In our case, this is the attachment of the Pairwise Importance Estimate Extension. This is then proceeded by training said model, where information for the estimate of feature importance is obtained. Finally, estimate of feature importance is calculated via analysis of the outcomes during training, and the estimates can be verified through different means such as ground truth or systematic methodology like Reduce and Retrain9,10. This outline is illustrated in Fig. 2a.Fig. 2(a) Outline of the principles behind the proposed approach. For the verification of estimated importance, the ground truth of importance is used when it is available. Otherwise, Reduce and Retrain is used to examine the retention of performance as the subset of features estimated to be important decreases.

Pairwise layer

A pairwise layer is common and essential in existing works1,17,18 in order for the regularisation of the NN to feature select. Additionally, other works10,26 have demonstrated that information regarding feature importance with respect to the NN can be obtained between the input and the NN. This is also supported by the hierarchy principle5–8,17 from statistic.

The pairwise layer functions as an element-wise multiplication, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X^{i} \odot C$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$X^{i}$$\end{document} is an input matrix with dimension \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n \times c$$\end{document}, and C is the pairwise layer which is a scalar variable with length c. A difference between existing works and the proposed approach is that the proposed approach does not only take the trained weights from regularisation1,17,18 nor the weights from additional NN mechanisms10,26 at face value. For simplicity, the pairwise layer and the analysis of it will be referred to as the Importance Estimate, IE. Given that classification is a function, f, of a neural network, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text {NN}$$\end{document}, with input, x. When IE is applied in the classification, it can be presented as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(x)= \text {NN}(\text {IE}(x))$$\end{document}, which can be simplified to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(x)= \text {NN}(x \odot C)$$\end{document}.

It is important to note that the pairwise layer is a type of linear layer, however it is not just any linear layer40. Therefore the replacement of a linear layer with the pairwise layer will not yield the same outcome. This is showcased between Fig. 3b and c. The difference is in the forward propagation and the backwards propagation. In the forward propagation, a linear layer performs a matrix multiplication with the input, where the weights of the layer is in the form of a matrix \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$c \times m$$\end{document}, where m is configurable depending on the parameter of the linear layer. During that multiplication, an element-wise multiplication cannot be guaranteed unless the matrix had a specific initialisation and setting that creates and maintains the diagonal matrix. In the backwards propagation, when the change in gradient is applied to the weights during gradient descent, the proposed approach aims to analyse these changes in order to estimate importance. This is achievable with pairwise weights, where the changes in the respective weight can be interpreted as a direct change for the corresponding feature. Importance estimate via Weight profiles

IE via Weight Profiling uses pairwise connected weights to observe the updates from gradient descent upon each input feature in order to create their corresponding weight profile.

Weight profile of feature importance

A weight profile is formulated through the pairwise layer extension between the input and the first layer of the model. In order to measure via the extension, the weights of the pairwise layer extension all share the same initialisaion of 1s in the beginning of training. This effectively means that the extension begins as the identity function, and gradually changes through the backpropagation process of training. This forces all the weights from the pairwise extension, where each correspond to an input feature, to share the same starting point in order to measure relative importance between the input features as the backpropagation process occurs. The weight profile for each input feature is thus formulated. The outline of such is available in Fig. 2b, and the structure of the NN is demonstrated in Fig. 3c.

The introduction of IE in classification models does not alter the existing loss function, i.e cross entropy, CE, but creates another trainable variable. This makes the loss function as follows \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min _{w,b, \text {IE}} \ \sum _{(x,y) \in D} \text {CE}(y, f(x))$$\end{document}. The pairwise weights are trained with the rest of the model where the pairwise weights are directly affected by the gradient descent algorithm recursively through backpropagation. In relation to IE, the loss function can be rewritten as \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min _{w,b, \text {IE}} \ \sum _{(x,y) \in D} \text {CE}(y, \text {NN}(\text {IE}(x)))$$\end{document}. The disparity between the pairwise weights at the end of training can be used in Weight based analysis.

Importance estimate via Gradient profiles

Similar to IE via Weight Profiles, IE via Gradient Profiles also uses pairwise connected weights to observe updates from gradient descent upon each input feature. However it achieves this differently by observing the updates from gradient descent without modifying the weights themselves via the addition of a Gradient Collection module within the Pairwise Importance Estimate Extension. This allows a gradient profile of each input features to be created from training.

The reasoning behind IE via Gradient Profiles is to be able to observe the process of backpropagation without interfering with the integrity of the original model, and to observe the process in greater detail. A weight profile produces an importance estimate for a corresponding input feature by realising the gradient changes from the corresponding pairwise weight during the backpropagation of training. However, by doing so, it changes the original model as the forward propagation will be affected. Furthermore, a weight profile is only concerned with the pairwise weight at the end of training, which limits the amount of perceivable information that can be used for further analysis. The pairwise weights from the pairwise layer allows information of each input feature to be reflected in the backpropagation of training, and the Gradient Collection module offers the alternative of observing all information from training without modifying the integrity of the model.

Gradient profile of feature importance

Similar to the process of formulating weight profiles, gradient profiles are also formulated through the pairwise layer extension between the input and the first layer of the model. In addition to the initialisation of 1s for the pairwise weights, the pairwise layer extension is also intialised to be immutable. Again, this effectively means that the extension begins as the identity function in order to measure relative importance between the input features. Moreover, with the pairwise layer being immutable, its weights will not be updated by the gradient changes of the backpropagation process. This means that the pairwise layer extension stays as the identity function throughout training. In relation to IE and the loss function, the loss function follows from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min _{w,b} \ \sum _{(x,y) \in D} \text {CE}(y, \text {NN}(\text {IE}(x)))$$\end{document} and because IE remains as the identity function, it can be simplified into \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\min _{w,b} \ \sum _{(x,y) \in D} \text {CE}(y, \text {NN}(x))$$\end{document}.

As mentioned previously, this is so that the process of backpropagation can be observed without interfering with the integrity of the original model, and to have all the information from each step of gradient descent available to create a distribution for a more in depth analysis rather than just using the final value of the pairwise weight. Therefore, instead of formulating profiles via the weights of the pairwise layer, profiles are formed from the gradient changes that occur during training. Specifically, a Gradient Collection module is in place along with the pairwise layer extension to collect the gradient changes from every batch of every epoch for each of the pairwise weights from the extension. This means storing a matrix with the dimension (E, b, F) where E is the number of epoch, b is the size of each batch, and F is the number of input features. This allows a distribution to be formulated which can later be combined with statistical methods in section “Gradient based analysi”. The outline of this process is demonstrated in Fig. 2b, and the structure of the NN is shown in Fig. 3d.

Verification

The presence of a ground truth makes the process of verification trivial, however this is not guaranteed. In the absence of any ground truth specifying which input features are important to the objective function of a model, the Reduce and Retrain9,10 approach can be used to verify claims of importance. This is an iterative approach that utilises subsets of features to retrain new models, and the performances are then compared.

There are 2 main ways of utilising the Reduce and Retrain approach.A systematic reduction of features where less features are used in subsequent retrain, this continues until the size of the subset of feature becomes 0. If the selection of features are actually important, the drop in performance should be gradual at first and drops exponentially when the significant features are omitted. This is similar to feature perturbation, but instead of systematically removing features one by one, groups of features are removed systematically. The overall retention of performance can be scored by calculating the Area under Curve36 of the systematically reduced and retrained models.A comparison of performance between the original set of features and different subsets of features. The original set of features is used as the baseline reference for the comparison, such that the subsets of feature should not perform worse than the baseline reference.

All Embedded NN based methods, such as DF and NFS, are expected to perform better than Weight-Naive due to the specificity of the constrains upon the pairwise weights. If not, they are at least expected to perform similarly to Weight-Naive due to the common principle used in Weight based analysis. Fisher and FScore should also have similar performance due to the similarity between the methods. Fisher and FScore are chosen for comparison because they are the fundamental benchmark statistical approaches from the scikit-feat42 library. The same is also expected between the Grad-AUC, Grad-ROC and Grad-STD, where the common principle of Gradient based analysis should result in similar performance. As Gradient based analysis is a combination of weight based analysis principle and statistical principles, it is expected that gradient based methods will perform better than the rest.

It is important to note that the performance of the methods that utilise a NN model is dependent on the configuration and architecture of the NN used, as well as the amount of data available. Unlike the similarity and statistical based methods or Random Selection, the quality of feature selection from deep learning methods is influenced by the baseline performance that the deployed NN can provide. DF and NFS are chosen for comparison because the two of them cover the fundamentals of embedded NN approaches. DF is based on the regularisation principle of embedded weights with respect to the input, and NFS is based on embedding layering of NNs. They are also the more stable variants from their respective approaches, that are less likely to be prone to issues such as fine tuning parameters and additional complication that are mentioned in “Related work” section.

Performance metric and measure

The Balanced Error Rate (BER10) and the F1-Score were used as the main performance metrics. BER represents the average of the error rate of each class, it prevents skewing of classes by taking each class into account. BER was also used in an existing study by Huang et al.10 to compare performance during Reduce and Retrain of different models. F1-Score was used as it is the standard metric for classification result due to its combined use of precision and recall to take True and False Positives into account. BER and F1-Score can be combined with Reduce and Retrain9,10 to measure the performance retention between the decreasing subsets of features for each method. Additionally, Area under Curve of the BER’s Reduce and Retrain curve can be calculated to provide a more concrete measure of each method’s overall performance retention.

In order to have a reliable measure of each method’s performance, 5 different train and validation splits were used for each dataset unless the split was predetermined by some established benchmark, i.e MNIST. Nevertheless, the average performance that each method achieved via the 5 different split of each dataset were used and compared. Furthermore, when different methods have achieved very similar results, the Paired T Test47,48 was used in order to discern if there really exists a real significance between the results of the methods involved. The Paired T Test takes the distribution achieved by the methods into account and compare them in a statistical manner to determine the t-value and the p-value. The t-value indicates if there is a difference between the distributions, and the p-value indicates the probability of achieving this difference. The p-value is used in conjunction with an alpha value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}, which is used as a threshold to determine whether the difference between the average of each method’s distribution can be considered significant. Therefore if the p-value is lower than the declared alpha, then it is considered significant. An alpha value \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document} of 0.01 is used, which correspond to a 99% confidence. A higher confidence compared to the usual 95% confidence is used due to the similarity between the methods, which could potentially lead to similar results anyway, therefore any claims of significance should have a stronger claim than usual.

The Paired T Test relies on the assumption that the pair of distributions have similar variance. This is defined by \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\frac{1}{2}< \frac{\sigma _{1}}{\sigma _{2}} < 2$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma _1, \sigma _2$$\end{document} are the respective standard deviation from the pair of distributions to be compared with the test. When this is not the case, the Welch’s T Test49 is used instead, which allows for the pair of distributions to have different variance.

Discussion

The research questions, posed previously in section “Approach: pairwise importance estimate extension”, have been addressed by the findings from the experiments.

RQ1: Which data channels are significant or redundant? We have estimated feature importance for each dataset from the different experiments via the proposed approach: Pairwise Importance Estimate Extension. Additionally, we have compared such results with existing methods in the experiments. The workings of how feature importance can be interpreted for data channels are documented in section “Importance estimate via weight profiles” and section “Importance estimate via gradient profiles”. We have detailed how different analysis can be conducted with the proposed method based on the weight and gradient profiles, and how they can inform the estimate of importance. Specifically, we have detailed in section “Gradient based analysis” how statistical methods can be combined with the gradients from the backpropagation of the neural network to arrive at the estimation of importance. We have shown visual representations of feature importance amongst the experiments in section “Visualisation of feature importance” when applicable. The visuals have been compared with ground truth or prior knowledge to verify claims of significance, as well as evaluating the information that can be obtained from such.

RQ2: What portion of redudant features can be omitted without affecting the model detrimentally? We investigated the removal of redundant features and their effect on model performance with Reduce and Retrain9,10. The results for the different datasets are detailed in section “Reduce and retrain: removal of features subset”, where the resulting performance curve from the systematic reduction of feature subsets are available in Fig. 4, 5, 6 and 7. The overall performance retention of each method’s Reduce and Retrain result quantified by the Area under Curve is also available in Table 1.

RQ3: Is it possible to apply the proposed method towards a model that is partially trained? We have investigated in the section “Attachable mechanism: partial training” where the evaluation showed no significant difference between the performance and importance estimate from models that were partially trained with the proposed method, and models that were trained fully with the proposed method.

Strengths and limitations of the proposed approach: Pairwise Importance Estimate Extension have been observed throughout the experiments. The strength of the method includes:Minimal complexity within the setup The set up of the proposed approach introduces minimal external mechanism, it only requires a one-to-one multiplication between the IE and the features, which is trivial to set up whilst allowing information of significance to be extracted from the input features. This makes the proposed approach easily deployable without the need to conduct any fine tuning of existing parameters, nor is there a need to fine tune any new parameters. This is an advantage over most existing deep learning feature selection methods1,10,26,29, as most of them require new mechanisms, which in turn calls for the fine tuning of parameters that is dependent on the dataset. This introduces additional cost of time and labour even though it could still result in subpar performance as shown by the experiments’ results.Preserves integrity of functioning models The architecture and parameters of functioning models are specific, however an unique advantage that the proposed approach offers is that via the combined use of the Gradient Collection Module and an immutable pairwise layer in Gradient based analysis, it is not only possible to not conduct any fine tuning of parameters, it is also possible for the extension to not interfere with the internal workings of the underlying functioning model. This is due to the fact that the Gradient Collection module collects information from the training that is later used via observations, which does not affect the forward or backwards propagation from the training process.Accessible information and attachable to functioning models The minimal complexity from IE allows for information of significance to be easily accessible. The linear transformation allows for information to be extracted in a straightforward manner, otherwise such information would have been obfuscated behind the non-linearity workings of many common NNs, which can make the analysis of each feature harder due to increased complexity from backpropogation. Conclusion

To conclude, we have proposed and demonstrated the Pairwise Importance Estimate Extension, which offers different approaches for analysing feature importance through the use of a pairwise layer. Notably, we introduced Importance Estimate via Gradient Profiles which offers Gradient based analysis to provide a more thorough insight into feature importance during the training of a model. The proposed extension builds upon previous work and expand upon it by combining statistical procedures with NN’s internal feature selection mechanisms. Furthermore, the proposed approach employs the use of a Gradient Collection Module in order to analyse the gradients from training, this is different to existing NN methods which are only concerned with extracting information from NN’s internal mechanisms but do not conduct any further analysis. The estimation of feature importance from such analysis has been verified with multiple experiments of different datasets. In the experiments, the different proposed approaches were compared with existing feature selection methods as well as baseline measures to provide a benchmark and basis for comparison. Some were verified by comparing to the ground truth or by visual inspection, whilst others were verified with the Reduce and Retrain9,10 verification approach. It was concluded that Grad methods have performed consistently better than existing methods during performance retention of Reduce and Retrain, as well as being able to match with the ground truth when available. With the results, we answered the posed research questions of identifying which data channels are important or redundant, and which subset of data channels can be removed without significantly affecting model performance. This is significant as existing NN methods usually merely provide the results after some feature selection, but rarely provide the information regarding what features are important. And even within literature that do identify importance of features, rarely do they verify that information with additional validation procedures. Additionally, we have also investigated the Partial Training paradigm, which suggests that the proposed extension can serve as an attachable mechanism that does not interfere with the convergence of partially trained models. This follows from the principle of Transfer Learning39, however it is not something that literature of existing NN methods have investigated nor tried, which makes the finding from this work contributory.

We have also examined the strengths and limitations of the proposed approaches. In terms of strengths, the proposed method offers a mechanism with minimal complexity where the importance estimate of features is accessible, and the mechanism itself is transferable via attaching the proposed approach to functioning architectures, which is specific to the use case, at minimum cost without requiring specific fine tuning. The mechanism of the proposed approach also preserves the integrity of the functioning architectures. Additionally, the proposed method offers Gradient based analysis which combines the principles from Weight based analysis, using the internal NN feature selection with statistical methods. This has allowed gradient based approaches to achieve higher performance retention in Reduce and Retrain, and higher consistency across the different datasets. A more in depth analysis will be beneficial in terms of understanding the information extracted, which will improve upon the feature importance estimate. This can be achieved by expanding upon the variety of statistical procedures that are used with Gradient based analysis, and investigate if the Partial Training paradigm still stands with fully trained models that are further Partial Trained.

In the future, we hope to expand upon Gradient based analysis, to create a more mature understanding of the estimates, especially their application with time series problems. We hope other researchers will find our research useful and make use of our method in their work.
