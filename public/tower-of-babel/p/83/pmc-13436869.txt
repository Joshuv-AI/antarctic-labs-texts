Analgesic efficacy and safety of transversus abdominis plane block in women undergoing cesarean delivery: An umbrella review of systematic reviews and meta-analyses
Authors: Abhijit Sukumaran Nair, Tuhin Mistry, Nitinkumar Borkar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntrathecal morphine (ITM) remains the benchmark for post-cesarean analgesia but is frequently associated with opioid-related adverse effects. Regional analgesia techniques such as transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) are increasingly used as an opioid-sparing alternative. Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses (SRMAs) have evaluated TAPB efficacy, yet findings remain fragmented, heterogeneous, and methodologically inconsistent. This umbrella review synthesizes and critically appraises the highest-level evidence on the analgesic efficacy and safety of TAPB after cesarean delivery (CD). A PROSPERO-registered umbrella review was conducted according to PRISMA 2020 and PRIOR guidelines. SRMAs of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating TAPB for post-cesarean analgesia were systematically searched across major databases until October 31, 2025. Methodological quality was assessed using AMSTAR-2, overlap quantified via corrected covered area (CCA), and pooled findings reanalyzed using the metaUmbrella platform. Evidence credibility was evaluated using Ioannidis’ criteria and certainty using GRADE. Fourteen SRMAs encompassing 125 RCTs (60 unique) were included. Study overlap was moderate (CCA 8.33%). AMSTAR-2 ratings revealed variable quality: four high-quality, eight low-quality, and two critically low-quality reviews. Reanalysis demonstrated that TAPB provided small but statistically significant reductions in 24-hour pain at rest (SMD − 0.11), pain on movement (SMD − 0.12), and opioid consumption (SMD − 0.19). All achieved Class IV (weak) evidence with low GRADE certainty. No significant differences were observed in early (6–12-hour) pain or time-to-rescue analgesia. TAPB showed a favorable trend toward reduced postoperative nausea and vomiting, though with high heterogeneity. ITM offered modestly superior early analgesia, but differences were below minimal clinically important thresholds and accompanied by more opioid-related side effects. Despite extensive literature, the overall credibility and certainty of evidence supporting TAPB after CD remain weak due to heterogeneity, methodological limitations, and review redundancy. TAPB provides modest analgesic benefit and improved tolerability, supporting its role when neuraxial opioids are omitted, contraindicated, or declined. High-quality RCTs, standardized TAPB techniques, and core outcome sets are needed to refine clinical recommendations.

Introduction

Cesarean delivery (CD) is one of the most frequently performed obstetrical surgeries worldwide. Adequate postoperative analgesia is crucial to enhance early ambulation, breastfeeding, and maternal–neonatal bonding, while reducing the risks of thromboembolic events and chronic pain.[1] However, contemporary evidence suggests that postoperative pain after CD remains substantial, with more than half of women experiencing severe pain within the first 24 h, despite the use of multimodal analgesia (MMA) strategies.[2] Traditionally, intrathecal morphine (ITM) has been considered the cornerstone of MMA following CD because of its potent and long-lasting effect.[3] However, despite its robust analgesic efficacy, ITM is frequently associated with opioid-related adverse effects such as postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), pruritus, urinary retention, and, rarely, respiratory depression.[4] These limitations, coupled with the increasing emphasis on enhanced recovery after CD (ERAC) protocols, have intensified the search for opioid-sparing regional anesthesia techniques.[5]

The transversus abdominis plane block (TAPB) provides somatic analgesia to the anterior abdominal wall by depositing local anesthetic (LA) between the internal oblique and transversus abdominis muscles, thereby targeting the thoracolumbar nerves (T6–L1).[6] With the advent of ultrasound guidance, the TAPB has gained popularity as a simple, reproducible, and low-risk technique for postoperative pain relief after CD.[7] Over the past decade, numerous randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and systematic reviews and meta-analyses (SRMAs) have evaluated the TAPB for post-CD analgesia, often comparing with ITM, wound infiltration, or placebo.[89101112131415161718192021] While most studies agree that the TAPB offers significant analgesic benefits in women not receiving ITM, its superiority diminishes when ITM is included in the analgesic regimen. Conversely, the TAPB is consistently associated with reduced PONV and pruritus compared with ITM.

Given the proliferation of overlapping SRMAs, the current body of evidence is fragmented and potentially redundant. Variations in inclusion criteria, comparator groups, block techniques (lateral, subcostal, or posterior), LA type and volume, and methodological quality have yielded conflicting conclusions. Such redundancy and variability hinder clear interpretation and the formulation of guidelines. An umbrella review, which systematically compiles and critically appraises evidence from existing SRMAs, is therefore warranted to provide a consolidated summary of the highest-level evidence.[22]

Accordingly, this umbrella review aims to synthesize and re-evaluate the analgesic efficacy and safety of the TAPB in women undergoing CD using standardized statistical frameworks. By addressing methodological overlap and evidence quality across reviews, this analysis seeks to clarify the comparative performance of TAPB versus ITM and other analgesic strategies and to provide evidence-based interpretation for clinical practice and future research.

Materials and Methods

This umbrella review systematically collated and evaluated published SRMAs examining the efficacy and safety of the TAPB in parturient undergoing CD. The protocol was prospectively registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO, registration number CRD420251177370 (registered on 27th October, 2025). The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for SRMAs (PRISMA 2020) and Preferred Reporting Items for Overviews of Reviews (PRIOR) guidelines.[2324] Ethical approval was not required as this study synthesized data from previously published literature.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search of PubMed, Scopus, CINAHL, and Google Scholar databases was conducted from the inception of the TAP block technique (June 1993)[25] until October 31, 2025 to identify eligible SRMAs evaluating the TAP block for post-CD analgesia. Complete search strategies for each database are provided in Appendix A: Supplementary File 1. Reference lists of included reviews were manually screened to identify additional eligible studies. In addition to electronic databases, relevant PROSPERO records were screened to identify completed or ongoing systematic reviews that met the inclusion criteria. No language or publication-status restrictions were applied. A list of full-text articles excluded after eligibility assessment, along with reasons for exclusion, is provided in Supplementary File 2.

Study selection

Two authors (AN and TM) independently screened titles and abstracts and retrieved potentially relevant full-text articles and applied the prespecified PICOS criteria. Discrepancies were resolved by discussion or, when necessary, consultation with a third author (NB). The selection process is illustrated in a PRISMA flow diagram [Figure 1].

Data extraction

Two authors (AN and TM) independently extracted data using a standardized Microsoft Excel spreadsheet. Extracted variables included bibliographic details (authors, year, and journal), number of RCTs and participants, comparator arms, primary and secondary outcomes (pain scores, opioid consumption, time-to-rescue, PONV, pruritus, satisfaction, adverse events), statistical model (fixed or random effects), heterogeneity (I²), publication bias assessment, and certainty of evidence (GRADE, if reported). A third author (NB) verified all extracted data for completeness and accuracy.

Quality assessment

The methodological quality of each included SRMA was independently appraised using the AMSTAR 2 (A MeaSurement Tool to Assess systematic Reviews, version 2) tool.[26] AMSTAR 2 comprises 16 domains, of which seven are considered critical: protocol registration (Item 1), adequacy of the literature search (Item 4), justification for exclusion of studies (Item 7), assessment of risk of bias (Item 9), appropriateness of meta-analytical methods (Item 11), consideration of risk of bias in result interpretation (Item 13), and assessment of publication bias (Item 15). Each item was rated as “Yes,” “Partial Yes,” or “No.” Overall confidence was graded as high, moderate, low, or critically low depending on the number and nature of critical weaknesses. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or discussion with a third author (NB).

Assessment of study overlaps

To examine the degree of overlap in primary studies across the included SRMAs, an UpSet plot was generated using the UpSetR web application (https://gehlenborglab.shinyapps.io/upsetr/) – a matrix-based visualization technique that efficiently displays intersections of sets and their frequencies.[27] A binary matrix (1 = included, 0 = not included) was created to represent the inclusion of primary RCTs across reviews. The corrected covered area (CCA) was calculated to quantify overlap[28]:

CCA = (Nr – Ns)/{(Np x Ns) – Ns}

where Nr = total number of RCTs included, Ns = total number of unique studies, and Np = number of systematic reviews and meta-analyses included. CCA values were interpreted as follows: <5% = slight overlap; 5–10% = moderate; 10–15% = high; >15% = very high overlap.

Statistical analysis

Quantitative synthesis and evidence reanalysis were performed using the ‘metaumbrella’ web-based application (https://www.metaumbrella.org/app), a validated platform for umbrella review analytics.[29] Continuous outcomes were analyzed as standardized mean differences (SMDs) and binary outcomes as odds ratios (OR), each with 95% confidence intervals, under random-effects models. Pooled estimates, heterogeneity (I²), prediction intervals, and small-study effects (Egger’s test, P > 0.10) were recalculated to ensure uniformity. The application also generates Equivalent Hedges’ g (eG), which is a standardized metric used to represent and compare variable effect size (OR, RR, SMD) for analysis in the forest plot.

Evidence credibility was graded using the Ioannidis criteria,[30] which evaluate statistical significance (P < 10-6), sample size adequacy (>1000 participants), significance of the largest study, low heterogeneity (I² <50%), 95% prediction interval excluding the null, absence of small-study effects, and lack of excess significance bias. Evidence classes were defined as: Class I (Convincing), Class II (Highly suggestive), Class III (Suggestive), Class IV (Weak/nominal significance), and nonsignificant (ns).

Certainty of evidence was also assessed using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach,[31] which considers risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Together, the Ioannidis and GRADE frameworks provided complementary, hierarchical assessments of the robustness and reliability of the pooled evidence.

Results

Search and study selection

The electronic database search yielded 74 records (titles and abstracts), as depicted in the PRISMA 2020 flow diagram [Figure 1]. After removal of duplicates and screening of titles and abstracts, 20 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Of these, 14 articles met all inclusion criteria, comprising 13 SRMAs and one stand-alone systematic review without meta-analysis. No additional records were identified through manual screening.

Deviation from the registered protocol

PubMed, Scopus, and CINAHL searches were specified in the protocol for this umbrella review, which was prospectively registered in PROSPERO. These databases produced a small number of eligible systematic reviews and meta-analyses pertinent to the research question during the review process. An additional Google Scholar search was conducted to guarantee thorough identification of relevant evidence and to reduce the possibility of missing pertinent reviews. This deviation did not change the predetermined eligibility criteria, outcomes of interest, or techniques for data extraction and synthesis; rather, it was used only to improve the evidence base’s completeness. Study Overlap using Corrected Covered Area (CCA):

Across the 14 included SRMAs,[89101112131415161718192021] a total of 125 RCTs were identified, of which 60 were unique and 65 overlapped among multiple reviews. The CCA was 8.33%, indicating moderate overlap among reviews. The UpSet plot [Figure 2] illustrated that the largest intersection comprised seven shared RCTs across several reviews. Multiple intersections containing five or more studies were observed, indicating frequent duplication of primary trials. Nonetheless, some reviews contained unique datasets, confirming moderate redundancy. Accounting for this overlap was critical when interpreting the aggregate evidence on TAPB efficacy and safety after CD.

Methodological quality (AMSTAR-2)

Methodological quality assessment using AMSTAR-2 across 16 domains demonstrated variable rigor among included SRMAs published between 2012 and 2025. Overall, four reviews were rated high quality,[11121518] seven low quality,[1013141617192021] and two critically low quality.[89] Common methodological limitations included a lack of protocol registration, incomplete evaluation of publication bias, and insufficient consideration of bias in result interpretation. Characteristics of included reviews

The comparators varied substantially across the 14 included reviews. ITM was the comparator in seven reviews.[891213151819] A sham block or placebo injection was used in five reviews.[89151721] Wound infiltration served as the comparator in two reviews.[1116] QLB was compared in two reviews,[1013] and IIN–IHN block appeared in one review.[20] No-block control was included in three reviews.[91317] All included reviews synthesized RCTs, except one,[20] which included both RCTs and observational studies. For the current umbrella reanalysis, only RCT data were extracted and included.

Primary outcomes

The TAPB was associated with a statistically significant reduction in 24-hour pain scores: at rest (SMD = −0.11, 95% CI;−0.20, −0.02, I² =55%), on movement (SMD = −0.12, 95% CI;−0.21, −0.03, I² =60%), and opioid consumption during the first 24 hours (SMD = −0.19, 95% CI;−0.34, −0.04, I² =84%). All three endpoints achieved Class IV evidence, suggesting that TAPB confers weak or nominally significant analgesic benefits in the immediate postoperative period.

Secondary outcomes

No significant differences were found in 6-hour or 12-hour pain scores (rest or movement) or time-to-first rescue analgesia (all P > 0.05). PONV occurred less frequently after TAPB, though the pooled estimate did not reach statistical significance (eG = −0.81, 95% CI: −1.69, 0.07) but demonstrated high heterogeneity (I² =96%).

None of the pooled effect estimates for pain outcomes reached the established minimal clinically important difference, indicating that observed effects, although statistically significant, were unlikely to be clinically meaningful.[32]

Heterogeneity and publication bias

Across all pooled analyses, heterogeneity ranged from moderate to high (I² =55–96%). Contributing factors included differences in study designs, block techniques, comparator interventions, differences in the control group, and MMA protocols. Egger’s regression tests were nonsignificant (P > 0.100), indicating the absence of small-study effects.

Evidence credibility and certainty

Using the Ioannidis framework, the 24-hour pain scores (rest and movement) and 24-hour opioid-consumption outcomes achieved Class IV (weak) evidence credibility, while all other outcomes were classified as nonsignificant. GRADE certainty for all outcomes was very weak, primarily due to high heterogeneity, inconsistency, risk of bias, and imprecision.

Discussion

This umbrella review provides a consolidated evaluation of the analgesic efficacy and safety of the TAPB for CD analgesia, synthesizing evidence from 14 SRMAs.[89101112131415161718192021] Despite the volume of literature, the overall credibility of evidence remains weak, and certainty is low, owing to methodological limitations, heterogeneity, and overlap among included SRMAs. ITM demonstrated modestly superior 24-hour analgesia and reduced opioid consumption compared with TAPB. However, the absolute differences were small, corresponding to less than 1 point on a 0–10 pain scale, which is below the accepted minimal clinically important difference for acute postoperative pain.[33] This suggests that the statistical superiority of ITM may not translate into a clearly perceptible difference for many women. TAPB consistently showed a favorable safety profile, particularly regarding opioid-related adverse effects, highlighting a trade-off between slightly better analgesia with ITM and better tolerability with TAPB.

Efficacy of TAPB in the presence and absence of ITM

When ITM was incorporated in the analgesic regimen, TAPB generally produced inferior or comparable pain relief at 24 hours, reflecting the well-established potency of neuraxial opioids. In contrast, multiple SRMAs and RCTs indicate that TAPB provides clinically meaningful analgesia when ITM is not used,[89101112141516172021] particularly for CD performed under general anesthesia or when neuraxial opioids are contraindicated. Thus, while ITM remains the benchmark for early postoperative analgesia, its clinical advantage over TAPB is modest and must be weighed against higher rates of opioid-related adverse effects, including nausea, vomiting, and pruritus.[34]

Safety and side-effect profile

A consistent finding across reviews was the reduction in opioid-related adverse events with TAPB, particularly PONV and pruritus. Although the pooled OR for PONV did not reach statistical significance, the consistent direction of effect across SRMAs favored TAPB over the control group. This aligns with the mechanistic rationale: TAPB provides somatic abdominal wall analgesia without neuraxial opioid exposure. This safety benefit is especially relevant in women with prior severe PONV, opioid intolerance, or a preference to avoid neuraxial opioids.

Relation to guideline recommendations

Updated PROSPECT 2023 recommendations continue to endorse neuraxial opioids (particularly ITM) as the primary neuraxial analgesic for perioperative analgesia for CD, complemented by paracetamol, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and intravenous dexamethasone as part of MMA.[35] Importantly, when ITM is not administered, PROSPECT recommends alternatives such as LA wound infiltration or fascial plane blocks, including TAPB, ESPB, and QLB. The ERAS Society similarly highlights that although ITM provides several hours of analgesia, they are frequently associated with nausea, vomiting, and pruritus; when omitted, TAPB is endorsed as an effective alternative supported by Cochrane evidence.[36]

The findings of this umbrella review align with these recommendations: ITM confers only a modest analgesic advantage yet carries a higher risk of opioid-related adverse effects, while TAPB offers a safer profile and remains a valuable option when ITM is contraindicated, declined, or not used. As higher-quality evidence emerges, TAPB may assume an expanded role in opioid-sparing ERAC pathways and individualized post-cesarean analgesia strategies.

Comparisons with other RA techniques

Comparisons between TAPB and other RA techniques showed variable findings. TAPB demonstrated no clear advantage over wound infiltration, reflecting overlapping somatic coverage. In contrast, limited evidence suggests that more posterior fascial plane blocks, such as QLB and ESPB, may provide broader somatic–visceral spread and potentially longer analgesic duration and opioid-sparing effects. However, these findings are based on small, heterogeneous datasets with inconsistent outcomes; therefore, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn.

Methodological limitations and evidence redundancy

More than half of the included reviews were low or critically low in quality according to the AMSTAR-2 tool, often lacking protocol registration, comprehensive bias assessment, or publication-bias evaluation. The CCA of 8.33% demonstrated moderate overlap among reviews, indicating frequent reuse of the same core RCTs. Such redundancy reduces the independence of synthesized results and may inflate the perceived strength of the evidence. These limitations highlight the need for more rigorous, standardized, and protocol-driven evidence synthesis.

Clinical implications

TAPB remains an important component of MMA for CD. Although ITM provides slightly superior early analgesia, the magnitude of benefit is small, and TAPB offers a more favorable adverse-effect profile. Recent evidence also shows that while ITM modestly improves several domains of obstetric quality of recovery, including mobilization, physical comfort, and the ability to care for the newborn, it simultaneously increases pruritus and other opioid-related effects, emphasizing the need for balanced, individualized analgesic choices.[37] TAPB is therefore especially useful in situations where ITM is contraindicated, declined, or not preferred, such as women at high risk for PONV, those with prior intolerance to neuraxial opioids, or patients undergoing CD under general anesthesia. These findings support the individualized selection of analgesic techniques and highlight the role of TAPB within opioid-sparing ERAC pathways.

This umbrella review is limited by the quality and heterogeneity of the included SRMAs and their primary RCTs. Although reviewing tools such as AMSTAR-2, CCA, Ioannidis criteria, and GRADE were applied, the credibility of pooled estimates remains constrained. Frequent overlap of primary studies reduces the independence of the findings. Variability in TAP technique, comparator interventions, anesthetic regimens, and MMA protocols contributed to substantial heterogeneity. Inconsistent outcome reporting and the absence of standardized core outcome sets further limited assessment of maternal satisfaction, functional recovery, and breastfeeding outcomes.

Future research should focus on conducting large, high-quality RCTs using standardized TAPB techniques and MMA pathways. Direct comparisons between TAP, QLB, ESPB, and a combination of fascial plane blocks are needed. Individual patient data meta-analyses may help identify subgroups of women most likely to benefit from each technique. The development and adoption of core outcome sets for maternal satisfaction, early mobility, breastfeeding outcomes, and functional recovery will improve comparability across studies. Further exploration of the transversalis fascia plane block and novel adjuvants may enhance block performance and prolong analgesic duration.[38]

Conclusion

TAPB consistently demonstrated improved tolerability with reduced adverse events like nausea and vomiting, and thus remains an important option in individualized MMA strategies for CD. Well-designed primary trials and methodologically robust SRMAs are needed to refine future practice recommendations.
