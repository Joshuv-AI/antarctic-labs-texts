Empirical Drug Dosage Validates Pharmacogenomic Associations in All of Us
Authors: Jan Matthias, Maria J Falaguera, Russ B Altman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe All of Us research program, a national longitudinal study conducted by the US National Institutes of Health, provides robust medical history, drug dosage and genomic data from a diverse population. All of Us offers an opportunity to discover novel correlations between drug dosage and genetic variation. However, first it is necessary to evaluate the quality and quantity of the data and its ability to replicate known associations. In this paper, we investigate whether known drug‐gene interactions can be recovered from the All of Us dataset, based on data from electronic health records. Focusing on the Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme family, which metabolizes approximately 90% of clinically available drugs, we evaluate 61 drugs metabolized by the enzymes. We then identify significant differences in drug dosages across CYP450 metabolizer phenotypes. Our results validate some known interactors of CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A5. However, we did not recover all validated PGx interactions, potentially due to noise, lack of doctors adjusting drug dosage or phenoconversion. Nevertheless, our findings highlight the potential of the All of Us dataset, which captures some known pharmacogenomic interactions.Keywords: CYP, dose response, pharmacogenomics

Introduction

Pharmacogenomics (PGx) explores the relationship between genetic variation and drug metabolism. Variants in genes encoding drug‐metabolizing enzymes, such as the Cytochrome P450 family (CYP450), influence drug pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics, leading to an altered drug response. Traditionally, PGx effects are studied through in vitro and in vivo experiments [1] which are deposited into the Pharmacogenomic Knowledgebase (PharmGKB) [2], and used to define guidelines for clinicians by the Clinical Pharmacogenetics Implementation Consortium (CPIC) [3]. In many cases, although no PGx data or guidelines are available, clinicians will incidentally adjust patient drug dosage due to lack of therapeutic response or the presence of adverse effects. The underlying cause for such changes is in part due to the genetic profile of the patient [4].

Among the most critical determinants of interindividual variability in drug response is the Cytochrome P450 (CYP450) enzyme family, with five of its members–CYP2D6, CYP2C9, CYP2C19, CYP3A4, and CYP1A2–being responsible for collectively metabolizing approximately 90% of all FDA‐approved drugs [5]. To systematically categorize and study genetic variations, researchers use the “star” allele nomenclature system. This is the most widely used approach for naming genetic haplotypes in the PGx field. The star allele system assigns a unique star number to specific genetic haplotypes, which represent sets of genetic variants that are inherited together [6]. Based on these haplotypes, CPIC guidelines categorize individuals into five metabolizer phenotypes [7]: poor, intermediate, normal, rapid and ultrarapid metabolizers. Metabolizer phenotype classification has direct implications in clinical decision making. For example, codeine is metabolized into its active form by CYP2D6 [8]. Patients who have two inactive copies of CYP2D6 (poor metabolizers), may have inadequate pain relief, leading to a change of pain reliever [9].

Recently, we validated known pharmacogenomic interactions using the UK Biobank data, thus demonstrating the utility of large biobanks for PGx analysis [10]. However, like many PGx studies, this research was primarily focused on the European population [11, 12], which represents 95% of UK Biobank participants [13], thereby overlooking large populations with potentially distinct pharmacogenomics profiles and different adverse drug reactions. This is not trivial, since, as reported in several studies [14, 15, 16], inter‐ethnic genetic variability can influence drug metabolizer properties and hinder their effectiveness. A well‐documented example of this is the CYP2C19 enzyme which exhibits significant differences across ethnic groups [17]: approximately 57% of Asian and Oceananian populations carry a CYP2C19 loss of function polymorphism [18], which makes them more susceptible to recurrent coronary artery events with clopidogrel [19].

In this context, the All of Us (AoU) biobank, which contains 51% of individuals who identify as non‐European, offers a promising alternative for PGx studies in a more genetically diverse cohort [20]. In this study we aim to determine whether pharmacogenomic interactions can be recovered using data from the All of Us Biobank. Specifically we focus on two main components: (1) to assess the degree to which we can validate known PGx interactions of CYP450 enzymes and (2) explore the potential of novel PGx interactions between CYP‐metabolized drugs and CYP450 enzymes. This study emphasizes the value of large biobanks in PGx studies and confirms that the AoU dataset, despite not being explicitly designed for this purpose, contains some valuable PGx information.

Methods

Data Source and Inclusion Criteria

We use data from the All of Us Research Program's Controlled Tier Dataset V8, with data collected between summer 2018 and October 1st 2023. The All of Us Research Program by the National Institutes of Health represents one of the most comprehensive efforts to build a diverse genomic resource. It integrates the corresponding electronic health records (EHR), including drug dosage information, allowing for a real‐world validation and exploration of PGx effects.

We derive metabolizer phenotypes for the CYP450 enzymes using the predicted phenotype calls available on the All of Us V8 platform and we include CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP2B6, CYP3A5, and CYP4F2 (All‐of‐Us‐Pharmacogenomics‐Star‐Allele‐Calling). Briefly, the AoU platform determines CYP2D6 star alleles and gene copy number using Cyrius v1.1.1. Subsequently, it assigns structural variation nomenclature and phenotype annotations via the cyp2d6_parser package. For the remaining CYP enzymes, the platform analyzes short‐read whole genome sequences (srWGS) data using Stargazer 2.0.2 to call star alleles and predict enzyme phenotype.

We include participants who have at least one characterized CYP450 metabolizer phenotype, along with data on sex, BMI, age, ancestral principal component analysis (PCA), drug dosage and the end time of their drug exposure. We calculate age using the participant's date of birth and the end date of their last prescription, which is directly provided within the All of Us dataset. We obtain ancestral PCA and predicted ancestry from the AoU research platform (All‐of‐Us‐Genomic‐data). Whereas we derive the patient's race and ethnicity from respectively the race or ethnicity columns from AoU (Race‐and‐Ethnicity‐Data‐Collection‐and‐Transformation).

Drug List and Maintenance Dose Calculations

We derive the list of CYP450‐interacting drugs (Table S1) from the CPIC curated list of drugs (cpic_gene‐drug_pairs.xlsx), downloaded on May 4th 2025, with CPIC guideline annotation and PharmGKB clinical annotation level of evidence. A drug is included in the PGx analysis if it has CPIC guideline annotation linking it to a CYP450 family enzyme. To ensure comprehensive coverage, drugs metabolized by multiple enzymes are included in the study. This process results in a list of known drugs metabolized by CYP450 enzymes.

We calculate maintenance drug dose by averaging the daily milligram dosage across the last five observations. More specifically, we calculate the daily drug quantity by dividing the total prescription quantity by the number of days supplied. Daily drug quantity is then multiplied by the dose per unit to obtain the daily drug dosage. The maintenance dose is defined as the average of the last five daily drug doses for each patient. If the 5th prescription shares the same end date with subsequent prescriptions, all tied prescriptions are included, and we calculate the maintenance dose as the average of these. For liquid formulations, we calculate dose per unit by multiplying volume times concentration. Whereas for inhalation products, we standardize the prescription quantity to account for the number of actuations per inhaler device. We exclude outliers with the interquartile range method (IQR). We calculate the first (Q1) and third (Q3) quartiles based on the 25th and 75th percentiles of the maintenance dose distribution. We calculate the IQR as the difference between Q3 and Q1 and remove maintenance drug doses which fall below Q1 − 1.5 × IQR or above Q3 + 1.5 × IQR. As a complementary filtration strategy, we also apply a more lenient outlier filtration by setting Q1 and Q3 to the 15th and 85th percentiles. We exclude combinations formulations containing two active ingredients from these calculations. Furthermore, we exclude inhalation drugs without information on the number of inhalations per device and eye drop formulations as the dose per unit is not provided.

CYP450 PGx Validation

As a first step, we validate established pharmacogenomic interactions between drugs and CYP450 enzymes. The CYP450 enzyme phenotypes from the AoU dataset are in five distinct metabolizer bins: poor, intermediate, normal, rapid, and ultrarapid, which reflect increasing enzyme activity. We exclude phenotype calls labeled as “likely” (i.e., likely intermediate metabolizer), “unknown” and “indeterminate”. We aggregate phenotypes into three bins: poor/intermediate<normal<rapid/ultrarapid. However, for genes where this aggregation was not feasible due to the absence of ultrarapid and rapid phenotypes, we analyze differences in three bins: poor<intermediate<normal. We include only drugs in the statistical analysis that have at least 50 unique patients. Additionally, we exclude drugs that do not have at least 10 unique data points per aggregated metabolizer phenotype or show no variance in maintenance dose within phenotypes.

For statistical testing for associations between maintenance dose and metabolizer phenotype we use the Kruskal–Wallis (KW) (Scipy.stats 1.11.4) and the Jonckheere–Terpstra (JT) test (Clinfun 1.1.5). The KW test evaluates whether there is a statistically significant difference in the median maintenance drug dose across different metabolizers. In contrast, the JT test extends this analysis by incorporating the ordered nature of the metabolizer phenotypes. To account for data ties in the Jonckheere–Terpstra, we perform 1000 permutations to obtain an accurate p‐value. We adjust all maintenance drug dosages to age, sex, BMI and ancestry PCA through linear regression. Each drug is then tested individually for dose differences across phenotypes. Multiple testing corrections are not applied, as the analyses aim to validate existing associations.

Population Subset Analysis

In a second step, we aim to assess how many pharmacogenomic interactions can be recovered using specific subsets of the entire cohort. More specifically, we investigate which PGx interactions can be recovered in participants with self‐reported race as White, African American or Black and of self‐reported ethnicity Hispanic or Latino. For the statistical analysis we apply the same methodology as described above.

Drug Use Duration Across Metabolizer Phenotype

We additionally assess whether the duration of drug use is associated with metabolizer phenotype. For this, we define drug use duration as the time interval between the end of a participant's first prescription and the end date of their last prescription. The cohorts in this analysis are the same as in Methods 2.4, ensuring consistency with the main PGx analysis. To evaluate differences in duration across metabolizer phenotypes, we apply the Kruskal–Wallis test. As the threshold for significance, we use a Bonferroni‐corrected p‐value.

Novel Association Discovery

To identify previously unreported pharmacogenomic interactions, we test all drugs included in this study against each of the CYP450 enzymes. Here we aim to uncover potential novel pharmacogenomic interactions, not currently recognized in CPIC guidelines. Statistical analysis was conducted with the above‐mentioned methodology, and due to the discovery nature of these statistical tests, we apply a Bonferroni correction to adjust p‐values for multiple hypothesis testing.

If an enzyme‐drug pair is significant, we further validate this potential novel interaction with the UK Biobank, utilizing previously published CYP450 phenotype calls [21]. We estimate the number of days supplied based on the time between prescriptions and calculate maintenance drug dose using the same approach as in the All of Us dataset. We then adjust maintenance dose using linear regression with covariates: sex, BMI, age, principal component 1 (PC1), and genotyping array. For statistical testing, we apply the same Kruskal–Wallis and Jonckheere–Terpstra methods described above.

Drugs Utilized in the Analysis and Drug Exposed Cohort

In total, our drug list comprises 129 unique drugs across 8 CYP enzymes: CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, CYP3A5, CYP3A4, CYP2B6, CYP4F2, and CYP2C8. Among these, CYP2D6 is involved in the metabolism of 78 drugs, whereas CYP2C19 has 27 drugs. A complete list of drugs and their corresponding CYP enzymes is provided in Table S1. Based on drug inclusion criteria, which require a minimum of 50 participants with at least 10 for each aggregated metabolizer phenotype, we test 61 drugs from the initial 129 drugs.

Known Pharmacogenomic Interactions

In a first step we carry out statistical testing to validate known CYP450‐drug associations across the entire AoU dataset. Due to our inclusion criteria, we test 50 known drug‐gene interactions. We successfully validate propranolol and metoprolol as known interactors of the CYP2D6 enzyme (Table 3). Furthermore we validate paroxetine as an interactor of CYP2C19 while confirming the interactions of warfarin with CYP2C9, as well as tacrolimus with CYP3A5.

Population Subset Analysis

In the subset of participants self‐identifying as White, we test 40 drug‐gene pairs and validate citalopram and metoprolol as CYP2D6 substrates, esomeprazole and paroxetine as CYP2C19 substrates, and warfarin as a CYP2C9 interactor. Among Black or African American participants, we validate sertraline, tramadol and venlafaxine out of the 33 statistical tests. For the Hispanic or Latino subset (self‐reported ethnicity), out of 14 tests, we validate omeprazole as a CYP2C19 interactor. Complete results from the analysis are available in Table S2, and the mean dose and standard deviation of the drugs analyzed in the four subsets are available in Figure S1. In addition, Table S3 reports the significant validated PGx interactions across all subsets, when applying the more lenient outlier filtration strategy. With this broader outlier filtration, we additionally validate doxepin, oxycodone and atorvastatin but the associations with tacrolimus and esomeprazole are not retained.

Discussion

Here we aim to validate known interactions and discover novel pharmacogenomic effects using AoU participants, focusing on CYP450 enzymes. One of the main aims of our work was to utilize a diverse cohort, which we achieved with a total of 64,924 participants, of whom 52.65% self‐reported as White and 21.48% as Black or African American. This is in stark contrast to PGx studies carried out by [22, 23] utilizing the UKBB where approximately 93% of participants were from European descent.

Due to our drug inclusion criteria—requiring at least 5 observations per patient, a minimum of 10 patients per aggregated metabolizer phenotype, and a minimum of 50 patients per drug—the number of unique drugs tested was reduced from 129 to 61. We averaged the last five drug dosages for each patient based on the assumption that clinicians adjust drug dosages in response to non‐responsiveness or the presence of adverse side effects. These most recent prescriptions are more likely to reflect PGx influences. Moreover, metabolizers which were labeled as “likely” intermediate or poor metabolizers were removed, as limited data is available to characterize their function [24]. The “likely”, “unknown” or “indeterminate” metabolizer phenotype status was primarily present in patients who self‐identified as Black or African American, with a median prevalence of 1.18% across all enzymes, compared with 0.04% in the White subset. When including the “likely” metabolizers (i.e., “likely intermediate” was treated as “intermediate”) within the validated PGx associations, it did not alter the recovered PGx interactions. However, the p‐value for the CYP2C19–venlafaxine association within the Black or African American subset decreased, suggesting that the “likely” classification may in fact reflect a true underlying phenotype.

Our statistical analysis examines differences across phenotypes, both with and without considering the ordered nature of the metabolizer phenotypes. Testing for the order is important, as an ultrarapid metabolizer will have higher enzymatic activity, leading to a faster drug metabolism and clearance [25]. Consequently, clinicians often prescribe higher dosages to these individuals due to their reduced drug responses. In principle, such changes, when they match the metabolizer phenotype, should be detectable by our statistical analysis.

We successfully validated known interactions for both the CYP2D6, CYP2C19, CYP2C9, and CYP3A5 enzymes. However, not all expected interactions reached statistical significance. When observing factors such as sample size, PharmGKB level of evidence or a CPIC guideline annotation, there is a trend favoring PGx interactions that have a stronger PharmGKB level of evidence with a larger sample size. However it should be noted that not all highly validated PGx interactions are significant. One possible explanation is that the drugs showing significant associations may have had less noise in the data. This is consistent with our observation that non‐significant interactions tended not to show clear dosage trends across metabolizer groups.

Another contributing factor may be the inherent difficulty of capturing therapeutic efficacy or adverse effects for some drugs [10]. This limitation can reduce the likelihood of dose adjustments, as clinicians may have insufficient information to guide changes. In contrast, drugs with clear, easily measurable outcomes are more likely to undergo frequent dose adjustments. For instance, warfarin dosing is commonly adjusted during the initial phase by monitoring the international normalized ratio (INR) [26]. Notably, among the validated PGx interactions, three involve cardiovascular agents (propranolol, metoprolol, and warfarin), which may be more frequently adjusted due to the importance of treatment and availability of straightforward biomarkers to assess therapeutic response. Warfarin response is also influenced by VKORC1 [27]; however, because the phenotype calls are provided as binary categories, similar to CYP4F2, we did not include it in our analysis.

One further explanation for the non‐replication of known PGx associations is that they may not generalize to a broader population. To investigate this, we tested whether a European‐only subset, on which many PGx interactions were discovered [28], would yield higher replication rates. However, we found no evidence of improved validations, suggesting that factors such as data noise and lack of dose adjustments in response to adverse effects may better explain the lack of replication. Interestingly, different pharmacogenomic interactions are replicated within specific subsets of the study cohort, suggesting that certain populations may be more susceptible to PGx effects. Our analysis utilized self‐reported race to create the subset, as predicted ancestry may misclassify individuals, particularly those with admixed or underrepresented backgrounds [29]. To account for genetic differences across participants, we adjusted all drug dose analyses for genetic ancestry using principal components.

As a next step we sought to explore novel drug‐gene PGx interactions within the All of Us dataset. The most promising association, based on the lowest p‐value across both statistical tests, was between meloxicam‐CYP2B6 and naproxen‐CYP3A5. However, this association did not replicate with the UKBB. Meloxicam, a non‐steroidal anti‐inflammatory drug used to treat rheumatic diseases [30], is primarily metabolized by CYP2C9 into a 5‐hydroxymethyl metabolite, with a minor contribution by CYP3A4 [31]. Naproxen is also primarily metabolized by CYP2C9 [32]. It is possible that CYP2B6 and CYP3A5 are involved with a downstream metabolite of meloxicam and naproxen. However, future research is needed to further investigate such interactions and their potential clinical implications.

The AoU platform provides a valuable opportunity to link genomic data to clinical data but its effectiveness is limited by the availability of the participants' medical records. Although we considered 129 drugs metabolized across 5 distinct CYP450 enzymes, many had to be excluded due to insufficient drug dosage data. Additionally, for some included drugs, small cohort sizes may have limited the statistical power of our tests, potentially leading to an underestimation of the true PGx effects in the dataset. Furthermore when removing drug dosage outliers we apply a broad filtration to all drugs rather than implementing a customized filtration specific to each drug. Our sensitivity analysis comparing strict versus lenient filtration approaches showed that while a core set of associations was robust, additional associations were dependent on the filtration threshold applied. However, future releases of data from the AoU platform, might provide a more complete view of the PGx interactions. Moreover, we do not consider the possibility of cytochrome P450 phenoconversion. Here the metabolizer phenotype of an individual can change due to a variety of reasons [33], such as the co‐administration of drugs metabolized by CYP450 enzymes [34]. For example, a rapid metabolizer might change phenotype to an intermediate one, thus altering the drug metabolism.

Despite these challenges, the All of Us dataset offers opportunities for future pharmacogenomic research. The dataset contains genomic data from a highly diverse population, creating the opportunity to identify PGx variants that may be enriched in certain populations. Furthermore, we did not investigate drug side effects in this analysis, which may also be influenced by underlying PGx effects.

Our findings demonstrate the potential value of large biobanks for the validation of PGx interactions. We confirm that some known associations replicate in a diverse population and observe that certain subgroups may be more susceptible to specific PGx effects. However not all known associations replicate, thus requiring further analysis. These results further underscore the role of PGx in precision medicine.
