Echocardiography assessment of aortic elasticity as a vascular marker in hypertensive vs. healthy individuals
Authors: Mahboobeh Gholipour, Mani Moayerifar, Maryam Jafari, Fatemeh Baharvand, Fatemeh Mirrazeghi, Mohammadreza Baay, Mohammad Ali Yazdanipour, Nona Shahmansourian, Ghazaleh Aghaei, Golshan Ghasemzadeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Hypertension contributes to arterial stiffness and may promote early atherosclerosis. Assessing aortic elasticity non-invasively could help identify early vascular changes before clinical atherosclerotic disease manifests. This study aimed to compare aortic elasticity parameters between hypertensive and healthy individuals using echocardiography to evaluate their association with hypertension and potential value in early vascular risk assessment. In this case-control study, 140 participants aged 30–60 were divided into two equal groups: newly diagnosed hypertensive patients and had no significant difference (P value > 0.05) in age- and sex with healthy controls. Transthoracic echocardiography was used to assess aortic elasticity indices, including aortic strain (relative change in diameter), distensibility (diameter change per pressure change), stiffness index (pressure-diameter relationship), and Peterson’s elastic modulus (a measure of arterial wall resistance to deformation). Logistic regression and ROC analysis were performed to assess diagnostic value. Hypertensive patients showed significantly lower strain and distensibility and higher stiffness index and elastic modulus compared to controls (p < 0.001). These parameters demonstrated high diagnostic performance, with area under the curve (AUC) values of 0.97–0.99 for identifying hypertensive individuals, suggesting their potential utility in detecting early vascular stiffening. Echocardiographic measures of aortic elasticity differ significantly between hypertensive and healthy individuals. These indices may serve as useful, non-invasive markers for early vascular changes associated with hypertension. However, as this was an observational study, further longitudinal research is warranted to confirm their predictive value for atherosclerosis. The correlation between increased stiffness and inflammatory markers such as CRP suggests that further research is warranted to explore additional inflammatory indices.

Introduction

Hypertension (HT) is described as having a systolic BP (SBP) greater than 140 mmHg, diastolic BP (DBP) greater than 90 mmHg, or being treated with antihypertensive medications1. Hypertension is often clinically silent in its early stages but may present with symptoms such as headaches or palpitations, dizziness and visual disturbance. Management can be complex, especially in resistant cases and it can lead to fatal complications if neglected2. High blood pressure accounts for a significant portion of the global burden of disease and it is the second most common cause of death in developed regions, accounting for 10.9% of deaths from non-communicable diseases and traumas3. As people age, their risk of developing hypertension rises, and the prevalence is 65.4% among those over 604. Hypertension was more common in low- and middle-income countries compared to high-income countries. In high-income regions, men had a higher prevalence than women. In contrast, in low- and middle-income areas, the rates were similarly high for both men and women5.However, the awareness, treatment, and control rates for hypertension in developing countries are increasingly approaching those found in developed regions6. Ventricular hypertrophy, heart failure, and accelerated atherosclerosis are among the consequences of uncontrolled hypertension7.

Atherosclerosis is an immunoinflammatory, multifactorial, slowly progressive condition that affects full spectrum of vascular territories including small muscular arteries such as coronaries which are clinically significant sites of disease. And is fueled by lipids. The main contributors to the development of this disease are endothelial cells, leukocytes, and intimal smooth muscle cells. It is the most common culprit behind coronary artery disease, carotid artery disease, and peripheral arterial disease8. The prevalence of atherosclerosis is 10.0% in African Americans and 8.4% in Whites9. The incidence of ischemic stroke, ischemic heart disease, and peripheral artery disease as the three clinical manifestations of atherosclerosis increased globally between 1990 and 2019. On the other hand, the death rate from atherosclerosis has been steadily decreasing. This decrease seems linked to improvements in medical technology that enable the identification and management of the condition earlier10. Atherosclerotic plaques in men tend to exhibit features such as larger lipid cores, thinner fibrous caps, and increased inflammatory cell infiltration, contributing to their instability.

The total area affected by plaque is larger in males, although individual plaque stenosis can be more significant in females11. Aortic atherosclerosis can be visibly seen, while coronary and cerebral arteries show varying levels of lumen stenosis on cross-sections under a light microscope12. In contrast to large muscular arteries like the brachial and radial arteries, the negative correlation between cardiovascular disease (CVD) outcomes and arterial stiffening, may be unique to the aorta13. Hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, and smoking are among the risk factors for both atherosclerosis and aortic stiffness that can also cause endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation14.

The aorta plays a central role in buffering the pulsatile output of the heart and maintaining steady blood flow. Its elastic properties are critical to cardiovascular function, and diminished aortic elasticity has been associated with adverse outcomes in hypertensive individuals. Aortic elasticity reflects the arterial wall’s ability to expand and recoil with each cardiac cycle, and it can be quantified using echocardiographic parameters such as compliance (the absolute change in aortic diameter per unit pressure), distensibility (relative diameter change per pressure), strain (percent change in diameter during systole), stiffness index (a pressure-independent measure of arterial rigidity), and Peterson’s elastic modulus (a pressure-normalized metric of wall stiffness)15.Recent studies suggest that parameters including compliance (C) which indicates the aorta’s ability to expand under pressure, distensibility (D), which measures how much the aorta can stretch relative to its diameter and pressure changes, strain (S), stiffness index (SI) that provides insight into arterial stiffness, an important predictor of cardiovascular risk, and Peterson’s elastic modulus (EM) can provide valuable insights in preclinical atherosclerosis research16. When the aorta’s elasticity is compromised, it is unable to lessen the significant pulsatile pressure generated by the left ventricle, resulting in increased strain on the cardiovascular system15.

Several prior studies have examined the impact of hypertension on arterial stiffness, including the use of pulse wave velocity (PWV) and augmentation index (AIx)17.However, fewer investigations have focused specifically on echocardiographic assessments of ascending aortic elasticity in newly diagnosed, untreated hypertensive patients, and even fewer have comprehensively compared these indices to healthy controls using a uniform protocol. This represents a critical gap in non-invasive cardiovascular risk stratification.

Echocardiography is a widely accessible, low-cost, and non-invasive modality that enables real-time assessment of aortic dimensions and elasticity15. This study aims to investigate differences in ascending aortic elasticity between hypertensive patients and healthy individuals using echocardiography and to evaluate the diagnostic potential of elasticity indices in identifying early vascular changes indicative of atherosclerosis.

Methods and materials

Study participants

This study is a case-control study within two groups, each consisting of 70 participants aged between 30 and 60 years old collected with consecutive sampling who visited the Heshmat Educational and Medical Cardiology Center in Rasht, Iran, with a final diagnosis of essential hypertension (EHTN). The included participants either have recently been diagnosed with hypertension or also have not yet undergone antihypertensive medication treatment, or they have had hypertension for less than five years and do not have cardiovascular risk factors.

Control group selection

The control group consisted of 70 healthy individuals recruited through consecutive sampling from patients presenting for routine medical examinations at the same cardiology center. The groups were not significantly different in terms of age and sex. To ensure the control group represented a truly healthy population, all participants underwent a screening process that included clinical history, physical examination, resting blood pressure measurement (SBP < 120 mmHg and DBP < 80 mmHg), normal electrocardiogram (ECG), and normal laboratory findings including fasting blood glucose, lipid profile, and C-reactive protein (CRP). Individuals with a history of hypertension, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, active heavy smoking(more than 25 cigarette per days), or use of antihypertensive or lipid-lowering medications were excluded.

Our study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the ethical committee of Guilan University of Medical Sciences (IR.GUMS.REC.1402.095).

Inclusion/Exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria include: absence of a history of vascular diseases, normal findings in electrocardiography, and chest X-rays. The exclusion criteria also include diagnosis of secondary hypertension, diabetes, congenital heart disease, cardiomyopathy, aortic disease, cerebrovascular disease, renal disease, moderate to severe heart valve regurgitation and severe arrhythmia.

Clinical data collection and biochemistry assessment

Past medical history and physical examination that included measurements of height and weight, body mass index (BMI), and smoking history (less than 15 cigarette per day) were conducted in this study. For analysis of glucose and lipid profile, Fasting blood samples were collected, and fasting plasma glucose (FPG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), total cholesterol (TCH), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were measured.

During echocardiography, the brachial artery’s blood pressure was assessed using a mercury sphygmomanometer. Systolic blood pressure (SBP) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) were recorded in the supine position after 10 min of rest in a quiet environment. The interval between the two measurements was more than 5 min. Based on SBP and DBP, mean arterial pressure (MAP) and pulse pressure (PP) were calculated.

An expert cardiologist performed conventional echocardiography on all participants using the Philips iE33 ultrasound system. M-mode echocardiography was used to measure parameters within the parasternal long-axis view. Participants lay on their left side, and the left atrial volume index (LAVI), end-diastolic interventricular septal thickness (IVSD), left ventricular posterior wall thickness (LVPWD), left ventricular end-systolic diameter (LVESD) and left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVEDD) were measured. The modified Simpson’s rule calculated the left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF). In the apical four-chamber view, early diastolic trans-mitral velocities (Ve) and late diastolic velocities (Va) were assessed by sampling between the tips of the mitral leaflets through pulsed-wave Doppler. Subsequently, the ratio of Ve to Va was computed. All echocardiographic exams were in accordance with the American Society of Echocardiography standards.

To measure the inner diameter of the ascending aorta at the end of diastole, imaging in the parasternal long-axis view was used with M-mode and ascending aorta was examined 3 centimeters above the aortic ring. The aortic systolic diameter (AoS) was measured while the maximal anterior movement of the aorta, and the aortic diastolic diameter (AoD) was determined above the QRS complex. Throughout the echocardiography session, electrocardiogram leads were connected to the bodies of all participants.

In this study, the variables related to aortic elasticity are examined according to the following formula:

All echocardiographic assessments were performed by a single expert cardiologist. To assess reliability, 20 randomly selected images were re-analyzed, yielding an intra-observer agreement > 95% for key measurements.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis was performed by IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows (Version 22.0, Armonk, NY: IBM Corp.; 2013). Continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, whereas categorical data was expressed as frequency (percentage). The continuous variables’ normality assumption was first examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Normally distributed continuous variables were compared using Student t-test. Statistical significance of differences between groups was analysed by Mann–Whitney U test for non-parametric continuous variables and categorical variables were compared using the Pearson Chi-square test. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to determine a cut-off point of aortic elasticity indices for essential hypertension. A multiple logistic regression analyses (backward LR) were used to evaluate the association between the elasticity of the ascending aorta as a vascular marker with essential hypertension after adjusting confounding and intervening factors. A p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that specific echocardiographic indices of aortic elasticity—namely, increased stiffness index, aortic systolic and diastolic diameters, and Peterson’s elastic modulus—are significantly associated with essential hypertension, while our findings demonstrated increased aortic systolic and diastolic diameters (AoS and AoD) in hypertensive patients, it is important to note that these measurements alone do not directly quantify arterial stiffness. Instead, measures such as compliance, distensibility, and strain are notably reduced in hypertensive patients compared to healthy individuals. These findings suggest that aortic stiffness may serve as a non-invasive early marker of vascular remodeling and preclinical atherosclerosis in patients with hypertension. The alterations in elasticity parameters likely reflect underlying pathophysiological changes, including endothelial dysfunction, increased vascular smooth muscle tone, and extracellular matrix remodeling, all of which are key contributors to atherogenesis. By identifying these changes through echocardiography—a widely accessible and cost-effective imaging modality—clinicians may be able to detect vascular impairment earlier and implement preventive strategies before irreversible damage occurs compared to the study by Song et al.18, our findings show notable differences in several key parameters. While both studies identified significant changes in compliance, strain, stiffness index, and Peterson’s elastic modulus in hypertensive patients, Song et al. reported no significant differences in aortic systolic and diastolic diameters (AoS and AoD) or in echocardiographic cardiac function parameters between hypertensive and control groups. In contrast, our study found that AoS and AoD were significantly larger in hypertensive individuals, and we also observed lower LVEF, E, and E’ values, along with higher LAVI, IVSD, and LVPWD, indicating both structural and functional cardiac changes. These discrepancies may stem from differences in study populations, as Song et al. included participants with a history of antihypertensive drug use and matched clinical variables such as smoking and lipid profiles.Additionally, methodological differences in echocardiographic measurement protocols and the timing of assessments may have contributed to the divergent findings.

In line with our results, Jia et al.19 Also mentioned the change in compliance, distensibility, and stiffness index of the aorta in normal population, prehypertension, and hypertension patients. Jia et al. also noted that AD was more sensitive than AC and ASI. However, the procedure that assessed the aorta elasticity was computed using tomography angiography (CTA). Compared with our results, echocardiography can yield results similar to CTA. This indicated that without CTA, because of higher cost, less availability, and the same effectiveness, the aorta elasticity could be assessed.

Also, the same to our results, the aorta elasticity indices not only correlated with essential hypertension with high sensitivity and specificity but also in the Yildiz et al. study the results showed that the Aortic Distensibility with 88.2% sensitivity and 84.6% specificity with 0.94 AUC and the Aortic Strain with 82.4% sensitivity and 87.2% specificity with 0.873 AUC correlated with coronary artery disease20 and reinforcing the clinical value of these parameters as early indicators of cardiovascular risk.

Arterial elasticity and compliance are critical indicators of cardiovascular health, particularly in cardiac disease and early atherosclerosis. Hypertension can lead to changes in arterial structure asymptomatically; this process damages the endothelial cells, increases collagen production, and causes the breakdown of elastic fibers, all of which hinder the artery’s capacity to manage blood flow properly21,22.Although invasive pulse wave velocity (PWV) measurements are considered a reference standard for assessing arterial stiffness due to their high accuracy23non-invasive techniques such as oscillometric or tonometric PWV and augmentation index (AIx) are widely used in clinical practice and have demonstrated substantial validity. Evsen A et al.24. noticed that the role of non-invasive oscillometric method to detect aortic stiffness in patients with subclinical hypothyroidism.Each method offers unique strengths: invasive approaches provide direct hemodynamic data but are limited by procedural complexity and patient risk, while non-invasive methods offer practical, reproducible, and safer alternatives for broader patient populations.

Notably, echocardiography is a non-invasive imaging method that allows for the assessment of aortic size and blood flow patterns. It is commonly utilized to evaluate different cardiovascular issues, such as arterial stiffness25. In our study, echocardiography-based assessment of aortic elasticity was employed as a feasible, non-invasive modality with demonstrated correlation to vascular stiffness. While we did not directly compare our findings with PWV or Aix and CCTA but suggesting that ultrasound-based assessment can provide comparable diagnostic insights with greater accessibility. Future studies integrating these established parameters could offer a more comprehensive evaluation of vascular health and validate the added diagnostic value of echocardiographic indices in the context of early atherosclerosis.

Studies have shown that prehypertension can become hypertension within 4 years26. Also, high blood pressure can eventually lead to atherosclerosis by increasing the shear stress, stimulating inflammatory responses, and the likelihood of thrombus formation27. Due to the asymptomatic onset of atherosclerosis in these stages, blood pressure control in people with a higher chance of atherosclerosis and changes in the structure of blood vessels can reduce the involvement of people with atherosclerosis and cause these people to be identified as soon as possible. In the stages before the formation of atherosclerosis, there is a change in the structure of the vessels, including a change in the thickness of the intima-media and the formation of atheroma28. However, our study was in accordance with previous studies by addition excluding other factors affecting aortic elasticity showed that aortic elasticity is influenced by essential hypertension. This could confirm the onset of atherosclerosis before other clinical and imaging features. As a result, due to the availability, not expensive, and non-invasive nature of echocardiography, the detailed examination of aorta elasticity provides evidence for guidelines for preventive treatment of atherosclerosis along with lifestyle modification.

Numerous studies indicate that individuals with prehypertension are at a higher risk of developing issues related to obesity, blood glucose, and blood lipid levels. These problems can negatively affect arterial elasticity and the functionality of both the cardiovascular and cerebral vascular systems29,30. The objective of our research was to eliminate individuals with diabetes in order to reduce the potential impact of these variables. However, we found that body mass index (BMI), blood lipid levels, and fasting blood glucose levels were still higher in the essential hypertension group than in those with normal blood pressure. Also, smoking is a recognized risk factor for impaired vascular function, and the higher prevalence of smoking in the hypertensive group may have contributed to differences in aortic elasticity. However, because multivariate analyses adjusted for smoking status, the independent association between hypertension and reduced aortic elasticity remained robust.

Celik et al.31, which showed increased inflammatory markers and elasticity in prehypertension patients, we also found that CRP was significantly higher in EHTN patients. Elevated CRP levels in hypertensive patients in our study further support the link between vascular stiffness and systemic inflammation. Given that CRP is an established marker of cardiovascular risk, this association warrants further investigation. Inflammation is related to hypertension, and CRP is one of the inflammatory markers. Early screening and intervention of CRP may delay the progression of hypertension32. Future studies should explore the role of additional inflammatory biomarkers such as white blood cell count, neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, and procalcitonin to better characterize the inflammatory pathways contributing to early atherosclerosis in hypertension. Longitudinal studies incorporating these markers alongside aortic elasticity indices could provide a more comprehensive risk stratification model for early cardiovascular disease detection.

Strengths and limitation

Our study has some strengths and limitations. To our knowledge, we conducted our study with participants with no history of antihypertensive drugs. We also considered inflammatory markers like CRP and compared them between the case and control groups. We excluded all factors that can affect the elasticity of the aorta except hypertension. Notably, our study revealed the sensitivity and specificity of the aortic elasticity and the cutoff in essential hypertension patients. However, one of the limitations was the cross-sectional design and failure to follow up with patients and not identifying the existence and specific role of atheroma as a pre-atherosclerosis lesion. Also, the high AUC values likely reflect both the well-defined study population and the case-control design, and that further longitudinal and validation studies are required before translation into clinical practice .Emphasizing that inflammatory markers (e.g., CRP) and metabolic factors (e.g., FBS) could also influence vascular stiffness and should be considered in future work, conducting a longitudinal study with a large population and multicenter studies with sufficient statistical power can reveal a more reliable effect of change in aorta elasticity and we clarify the underlying exact mechanism and the order of change in elasticity factors to predict atherosclerosis.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study highlights the influence of essential hypertension on aortic elasticity compared to healthy individuals. While reduced elasticity may reflect early vascular changes, further longitudinal and imaging-based studies are needed to evaluate its role in predicting atherosclerosis. We found that decreased aortic elasticity is associated with hypertension, and this shows the importance of monitoring aortic elasticity in clinical practice, particularly among essential hypertensive patients, as it may provide valuable insights into starting atherosclerosis formation. Moreover, the results remain that echocardiographic techniques are non-invasive and effective in evaluating aorta elasticity. Further longitudinal studies should investigate the efficacy of monitoring and preventing the treatment of essential hypertension in atherosclerosis formation and aorta elasticity.
