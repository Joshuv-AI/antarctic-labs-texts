Diversity of stem endophytes communities from wild asparagus resources and the biocontrol potential on Phomopsis asparagi
Authors: Yuling Yin, Meng Fan, Qingling Zhang, Yanying Ye, Qixin Xie, Bingbing Zhang, Yongping Tang, Shaochun Luo, Jinsong Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAsparagus stem blight, caused by Phomopsis asparagi, is a devastating disease leading to significant yield losses in asparagus cultivation. Current control methods, such as greenhouse cultivation or extensive pesticide use, are either costly or environmentally unsustainable. Endophytes, residing within healthy plant tissues, offer a promising biocontrol alternative due to their antagonistic activities against pathogens.MethodologyThis study investigated the diversity and biocontrol potential of endophytic fungi and bacteria in the stems of one cultivated variety (ATL) and three wild asparagus resources (NFY, YAY, YDL). We employed high-throughput sequencing to analyze endophytic community composition and diversity. We conducted predictive functional profiling to assess potential metabolic capabilities. Additionally, endophytic strains were isolated from stem tissues and screened for antagonistic activity against P. asparagi using dual-culture assays.ResultsHigh-throughput sequencing revealed distinct endophytic communities between cultivated (ATL) and wild (NFY, YAY, YDL) asparagus. Wild varieties exhibited significantly higher microbial diversity and more unique Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs). Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) confirmed separate clustering, with wild samples grouping apart from ATL. Community composition differed markedly: in fungi, Russula was abundant in NFY, while Incertae_sedis dominated others; in bacteria, Pseudomonadota prevailed in wild types versus Armatimonadota in ATL. Functional prediction showed wild endophytes enriched in genes for direct antagonism (e.g., chitinase), stress resistance, and competition (e.g., ABC transporters, quorum sensing). Cultivated endophytes were geared toward core metabolism. From wild stems, 14 endophytic strains were isolated. Four bacterial isolates strongly inhibited Phomopsis asparagi (> 90% inhibition), with one (YDJ119602) achieving 100% inhibition via fermentation broth.ConclusionsIn conclusion, wild asparagus exhibits rich endophytic diversity and represents a promising source of biocontrol agents against stem blight, offering a sustainable disease management strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05333-9.

Background

Asparagus (Asparagus officinalis L.), a perennial herbaceous plant of the Asparagaceae family, is a popular medicinal and edible vegetable known as the “King of Vegetables“ [1]. As a high-value economic crop, it has been widely cultivated in China and remains highly popular both domestically and internationally. However, asparagus is a perennial plant, and continuous growth creates a unique field environment prone to diseases. Among these, asparagus stem blight is particularly devastating and is often termed the “cancer” of asparagus. This disease is caused by the fungus Phomopsis asparagi (Sacc.) Bubak and is highly destructive, capable of completely destroying entire fields, resulting in total crop loss and significant financial damage for growers [2, 3]. Asparagus stem blight occurs in almost all asparagus-producing regions in China. In practice, greenhouse rain-sheltered cultivation can help mitigate stem blight occurrence [4]. However, the construction and maintenance of such greenhouses significantly increase cultivation cost, raising the entry barrier for growers. Conversely, open-field cultivation requires extensive pesticides use to control stem blight, which not only increases production costs but also poses risks of environmental pollution and food safety concerns. Therefore, there is an urgent need to develop safe and effective methods for controlling asparagus stem blight.

Endophytes are microorganisms that live within the organs and tissues of healthy plants without causing obvious external symptoms of infection in the host [5]. They are natural components of the plant micro-ecosystem [6]. Endophytes have been isolated from a variety of plants. Through co-evolution with their hosts, they not only fulfill their own survival needs but also enhance the host’s adaptability to the external environment, establishing a mutually beneficial relationship [7–9].The antagonistic effect of endophytes refers to the ability of certain endophytes to inhibit the growth of pathogenic or other harmful microorganisms through metabolic products or competitive mechanisms, thereby protecting the host plants from disease infection [10]. This characteristic makes endophytes highly valuable for applications in agriculture, ecological restoration, and biological control [11].

Endophytes originate from the host plant or its ecological environment. Through long-term co-evolution, they have developed a high degree of adaptability to the host and can stably colonize and reproduce within plant tissues [12]. An important function of plant-associated bacteria is involvement in pathogen defense [13, 14]. They are better able to adapt to the micro-environment inside the host, once successfully established, they can potentially function throughout most or all the growing season, reducing the need for frequent applications [15–17]. Collective evidence demonstrates that endophytic bacteria and fungi, isolated from the stems or the roots of vegetable crops (e.g., tomato, potato, cucumber, pepper, lettuce, Chinese cabbage), exhibit potent antagonism against major fungal, bacterial, and oomycete pathogens (Fusarium oxysporum, Ralstonia solanacearum, Stem canker et al.). These endophytes, including genera such as Bacillus, Streptomyces, Pseudomonas, Paenibacillus, Trichoderma, Acinetobacter, Serratia, and Pantoea, funcitons through the production of antimicrobial compounds (e.g., lipopeptides, antibiotics, chitinases, bacteriocins), competition for niches, and the induction of systemic resistance in the host plant, leading to significant disease suppression across different pathosystems [18–23].

Healthy plants, particularly their wild resources, may harbor endophytes with highly efficient antagonistic activity [24]. Wild resources, having undergone long-term evolution and natural selection in their natural environments, generally possess greater genetic diversity and stronger stress tolerance, including disease resistance, compared to cultivated varieties [25]. The unique physiological and genetic background of wild resources may harbor more diverse and functionally specific endophytic communities, which could be key to their enhanced disease resistance [26].

A limited number of studies have focused on endophytic fungi of medicinal wild asparagus (Asparagus racemosus). Endophytic fungi isolated from medicinal asparagus have been shown to produce antioxidant active substances [27].The colonization and isolation rates of foliar fungal endophytes were higher during the wet periods (September-November) than the other three seasons in Asparagus racemosus [28]. Regarding pathogen control, Pielou’s evenness index of endophytic fungi was the highest in stem tissues of Asparagus racemosus compared to other plant parts. Among these, 12% of fungal endophytes demonstrated antifungal activity against causal agents of four distinctive plant diseases (grey mould, stem rot, root rot and wilting, i.e. Botrytis cinerea, Sclerotinia sclerotiorum, Rhizoctonia solani and Fusarium oxysporum, respectively) [29]. Different varieties of Asparagus officinalis exhibited a certain diversity in root-associated endophytic bacterial community composition [30]. However, research on the diversity of fungi and bacteria in the stems of wild asparagus and their antifungal activity against asparagus stem blight has not yet been reported. This study focused on wild resources of asparagus collected by our team which present the disease resistance to clarify differences in the diversity of endophytic fungi and bacteria in the stems among these asparagus varieties and to explore endophytic strains with antagonistic effects against stem blight. This could provide new approaches for preventing and controlling asparagus stem blight caused by the invading blight fungus.

Materials and methods

Sample collection

In this study, the experimental materials comprised the commercial cultivar of Asparagus officinalis (ATL), the wild resource of Asparagus africanus (NFY), the wild resource of Asparagus maritimus (YDL) and another asparagus wild resource (YAY) which were grown in the field at the Institute of Vegetables and Flowers, Jiangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Jiangxi Province, China (28° 11′ N, 115° 56′ E) (Table S1). Their botanical names were identified by the reference [31]. For each of the four asparagus varieties, five healthy plants were randomly selected in the field as a single biological replicate. A 5 cm stem segment, starting 5 cm above the soil surface, was collected from each of the five plants, and the five segments were combined into one composite sample per replicate. Three such biological replicates (each composed of five plants) were prepared per variety and transported to the laboratory.

In a vertical flow clean bench, to remove epiphytic microorganisms, the stem segments from each replicate were surface-sterilized together as follows: immersion in 75% (v/v) ethanol for 30 s, rinsed twice with sterile distilled water, transferred to 3% (v/v) sodium hypochlorite solution for 8 min, followed by six rinses with sterile water [32]. After sterilization, both ends of each stem segment were removed with sterile scissors, and the remaining sections were cut into small pieces. From each biological replicate, a 0.5 g aliquot of stem pieces was immediately stored at -80 °C for total DNA extraction, and another separate 0.5 g aliquot was used for cultivable microbe isolation.

To confirm the effectiveness of the surface sterilization procedure, a sterility validation test was performed based on the protocol with modifications [33]. The sterile stem segments after with sodium hypochlorite solution treatment were transferred on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) plates. The plates were incubated at 28–30 °C for 3–5 days and monitored daily for microbial growth. Sterilization success was confirmed when no visible bacterial or fungal colonies appeared on any of the incubated plates.

DNA extraction

Total genomic DNA was extracted from surface-sterilized stem tissues. Tissues were first ground to a fine powder in liquid nitrogen using a sterile mortar and pestle. Approximately 100 mg of the ground powder was used for DNA extraction. Extraction was performed using the FastDNA® Spin Kit (MP Biomedicals) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with a minor modification: the incubation step with the lysis buffer was extended to 30 min to ensure complete disruption of both bacterial and fungal cells. The quality and concentration of the extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and by electrophoresis on a 1% (w/v) agarose gel. DNA extracts were stored at -20 ℃.

PCR amplification and sequencing

To characterize the bacterial and fungal endophytic communities, polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplification of specific phylogenetic markers was performed. For bacterial community analysis, the hypervariable V5-V7 region of the 16 S ribosomal RNA (rRNA) gene was amplified using the primer pair 799 F (5′-AACMGGATTAGATACCCKG-3′) and 1193R (5′-ACGTCATCCCCACCTTCC-3′) [34]. For fungal community analysis, the internal transcribed spacer 2 (ITS2) region was amplified using the primer pair ITS1F (5′-CTTGGTCATTTAGAGGAAGTAA-3′) and ITS4 (5′-TCCTCCGCTTATTGATATGC-3′) [35]. PCR reactions were carried out in triplicate for each sample to minimize amplification bias. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 ℃ for 3 min; followed by 25–30 cycles of denaturation at 95 ℃ for 30 s, annealing at 55 ℃ for 30 s, and extension at 72 ℃ for 30 s; with a final extension at 72 ℃ for 5 min. The resulting PCR products were purified using a PCR Clean-Up Kit (C01-10000) and quantified. Equimolar amounts of the purified amplicons from each sample were pooled. The pooled library was sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) using a 2 × 300 bp paired-end strategy, following the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Data processing and bioinformatic analysis

Raw sequencing data were processed using QIIME 2 (version 2023.5). Briefly, sequence reads were demultiplexed and quality-filtered using the q2-demux plugin, followed by denoising with DADA2 to correct errors and remove chimeras, resulting in amplicon sequence variants (ASVs). For bacterial 16 S rRNA gene data, taxonomy was assigned against the SILVA reference database (version 138). For fungal ITS data, taxonomy was assigned against the UNITE database (version 9.0). Subsequent analyses, including alpha-diversity (e.g., Shannon index, Observed ASVs) and beta-diversity (e.g., Bray-Curtis dissimilarity, PCoA), were performed within the QIIME 2 framework [36–38].

Operational Taxonomic Units (OTUs) were clustered with 97% similarity cutoff using UPARSE [39, 40] (version 7.1 http://drive5.com/uparse/), and chimeric sequences were identified and removed using UCHIME. The taxonomy of each 16 S rRNA gene sequence was analyzed using the RDP Classifier algorithm (https://sourceforge.net/projects/rdpclassifier/) against the Silva (Release132, http://www.arb-silva.de) 16 S rRNA database using confidence threshold of 70%. Alpha diversity analysis were performed using QIIME and visualized with R software. Functional differences were analyzed by incorporating Clusters of Orthologous Groups (GOD) information using PICRUSt (Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States) software.

Isolation medium preparation

Fungal pathogen (Phomopsis asparagi) was obtained from the Institute of plant protection, Jiangxi Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Jiangxi Province, China. Phomopsis asparagi (asparagus stem blight fungus) were incubated on Potato Dextrose Agar (PDA) and at 25 ℃for 24–48 h. A mycelial plug (5 mm diameter) from the actively growing margin of a pathogenic fungus was picked and inoculated into 100 mL of PA (Potato Dextrose) broth in a 250 mL flask, followed by incubation at 25 ℃ with shaking at 180 rpm for 40–48 h. A volume of 1 mL of P. asparagi fermentation broth was added to a plate with 19 mL of un-solidified PDA, then solidified for isolation of anti-microbial endophytes.

Isolation of anti-microbial endophytes

A serial dilution technique was employed to isolate of endophytes. Following surface sterilization (as described above), the stem pieces (approximately 0.5 g) were placed in a sterilized tube with 1 mL sterilized water and a bead, then aseptically ground twice for 1 min using a Tissue Lyser. The resulting liquid was transferred to a test tube containing 9 mL of sterile water, creating a 10− 1 dilution. Subsequently, 1 mL of the 10− 1 suspension was aseptically transferred into a new tube containing 9 mL of sterile diluent, creating a 10− 2 dilution. This process was repeated sequentially to achieve a dilution series up to 10− 4. Each dilution was then performed to obtain isolated microbial colonies.

Screening for antagonistic activity against pathogens

Each dilution was separately spread onto the surface of the PDA plates with 1 mL of pathogen fermentation added before the PDA solidified, as a special selective medium. The plates were incubated at 26 ℃ for 3–7 days and monitored daily for colonial growth.

Purification of endophytic isolates

After incubation, colonies exhibiting distinct morphologies (differing in size, color, shape, elevation, and margin) were carefully selected and streaked onto fresh agar plates of the same medium using a sterile loop. This purification process was repeated at least twice for each isolate until a pure culture, confirmed by uniform colonial morphology, was obtained. Pure microbes isolates were stored at 4 ℃on agar slants for short-term use and in 20% (v/v) glycerol at -80 ℃ for long-term preservation.

Antimicrobial test in a dual-culture assay

The antagonistic activity of purified endophytic isolates was tested against Phomopsis asparagi (p. asparagi) using a dual-culture assay [41].

A mycelial plug (5 mm diameter) from the actively growing margin of a pathogenic fungus was placed in the center of a fresh PDA plate. Test endophytic bacteria or fungi were inoculated at a distance of 2.5 cm from the pathogen plug. Plates inoculated with the pathogen alone served as controls. All plates were incubated at 28 ℃ until the fungal mycelium in the control plates grew to the edge. where R is the radial growth of the pathogen in the control plate, and r is the radial growth of the pathogen towards the antagonist.

Antimicrobial test of fermentation broth

Strains were incubated on luria-bertani (LB) agar and at 28 ℃ for 24–48 h. A single colony was then picked and inoculated into 100 mL of LB broth in a 250 mL flask, followed by incubation at 28 ℃ with shaking at 180 rpm for 12–16 h. Two methods were used to clearly make sure the antimicrobial effects [42]. (1) 100ul of strain fermentation broth was spread evenly on the surface of a PDA plate, after which a pathogen mycelial plug (5 mm diameter) was placed in the center of the medium. (2) 1 mL of strain fermentation broth was added to a plate with 19 mL of un-solidified PDA. After the PDA solidified, a pathogen mycelial plug (5 mm diameter) was placed at the center of the medium. Plates were incubated at 25℃ for 5–7 days until the control pathogen fully covered the plate.

Results

Shared and unique endophytes communities among asparagus

Venn diagram depicting shared and unique OTUs illustrate differences among fungal and bacterial endophyte communities in four samples. NF showed the highest OTU abundance of endophytic fungi, whereas YDL was the lowest. All the four asparagus varieties shared 17 fungal OTUs accounting for 23.94% of the total, while harboring 8 (11.27%), 10 (14.09%), 3 (4.23%), and 4 (5.63%) unique OTUs in ATL, NFY, YAY, and YDL, respectively (Fig. 1a). YDL showed the highest OTU abundance of endophytic bacteria, whereas ATL was the lowest. All the four asparagus varieties shared 67 bacterial OTUs, accounting for 7.57% of the total, while harboring 68 (7.68%), 165 (18.64%), 124 (14.01%), and 120 (13.56%) unique OTUs in ATL, NFY, YAY, and YDL, respectively (Fig. 1b).

Distribution of microbial community

Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) revealed distinct clustering patterns of the microbial communities in the stems of four asparagus resources (Fig. 2). For the fungal community (Fig. 2a), the first two principal components, PC1 and PC2, explained 55.35% and 21.95% of the variation, respectively. The NFY and YAY samples were separated from each other and were distant from the other two samples. The ATL and YDL samples were closer to each other in the PCoA plot. For the bacterial community (Fig. 2b), PC1 and PC2 explained 80.51% and 6.82% of the variation, respectively. The groups of YDL, YAY, and NFY clustered closely together and were distinctly separated from the ATL group.

Differences at the microbial genus level

At the genus level, wild asparagus resources showed significant differences in the proportions of bacterial and fungal endophytes in their stem (Fig. 5). For fungi, Acremonium accounted for a relatively large proportion in the wild resources NFY and YAY, while Aspergillus was more abundant in the wild resource YDL and the cultivated variety ATL, both showing significant differences (Fig. 5a). For bacteria, Aquabacterium, Nesterenkonia, Brevundimonas, Delftia, Bradyrhizobium, Microbacterium, Ralstonia, Caulobacteraceae, and Agrobacterium showed the most significant differences among the tested asparagus resources. Among them, Dietzia and Nesterenkonia were exclusively present in the cultivated variety ATL, accounting for approximately 70% and 20%, respectively. The other bacterial genera were predominantly found in wild asparagus resources, showing significant variation. Delftia was more abundant in NFY compared to other wild resources, accounting for approximately 15% (Fig. 5b).

Functional potential of fungal and bacterial endophytes in asparagus

Prediction of the functional potential of the fungal endophytic communities using PICRUSt2 analysis of Enzyme Commission (EC) numbers revealed significant differences between the cultivated variety (ATL) and the wild accessions (YDL, YAY, NFY) (Fig. 6a). The wild accessions were characterized by notable enrichment of enzymes and pathways associated with defense and stress resistance. This included a significantly higher predicted abundance of chitinase (EC 3.2.1.14), a key enzyme that directly lyses fungal pathogens by degrading chitin in their cell walls. Enhanced potential for xenobiotic degradation and oxidative stress response was also indicated by the enrichment of glutathione S-transferase (EC 2.5.1.18) and cytochrome P450 enzymes (e.g., EC 1.14.14.1). Furthermore, the enrichment of transporters, such as co-transporting ATPase (EC 3.6.3.14), suggested superior competitive ability for essential trace elements, potentially limiting their availability to pathogens. In contrast, the fungal endophytes of the cultivated ATL variety were predominantly enriched in functions related to genetic information processing (e.g., DNA-directed RNA polymerase (EC 2.7.7.6), DNA ligase (EC 6.5.1.1)) and core metabolism.

Fungal (a) and Bacterial (b) endophytes community heatmaps analysis on OTU level in asparagus

Prediction of metagenomic function using PICRUSt2 revealed a profound functional divergence in the bacterial endophytic communities between the cultivated asparagus variety (ATL) and its wild resources (YDL, YAY, NFY) (Fig. 6b). As illustrated in the heatmap of KEGG Pathway Level 3 profiles, samples clustered distinctly according to host genotype, with all ATL samples forming a separate cluster from the wild accessions. The bacterial communities of wild asparagus were significantly enriched in pathways related to environmental sensing, defense, and resource competition. Enriched functions in wild resources included two-component systems, quorum sensing, bacterial secretion systems, and ABC transporters, indicating an enhanced potential for direct antagonism against pathogens and a highly competitive capability for nutrient uptake. In contrast, the endophytic bacterial community of the cultivated ATL variety was predominantly enriched in core metabolic pathways supporting rapid growth and reproduction, such as biosynthesis of amino acids, purine metabolism, pyrimidine metabolism, ribosome function, and central energy metabolism.

Discussion

Endophyte community diversity

Host genotype has been shown to be a key factor in shaping plant microbiome assembly and function [43–45]. The significant differences observed in both fungal and bacterial endophyte communities between the two maize cultivars studied align with the growing consensus that host genetics plays a non-negligible role in microbiome assembly [46]. Community composition of fungal and bacterial endophytes differed in the fruits of three wild berry species (bilberry Vaccinium myrtillus L., lingonberry Vaccinium vitis-idaea L. and crowberry Empetrum nigrum L.) and fungi predominating over bacteria in total endophytic taxa [47]. Root-associated endophytic microbial communities also exhibited differences among different Asparagus officinalis varieties [30]. Our findings reveal large variability in the community structure of both endophytic fungi and bacteria between the wild resources and the cultivated variety, with marked differences in dominant bacterial communities (Figs. 3 and 4). In PCoA, the NFY and YAY samples were separated from each other, and from the other two samples (Fig. 2a). The ATL and YDL samples were closer to each other. For bacteria, the groups of YDL, YAY, and NFY clustered together and distinctly separated from ATL (Fig. 2b). This study demonstrates significant differences in the diversity and community structure of endophytic fungi and bacteria between cultivated and wild asparagus varieties. Wild asparagus resources (NFY, YAY, YDL) harbored more diverse and unique endophytic communities compared to the cultivated cultivar ATL, as evidenced by diversity analyses (Tables 1 and 2; Fig. 2). This aligns with previous findings that wild plants, through long-term evolution and natural selection, often possess greater genetic diversity and host more diverse and functionally specific endophytic microbiomes [25, 26].

Antimicrobial function of endophytes

Endophytes include bacterial or fungal microorganisms that inhabit roots, stems, and leaves of plant tissues [48, 49]. They can produce a wide range of bioactive compounds, enabling plants to better adapt to various stresses, including improved growth and disease resistance [50–52]. Plant endophytes exhibit diversity in abundance and species, and their secondary metabolites display various antimicrobial effects [53]. Thirty-five distinct endophytic fungi isolated from the roots of Asparagus racemosus were found to efficiently produce saponins, which are generally known for their antioxidants functions [54]. PICRUSt2 can be utilized to predict the functional potential of the endophytic bacterial communities [55]. Endophytes in wild asparagus were significantly enriched in functions related to direct pathogen antagonism and stress resistance (Fig. 6). The predicted high abundance of chitinase (EC 3.2.1.14) suggests a potent mechanism for directly lysing fungal pathogens like P. asparagi by degrading their cell walls [24]. Similarly, the enrichment of glutathione S-transferase and cytochrome P450 enzymes indicates an enhanced capacity for xenobiotic degradation and oxidative stress response, potentially contributing to a hostile environment for pathogens [20]. Furthermore, bacterial endophytes in wild resources showed enrichment in quorum sensing, bacterial secretion systems, and ABC transporters, which are crucial for inter-microbial communication, effector delivery, and nutrient competition-all recognized mechanisms of biocontrol [14, 17]. In contrast, the endophytic community of the cultivated ATL was predominantly geared towards basic metabolism and growth. While differences in endophyte diversity influence functional variation, it is not necessarily the case that the higher diversity of endophytic bacteria correlates with higher levels of functional secondary metabolites. It is speculated that the abundance of specific beneficial bacteria is a key factor influencing microbial functional impact [56].

Isolation of antagonistic endophytes

Microbes residing within plant tissues have been known for more than a century without causing substantive harm to their host. Several endophytic microbes have been reported from diverse source and could be used as biofertilizers and biopesticides for agricultural sustainability [57–59]. Endophytic fungi from ramie showed the potential antifungal activities against all the pathogens of the ramie with high inhibition [60]. Comparative microbiome studies of disease-resistant and susceptible tomato varieties revealed that resistant lines consistently harbored a greater relative abundance of bacterial endophytes from genera such as Bacillus and Pseudomonas. The endophytes were shown to systemically colonize the plant and directly inhibit the soil-borne pathogen Ralstonia solanacearum through competition and antibiosis [61]. The relative abundance of some beneficial fungal communities was significantly higher in resistant konjac (Amorphophallus spp.) species, and all three strains of Cladosporium isolated strongly inhibited to Pcc growth, highlighting the important role of endophytic microbial communities in resisting bacterial pathogen infections [23]. Three endophytes isolated from tomato healthy plants were considered effective against the growth of fungal pathogens early blight disease in vitro [62]. Aspergillus terreus, isolated as an endophyte from the seed of the rubber tree Hevea brasiliensis, inhibited the growth of pathogens Rigidoporus microporus and Corynespora cassiicola by 64 and 70%, respectively, in dual culture assays [63]. Our findings are consistent with studies on other plants, where endophytes from wild resources have proven valuable for biocontrol [24]. For instance, research on Asparagus racemosus also highlighted the antifungal potential of its endophytic fungi [29]. This study expands this understanding to the stem endophytes of wild asparagus resources and their specific activity against its most destructive pathogen, P. asparagi (Table 3; Fig. 7). The high inhibition rates (> 90%), even with diluted fermentation broth, underscore the efficacy of these isolates. Endophytes applications have already been explored in the field of agriculture biocontrol [64]. The direct isolation of antagonistic endophytes from resistant or healthy plants for biocontrol applications has shown great promise in vegetable pathosystems.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that wild asparagus resources harbor a significantly more diverse and functionally distinct endophytic microbiome compared to their cultivated counterpart. This divergence is characterized not only by community structure but, crucially, by a functional enrichment in wild genotypes for direct pathogen antagonism, stress resistance, and competitive traits. The successful isolation of highly antagonistic endophytes from wild plants, coupled with predictive functional analyses, validates that these untapped microbial reservoirs are a rich source of potential biocontrol agents. Our findings underscore the importance of conserving wild plant genetic resources and leveraging their associated microbiomes to develop sustainable strategies for enhancing disease resistance in cultivated crops.

Future research should focus on in planta efficacy and field application of these antagonistic endophytes. This includes UPLC-MS-based identification of bioactive metabolites (e.g., lipopeptides, polyketides, or siderophores), as well as greenhouse and field trials to evaluate biocontrol performance and colonization. Integrating compound identification with in vivo efficacy testing will facilitate microbe-based biocontrol strategies for sustainable asparagus production.
