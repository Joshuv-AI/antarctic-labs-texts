Safety assessment of enterocin-producing Enterococcus strains isolated from sheep and goat colostrum
Authors: Burak Geniş, Hüseyin Öztürk, Banu Özden Tuncer, Yasin Tuncer
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigates the safety evaluation of enterocin-producing 11 E. mundtii and two E. faecium strains previously isolated from small livestock colostrums. Enterococcus species do not possess Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status. Hence, it is critical to scrutinize enterococci’s antibiotic resistance, virulence characteristics, and biogenic amine production capabilities in order to assess their safety before using them as starter or adjunct cultures.ResultsEnterococcus strains showed susceptibility to medically significant antibiotics. Multiple-drug resistance (MDR) was found in only E. faecium HC121.4, and its multiple antibiotic resistance (MAR) index was detected to be 0.22. The tetL and aph(3')-IIIa were the most commonly found antibiotic resistance genes in the strains. However, E. mundtii strains HC56.3, HC73.1, HC147.1, and E. faecium strain HC121.4 were detected to lack any of the antibiotic resistance genes examined in this study. Only E. mundtii HC166.3 showed hemolytic activity, while none of the strains engage in gelatinase activity. The strains were identified to have virulence factor genes with a low rate. None of the virulence factor genes could be detected in E. mundtii HC26.1, HC56.3, HC73.1, HC165.3, HC166.8, and E. faecium HC121.4. The E. mundtii HC73.2 strain displayed the highest presence of virulence factor genes, namely gelE, efaAfs, cpd, and ccf. Similarly, the E. mundtii HC112.1 strain showed a significant presence of genes efaAfm, ccf, and acm. There was no decarboxylation of histidine, ornithine, or lysine seen in any of the strains. Nevertheless, E. faecium HC121.4 and HC161.1 strains could decarboxylate tyrosine, but E. mundtii HC26.1, HC56.3, HC73.1, HC73.2, HC112.1, HC147.1, HC155.2, HC165.3, HC166.3, HC166.5, and HC166.8 strains only showed a limited capacity for tyrosine decarboxylation. None of the strains possessed the hdc, odc, or ldc genes, but all of them had the tdc gene.ConclusionThe E. mundtii HC56.3 and HC73.1 strains were deemed appropriate for utilization in food production. Using the remaining 11 strains as live cultures in food production activities could pose a possible risk to consumer health.Keywords: Colostrum, Enterocin, Enterococcus, Antibiotic resistance, Virulence factors, Biogenic amines

Background

Enterococcus is a genus of lactic acid bacteria (LAB) mostly found in the gastrointestinal tracts of humans and animals, but also in diverse ecosystems. These bacteria have a tolerance to harsh environmental conditions like high salt concentrations and an extensive pH range, which allows them to adapt to numerous fermented foods, including traditional types of cheese and sausages [1, 2]. Enterococci contribute to the typical taste and aroma of these foods through their technological properties, such as proteolysis, lipolysis, pyruvate, and citrate metabolism. They also improve the microbial safety of end products via bacteriocin production [3]. However, Enterococcus species do not possess GRAS status by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and are not involved in the Qualified Presumption of Safety (QPS) list by the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) [1]. Certain members of the Enterococcus species, especially E. faecium and E. faecalis, are acknowledged as nosocomial pathogens that cause clinical illnesses including endocarditis, bacteremia, and infections of the urinary system [4]. The safety of foodborne enterococci is assessed based on two primary parameters: antibiotic resistance and virulence factors [5]. Antibiotic resistance is a serious worldwide health threat, as resistance has been identified to all antibiotics used in medical practice, and only a limited number of new medications are being developed [6]. Enterococci may possess both intrinsic and acquired resistance to antibiotics that are crucial in clinical applications [4, 7]. Merely possessing antibiotic-resistance genes does not always imply that a strain is capable of causing disease. Nevertheless, the combination of antibiotic resistance genes and virulence characteristics in a strain might render it hazardous [8]. Virulence factors are molecules that enhance a bacterium’s ability to cause disease and help it survive and establish itself within the host [9]. Enterococcus species possess several virulence factors, which can be classified into three types: (i) surface factors, including aggregation substance, collagen binding protein, cell wall adhesin, and enterococcal surface protein, that influence host cell colonization; (ii) secreted agents, such as gelatinase, cytolysin, and hyaluronidase, that damage tissues; and (iii) sex pheromones that promote the transfer of pheromone-responsive plasmids between cells through conjugation [3, 8]. Another feature of enterococci that poses a risk for customers’ health is their production of biogenic amines (BAs). The presence of BAs in fermented foods is mostly due to the microbial activity that causes the decarboxylation of amino acids. BAs provide a significant risk of food poisoning and are marked by symptoms such as elevated blood pressure, vomiting, intense headaches, and allergic responses [10]. BAs production by enterococci isolated from fermented foods has been reported, but BAs have not yet been linked to enterococcal disease [3, 10].

An exhaustive safety analysis of enterococci strains intended for utilization as starter or probiotic cultures is imperative due to these various factors. This study used both phenotypic and genotypic methods to identify possible risk factors—such as antibiotic resistance, virulence factors, and biogenic amine production—in enterocin-producing Enterococcus strains previously isolated from small ruminant colostrums and studied for starter culture [11] and probiotic properties [12].

Materials and methods

Bacteria and culture media

In this research, enterocin-producing 11 E. mundtii and two E. faecium strains previously isolated from sheep and goat colostrums were used. Colostrum samples were milked from 81 sheep and 108 goats grown in Antalya and Isparta cities, Türkiye, during two distinct birth seasons between October 2018 and March 2020. Enterococcus strains were identified using 16 S rDNA sequence analysis and species-specific polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The genetic differences between strains were identified using phylogenetic analysis of the 16 S rRNA gene sequence and random amplified polymorphic DNA [11]. All Enterococcus strains were grown in de Man Rogosa and Sharpe broth (MRS, Biokar Diagnostics, Beauvais, France) and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25,923 was grown in Triptic soy broth (Neogen, Lansing, MI, USA) containing 0.5% yeast extract (TSBYE) at 37 °C for 18 h. All bacteria used in this study were obtained from Bacterial Genetic Laboratory Culture Collection (Süleyman Demirel University, Food Engineering Department, Isparta/Türkiye). Stock cultures of bacteria were stored in a 20% (v/v) sterile glycerol-added appropriate broth at -32 °C.

Detection of antibiotic resistance and resistance genes

The disc diffusion test was used to investigate the antibiotic susceptibility and resistance features of Enterococcus strains [13]. The following commercial antibiotic discs obtained from Oxoid Ltd. (Basingstoke, England) were used in the disc diffusion test: ampicillin (2 and 10 µg), imipenem (10 µg), tigecycline (15 µg), penicillin G (10 U), vancomycin (30 µg), teicoplanin (30 µg), erythromycin (15 µg), tetracycline (30 µg), doxycycline (30 µg), minocycline (30 µg), ciprofloxacin (5 µg), levofloxacin (5 µg), norfloxacin (10 µg), nitrofurantoin (100 µg and 300 µg), rifampin (5 µg), chloramphenicol (30 µg), quinopristin-dalfopristin (15 µg), linezolid (30 µg), gentamicin (30 µg and 120 µg), and streptomycin (300 µg). The disc diffusion test results of strains were evaluated by using the breakpoint table of the European Committee on Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing [14] and the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute [15]. Antibiotic disc diffusion results were catergorized as susceptible, intermediary or resistant. The E. faecalis ATCC 29,212 was used as a control. The MAR index for each isolate was computed by dividing the quantity of antibiotics to which it was resistant to by the number used in testing. This study expressed MDR as the ability to resist at least one antibiotic from three or more antibiotic classes [16].

Genomic DNA from Enterococcus strains was extracted as described by Cancilla et al. [17]. The genomic DNA samples were kept at -20 °C until use. The existence of erythromycin (ermA, ermB, and ermC), tetracycline (tetM, tetL, tetS, tetK, and tetO), high-level aminoglycoside (aph(3')-IIIa, ant(4')-Ia, ant(6')-Ia, aac(6')-Ie-aph(2'')-Ia, aph(2'')-Ib, aph(2'')-Ic, and aph(2'')-Ic), and vancomycin (vanA, vanB, vanC1, vanC2, vanC3, vanD, vanE, and vanG) resistance genes in Enterococcus strains were investigated by PCR [18–23]. The primer pairs and PCR conditions utilized for identification of antibiotic resistance genes are listed in Table 1. For PCR analysis, a 50 µL PCR mix was prepared using 25 µL of 2X PCR master mix (Catalog number K0172; Thermo Scientific, Vilnius, Lithuania), 20 µL of nuclease-free water, 1 µL of each primer (100 pM/µL), and 3 µL of template DNA. This mix was used in a gradient thermal cycler (Turbo Cycler 2, Blue-Ray Biotech. Ltd., Taiwan). E. gallinarum DYE22 (vanC1+, vanD+), E. casseliflavus DYE26 (tetS+), E. casseliflavus DYE43 (tetL+, tetM+, vanC2+, vanC3+), E. gallinarum DYE45 (ermA+, ermB+) [7], E. durans RG36.3 (aph(3')-IIIa+, ant(6')-Ia+, aph(2'')-Ic+) [24], E. faecium MSM14.1 (ant(4')-Ia+, aph(2'')-Ib+) [25], E. faecium FYE41 (ermC+) [26], E. faecalis ATCC 51,299 (aac(6')-Ie-aph(2'')-Ia+, vanB+), and E. faecium ATCC 51,559 (vanA+) strains were used as controls. Amplified PCR products were electrophoresed on 2% (w/v) agarose gels (Prona Agarose Biomax), stained with ethidium bromide (Amresco, Solon, Ohio, USA) and filmed under ultraviolet light. The GeneRuler™ 100 bp DNA ladder (Catalog number SM0244; Thermo Scientific) was utilized as a molecular marker to estimate PCR fragment sizes.

Virulence factors

Hemolytic and gelatinase activities

Overnight cultures of strains were streaked onto blood agar containing 5% (v/v) defibrinated sheep blood (Liofilchem, Roseto degli Abruzzi, Italy) with a platinum loop, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The zone formation around the Enterococcus colonies was evaluated as follows: the clear zone surrounding the colony as β-hemolytic, the fuzzy greenish zone formation as α-hemolytic, and the absence of a zone as γ-hemolytic [13]. The S. aureus ATCC 25,923 strain was employed as a positive control to assess β-hemolytic activity.

The gelatinase activity of Enterococcus strains was tested on Todd-Hewitt agar (Neogen Culture Media, Heywood, Lancashire, UK) containing 3% (w/v) gelatin (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). Overnight Enterococcus cultures were streaked onto gelatine-containing Todd-Hewitt agar. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, Petri plates were stored in the refrigerator at 4 °C for 5 h, and the formation of an opaque zone around the colonies at the end of the period were assessed as a positive result as described by Eaton and Gasson [27]. The gelatinase-producing E. faecalis RG22.4 strain was utilized as a positive control [24].

Detection of virulence factor genes

The presence of aggregation protein (agg), gelatinase (gelE), cell wall adhesin (efaAfs and efaAfm from E. faecalis and E. faecium, respectively), extracellular surface proteins (espfs and espfm from E. faecalis and E. faecium, respectively), sex pheromones (cpd, cob, ccf, and cad), collagen binding protein (ace and acm), cytolysin (cylM, cylB, and cylA) and hyaluronidase (hyl) encoding genes in Enterococcus strains were examined by PCR [27–31]. The primer pairs and PCR conditions utilized for identification of virulence factor genes are listed in Table 1. PCR analysis was conducted using 50 µL of PCR mix in a Turbo Cycler 2 gradient thermal cycler (Taiwan). E. faecalis ATCC 29,212 (aag+, efaAfm+, espfm+, espfs+, ace+, cylM+, cylB+, cylA+, cpd+, cob+, ccf+, cad+), E. faecalis ATCC 51,299 (efaAfs+, gelE+), and E. faecium ATCC 51,559 (acm+, hyl+) strains were used as controls. Amplified PCR fragments were analyzed on 1.5% (w/v) agarose gels (Biomax) and photographed as described above.

Biogenic amine production: phenotypical and genotypical determination

Amino acid decarboxylase agar recommended by Bover-Cid and Holzapfel [32] was utilized to determine the biogenic amine production characteristics of Enterococcus strains. Lysine, tyrosine, ornithine, and histidine (Merck) precursor amino acids with a final concentration of 1% (w/v) were added separately to the prepared basal decarboxylase medium: 5 g/L of tryptone, 5 g/L of yeast extract, 5 g/L of meat extract, 2.5 g/L of sodium chloride, 0.5 g/L of glucose, 1 g/L of tween 801T, 0.2 g/L of MgSO4, 0.5 g/L of MnSO4, 0.4 g/L of FeSO4, 2 g/L of ammonium citrate, 0.01 g/L of thiamine, 2 g/L of K2PO4, 0.1 g/L of CaCO3, 0.05 g/L of pyridoxal-5- phosphate, 0.06 g/L of bromcresol purple, 20 g/L of agar. The pH was adjusted to 5.3 before autoclaving for 10 min at 121 °C. Enterococcus strains were grown five times in MRS broth with 0.1% w/v of each precursor amino acid. Subsequently, 10 µl of these cultures were transferred to the surface of amino acid decarboxylase agar, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 to 5 days. From yellow to purple color changes around the colonies on decarboxylase agar for lysine, ornithine, or histidine or a precipitation ring observed in media containing tyrosine were assessed as indicators of biogenic amine formation by decarboxylating precursor amino acids. The basal decarboxylase agar was used as a control.

Enterococcus strains were tested for amino acid decarboxylase genes by PCR as described by de Las Rivas et al. [33]. The primer pairs and PCR conditions are given in Table 1. Amplified PCR fragments were electrophoresed on 1.5% (w/v) agarose gels (Biomax) and visualized as previously mentioned. The tyraminogenic E. faecalis NYE54 (tdc+) strain was used as a positive control when performing both phenotypic and genotypic tests [34].

Results

Antibiotic resistance and resistance genes

The disc diffusion test revealed that all bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains were susceptible to ampicillin, chloramphenicol, doxycycline, gentamicin, levofloxacin, linezolid, minocycline, penicillin G, streptomycin, teicoplanin, tetracycline, tigecycline, and vancomycin (Table 2). Enterococcus strains were found resistant to rifampin (3/13, 23.08%), erythromycin (2/13, 15.39%), ciprofloxacin (1/11, 7.69%), norfloxacin (1/11, 7.69%), nitrofurantoin (1/11, 7.69%) and quinopristin-dalfopristine (1/11, 7.69%). In addition, 100% (13/13) and 84.62% (11/13) of strains exhibited intermediate resistance to imipenem and erythromycin, respectively (Table 3). The MAR index of Enterococcus strains varied between 0.00 and 0.22, with an mean value of 0.04. The highest MAR index was detected in the E. faecium HC121.4, identified as being multiple-drug resistant (Table 4).

Virulence factors

Hemolytic and gelatinase activities

The hemolytic activity test revealed that only E. mundtii HC166.3 was β-hemolytic. In addition, E. mundtii HC112.1, E. faecium HC121.4, and HC161.1 strains showed weak α-hemolytic activity, while the remaining nine Enterococcus strains showed γ-hemolytic activity. None of the strains exhibited gelatinase activity.

Virulence factor genes

The E. mundtii HC26.1, HC56.3, HC73.1, HC165.3, HC166.8, and E. faecium HC121.4 strains lacked all virulence factor genes. The gelE, efaAfs, cpd, and ccf were detected in the E. mundtii HC73.2, while the efaAfm, ccf, and acm were detected in the E. mundtii HC112.1. Furthermore, the acm was detected in the E. mundtii HC147.1, HC155.2, HC166.3, HC166.5, and E. faecium HC161.1 strains. Discussion

The widespread antibiotic resistance in bacteria has become a critical global issue. Current predictions suggest that within 25 years, nearly 100% of bacteria will be resistant to most antibiotics commonly used in healthcare [35]. A United Kingdom government-commissioned report on antimicrobial resistance predicts that by 2050, antibiotic-resistant bacteria could cause up to 10 million deaths annually worldwide [36]. Enterococci, due to intrinsic or acquired resistance mechanisms, have developed resistance to almost all antibiotics commonly used in medical settings, including β-lactams, chloramphenicol, fluoroquinolones, macrolides, tetracyclines, aminoglycosides, and glycopeptides [37, 38]. Furthermore, they have also developed resistance to newer antibiotics, such as quinupristin-dalfopristin, linezolid, daptomycin, and tigecycline, which were expected to provide a solution for the treatment of enterococcal infections [38]. The inherent resistance of enterococci to the antibiotics used to treat humans and animals is an essential factor that increases the pathogenicity of these bacteria [4, 27, 39]. Antibiotic resistance is an important part of figuring out whether Enterococcus species are safe or not [40]. Investigation of antibiotic resistance in the safety evaluation of bacteria intended for use as probiotics and starter cultures is essential, as these strains can be potential reservoirs for antibiotic resistance genes [1–3]. The primary factor for the safety evaluation of enterococci is their susceptibility to the glycopeptide antibiotic vancomycin [40], which is used as a last-resort for the treatment of infections caused by multidrug-resistant enterococci [38]. For this reason, the fact that bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains are susceptible to the glycopeptide-group antibiotics, vancomycin and teicoplanin, is advantageous. Similar to our results, Zommiti et al. [40] noted that all five enterocin-producing E. faecium strains isolated from the meat product Ossban in Tunisia were sensitive to vancomycin. Furthermore, Özkan et al. [41] indicated that one of nine E. faecium strains obtained from Tulum cheese in Türkiye was resistant to vancomycin. β-lactam-group antibiotics inhibit bacterial cell wall synthesis by binding to enzymes involved in peptidoglycan cross-linking, such as transpeptidase and carboxypeptidase [35]. Although enterococci are generally inherently resistant to β-lactam-group antibiotics, all bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains showed sensitivity to ampicillin, and except for E. faecium HC121.4, all were found to be susceptible to penicillin G. Zommiti et al. [40], Özkan et al. [41], and Amaral et al. [42] recently identified findings similar to these results. Tetracycline-group antibiotics target the 30 S subunit of ribosome, impeding the protein synthesis [35]. All bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains were identified as susceptible to tetracycline-group antibiotics. Similarly, Zommiti et al. [40] identified that all bacteriocin-producing E. faecium strains were susceptible to tetracycline. Tetracycline resistance is frequently identified in clinical and animal-originated isolates [39]. Chloramphenicol works by inhibiting protein synthesis, specifically targeting the 50 S subunit of the ribosome, which plays a role connecting to t-RNA [35]. We found that all bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains were susceptible to chloramphenicol. This result is consistent with the findings published by Zommiti et al. [40] and Zheng et al. [43]. Linezolid is a significant therapeutic drug employed to treat infections caused by Gram-positive bacteria, specifically vancomycin-resistant enterococci [44]. All Enterococcus strains were determined to be sensitive to linezolid, which is an advantage. Similarly, Amaral et al. [42] noted that all E. faecium and E. durans strains obtained from cheese were sensitive to linezolid. In contrast, Özdemir and Tuncer [24] found that 30.50% of high-level aminoglycoside-resistant raw milk and cheese-originating Enterococcus strains exhibited resistance to linezolid, while 22.03% showed intermediary resistance. Enterococci generally exhibit moderate intrinsic resistance to low-level aminoglycosides due to their limited cell wall permeability. However, they can acquire resistance to high-level aminoglycosides through aminoglycoside-modifying enzymes (AMEs) encoded by transferable genes [23, 24]. All bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains showed susceptibility to the aminoglycoside-group antibiotics gentamicin and streptomycin, both at low and high concentrations. However, previous studies have demonstrated that aminoglycoside-resistant Enterococcus strains can be isolated from raw milk or dairy products in Türkiye [24, 41]. Mobile genetic elements can transfer the genetic determinants of acquired antibiotic resistance mechanisms between bacteria. The global dissemination of antibiotic-resistant bacteria has emerged as a significant public health concern during the past two decades [45, 46]. The susceptibility of bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains to clinically significant antibiotics is advantageous for their application as starter cultures or probiotics.

We found E. faecium HC121.4 to be the most antibiotic-resistant strain among bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains. Previous researches have demonstrated that E. faecalis and E. faecium species, which were isolated from various sources, have higher antibiotic resistance compared to other Enterococcus species [24, 26], as verified in this research. It was determined that Enterococcus strains were relatively more resistant to rifampin (23.08%), and erythromycin (15.39%) compared to other antibiotics. Miller et al. [47] reported that 65.9% of E. faecium isolates in the USA and 67.5% of E. faecium isolates in Europe were resistant to rifampin. Erythromycin resistance is another common antibiotic resistance in enterococci strains. Other studies conducted in recent years have shown that Enterococcus strains found in animal foods are highly and moderately resistant to erythromycin [24, 26, 40]. The extensive utilization of macrolide-group drugs in animal breeding might be the reason for the emergence of many tetracycline-resistant strains [48].

All Enterococcus strains were found to have a MAR index below 0.20, except the multidrug-resistant E. faecium HC121.4. Bacteria resistant to multiple antibiotics pose a public health risk by reducing treatment options and exacerbating the problem of antimicrobial resistance [49]. Therefore, the use of the E. faecium HC121.4 as a live culture in food production processes may pose a risk to consumer health. The MAR index results indicate that all Enterococcus strains except HC121.4 are low-risk for public health. A MAR index greater than 0.2 indicates that the tested isolates came from a high-risk contamination source where antibiotics are commonly used [50]. The fact that the MAR indexes of enterocin-producing Enterococcus strains are generally less than 0.2 may be due to the limited use of antibiotics in the breeding of animals from which colostrum samples are collected. Contrary to our findings, Bouymajane et al. [51] indicated that the MAR indexes of enterococcal strains obtained from raw cow’s milk varied between 0.2 and 0.6. Additionally, they reported that over 65% of the strains exhibited MAR index values equal to or higher than 0.4. Similarly, Ozmen Togay and Karayigit [52] found that 36.7% of enterococci from traditional Urfa cheese manufactured from unpasteurized raw milk in Türkiye had a MAR index higher than 0.2.

Tetracycline resistance in enterococci is commonly linked to the presence of the tetM, which codes for the ribosomal protection protein, and the tetL gene, which codes for membrane-associated ATP-dependent efflux proteins [39, 53–55]. PCR analysis revealed the presence of tetracycline resistance genes, tetL and tetM, in seven and five of the 13 bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains, respectively (Table 2). Although the tetL and/or tetM were identified in E. mundtii HC26.1, HC73.2, HC112.1, HC155.2, HC165.3, HC166.3, HC166.5, HC166.8, and E. faecium HC161.1, the disk diffusion test showed that all of these strains were sensitive to tetracycline (30 µg/disc). Likewise, Cauwerts et al. [56] found that two of the three E. faecium strains that were phenotypically sensitive to tetracycline had the tetM gene, and one had the tetL gene. Demirgül and Tuncer [26] also reported the identification of both tetM and tetL in the tetracycline-susceptible E. faecium FYE41. The tetK, tetS, and tetO were not detected in any Enterococcus strains. Previous studies have shown that enterococci more frequently contain the tetM and tetL than the tetK, tetS, and tetO [26, 53].

In enterococci, resistance to all aminoglycosides except streptomycin occurs through AAC(6')-APH(2''), a bifunctional AME encoded by the aac(6')-Ie-aph(2'')-Ia gene. The aac(6')-Ie-aph(2'')-Ia is related to high level of gentamicin and kanamycin resistance. The monofunctional AMEs encoded by aph(2'')-Ib, aph(2'')-Ic, and aph(2'')-Id genes are responsible for high-level gentamicin resistance, while aph(3')-IIIa, ant(4')-Ia, and ant(6')-Ia genes are responsible for resistance to various aminoglycosides other than gentamicin [20, 23, 57]. The aph(3')-IIIa, ant(6')-Ia, aac(6')-Ie-aph(2'')-Ia, and ant(4')-Ia were detected in seven, three, one, and one bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains by PCR, respectively (Table 2). Conversely, it was determined that all strains were sensitive to the aminoglycoside-group antibiotics by the disk diffusion test. In line with our findings, Hauschild et al. [58] identified the presence of the aac(6')-Ie-aph(2'')-Ia gene in two gentamicin-sensitive Staphylococcus aureus isolates, whereas Rahimi [59] detected this gene in 16 gentamicin-sensitive S. aureus isolates. The presence of AMEs genes in E. mundtii HC26.1, HC73.2, HC112.1, HC155.2, HC165.3, HC166.3, HC166.5, and HC166.8 strains, which were phenotypically susceptible to gentamicin and streptomycin, might not be sensitive to aminoglycoside antibiotics other than gentamicin and streptomycin.

There are several ways that enterococci can resist macrolides, but the most common way is through erm genes. These genes modify the subunit of 23 S rRNA through methylation, which hinders the binding of macrolides to the target site on the ribosome. The ermB is the most prevalent erm gene in enterococci; however, other erm genes have also been identified in these bacteria [39]. The presence of ermA, ermB, or ermC genes was not identified in any of the bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains, which are phenotypically moderately susceptible or resistant to erythromycin. Similarly, Jahan et al. [54], Demirgül and Tuncer [26], and Türkyılmaz et al. [60] indicated that some phenotypically erythromycin-resistant enterococci strains isolated from different sources did not contain the erm genes.

Vancomycin is a glycopeptide-group antibiotic utilized as a last resort for treating diseases caused by enterococci with multiple antibiotic resistance [55]. None of the Enterococcus strains put forth a vancomycin resistance gene. These findings confirm the outcomes of the antibiotic disc diffusion test. The main criterion for the safety assessment of enterococci is their sensitivity to glycopeptides such as vancomycin [40]. Therefore, the fact that bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains are sensitive to vancomycin and do not contain a transferable vancomycin resistance gene is an advantage in terms of the strains’ safety. Similarly, Nami et al. [61] determined that the sheep colostrum isolate bacteriocin-producing E. faecium CM33 did not contain any of the vanA, vanB, or vanC2 genes. Ben Braïek et al. [62] also determined that none of the eight bacteriocin-producing E. lactis strains contained vanA or vanB genes. Zommiti et al. [40] determined that five bacteriocin-producing E. faecium strains isolated from the Tunisian fermented meat product Ossban did not possess the vanA or vanB genes. Chajcka-Wierzchowska et al. [55] showed that two of 182 isolates from 320 ready-to-eat dairy products were found to be vancomycin-resistant but did not have vanA and vanB genes. Tsanisadou et al. [63] found that enterocin-producing six E. faecium and three E. durans strains from cheese did not contain the vanA and vanB genes. However, the presence of the vanA gene in four strains of E. faecalis out of 11 bacteriocin-producing enterococci strains that were obtained from traditional Turkish cheeses was reported [64].

Hemolysin/cytolysin, one of the virulence factors released by enterococci, is a bacterial toxin. Hemolytic activity, encoded by plasmid or chromosomal DNA, plays a significant role in human infections. It is undesirable to isolate enterococcal strains with β-hemolytic activity from foods. Clinical isolates of E. faecium and E. faecalis essentially exhibit this type of hemolytic activity [65]. It is not advisable to use enterococcal strains exhibiting β-hemolytic activity as starting cultures for producing fermented foods [66]. Due to safety concerns, the E. mundtii HC166.3, which showed β-hemolytic activity, shouldn’t be used as a starter, an adjunct, or a probiotic culture in the production of fermented foods. However, considering its hemolytic activity feature, it is safe to use the other bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains. Similar to our result, Trivedi et al. [67] identified a β-hemolytic E. mundtii from milk. However, compared to other enterococcal species present in food, E. faecalis and E. faecium have higher levels of β-hemolytic activity [27]. Our study revealed that the β-hemolytic activity of foodborne enterococcal isolates was either absent or occurred less frequently compared to α-hemolytic or γ-hemolytic activity, as reported by Câmara et al. [68] and Zarzecka et al. [69].

Enterococci produce gelatinase, an extracellular metalloendopeptidase that hydrolyzes casein, collagen, gelatin, hemoglobin, insulin, and some bioactive peptides [70]. It was determined that none of the bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus isolates showed gelatinase activity. In support of the findings, it was reported that gelatinase activity was identified with a very low frequency in food-borne Enterococcus isolates [34, 67, 71]. However, several studies have observed a high frequency of gelatinase activity in Enterococcus, particularly E. faecalis strains [68, 69].

The PCR tests showed that E. mundtii HC26.1, HC56.3, HC73.1, HC165.3, HC166.8, and E. faecium HC121.4 strains do not contain the virulence factor genes, while other enterococci strains have a limited number of virulence factor genes (Table 2). Similar to our findings, previous studies have reported the absence of virulence factor genes in some enterocin-producing enterococci strains isolated from Tunisian meat Ossban [40], human milk [72], rabbit fecal samples [73], and dromedary raw milk and natural saline water samples [74]. However, numerous studies have shown the presence of specific virulence factor genes in bacteriocin-producing enterococci strains isolated from Gueddid, a Tunisian traditional dry fermented meat [31], dried Ossban [40], human milk [72], and dromedary raw milk and natural saline water samples [74], and Turkish Tulum cheese [75].

The gelE gene encodes gelatinase, is an extracellular zinc-metalloprotease identified as approximately 30 kDa in size [76]. The gelE was only detected in the E. mundtii HC73.2 strain, which phenotypically gelatinase negative. The fsrABC locus regulates the FsrB transmembrane protein, which is in charge of controlling the gelE. Deletions occurring in the fsr locus cause the formation of mutants that contain the gelE gene but cannot produce gelatinase [8]. This situation may have occurred due to a mutation in the fsr locus in the E. mundtii HC73.2 strain. Also, different researchers have found the silent gelE gene in enterococci, which is not expressed in their phenotype [13, 27, 73, 77].

The efaAfs and efaAfm encode the endocarditis antigen (efaA) in E. faecalis and E. faecium, respectively. According to Lowe et al. [78], efaAfs plays a crucial role in enterococci adhesion to host cells and thus affects adhesion in endocarditis. It is an advantage that among the 13 bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains, except for E. mundtii HC73.2 (efaAfs+) and E. mundtii HC112.1 (efaAfm+), the strains do not contain the efaA gene. Previous research has shown that the efaAfs gene has a significant impact on the pathogens’ capability to cause infection, as seen in animal models. Nevertheless, the impact of the efaAfm on pathogenicity remains unproven [13, 31]. E. mundtii HC73.2 may pose a risk for use as a starter or adjunct culture in food processing operations owing to the presence of the efaAfs gene.

The cad, ccf, cpd, and cob genes present on chromosomal DNA encode sex pheromones that are short peptides with 7–8 amino acids. These pheromones help in the transmission of plasmid DNA across different strains [76]. The synthesis of sex pheromones facilitates the dissemination of antibiotic-resistance and virulence factor genes among enterococcal strains via pheromone-responsive conjugative plasmids [3]. Therefore, it may not be safe to use E. mundtii HC73.2 (cpd+, ccf+) and E. mundtii HC112.1 (ccf+) strains in food production processes.

The acm encodes an collagen adhesin (Acm) that is identified in E. faecium. Acm binds to type I collagen and belongs to a subfamily of bacterial adhesins called MSCRAMM (Microbial Surface Components Recognizing Adhesive Matrix Molecules) that specifically adhere to the protein layer of the host’s extracellular matrix. The MSCRAMM binds to fibrinogen, fibronectin, collagen, and/or laminin, which are released after the host tissue is injured. While adhesins aid host tissue colonization, other proteins help evade host defenses and may potentially result in infection [79]. The most frequently detected virulence factor gene (46.15%, 6/13) in bacteriocinogenic enterococci strains was determined to be acm (Table 2). Similarly, Aun et al. [80] reported that 74% of the E. faecium strains obtained from various sources harbored the acm gene. In addition, Toplu and Özden Tuncer [81] reported that the bacteriocin-producing E. faecium BT29.11 from Beyaz cheese sample possessed only the acm gene out of the 16 virulence factor genes investigated by PCR. Therefore, E. mundtii HC112.1, HC147.1, HC155.2, HC166.3, HC166.5, and E. faecium HC161.1 strains may not be safe to use in food production processes. On the other hand, according to another view, the collagen-binding protein is not considered a actual virulence factor but rather an attribute that enhances colonization and long-term presence in the gastrointestinal tract [82]. The acm gene was identified in probiotic strains including E. faecalis Symbioflor 1 [82], E. faecium SF68 [83], and E. faecium LBB.E81 [84].

Cytolysin production is under control of the operon containing the cylR1, cylR2, cylLl, cylLs, cylM, cylB, cylA, and cylI genes. The operon can be found either on highly conserved pheromone-dependent conjugative plasmids, such as pAD1, or inside the pathogenicity island in the bacterial chromosome [8]. Cytolysin/hemolysins found in E. faecalis and E. faecium strains are defined as an important virulence factor that hemolyzes rabbit, human, horse, and/or cattle erythrocytes [85]. The existence of cylM, cylB, and cylA genes was not found in any of the bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains found to have α- and β-hemolytic activity in current study. Likewise, Poeta et al. [86] reported that the cyl genes were not detected in seven E. faecium and one E. durans strains showing α-hemolytic activity isolated from poultry feces in Portugal. Maasjost et al. [77] reported that most of the E. gallinarum strains from turkey meat, as well as the reference strain E. gallinarum ATCC 49,573, did not have the cyl gene, but they did have β-hemolytic activity.

Microbial decarboxylation of free amino acids is the main way that biogenic amines are made in foods [87]. Histamine, phenylethylamine, cadaverine, putrescine, tryptamine, and tyramine are not desired in foods due to their toxic effects [88]. Histamine and tyramine are the most extensively researched biogenic amines because of their vasoactive and psychoactive toxicological effects [89]. In sensitive people, tyramine can cause food-induced migraines and hypertension crises. Monoamino-oxidase inhibitor drugs treat this genetically predisposed condition [90]. The phenotypic testing of amino acid decarboxylase activities in 13 bacteriocin-producing Enterococcus strains revealed that none of them could decarboxylate histidine, lysine, or ornithine. Conversely, it was determined that E. faecium HC121.4 and HC161.1 decarboxylated tyrosine, but eleven E. mundtii strains decarboxylated it weakly. Tyramine, a byproduct of the decarboxylation of tyrosine, is synthesized by LAB (lactobacilli, lactococci, enterococci, and carnobacteria) as well as some staphylococci found in several fermented foods. Tyramine is the predominant biogenic amine found in fermented foods, including sausage and cheese [90]. The results of PCR experiments investigating amino acid decarboxylase genes were found to support the phenotypic test results. The tdc was identified in all strains while other decarboxylase genes were not detected. The presence of the tdc gene has been found to have a 100% correlation with tyramine synthesis. In previous studies, it was reported that tyrosine decarboxylation was frequently observed in enterococci isolated from various origins [32, 34, 40, 64, 91, 92].

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study investigated the safety of 13 enterocin-producing enterococci from small livestock colostrum samples. Most strains were susceptible to clinically important antibiotics, except for E. faecium HC121.4, which was found to be multidrug-resistant. E. mundtii strains HC56.3, HC73.1, HC147.1, and E. faecium HC121.4 did not carry any antibiotic resistance genes. Hemolytic activity was observed only in E. mundtii HC166.3, while none of the strains exhibited gelatinase activity. No virulence factor genes were detected in E. mundtii HC26.1, HC56.3, HC73.1, HC165.3, HC166.8, or E. faecium HC121.4. E. faecium strains were able to decarboxylate tyrosine, whereas E. mundtii strains demonstrated limited ability to do so. All strains possessed the tdc gene. Based on the safety experiments, it was concluded that the strains E. mundtii HC56.3 and HC73.1 are suitable for use in food operations. The use of the remaining 11 strains as live cultures in food manufacturing could potentially pose a risk to consumer health. However, purified bacteriocins from these strains may be used for food preservation. Further studies are necessary to evaluate the behavior of live cultures of E. mundtii HC56.3 and HC73.1, as well as purified bacteriocins, in a food matrix.
