Impact of personalized biomedical risk assessment on smoking cessation counselling in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease
Authors: Mark William Youssef, Ronesh Sukhdeo, Lana Mogic, Rajesh Philip Girdhari, Eva Leek, Mohsen Sadatsafavi, Don D. Sin, Jenna Sykes, Andrea S. Gershon, Samir Gupta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSmoking cessation reduces mortality and improves quality of life in chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD). Brief physician counselling is effective for smoking cessation, but counselling is often not provided. We designed a personalized biomedical risk assessment infographic depicting predicted future lung function decline and corresponding clinical consequences occurring with versus without smoking cessation, to trigger and facilitate smoking cessation counselling at the point-of-care. Herein, we determined feasibility of its real-world implementation and impact on care.MethodsThis was a feasibility study of usual care (pre-intervention period) (year 1) followed by the infographic (intervention period) (year 2), at a tertiary care center (St. Michael’s Hospital – SMH, University of Toronto). We recruited pulmonologists and primary care providers who refer patients to the SMH pulmonary function laboratory (PFL). Current smokers with COPD referred for spirometry by consenting providers were provided with the infographic through the PFL software, with instructions to bring this to their provider for discussion. Co-primary behavioural outcomes measured through e-chart review were: changes in the proportion of visits in which patients (1) received smoking cessation counseling; and (2) were prescribed/recommended smoking cessation pharmacotherapy.ResultsThere were 87 (median age 66.0 years; 60.0% male) and 104 (67.5 years; 63.0% male) eligible patients in the pre-intervention and intervention periods, respectively. Smoking cessation counselling was delivered in 58/86 (67.4%) versus 96/119 (80.7%) eligible visits (p = 0.045) and smoking cessation pharmacotherapy in 10/86 (11.6%) versus 22/119 (18.5%) visits (p = 0.25) in the pre-intervention period and intervention period, respectively. Changes in respiratory medications were more common in the intervention period (33.6%) compared to the pre-intervention period (22.6%) (p < 0.001). These changes were seen despite successful delivery of the infographic in only 60/119 (50.4%) eligible visits. Provider feedback was highly favorable.ConclusionsDespite an intervention fidelity of only 50%, our study suggests that a personalized biomedical risk assessment infographic increases smoking cessation counselling rates and may alter COPD management. Generation of the infographic should be automated to improve delivery, and the intervention should then be evaluated for its impact on smoking cessation.Trial registrationThe study was registered on October 7th 2019 at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04119934).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12890-026-04483-w.

Background

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) affects over 400 million people globally, leads to 3.2 million deaths each year, and is predicted to cost $50 trillion annually by 2030 [1, 2]. The most successful way to reduce the burden of COPD is through smoking cessation [3]. Despite this, up to 70% of patients with COPD continue to smoke after their diagnosis [4]. There is strong evidence that even brief smoking cessation counselling increases smoking cessation rates [5], particularly when coupled with evidence-based pharmacotherapy such as varenicline [6], bupropion [7], and/or nicotine replacement therapy [8]. Although there are strong guideline recommendations for healthcare professionals to offer smoking cessation counseling at each patient encounter [5], less than two thirds of patients receive such counselling during a physician encounter in a given year [9].

Studies have found that providers treating those with COPD feel powerlessness, frustration, pessimism, and lack of self-efficacy when engaging in smoking cessation interventions [10, 11]. Providers also do not consistently believe that their advice will increase the likelihood of a successful quit (poor outcome expectancy) [12, 13]. Additionally, they report lacking time and simply forgetting to deliver advice in their busy clinical workflows [12, 13]. A personalized biomedical risk assessment (personalized feedback on the physiologic effects of smoking) might address self-efficacy, outcome expectancy, and time barriers by empowering providers with high-quality data for more effective, meaningful, and efficient point-of-care counselling, especially if delivered through visual information display [14]. Accordingly, we espoused a theory-based approach to develop a personalized biomedical risk assessment infographic for use as a smoking cessation aid by providers, for people with COPD who smoke [15]. This tool leverages our previously-validated personalized risk calculator for forced expiratory volume in one second (FEV1) decline in COPD [16, 17]. Using this model, the system calculates an individualized projected FEV1 change slope with ongoing smoking versus smoking cessation, and couples this with patient-friendly characterizations of projected symptom and disability burden at each spirometric stage of COPD (based on Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Lung Disease (GOLD) criteria) [18]. Infographic content and format were developed by COPD experts, iteratively improved, and validated through a mixed-methods rapid-cycle development process involving people living with COPD and pulmonologists [15].

Having developed the complex intervention, herein, we sought to assess feasibility of the intervention and evaluation design, and to collect pilot data on any observed behavioral (process) effects of the smoking cessation infographic in real-world practice.

Methods

Study design, setting, population

This was a 2-year pre-post feasibility study of usual care (pre-intervention period) (year 1) followed by implementation of the COPD infographic (intervention period) (year 2), conducted between January, 2022 and January, 2024. The study was approved by the Unity Health Toronto Research Ethics Board (#19–231), registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT04119934), and carried out at a tertiary care center (St. Michael’s Hospital - SMH) in Toronto, Ontario. All pulmonologists, primary care providers (PCPs), and nurse practitioners (NPs) at this site were invited to participate. Invitations were made through: (1) presentations by team members SG (SMH pulmonologist) and RG (SMH primary care physician), to their peers at a monthly staff meeting; and (2) emails sent to all providers, by site leadership. Provider informed consent was acquired electronically and patient consent was waived by the Unity Health Toronto Research Ethics Board, as this was considered a minimal risk quality-improvement intervention.

We included all patients of consenting providers who received a spirometry at SMH, with: (1) suspected or known COPD (a spirometry “reason for referral” field containing the any of the terms “COPD/emphysema/chronic bronchitis”); (2) an FEV1/FVC ratio < 0.7 (based on post-bronchodilator [or if unavailable, pre-bronchodilator] spirometry results); and (3) current smoking (based on the “smoking status” field in the spirometry report). We excluded patients with a baseline FEV1 that was less than 30% or greater than 100% predicted, as our FEV1 decline calculator was not validated in these populations.

Intervention

Infographic

The validated infographic was designed for clinicians to use as a facilitator for smoking cessation intervention delivery with their patients [15]. As noted, it demonstrates expected FEV1 decline with and without smoking cessation (this calculator is available free for use by the public through the R software package at: https://github.com/resplab/fev1); and corresponding patient-relevant morbidities (page 1). It also provides clinicians with guidance for smoking cessation pharmacotherapy prescriptions (page 2) (Fig. 1) [15].

The infographic was personalized for each patient by including their name at the top of the graph; sex-specific cartoon figures in the right-hand panel depicting clinical correlates of FEV1 decline; current age as the starting point of the FEV1 decline projection graph; and a personalized FEV1 decline graph.

Pulmonary Function Lab (PFL) workflows were developed by the PFL lead. We built an infographic software and integrated it with the existing PFL software used by technicians as part of routine testing at SMH (Medisoft Expair). Upon identifying any patient receiving spirometry who was a current smoker and had a reason for referral consisting of suspected or confirmed COPD (data fields that PFL technicians routinely enter into the PFL software at the time of test initiation), technicians were asked to export a report to the infographic software (a process requiring 3 additional mouse clicks). The infographic software received the spirometry report from the PFL software as an image file, applied optical character recognition (OCR) to identify and extract relevant data elements (name, sex, age, and the remaining variables required to calculate FEV1 decline: baseline FEV1, weight, height, and prior FEV1), mapped these to the corresponding required parameters, determined patient eligibility accordingly, and if eligible, calculated the FEV1 decline curve and generated the infographic in real-time (in .pdf format), and prompted the PFL technician to download and print it, through a Windows™ notification. The PFL technician then printed and provided the infographic to the patient, and asked them to bring it to their provider for discussion (most appointments occurred on the same day as the PFL visit), acting as a prompt for them to discuss smoking cessation. Simultaneously, the infographic software auto-faxed a copy of the infographic to the provider who had ordered the spirometry test, for ease of integration into the electronic health record. This workflow was piloted and optimized in the SMH PFL for 1 month before formal intervention launch.

Participant and team engagement

In addition to presentations made for enrolment and consent (above), a reminder and informational email was sent to all consenting pulmonologists, primary care physicians, and nurse practitioners before launch and every 3 months after intervention launch. Workflows were presented to PFL technicians before study launch, a simple patient identification and infographic export workflow diagram was posted adjacent to each spirometry station as a reminder, an index card reminder was attached to each PFL computer monitor as an additional reminder, and technicians received monthly email reminders about the study as well.

Outcomes

The co-primary behavioural outcomes were the odds of having a visit in which current smokers with COPD: (a) received smoking cessation counseling from their visit provider; and/or (b) were prescribed or recommended smoking cessation pharmacotherapy, during the one-year intervention period compared to the pre-intervention period.

Secondary outcomes included the odds of having a visit in which smoking patients with COPD: (1) received a referral or recommendation to visit a smoking cessation counselor or program; (2) received a recommendation for other (non-pharmacotherapy) smoking cessation strategies; and (3) had changes in respiratory medication prescriptions (new initiations or discontinuations). We evaluated intervention uptake, and provider feedback on the intervention, the infographic itself, barriers/enablers to its use, and its impact, through an electronic Likert-scale questionnaire sent to physicians after their first instance of infographic use.

Sample size

In planning the study, we evaluated SMH PFL tests performed between June 2019 – May 2020, and identified 193 eligible patients in that 1-year period. Applying a baseline smoking cessation counseling rate of 54% (104/193 patients counseled to quit at the first visit after spirometry performance), we estimated that demonstrating a clinically significant absolute increase of 10–15% would require a ~ 1-year intervention period [19]. Specifically, a 1-year intervention would detect a 14% absolute increase in smoking cessation counseling to 68% (130/193 patients) with a 2-sided alpha of 0.05, and a power of 0.8.

Data collection

Eligible patients were identified through PFL data outputs. For co-primary outcomes and secondary outcomes 1–3, we reviewed the first clinical visit following the PFL visit in which patients qualified to receive the infographic. For co-primary outcomes and secondary outcomes 1 and 2, we excluded patients who were already part of a counselling program and/or who were already on smoking cessation pharmacotherapy. Visits were reviewed through a remote electronic chart review, using an Excel spreadsheet to capture data, by two reviewers. Reviewers initially extracted data from four visits in duplicate, and met to resolve any discrepancies. This process was repeated after duplicate extraction of another six visits, at which point no significant discrepancies remained, and further visits were extracted independently.

Provider feedback was acquired thorough an electronic questionnaire (Supplementary 1) that was developed for this study and was administered through SurveyMonkey™, delivered by email.

Data analysis

Categorical variables are described as frequencies and proportions and continuous variables as medians and ranges. Baseline characteristics were compared using the Fisher’s exact and Chi squared tests for categorical variables, and the Mann-Whitney test for continuous variables. As we had repeated measurements on some patients in both the pre-intervention and intervention periods, we used a generalized estimating equation (GEE) with a logit link and an exchangeable correlation structure to compare pre-/intervention outcomes. This technique accounts for the repeated measures design by accounting for both a between-patient and within-patient variability. These analyses were also adjusted for site. Questionnaire Likert scale responses are presented visually and summarized descriptively. All analyses were conducted using the open-source software R version 4.3.1 [20]. All p-values are two-sided and assessed at p < 0.05.

Results

We successfully recruited 16 out of 17 (94%) respirologists and 72 out of 94 (77%) approached PCPs and NPs. Physicians had been in practice for 15 ± 11 years (range 2–48 years) and 29 of 88 (33%) were male.

There were 87 eligible patients seen in the pre-intervention period (93 total PFL visits), and 104 patients seen in the intervention period (125 total PFL visits). There were 33 patients seen and counted in both time periods. There were 5 patients (7 visits) in the baseline period and 3 patients (6 visits) in the intervention period who were already taking part in a smoking cessation program.

Intervention uptake

Of the 119 co-primary outcome-eligible visits in the intervention period, the infographic was successfully generated and provided to the patient in 60 (50.4%) visits. However, all visits were counted in analyses (intention-to-treat).

Primary behavioral outcomes

Odds of a visit with smoking cessation counselling were 1.99 [95% confidence internal (CI): 1.02–3.85; p = 0.042] times higher with the intervention than pre-intervention. There was a non-significant increase in the odds of a visit with provision of smoking cessation pharmacotherapy [OR 1.89 (0.87–4.12; p = 0.11)] with the intervention compared to pre-intervention (Table 2).

Secondary behavioral outcomes

The only quantitative secondary outcomes that changed significantly were higher odds of visits in which respiratory medications were newly prescribed or discontinued, in the intervention period compared to the pre-intervention period (Table 2).

Provider feedback

Of the 17 providers who managed one or more eligible patient, 7 (41.2%) provided feedback questionnaires. Provider responses to Likert scale questions are depicted in Fig. 2. All responders agreed that the information in the infographic was useful. Most (86%) wanted to keep receiving infographics after study completion, and agreed that the tool was easy to use, visually appealing, effectively prompted them to deliver a smoking cessation intervention, rendered the delivery of the intervention more efficient, and resulted in greater patient interest in smoking cessation and a higher likelihood of considering a quit. Most (71%) also indicated that the infographic improved their comfort level in delivering the smoking cessation intervention, the quality of that intervention, and the likelihood that they would deliver it. Interestingly, only one respondent (14%) preferred a digital copy of the infographic over a paper copy.

Discussion

We previously leveraged a validated FEV1 decline prediction calculator to build a preference-based personalized biomedical risk assessment infographic. Here, in a feasibility study, we demonstrated that this infographic can feasibly be implemented into a complex respiratory care workflow. Our pilot data also suggest a favorable impact on physician delivery of smoking-cessation counselling.

Our study employed a behavioural intervention designed to address well-described barriers surrounding delivery of smoking cessation counselling, at both the provider and patient levels. Specifically, our intervention addressed provider self-efficacy, outcome expectancy, and time barriers by providing personalized data and embedding these within a visually appealing infographic [15]. Personalization was previously cited as a major advantage of our tool [15], and prior studies confirm that personalized feedback increases the effectiveness of smoking cessation interventions [21–25]. Prior studies also show that infographics are highly effective for risk communication by providers and improve health information uptake by patients (including for smoking cessation, and in low health literacy populations) [21, 22, 26, 27], while visual depictions of the consequences of smoking act as strong quit motivators [28]. The presence of these effects was supported by provider feedback that the infographic increased their comfort level in delivering a smoking cessation intervention, the quality of that intervention, and their belief that patients were more likely to consider smoking cessation after seeing the infographic (Fig. 2). Our approach addressed time barriers by enabling a more efficient educational intervention through a visual medium and rapid smoking cessation aid prescriptions through a guided prescription template [15]. This is supported by user reports that the infographic rendered their smoking cessation intervention more efficient, that they had sufficient time to review the infographic, and that it did not add a significant time burden to clinical workflows (Fig. 2). Finally, we addressed memory barriers by utilizing a patient-prompted approach, corresponding with a Cochrane review suggesting that prompts and reminders successfully improve smoking cessation counselling rates [29]. Most (86%) users indicated that having the patients bring the infographic to the clinical visit acted as an effective prompt for a smoking cessation intervention. Overall, 71% indicated that the infographic rendered them more likely to deliver a smoking cessation intervention.

Our intervention was also unique in that the form of personalization was that of a biomedical risk assessment with a patient-facing infographic, which has not previously been reported in patients with COPD, to our knowledge. Although various forms of biomedical risk assessment have had mixed results for smoking cessation [14], providing people who smoke with a personalized spirometric “lung age” was shown to improve short- and long-term quit rates [30, 31]. Rather than providing a static assessment, our tool builds on this earlier work by providing a future projection of lung function over time, linking lung function to patient-relevant outcomes such as expected need for oxygen therapy, exacerbation frequency, and exercise capacity, and providing a quantitative demonstration of the mitigating effect of smoking cessation on all of these factors. Of interest, > 70% of providers indicated that they would not prefer a digital copy of the infographic over a paper copy. Although care is increasingly delivered exclusively through electronic health record systems, and technology-based interventions such as ours are often displayed through digital interfaces or sent directly to patient emails and/or patient portals, an infographic may lend itself to a tactile communication approach. Providers may also have valued patients’ ability to take the printout home to reinforce and sustain messages. Finally, it is possible that the observed increase in new respiratory medication prescriptions in the intervention period may represent a “halo” effect of the infographic, reminding providers and patients of the importance of mitigating lung function decline and its consequences, with aggressive pharmacotherapy. However, this may have been confounded by temporal changes in prescription patterns, and requires future prospective evaluation. Although we can not be certain that medication changes were guideline-concordant, encouraging previous data suggest that pharmacotherapy intensification in response to spirometry data can reduce hospitalizations and overall mortality in COPD [32].

Although we did note a significant increase in counselling rates, we note that counseling was already being provided in 67% of visits before the intervention. This is higher than reported rates as low as 37 to 54% [33, 34] across all physician types, and is likely explained by the fact that the majority of our cohort consisted of pulmonologists, who have been shown to have higher counselling rates than primary care physicians [35, 36]. These high baseline rates may have created a ceiling effect on the intervention, whereby implementation in a more diverse physician population might prove to be even more impactful, and should be explored in a future study.

The fact that only a small number of eligible patients were referred by primary care physicians likely partly reflects the well-documented underuse of spirometry in primary care settings [37]. While this represents an implementation barrier to our intervention, an infographic that effectively facilitates smoking cessation counselling may be perceived as an attractive add-on to the spirometry report itself, possibly eventually driving use of spirometry in this population. This potential impact merits evaluation in a future study, along with ongoing work to address barriers to use of spirometry in primary care [38].

An important feasibility finding in this study was low intervention fidelity. The infographic was successfully delivered in only about half of eligible patients. We believe that this is due to the required workflow changes by PFL technicians. Despite minimal time requirements for infographic generation, technician engagement efforts before and throughout the intervention period, workflow posters, and reminder cue cards, it is likely that PFL technicians often forgot about the study or did not find the time to carry out required steps when clinical workloads were high. Staff turnover, part-time staff with few opportunities to entrench workflows through repetition, and simultaneous introduction of new pandemic-related infection control precautions in the PFL all likely reduced uptake. A lack of comfort with smoking cessation concepts may have further discouraged technician engagement. In a future large trial of the intervention, automation of infographic generation with integration into the pulmonary function test report (through PFL software or electronic health record system integration) could overcome time/recall barriers. In this regard, our use of OCR technology is beneficial in that it can be easily customized, circumventing the need to develop individual plug-ins to retrieve data from various PFL softwares in use across centers. Furthermore, online delivery of smoking cessation education to PFL technicians may reduce engagement barriers. In further optimizing the system before considering any efficacy evaluation, system design input and qualitative feedback before and during implementation should be sought from PFL technicians. We also note that drug access and costs are important external barriers to use of pharmacologic smoking cessation aids (a co-primary outcome) [39]. Government drug insurance programs in our jurisdiction covered only some pharmacologic smoking cessation aids (bupropion, varenicline), in only some patients (those aged ≥ 65 years and/or below a certain income level). Impacts on prescription/recommendation of pharmacologic smoking cessation aids might be expected to be reduced in jurisdictions with even more restrictive medication coverage regimens, and increased in jurisdictions with more liberal coverage.

This was a pre-post feasibility study without a control group. As with any such design, outcomes data are subject to temporal confounders to provider behaviour change. As such, although critical for future trial design and planning, our findings regarding behavioral outcomes can only be considered speculative, and require confirmation in a prospective randomized-controlled trial (RCT). Another study limitation is the small sample size. Although we report a statistically significant increase in the provision of smoking cessation counselling, the sample size proved smaller than that anticipated in sample size calculations. This was due to the fact that the study was conducted after the start of the pandemic, during which non-essential tests such as spirometry were often deferred, and PFL infection control protocols limited patient volumes. It is possible that larger volumes in the future prospective study may demonstrate a significant effect on other study outcomes.

Conclusion

Given the prevalence, quality of life impact, and healthcare cost of COPD, along with the established benefit of smoking cessation and the demonstrated effectiveness of brief smoking cessation advice, it is imperative that we design strategies to increase delivery of this low-cost intervention, which remains underutilized across healthcare settings. Our personalized biomedical risk assessment infographic shows both feasibility for implementation and promise for effective provider behaviour change in a real-world, complex environment. However, before any further efficacy evaluation, intervention fidelity should be addressed through strategies such as system automation. Only after this has been addressed should this intervention be evaluated in a large RCT, with a validated smoking cessation endpoint. If successful in an efficacy trial, our use of OCR technology could then enable rapid scalability of this tool across systems and settings.
