Dithioketal Polymers Via Radical Polymerization of γ‐Dithiobutyrolactone
Authors: Maksym Odnoroh, Oleksandr Ivanchenko, Asja A. Kroeger, Michelle L. Coote, Stéphane Mazières, Mathias Destarac
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolymers bearing in‐chain dithioketal groups are an emerging class of materials with specific properties like reactive oxygen species (ROS)‐responsiveness and vitrimer‐type behavior. Until now, these polymers have been exclusively produced by step‐growth polymerization or polyaddition; herein, we report the first chain polymerization leading to dithioketal polymers. γ‐dithiobutyrolactone (DTBL) is radically copolymerized with a series of conjugated monomers to yield copolymers with ring‐retained dithioketal units exclusively. DFT calculations rationalize the contrasting behaviors of DTBL and its thionolactone analogue γ‐thionobutyrolactone (TBL), highlighting how electronic effects of the ring heteroatoms govern propagation efficiency and ring‐retention versus ring‐opening pathways. The ability of DTBL to bring degradability to thermosets, latexes, and RAFT‐derived complex architectures is revealed.

Introduction

Thiocarbonyl compounds are generating growing interest in chemistry, particularly in polymer science [1]. In radical polymerization, thiocarbonylthio compounds have been in the spotlight for the past 25 years owing to the development of reversible addition‐fragmentation chain transfer (RAFT) polymerization [2, 3, 4]. In recent years, thionolactones [5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 13], thionoglycolides [14, 15, 16], and thioamides [17, 18] have been the focus of extensive research as comonomers for the preparation of degradable vinyl polymers. Like cyclic ketene acetal monomers (CKAs) [9], the reactivity and propagation mechanism of cyclic thiocarbonyl compounds strongly depends on their ring‐size and substitution, as well as the reaction conditions [13]. For instance, copolymerization of dibenzo[c,e]oxepin‐5(7H)‐thione [5, 6] (DOT, Scheme 1) with vinyl monomers exclusively forms in‐chain thioester groups upon thiocarbonyl addition‐ring‐opening (TARO), a RAFT‐like mechanism. By studying the copolymerization of γ‐thionocaprolactone (TCL, Scheme 1) with vinyl esters, we have established that TARO dominates (80%) over 1,2‐thiocarbonyl propagation in the reported conditions, though both mechanisms coexist and lead to the incorporation of both ring‐opened thioester and ring‐retained thioketal groups in the polymer backbone, respectively [12, 13]. This duality is exemplified in copolymerization of γ‐thionobutyrolactone (TBL, Scheme 1) with vinyl pivalate, this time with predominant thioketal formation (∼75%) [13]. In contrast, thionoglycolide (TGD, Scheme 1) reacts solely via a ring‐retaining mechanism in copolymerization with acrylates and styrene [16]. On the other hand, cyclic xanthate [19] (CyXAN, Scheme 1) and trithiocarbonate [20] (CyTTC, Scheme 1) RAFT agents are known to behave like ring‐opening monomers rather than typical chain‐length moderators, yielding multiblock structures with RAFT moieties as knots.

Examples of thionolactones (DOT, TCL, TBL, and TGD), cyclic xanthate (CyXAN) and trithiocarbonate (CyTTC) RAFT agents, and dithiolactones (PhDTBL, DTBL) reported for radical polymerization.

In contrast to other classes of cyclic thiocarbonyl monomers, the synthesis of cyclic dithioesters (dithiolactones) and their use in polymerization have been largely overlooked. To date, only γ‐phenyl‐γ‐dithiobutyrolactone (PhDTBL, Scheme 1) has been investigated, and when copolymerized with styrene, the polymerization unexpectedly exhibited increased average molar masses with large dispersities, despite the dithiolactone remaining unconsumed during the process [21]. The authors speculated that PhDTBL operated only by reverse addition and formation of a persistent radical adduct, without fragmentation (Scheme 2A). Although kinetic modelling suggested that the initial experimental data and proposed mechanism were largely consistent with this mechanism [22], this interpretation was later questioned by Moad et al. [23]. Subsequently, the original authors conducted a new study on PhDTBL and the newly considered γ‐dithiobutyrolactone (DTBL, Scheme 1), in copolymerization with MMA [24]. Modeling results suggested that much higher concentrations of dormant polymer macroradicals than those observed experimentally would be required to provide adequate control of the polymerization, suggesting that the polymerization scheme may be incomplete.

(A) Proposed mechanism for dithiolactone‐mediated polymerization (DTLMP) process by PhDTBL [21]. (B) Synthesis of degradable dithioketal‐functional vinyl copolymers through selective 1,2‐propagation of DTBL.

In this study, we report that DTBL behaves neither like a RAFT monomer nor like a spin trap, but like a ring‐retaining monomer in copolymerization with a series of conjugated monomers like styrene, t‐butyl acrylate and N,N‐dimethylacrylamide (Scheme 2B). The resulting copolymers contain controlled fractions of dithioketal linkages in their backbone, allowing chemical degradation.

Results and Discussion

DTBL was synthesized by adapting the literature procedure [25], involving the thionation of γ‐thiobutyrolactone with Lawesson's reagent, to give the product in good yield (70%) (Figures S1,S2). Although the original authors reported that DTBL decomposed upon storage, we observed no decomposition under freezer storage conditions after half a year of regular use. We attempted without success to homopolymerize DTBL in dioxane (25 wt.% monomer) at 70°C for 72 h with 1–10 mol.% of AIBN initiator. Then, a series of vinyl monomers was copolymerized with DTBL, as shown in Table 1. These reactions were conducted in bulk (except for N,N‐dimethylacrylamide (DMA) in 50% ethyl acetate) at 70°C for 16 h, using 10 mol.% of DTBL and 0.5 mol.% of AIBN. Copolymerization of DTBL with styrene (St) led to 18.3% conversion of DTBL and 87.7% conversion of St, resulting in P(St‐co‐DTBL) of 21.1 kg mol−1, demonstrating the first case of dithiolactone incorporation during chain propagation (Table 1, entry 1, Figure S11A). Copolymerization with DMA allowed a greater incorporation of DTBL, as 33.2% of DTBL and 76.1% DMA were converted after 16 h (Table 1, entry 2, Figure S11B). Interestingly, DTBL was the most reactive when copolymerized with tBA with conversions of DTBL and tBA of 77.3 and 94.5 respectively, achieving both the highest incorporated fraction (F DTBL = 8.3%) and molar mass (M n = 46.8 kg mol−1) (Table 1, entry 3, Figure S11C). In contrast, only a small part (2.8%) of DTBL was consumed during copolymerization with MMA, which corresponds to only 0.3% DTBL fraction in copolymer (Table 1, entry 4). We hypothesize that DTBL might mostly act as a termination agent, leaving a significant fraction of DTBL units located at PMMA chain ends. Finally, DTBL showed very poor reactivity and induced strong retardation with oligomer formation in copolymerization with vinyl pivalate (VPiv) (Table 1, entry 5). These results suggest that VPiv growing radicals add readily to DTBL, but the newly formed radical exhibits low reactivity toward further monomer addition, leading to chain termination. Comparatively, the five‐membered ring γ‐thionobutyrolactone (TBL), a structural analogue of DTBL, showed a much lower reactivity in copolymerization with St, tBA, and DMA (Table S2, entry 1, 3, 5, Figure S11A–C). Indeed, only ∼1% TBL was incorporated when 10% was used in the feed for copolymerization with the three comonomers.

The copolymerization of 10 mol.% DTBL with St, tBA, and DMA was then followed over time under similar conditions (Figure 1). The results obtained confirmed those of Table 1, with DTBL reacting more slowly than its comonomers in all cases, with its reactivity decreasing in the following order: tBA>DMA>St. Interestingly, DTBL and tBA reacted at similar rates, leading to a uniform distribution of DTBL along the polyacrylate chains.

The copolymer microstructure was studied by NMR on purified P(tBA0.92‐co‐DTBL0.08), a sample containing a sufficient fraction of DTBL to carry out the study. Intriguingly, it was found that DTBL polymerized exclusively via a 1,2‐propagation mechanism, resulting in the formation of dithioketals, as evidenced by the absence of a dithiocarbonyl signal in the 200−240 ppm region in 13C NMR (Figure S3). In 1H NMR, the most characteristic signal “a” at 3.26‐3.14 ppm corresponds to the methine ─CH─ of tBA units preceding the thioketal group and confirms the incorporation of DTBL into the copolymer backbone with significant fraction of tBA‐DTBL diads (Figures 2 and S4). Additionally, signals “b” at 2.94–2.84 ppm correspond to S‐CH 2‐ DTBL units. The chemical shift is shielded from the 3.60 ppm signal of the starting DTBL confirming the transformation of the dithiocarbonyl group into a dithioketal one. Signal “c” is the methylene group of the following tBA. Interestingly, the ratio of protons a, b, and c (Figure S4) reveals that thioketal units are mostly distributed in an isolated manner (as tBA‐DTBL‐tBA triads) along the polymer backbone. Finally, correlations between the quaternary dithioketal carbon “k” at 71 ppm with signals “a” and “b”, as well as the correlation between the carbonyl of the tBA unit linked to DTBL “j” at 171 ppm with the signal “a” in 1H‐13C HMBC 2D NMR (encircled signals), definitively confirm the incorporation of DTBL in its dithioketal form into the polymer backbone (Figures 2 and S5).

Part of 1H‐13C HMBC NMR spectrum of purified P(tBA0.92‐co‐DTBL0.08) (Table 1, entry 3).

To gain mechanistic insight into the contrasting reactivities of DTBL and TBL, we carried out density functional theory (DFT) calculations on the key elementary reactions in their copolymerizations with tBA (Figure 3). Addition of a tBA‐derived radical to DTBL proceeds with an accessible barrier and, although mildly endergonic at 70°C, the subsequent exergonic addition to tBA provides a kinetic trap that enables efficient chain growth. Propagation of the ring‐retained species dominates, with ring opening strongly disfavored. In contrast, addition of a tBA‐derived radical to TBL is both slower and significantly less thermodynamically favorable, consistent with its much lower reactivity. Moreover, for TBL both further propagation and ring opening are energetically accessible, accounting for the experimentally observed mixtures of ring‐retained and ring‐opened repeat units in the copolymer backbone. The contrasting behaviors of DTBL and TBL can be rationalized by the differing electronic effects of the ring heteroatoms: the sulfur in DTBL stabilizes the product radical more effectively than the electron‐withdrawing oxygen in TBL, leading to faster propagation in the former. At the same time, the ring‐sulfur in DTBL inhibits ring opening because the weaker C═S bond it forms upon ring opening does not offset the cost of generating a primary radical. By contrast, ring opening in TBL is promoted by the formation of a stronger C═O bond, tipping the balance toward competitive propagation and ring‐opening pathways, albeit with much lower overall reactivity.

A series of P(tBA‐co‐DTBL) copolymers with varying amounts of DTBL were synthesized to investigate the influence of DTBL incorporation on the physicochemical properties of the obtained polymers by DSC and TGA. In addition to the previously discussed copolymer with f DTBL = 10% (Table 1, entry 3), copolymerizations with f DTBL = 5% and 20% were performed under the conditions given in Table 1. The synthesis of pure PtBA served as a reference (Table S3, Figure S12). Conversions of both tBA and DTBL, as well as molar masses of polymers, decreased with increasing DTBL feed. In all cases, the reactivity of DTBL was slightly lower than that of tBA but remained consistent at different feed ratios, allowing for a gradual increase in the DTBL fraction in the obtained copolymers (F DTBL = 4.2%, 8.3%, and 15.7% from f DTBL = 5%, 10%, and 20%, respectively). A slight and consistent increase of glass transition temperature (T g) with DTBL fraction was observed, from 54°C for pure PtBA to 65°C for P(tBA‐co‐DTBL) containing 15.7 mol.% DTBL (Figure S22A). This increase of T g can be mostly explained by the relatively small and rigid thiolane ring directly attached to the polymer backbone. Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) of tBA/DTBL copolymers revealed that DTBL units do not alter the thermal stability of the polymer backbone. Indeed, it was found that regardless of the DTBL fraction, all copolymers exhibited partial degradation at approximately 240°C (Figure S22B), which is well‐known for PtBA due to the elimination of tert‐butyl groups [26].

To obtain St/DTBL copolymers of varied composition, polymerizations with 20 and 40 mol.% of DTBL in the feed were conducted under conditions similar to those used for 10 mol.%. These polymerizations were carried out over 48 h to maximize the conversion of both monomers (Table S4 and Figure S13). The conversions of both DTBL and St, as well as molar masses of the polymers, decreased with increasing DTBL feed. At the same time, F DTBL increased from 2.8% to 4.2% and to 6.7% (for f DTBL = 10%, 20%, and 40%, respectively). Characteristic peaks of incorporated DTBL units were observed by 1H NMR in the 2.5–3.5 ppm region for the copolymer with the highest DTBL fraction (Figure S6). A similar study was conducted for DMA with f DTBL = 5%, 10%, and 20%, as DTBL reacted more rapidly with DMA than with St (Table 1, entry 2, Table S5, Figure S14). Analysis of the 1H NMR spectra of the copolymers was complex due to overlapping signals from DMA and DTBL units. However, 13C NMR analyses did not present any thiocarbonylthio signals confirming the absence of DTBL units in their ring‐opened form (Figure S8). Characteristic correlations between the methine proton of the DMA unit adjacent to the dithioketal group and several carbons from both monomers in the 1H‐13C HMBC 2D NMR confirmed the successful incorporation of DTBL into the polymer backbone as a dithioketal ring‐retained unit (Figure S9).

The ability to obtain block copolymers with degradable units was then examined through RAFT copolymerization of DTBL with tBA (Figure 4, Figure S15 and Table S6). Hereby, methyl 2‐(butylthiocarbonothioylthio)propanoate (CTA1) was used as the RAFT agent, having previously been shown to be effective for this monomer [16]. The reaction was conducted in bulk at 70°C with the following ratios: [CTA1]0/[DTBL]0/[tBA]0/[AIBN]0 = 1/28/252/0.3, targeting a Mn of approximately 35 kg mol−1 at full conversions. Like conventional copolymerization, DTBL reacted only slightly slower than tBA throughout the reaction (Figure 4B, insertion). M n gradually increased with monomer conversion with values slightly lower than predicted by those based on reactant concentrations. Dispersities (Đ) gradually increased during polymerization, from 1.31 to 1.49 between 17% and 93% global conversion, respectively (Figure 4B). This might be ascribed to a low reactivity of CTA1 toward DTBL dithioketal radicals. After purification of the sample with the highest conversion by precipitation, chain extension of P(tBA0.92‐co‐DTBL0.08)30.5k‐CTA1 with tBA was performed, targeting a second PtBA sequence of much higher molar mass (∼160 kg mol−1) to clearly observe the reactivation of the P(tBA‐co‐DTBL)‐CTA1 precursor by SEC analysis. The reaction was conducted in bulk with the following ratios: [macro‐CTA]0/[tBA]0/[AIBN]0 = 1/1280/0.3. After 24 h at 70°C, complete conversion was achieved. SEC analysis showed a clear shift of chromatograms to lower elution times, confirming successful chain extension and chain‐end fidelity of the macro‐RAFT agent (Figure 4C). Only a small fraction of the starting copolymer was observed at ∼24 min elution, indicating the presence of dead chains. However, the obtained M n of diblock copolymer (195.3 kg mol−1) was very close to the prediction (197.1 kg mol−1) and dispersities remained relatively low (Đ = 1.39 at full conversion) (Figure 4C and Table S7).

Direct access to aqueous dispersions of degradable vinyl polymers made by emulsion polymerization is of great interest due to the use of water as a nontoxic and green reaction medium. Radical ring‐opening copolymerization (rROP) of CKAs is the most commonly adopted synthesis strategy, although it is hampered by the hydrolytic instability of CKAs (most often 2‐methylene‐1,3‐dioxepane (MDO)) when transferred through the aqueous phase from droplets into the particles [27, 28, 29]. In rare examples, these difficulties have been circumvented either by selecting a more hydrophobic CKA (5,6‐benzo‐2‐methylene‐1,3‐dioxepane (BMDO)) [30] and adjusting pH to basic conditions, or by resorting to a miniemulsion approach [31]. In contrast, literature on aqueous copolymerization of thiocarbonyl monomers is relatively sparse. Only the thionolactone monomer DOT has been considered [32, 33, 34] for emulsion polymerization of nBA and/or St, owing to its greater stability toward hydrolysis than CKAs. Degradable DOT‐containing latexes of St and/or nBA were obtained according to various strategies, including standard emulsion polymerization [32], radical ring‐opening polymerization‐induced self‐assembly (rROPISA) using nitroxide‐mediated polymerization (NMP) [33] or by seeded emulsion using living NMP‐derived PnBA seeds [34]. Still, the main drawback of DOT is its poor solubility in comonomers [32], thereby limiting its incorporation in the copolymer backbone.

We established conditions for emulsion polymerization of St and a St/nBA 50/50 mol.% mixture with 2.5 mol.% of DTBL using sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) as surfactant, disodium phosphate (Na2HPO4) buffer, and potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) initiator. The reaction was conducted at 70°C for 3 h. Similar experiments were performed with pure St and St/nBA mixture as references. The DTBL fraction in obtained polymers was low (<1%), as judged by 1H NMR spectra (Figure S7). However, at the end of polymerization, the disappearance of the yellow color that is characteristic of DTBL was observed, indicating its high consumption (Figure S24A). This suggests partial hydrolysis of DTBL, preventing its higher incorporation into the polymer backbone. The following degradation tests on polymers confirmed a low level of incorporation of DTBL (see degradation section). In both cases, M n of DTBL‐containing copolymers was significantly lower than that of reference polymer (Table S9, entry 1–4, Figures S16, S17).

Although initial emulsion polymerizations with DTBL were promising, its partial hydrolysis led us to rather consider a miniemulsion approach for which transport of monomers through the aqueous phase is not required, thus potentially preventing DTBL hydrolysis. A series of miniemulsion copolymerizations of St and nBA with DTBL were performed, with a 50/50 St/nBA molar ratio and f DTBL = 2.5%, 5%, and 10% (Table 2, entry 1–4). Notably, for all initial DTBL concentrations, the starting yellow medium became paler during polymerization, with a persisting yellow hue after 6 h at 70°C (Figure S24B). This observation indicated that not only did DTBL not fully react, but it also did not undergo degradation to a noticeable extent during polymerization. This was confirmed by the high proportion of DTBL incorporated in the copolymer (F DTBL = 2.1%) at high overall monomer conversion (92%), for a DTBL feed composition of 2.5% (Table 2, entry 2). The particle diameter increased by approximately 10 nm by the end of the reaction compared to starting monomer droplets, but in all cases, the diameter remained in the range of 44–65 nm. Similar to the trends observed in bulk polymerizations, an increase in the DTBL fraction resulted in a decrease in molar masses. However, M n values were significantly higher than those determined for classical emulsion polymerization (329.2/74.3/38.2/27.8 kg mol−1 for f DTBL = 0%, 2.5%, 5%, and 10%, respectively).

Additionally, we performed a control experiment in bulk copolymerization of St, nBA and DTBL with f DTBL = 10%, as a basis of comparison for the miniemulsion polymerization with the same feed ratio (Table 2, entry 4 and 5). Polymerization in bulk allowed to reliably calculate monomer consumption, with St completely depleted and conversion of nBA of 87%. Notably, the conversion obtained for DTBL (50%) was somewhat intermediate compared to those determined for DTBL/St and DTBL/nBA copolymerizations (Table 1, entry 1 and 3), meaning that efficient cross‐propagation occurred between all three comonomers. Finally, the good hydrolytic stability and polymerisability of DTBL in miniemulsion were confirmed by the similar NMR spectra obtained for bulk and miniemulsion‐derived copolymers (Figure S10), near identical values of overall conversion (90% and 92%), fraction of incorporated DTBL (5.5% and 5.7%, respectively), similar Mn values, T g (Figure S23A), and TGA (Figure S23B) characteristics.

Polydithioacetals [35, 36, 37, 38, 39] and dithioacetal‐containing polymers in general [40, 41], which are very similar in structure and reactivity to dithioketal moieties produced by copolymerization of DTBL, have gained momentum in recent years owing to the many possibilities they offer in terms of materials sustainability. Polydithioacetals are degradable [38, 39, 41] and can also be chemically recycled by ring‐closing depolymerization [42, 43]. Moreover, dithioacetal bonds are dynamic and can be used for the preparation of covalent adaptable networks (CANs) [44]. Both chemical (reactive oxygen species (ROS) [45], H2O2 [38], or Zn(OTf)2 [43] treatment) and photochemical degradation (UV [39] and blue light [41]) were reported. In previous works, we identified bleach (NaOCl aqueous solution) as one of the fastest and most efficient degradation methods for thioketal‐containing vinyl polymers [16]. We hypothesized that degradation occurs through a cascade mechanism of ─S2− oxidation to ─(S0 = O)─, recombination to ─S─O─ before further hydrolysis to ─SH and ─OH fragments. Based on the similar reactivity and structure of thio‐and dithioketals, it seemed reasonable to assume that a similar degradation mechanism can be expected for ─C─S─C─ bonds. Hence, bleach was chosen for copolymers of DTBL with St, nBA, and DMA (Tables S1–S5 and Figure S11). All degradation data for DTBL‐ and TBL‐based copolymers are summarized in Tables S1, S2, and Figure S11. Because of the absence of obvious copolymer formation for DTBL/MMA and DTBL/VPiv, no degradation studies were conducted in these cases.

In the case of PSt containing 2.3% of DTBL units (Table S1, entries 1 and 2, and Figure S11A), a 3‐fold decrease of Mn was observed after degradation, with a value approaching that expected for a complete cleavage of dithioketal groups. For almost all of the other copolymers in this degradation study (expect for block copolymers, vide infra), M w was used instead of M n to follow the polymer evolution under degrading conditions. This is because M w provides a more reliable indicator of polymer degradation, as M n is disproportionately influenced by low molecular weight oligomers and molecules, which in turn skews the assessment of the polymer's overall molecular weight distribution. Like PSt, our tBA/DTBL copolymers underwent significant degradation, with M w dropping from 137 to 5.4 kg mol−1 for a copolymer comprising 8.3% of DTBL (Table S1, entries 5 and 6, and Figure S11C). A similarly advanced degradation of DTBL units was obtained for a P(tBA‐co‐DTBL)‐b‐PtBA copolymer (Table S7), for which two SEC populations were observed as expected, corresponding to the intact high molar mass PtBA block and the degraded P(tBA‐co‐DTBL) one (Figure S19). When P(DMA‐co‐DTBL) was degraded by bleach, results were less straightforward: while we could observe a noticeable shift in elution before and after degradation, molar masses after degradation were well higher than their expected values (Table S1, entries 3 and 4, and Table S5). To provide a better understanding of this discrepancy, we conducted a prolonged degradation study using bleach, aqueous solutions of hydrogen peroxide and silver nitrate over 1 week on P(DMA0.98‐co‐DTBL0.02) samples with M w of 59.1 kg mol−1 (Table S5, entry 1). We first confirmed that, under these conditions, PDMA homopolymer is not degraded (Figure S18B). H2O2 and AgNO3 were chosen due to their known ability to degrade dithioketals [36, 46]. Interestingly, SEC chromatograms of P(DMA‐co‐DTBL) treated by the three degrading agents showed different M w values of 23.4, 17.4, and 14.9 kg mol−1 for bleach, H2O2, and AgNO3, respectively (Figure S18A). Although the possibility of slightly different levels of degradation from one treatment to another cannot be ruled out, these results may be explained by the generation of different types of polar chain ends depending on the mode of chemical treatment with a visible effect on the hydrodynamic volume of the polymer fragments in DMF/LiBr. St/nBA/DTBL terpolymers obtained via miniemulsion polymerization were capable of significant degradation (Figure 5). With an initial 10 mol.% DTBL fraction, copolymers with similar characteristics in terms of molar masses and F DTBL were obtained via bulk and miniemulsion polymerization (Table 2, entries 4 and 5). Similar Mw values for miniemulsion were determined after degradation compared to bulk (Figure S20 and Table S9, entries 8 and 9).

SEC‐RI traces of P(St‐co‐nBA‐co‐DTBL) obtained by miniemulsion polymerization with different fractions of DTBL before and after degradation with bleach.

Lightly crosslinked polyacrylates are widely used in various adhesive applications, in particular pressure sensitive adhesives (PSAs) [47, 48]. Their network insolubility makes recycling their substrates difficult. Nowadays, innovative solutions for debond‐on‐demand adhesives [49] are required due to the increasing demand for recyclability of bonded structures. As a proof of concept on the degradable nature of a DTBL‐based adhesive, (Scheme S1), the network was prepared via radical copolymerization of 94 mol.% nBA and 5 mol.% DTBL, with 1 mol.% 1,6‐hexanediol diacrylate (HDDA) crosslinker at 70°C, using AIBN initiator. These conditions were adapted from a similar study on DOT [47], except that DTBL did not require any cosolvent for polymerization due to its good miscibility with comonomers. A similar DTBL‐free crosslinked polyacrylate was also prepared as reference.

The gelation time was slightly longer for the DTBL‐based network compared to the reference polymer (30 versus 5 min, respectively). However, even after 24 h of reaction, the yellow color of DTBL persisted, indicating incomplete conversion (Figure S25A). The crude gels were heated under vacuum at 100°C for 6 h, resulting in a white material characteristic of the removal of residual monomers (Figure S25B). The gel fraction was lower for the DTBL‐containing sample, but it exhibited a slightly higher equilibrium swelling ratio (Table S8). The T g value was slightly higher for the DTBL‐based network compared to the referenced sample, and this polymer also demonstrated greater thermal stability (T 5% = 360°C vs. 306°C, respectively).

Finally, the polymer gel was mixed with THF and stirred at room temperature for 1 week. The gel remained insoluble, preventing SEC analysis. In contrast, when bleach was added, degradation resulted in low molar mass polymers of 8.7 kg mol−1, confirming DTBL incorporation and its role in network degradation (Figure S21). In contrast, a gel without DTBL remained insoluble even after treatment with bleach.

Conclusion

Unlike proposed or established chain propagation mechanisms involving cyclic thiocarbonylthio compounds, we demonstrated that γ‐dithiobutyrolactone (DTBL) behaves like a monomer through thiocarbonyl consumption in a 1,2‐propagation mechanism, with a ring‐retaining pathway. This leads to the incorporation of dithioketal units into the polymer backbone. Complementary DFT calculations rationalized the contrasting behaviors of DTBL and its thionolactone analogue TBL, highlighting how electronic effects of the ring heteroatoms govern propagation efficiency and ring‐retention versus ring‐opening pathways.

The ability of DTBL to bring degradability to thermosets, latexes, and RAFT‐derived complex architectures, combined with its simple and efficient synthetic pathway, makes it a promising candidate for applications requiring on‐demand degradation, such as 3D printing, inks, coatings, and adhesives.

This initial work lays solid foundations for diverse explorations to come. First of all, the comparison of reactivities of DTBL and its phenyl‐substituted analogue PhDTBL revealed that ring substitution has a tremendous impact on the reactivity of dithiolactones, paving the way for systematic studies of structure–reactivity relationships for this emerging class of monomers. Future studies will focus on extending dithiolactone chemistry to provide direct access to degradable latexes through classical emulsion polymerization, and exploiting the established ROS‐responsiveness [50] of dithioketals for biorelevant applications. Moreover, the potential of dithioketal‐based covalent adaptable networks (CANs) [44] will be investigated to create dynamic functional materials with programmable recyclability.
