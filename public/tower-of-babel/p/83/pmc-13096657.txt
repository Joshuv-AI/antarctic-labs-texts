Reaction-induced modification of Co nanoclusters driven by Co-Mn interfacial sites to control selectivity in CO2 hydrogenation
Authors: Hui Kang, Rong Cao, Yanlin Zhang, Xu Wang, Luming Li, Wei Chu, Riguang Zhang, Siglinda Perathoner, Gabriele Centi, Yuefeng Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Controlling the selectivity of chemical products on small Co nanoparticles is crucial in many catalytic applications. Reaction-driven structural changes offer an alternative methodology to regulate their properties. Herein, carbon-induced surface restructuring occurs on a 2Co/MnOₓ catalyst (cobalt nanoclusters with 2% mass loading on manganese oxide) during thermal CO2 hydrogenation, driven by the formation of bridging Co-C-O-Mn interfacial sites. This leads to a shift in selectivity from methane to CO, with a remarkable enhancement of the CO/CH4 product ratio from 0.89 to 13.4. Such a Co/MnOx system has unique interfacial properties, including strong carbonophilic and oxophilic characteristics. It chemisorbs reaction-derived CO and facilitates C-O bond breaking, promoting rapid CO dissociation and subsequent carbon coverage on Co nanoclusters. This restructuring of Co nanoclusters suppresses the hydrogenation of CO intermediates to methane. This effect is unique to 2Co/MnOₓ and absent at higher/lower Co loadings or other oxide supports. This insight shows how structural evolution during catalysis enables precise surface engineering, overcoming the structure-sensitivity limits of Co nanoclusters.

Introduction

Cobalt nanoclusters serve as active elements in numerous industrially relevant applications, including Fischer-Tropsch synthesis (FTS), CO2 methanation, alkane dehydrogenation, bioethanol conversion, and electrocatalytic nitrogen reduction, among others1–5. Often, these reactions are structure-sensitive. As exemplified in FTS, where the optimal cobalt particle size is around 6–8 nm6, the use of smaller nanoparticles would be preferable for metal utilisation. Co nanoclusters exhibit remarkable capabilities to activate H-H and C-O bonds and facilitate C-C coupling reactions, establishing them as widely utilized active centres in FTS for producing high-value-added multi-carbon compounds (CxHyOz) from synthesis gas (CO + H2)7,8. The methanation reaction is an undesired process and needs be suppressed1,9. Carbonisation of Co during the FTS process modulates catalytic selectivity10,11. Carbon encapsulation of Co nanoparticles and the formation of cobalt carbide (Co2C) serve as methods to tune selectivity, particularly promoting olefin and oxygenate formation in FTS9,12. However, these transformations simultaneously inhibit the water-gas shift reaction and exhibit instability under CO2/H2 feeds compared to syngas, rendering them unsuitable for direct CO2-to-olefins (FTO) production13,14. Enhancing the CO/CH4 ratio during CO2 hydrogenation provides a crucial foundation for subsequent Fischer-Tropsch and methanol industries that utilize CO as a key platform molecule15,16. This strategy is particularly advantageous as it can be seamlessly integrated into existing industrial frameworks17. This depends on the controlled modulation of the catalyst’s surface microstructure.

Reaction-driven structural changes offer an alternative methodology with industrial potential for regulating catalyst structure and overcoming these limitations18. Unravelling the complex mechanisms driving these gas-induced structural changes represents a pioneering approach in catalyst design, offering new perspectives for the development of tailored catalysts19. A catalyst structure that is deactivated toward one reaction may serve as an excellent active site for another reaction. In other words, strategically designing the reaction atmosphere to induce the specific structure required for a target reaction represents a valuable catalyst design principle with industrial potential. In particular, the surface carbon species formed during CO/CO2 hydrogenation reactions have a complex impact on the reaction process, including phenomena such as carbon coating20, carbon infiltration21, and carburization22. The tunability of these fine structures is crucial for both catalyst design and advancing our understanding of the reaction mechanisms. It is necessary to clarify how structural changes depend on the inducing atmosphere and material properties.

By adjusting catalyst composition (support type and Co loading) and pretreatment conditions under various gases (such as pure CO2, CO, and H2, as well as CH4, CO, or CO2 mixed with H₂), we unravelled the switches governing the structural evolution of Co-based catalysts during reaction and related selectivity modulation during CO2 hydrogenation (reverse water gas-shift reaction). Notably, the 2Co/MnOx catalyst, with 2% cobalt on MnOx, exhibited a pronounced, rapid surface carbon species formation instead of carbide (CoxC) generation during CO2 hydrogenation, shifting the primary product from CH4 to CO. Comprehensive surface structure characterization and in-situ experiments revealed that the carbon-reconstructed Co surface decisively weakened CO adsorption and hydrogenation. This structural evolution critically depends on the strong oxophilicity of the MnOx support and the carbonophilic nature of the Co metal, which facilitates CO chemisorption in the Co-C-O-Mn configuration at the Co/MnO interface. The disproportionation of adsorbed CO follows this to form C atoms that migrate across the Co nanoclusters’ surface. They thus represent a novel advance in inducing controlled gas-induced modifications for Co nanoclusters beyond current carbon-modified Co nanocatalysts (including Mn-promoted materials)23–25. This study employs a controllable gas-induced strategy to modify the surface properties of active metals. It elucidates how evolved structures mechanistically affect reaction performance and further highlights the critical significance of such gas-induced strategies in regulating catalyst surface structures at the nano- and even atomic levels.

Results

Reaction-induced Co/MnOx catalyst changes modulate the RWGS behaviour

The catalytic performance of Co/MnOx catalysts was evaluated in CO2 hydrogenation at ambient pressure (H2/CO2 ratio = 4). Figure S1 shows that the reactivity of the 2Co/MnOx catalyst changes significantly between 300 and 500 °C, with CO selectivity rising from 52.6% to nearly 100% before stabilizing at ~88% at 500 °C. In the second run, high CO selectivity (e.g., 90.2% at 400 oC) is maintained across the entire temperature range tested. When the reaction temperature is maintained at 400 oC, the CO selectivity of the 2Co/MnOx catalyst rises from 45.7% to 94.0% after 5 h, while CO2 conversion decreases (Fig. 1a and S2). The initial-to-final reaction stage selectivity shift (CO/CH4 ratio) for the 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Fig. 1b) surged from 0.89 (initial, ~1 h) to 13.4 (final, ~18 h). The CO2 conversion trend aligns with the literature, indicating that the RWGS reaction rate is slower than the methanation reaction rate26 due to the higher thermodynamic equilibrium conversion for methanation27 and CO poisoning of active sites28. Despite these changes, the RWGS performance of 2Co/MnOx remains superior to other Co-based catalysts (Table S1). The Co-loading significantly impacts the rate of this structural evolution (Fig. 1a and S2). For the 1Co/MnOx catalyst (1 wt% Co), CO selectivity increases from 89% to 96% within 20−40 min of reaction. In contrast, the 5Co/MnOx catalyst (5 wt% Co) shows low CO selectivity even after 18 hours on stream. Other conventional oxide-supported cobalt catalysts (2Co/TiO2 and 2Co/SiO2) were further synthesized and compared with 2Co/MnOx in RWGS at 400 °C (Figs. 1a, 1b, S2, and S3). These catalysts display minimal changes in CO2 conversion and product selectivity (CO/CH4 ratio), highlighting that the MnOx support and moderate Co loading are crucial for this transformation.

During CO2 hydrogenation, the catalyst undergoes structural changes, but the specific gas species driving this transformation was unclear due to complex gas interactions. To better understand the performance transition, simplified gas mixtures were used to treat the 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Figs. 1c, S4, and S5). Figure 1c compares the CO₂ hydrogenation performance of the 2Co/MnOx catalyst after various gas treatments, including pure CO2, CO, and H2, as well as CH4, CO, or CO2 mixed with H2. Notably, CO selectivity exceeded 90% after treatment with CO or CO2-containing gases (COx), whereas H2 or CH4 + H2 treatment failed to trigger rapid, selective transformation. A CO2:H2 ratio of 1:4 was optimal, balancing high CO selectivity with good RWGS reactivity (Fig. 1c). The catalytic performance of the 2Co/MnOx catalyst varies with time on stream at 300 °C, 400 °C, and 500 °C (Figure S6). This is likely due to intrinsic reaction differences from thermodynamic and kinetic variations at different temperatures. However, when the catalysts pretreated at these three temperatures for 18 h were tested at a constant 400 oC, they showed similar catalytic performance (Fig. 1d). Thus, the surface in-situ restructuring between 300 and 500 °C is temperature-independent but kinetically influenced by temperature.

Identification of the in-situ changes during the reaction

The selectivity in CO2 hydrogenation is often associated with the type of surface intermediates, particularly formate and CO species29,30. CO2 + H2-DRIFTs analysis of the 2Co/MnOx catalyst before and after (denoted as 2Co/MnOx-U) reaction gas treatment revealed identical intermediate formation. The gas treatment did not significantly alter the surface structure of MnOx support related to these intermediates (Figure S7)30. Thus, the change in catalyst selectivity is attributed to alterations in the Co species or the Co-MnOx interface, which affect the subsequent hydrogenation pathways of the intermediates (i.e., formate or CO). The 2Co/MnOx sample exhibited complex CO adsorption configurations (Fig. 2a), with infrared vibrations at ~2022 cm−1 corresponding to linear CO adsorption and three peaks below 2000 cm−1 indicating bridge CO adsorption. This suggests that MnOx enhances bridge-bonded CO formation on Co-based catalysts31. Temperature-programmed CO-DRIFTs on the 2Co/MnOx sample revealed a gradual decrease in surface CO species (Fig. 2c). Notably, the linear CO adsorption peak ( > 2000 cm−1) experienced a blue shift (towards higher wavenumbers) during heating, indicating weakened CO adsorption. However, reduced CO coverage would typically be expected to cause a red shift (toward lower wavenumbers) due to altered chemisorption bond strength, as demonstrated by Bertolini et al.32 and Kalhara et al.33 through experiments and DFT calculations. The anomalous results from the temperature-programmed CO-DRIFT experiment suggest that the cobalt active centres undergo an intrinsic structural change during CO treatment, progressively weakening CO adsorption. Temperature-programmed reaction (TPR) profiles of CO2 + H2 and CO + H2 over the 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Fig. 2d) showed a rapid decline in CO2 methanation activity at 417 oC. In comparison, two separate peaks emerged at 329 oC and 497 oC for CO + H2-TPR. The results indicate that the restructured cobalt sites increased the energy barrier for CO hydrogenation. This strongly suggests that CO may be a key species in driving the catalyst’s structural evolution.

XRD patterns of 2Co/MnOx and 2Co/MnOx-U catalysts exhibited only MnO diffraction lines with no detectable Co species (Figure S8). At the same time, TEM images showed moiré fringes adjacent to Co nanoclusters (Fig. 2e, g) due primarily to the overlap of lattice fringes between Co and MnO34. Though the fresh 2Co/MnOx catalyst exhibited Co nanoparticles with an average diameter of ~4.0 nm, a modest increase to 4.7 nm is observed after 18 h of time on stream at 400 oC (Fig. 2e–h). The 2Co/MnOx catalyst maintains Co species in nanoparticle form before and after the reaction, as confirmed by EDS mapping of elemental distribution (Figure S9, S10). Cobalt-based catalysts exhibit significant structure sensitivity in CO2 hydrogenation1, with small Co nanoparticles (single atoms or nanoclusters) favoring RWGS35, while larger Co nanoparticles promote CH4 production36. This analysis clearly indicates that the increased CO selectivity is not due to variations in Co particle size.

Furthermore, semi-in-situ XPS analysis of 2Co/MnOx and 2Co/MnOx-U (before and after reaction gas treatment) detected no CoCx species (at 283.1 eV)14 (Figure S11a). As indicated in the introduction, particle size and the formation of a Co-carbide species are two key factors to alter CO2 hydrogenation selectivity when the catalyst composition remains unchanged. Even with the enhanced surface elemental resolution afforded by synchrotron NAP-XPS, no formation of CoCx species is discernible under CO2 + H2 atmospheres (Figure S11b). This fact is justified by the pretreatment temperature (400 oC) used in this study, which exceeds the stable temperature of CoCx (260 oC)14,37. Thus, neither changes in particle size nor carbide formation account for the evolution of selectivity, highlighting an alternative restructuring mechanism. Sun et al.38 reported that CO dissociation into surface carbon species inhibited subsequent CO adsorption on the Pt/FeOx catalyst due to the excessively strong CO adsorption, which led to a low reactivity39. Analogously, the fresh 2Co/MnOx catalyst exhibited strong CO adsorption ability with a bridged configuration (Fig. 2a). In contrast, temperature-programmed CO treatment revealed a reduced CO adsorption strength (Fig. 2c), suggesting that reactant gas exposure induced a notable suppression of CO adsorption capacity on the 2Co/MnOx catalyst, consistent with the deposition of surface carbon species.

Further temperature-programmed hydrogenation (TPH) and temperature-programmed oxidation (TPO) analyses of the catalyst after reaction at 400 oC for 5 and 18 h (Fig. 3a and S12) provided definitive evidence of surface carbon deposition (identified as polymeric carbon species), with TPH showing three distinct peaks of CH4 formation at 197 oC (highly reactive surface atomic carbon species), 325 oC (metal-carbon species), and 550–590 oC (polymeric carbon species)40,41. Analogously, the TPO analysis revealed three CO2 evolution peaks at 116 oC, 237 oC, and 363–378 oC, which sequentially align with the carbon species identified in the TPH analysis42. The lower temperatures observed in the TPO compared to TPH suggested a higher sensitivity of surface carbon species to oxidation by O2. The comparison of results for samples after 5 h and 18 h of catalytic testing shows that surface atomic carbon and metal-carbon species increased with time on stream, while polymeric carbon species tended to accumulate progressively over time (Fig. 3a). The similar Mn 3 s and O 1 s spectra of semi-in-situ XPS before and after the reaction over a 2Co/MnOx catalyst suggest that the valence state of Mn (Mn2+) in the MnO support and the surface oxygen species remain largely unaffected by carbon deposition (Figures S13a−13b). There is also a similar Co 2p spectra at 780.3 eV after carbon deposition (Figure S13c). However, due to the minimal number of interfacial Co-C-Mn sites, the sensitivity is insufficient to prove that the average charge of Co atoms decreases after C deposition. Thereby, DFT calculations were employed to demonstrate that surface charge transfer in Co species after carbon deposition inhibits subsequent CO adsorption (Figures S14, S15). Bader charge analysis reveals that the Co atoms in the C-deposited Co cluster have lower average charges compared to the pristine Co cluster, indicating electron transfer from Co to the carbon species (Figure S15a). This electron depletion weakens Co’s adsorption of CO molecules. The projected density of states (PDOS) in Figure S15b also shows that the d-states of Co in the clean cluster are closer to the Fermi level, allowing stronger interaction and easier electron donation to the antibonding orbitals of CO, thereby enhancing CO activation. In Figure S15c, the calculated adsorption energies indicate that CO adsorption is weaker on the C-deposited Co cluster than on the pristine Co cluster. We also examined CO adsorption at next-nearest neighboring sites of the deposited carbon. While adsorption is stronger at these sites than at the nearest sites, it is still weaker than on the clean Co cluster. These results confirm that the charge transfer caused by carbon deposition inhibits CO adsorption. The surface carbon species content was maintained at a moderate, low level (Figure S16), with no evidence for the formation of carbon nanotubes or carbon fibre-like species (Figure S17)43. However, a layered graphitic carbon species was observed on the surface of 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Fig. 3c1, c2, and S18, S19) after the reaction, confirming the occurrence of surface carbon deposition. In addition, the activity tests indicated that after ~5 h of reaction, CO selectivity and CO2 conversion over 2Co/MnOx catalyst remained largely stable, suggesting that only initial surface carbon deposition during the early stages of the reaction influenced the reactivity of Co-based catalyst. The activity also remains nearly stable after the abrupt change in selectivity (Figure S3), indicating that this layered graphitic carbon is sufficiently porous to guarantee reactant access to the Co surface while altering surface reactivity. Thus, our results rule out conventional fouling and confirm a carbon-induced surface restructuring of Co nanoclusters. This mechanism fundamentally differs from C-encapsulated Co or Co-carbide species9,37,44,45, which are formed under different conditions, establishing a novel restructuring pathway.

TPH results confirmed surface carbon species (particularly polymeric carbon) could be eliminated via H2 treatment at elevated temperatures, leading to the generation of CH4, with atomic carbon and metal-C bond hydrogenated below 400 oC. In contrast, surface polymeric carbon species require higher temperatures for elimination. The 2Co/MnOx catalyst, after 18 h of reaction followed by 4 h of H2 treatment at 400 oC, exhibited high CO selectivity and unaltered catalytic performance (Fig. 3d and S20), demonstrating that atomic carbon and metal-carbon species were not selectivity-determining factors, whereas increasing the post-reaction H2 treatment temperature of the 2Co/MnOx catalyst to 500 oC rapidly restores fresh-catalyst selectivity (Fig. 3e and S21), proving polymeric carbon deposition drives the catalytic behavior shift. This interpretation aligns with results from multiple reaction-reduction-reaction cycles, confirming that surface polymeric carbon species critically modulate catalytic selectivity. The CO2 hydrogenation atmosphere promotes the formation of carbon-modified Co active sites on the 2Co/MnOx catalyst, thereby shifting selectivity from CH4 to CO. At the same time, H2 treatment at 500 oC effectively eliminates surface carbon species, thereby restoring the catalyst’s high CH4 selectivity (Fig. 3f).

The Co/MnOx interface role in the in-situ restructuring

The above results indicate that surface carbon deposition significantly affects the reaction performance of 2Co/MnOx. In contrast, analogous catalysts, such as 2Co/TiO2 and 5Co/MnOx, exhibited a markedly slower surface carbon deposition rate under identical treatment conditions, indicating a unique mechanism specific to 2Co/MnOx. CO-TPD experiments on the 2Co/MnOx, 2Co/TiO2, and 5Co/MnOx catalysts (Figure S22) revealed not only the desorption of CO (m/z = 28) but also substantial generation of CO2 (m/z = 44). The CO2 may originate from either the disproportionation of CO (2CO → CO2 + C)46–48 or the direct oxidation of CO (CO + O* → CO2)49. However, the catalyst undergoes high-temperature hydrogen reduction followed by inert gas purging, which removes surface-active oxygen species. The amount of CO2 formed is about half of that of CO converted, as expected from the Boudouard reaction, and different from what would occur in the case of CO oxidation.

Additionally, there is an enhanced presence of surface carbon species on the 2Co/MnOx catalyst (as shown by TPH, TPO, TEM, etc.), compared to 5Co/MnOx and 2Co/TiO2 cases. All these indications strongly support the occurrence of CO disproportionation. The CO2 production in CO-TPD experiments enables quantification of surface carbon deposition. Specifically, 2Co/MnOx and 5Co/MnOx catalysts exhibited pronounced CO2 desorption peaks below 400 oC, whereas 2Co/TiO2 primarily desorbed CO with only minor CO2 desorption above 600 oC. Temperature-programmed CO-DRIFTs data confirm CO disproportionation on the 2Co/MnOx through emerging gaseous CO2 bands at 2300–2400 cm−1 during the heating process (Fig. 2c)50.

Figure 4a and S22 comparatively analyze the integrated areas of CO and CO2 desorption peaks from CO-TPD experiments across three catalysts, using the desorbed CO2 quantity to quantify CO disproportionation capability or carbon deposition propensity. Both 2Co/MnOx and 5Co/MnOx catalysts exhibit significantly higher CO2 desorption than 2Co/TiO2, underscoring the role of MnOx in enhancing CO disproportionation; while 5Co/MnOx exhibits greater CO2 desorption than 2Co/MnOx alongside a lower disproportionation onset temperature of ~57 oC (Figure S22d)-attributed to higher cobalt loading and larger nanoparticles sizes (Figure S23), which elevating the number of CO disproportionation sites. The 5Co/MnOx catalyst exhibited a slower selectivity shift than 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Figure S24), indicating a delayed in-situ restructuring process of Co nanoclusters, which is reasonably associated with its initially low CO selectivity of the 5Co/MnOx catalyst during CO2 hydrogenation (Fig. 1a), which limited the initial CO partial pressure and thus also the initial CO disproportionation kinetics. The 5Co/MnOx catalyst has a lower initial CO concentration under reaction conditions than the 2Co/MnOx catalyst, which was attributed to its higher Co loading and larger metal particle size, both of which facilitated CO2 methanation36. For longer times on stream and amount of surface carbon deposition, the 5Co/MnOx catalyst shows high CO selectivity comparable to that of the 2Co/MnOx catalyst. TPH analysis confirmed that deposited surface carbon species underwent hydrogenation by gas-phase H2, establishing a dynamic equilibrium during carbon-modified Co active site formation between carbon-generating CO disproportionation and hydrogenation-mediated carbon removal. The 5Co/MnOx catalyst, with a higher Co loading, provides more H2 adsorption and dissociation sites compared to the 2Co/MnOx catalyst. The much larger desorption peak area observed in the H2-TPD results for the 5Co/MnOx catalyst supports this (Fig. 4b and S25), thereby enhancing the consumption of deposited carbon species and reducing the overall carbon deposition efficiency, consistent with the mechanistic framework. Furthermore, the HR-TEM analysis of the 5Co/MnOx catalyst surface after reaction reveals only a small amount of onion-like carbon species (Figure S26), primarily consisting of some amorphous carbon layers (Figure S27). The carbon amount after the same reaction time (5 h) is significantly lower on the 5Co/MnOx catalyst compared to the 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Figures S18, S19). The H/C balance on this catalyst determines the carbon deposition efficiency, similar to how the Co nanocluster surface favors H2 dissociation, while the peripheral edge sites drive CO dissociation and disproportionation processes51.

In addition, the catalyst underwent notable structural evolution only in the presence of a COx pretreatment atmosphere (see Fig. 1c), reinforcing the notion that in-situ gas-induced selectivity modulation stems from the sequential transformation pathway (CO2) → CO → C, with the CO disproportionation reaction constituting the fundamental mechanism driving the observed structural evolution of Co-based catalysts.

CO chemisorbs at the Co/MnO interfacial sites on the 2Co/MnOx catalyst and readily dissociates into C atoms, which migrate onto the Co nanocluster surface. The O atoms from CO remain bound to MnO. These O atoms can either undergo disproportionation with another CO molecule (triggered by the interfacial sites) or react with H spillover generated from H2 activation on Co nanoclusters. These two mechanisms coexist, with their relative dominance depending on the reaction conditions. Both mechanisms maintain the catalytic activity of the interfacial sites and enable efficient C atom migration to Co nanocluster sites. This leads to the restructuring of Co nanoclusters, explaining the different behavior compared to other supports, such as TiO2, and the effect of Co loading on MnOx. This behavior was evidenced by CO adsorption at 1884 and 1848 cm−1 on the 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Fig. 4c), attributed to the formation of Co-C-O-Mn bridging carbonyls at the Co/MnOx interface, which is consistent with CO stretching frequencies of multi-nuclear complexes52–54. These features are absent in unsupported MnOx or 2Co/TiO2. The strong metal-support interaction (SMSI) leads to a weak CO adsorption vibration peak for the 2Co/TiO2 catalyst55. However, the bare Co surface observed in the EDS elemental mapping suggests that no SMSI effect is present before or after the reaction for the 2Co/MnOx catalyst (Figures S9, S10), consistent with our previous results29,30. The bridging carbonyl can rearrange into a Co-C-O-Mn precursor structure, facilitating CO dissociation due to the synergistic effect of the carbon affinity of Co and the oxygen affinity of Mn. This suggests that the Co/MnOx interfacial sites play a crucial role in driving the in-situ restructuring of Co nanoclusters.

The well-established carbon affinity of Co metal provides a preferred site for CO adsorption9 and a substrate for carbon material growth56. Co itself can undergo CO disproportionation to generate carbon57,58, while the formation of the Co/MnO interface significantly promotes this process due to the enhanced bridged Co-C-O-Mn adsorption configuration59. NO chemisorption DRIFT (NO-DRIFT) (Fig. 4d, e and S28) and O2-H2 titration (Fig. 4f) studies confirmed the oxygen affinity of MnOx species. NO-DRIFTs revealed two distinct infrared vibrational peaks in the 1700-1900 cm−1 region, corresponding to linear (NO+) and bent (NO-) adsorption at the oxygen and metal sites, respectively60. With increasing NO coverage, both 2Co/MnOx and 5Co/MnOx catalysts exhibited varying degrees of blue shifts (Fig. 4d and S28). In contrast, no shift was observed on the 2Co/TiO2 catalyst (Fig. 4e), directly demonstrating the oxygen-binding capability of MnOx. Figure 4f presents O2-H2 titration results for 2Co/MnOx and 2Co/TiO2 catalysts. After reduction at 500 oC, followed by O2 pretreatment at 50 oC and subsequent temperature-programmed H2 exposure, the 2Co/MnOx catalyst displayed significantly more surface O2 adsorption sites than the 2Co/TiO2 catalyst. This confirmed the enhanced oxophilicity of MnO, which facilitated partial oxidation of Co species, thereby weakening NO adsorption and causing the observed blue shifts in NO-DRIFT experiments.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations investigated the CO conversion path during the in-situ reaction process (Fig. 5a–f and S29–S35), revealing CO dissociation energy barriers on the Co nanoclusters (Co13), the MnO support with an oxygen vacancy (MnO), and the interface of Co13/MnO (Fig. 5a). The results provided compelling evidence that the Co/MnO interface facilitates the formation of a Co-C-O-Mn structure, exhibiting the lowest activation energy barrier for C-O bond dissociation (2.05 eV), indicating that the interfacial sites critically enable efficient CO activation. At the Co13/MnO interface, the dissociation barrier for a second CO molecule is remarkably low (0.92 eV), much lower than the coupling barrier of 1.70 eV (Fig. 5b). In contrast, the C-C coupling barrier on the isolated Co13 surface is 0.93 eV (Fig. 5c). This indicates that CO dissociates rapidly at the interface into C* (surface-chemisorbed carbon atoms). With migration barriers of only 0.82 eV (interface-to-Co) and 0.21 eV (on Co surface), the diffusion of C* atoms is kinetically facile compared to the more demanding steps of CO dissociation and coupling (Figures S33, S34). This efficient diffusion provides a mechanistic basis for how interface-generated carbon atoms can populate the Co surface and, ultimately, form polymeric carbon species and trigger the restructuring of Co nanoclusters. Meanwhile, the O* from CO dissociation remains on the MnO surface. It is efficiently removed either by reacting with CO to form CO2 through a near-barrierless process (0.06 eV, Fig. 5d) or via spillover H from Co nanoclusters. The above DFT calculations are consistent with our above experimental observations and describe the in-situ transformation of CO molecules on the Co/MnO catalyst. Furthermore, the C-modified Co/MnO interface (C@Co13/MnO) was studied for CO dissociation and CO hydrogenation. Compared to unmodified interfaces (Co13/MnO), C@Co13/MnO exhibits higher energy barriers for CO dissociation (2.36 eV vs. 2.00 eV, Fig. 5e) and CO hydrogenation (1.46 eV vs. 0.87 eV, Fig. 5f), respectively. This shows carbon modification inhibits both CO dissociation and hydrogenation, aligning with the observed shift from CH4 to CO as the main catalyst product during CO2 hydrogenation.

The pronounced oxophilicity of MnOx served as the foundation for the Co-C-O-Mn) generation, facilitating carbon deposition during CO2 hydrogenation (Fig. 5g). Meanwhile, CO and H2 simultaneously remove excess surface oxygen species and maintain a dynamic equilibrium between carbon deposition and methanation. As the reaction progresses, carbon-modified Co active centres gradually emerge. Here, deposited carbon species reduce the adsorption of small molecules, such as CO and H2, on the metallic Co surface, thereby suppressing methanation in CO2 hydrogenation and shifting selectivity toward CO2 hydrogenation products. Conventional supports such as TiO2 exhibit weaker CO dissociation ability, enabling Co-dominated methanation at reaction temperatures.

In contrast, the properties of these reconstructed Co nanoclusters differ from those reported in the literature (i.e., Co-carbide or C-encapsulated Co nanoparticles12,44,61–64) as they uniquely suppress methanation activity while preserving CO2 conversion activity and stability. Previous studies have proposed that enhancing CO adsorption improves methane selectivity65,66. Despite inconsistent findings in the literature39,49, this study reveals an alternative mechanism for selectivity control in CO2 conversion. The Co/MnOx interface simultaneously enhances CO adsorption and promotes in-situ generation of C atoms. This dual action triggers the dynamic restructuring of Co nanoclusters, suppressing methanation activity while maintaining key catalytic functions.

Discussion

Controlling selectivity in CO2 conversion over small Co nanoclusters is crucial for many catalytic applications, and reaction-driven structural changes offer an alternative means to induce tailored in-situ restructuring for performance regulation. We reported here the structural evolution and reactivity relationships of supported Co-based catalysts during CO2 hydrogenation. The 2Co/MnOx catalyst exhibited a pronounced selectivity shift from CH4 to CO in CO2 hydrogenation at the same reaction time compared to other catalysts (5Co/MnOx, 2Co/TiO2, and 2Co/SiO2). The initial 2Co/MnOx catalyst undergoes in-situ transformation ( ~ 5 h) that increases CO selectivity from 45.7% to ~94%. Modulating the pretreatment gas composition reveals that the presence of CO or CO2 facilitated this selectivity shift. In contrast, pretreatment temperature exerted a negligible influence on the final structure.

Multivariate experiments definitively established that carbon deposition on the Co surface, which weakens CO adsorption, directly governed the structural factor for the selectivity shift. A dynamic equilibrium between CO disproportionation and H2-assisted carbon removal controlled the rate of structural evolution and in-situ restructuring. Efficient CO disproportionation critically relied on Co-Mn interface sites, where the oxyphilicity of MnOx and the carbophilicity of Co species facilitated breaking the C-O bond in CO molecules, thereby bridging the Co-Mn interface. Co-loading governs surface H concentration, thereby modulating carbon removal capability. These findings revealed the specific roles of metallic Co and the MnOx support in surface carbon modification under COx-containing gases, revealing mechanisms for gas-environment-driven structural evolution. A systematic study of the relationship between atmospheric conditions (such as gas composition, energy conditions, and material properties) and structural evolution provides a fundamental basis for achieving controllable regulation of catalyst surface nanostructures through atmospheric conditions. This research approach can be effectively extended to the top-down regulation of valence states, interfaces, crystal phases, compositions, sizes, and other properties.

The proposed mechanism of in-situ surface dynamic restructuring of Co nanoclusters induced by C atoms results in different reactivity properties compared to CoxC or C-encapsulated Co nanoparticles. The Co/MnOx interface is crucial for achieving this objective. These catalysts, along with insights into their in-situ restructuring mechanisms and distinct CO2 reactivity characteristics relative to other Co-based catalysts, open new possibilities for designing novel CO2 conversion catalysts and anti-CO-poisoning catalysts.

Methods

Synthesis of the catalysts

Preparation of MnOx support

MnOx was synthesised by the carbonate precipitation method30. Specifically, a 1 M solution of manganese sulfate was slowly added dropwise to a 1 M solution of ammonium carbonate. After continuous stirring for 2 h, the mixture was filtered and washed. The obtained solid was then dried overnight at 60 oC and subsequently calcined at 450 oC in a muffle furnace for 3 h to obtain the MnOx support.

Preparation of Co/support catalysts

The Co/support catalysts were synthesised by the impregnation method, with commercial SiO2, TiO2 (P25), and synthesised MnOx serving as the supports, respectively. Taking the preparation of 2Co/MnOx with 2 wt.% Co loading as an example, 0.980 g of MnOx support was mixed with 0.099 g of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate in 30 mL of deionised water in a beaker. The mixture was continuously stirred in a water bath at room temperature for 1 h, then heated to 50 oC and stirred until no visible moisture remained. The resulting mixture was dried overnight at 60 oC and calcined at 350 oC (5 oC·min−1) for 3 h to obtain 2Co/MnOx. Unless otherwise specified, the theoretical Co loading was 2 wt.%. For loading levels of 1 wt.% and 5 wt.%, the amount of cobalt nitrate hexahydrate was adjusted to 0.049 g and 0.247 g, respectively. The above-mentioned catalysts were labeled as 1Co/MnOx, 2Co/MnOx, 5Co/MnOx, 2Co/SiO2, and 2Co/TiO2, respectively.

Characterisation

The crystalline structure of the samples was analysed by X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) on the X’pert-Pro diffractometer from PANalytical (Holland), using Cu Kα radiation at 40 kV and 40 mA. High-resolution transmission electron microscope (TEM) images were obtained using a JEM-2100 operated at 200 kV. The surface element valence analysis was conducted using semi-in-situ XPS on the Thermofisher Escalab 250 Xi+ with monochromated Al Kα radiation. The used catalyst was pretreated with CO2 + H2 for 5 h at 400 oC. In-situ synchrotron radiation XPS was performed at the BL02B beamline station of Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). The 2Co/MnOx catalyst was first reduced and reacted for 5 h in the reactor, then transferred to the XPS equipment and treated with a CO2 + H2 reaction gas at 400 °C for 1 h. The X-ray energy was 480 eV for surface analysis of C species. In-situ Raman spectroscopy measurements of 2Co/MnOx during CO2 hydrogenation were conducted on a Renishaw Raman microscope with a 532 nm laser. The adsorption and reaction properties of the catalysts were investigated using DRIFTs on a VERTEX 80 V (Bruker). The catalysts were pre-reduced at 500 oC for 1 h in H2 and then purged in Ar for 30 min. Treatment process of CO2 + H2-DRIFTs: Stabilised the reaction at room temperature, then programmed the temperature to rise and stabilised at 350 oC, followed by switching to H2 treatment, observing the characteristic changes in the infrared spectrum. Both H2 and Ar were highly pure, and the reacting gas was composed of 5% CO2, 15% H2, and 80% Ar. The CO-DRIFTs and NO-DRIFTs experiments underwent the same pretreatment strategy, followed by cooling to room temperature. The corresponding spectral data were collected during CO and 5% NO/Ar adsorption for 30 minutes, respectively. The CO-DRIFTs were made by adsorption at room temperature, followed by a linear temperature increase to 350 oC. The TPH and TPO experiments on the catalysts were conducted by heating the fresh catalysts or those after reaction in 5% H2/Ar or 5% O2/He, and the CH4 or CO2 signals were analysed by mass spectrometry (MS). CO or H2 temperature-programmed desorption (CO-TPD or H2-TPD) was carried out using an AutoChem 2920 from Micromeritics Instrument Corp. with a mass spectrometer (MS) detector. The catalyst was reduced at 500 °C for 1 h, then swept with Ar for 30 minutes and cooled to room temperature. It was then pre-adsorbed with CO or H2, switched to Ar, and the CO, CO2, or H2 signals were recorded at programmed temperatures. For the O2-H2 titration experiments, 30 ml·min−1 of 5% O2/He was pre-adsorbed at 50 °C for 60 min, then switched to 5% H2/Ar for 60 minutes until the baseline stabilised. The MS signals of m/z = 2 and m/z = 18 were used to record the consumption of H2 and the formation of H2O as the reaction temperature increased linearly (at a rate of 10 °C·min1) from 50 °C to 600 °C.

Catalytic evaluation

The catalyst performance was evaluated in a fixed-bed reactor with a 8 mm inner diameter quartz reaction tube. A 100 mg sample of the catalyst with a particle size of 40–60 mesh was pre-reduced at 500 oC for 2 h in H2. The reactant gas flow rate was set at 100 ml·min−1, and the reactant gas composition, unless otherwise specified, was CO2: H2: N2 = 19%: 76%: 5%.
