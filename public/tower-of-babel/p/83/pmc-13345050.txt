Magnetic Resonance Imaging-based Prostate Cancer Diagnosis Using Principal Component Analysis and Machine Learning Algorithms
Authors: N. Samta, C. S. Sureka, Sivananthan Sarasanandarajah, R. K. Jeevanram
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Prostate cancer is a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among men, necessitating accurate and early diagnostic methods. This study evaluates the effectiveness of principal component analysis (PCA) combined with machine learning (ML) for classifying benign and malignant prostate lesions using magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).Materials and Methods:MRI data from 26 biopsy-confirmed prostate cancer patients were obtained from The Cancer Imaging Archive. Preprocessing included resizing and intensity normalization of dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI images. PCA was applied to extract dominant features capturing key tissue variations. A comparative analysis was performed using three feature representation strategies: raw pixel-based features, PCA-transformed features, and radiomics-based features. The extracted features were classified using Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) classifiers.Results:PCA-based features demonstrated separation between benign and malignant tissue regions. Statistical analysis using an independent two-sample t-test (Welch’s correction) revealed a highly significant difference in mean PC1 values between the two groups (P < 2.2 × 10⁻16). Among the classifiers, RF achieved the highest performance, followed by SVM and KNN, as confirmed by receiver operating characteristic analysis (area under the curve [AUC] = 0.909, 0.805, and 0.785, respectively), where higher AUC values indicate the model’s ability to distinguish between benign and malignant lesions. Comparative evaluation indicated that PCA-based models outperformed raw pixel-based approaches, while radiomics-based features yielded additional gains.Conclusion:PCA-based feature extraction may enhance classification performance by reducing dimensionality and noise in MRI data. The proposed PCA–ML framework offers an interpretable, efficient, and clinically relevant approach for prostate cancer diagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Prostate cancer is one of the primary causes of cancer-related mortality among men, making early and accurate detection extremely important for improving prognosis and survival rates.[12] In 2025, the burden of prostate cancer in India is expected to increase further due to population growth, increasing life expectancy, and an increase in the number of aging males in the country. It is predictable to reach 47,068 incident cases by 2025. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) has emerged as a highly valuable tool in prostate cancer evaluation due to its superior soft-tissue contrast, ability to image the prostate gland in multiple planes, and its capability to provide both anatomical and functional information through sequences such as T2-weighted imaging, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast-enhanced (DCE)-MRI.[34] These multiple sequences also provide quantitative information, particularly apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) maps derived from DWI, which are widely used to assess tissue cellularity and malignancy. Despite these advantages, interpretation of prostate MRI remains challenging because it requires integrating information from multiple sequences, recognizing subtle textural or morphological differences, and distinguishing benign abnormalities from clinically significant malignancies. These complexities contribute to variability in radiologist performance and increase the likelihood of missed or ambiguous findings.[5]

To address these limitations, computer-aided diagnostic (CAD) systems have been increasingly integrated into clinical workflows. CAD tools assist radiologists by automatically analyzing imaging data, highlighting suspicious regions, and providing quantitative metrics that support decision-making.[6] Modern CAD systems rely heavily on computational algorithms and machine learning (ML) techniques, which enable automated feature extraction, dimensionality reduction, lesion characterization, and pattern recognition. ML algorithms can analyze large volumes of imaging data, identify subtle differences not easily perceived by the human eye, and learn discriminative features associated with malignant or benign tissue. Techniques such as principal component analysis (PCA), convolutional neural networks, Support Vector Machines (SVMs), and ensemble classifiers contribute to improved diagnostic accuracy, reproducibility, and efficiency.

Overall, the integration of ML and CAD systems represents a significant advancement in prostate cancer imaging, helping reduce observer variability, enhance early detection, and support precision medicine approaches. Computational algorithms and ML techniques are used to analyze imaging data and extract features.[78]

Among ML techniques, PCA has been recognized as an important method in medical imaging.[9] PCA is an unsupervised statistical method that converts a large number of original features into a few principal components (PCs) that capture most of the significant information.[10] This not only reduces the high dimensionality of MRI scans but also makes them cleaner and simpler for precise machine learning-based analyses. The present study uses an unsupervised technique, a PCA-based feature extraction framework in conjunction with supervised methods, Random Forest (RF), SVM, and K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithms for the classification of prostate MRI datasets.[11]

The principal aim of this work is to show that the PCA-generated features in conjunction with ML classifiers such as RF and SVM may improve diagnostic performance in the identification of benign and malignant lesions of the prostate.[12] However, we recognize that diagnosis, when based solely on morphological features or texture analysis, may be limited; thus, it should be treated as a complementary decision-support tool alongside functional imaging, quantitative parameters (such as ADC), and clinical assessment.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

To evaluate the effectiveness of dimensionality reduction and feature engineering in prostate cancer classification, three modeling strategies were comparatively assessed: (i) raw pixel-based ML, where flattened image intensities were directly used as input features; (ii) PCA followed by ML, where high-dimensional pixel data were transformed into a lower-dimensional space retaining maximum variance; and (iii) radiomics-based ML, where handcrafted statistical and texture features were extracted from the images before classification. This comparative framework was designed to systematically investigate whether PCA-based feature reduction or radiomics-driven feature extraction improves classification performance over conventional raw pixel inputs.[13]

Patient cohort

This work employs the open-access DCE prostate MRI dataset provided by The Cancer Imaging Archive (TCIA),[13] which was created in collaboration with the National Cancer Institute, Bethesda, Maryland, USA (DOI: 10.7937/K9/TCIA.2016.6046GUDv). The dataset included 6812 DCE-MRI images taken from 26 biopsy-confirmed prostate cancer patients and was spread over 182 series, with each subject having around 262 images.[14] All patients were clinically diagnosed with prostate cancer using the biopsy method. Every patient had a prostate-specific antigen test, digital rectal examination, and, after which, a transrectal ultrasound (TRUS)-guided biopsy was performed for the final confirmation.[15] The detailed dataset was therefore an excellent foundation for image processing, feature extraction, and the development of PCA- and ML-based classification models to separate benign from malignant tissue in the prostate visually. The representative histopathological biopsy slides from the validation study, used for qualitative comparison of MRI-based PCA and machine learning-assessed classification results, served as the gold standard against which we assessed the accuracy of our methodology. For DCE-MRI, all available dynamic phases from each series were included in the analysis, rather than a single fixed time point, to incorporate temporal enhancement information and reduce bias from interindividual differences in contrast uptake and blood flow. Although TRUS-guided biopsy is the accepted clinical reference standard, we acknowledge that it may not always sample the most aggressive or heterogeneous regions of a tumor. All included subjects were biopsy-confirmed prostate cancer patients, and no separate healthy control cohort was available. Consequently, benign samples in this study correspond to nontumorous prostate tissue regions within cancer patients, while malignant samples represent tumor-involved regions confirmed by biopsy.

Data acquisition and transformation

Image processing and analysis were conducted in R (version 4.2.1); R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, using specialized libraries for efficient image handling and segmentation.[1617] While the native spatial resolution and sequence parameters of the original MRI images were preserved, the images were resampled to a uniform matrix size as part of the preprocessing in this work to ensure consistent computations. MRI scans were adjusted to a standard size of 128 by 128 pixels so that the computations would be less demanding, but the important anatomical features would still be preserved.[17] This resizing was applied uniformly across all images to maintain consistency and was performed using interpolation methods that preserve relative intensity patterns. The pixel intensity values were scaled to the range 0–1 to improve image contrast for subsequent analysis.[17] The features considered in the images primarily concerned color and texture, which in turn captured the structural features necessary for correct classification.[18] This study utilizes DCE-MRI sequences exclusively to evaluate the effectiveness of PCA-based feature extraction within a single imaging modality. Other multiparametric MRI (mpMRI) components, such as T2-weighted imaging and DWI (ADC maps), were not included in the present analysis.

Principal component analysis

PCA enables the transformation of high-dimensional MRI signal-intensity space into orthogonal components that represent dominant variance structures.[9] Before conducting PCA, each feature was standardized using z-score normalization, so each feature had a mean of zero and a standard deviation of 1. Standardization was an essential step in putting all features on the same scale, ensuring that features with a larger numerical range would not dominate the PC space. The subsequent analysis was based on the first two PCs, which accounted for the largest share of the total variance in the feature set. The number of retained PCs was determined based on the cumulative explained variance and inspection of the eigenvalue spectrum (scree plot), with PC1 and PC2 together capturing the dominant proportion of the dataset’s total variance.

PC1 and PC2 refer to the first and second PCs of the covariance matrix of standardized MRI features, respectively. PC1 is the component that explains the largest amount of total variance, while PC2 explains the second largest amount of variance in an orthogonal direction. Higher-order components were evaluated but were found to contribute substantially less additional variance and did not materially improve class separability; therefore, they were not included in subsequent analyses to maintain model simplicity and interpretability. For each MRI scan, a PC1 score is computed as a weighted linear combination of its original standardized features, with weights given by the eigenvector loadings. These scores have no units because PCA is a transformation of standardized, dimensionless inputs.

Label assignment

The label assignment was based on the results of feature extraction with PCA and a supervised ML algorithm. MRI slices were categorized as benign or malignant using PC1 values and predictions from the trained ML model. The ground truth was the biopsy-verified diagnosis derived from histopathological evaluation. Accordingly, classification was performed at the image/slice level, distinguishing benign and malignant tissue regions within the same patient rather than between independent patient groups.

Principal component analysis scatter and density plot

The PCA scatter plot illustrates various MRIs along with their slices, transformed into PC1 and PC2 scores. These scores were obtained by PCA on the standardized image matrix. It was plotted as a two-dimensional plane using ggplot2, with each point representing an image and color indicating the biopsy-confirmed benign or malignant label.

The density plot is created by applying kernel density estimation (KDE) to the PC1 scores from the PCA-transformed MRI dataset. In the present analysis, KDE has been used separately for the benign and malignant classes to smooth the distribution of PC1 values into a continuous, unitless probability curve that visualizes how the two groups are distributed along PC1.

Model training and classification

Three ML classifiers, RF, SVM, and KNN, were trained on PCA-transformed features.[1920] To separate data, RF used a forest of decision trees, SVM found the best boundary in a high-dimensional space, and KNN identified the closest sample for classification (k = 5). All the models were tested for their performance in distinguishing healthy from cancerous tissues.

The PCA-transformed dataset was divided into 70% for training and 30% for testing in training models. Importantly, this split was performed at the patient level rather than the slice level, so that all MRI slices from a given patient were assigned exclusively to either the training or testing set, thereby preventing data leakage and ensuring unbiased performance estimation. Each classifier used the default hyperparameters, except KNN, which was optimized for k = 5 through repeated cross-validation.[2021] The trained classifiers then predicted labels for the test set, and the predictions were compared with biopsy-confirmed labels using accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity.[22] The KNN classifier was included as a baseline distance-based method for comparative evaluation and descriptive performance analysis and was not excluded from model training; however, inferential statistical testing was focused on models with the most divergent performance characteristics. Model performance was further evaluated using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis, which assesses the ability of classifiers to distinguish between benign and malignant classes across different decision thresholds. The ROC curve plots sensitivity (true positive rate) against false-positive rate, and the area under the curve (AUC) provides a threshold-independent measure of classification performance. Higher AUC values indicate better discriminative capability of the model.

Clustering and visualization DBSCAN

This study visualized clustering results from the density-based spatial clustering of applications with noise (DBSCAN) DBSCAN algorithm using the ggplot2 package by plotting the first two PCs on the x- and y-axes to display the reduced-dimension data distribution with color-coded clusters and transparency for overlapping points, and all DBSCAN-PCA visualizations were generated from the postprocessed TCIA dataset used in this work.[23] Each point on the scatterplot represents an MRI slice projected into PCA space. The resulting visual clusters illustrate how data from PCA-transformed MRI slices are distributed in a lower-dimensional space.[2425]

Classification models derived from RF and KNN were evaluated using confusion matrices and converted to a data frame for visualization as a heatmap to better illustrate differences in classification performance.[232425]

Statistical validation

To statistically evaluate differences in the performance of PCA-based ML classifiers, we utilized a paired t-test to compare classification accuracies between models evaluated on the same cross-validation folds. To compare PC1 values between benign and malignant groups, an independent two-sample t-test (with Welch’s correction) was used.[26] A paired t-test was conducted to assess whether the difference in average classification accuracies between the two models with the largest performance gap, i.e., RF and SVM, was statistically significant for the same cross-validation folds. The input to the paired t-test was the classification accuracies generated from repeated cross-validation folds for each respective model (RF, SVM, and KNN). Each fold produced a pair of accuracy values from the same data partition, allowing valid use of a paired statistical comparison. Ultimately, this accounts for differences in model performance that cannot be attributed to random sampling differences but rather to the model’s real-world performance.

Although all the three models (RF, SVM, and KNN) were analyzed, we limited the t-test to a paired comparison between RF and SVM, as these models exhibited the most distinct classification characteristics.[1920] KNN was therefore retained in the study for completeness and comparative benchmarking, but was not included in paired inferential testing because its performance was intermediate and did not represent the most contrasting case. RF achieved relatively high sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy, whereas SVM consistently produced the lowest values across metrics. Comparing these two extreme values enables statistical analysis: we can say that the observed difference in predictive performance between the two models is statistically significant and not due to chance. Importantly, these classifier comparisons are intended to support and validate the discriminative capability of PCA-derived features, rather than shift the primary methodological focus away from PCA as the central feature extraction and interpretability framework of this study.

RESULTS

Figure 1 presents representative prostate MRI slices from the benign (top row) and malignant (bottom row) groups, illustrating the visual and statistical distinctions captured by PCA. The benign images [Figure 1a and b] exhibit largely intact anatomical structures, uniform intensity distribution, and well-defined edges, reflecting tissue homogeneity and corresponding lower (or negative) PC1 scores. In contrast, the malignant images [Figure 1c and d] display disrupted tissue architecture, heterogeneous intensity patterns, and increased visual texture and contrast, consistent with higher PC1 values that represent greater variance in the PCA space. Collectively, these differences demonstrate that PCA can effectively separate benign and malignant tissues, providing clear visual and computational evidence of its potential to label and classify prostate MRI images based on underlying pathological characteristics.

A density plot was generated using KDE to visualize the sample distribution. Figure 3 shows that the density plot tracks out benign lesions that correspond to higher PC1 values, while malignant lesions are at lower ranges. Density values are unitless probability estimates derived from KDE. The estimate was made possible through KDE, which determines the density of a cluster of points by smoothing the PCA scores. The slight overlap between benign and malignant densities reflects the natural overlap of imaging features in transitional anatomical regions. The overlap is handled through ML classification using multidimensional PCA features, not PC1 alone. Accordingly, PC1 should be interpreted as a relative axis of variance, and references to higher or lower PC1 values describe dataset-specific distributions rather than a universal malignancy rule.

The comparative analysis demonstrated distinct differences in model performance across the three feature representation strategies. The raw pixel-based models exhibited comparatively lower discriminative ability, indicating the limitations of high-dimensional, unstructured input features. In contrast, PCA-based models improved classification performance by reducing noise and redundancy in the data. Notably, radiomics-based models achieved the highest performance, suggesting that domain-specific feature extraction provides more informative representations for distinguishing benign and malignant prostate lesions.

STATISTICAL VALIDATION RESULTS

The paired t-test comparing the accuracies of RF and SVM yielded t (9) =21.857, P < 0.001, indicating a meaningful improvement in classification accuracy with RF over SVM. The mean difference in classification accuracy between the two models was 0.051, indicating an average improvement of about 5.1% with RF compared to SVM. The 95% confidence interval (CI) for this difference (0.0457, 0.0563) provides a range in which we can be 95% confident that the true difference in mean classification accuracy lies, supporting the conclusion of improved performance, as the CI does not include zero. As shown in Figure 6, the distribution of PC1 values clearly separates benign and malignant groups, with benign samples clustering at higher PC1 values and malignant samples at lower values.

An independent two-sample t-test (Welch’s correction) was performed to compare mean PC1 values between biopsy-confirmed benign and malignant samples obtained from PCA analysis, as these groups represent statistically independent observations rather than paired measurements [Figure 7]. The results demonstrated a highly statistically significant difference between the two groups (t = 19.75, df = 107.94, P < 2.2 × 10 ⁻ 16). The mean PC1 value for benign samples (83.63) was substantially higher than that for malignant samples (−78.14), indicating a strong separation along the PC axis. The 95% CI for the mean difference (145.54, 178.01) further supports the robustness of this distinction.

Although the Shapiro–Wilk test indicated deviations from normality in both the groups (P < 0.05), the large sample size justifies using the t-test. In addition, a nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was performed to validate the findings without assuming normality, which also demonstrated a highly significant difference (P < 2.2 × 10⁻16).

Overall, these findings support the conclusion that (1) PCA-derived features have high discriminative ability, and (2) the RF classifier, which utilizes PCA-derived features, has the highest statistically sound diagnostic performance compared to competing models. These results were presented to demonstrate the effectiveness of PCA-derived feature space, with classifier performance serving as supportive evidence rather than the primary contribution of the study. The paired t-test comparing RF and SVM accuracies (t[9] =21.857, P < 0.001) compared the classification accuracies of RF and SVM models across cross-validation folds, whereas the Welch’s independent t-test (t = 19.75, df = 107.94) compares the PCA-derived PC1 values between benign and malignant tissue groups. Thus, the former evaluates model performance, while the latter evaluates tissue-group separation in PCA space.

DISCUSSION

This study presents a preliminary, interpretable PCA-based feature extraction framework coupled with classical ML classifiers to improve diagnostic interpretation of prostate MRI, especially DCE sequences. The primary contribution of this work is the use of PCA as an interpretable, data-driven feature extraction framework. At the same time, the role of machine learning classifiers is intentionally secondary, serving to demonstrate the clinical utility of PCA-derived features. Compared to conventional methods, this approach may improve classification performance in prostate cancer diagnosis, as the PCA-based feature space is free of redundancies, it is clearer for the distinction of noncancerous and cancerous tissues, and it gives the possibility for classical ML models, especially RF, to obtain a better sensitivity and specificity result when they are trained on raw MRI data only. PCA transformed high-dimensional MRI data to a reduced, decorrelated feature space to describe dominant sources of variation associated with tissue heterogeneity. A key limitation of this study is its reliance on DCE-MRI. In contrast, contemporary prostate cancer diagnosis relies on mpMRI, including T2-weighted imaging and DWI with ADC maps. ADC values, in particular, provide important information related to tissue cellularity and are widely used for lesion characterization. The absence of these complementary imaging modalities may limit the clinical generalizability of the proposed framework.

Density-based clustering organizes image pixels into relevant areas based on similar features, yielding a better understanding of object or region classes than thresholding with fixed boundaries. Scatter plot visualizations of the clusters indicated well-defined spatial clusters associated with an object or a region of tissue morphology, illustrating the structural patterns. A combination of color and textural features provides a layered understanding of pixel relationships and patterns within latent images.

The study used this classification scheme on the premise that PC1 explained most of the variance in imaging features of intensity and texture, which can primarily distinguish malignant from benign tissue regions. Figures included in this article were generated directly from the TCIA DCE-MRI dataset used for this analysis and are original to this work, not reproduced from any other source.

To handle, standardize, and display prostate MRI images, a detailed preprocessing pipeline was put together. The images were tagged as either benign or malignant based on biopsy-confirmed histopathology reports, which served as the gold standard reference for ground-truth classification. This labeling also enabled visual comparison of representative benign and malignant regions to assess imaging differences in prostate MRI qualitatively.

Although previous studies used PCA in prostate cancer research, most relied on radiomic feature sets or deep learning architectures that are complex, less transparent, and prone to overfitting on small datasets. In this study, PCA-derived features provided an interpretable representation of imaging variability, supporting improved classification performance. Therefore, PCA should be considered an interpretable and complementary feature reduction approach that can support, rather than replace, radiomics-based methods.

Different recent research works have acknowledged the role of Radiomics and artificial intelligence in the identification of prostate cancer from MRI. At the same time, in their review of radiomics applications, Ferro et al. stressed the need for simpler, more interpretable models for clinical use. Mylona et al. found that feature selection and dimensionality reduction significantly improve classifier performance, thereby providing a rationale for the PCA-based method used in this study. Chatterjee et al. provided evidence that quantitative MRI features can be used to differentiate malignant from benign lesions with high accuracy, which is consistent with the clinical importance of feature-based ML models.[101112] Radiomics achieved higher performance; however, PCA offers advantages in interpretability, reduced computational complexity, and direct applicability to raw imaging data. This highlights that PCA serves as an interpretable dimensionality reduction step, while radiomics provides a richer feature representation.

The PC1 visualization in Figure 2 showed a separation between benign (homogeneous) and malignant (irregular and disrupted) prostate lesions. We emphasize that while PC1 provides strong separation, its numerical direction (higher vs. lower values) depends on the PCA loading orientation and dataset composition; therefore, clinical classification is based on ML predictions trained on biopsy labels, not on PC1 polarity alone. Benign regions had correspondingly higher PC1 scores, while malignant regions exhibited lower PC1 scores. This finding is entirely consistent with PC1 demonstrating the primary direction of variance differentiating normal glandular tissue from cancerous tissue, and the statistical comparison of tissue groups corroborated such definitive separation, confirmed as malignant and benign at biopsy (t (104.48) =19.972; P < 2.2 × 10⁻16; 95% CI [145.7970, 177.9392]). The strong separation between malignant and nonmalignant lesions indicates that the PCA transformation captured clinically relevant variance associated with malignancy. The main division is clearly shown in Figure 2, where the two visually distinct clusters can be traced along the PC1 axis: cancerous images are mainly located at lower PC1 values, whereas noncancerous images tend to cluster at higher PC1 values. Thus, PCA not only served as a dimensionality reduction technique but also created an interpretable map of the biological, structural, and functional landscape of prostate tissue. The statistical comparison demonstrated that the PCA-derived PC1 values for biopsy-confirmed benign and malignant tissues differed significantly. The Welch’s independent t-test produced a very high t-value (t(104.48) = 19.972) and an extremely small P value (P < 2.2 × 10 − 16), indicating that the observed separation between the two tissue groups was highly statistically significant and unlikely to have occurred by chance. This result supports the ability of PCA-derived features to distinguish malignant from benign prostate tissue effectively.

Raw pixel-based models demonstrated comparatively lower discriminative power, reflecting the limitations of high-dimensional, unstructured feature representations. In contrast, PCA-based models showed improved performance by reducing noise and redundancy in the data. Furthermore, radiomics-based models achieved the highest performance, consistent with previous studies demonstrating that domain-specific quantitative imaging features provide more informative representations for distinguishing benign and malignant prostate lesions.[13]

In the PCA scatter plot, the separation between benign and malignant images occurs primarily along the direction of spread within the high-PC1 cluster, even though both classes appear in the same general region. Within this cluster, benign samples consistently occupy the rightmost, higher-PC1 positions and are tightly grouped, while malignant samples appear more dispersed and shifted toward lower PC1 or more negative PC2 values. Thus, the division is not a single vertical boundary but a pattern of separation in the distribution and spread of points within the PC1–PC2 space, which is confirmed quantitatively by the significant difference in PC1 scores between the two groups.

Figure 5 was created using the ggplot2 package, which illustrates how the predicted classes interact with the actual classes, allowing the reader to visualize performance on a false- and true-positive scale. Thus, heat maps are yet another useful way to visualize and present the power of models in classifying prostate cancer and to identify the RF algorithm as the most reliable approach for accurate classification. The ROC curve analysis further supports these findings by providing a comprehensive evaluation of classifier performance across multiple thresholds. Unlike accuracy alone, ROC analysis considers both sensitivity and specificity simultaneously, offering a more robust assessment of model performance. The higher AUC observed for the RF model indicates its consistent ability to distinguish benign from malignant tissues, which is particularly important in clinical diagnostic applications, where both false positives and false negatives have significant implications.

The RF classifier had the darkest diagonal and the fewest false predictions, which corresponds to the lowest overall error rate. The KNN classifier had similar accuracy to RF; however, it had slightly more false positives and false negatives. The SVM classifier had the largest number of misclassifications, reducing its reliability with this dataset. These observations highlight that ensemble-based methods, such as RF, performed better than margin-based (SVM) or distance-based classifiers (KNN) at separating nonlinear cases and handling noise in PCA-compressed imaging data. In the heatmaps, “error values” provide a quantitative summary of false positives (benign falsely predicted as malignant) and false negatives (malignant falsely predicted as benign) from each confusion matrix, calculated as (False Positive [FP] + False Negative [FN])/total samples. This visualization mechanism provides an easy comparison across models and complements the numerical results for model accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity produced through quantitative methods.

Aside from the visual evidence of the RF classifier, statistical corroboration supports its efficacy. A paired t-test on accuracies from repeated folds of cross-validation demonstrates a statistically significant difference in mean classification accuracy between RF and SVM (t(9) = 21.857, P < 0.001). The 95% CI for the mean improvement of 0.051 (≈5.1%) indicated that the difference was unlikely to be due to chance. The CI provides a quantitative statement about the long-run or population mean difference and asserts that we are 95% confident it is true. The RF and SVM comparisons were selected because these classifiers differed most markedly, thereby demonstrating the model difference most clearly. That is, KNN is considered to perform relatively well but was statistically intermediate and, correspondingly, is used here only for descriptive comparisons of statistical terms and not for inferential purposes.

Using PCA and ML classification together, we gain two main benefits. First, redundant and correlated MRI features are collapsed or grouped into fewer, interpretable components that improve computational efficiency. Second, the PCA approach offers greater model transparency by linking PCs to biological and structural tissue characteristics. The classification approach that used PCA and RF provided the most reliable and reproducible classification of malignant versus benign lesions and clearly has the potential to be applied to CAD development for prostate cancer. Using biopsy-proven pathology as the gold standard enhanced the study’s clinical significance by providing a validated ground truth for model performance evaluation during both training and testing.

Those results are consistent with PCA’s effectiveness in reducing data redundancy and improving the extraction of diagnostic features, as emphasized in previous research.[27] provided evidence that PCA on DCE-MRI can be used to distinguish cancerous from benign areas in the prostate using maps of component coefficients, which is consistent with our understanding of PC1 as the main variance descriptor.[2829] In contrast to deep learning black-box models, this approach is more acceptable in the clinical setting, as it can still be validated by confirming biopsy pathology through direct linkage to imaging features. As a result, it can deliver excellent diagnostic performance even with small data sizes, underscoring its usefulness and potential for implementation in clinical settings and more practical research.

A limitation of this study is that the dataset consisted exclusively of biopsy-confirmed prostate cancer patients and did not include a separate healthy control cohort. Consequently, the proposed framework was designed to differentiate benign and malignant tissue regions within affected individuals at the slice- or tissue-level rather than to perform patient-level diagnosis or population-level screening. Nevertheless, using biopsy-confirmed histopathology as the reference standard provides a clinically reliable basis for evaluating the discriminative capability of the PCA-derived imaging features. In addition, this study did not include direct comparison with Prostate Imaging Reporting and Data System (PI-RADS) scoring or expert radiologist assessment, as the primary objective was to investigate the interpretability and classification potential of PCA-derived MRI features within a machine learning framework. Future studies incorporating healthy controls, multicenter datasets, PI-RADS evaluation, and radiologist-based interpretation would further strengthen the clinical applicability, robustness, and generalizability of the proposed approach.

CONCLUSION

The results of the research indicated that PCA on MRI data is a reliable and promising approach for the identification of prostate cancer. PCA extracted image features that were most informative for distinguishing between benign and malignant lesions. Histopathology from biopsy was considered the gold standard against which these image features were measured. Thus, the close concordance between PCA-derived imaging features and pathology substantiates the reliability of the proposed diagnostic framework. While PCA generates a more concise version of data, higher sensitivity and specificity may be obtained using more sophisticated classifiers such as RF, SVM, and KNN. In general, a method that uses PCA-based feature extraction combined with ML algorithms is a statistically reliable, interpretable, and clinically feasible model that may support improved diagnostic performance and better clinical outcomes in prostate cancer.

Compared to traditional methods, this approach demonstrates methodological advantages, as PCA effectively reduces the dimensionality of noisy MRI data while retaining the variance important for clinical use. As a result, classical ML classifiers can differentiate cancerous from noncancerous tissues with higher accuracy, reduced overfitting, and improved interpretability, while reducing feature redundancy within the proposed PCA-based framework, without implying direct superiority over raw image-based models. The current investigation is an innovative approach that uses PCA as the primary feature extraction technique directly on prostate MRI data. After that, the PCA-derived features are employed for ML classification, a combination that has seldom been reported in the literature. The use of PCA in this manner improved diagnosis by eliminating redundancies, enhancing feature clarity, and enabling classifiers, especially RF, to differentiate benign from malignant lesions more effectively. Thus, PCA represents the central methodological advancement of this study, with downstream classifiers serving as supportive tools to validate its discriminative and clinical value. The study is limited by a small data size and slice-based analysis, and we did not directly use time–intensity curve (TIC) and ADC values. In future, we need larger datasets and direct use of functional parameters to strengthen the method’s clinical performance. Future work will focus on integrating mpMRI data, including T2-weighted and ADC-derived features, with PCA-based dimensionality reduction to enhance diagnostic accuracy and better align with clinical imaging protocols.
