Coronary artery calcium should be the complement to and not the end result of clinical risk scores
Authors: Eric J Brandt, Romit Bhattacharya
Published: 2026

Cardiovascular risk prediction is the mainstay of decision making for preventive therapies, including blood pressure and lipid management. While the standard has been that these decisions have been guided by risk calculators powered by static mathematical models, the tension between population-level accuracy and individual-level precision in prediction fuels a need for improved risk prediction for the patient in the clinic room. A major shift has come with CT imaging of subclinical atherosclerosis in the form of vascular calcification and atherosclerotic plaque volume. Visualizing plaque consistently leads to surprises, such as the ‘low-risk’ individual with premature disease, and the ‘high-risk’ individual with CAC = 0, demonstrating potential to assist with weaknesses of the risk calculator-based approach. However, while CAC is in the guidelines as a ‘risk enhancing factor’ to support decision making, it had not been adopted as a universal screening tool - in part due to concerns about cost, radiation exposure, and the need for additional data that inform whether universal CAC scoring and more broad use of coronary CT angiogram leads to improved health outcomes. Since risk estimation using traditional biomarkers and clinical variables generally carries no additional costs for care and continues to be refined for broader populations (e.g., the recent PREVENT equations, QRISK-3, etc.) this will likely remain the cornerstone for risk prediction. An ideal approach would merge these strategies: using risk scores to identify candidates most likely to benefit from coronary imaging. However, a fundamental mismatch exists - current risk scores were developed to predict clinical events, not subclinical disease burden. Soh et al. directly address this critical question in their analysis published in this edition of AJPC [1].

A major challenge to using risk scores for informing imaging utilization is that widely used risk scores are calibrated for detecting clinical events and not for detecting subclinical disease. Soh et al. sought to explore this intersection by evaluating whether cardiovascular risk scores correlate with coronary plaque and its progression in a cohort uniquely enriched for family history of premature CAD, in a post-hoc analysis of the CAUGHT-CAD study [1]. Among 365 asymptomatic individuals with intermediate risk and CAC scores of 1–400, they observed that current risk scores (i.e., the Pooled Cohort Equations, PREVENT, PREDICT, AusCVD, and QRISK3) had weak and essentially negligible associations with baseline CAC, total plaque volume, and plaque progression over three years. Only QRISK3—the sole score incorporating family history—showed statistically significant association with plaque progression, though the correlation remained quite modest. These findings highlight an important limitation of current risk estimation tools when applied to the prediction of subclinical coronary disease, particularly in populations with inherited susceptibility and lifetime exposure may outweigh short-term risk as is captured by traditional models.

At first glance, these observations appear to contrast with prior data from large serial coronary CT angiography cohorts, most notably the PARADIGM study, which demonstrated a graded association between traditional cardiovascular risk burden and both baseline plaque volume and plaque progression [2,3]. In PARADIGM, higher atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease risk scores were associated with greater total plaque burden, accelerated progression of noncalcified and high-risk plaque subtypes, and increased development of adverse plaque characteristics over time. However, PARADIGM was a natural history study, looking at progression of disease by enrolling a broad, heterogeneous population spanning low-, intermediate-, and high-risk individuals, without restriction to CAC positivity or family history. In contrast, the Soh et al. analysis was conducted within a deliberately selected cohort restricted to intermediate-risk, asymptomatic individuals with CAC scores between 1 and 400 and enriched for family history of premature coronary disease. This conditioning on both CAC positivity and inherited risk likely compresses the distribution of traditional risk factors and attenuates the discriminative capacity of conventional risk scores. In such settings, much of the variance in subclinical disease burden and progression may be driven by factors not fully captured by short-term, event-focused risk models, including cumulative lifetime exposure, genetic susceptibility, and early plaque biology. Thus, rather than being contradictory, the findings from Soh et al. and PARADIGM are best understood as complementary, with each illuminating how the performance of risk scores depends critically on population context and the disease phenotype being interrogated.

These findings are also important in the context that the majority of first CHD events occur among those predicted to be at low or borderline risk for CVD by traditional risk calculation (PCE and PREVENT) [4]. In another cohort of young South Korean adults with MI ≤ 50 years, risk was low on average (median 10-year risk 4.8 %), yet most (83 %) had detectable risk factors [5]. Thus, Soh et al.’s data can be integrated into the broader context that clinical risk scores may need complementary tools to inform risk, particularly among young individuals who are generally calculated at low risk due to the strong impact of age on risk calculation. Also, the potential for understanding how we can predict the development of subclinical disease, which will require broad research to inform how this type of additional layer of prediction could be valuably integrated into clinical decision making. For example, future work could focus on developing tools to better inform the ideal candidates for identifying those with subclinical disease.

Lastly, we should be mindful of the prior literature regarding the broad use of clinical imaging in studies such as DANCAVAS and DANCAVAS II trials [6,7]. The DANCAVAS trials showed a potential benefit for non-mortality outcomes, including myocardial infarction, aortic dissection, and aortic rupture) of broad screening for subclinical CV disease, although at the potential risk for increased bleeding. Trials like DANCAVAS and health-economic analyses suggest benefit may depend on who is scanned, not simply whether scanning occurs. Within the context of current evidence, the low risk nature of lipid lowering therapies, and Soh et al.’s observation that risk scores and CAC didn’t correlate, and may be considered as separate, complementary tools, we propose that clinicians should selectively integrate imaging to detect subclinical atherosclerosis that would inform treatment decisions as outlined in current guidelines, which recommend imaging for subclinical atherosclerosis primarily in the context of patient hesitancy, borderline risk estimation, and other selection populations.
