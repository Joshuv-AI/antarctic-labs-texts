Recurrence patterns stratify post-progression survival after early recurrence of colorectal liver metastases following hepatectomy: a single-center retrospective study
Authors: Taiki Mori, Nozomu Sakai, Tsukasa Takayashiki, Shigetsugu Takano, Isamu Hosokawa, Takanori Konishi, Kensuke Suzuki, Hitoe Nishino, Shinichiro Nakada, Masayuki Ohtsuka

ABSTRACT

Background

Colorectal cancer frequently metastasizes to the liver, and hepatectomy offers the best chance for long-term survival in selected patients with colorectal liver metastases (CRLM) [1, 2]. However, recurrence after curative-intent hepatectomy is common and remains the principal driver of long-term mortality. Risk stratification is therefore essential for postoperative counseling, surveillance, and selection of perioperative systemic therapy, and increasingly incorporates molecular features such as RAS status [3–7]. Time to recurrence is widely used as a surrogate for tumor aggressiveness [5]. Early recurrence, typically defined as recurrence within 6–12 months, is consistently associated with inferior survival and is often interpreted as evidence of occult systemic disease [8, 9].

Nevertheless, early recurrence represents a heterogeneous clinical entity. In clinical practice, some patients present with recurrence confined to a single site, whereas others develop multi-site disease at initial relapse, suggesting differences in disease burden, resectability, and prognosis [10–13]. Despite its clinical relevance, whether the recurrence pattern at first relapse further stratifies outcomes among patients with early recurrence after hepatectomy for CRLM remains unclear [10–13].

Recurrence heterogeneity reflects not only timing but also the extent of the first recurrence [12–17]. Patients with single-site recurrence may still be candidates for additional local therapies in select settings, whereas multi-site recurrence more often indicates disseminated disease with limited potential for durable local control [14, 18]. This distinction may be particularly important in early recurrence, which is frequently regarded as a uniformly high-risk group, despite variation in post-recurrence trajectories. Prior studies have often combined recurrence patterns or focused on overall survival, which conflates time to recurrence with outcomes after recurrence [8, 11, 19]. Thus, a recurrence-centered endpoint may better capture the clinical heterogeneity of early recurrence [20].

Interpretation of salvage therapies is also complicated by treatment selection and immortal time biases, as only patients who survive and remain resectable are eligible [21, 22]. Therefore, rather than focusing on treatment, we examined the anatomical extent of the disease at first recurrence. We hypothesized that recurrence pattern—single-site versus multi-site—could provide a simple and clinically meaningful prognostic stratification. Accordingly, we evaluated its association with post-progression survival (PPS) among patients with early recurrence after curative-intent hepatectomy for CRLM.

Methods

Study design and patient population

This retrospective cohort study included consecutive patients who underwent initial curative-intent hepatectomy for CRLM at Chiba University Hospital between January 2012 and December 2022. Curative intent was defined as complete macroscopic treatment of all liver metastases, with no unresected extrahepatic disease. Patients were excluded if hepatectomy was performed for non-colorectal primary tumors, if macroscopic residual disease (R2) remained, or if postoperative follow-up data were unavailable. Patients undergoing combined local treatments (e.g., hepatectomy with thermal ablation) were included if all lesions were treated with curative intent.

The analysis focused on patients who developed recurrence within 12 months. Postoperative surveillance generally included physical examination and tumor markers every 3–6 months and contrast-enhanced computed tomography of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis at similar intervals during the first 2 years, followed by longer intervals thereafter, which is broadly consistent with surveillance strategies reported in prior studies and systematic reviews [23]. Surveillance was not strictly protocolized and varied by clinical judgment. Follow-up was updated to December 31, 2025.

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chiba University Hospital (approval code: HK202507-08). The requirement for individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design and the use of de-identified data. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE statement for observational studies.

Exposure assessment

The primary exposure was the recurrence pattern at first relapse within 12 months, classified as single-site or multi-site. Recurrence was identified by imaging and/or pathological confirmation when available; otherwise, it was determined based on serial imaging and multidisciplinary assessment.

Recurrence patterns were classified according to the anatomical extent of disease at first relapse. Each involved organ or anatomically distinct extra-organ compartment was considered a single anatomical site. Multiple lesions confined to the same organ were classified as single-site recurrence, irrespective of their number or distribution within that organ. Thus, multifocal intrahepatic recurrence involving different liver segments was classified as single-site recurrence. Recurrence involving two or more organs or anatomically distinct compartments was classified as multi-site recurrence. Accordingly, intrahepatic recurrence accompanied by hepatic hilar lymph node metastasis was classified as multi-site recurrence because the liver and regional lymph nodes were considered anatomically distinct sites. Single-site recurrences were further categorized as liver, lung, or other for descriptive purposes.

Outcome assessment

The primary endpoint was PPS, defined as the interval from the first recurrence to death from any cause or to the last follow-up. Surviving patients were censored at the last follow-up.

Post-recurrence treatments, including salvage metastasectomy, were recorded. Salvage metastasectomy was defined as repeat curative-intent resection of recurrent disease, including repeat hepatectomy, pulmonary metastasectomy, or resections at multiple sites as part of the same treatment strategy [18].

Other variables

Baseline clinicopathological variables were extracted from medical records and operative and pathological reports, including age, sex, primary tumor location, primary lymph node status, synchronous versus metachronous liver metastasis, number of liver metastases, largest tumor size, bilobar involvement, major hepatectomy (≥ 3 segments), and resection margin status. Molecular tumor data, including RAS and BRAF mutation status, were recorded when available.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as medians (interquartile ranges), and categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages). Baseline characteristics were compared between the single-site and multi-site recurrence groups using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables, as appropriate.

PPS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method and compared with the log-rank test. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Covariates for multivariable analysis were selected a priori based on clinical relevance and the number of events. A two-sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Analyses were performed using JMP (SAS Institute, Cary, NC, USA) and R (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient cohort and early recurrence patterns

A total of 151 patients underwent initial hepatectomy for CRLM. Early recurrence within 12 months occurred in 78 patients (51.7%), while 73 patients were classified as not having early recurrence, including 31 with late recurrence (≥ 12 months) and 42 without recurrence during follow-up (Fig. 1).

For the primary analysis, patients with early recurrence were stratified according to the anatomical extent of disease at first relapse into single-site and multi-site recurrence groups. Among the 78 patients with early recurrence, 43 (55.1%) had single-site recurrence and 35 (44.9%) had multi-site recurrence. In the single-site recurrence group, the first recurrence site was the liver in 25 patients (58.1%), the lungs in 14 (32.6%), and other sites in 4 (9.3%). Salvage metastasectomy was performed in 35 patients (44.9%) overall, including 20 of 43 patients (46.5%) with single-site recurrence and 15 of 35 patients (42.9%) with multi-site recurrence. The proportion of patients undergoing salvage metastasectomy did not differ significantly between the two groups (p = 0.821, Fisher’s exact test).

Supplementary analyses

Overall survival after initial hepatectomy was lower in patients with early recurrence (< 12 months) than in those with late recurrence (≥ 12 months) (Figure S1).

An exploratory organ-specific analysis of first recurrence among patients with early recurrence is shown in Figure S2; lung-only recurrence appeared to have the most favorable PPS, although the difference was not statistically significant. A 3-month landmark analysis evaluating salvage metastasectomy (Figure S3) showed a similar trend toward improved PPS, but it was not statistically significant. RAS mutation status was available for 119 patients (78.8%), and mutations were present in 44 (37.0%). Among recurrent cases with available data, RAS mutations were more frequent in early than in late recurrence (31/67 [46.3%] vs. 6/24 [25.0%]; p = 0.091) (Table S3 and Figure S4).

Discussion

In this single-institution cohort of patients with early recurrence within 12 months after hepatectomy for CRLM, prognosis after recurrence differed according to whether the first relapse was confined to a single site or involved multiple sites. Importantly, the main finding was not merely that multi-site recurrence was associated with poorer survival, but that early recurrence itself comprised prognostically distinct subgroups according to the anatomical extent of the first relapse. Patients with single-site recurrence had a significantly longer PPS than those with multi-site recurrence, and this association remained significant after adjustment for primary lymph node status, number of liver metastases, and resection margin status. The comparable rates of salvage metastasectomy between the two groups indicate that the observed difference in PPS was unlikely to be explained solely by the overall frequency of salvage surgery. These findings suggest that early recurrence is not a uniform clinical entity; rather, the anatomical extent at first relapse captures clinically relevant heterogeneity in post-recurrence outcomes, consistent with prior reports linking recurrence pattern to survival after CRLM resection [10, 11, 15, 24].

Early recurrence is often considered a surrogate of aggressive tumor biology and occult systemic disease [25]. However, our results suggest that this view is incomplete. While time to recurrence remains useful for postoperative counseling and surveillance, prognosis after recurrence is also strongly influenced by disease extent at relapse. By focusing on PPS, this study more directly reflects post-recurrence decision-making than overall survival. As expected, early recurrence was also associated with inferior overall survival compared with late recurrence (Figure S1). However, overall survival reflects both time to recurrence and post-recurrence course, supporting PPS as a more adequate endpoint in this context [19, 26].

Several mechanisms may explain the longer PPS observed in single-site early recurrence. Disease confined to one anatomical site may be more amenable to durable local control, including repeat hepatectomy or pulmonary metastasectomy in selected patients, [14, 18, 27, 28] whereas multi-site recurrence more often reflects a greater systemic tumor burden and limited potential for durable local control [22]. Differences in detectability, resectability, and metastatic organotropism may also contribute.

Salvage metastasectomy was performed in 44.9% of patients with early recurrence. Contrary to the possibility that the survival difference was primarily attributable to greater use of salvage surgery in patients with anatomically limited recurrence, the proportion undergoing salvage metastasectomy was similar between the single-site and multi-site groups. This finding suggests that the observed difference in PPS was unlikely to be explained solely by differences in the overall frequency of salvage surgery and supports the prognostic relevance of the anatomical extent of recurrence. Nevertheless, the present analysis did not account for the site, extent, timing, or completeness of salvage resection, and treatment selection was inherently influenced by disease distribution, technical resectability, patient condition, and multidisciplinary judgment. The supplementary landmark analysis stratified by salvage metastasectomy status provides additional exploratory information but should be interpreted cautiously because of treatment-selection and time-related biases [21, 22].

Our findings may have practical implications for post-recurrence stratification of patients with early relapse after hepatectomy for CRLM. Although patients with early recurrence are often grouped as having a poor prognosis, our results suggest that this classification alone may be overly simplistic. Patients with single-site recurrence, particularly those with disease confined to the liver or lungs, may still have an opportunity for prolonged survival. However, single-site recurrence should not be interpreted as uniformly favorable disease, as some patients still have multiple lesions or otherwise unresectable recurrences within a single organ. This classification may serve as a practical first step for post-recurrence stratification, helping to identify patients who need careful multidisciplinary assessment for possible salvage local treatment. In contrast, multi-site recurrence appears to indicate a less favorable post-recurrence course and may warrant prioritizing systemic therapy, with local intervention reserved for highly selected situations [14, 18, 27, 29].

Although our molecular analyses were exploratory, the observed trend is broadly consistent with prior reports that suggest associations among RAS mutation status, recurrence pattern, and salvageability, with variable prognostic impact depending on disease timing [16, 17, 30].

This study has several limitations. First, this was a retrospective, single-institution study and was therefore subject to residual confounding. Second, the sample size was modest, particularly for the subgroup and molecular analyses. Third, post-recurrence treatment was not standardized and may have varied according to patient condition, disease distribution, technical resectability, physician judgment, and changes in systemic therapy over time. Although the overall proportion of patients undergoing salvage metastasectomy did not differ significantly between the recurrence-pattern groups, residual treatment-related confounding cannot be excluded. The type, extent, timing, and completeness of salvage resection, as well as subsequent systemic treatment, may have differed between patients and were not fully incorporated into the multivariable model. Moreover, although salvage metastasectomy was associated with longer PPS, treatment-selection and time-related biases could not be fully eliminated, and this association should not be interpreted as a direct treatment effect. Similarly, the absence of a statistically significant difference in salvage metastasectomy rates should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence between the recurrence-pattern groups, given the limited sample size. Finally, molecular testing, including RAS and BRAF mutation status, was not available for all patients; therefore, these analyses should be considered exploratory.

Despite these limitations, this study had several strengths. It was based on a consecutive surgical cohort, used a clinically intuitive classification of early recurrence as single-site versus multi-site disease, and focused on the PPS to more directly characterize the post-recurrence course. These findings support the concept that patients with early recurrence after hepatectomy for CRLM should not automatically be regarded as a homogeneous group with a poor prognosis. Larger multi-institutional studies are needed to validate this recurrence pattern-based stratification and to determine how the anatomical extent at first recurrence can be integrated with molecular and radiologic markers to improve the prediction of salvageability and long-term outcomes.

Conclusion

Among patients with early recurrence after hepatectomy for CRLM, the recurrence pattern at first relapse further stratified PPS. Single-site recurrence was associated with significantly better PPS than multi-site recurrence, suggesting that early recurrence is clinically heterogeneous rather than uniformly aggressive. Classifying the first recurrence as single-site or multi-site may help identify patients with different post-recurrence prognoses, supporting multidisciplinary treatment planning.
