Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of the Incidence of Chronic Kidney Disease After Pediatric Critical Illness
Authors: Olugbenga Akinkugbe, Luca Marchetto, Isaac Martin, Shin Hann Chia
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVE:Survivors of pediatric critical illnesses are at risk of significant long-term organ sequelae. Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a complication of critical illness (and ICU interventions) associated with growth impairment, cardiovascular disease, and early death. Our objective was to synthesize the evidence on the incidence of CKD among survivors of pediatric critical illness.DATA SOURCES:MEDLINE, Embase, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, and Cochrane Register of Controlled Trials from inception to February 2024.STUDY SELECTION:Observational studies reporting the incidence of de novo CKD among survivors of pediatric critical illness.DATA EXTRACTION:Two reviewers independently extracted data on study design, setting, population, demographics, diagnostic criteria, and outcome.DATA SYNTHESIS:Meta-analysis was used to describe the incidence of CKD among survivors, risk of bias (RoB) assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Tool, and strength and reliability of evidence assessed with GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations). CKD was quantified as an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) less than 90 mL/min/1.73 m2 (outcome 1), eGFR less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2 (outcome 2), and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) as eGFR less than 15 mL/min per 1.73 m2 (outcome 3). Twelve studies (3642 patients) met selection criteria and reported at least one measure of CKD. The median duration of follow-up was 2, 3.6, and 5 years, respectively, for outcomes 1, 2, and 3. For each threshold, the pooled estimate of CKD incidence was 24% (95% CI, 16–32%) for eGFR less than 90, 14% (95% CI, 6–23%) less than 60, and 4% (95% CI, 0–7%) for ESRD. The overall quality assessment indicated a moderate RoB.CONCLUSIONS:Among a heterogenous population of pediatric critical illness survivors, an important minority of survivors developed CKD or ESRD. This study highlights the importance of diagnostic criteria for reporting, a greater focus on postcritical care surveillance and follow-up to identify those with CKD. Further study would facilitate the delineation of high-risk groups and strategies for improved outcomes.

KEY POINTS

Question: Synthesize the evidence describing the incidence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) following pediatric critical illness.

Findings: Among pediatric patients, 14% (95% CI, 6–23%) developed CKD (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m²) over a median period of 3.6 years following critical illness. Additionally, 14% (95% CI, 9–7%) developed end-stage renal disease (ESRD) over a median follow-up period of 5 years.

Meanings: These data prompt further work to better understand the contributory and potentially modifiable factors leading to CKD among survivors of pediatric critical illness and highlight the need for improved postcritical care follow-up and surveillance programs to facilitate early intervention to modify the progression to ESRD.

Life-threatening illnesses such as sepsis can cause organ injury and irreversible damage to organ functions. Children who survive critical illness are at risk of significant short- and long-term complications. Injury to the kidneys that results in long-term loss of function is recognized as chronic kidney disease (CKD), defined as functional abnormalities of the kidney persisting for 3 months or more. In the critically ill population, this may result from: 1) renal system injury from the inciting illness leading to persisting loss of organ function or increased susceptibility to loss of function, 2) treatments and supportive interventions impact kidney function (e.g., nephrotoxic medications, fluid removal resulting in hypovolemia), and 3) poor identification of children at risk due to inadequacy of existing tools (creatinine-based estimation of renal function may be confounded by low muscle mass).

CKD is graded according to the severity of renal functional impairment, as defined by the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) in the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) Practice Guidelines initially published in 2012 and recently updated in 2024 (Supplemental 1, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B375) (1, 2). The KDIGO thresholds are widely applied in the diagnosis and management of CKD in the pediatric population (3, 4).

Childhood CKD is associated with long-term consequences including hypertension and anemia which can result in progressive cardiovascular disease, impaired neurocognitive ability, reduced exercise capacity, growth impairment, and death (5–7). Chronic organ dysfunction and the impact of treatments disrupt normal childhood educational and social development and place significant demands on families, caregivers, and society (3, 5–9). Children with CKD have poorer health-related quality of life when compared with healthy children (10). Mortality for children with end-stage renal disease (ESRD) receiving dialysis has been estimated as 3–150 times higher than the general pediatric population and life expectancy for this cohort is reduced by around 50 years (4).

The pathophysiologic pathways and risk factors associated with acute kidney injury (AKI) have been described previously (11–14). Observational studies have reported up to 30% of children admitted to PICU develop AKI, and up to 12% develop severe AKI (stage 2 or 3 according to the KDIGO AKI guidelines). Severe AKI has been associated with greater PICU mortality and 1-year mortality, greater duration of mechanical ventilation and vasoactive support, and greater lengths of PICU and hospital stay. Among children admitted to PICU, AKI was independently associated with higher 5- to 7-year mortality (adjusted hazard ratio 3.10 [95% CI, 1.46–6.57] and 3.38 [95% CI, 1.63–7.02], respectively) (15, 16).

The progression from life-threatening critical illness syndromes and AKI to CKD is not well understood in children and there is uncertainty on the proportion of PICU patients who subsequently receive a diagnosis of CKD. Because of poor recognition, heterogenous clinical practice, and resource limitation, many cases globally are not identified, resulting in inadequate care for many children (17, 18). Prior studies (3, 6, 17, 19) have focused on renal outcomes of critically ill children with AKI, or those in receipt of renal replacement therapy (RRT). However, a diversity of risk factors other than AKI and RRT may be relevant during and after critical illness and associated with the development of CKD, in particular for specific conditions.

The objective of this study was to synthesize the evidence describing the incidence of CKD among children following critical illness.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

We included observational studies describing the incidence of CKD following episodes of critical illness occurring under 18 years old. Studies which did not identify patients with preexisting renal tract disease were excluded. Eligible studies reported original data in peer-reviewed journals published in English, French, Spanish, and Italian (based on authors’ fluency).

Outcome Measures

The main outcome measure was the incidence of CKD diagnosis after critical illness, presented as a percentage of those eligible for follow-up. Owing to differences in reporting, we analyzed the incidence of CKD according to three CKD thresholds: 1) eGFR less than 90 mL/min per 1.73 m2 (outcome 1), 2) eGFR less than 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 (outcome 2), and 3) ESRD (outcome 3) as no single CKD threshold was used across all eligible studies. This was a deviation from the protocol as the reporting of multiple outcome measures was not anticipated. Timing of outcome assessment was noted, where reported. Studies did not specify the timing of incident outcomes at the patient level.

Information Sources and Search Strategy

We developed an inclusive search strategy with the assistance of an expert medical librarian to identify relevant observational studies. Four key concepts informed our search strategy; 1) pediatric population, 2) CKD, 3) incidence, and 4) critical illness syndromes. Comprehensive searches of the following databases were performed from inception through to February 29, 2024: Ovid Medline (and Epub ahead of Print, In-Data-Review, In-Process), Embase, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature, and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (Supplemental 2, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B375).

Data Management and Study Selection

Citations identified from the literature search were exported to COVIDENCE software (www.covidence.org) for abstract screening, full-text review, and data extraction. All references were extracted to Mendeley (www.mendeley.com) for referencing. Title and abstract screening were performed independently by two authors to identify potentially relevant studies. Full-text review of selected studies was performed independently. Differences in screening decisions were addressed by a third author with reference to the eligibility criteria.

Data Collection Process

Data were extracted to Microsoft Excel documents independently by two authors and compared. Where divergence occurred, relevant studies were discussed collectively.

Risk of Bias in Individual Studies

Risk of bias (RoB) was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist for Prevalence Studies (20).

Data Synthesis

For each outcome, we performed a meta-analysis with a random effects model. Results were presented as proportions. We assessed heterogeneity between studies using visual inspection of the Forest plots, the chi-square test for homogeneity (p < 0.1 indicating important heterogeneity), and the I2 statistic (for which a value of 50% or greater was considered reflective of potentially important heterogeneity). In anticipation of population heterogeneity, we conducted subgroup analyses for patients with sepsis, and those who were recipients of non-kidney solid organ transplants. We performed sensitivity analyses excluding the latter group.

We did not place temporal restrictions on the studies included but recognized the potential for heterogeneity among studies before and after the publication of the 2012 KDIGO Guidelines for the Management of CKD (21). The two studies published before 2012 reported the criteria used for the diagnosis of CKD.

All statistical analyses were performed using the “meta” statistical package available in R, version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; www.r-project.org).

Confidence in Cumulative Evidence

The strength and reliability of the body of evidence were assessed with the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations framework adapted from recommendations for prognosis reviews (22, 23).

Registration and Approval

The updated Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement was used to guide the design and reporting of this systematic review. This systematic review did not require institutional review board approval. We registered the protocol for this systematic review on the PROSPERO (www.crd.york.ac.uk).

RESULTS

The literature search identified a total of 6768 studies. After removing 2293 duplicate studies, abstract screening was performed on 4475 studies, resulting in the exclusion of 4351 studies. We performed full text screening of 119 studies excluding a further 107 studies. We extracted data from 12 studies (Fig. 1) (24–35).

Characteristics of Included Studies

There were 12 eligible retrospective studies that enrolled patients between 1991 and 2021 (Table 1). Five were multicenter studies and 7 were single center. Eleven of the 12 studies were conducted in Europe or North America. One study was conducted in India. The median age of participants for each study was between 0.5 and 9 years. The median proportion of females was 48%. Study size varied from 22 to 1688 eligible participants. Median follow-up time across studies ranged from 3 months to almost 10 years. Three studies focused on patients with sepsis or septic shock and two studies focused on recipients of non-renal solid organ transplants (Table 1).

Risk of Bias

RoB was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute Checklist for Prevalence Studies (Supplemental 3, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B375). The assessment indicated a high RoB for two studies included in the meta-analysis. The relevant studies lacked detail in descriptions of the study populations but otherwise met eligibility criteria.

Outcomes

Seven studies reported more than one outcome measure of CKD (Tables 1 and 2). Outcome 1 (eGFR < 90 mL/min per 1.73 m2) was reported by seven studies, accounting for 958 patients with median follow-up of 2 years. The pooled estimate of the incidence of outcome 1 was 0.24 (0.16–0.32). Nine studies reported outcome 2 (eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2) for 1798 participants with median follow-up of 3.6 years. The pooled estimate for outcome 2 was 0.14 (0.06–0.23). Eight studies reported outcome 3 (ESRD) for 3012 participants with median follow-up of 5 years. The pooled estimate for outcome 3 was 0.04 (0.00–0.07), (Fig. 2; Supplemental 4, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B375).

Across all studies, there was variable reporting of follow-up duration, and outcomes measured. Seven studies reported duration of follow-up with at least one of the two more severe outcome measures (eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 and ESRD).

Subgroup and Sensitivity Analyses

Three studies reported the incidence of CKD (eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2) among children who survived sepsis. The pooled estimate was 0.05 (0.03–0.07) for 555 participants. Mean follow-up time was reported by one study (8.7 y). Two studies reported the incidence of CKD (eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2) among nonrenal solid organ transplant recipients. The pooled estimate was 0.11 (0.03–0.18) for 373 participants. Mean follow-up time was reported by one study (9.3 y) (Tables 1 and 2).

Sensitivity analyses were performed excluding nonrenal solid organ transplant patients (Table 2). Two studies of transplant recipients were removed from outcomes 2 and 3 (the two studies did not report outcome 1). This did not change the pooled estimates, respectively, of 0.14 (0.03–0.24) for eGFR less than 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2 and 0.04 (0.00–0.07) for ESRD (Table 2).

DISCUSSION

This systematic review and meta-analysis of observational studies provides an estimate of the risk of CKD and ESRD after critical illness in pediatric patients. Meta-analyses were performed for three different outcome measures resulting in pooled estimates for each grade of CKD severity: 1) 24% (95% CI, 16–32%), for outcome 1: eGFR less than 90 mL/min per 1.73 m2, 2) 14% (95% CI, 6–23%) for outcome 2: eGFR less than 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2, and 3) 4% (95% CI, 0–7%) for outcome 3: ESRD. These data quantify organ-specific sequelae of a diversity of pediatric critical illnesses. With the GRADE assessment, we can be moderately confident that the probability for all three outcomes is close to the population estimate (Supplemental 5, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B375) (22, 23). The assessments were affected by a high I2 statistic (indicating important heterogeneity) (Table 2; Fig. 2).

A deliberate key aim of this study was to include studies representing the heterogeneity of conditions among the pediatric population with critical illness. Importance was given to filling the gap of a CKD estimate broadly applicable to the diversity of conditions managed by pediatric critical care providers. In contrast with prior studies (3, 6, 17, 19), it was decided not to restrict participants to those with specific conditions, those who developed AKI, or those in receipt of CRRT. The intention was to recognize a diversity of risk factors to which patients are exposed during and after critical illness that potentially contribute to the progression of CKD. The study by Weiss et al (36) showed that although the risk of CKD is higher in patients with a history of AKI, CKD still occurred in patients without AKI, raising the importance of vigilance for long-term kidney outcomes following critical illness, whether or not AKI is recognized.

Epidemiologic work on childhood CKD is challenging for numerous reasons. It is a poorly recognized condition, causes and risk factors are varied and distinct from the adult population, and CKD represents a continuum of kidney function ranging from asymptomatic disease to ESRD on RRT and kidney transplant candidacy. Despite the opportunity for consensus and harmonization of practice following the publication of the KDIGO guidelines in 2012, there remain significant challenges in this area. Because of poor recognition, heterogenous clinical practice, and resource limitation, many cases globally are not identified resulting in inadequate care for many children (17, 18). The consequences are missed opportunities to intervene in ways that may improve outcomes (37).

Achieving optimal functional and developmental outcomes for children is a central goal of pediatric critical care medicine. Quantifying the incidence of CKD after pediatric critical illness with a systematic review highlights CKD among the priority outcome measures of critical care. These data will provide the basis to evaluate the effectiveness of measures and strategies in the critical care setting and beyond to reduce CKD. They can support the case for follow-up, particularly among his-risk groups of children surviving critical illness (8, 37, 38).

Across the studies identified, investigators used a diversity of tools and criteria for the measurement of CKD, necessitating a stratified approach to data synthesis and reporting. Timing and duration of follow-up also varied between studies (Supplemental 4, http://links.lww.com/CCX/B375). This heterogeneity of measures and thresholds raises a challenge for accurately quantifying the risk of CKD in this population. We suggest that consensus on renal outcome measures would simplify the incorporation of long-term renal function into developing postcritical illness follow-up programs. It presents an opportunity to improve recognition and management of renal sequelae, and potentially mitigate progressive deterioration of renal function and other adverse outcomes in affected children.

The main limitations of our study are the heterogenous population, the moderate number of studies that reported each outcome measure, and variable follow-up duration. Although a conscious choice was made to include a heterogenous population, it is appreciated that results will need to be interpreted in light of specific subgroups for whom specific renal risk factors may differentially apply. We did not consider trials reporting renal outcomes as such studies typically recruit selected populations that would not have been representative of all critically ill children. Of the 12 studies that were included in this review, every single measure was reported by 7–9 studies, accounting for between 958 and 3012 patients for each outcome.

The majority (n = 7/12) of the studies in our review reported median follow-up of at least 1 year (Table 1). However, two studies reported follow-up at 3 months and three studies did not report the duration of median follow-up. Timing and duration of follow-up are of importance for identifying the progression of renal injury following critical illness. Longer duration of follow-up would be informative given the age and development-related changes in renal function observed in childhood. These limitations highlight the need for alignment of CKD follow-up and diagnostic criteria for reporting outcomes in this population.

We acknowledge the potential utility of estimating the incidence of CKD among those who had AKI compared with those who did not. It is a limitation of the review that we were unable to specifically delineate patients without AKI. Based on the reported data were not able to specifically differentially extract the outcome measure (incidence of CKD) from the general population of eligible participants, from those who had AKI. Where the population with AKI was reported, we were unable to separately extract the incidence of CKD.

Although resource constraints may necessitate targeting of follow-up to higher-risk populations initially, these data demonstrate the importance of a consensus approach to harmonized monitoring of renal outcomes in children who survive critical illness. Increased follow-up using standardized parameters would improve understanding of post-ICU renal morbidity and inform prognostication, service planning, and delivery.

CONCLUSIONS

Approximately 14% (95% CI, 6–23%) of pediatric patients may develop CKD (eGFR < 60 mL/min per 1.73 m2) over a median of 3.6 years after critical illness. An estimated 4% (95% CI 9–7%) may develop ESRD over a median 5 years of follow-up. Our evidence synthesis revealed significant heterogeneity, likely attributable to the diversity of pediatric critical illnesses among the included studies.

There may be potential benefits of standardizing: 1) diagnostic criteria, 2) timing of follow-up, and 3) reporting. These data may prompt further work to better understand the contributory and potentially modifiable factors leading to CKD among survivors of pediatric critical illness. They also highlight the need for post-critical care follow-up and surveillance programs with the goal of facilitating early intervention in the progression to ESRD.
