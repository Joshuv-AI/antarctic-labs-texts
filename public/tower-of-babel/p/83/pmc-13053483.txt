CCR4-associated factor CAF1I promotes plant growth and ectomycorrhizal formation in Populus tomentosa
Authors: Ying Liu, Chaofeng Li, Ziwei Yang, Norihisa Matsushita, Chunlan Lian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEctomycorrhizal (ECM) fungi preferentially form symbiotic associations with many woody plants. However, the genes regulating ECM formation and their functions in host plants remain poorly understood. In this study, PtoCAF1I gene from Populus tomentosa was isolated and functionally characterized. Bioinformatics analysis revealed that PtoCAF1I contains a conserved RNase D structural domain. Tissue expression analysis showed that PtoCAF1I was more highly expressed in roots than other tissues (leaves and stems). Overexpression of PtoCAF1I in P. tomentosa resulted in significantly greater shoot height, number of branches, and biomass in potted seedlings compared with wild-type (WT) plants. Inoculation experiments revealed that overexpression of PtoCAF1I significantly increased the total ECM rate compared with WT poplar inoculated with Cenococcum geophilum, whereas PtoCAF1I RNA interference (RNAi) lines presented an opposite trend. These results suggest that PtoCAF1I not only promotes poplar growth and branch development but also enhances the formation of ECM roots upon inoculation with C. geophilum.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00572-026-01258-3.Keywords: PtoCAF1I, Plant development, Ectomycorrhizal formation, Populus tomentosa, Cenococcum geophilum, Mycorrhiza

Introduction

Ectomycorrhizal (ECM) symbiosis represents a widespread and ecologically significant association between the fine roots of host trees and fungal hyphae, embedded within the complex network of plant–microorganism interactions in forest ecosystems (Garcia et al. 2015; de Freitas Pereira et al. 2018). During the establishment of this mutualistic relationship, fungal hyphae extend between the epidermal and cortical cells of roots, forming a highly branched structure known as the Hartig net, which facilitates the bidirectional exchange of metabolites between symbiotic partners (Li et al. 2024). Through this interaction, ECM fungi significantly enhance the host’s acquisition of essential mineral nutrients, particularly nitrogen and phosphorus, in return for plant-derived carbon (Smith and Read 2008). Beyond nutrient facilitation, ECM symbiosis also contributes to host fitness by modulating plant growth and development, increasing resistance to biotic and abiotic stresses, and shaping root system architecture. Plants require a multifaceted adaptive immune system to survive in unfavorable environments, and establishing beneficial symbiotic relationships is particularly crucial (Plett et al. 2020).

ECM symbiosis between fungi and host plants is orchestrated through a series of bidirectional signaling and response events, beginning with mutual recognition of symbiotic signals, progressing to the Hartig net formation, and culminating in the tightly regulated nutrient exchange that supports sustained mutualism (Garcia et al. 2015). Plant roots secrete a chemically diverse repertoire of primary and secondary metabolites, many of which possess bioactive properties that influence microbial behavior, including enzymes, phytohormones, and sugars (Pascale et al. 2020). Within this symbiotic system, a number of mycorrhiza-induced small secreted proteins (MiSSPs) are markedly upregulated during ECM establishment and are crucial for the development of a functional symbiotic interface with the host plant (Plett et al. 2014, 2020; Pellegrin et al. 2019; Daguerre et al. 2020; Kang et al. 2020). In the jasmonic acid (JA) signaling pathway, the Laccaria bicolor effector MiSSP7 stabilizes the JA corepressor JAZ6, thereby suppressing the activity of Populus MYC2 transcription factors and ultimately influencing ECM formation (Plett et al. 2011; Plett et al. 2014; Daguerre et al. 2020; Marqués-Gálvez et al. 2024). On the host side, sugar transporters such as SWEET1c mediate carbohydrate transfer during ECM symbiosis (Li et al. 2024). Notably, new evidence reveals that multiple microRNAs are involved in the formation of ECM symbiosis, among which Pmic_miR-8 is predicted to target host transcripts containing NB-ARC domains (Wong-Bajracharya et al. 2022; Tao et al. 2023). Despite these advances, how host post-transcriptional regulators such as RNA decay factors modulate ECM formation remains largely unclear. Understanding these mechanisms may shed light on the molecular basis of stable plant–fungus symbiosis.

The CCR4–NOT complex is an evolutionarily conserved multi-subunit protein complex present in eukaryotes, including plants, and plays pivotal roles in chromatin modification, transcriptional elongation, mRNA degradation, microRNA-mediated or small interfering RNA-mediated deadenylation (Sun et al. 2022). Within this complex, CCR4-associated factor 1 (CAF1, also known as POP2) is characterized by an RNase D DEDD domain and functions primarily by catalyzing the removal of poly(A) tails from mRNAs, thereby promoting mRNA decay and regulating transcript stability (Liang et al. 2009; Walley et al. 2009; Passmore and Coller 2022). Despite their conserved catalytic domains, variations in untranslated regions (UTRs) and intron-exon structures among family members suggest additional levels of regulation, including tissue-specific expression and responsiveness to environmental stimuli (Sun et al. 2022).

In plants, CAF1 has been extensively studied and is known to play crucial roles in growth and development, pathogen defense, and responses to environmental stresses (Walley et al. 2010). In Arabidopsis thaliana, AtCAF1 negatively regulates wound-induced disease resistance (Tackmin et al. 2011). AtCAF1a and AtCAF1b not only regulate mRNA deadenylation (Liang et al. 2009) but also contribute to plant defense against pathogens and responses to abiotic stresses (Walley et al. 2009). Expression of CpCAF1 from Chimonanthus praecox in A. thaliana has been reported to promote flowering and enhance cold tolerance (Lv et al. 2023). In Capsicum annuum, CaCAF1 is essential for plant development and defense, and its activity increases the susceptibility of Xanthomonas axonopodis pv. vesicatoria, the bacterial spot pathogen (Sarowar et al. 2007). Under abiotic stress conditions, each CAF1 gene in Oryza sativa plays a distinct role in plant development and stress response (Chou et al. 2014). OsCAF1 regulates chloroplast development by modulating the splicing of group II introns in rice (Zhang et al. 2019). Furthermore, OsCAF1B has been implicated in the shortening of the poly(A) tail of αAmy3 mRNA, contributing to rice seed germination and seedling development (Fang et al. 2020), as well as improving cold stress tolerance in young rice seedlings (Fang et al. 2021). In the halophilic alga Dunaliella salina, DsCAF1 is involved in environmental stress responses (Chen et al. 2016). In Populus, overexpression of PtCAF1Q has been shown to improve drought tolerance (Wang et al. 2022), and PtCAF1I has been implicated in promoting poplar development and enhancing resistance to Septotinia populiperda (Wang et al. 2023). However, the function of the CAF1 gene in poplar ECM formation remains unclear. Notably, PtCAF1I exhibits preferential expression in roots compared with other tissues (Wang et al. 2022). Given that interaction between ECM fungi and roots occurs in root systems, this root-preferential expression pattern strongly suggested a potential role in root-associated biological processes. In addition, plant defense pathways and ECM symbiosis may share overlapping signaling components and regulatory mechanisms (Plett et al. 2014), which raises the possibility that CAF1I may participate in ECM-related processes.

In this study, we performed a bioinformatic analysis of the CAF1 gene family and investigated the differential expression of PtoCAF1I across various tissues. PtoCAF1I was introduced into P. tomentosa using Agrobacterium-mediated transformation to generate transgenic plants. Morphological analysis demonstrated that PtoCAF1I plays an important role in the growth and development of P. tomentosa. Inoculation experiments further confirmed that PtoCAF1I exerts a positive effect on ECM formation in poplar. By extending the current understanding of the CAF1 gene family functions from model systems to perennial woody plants, our study provides new insights into the genetic regulation of mutualistic interactions in forest trees.

Materials and methods

Biological materials and growth conditions

Populus tomentosa (clone 741) was cultivated on sterile woody plant medium (WPM) (McCown and Lloyd 1981) in a growth chamber under a long-day photoperiod (16 h light/8 h dark) with day/night temperature of 25/22 °C, and light intensity of 10,000 lx. The plants were subcultured at intervals of approximately one month. Cenococcum geophilum isolate JaCg010 (Tao et al. 2023) was pre-cultured in 90 mm Petri dishes containing modified Melin–Norkrans (MMN) agar medium (Marx 1969) at 25 °C in darkness for 30 days before use.

Bioinformatics analysis

All available amino acid sequences of the CAF1 were downloaded from the Phytozome database (Tuskan et al. 2006; Goodstein et al. 2012; Lamesch et al. 2012). The CAF1 members of P. tomentosa were identified based on the published genome assembly (Tong et al. 2022) through sequence alignment. All CAF1 protein names and sequences are provided in the Supplementary File. Multiple sequence alignments of CAF1 proteins from different species were performed using DNAMAN software. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using MEGA11 (Tamura et al. 2021) by the neighbor-joining method (NJ). The phylogenetic tree was visualized and refined using the iTOL platform (v7.2, https://itol.embl.de/) (Letunic and Bork 2021). Protein structural prediction was conducted using the SWISS-MODEL platform (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive) (Waterhouse et al. 2018). Unless stated otherwise, the default settings recommended by the developers were applied.

For species other than A. thaliana, Populus trichocarpa, and P. tomentosa, multiple members of the CAF1 gene family have been identified. In this study, rather than including all predicted CAF1 homologs from each species, we selected CAF1 genes that have been functionally characterized in previous reports. We therefore chose these genes as representative reference sequences, as incorporating functionally validated members may provide useful context for inferring the potential roles of our target gene.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analyses

Total RNA was extracted from different tissues (leaves, stems, and roots) of well-grown P. tomentosa using RNAiso Plus (Takara, Shiga, Japan) by grinding in liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Then, cDNA was synthesized by reverse transcription using the PrimeScript™ RT reagent Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). cDNA was stored at -30 °C and used as a template for gene cloning and qRT-PCR analyses. qRT-PCR was performed using the Taq Pro Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix Kit (Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Thermal cycling conditions on the LightCycler® 96 system (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) were as follows: 95 °C for 30 s; 95 °C for 10 s, 60 °C for 20 s for 40 cycles, followed by a melting curve. The primer sequences are shown in Supplementary Table S1.

PtoCAF1I gene cloning and vector construction

The full-length coding sequence (CDS) of PtoCAF1I was amplified by PCR using gene-specific primers (Supplementary Table S1). The cDNA synthesized from total RNA extracted from leaves of P. tomentosa was used as the template. The PCR products were ligated into the plant binary vector pCXSN, which had been digested with XcmI and contains the CaMV 35S promoter. The 329-bp fragment of the PtoCAF1I coding sequence and its reverse complementary sequence were amplified from P. tomentosa cDNA using the primer pair PtoCAF1I-RNAi-F/R (Supplementary Table S1). A 164-bp LOOP fragment was synthesized by Tsingke Biotech (Beijing, China). All three fragments were assembled into the pCXSN vector via homologous recombination using the NEBuilder® HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix Kit (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), to generate the plasmid PtoCAF1I-RNAi. The vectors were transformed into Escherichia coli DH5α for sequence verification. The PCR reaction system (100 µL) consisted of 0.8 µL BIOTAQ™ DNA polymerase (Meridian Bioscience, Memphis, TN, USA), 8 µL of 10 mM dNTP mixture, 2.7 µL of 50 µM MgCl₂, 5 µL of forward primer (10 µM), 5 µL of reverse primer (10 µM), 100 ng of cDNA template, and ddH₂O reach the final volume. The PCR procedure was as follows: 94 °C for 5 min; 30 cycles of 94 °C for 30 s, 58 °C for 60 s, and 72 °C for 90 s; followed by a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min.

Genetic transformation of Populus tomentosa

The 35S:PtoCAF1I and RNAi-PtoCAF1I binary vectors were transformed into Agrobacterium tumefaciens GV1301 using the freeze-thaw method. Leaf disc transformation was performed to infect P. tomentosa leaves. The A. tumefaciens harboring the vectors were cultured in YEP medium supplemented with 50 mg/L kanamycin and 50 mg/L rifampicin until the optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) reached 0.2–0.4. Following centrifugation, A. tumefaciens cells were resuspended in liquid WPM medium and incubated for 40 to 60 min before being used for infection. Poplar leaf discs were immersed in the A. tumefaciens suspension for 10 min, blotted dry with sterile filter paper, and transferred to co-cultivation medium (WPM + 30 g/L sucrose, 7.6 g/L agar, 100 µM of acetosyringone (AS), 1.0 mg/L of naphthaleneacetic acid (NAA), and 2.0 mg/L of zeatin (ZT)) for 2 days. Subsequently, the leaf discs were transferred to selection medium (WPM + 30 g/L sucrose, 7.6 g/L agar, 9 mg/L hygromycin, 1.0 mg/L NAA, 2.0 mg/L ZT, and 400 mg/L cefotaxime (Cef) for further culture. Once calli formed, they were transferred to shoot induction medium (WPM + 30 g/L sucrose, 7.4 g/L agar, 9 mg/L hygromycin, 0.1 mg/L NAA, 2.0 mg/L ZT, and 400 mg/L Cef). Resistant buds were then moved to rooting medium (WPM + 30 g/L sucrose, 8.0 g/L agar, 9 mg/L hygromycin, 0.1 mg/L NAA, and 400 mg/L Cef). Transgenic poplars were identified by PCR amplification of the hygromycin resistance gene using primers listed in Supplementary Table S1. Leaf DNA was extracted using a modified cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB) method (Lian et al. 2001). The selection of transgenic lines with high expression levels was performed using qRT-PCR, as previously described, on root tissues sampled from uniformly grown plants to ensure comparable developmental status.

Morphological measurements and inoculation experiments

For morphological analysis, uniformly grown 21-day-old tissue-cultured seedlings of P. tomentosa were transplanted into plastic pots (410 × 160 × 170 mm) containing Akadama soil (autoclaved at 121 ℃ for 3 h) and cultivated in a growth chamber at 25 ℃ under a 16/8 h light/dark cycle for 7 days. Subsequently, seedlings were individually transferred into cylindrical plastic cups (450 mL capacity) filled with autoclaved Akadama soil and maintained under the same growth conditions. Final morphological measurements, including shoot height, branch number, and biomass, were conducted after two weeks. In addition, total root length and number of root tips were quantified using a root scanner and WinRhizo software (Regent Instruments, Quebec City, Canada) (Augé et al. 2003).

For the inoculation experiment, similarly prepared uniform seedlings (cultivated for 10 days in large pots) were selected. A soil mixture of Akadama soil and Shibanome soil (3:1, v/v), autoclaved at 121 ℃ for 3 h, was placed into transparent plastic boxes (235 × 85 × 16 mm). Two 30-day-old C. geophilum cultures were cut into small agar plugs, placed on the soil surface in each box, and covered with a nylon mesh (100 μm pore size) to physically separate the fungal plugs from the seedling roots, thereby minimizing interference from agar residues during subsequent ECM assessment. Seedling roots were gently spread over the mesh, and the boxes were arranged to maintain root zone darkness. The seedlings were cultivated under the same growth conditions as in the morphological measurements for an additional 45 days. Throughout the cultivation period, seedlings were irrigated biweekly with a nutrient solution containing 0.2% (v/v) Hyponex (Hyponex Japan, Osaka, Japan), 150 mg/L MgSO₄·7H₂O, 50 mg/L CaCl₂, and 10 mg/L FeCl₃. Sterilized tap water was added as needed to maintain soil moisture, typically every 3–4 days, when slight surface drying was observed. At the end of the inoculation period, ECM root tips were counted under a stereomicroscope. All root tips located within the colonization zone defined by the uppermost and lowermost ECM tips visible along the vertical root axis in the transparent growth box were enumerated and classified as either ECM or non-ECM. ECM rate was calculated as the ratio of ECM root tips to the total number of root tips within this region.

Results

Identification and phylogenetic analysis of PtoCAF1I

To investigate the potential role of Populus CAF1 genes, the amino acid sequences were subjected to phylogenetic analysis and multiple sequence alignment. A total of ten CAF1 gene family members were identified in P. tomentosa. A phylogenetic tree was constructed with PtoCAF1I and all CAF1 genes from A. thaliana and other species, as shown in Fig. 1a. The results revealed that all selected CAF1 genes clustered into five clusters, and PtoCAF1I was distributed in an independent clade together with PtCAF1I/M/P/K, PtoCAF1M/P, CsCAF1, AtCAF1a/b, and CaCAF1 (Fig. 1a). Notably, AtCAF1a/b, PtCAF1I, CsCAF1, and CaCAF1 within this clade have been reported to be involved in plant development and defense responses against pathogens (Walley et al. 2009; Wang et al. 2023; Shimo et al. 2019; Sarowar et al. 2007). Based on sequence similarity, multiple sequence alignment of these homologous genes was performed, showing that CAF1 protein sequences from all species shared a conserved RNase D domain and exhibited high sequence similarity, particularly in the C-terminal region (Fig. 1b). The predicted 3D structure of PtoCAF1I revealed a canonical RNase D-like α/β fold, consisting of a central β-sheet core surrounded by several α-helices (Fig. 1c). This topology is consistent with members of the DEDD family of exonucleases, suggesting a conserved catalytic mechanism underlying mRNA deadenylation.

Tissue-differential expression patterns of PtoCAF1I

To examine the tissue-specific expression of PtoCAF1I in P. tomentosa, qRT-PCR analyses was conducted using total RNA extracted from the roots, stems, and leaves of one-month-old seedlings. PtoCAF1I transcripts were detected in all examined tissues, with the highest expression observed in roots, followed by stems, and the lowest in leaves (Fig. 2). Notably, transcript levels in roots and stems were approximately 28-fold and 15-fold higher than those in leaves, respectively, indicating a strong root- and stem-preferential expression pattern. Given its predominant expression in roots and stems, PtoCAF1I was selected for further functional studies related to plant development and ECM fungal symbiosis.

Identification and analysis of transgenic poplars

To investigate the role of PtoCAF1I in plant growth, development, and ECM formation, its CDS was amplified from WT P. tomentosa and inserted into an overexpression vector under the control of the CaMV 35S promoter (Fig. 3a). Two transgenic poplar lines, designated Line 7 and Line 8, were successfully generated (Fig. 3b), as confirmed by PCR amplification of the hygromycin resistance gene following DNA extraction. Subsequently, qRT-PCR analyses confirmed that PtoCAF1I expression in these two transgenic poplar lines was significantly higher than that in WT plants (Fig. 3c), which were selected for subsequent functional analyses. Furthermore, an RNA interference (RNAi) vector was constructed, and two independent RNAi transgenic lines were successfully obtained and verified (Fig. 3d, e). qRT-PCR analyses demonstrated that the transcript levels of the target gene in both RNAi lines were reduced compared with those in WT plants (Fig. 3f).

PtoCAF1I promotes shoot branching and plant development in transgenic seedlings of Populus tomentosa

To assess the role of PtoCAF1I in P. tomentosa growth and development, morphological analyses were performed on the transgenic plants. Compared with WT plants, PtoCAF1I-overexpressing lines displayed pleiotropic phenotypes, including increased shoot height, branch numbers, biomass, number of root tips and total length of root (Fig. 4a-g). After two weeks of growth in the growth chamber, the shoot height of WT plants was 8.02 ± 0.63 cm, while that of PtoCAF1I-overexpressing seedlings of line 7 and line 8 was 12.62 ± 0.34 cm and 11.78 ± 1.0 cm, respectively (Fig. 4b). Notably, the number of lateral branches was significantly higher in PtoCAF1I-overexpressing plants (Fig. 4c). Regarding biomass, the fresh weight of transgenic plants was also significantly higher than that of WT plants, averaging 2.00 ± 0.06 g and 1.89 ± 0.16 g in overexpression lines 7 and 8, respectively, compared with 0.89 ± 0.05 g in WT plants (Fig. 4d). Correspondingly, the dry weight of transgenic plants was also significantly higher than that of WT plants, averaging 0.28 ± 0.01 g and 0.27 ± 0.02 g in the overexpression lines compared with 0.13 ± 0.01 g in WT plants (Fig. 4e). Additionally, the number of root tips in PtoCAF1I-overexpressing plants was significantly higher than that in WT plants (Fig. 4f; Supplementary Fig. S1a). Meanwhile, the total root length of overexpressing line 8 was significantly greater than that of WT plants (Fig. 4g; Supplementary Fig. S1a). Conversely, PtoCAF1I-RNAi plants exhibited a significant reduction in root tip number, and the total root length of the RNAi-PtoCAF1I-3 was markedly decreased compared with the wild type (Fig. 5f, g; Supplementary Fig. S1b). However, PtoCAF1I-RNAi plants did not show any difference in plant growth and development when compared to control (Fig. 5a–e). These results indicate that PtoCAF1I promotes not only shoot elongation and branching but also root development in P. tomentosa.

PtoCAF1I positively regulates ectomycorrhizal formation in seedlings of Populus tomentosa

To investigate the role of PtoCAF1I in ECM formation, PtoCAF1I-overexpressing and WT P. tomentosa seedlings were co-cultivated with C. geophilum mycelia. A transparent rectangular box system was employed to simulate ECM development under controlled conditions, enabling visual monitoring of symbiotic progression (Fig. 6a). At 45 days post-inoculation, both OE-PtoCAF1I-7 and OE-PtoCAF1I-8 plants exhibited significantly more ECM root tips than WT plants (Fig. 6b; Supplementary Fig. S2a). The mean number of ECM root tips reached 467 ± 47 and 421 ± 55 in the two transgenic lines, respectively, whereas WT plants showed only 264 ± 22 (Fig. 6b; Supplementary Fig. S2a). Consistently, ECM rate was markedly elevated in PtoCAF1I-overexpressing lines compared with WT at 45 days post-inoculation (Fig. 6c; Supplementary Fig. S2a). The percentage of ECM root tips was 52% in WT, whereas it reached 72% and 73% in the overexpressing lines (Fig. 6c; Supplementary Fig. S2a). In contrast, the two RNAi lines exhibited opposite trends, showing significantly fewer ECM root tips and lower ECM rate compared with WT (Fig. 6d, e; Supplementary Fig. S2b). The number of ECM root tips in RNAi plants was only 53 ± 6 and 133 ± 25, while the ECM rate was merely 21% and 36% (Fig. 6d, e; Supplementary Fig. S2b). Together, these results indicate that PtoCAF1I positively regulates ECM formation in P. tomentosa, potentially enhancing symbiotic compatibility or fungal colonization efficiency.

Discussion

In this study, we explored the functional role of PtoCAF1I, a member of the CAF1 family in P. tomentosa. Our results show that PtoCAF1I is predominantly expressed in roots and stems and its overexpression significantly enhances shoot branching and promotes overall plant growth. Notably, compared with the WT controls, PtoCAF1I-overexpressing seedlings exhibited significantly increased numbers of ECM root tips and higher colonization rates, whereas the RNAi seedlings showed the opposite trend. These findings uncover a previously uncharacterized role for a CCR4-associated factor in modulating both plant developmental processes and symbiotic interactions in a woody perennial species.

The presence of a conserved DEDDh catalytic domain within PtoCAF1I strongly suggests its involvement in mRNA deadenylation and post-transcriptional gene regulation (Wahle and Winkler 2013). As a core component of the CCR4–NOT complex, CAF1 proteins are central to the regulation of mRNA decay, thereby indirectly influencing diverse biological functions. It is plausible that PtoCAF1I regulates transcripts associated with root architecture and symbiotic compatibility. Supporting this hypothesis, transcriptomic analysis of Populus davidiana × P. bolleana plants overexpressing CAF1I has revealed significant upregulation of genes involved in the phenylpropanoid biosynthesis pathway, including leucoanthocyanidin dioxygenase, UDP-glucosyl transferase 72E1, and peroxidase superfamily genes (Wang et al. 2023). These genes have been implicated in cell wall remodeling, reactive oxygen species (ROS) homeostasis, and flavonoid-mediated signaling, all of which are essential biological processes occurring at the root-fungus interface during ECM establishment.

CAF1 family genes have been extensively studied in a variety of plant species, primarily in relation to developmental regulation and abiotic stress responses. In Populus, 19 CAF1 genes have been identified, with the expression patterns of 16 of them characterized under drought conditions (Walley et al. 2010; Wang et al. 2022). Both PtCAF1Q and PtCAF1I have been implicated in responses to salinity, osmotic stress, oxidative stress, and phytohormonal treatments (ABA, SA, MeJA), while PtCAF1I also contributes to responses to low temperatures, mechanical wounding, and pathogen exposure (Wang et al. 2022, 2023). Despite these functional annotations, our study is the first to directly implicate a CAF1 homolog in the regulation of ECM formation, thus expanding the functional repertoire of CAF1 genes beyond classical roles in stress physiology and development to include mutualistic symbioses in woody plants. Notably, although overexpression of PtoCAF1I promoted both aerial and root growth, RNAi plants mainly showed impaired root development. This difference may reflect its tissue-differential expression patterns and functional redundancy within the CAF1 family. As PtoCAF1I is predominantly expressed in roots, reducing its expression may have limited effects in aerial tissues, where other homologs may partially compensate for its loss. Given that PtoCAF1I belongs to a multigene family, future work will aim to determine whether its overexpression influences the transcriptional dynamics of other CAF1 members and to elucidate whether additional CAF1 homologs also contribute to ECM symbiosis.

The formation of ECM symbioses in woody plants is a highly regulated and complex process that involves the coordinated action of numerous plant genes and phytohormonal pathways. Several plant genes involved in ECM formation, including PtJAZ6, PtTrihelix1, PtTrihelix2, and EgAdoMetDC, have been identified as primary targets of MiSSPs derived from ECM fungi, through which they play important roles in the process of symbiotic interactions (Plett et al. 2014, 2020; Pellegrin et al. 2019; Daguerre et al. 2020; Kang et al. 2020). In addition to investigations centered on plant targets of fungal MiSSPs, recent studies have highlighted the promotive role of PtSWEET1c, a glucose transporter implicated in host sugar allocation, in facilitating ECM formation (Li et al. 2024). Additionally, fungus-induced auxin plays a central role in regulating lateral root density and elongation, thereby increasing the root surface area available for fungal colonization (Felten et al. 2009). However, most previous studies have focused on transcriptional regulators or signaling components involved in ECM establishment, whereas little is known about the roles of post-transcriptional regulatory factors, such as mRNA decay–associated proteins, in this process. Our findings provide new evidence that PtoCAF1I, a CCR4–NOT complex–associated deadenylase, contributes to ECM development in poplar, suggesting that RNA stability regulation may represent an additional layer of control during symbiotic interactions. Although our findings have objectively demonstrated the involvement of PtoCAF1I in plant growth and ECM formation, its potential connections to phytohormone signaling, root architecture, and cell wall plasticity remain unexplored.

Our study suggests that ECM formation may be accompanied by increased plant branching. The degree of axillary bud initiation is a key determinant of shoot branching patterns in plants (Hou et al. 2021). Sucrose, a mobile carbon source, not only provides energy but also acts as a signaling molecule to activate dormant buds (Mason et al. 2014). Given that ECM fungi require substantial carbon input to efficiently acquire nitrogen and maintain symbiotic homeostasis (Garcia et al. 2015), sucrose-regulating proteins may play a facilitative role in ECM development (Li et al. 2024). Together, these findings support an evidence-based hypothesis underlying our observations. To further investigate whether ECM formation benefits from increased shoot branching, future studies should focus on nutrient dynamics in relevant plant mutants.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that PtoCAF1I positively regulates shoot branching, plant growth, and ECM formation in P. tomentosa. Despite these advances, several limitations remain. While PtoCAF1I clearly contributes to ECM development, the specific downstream target genes and regulatory pathways remain to be elucidated. Future studies will focus on systematic analyses of the regulatory network involved, combining transcriptome profiling, gene co-expression analysis, and qRT-PCR validation to uncover the molecular mechanisms underpinning PtoCAF1I-mediated regulation.
