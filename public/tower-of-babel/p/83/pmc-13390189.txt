Disaster–Related Medical Geology Research: A Bibliometric Mapping of Geogenic Exposure Pathways and Earth–Health Integration (1980–2025)
Authors: Serdar Baler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMedical geology (MG) examines the interactions between geological materials, environmental processes, and human health, with particular importance in disaster‐prone regions, where rapid geological changes can abruptly intensify geogenic exposures and necessitate immediate public health responses. Geological events such as earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, landslides, and dust storms can mobilize hazardous geogenic materials, enhancing atmospheric dispersion and inhalation exposure. Although the relevance of disaster‐driven geogenic hazards is increasingly recognized, quantitative evidence of the thematic transition from mineral‐pathology to integrated exposure science remains limited. This study presents a bibliometric science‐mapping analysis of disaster‐related MG research using 64 publications indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection (1980–2025). Using keyword co‐occurrence networks, temporal trend analysis, and conceptual structure mapping, the field's intellectual organization, thematic development, and global collaboration patterns were examined. The results indicate that the literature is structured around three major thematic domains: (a) naturally occurring fibrous minerals and associated disease outcomes; (b) disaster‐triggered exposure pathways; and (c) environmental particulates and population‐level health impacts. While mineral‐specific diseases such as mesothelioma remain central, publications from 2022 to 2025 show a clear shift toward exposure‐oriented frameworks, with “exposure” emerging as the most frequent Keywords Plus term (n = 6). This transition is particularly evident in temporal overlay visualizations and multiple correspondence analysis conceptual maps. Overall, disaster‐related MG research has evolved from a predominantly disease‐centered perspective toward an integrated exposure science positioned at the interface of geosciences, atmospheric processes, and public health. These findings underscore the importance of incorporating geogenic exposure metrics into disaster risk reduction and environmental health governance.

Introduction

Medical geology (MG) is an interdisciplinary research field that investigates the interactions between natural geological materials, environmental processes, and human health (Centeno et al., 2016; Selinus et al., 2005, 2010). The field seeks to understand how geogenic hazards—such as naturally occurring fibrous minerals, mineral dust, crystalline silica, and fine particulate matter (PM)—are mobilized within the Earth system and how these materials contribute to adverse health outcomes. Respiratory diseases and malignancies linked to minerals including asbestos and erionite represent some of the most extensively documented health effects in the MG literature (Carbone et al., 2012, 2019; Emri & Demir, 2004; Hillerdal, 1999; Hodgson et al., 2005).

Geological disasters represent critical perturbations within the lithosphere–atmosphere system. Events such as earthquakes, volcanic eruptions, dust storms, and landslides can fracture mineral‐bearing formations, disturb surface deposits, and mobilize particulate matter into the atmosphere, thereby increasing inhalation exposure in affected communities (Fubini & Fenoglio, 2007; Horwell & Baxter, 2006). These disturbances may generate both acute exposure episodes and prolonged environmental persistence through secondary resuspension and post‐disaster reconstruction activities (Brook et al., 2010; Schraufnagel et al., 2019). From an Earth–health perspective, such processes constitute transient but high‐impact emission events that alter local and regional particulate flux.

Within this evolving context, MG research has expanded beyond mineral‐specific toxicity and localized disease clusters toward broader frameworks incorporating environmental exposure pathways, atmospheric transport processes, and population‐level public health impacts. Growing evidence on the health consequences of air pollution, aerosols, and particulate matter has further strengthened the integration of geogenic exposure research with environmental epidemiology, exposure science, and global health surveillance systems (Schraufnagel et al., 2019; World Health Organization [WHO], 2018). This conceptual shift reflects movement from retrospective assessments of mineral‐related disease toward forward‐looking strategies centered on risk assessment, environmental monitoring, and disaster preparedness.

Despite the increasing recognition of disaster‐driven geological exposures as Earth system health challenges, the intellectual structure and thematic evolution of this research domain have rarely been examined using systematic quantitative approaches. Existing studies often focus on specific minerals, localized exposure clusters, or individual disaster events, while comprehensive syntheses of how disaster contexts reshape research priorities remain limited (Baumann et al., 2013; Centeno et al., 2016). As a result, interdisciplinary connections between geological disturbance, atmospheric mobilization, exposure pathways, and policy‐relevant health implications are not always clearly delineated.

Recent research has increasingly emphasized post‐disaster particulate persistence, indoor radon anomalies, resuspension of asbestos‐containing debris during reconstruction, advances in mineral fiber detection, and integration of geological exposure assessment into disaster risk governance and environmental monitoring frameworks. These developments indicate a progressive reframing of disaster‐related MG within a broader Earth–health integration paradigm. However, the rapid expansion of the literature complicates efforts to identify dominant research trajectories, thematic convergence, and global collaboration patterns. Together, these emerging directions signal a transition from mineral‐centered pathology toward integrated Earth system exposure assessment frameworks.

Accordingly, the present study aims to systematically map scientific production addressing disaster‐related geogenic exposures using bibliometric science‐mapping methods. Based on articles and reviews indexed in the Web of Science Core Collection (WoSCC), the analysis examines (a) the intellectual structure of the field, (b) temporal evolution of research themes, (c) dominant thematic clusters linking geological disturbance and health outcomes, and (d) country‐level productivity and collaboration networks. By integrating quantitative bibliometric indicators with an Earth–health systems perspective, this study provides a structured assessment of how disaster‐driven geological processes are framed within environmental health research and contributes to strengthening evidence‐informed monitoring and disaster preparedness strategies (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017; Donthu et al., 2021).

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This study was designed as a descriptive bibliometric analysis aimed at revealing the intellectual structure, thematic evolution, and research trends of scientific production in disaster‐related MG. The analytical process followed established principles for systematic searching, transparent reporting, and data cleaning in bibliometric research.

The bibliometric data set was retrieved from the WoSCC, selected due to its standardized citation indexing, interdisciplinary coverage, and suitability for reproducible science‐mapping analyses. WoSCC is widely recognized as a reliable source for bibliometric investigations.

Search Strategy and Study Selection

The literature search targeted three interconnected dimensions within the MG framework: (a) geological‐origin mineral exposures, (b) inhalation‐related health effects, and (c) disaster‐triggered environmental risks.

Although secondary geogenic exposures may occur after tsunamis or floods during debris cleanup, the present search strategy specifically focused on lithospheric‐driven disasters (earthquakes, landslides, volcanic eruptions, and dust storms) that directly mobilize mineral‐bearing materials into the atmosphere. The initial search yielded 79 records. The study selection process was conducted in accordance with PRISMA 2020 guidelines for transparent reporting of literature identification and screening. Inclusion, Exclusion, and Data Cleaning

Publications classified as Proceedings Papers (n = 4), Book Chapters (n = 4), and Editorial Materials (n = 1) were excluded to ensure methodological consistency. Only English‐language publications were included; one German‐language record was excluded.

Duplicate records were removed using DOI matching and cross‐checking of Early Access versions to prevent double counting. Following screening and data cleaning, the final data set consisted of 64 publications (articles and reviews).

Conceptual Scope

The search strategy was designed to ensure high specificity and thematic coherence. While disaster‐related exposure contexts constituted the primary analytical focus, studies addressing mineral toxicity and respiratory health outcomes were retained due to their foundational role in MG research. This approach prioritized conceptual continuity and thematic consistency over assembling a quantitatively broader literature corpus.

Bibliometric Analysis

Bibliometric analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.5.2) according to the standarts (R Core Team, 2025) with the bibliometrix package (version 5.2.1) as explained in detail elsewhere (Aria & Cuccurullo, 2017). Network visualization was performed using VOSviewer (version 1.6.20) as explained elsewhere (van Eck & Waltman, 2010).

The analytical framework included: Descriptive Analysis: Annual publication trends and keyword frequency distributions were calculated to characterize overall scientific production. Trend Topic Analysis: Temporal changes in research themes were examined using Keywords Plus (ID). Overlay mapping was applied, with keywords color‐coded according to average publication year (APY) to assess temporal evolution. Conceptual Structure Analysis: Multiple Correspondence Analysis (MCA) was applied to highly cited publications to identify major conceptual clusters shaping the intellectual structure of disaster‐related MG research.

Validation of Search Strategy and Analytical Robustness

The search query was iteratively refined and pilot‐tested prior to final data extraction. Preliminary results were manually screened to ensure inclusion of foundational MG publications addressing asbestos‐, erionite‐, and disaster‐related exposure contexts. To further verify the adequacy of the search strategy, preliminary search results were manually inspected to confirm that well‐established studies within the MG literature were captured by the query. This validation step ensured that the search strategy effectively represented the core body of research related to disaster‐driven geogenic exposure pathways.

Data set integrity was verified through DOI‐based duplicate removal and Early Access cross‐checking. Analytical robustness was assessed by comparing clustering patterns across multiple bibliometric techniques. Convergent results from keyword co‐occurrence networks, overlay visualization, and MCA supported the internal consistency and methodological stability of the findings.

Narrative Synthesis of Recent Literature (2022–2025)

To contextualize recent developments, a focused narrative synthesis was conducted for publications from 2022 to 2025 addressing disaster‐related geological exposures. Topics included post‐earthquake radon anomalies, resuspension of asbestos‐containing debris, advances in the detection of naturally occurring fibrous minerals, and population‐level public health implications.

This synthesis was not intended as a systematic review but as an interpretive complement to the bibliometric mapping.

Reporting and Reproducibility

The literature identification and screening process is summarized in Figure 1. All analytical procedures were conducted using publicly available software packages. The bibliometric data set can be reproduced using the specified search query in WoSCC.

This study is based exclusively on published bibliographic data and did not involve human participants, animal subjects, or primary data collection. Ethical approval was therefore not required.

During the preparation of this manuscript, generative artificial intelligence (AI) tools were used solely to improve language, grammar, and readability. All AI‐assisted outputs were carefully reviewed, revised, and validated by the author, who takes full responsibility for the final content of the manuscript. No generative AI tools were used for data collection, data analysis, interpretation of results, or the formulation of scientific conclusions.

Results

Descriptive Characteristics of the Data Set

The final data set comprised 64 English‐language publications (articles and reviews) indexed in WoSCC (SCI‐EXPANDED, SSCI, ESCI), spanning the early 1980s to 2025 and addressing disaster‐related geogenic exposures and associated health outcomes.

Annual scientific production showed a marked acceleration after 2012, with an average annual growth rate of 5.24% (Figure 2). Early publications primarily focused on isolated mineral‐specific disease cases, whereas more recent studies increasingly address broader exposure dynamics.

Annual scientific production (1980–2025) of studies addressing disaster‐related geogenic exposures and associated health impacts (Web of Science Core Collection).

Year‐to‐year fluctuations suggest that research activity does not follow a strictly linear trajectory but appears responsive to major geological events, environmental risk awareness, and emerging public health concerns. Overall, the temporal distribution reflects a gradual transition from case‐based mineral pathology toward more systemic, exposure‐oriented research.

Notable peaks in publication output appear to align with major geological events, such as the 2011 Great East Japan Earthquake and the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquakes in Türkiye, highlighting the field's responsiveness to significant disaster occurrences.

In addition, the data set exhibited an h‐index of 22, indicating that at least 22 publications within the data set have received 22 or more citations, thereby highlighting a substantial core of influential studies shaping the intellectual development of disaster‐related MG research.

Most Frequent Keywords

Keyword frequency analysis using DE and ID reveals the dominant conceptual architecture of the field.

In DE (Figure 3a), erionite, asbestos, and mesothelioma emerged as the most frequent terms, confirming that naturally occurring fibrous minerals and malignant respiratory outcomes form the traditional core of disaster‐related MG research. Frequent co‐occurrence with lung, silica, fluoro‐edenite, and tremolite highlights the mineral‐specific focus of early investigations.

The prominence of “earthquake” suggests that geological disasters may act as contextual amplifiers of exposure. The appearance of volcanic ash, mineral dust, PM, and aerosols signals increasing attention to airborne particulate dynamics and inhalation pathways.

ID analysis (Figure 3b) broadens this perspective. The term “exposure” is the most frequent ID term (n = 17; rank = 1), while it appears only marginally in DE (n = 2; lower rank), indicating an emerging conceptual shift toward exposure‐oriented frameworks when broader indexing terms are considered. Terms such as crystal chemistry, fibers, and crystalline silica emphasize the importance of physicochemical characterization in evaluating environmental persistence and inhalation risk.

Collectively, DE and ID analyses demonstrate that disaster‐related MG research is structured around three interconnected pillars: fibrous minerals, exposure mechanisms, and respiratory health outcomes.

Discussion

Overall, the bibliometric findings indicate that disaster‐related MG research has evolved through a combination of conceptual continuity and thematic expansion. Historically, the literature has been anchored in mineral‐specific toxicity and disease outcomes, particularly those associated with naturally occurring fibrous minerals such as asbestos and erionite (Carbone et al., 2012; Emri & Demir, 2004; Selinus et al., 2010). These mineral–disease relationships established a strong toxicological and pathological core that shaped the early clinical and epidemiological foundations of the field.

However, the present analysis demonstrates a progressive reframing of the field driven by geological disasters—most notably earthquakes. Disasters transform static mineral–disease associations into dynamic exposure risks through a range of aerosolization mechanisms, including building collapse–generated dust clouds, fault rupture–induced rock fracturing, and the post‐disaster resuspension of settled mineral particles during reconstruction activities.

These processes facilitate the transfer of geogenic materials from the lithosphere to the atmosphere, enabling inhalation exposure and expanding the spatial and temporal scale of health risks. Disaster‐related processes increasingly function as dynamic connectors linking mineralogical properties, environmental mobilization, and inhalation exposure pathways.

Rather than serving merely as contextual triggers, disasters emerge as Earth‐system disturbances at the lithosphere–atmosphere interface, enhancing aerosolization, particulate transport, and the environmental persistence of hazardous materials (Fubini & Fenoglio, 2007; Horwell & Baxter, 2006). This process‐oriented interpretation situates geogenic hazards within broader environmental and atmospheric systems, expanding the analytical lens beyond static mineral–disease associations.

The year 2012 appears as a notable turning point, coinciding with a period of increasing global emphasis on disaster risk reduction under the Hyogo Framework for Action and the lead‐up to the Sendai Framework for Disaster Risk Reduction, which likely encouraged greater integration of geogenic exposure concerns into public health research (Aitsi‐Selmi et al., 2015; UNDRR, 2015; United Nations, 2005).

Temporal analyses further clarify this transition toward exposure‐oriented and population‐level frameworks. Early research emphasized generalized exposure concepts such as dust, toxicity, and volcanic ash. From the early 2000s onward, more specific mineral–disease associations—including asbestos‐related lung disease and mesothelioma—became dominant. Since 2010, increasing attention to environmental exposure, aerosols, air pollution, and population‐level outcomes reflects a growing integration of environmental health science with public health surveillance and risk assessment approaches (Brook et al., 2017; Schraufnagel et al., 2019; WHO, 2021). This trajectory indicates convergence between MG and broader Earth–health research domains.

The transformation becomes particularly evident in the most recent literature. The keyword frequency analysis of publications from 2022 to 2025 (Table 3) reveals that exposure‐oriented terminology—including exposure, mineral dust, and crystalline silica—now predominates. This shift signals movement away from a predominantly disease‐centered paradigm toward a framework grounded in exposure pathways, atmospheric processes, and systemic health risk evaluation. Concurrent emphasis on physicochemical characterization (e.g., crystal chemistry, electron diffraction) alongside exposure terminology suggests an integrated approach linking mineral properties with environmental behavior, aerosol dynamics, and inhalation risk. The conceptual thematic framework (Figure 10) synthesizes this evolution, illustrating how geological materials, disaster‐triggered exposure pathways, and population‐level health risks are increasingly conceptualized as interconnected components of an Earth–health system. This interpretation is further supported by the MCA conceptual structure analysis, which captures 38.41% of the total variance (Dim 1: 24.16%, Dim 2: 14.25%), indicating a coherent and interpretable organization of the field's thematic structure.

From a publication perspective, the influence of journals further reflects this conceptual transition. Archives of Environmental Health exhibited the highest citation impact (TC = 174), indicating that health‐oriented publication venues serve as key intellectual anchors within the field, where geogenic exposure is interpreted through a public health and risk assessment perspective. This pattern suggests that research impact is increasingly shaped by contributions positioned at the interface of environmental exposure and health outcomes.

From a public health perspective, this paradigm shift carries important operational implications. It underscores the necessity of moving beyond isolated clinical case documentation toward longitudinal monitoring of airborne particulates, environmental persistence, and cumulative inhalation exposure (Brook et al., 2010; Schraufnagel et al., 2019; WHO, 2021). Integrating MG evidence into disaster risk reduction strategies may support early warning mechanisms, standardized air quality surveillance, and post‐disaster environmental assessment protocols, particularly in regions characterized by known geogenic hazards (Centeno et al., 2016; Neira & Prüss‐Ustün, 2016). The increasing prominence of exposure‐based frameworks suggests that disaster‐driven geogenic particulates should be incorporated into routine environmental monitoring infrastructures capable of detecting aerosolized mineral fibers and fine dust fractions in vulnerable populations.

Beyond disciplinary development, the findings have broader implications for Earth–health governance. Enhanced collaboration between geoscientists, atmospheric scientists, environmental epidemiologists, and public health authorities could facilitate the development of harmonized exposure indicators and integrated monitoring strategies. Embedding geogenic hazard metrics within disaster preparedness planning and environmental health policy frameworks may strengthen resilience, preparedness, and long‐term mitigation strategies. In this context, disaster‐related MG research contributes not only to toxicological insight but also to advancing integrated Earth–health risk governance models that bridge environmental processes and population health protection.

Finally, country‐level production and collaboration analyses reveal a geographically concentrated research structure. The prominence of Italy and the United States reflects both historical exposure contexts and established research infrastructures in mineral‐related health studies (Baumann et al., 2013). However, the underrepresentation of regions with high disaster vulnerability highlights persistent inequities in research capacity and environmental surveillance systems. Addressing these disparities through interdisciplinary collaboration, capacity‐building initiatives, and expanded monitoring networks will be essential for advancing global environmental health equity and strengthening resilience to disaster‐driven geogenic exposure risks.

It is also important to interpret the size of the bibliometric corpus within the conceptual boundaries of the study. The data set identified in this analysis reflects a highly specific thematic intersection linking geogenic mineral exposure, inhalation‐related health outcomes, and disaster‐triggered environmental processes. Consequently, the final corpus represents a focused body of literature addressing disaster‐related MG rather than the broader MG domain. The relatively limited number of publications therefore reflects the emerging and specialized nature of this interdisciplinary research area rather than a limitation of the search strategy. These interpretations are supported by existing literature and should be considered as conceptual extensions rather than direct outcomes of the bibliometric analysis.

Conclusion

This bibliometric study provides a systematic mapping of disaster‐related MG research and demonstrates a clear conceptual evolution within the field. While early research was predominantly centered on mineral‐specific toxicity and disease outcomes, particularly those linked to naturally occurring fibrous minerals, recent decades reveal a progressive transition toward exposure‐oriented, environmentally contextualized, and population‐level frameworks.

The findings indicate that geological disasters are increasingly conceptualized as dynamic Earth‐system disturbances that enhance the atmospheric mobilization, transport, and persistence of geogenic particulates. In this context, disaster‐driven aerosolization processes, inhalation pathways, and environmental persistence mechanisms are becoming central analytical components of the literature. The predominance of exposure‐related terminology in recent publications (2022–2025) confirms this ongoing shift toward integrated Earth–health perspectives.

Beyond its intellectual structure, the field demonstrates growing relevance for environmental monitoring, disaster risk reduction, and public health governance. The integration of geogenic exposure assessment into standardized air quality surveillance systems and post‐disaster monitoring frameworks represents an important translational direction. Embedding geogenic hazard metrics within disaster preparedness strategies may strengthen early warning capacity, risk communication, and long‐term resilience in vulnerable regions.

At the same time, the geographically concentrated research landscape highlights the need for broader global participation. Expanding interdisciplinary collaboration, strengthening research capacity in disaster‐prone regions, and improving environmental surveillance infrastructure will be essential for advancing equitable Earth–health protection.

Overall, disaster‐related MG is evolving from a mineral‐centered toxicological field toward an integrated exposure science positioned at the intersection of geosciences, atmospheric processes, and global public health. Continued integration across disciplines and governance frameworks will be critical for addressing the complex health risks associated with disaster‐driven geogenic exposures.

Strengths and Limitations

The primary strength of this study lies in its systematic and transparent bibliometric design, which enables an integrated assessment of the intellectual structure, thematic evolution, and global research patterns of disaster‐related MG. The combined application of keyword co‐occurrence analysis, temporal overlay visualization, MCA, and a focused narrative synthesis of recent developments (2022–2025) provides a multidimensional understanding of both conceptual continuity and disciplinary transformation. By utilizing standardized citation data from the WoSCC and established analytical tools such as bibliometrix (R) and VOSviewer, the study ensures methodological rigor, reproducibility, and transparency—key elements for strengthening evidence synthesis in environmental health and Earth–health research.

An additional strength is the explicit emphasis on disaster‐triggered exposure pathways and population‐level public health implications. By situating geogenic hazards within disaster risk contexts, the study bridges mineralogical toxicology with exposure science, environmental monitoring, and health governance frameworks. This interdisciplinary positioning enhances the translational relevance of the findings, particularly for integrating geogenic exposure assessment into disaster preparedness planning, air quality surveillance, and risk mitigation strategies in vulnerable regions.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the data set was restricted to WoSCC‐indexed publications. Although WoSCC provides high‐quality and standardized citation data suitable for bibliometric mapping, relevant studies indexed exclusively in databases such as Scopus or PubMed may not have been captured. Differences in database coverage could influence the comprehensiveness of identified thematic patterns and geographic distributions.

Second, bibliometric methods are inherently quantitative and rely on proxies such as keywords, citation counts, and co‐occurrence structures. These indicators do not fully capture methodological rigor, contextual nuance, or causal inference within individual studies. Therefore, the findings reflect macro‐level research trends rather than detailed qualitative assessments of study design or evidentiary strength.

Third, the data set was limited to English‐language articles and reviews, potentially introducing language and publication‐type bias. In a globally relevant field such as MG, region‐specific research published in other languages may remain underrepresented. Furthermore, the targeted search strategy ensured conceptual specificity for disaster‐related contexts but may have excluded complementary themes within the broader MG domain.

Finally, while the temporal analysis highlights emerging shifts up to 2025, the field continues to evolve rapidly. Future publications may further refine the integration of atmospheric exposure science, Earth‐system processes, and health governance frameworks.

Despite these limitations, the study's transparent methodology and reproducible analytical workflow strengthen its contribution to interdisciplinary Earth–health research. Future bibliometric investigations could expand the scope through multi‐database integration, multilingual inclusion, and hybrid quantitative–qualitative designs to deepen understanding of how geogenic hazards intersect with disaster resilience and global public health systems.
