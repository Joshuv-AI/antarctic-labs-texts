Mechanisms of HIF1A-mediated immune evasion in gastric cancer and the impact on therapy resistance
Authors: Hao Qi, Xiaoyu Ma, Yu Ma, Liuyu Jia, Kuncong Liu, Honghu Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe high prevalence and detrimental effects on patient outcomes make gastric cancer (GC) a significant health issue that persists internationally. Existing treatment modalities exhibit limited efficacy, prompting the exploration of immune checkpoint inhibitors as a novel therapeutic approach. However, resistance to immunotherapy poses a significant challenge in GC management, necessitating a profound grasp of the intrinsic molecular pathways.MethodsThis study focuses on investigating the immunosuppressive mechanisms of quiescent cancer cells (QCCs) in GC, particularly their resistance to T-cell-mediated immune responses. Utilizing mouse models, gene editing techniques, and transcriptome sequencing, we aim to elucidate the interactions between QCCs, immune cells, and key regulatory factors like HIF1A. Functional enrichment analysis will further underscore the role of glycolysis-related genes in mediating immunosuppression by QCCs.ResultsThe cancer cells that survived GC treated with T-cell therapy lost their proliferative ability. QCCs, as the main resistance force to immunotherapy, exhibit stronger resistance to CD8+ T-cell attack and possess higher cancer-initiating potential. Single-cell sequencing analysis revealed that the microenvironment in the QCCs region harbors more M2-type tumor-associated macrophages and fewer T cells. This microenvironment in the QCCs region leads to the downregulation of T-cell immune activation and alters macrophage metabolic function. Transcriptome sequencing of QCCs identified upregulated genes related to chemo-resistance, hypoxia, and glycolysis. In vitro cell experiments illustrated that HIF1A promotes the transcription of glycolysis-related genes, and silencing HIF1A in QCCs enhances T-cell proliferation and activation in co-culture systems, induces apoptosis in QCCs, and increases QCCs' sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors. In vivo, animal experiments showed that silencing HIF1A in QCCs can inhibit GC growth and metastasis.ConclusionUnraveling the molecular mechanisms by which QCCs resist T-cell-mediated immune responses through immunosuppression holds promising implications for refining treatment strategies and enhancing patient outcomes in GC. By delineating these intricate interactions, this study contributes crucial insights into precision medicine and improved therapeutic outcomes in GC management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s10565-024-09917-x.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Quiescent cancer cells, Single-cell sequencing, Immune-suppressive microenvironment, T cells, HIF1A, Glycolysis, Drug resistance

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) is a commonly occurring malignant tumor worldwide, ranking at the top not only in terms of incidence but also posing a significant threat to patients' health (Sung et al. 2021; Norwood et al. 2022; Alsina et al. 2023). However, traditional treatment methods have limited effectiveness in treating GC (Jiang et al. 2022a, b; Xu et al. 2022). In recent years, immune checkpoint inhibitors have garnered attention and application as a new therapeutic strategy (Albertelli et al. 2021; Cen et al. 2021). By activating the patient's immune system, immune checkpoint inhibitors inhibit the growth and spread of tumor cells, achieving certain clinical efficacy (Yi et al. 2022; Pu and Ji 2022; Passaro et al. 2022). However, challenges persist in the utilization of immune checkpoint inhibitors for treating GC, especially the issue of drug resistance (Cen et al. 2021; Guo et al. 2023). Currently, understanding the molecular pathways associated with drug resistance in patients diagnosed with GC remains an elusive goal. Therefore, revealing the mechanisms of drug resistance is crucial for overcoming resistance and improving the effectiveness of immunotherapy (Chen et al. 2021; Baxter et al. 2021).

Quiescent cancer cells (QCCs) refer to non-proliferating cells in a dormant state within the G0 phase, marked by decreased Ki67 expression and increased p27 levels (Lindell et al. 2023). The presence of QCCs has been detected in multiple cancer types, among which lung cancer stands out (Sharma et al. 2010), glioblastoma (Chen et al. 2012), and breast cancer (Malladi et al. 2016). QCCs are implicated in cancer recurrence, as under favorable conditions, they have the potential to re-enter a proliferative state (Yano et al. 2014, 2017). Extensive literature suggests that QCCs display a resilience towards drug interventions (Francescangeli et al. 2020; Li et al. 2021a). Therefore, it is imperative to grasp the traits of QCCs, elucidate the mechanisms that oversee the transition from cancer cell proliferation to dormancy, and devise new methodologies for eradicating QCCs within solid tumors (Ewton et al. 2011; Menegakis et al. 2021). Tumor immunotherapy encounters a considerable hurdle with the existence of QCCs, as they can form immune-deprived clusters, exhibit increased levels of chemotherapy resistance and stemness genes, and create an immunosuppressive microenvironment through regulating hypoxia, leading to T cell dysfunction (Baldominos et al. 2022).

To uncover the molecular mechanisms of drug resistance, the investigation focuses on understanding the resistance of QCCs within GC towards immunotherapy. We hypothesize that immunosuppressive conditions generated by QCCs enable resistance to T-cell attacks, leading to resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors. To achieve this goal, we use mouse models, gene editing techniques, and other methods to study the immunosuppressive mechanisms of QCCs. In addition, we deeply investigate the interactions between QCCs and immune cells, as well as the portrayal of glycolysis-related genes and the role of HIF1A through techniques such as transcriptome sequencing and functional enrichment analysis (Backman et al. 2021; Theocharidis et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2022).

The expected results of this study will help reveal the resistance of QCCs to T-cell attacks, uncover the cancer-initiating potential of QCCs, and elucidate the interactions between QCCs and immune cells. These findings will provide important theoretical and experimental evidence for deciphering the mechanisms of immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance, guiding the advancement of fresh treatment tactics, and improving the prognosis of GC patients (Llovet et al. 2022; Yi et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2022). Uncovering the molecular mechanisms by which QCCs in GC resist T-cell attacks by creating an immunosuppressive environment carries significant scientific and clinical implications, focusing on precise treatment approaches for GC to elevate patient survival rates (Li et al. 2023).

In conclusion, this research endeavors to unveil the molecular mechanisms responsible for the evasion of T-cell attacks by QCCs in GC by creating an immunosuppressive environment, resulting in resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Through in-depth research on this mechanism, we will provide theoretical and experimental evidence for the exact handling of GC and the improvement of patient survival rates, making significant contributions to the establishment of pioneering management strategies and the prognosis of GC patients.

Materials and methods

Cell cultivation and handling

Culturing the MFC mouse GC cell line (catalog number: MZ-0120, Ningbo Mingzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was performed in RPMI-1640 medium (catalog number: 11875093, Gibco, USA) enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) to ensure optimal cell growth. Through lentiviral vector-mediated transduction, the cells were engineered to consistently produce a green fluorescent protein (GFP) and a fluorescent marker, as well as a specific T cell receptor (TCR) targeting GFP in CD8+ T cells (source: HuiZhi Biological Technology Co., Ltd., Suzhou, Jiangsu, China) (Baldominos et al. 2022). The transduced cells were maintained in a T cell growth medium supplemented with IL-2 (catalog number: A1048501, Gibco, USA) to sustain their activity and proliferation (Baldominos et al. 2022).

Mouse CD8+ T cells lacking programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) were generated utilizing CRISPR/Cas9 technology. The PD1-sgRNA: 5'-CAGCTTGTCCAACTGGTCGGAGG-3' (PAM: NGG) was integrated into the Lenti-CRISPR v2 vector housing the Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 nuclease gene (source: Hanheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai). Application of lentiviral Lenti-CRISPR v2 vector resulted in cell transduction, and the PD1-knockout CD8+ T cells were achieved through the CRISPR/Cas9 editing system. The cells transfected with sgRNA plasmids and donor sequences were selected employing 4 μg/mL puromycin (catalog number: HY-K1057, MCE, USA). The PD1 knockout in CD8+ T cells was authenticated by Western blot analysis (Fig. S1) (Zeng et al. 2023; Rahimi et al. 2019).

Western blot

Extraction of cellular total protein was conducted by employing RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C, Bioteke, Shanghai, China) containing PMSF. Upon ice incubation lasting 30 min, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C and 8000g for 10 min to accumulate the supernatant fluid. The total protein content was measured with the BCA assay kit (Catalog No: 23227, ThermoFisher, USA). After being dissolved with 2 × SDS loading buffer, the protein sample of 50 μg was boiled for 5 min and then underwent electrophoresis on an SDS-PAGE gel. To transfer the proteins, a PVDF membrane was employed, followed by blocking with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature. Next, primary antibodies PD1 (1:1500, NBP1-77276, Novusbio) and GAPDH (1:1000, NB100-56875, Novusbio) were diluted and incubated with the membrane overnight at 4 °C. The membrane was rinsed with TBST on three occasions, each wash lasting 10 min, followed by a 1-h incubation with the HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG H&L secondary antibody (ab97051, 1:2000 dilution, Abcam, based in Cambridge, UK). Post TBST rinsing steps, the membrane was situated on a clear glass panel. A suitable amount of Solution A and Solution B from the ECL fluorescence testing kit (Catalog No: abs920, Abiotech (Shanghai) Biotechnology Co., Ltd, Shanghai, China) were blended in the dark and dropped onto the membrane. The membrane was then photographed through the Bio-Rad imaging system (BIO-RAD, USA). Analysis was carried out using Quantity One v4.6.2 software, where the protein content was indicated by the ratio of grayscale values between the protein band and the GAPDH band. Iterated thrice, the experiment yielded the average results (Liu et al. 2019).

Experimental animals

Wild-type BALB/c mice and nude mice (catalog numbers 211, 401, obtained from Beijing Weitonglihua Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd.), at the age of 6 weeks, were accommodated in an SPF-level animal facility where the humidity levels were kept at 60–65% and the temperature maintained within the range of 22–25 °C. After acclimatization for one week, experiments were initiated with a prior health assessment of the mice. This experimental protocol and animal handling procedures were authorized by the Animal Ethics Committee of the First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University.

PD1−/− CD8+ T cell cytotoxicity experiment

MFC GC cell lines expressing GFP and red fluorescent protein (mCherry) were mixed in a 1:1 ratio. Approximately 1 × 106 GC cells were subsequently administered subcutaneously into the right abdominal region of the mice. Within 7 days after tumor establishment, PD1−/− CD8+ T cells were intravenously injected via the tail vein, with approximately 2 × 107 T cells per mouse. After 3 weeks, the tumor tissue was harvested, and the cytotoxicity of T cells against cancer cells was quantitatively assessed through flow cytometry. Additionally, the infiltration of T cells in tumor tissue was assessed through immunofluorescence experiments (Baldominos et al. 2022).

Immunofluorescence

Treatment of tumor tissue sections involved deparaffinization and hydration steps. The tissue samples were subjected to antigen retrieval in sodium citrate buffer for 5 min, followed by an hour-long exposure to 5% goat serum to block non-specific antibody binding. Application of the primary antibody was succeeded by an overnight incubation at 4°C, followed by exposure to the secondary antibody for 1 h at room temperature in a light-protected setting. The slices were treated with a nuclear marker, DAPI, for 5 min under light protection and analyzed utilizing an Olympus BX51 fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corp, Tokyo, Japan). The following antibodies were used: rabbit anti-CD3 (1:100, ab231775, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CD4 (1:50, ab288724, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), CD8 (1:200, NBP1-49045, novusbio), CD45 (1:200, #70257, CST), Ki67 (1:200, ab15580, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), GFP (1:500, ab183734, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and the secondary antibodies: Goat anti-rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor 488) (1:500, ab150077, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), Goat anti-rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor 405) (1:800, ab175652, Abcam, Cambridge, UK).

The assessment of tumor hypoxia involved administering Pimonidazole (75mg/kg body weight) via intraperitoneal injection 30–60 min before euthanasia, followed by staining using Hypoxyprobe® (Catalog number: H10498, Invitrogen, USA) as directed by the manufacturer's guidelines (Ko et al. 2020).

Flow cytometry

After collecting tumor tissue, it was minced and relocated into a 10 mL tube. Then, a solution consisting of 5 mL of digesting mixture (400U/mL Collagenase IV, 6.8U/mL Hyaluronidase, 20μg/mL DNase I, and 10% FBS in HBSS) was introduced, and the samples underwent an incubation process utilizing a rotator set at 37 degrees Celsius for a period of 20 min. Once digested, the specimens underwent filtration via a 100 μm cell strainer to remove impurities. The single-cell suspension was blocked with 1:100 diluted TruStain FcX (#101319 Biolegend) in flow buffer (2 mM EDTA, 0.1% BSA, PBS). Finally, the samples were analyzed using a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman) based on the fluorescence emitted by the fluorescent proteins, and FlowJo v.10 software was employed for data analysis purposes (Baldominos et al. 2022).

Assessment of cancer cell proliferation capacity

To evaluate the in vivo proliferation of cancer cells, mice received intraperitoneal injections of EdU at a dose of 10 mg/Kg daily for the final 5–8 days of tumor development. As described above, tumor tissue was collected and subjected to immunofluorescence staining. By adhering to the directions stipulated by the manufacturer, the immunofluorescence BCK-EdU647 staining kit (BaseClick, Sigma) was used to stain EdU in the tumor tissue, allowing for the assessment of cancer cell proliferation (Baldominos et al. 2022).

Contribution of QCCs in PD1−/− CD8+ T cell therapy

To investigate the involvement of QCCs in PD1−/− CD8+ T cell therapy, we constructed MFC tumor cells labeled with mVenus-p27K as a quiescent reporter. Immunofluorescence staining of mVenus-p27K_High cells was performed with Ki67 to validate their proliferative status. To further confirm the cell cycle arrest of mVenus-p27K_High cells, EdU experiments were conducted. Additionally, QCCs were marked by introducing tdTomato-p27K into GFP+ and miRFP670+ GC cells. Subcutaneous injection of a mixture of GFP+ and miRFP670+ labeled GC cells was performed in mice, with miRFP670 (far-red) used as a non-immunogenic control. Immunofluorescence and flow cytometry techniques were utilized to evaluate the cytotoxic impact of PD1−/− CD8+ T cell therapy on GFP+ tumor cells, as well as the changes in QCCs before and after treatment in GFP+ and miRFP670+ tumor cells, and the relative proportion of QCCs in the two tumor cell types (Baldominos et al. 2022).

Tumor initiation assay

To assess the tumor-initiating potential of QCCs, mCherry + mVenus-p27K_High (QCCs) and mCherry + mVenus-p27K_Neg cancer cells were sorted from MFC tumor tissue using FACS and then transplanted into the right abdominal areas of BALB/c mice (1000 cells). The tumor-initiating potential was evaluated, and T cell infiltration in tumor tissue was assessed using immunofluorescence experiments. The antibodies that were utilized are listed below: rabbit anti-CD3 (1:100, ab231775, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-CD4 (1:50, ab288724, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), anti-CD8 (1:200, NBP1-49045, novusbio), anti-CD45 (1:200, #70257, CST), goat anti-rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor 488) (1:500, ab150077, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and goat anti-rabbit IgG (Alexa Fluor 405) (1:800, ab175652, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) (Baldominos et al. 2022).

Single-cell sequencing of intragroup and intergroup regions of QCCs

The samples included in the sequencing comprised two regions of tumor tissue: p27K_High (n = 2) and p27K_Neg (n = 2). Initially, the tumor tissue from these regions was washed in cold PBS. Subsequently, the tissue was digested using 1mg/mL of collagenase (Sigma-Aldrich, USA, catalog number: C2674) and exposed to 37 °C for a time span of 10 min. Then, the tissue was incubated with trypsin/EDTA (Gibco, USA, catalog number: 25200072) at 37 °C for 5 min to generate a single-cell suspension. The single-cell suspension was captured using the C1 single-cell auto-preparation system (Fluidigm, Inc., South San Francisco, CA, USA). Once captured, the cells underwent lysis within the chip, releasing mRNA, which was subsequently reverse transcribed, generating cDNA. The cDNA, post-lysis and reverse transcription underwent pre-amplification in microfluidic chips for subsequent sequencing. The amplified cDNA was used to construct libraries for single-cell sequencing utilizing the HiSeq 4000 Illumina platform (parameters: paired-end reads, read length of 2 × 75 bp, approximately 20,000 reads per cell) (Keefe et al. 2023).

The Seurat software package was used for scRNA-seq data analysis. Firstly, data quality control was performed based on the criteria of nFeature_RNA > 500 & nCount_RNA > 3000 & nCount_RNA < 20,000 and percent.mt < 5. Subsequently, the LogNormalize function was applied for normalization. Next, applying the RunPCA function allowed for the execution of Principal Component Analysis (PCA) on a subset of 2000 genes known for their significant variability, and important principal components (PCs) were identified for UMAP clustering analysis through the utilization of the JackStrawPlot and ElbowPlot functions. Utilization of the FindAllMarkers function facilitated the identification of distinct marker genes associated with each cell cluster, while cell classification was achieved through consultation of the CellMarker database. Finally, scatter plots and violin plots were produced to visually represent the expression of marker genes across various cell clusters using the FeaturePlot and VlnPlot functions (Zhang et al. 2019).

Transcriptome sequencing of QCCs

RNA-seq was performed on QCCs (mVenus-p27K_High; n = 6) and non-quiescent tumor cells (mVenus-p27K_Neg; n = 6). Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (15596026 Invitrogen, Car, Cal, USA), and analysis of the RNA samples' concentration and purity involved the use of a Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (1011U, Nanodrop, USA). Total RNA samples that fulfilled the specified conditions were employed in the ensuing assays: RNA Integrity Number (RIN) ≥ 7.0 and 28S:18S ratio ≥ 1.5.

The generation and sequencing of libraries were carried out at CapitalBio Technology, located in Beijing, China. 5 μg of RNA was allocated for each sample. Briefly, Ribo-Zero™ Magnetic Kit (MRZE706, Epicentre Technologies, Madison, Wisconsin, USA) was used to remove ribosomal RNA (rRNA) from the total RNA. Utilization of NEB Next Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit (#E7775, NEB, USA) enabled the creation of libraries for Illumina sequencing. The RNA fragments were initially fragmented into around 300 base pair (bp) long segments using the first-strand synthesis reaction buffer (5 ×) by NEB Next. The reverse transcriptase primer and random primers were utilized for the synthesis of the first cDNA strand, whereas the second cDNA strand was synthesized in the reaction buffer containing dUTP Mix (10 ×). The cDNA fragments underwent end-repair, which entailed adding poly-A tails as well as sequencing adaptors. After the successful ligation of the Illumina sequencing adaptors, a strand-specific library was generated by digesting the second cDNA strand with USER Enzyme (#M5508, NEB, USA). The library DNA was amplified, purified, and enriched by PCR. The assessment of the library involved the application of Agilent 2100 and quantification was achieved using the KAPA Library Quantification Kit (KK4844, KAPA Biosystems). Ultimately, the NextSeqCN500 (Illumina) sequencer was utilized for paired-end sequencing.

Quality assessment of the sequencing data's raw paired-end reads was executed using FastQC software version 0.11.8. The preprocessing of unprocessed data was performed using Cutadapt software v1.18, which entailed the elimination of Illumina sequencing adapters and poly(A) tails. Perl script was used to discard reads with more than 5% N content. The extraction operation was carried out through the utilization of FASTX Toolkit software 0.0.13, focusing on reads with a base quality greater than 20 and comprising 70% of the total bases. The paired-end sequences underwent repair with the aid of BBMap software. Finally, the hisat2 software (0.7.12) was applied to align the filtered high-quality read fragments with the reference genome (Landolt et al. 2016).

Selection of differentially expressed genes (DEGs)

The analysis of gene differential expression on sequencing data was executed through the "limma" package in R software. The false discovery rate (FDR) approach was utilized to correct the diverse p-values. The threshold for selecting DEGs was set as FDR < 0.05 and |logFC|> 1 to identify significantly DEGs. Utilization of the "pheatmap" and "ggplot2" packages in R facilitated the generation of the heat map and volcano plot, respectively. The comparison between the two groups of data was analyzed using the Wilcox. test. The analyses conducted in this research were carried out utilizing R version 4.2.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing) (Wu et al. 2022).

GO and KEGG functional enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analysis was performed on DEGs via the ClusterProfiler package in R language. The enrichment outcomes from KEGG were visualized using the gglot2 package. Differential gene enrichment was analyzed to identify significantly enriched cellular functions and signaling pathways at a critical level of P less than 0.05 (Yuan et al. 2018).

Gene selection

Leveraging the outcomes of differential analysis from transcriptome sequencing data, the protein produced by the top 100 genes showcasing upregulation was submitted to the String database (https://string-db.org) to investigate protein–protein interaction (PPI). The exportation of the PPI network and its corresponding node files facilitated the generation of a graphical representation showcasing node statistics via R software. Selected as the central genes in the network, those genes exhibiting the greatest number of connections were subject to detailed analysis (Liu et al. 2020).

RT-qPCR

TRIzol (15596026, ThermoFisher, USA) was employed to extract total RNA from cells, with subsequent measurement of RNA concentration and purity performed using the Nanodrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher, USA). The mRNA was converted to cDNA via reverse transcription with the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (Takara Code: RR047A, Takara, Japan) as directed by the manufacturer's guidelines and recommendations. TaKaRa produced gene-specific primers through synthesis (Table S1). The real-time fluorescence quantitative PCR procedure, performed through the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR system (4351106, ThermoFisher, USA), was executed under defined reaction conditions: pre-denaturation at 95 °C for 10 min, denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, annealing at 60 °C for 20 s, extension at 72 °C for 34 s, for a total of 40 cycles. The relative transcription levels of the target gene were calculated using the comparative Ct method (2−ΔΔCt method), with GAPDH as the reference gene. ΔΔCt was calculated as ΔCt experimental group—ΔCt control group, where ΔCt = Ct (target gene)—Ct (reference gene). The relative transcription levels of the target gene mRNA were expressed as 2−ΔΔCt (Sun et al. 2020). The experiment was reiterated three times in total.

Construction of Lentiviral Vectors

Acquisition of the lentiviral interference vector pSIH1-H1-copGFP (sh-, interference vector, catalog: SI501A-1, System Biosciences, USA) facilitated the construction of a lentivirus-based HIF1A gene interference vector. The lentiviral particles carrying the vector were introduced into HEK-293T cells (catalog number: iCell-h237, Sibang Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) using the lentivirus packaging kit (catalog number: A35684CN, Invitrogen, USA). The lentivirus, with a titer of 1 × 108 TU/ml, was extracted from the cell supernatant after 48 h. The sh-NC sequence was AGGCTACAATGATCAGACTAAT, while the sh-HIF1A-1 sequence was GCCACTTTGAATCAAAGAAAT, and the sh-HIF1A-2 sequence was GCCGCTCAATTTATGAATATT (Jiang et al. 2017).

Dual-Luciferase reporter assay

To investigate the impact of HIF1A on the transcriptional activity of ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 promoters, sh-NC and sh-HIF1A were separately co-transfected into human embryonic kidney HEK293T cells with dual-luciferase reporter gene vectors containing the promoter sequences of ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2, along with their mutant binding sites. Lipofectamine 2000 reagent (Catalog Number: 11668019, Thermo Fisher, USA) was used for co-transfection. The cells were harvested and lysed after 48 h, followed by the assessment of luciferase reporter gene expression employing a luciferase assay kit (K801-200, BioVision, USA). The detection of the luciferase reporter genes was performed using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (Promega, Madison, WI, USA). Employing Renilla luciferase as the internal reference gene, the comparison of the activation levels of the target reporter genes was achieved by dividing the relative light units (RLUs) of firefly luciferase by the RLUs of Renilla luciferase (Taniue et al. 2016).

ChIP experiment

Assessing the enrichment of HIF1A in the promoter sequences of ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 genes was accomplished through the ChIP assay. The ChIP analysis was executed employing the ChIP Kit (KT101-02) provided by Sai Cheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. in Guangzhou, China. The following steps were carried out: initially, cells were cultured until they reached 70–80% confluency, followed by fixation with 1% formaldehyde at ambient temperature for 10 min to cross-link DNA and proteins within the cells. After cross-linking was completed, DNA fragmentation in cells was achieved through sonication to create random DNA fragments of suitable sizes. Sonication was performed for 10 s with a 10-s pause, repeated 15 times. Subsequent to centrifugation at 13,000 rpm at a temperature of 4 °C, the resulting supernatant was segregated into two tubes. One tube was treated with a rabbit IgG negative control antibody (ab172730, 1:100 dilution, Abcam, UK), while the other tube was incubated with a rabbit-specific antibody specific to the target protein HIF1A (1:100 dilution, Catalog #36169, CST), both maintained at 4 °C for an overnight period. Utilizing Protein Agarose/Sepharose beads, we precipitated the DNA–protein complexes, then performed a brief centrifugation to separate the supernatant and wash away any nonspecific complexes. Later on, the complexes were de-crosslinked at 65 °C for an extended period, and the DNA sections were cleansed through phenol/chloroform extraction for further analysis by qPCR (Table S2) to detect the promoter regions of the ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 genes (Nelson et al. 2006).

Co-culture of QCCs with T cells

CFSE (Catalog Number: C34554, ThermoFisher, USA) was diluted in a 1:1000 ratio and carefully blended with the T cell mixture. CFSE-labeled T cells underwent co-culture with QCCs in which HIF1A had been silenced. In the co-culture system, 10μg/mL of the immune checkpoint inhibitor anti-PD1 (ab214421, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) was added, with anti-IgG (ab172730, Abcam, Cambridge, UK) as a control. Flow cytometry was employed to measure CFSE density in each group (Zha et al. 2022).

T-cell proliferation and activity assay

T-cells were collected and labeled with a quantitative fluorescent antibody against CD8_FITC (NBP1-49045F, novusbio) to identify T-cell subgroups. Cells were then fixed and permeabilized before staining with antibodies against Ki-67_PE (NB500-170PE, novusbio) and IFN-γ_PerCP (FAB485C, novusbio). Lastly, the samples were subjected to analysis with a BD FACS Canto II flow cytometer (BD Immunocytometry Systems) to assess T-cell proliferation and activity.

Flow cytometry is used to detect cell apoptosis

The cells were amassed in a flow cytometry tube, subjected to centrifugation, and the liquid above them was removed. Three rounds of cold PBS washes were performed on the cells, with subsequent discarding of the supernatant post-centrifugation. The preparation of the Annexin-V-FITC/PI staining solution involved mixing Annexin-V-FITC, PI, and HEPES buffer in a 1:2:50 ratio, as instructed by the Annexin-V-FITC Cell Apoptosis Detection Kit (C1065, Biyun Biotechnology Company, Shanghai, China). Upon resuspension in 100 μL of staining solution, each 1 × 106 cells underwent gentle mixing and subsequent incubation at room temperature for 15 min. Then, 1 mL of HEPES buffer was added and mixed gently. Fluorescence was measured with a flow cytometer to assess cell apoptosis. The excitation wavelength was set at 488 nm, while detection wavelengths of 525 nm for FITC and 620 nm for PI fluorescence were employed in the evaluation process. The experiment was independently repeated three times. Apoptosis Index (AI): AI = (number of apoptotic cells) / (number of apoptotic cells multiplied by the number of normal cells) (Zhu et al. 2017).

Construction of an In vivo GC model

QCCs were prepared in cell suspension with a density of 2 × 107/mL for sh-NC and sh-HIF1A groups, respectively. Into the left axilla of BALB/c nude mice, 0.2 mL of the cell suspension was injected subcutaneously to establish the subcutaneous xenograft model of GC. Six nude mice per group were used, and after inoculation, the mice were relocated to a SPF animal housing facility. The progression of the tumor was monitored, and observations were documented at intervals of 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, and 24 after inoculation. On day 25, mice in each group were euthanized via cervical dislocation, followed by excision and weighing of the tumor tissues.

In addition, a volume of 0.2 mL of QCCs-lucifer cell suspension was administered into the gastric wall of BALB/c mice using a 1 mL syringe to establish the GC orthotopic tumor model. Tumor size was monitored using bioluminescence imaging. After 4 weeks, cervical dislocation was performed to euthanize the mice in every group, and inguinal lymph nodes were collected to observe lymph node metastasis. Tumor tissues were processed into single-cell suspensions, and T cell content was analyzed by flow cytometry (Miao et al. 2023).

Immunohistochemistry

GC in situ tumor tissue was immersed in a 10% formalin solution for fixation and subjected to two rounds of deparaffinization using xylene for 10 min each time. Hydration was performed using a gradient of ethanol concentrations (100%, 95%, 75%, 50%), followed by 10-min incubation at room temperature. After adding 0.01mol/L citrate buffer, microwave retrieval was carried out for 20 min, and then 1 drop of 5% goat serum was added. Incubation at room temperature was continued for 5 min, followed by removal of the serum. Incubation at 4 °C overnight with rabbit anti-CD8 primary antibody (NBP2-29475, novusbio) commenced. Incubation at 37 °C for 1 h was conducted, followed by the addition of biotinylated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:500, ab150077, Abcam, USA) for a 30-min incubation at the same temperature. Then, a freshly prepared DAB chromogenic solution (Catalog No: DA1015, Tianjin Solabao Technology Co., Ltd, China) was added, and the staining process lasted for 1–2 min. Counterstaining was done with hematoxylin (Catalog No: G1080, Tianjin Solabao Technology Co., Ltd, China) for 1 min, followed by dehydration and transparency using a neutral mounting medium. Five representative high-power fields were chosen at random for observation under an optical microscope (Zong et al. 2021).

Statistical analysis

The data for this study were analyzed using SPSS software (version 21.0, IBM, United States). Mean ± SD was used to represent quantitative data. Two data sets were compared using the Unpaired Student's t-test, assuming a normal distribution. Employing one-way ANOVA, a comparative analysis of multiple groups was conducted, with Tukey's post hoc test utilized for subsequent analysis.

Results

Investigating the resistance mechanisms of GC cells to T cell attack

GC stands out as a highly prevalent form of cancer across the globe. In recent years, significant progress has been made in the fields of chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy (Guan et al. 2023). However, despite the potential shown in treatment, immune checkpoint inhibitors still face challenges in high recurrence rates for most patients, as they do not provide a fundamental solution. Therefore, it is urgent to uncover the mechanisms of resistance to develop more effective targeted treatment strategies.

The primary cause of immunotherapy's ineffectiveness lies in cancer cells' resistance to T cells (Vesely et al. 2022). To address this problem, we first used GFP as a visible neoantigen for GC cells. Then, CD8+ T cells were modified to express specific T cell receptors that can selectively recognize and attack GFP-labeled GC cells. At the same time, PD1, an immune checkpoint on CD8+ T cells, was knocked out to simulate the therapeutic effect of immune checkpoint inhibitors. The focus of this study was on exploring the resistance of cancer cells expressing targetable antigens to T cell killing (Fig. 1A). In a mouse model, a mixture of GFP+ and mCherry + GC cells was injected subcutaneously. Since the modified CD8+ T cells could not recognize mCherry, mCherry + cells served as labeled controls in the same environment. After treatment with PD1−/− CD8+ T cells, it was observed (Fig. 1B-D) that most GFP+ tumor cells were effectively killed, but a small fraction of GFP+ tumor cells survived and formed clusters. Compared with the mCherry + region, on Day 14, there was a slight decrease in the T cell infiltration level within the clusters of GFP+ tumor cells. However, by Day 21, the extent of T cell infiltration within the GFP+ tumor cell clusters had decreased by nearly twofold (Fig. 1E). This suggests that the surviving GFP+ tumor cell clusters can limit T cell infiltration, which may be a mechanism of immune therapy resistance.

To determine whether these surviving GFP+ tumor cell clusters are in a cell cycle arrest phase, we treated tumor-bearing mice with EdU and then performed adoptive therapy with PD1−/− CD8+ T cells. Following a 6-day treatment regimen, most mCherry + tumor cells displayed EdU-positive characteristics, whereas a lower level of EdU signal was observed in GFP+ tumor cells (Fig. 1F-G). This indicates that most GFP+ tumor cells did not proliferate before and after adoptive T cell therapy. Therefore, we consider the GC cells escaping attack by CD8+ T cells as QCC clusters. Thus, we speculate that surviving GFP+ tumor cells in GC tissue may resist T cell attack through certain pathways, thereby promoting resistance to GC immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Enhanced resistance of QCCs to CD8+ T cell attack under immune checkpoint inhibition

To further investigate the resistance of QCCs to CD8+ T cell attack under immune checkpoint suppression, we first verified the expression of mVenus-p27K in MFC tumors using a static reporter. Immunofluorescence results revealed that all mVenus-p27K_High cells were negative for Ki67, indicating cell cycle arrest (Fig. 2A). Additionally, EdU treatment in vivo showed that mVenus-p27K_High cells were negative for EdU, confirming their cell cycle arrest state (Fig. 2B). These findings collectively demonstrate that mVenus-p27K_High cells are QCCs.

QCCs exhibit enhanced tumor initiation potential in an immune-restricted microenvironment

To investigate the ability of untreated tumors to reintegrate QCCs into the cellular cycle, we isolated mCherry + mVenus-p27K_High (QCCs) and mCherry + mVenus-p27K_Neg cancer cells from MFC tumor tissue (Fig. 3A). The RT-qPCR results indicated that the mVenus-p27K_High cells were Ki67-negative, while the mVenus-p27K_Neg cells were Ki67-positive (Fig. 3B). The flow cytometry results indicate (Fig. 3C) that cells expressing mVenus-p27K_Neg are more prone to undergoing apoptosis compared to those expressing mVenus-p27K_High. We then subcutaneously injected these cells into mice and found that the mVenus-p27K_High cells regrew tumors, demonstrating that they were not in a static terminal state (Fig. 3D-E). Furthermore, the mVenus-p27K_High cells exhibited a stronger tumor-initiating potential compared to the mVenus-p27K_Neg cells, suggesting that QCCs not only reentered the cellular cycle and commenced tumor expansion but also accomplished this more effectively than proliferative cancer cells.

Immunofluorescence staining of mVenus-p27K-expressing tumor tissue in untreated mice revealed that T-cell infiltration was mainly concentrated in the mVenus-p27K_Neg area, while it was minimal in the mVenus-p27K_High area (Fig. 3F-G). Staining for CD4 and CD8 validated the sparse presence of these T-cell types in QCC clusters (Fig. 3H). Additionally, analysis of anti-CD45 staining (Fig. 3I) showed that immune infiltration was poorer in the QCCs region compared to the cell proliferation area. The outcomes imply the presence of QCCs in a microenvironment where there is a decrease in the immune cell infiltration.

In conclusion, this study indicates that QCCs may possess stronger cancer-initiating potential through the formation of an immune-restricted microenvironment, leading to immune evasion.

scRNA-seq reveals heterogeneity and immune suppression in QCCs

Drawing on the previously mentioned research, we proceeded to examine the immune infiltrating cells in the intra-cluster and extra-cluster regions of QCCs within the same tumor tissue using scRNA-seq analysis, aiming to reveal the heterogeneity of immune cells in different regions and their specific roles in the formation of the immunosuppressive environment (Fig. 4A).

Firstly, the sequenced samples included two regions of tumor tissue, p27K_High and p27K_Neg. The R package "Seurat" was utilized for scRNA-seq data analysis. Upon quality assurance and standardization, the correlation coefficient between nCount and percent.mt was calculated as r = -0.01, and the correlation coefficient between nCount and nFeature was calculated as r = 0.88 (Fig. S2A), indicating the good quality of the filtered cells. Next, the RunPCA function was applied to perform PCA dimension reduction on the top 2000 highly variable genes (Fig. S2B). The results showed no obvious batch effects between the tissue samples (Fig. S2C). The JackStrawPlot function was used for visualizing the top 40 PCs (Fig. S2D). Here, we present the main constituent genes of the first four PCs (Fig. S2E-F).

Subsequently, UMAP analysis was conducted to explore the heterogeneity of cells in p27K_High and p27K_Neg regions. The results revealed that all cells could be classified into 16 cell clusters (Fig. 4B). Annotation of the cell clusters using known cell marker genes identified seven cell types (Fig. 4C-D): T cells, Macrophages, Fibroblasts, B cells, Monocytes, Endothelial cells, and Granulocytes. These seven cell types were present in both intra- and extra-cluster regions of QCCs. Notably, there were fewer T cells in the p27K_High region, consistent with our previous experimental results. Furthermore, the p27K_High region exhibited a higher abundance of Macrophages, including two subtypes of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), M1 and M2. Verification using marker genes for macrophages revealed that M2 macrophage marker genes (Arg1, Cd163, Il10) were highly expressed, while M1 macrophage marker genes (Cd86, Nos2, Tnf) showed no significant expression (Fig. 4E-F), indicating a predominant M2-like phenotype of the Macrophages. This further contributes to the immunosuppressive environment in the QCCs region.

Differential gene expression analysis in T cells and macrophages in the QCC region

In order to elucidate why the rare T cells in the QCCs region fail to kill QCCs, an in-depth analysis was carried out on the gene expression profiles of T cells and Macrophages within and outside the QCCs region. It was discovered that when contrasted with the p27K_Neg region, the T cells in the p27K_High region demonstrated significant downregulation of 148 genes and upregulation of 186 genes (Fig. 5A). Likewise, the Macrophages exhibited notable downregulation of 1136 genes and upregulation of 1518 genes (Fig. 5B). Through functional enrichment analysis of the DEGs, it was found that the downregulated genes in T cells were predominantly concentrated in functions associated with immune cell activation and differentiation, such as T cell activation, differentiation, and B cell differentiation (Fig. 5C), suggesting that various immune functions were downregulated in T cells within the QCC microenvironment. Conversely, the downregulated genes in Macrophages were mainly enriched in functions linked to the regulation of mRNA and protein metabolism (Fig. 5D), which are instrumental in modulating the immune status of the tumor microenvironment (TME). Altered expression of these genes may influence the immunosurveillance function of TAMs on tumor cells and promote immune evasion by the tumor.

Transcriptomic analysis reveals resistance mechanisms of QCCs

To delve deeper into the mechanism of drug resistance in QCCs, we utilized RNA-seq analysis on QCCs (mVenus-p27K_High) and non-quiescent tumor cells (mVenus-p27K_Neg) (Fig. 6A). Differential analysis results (Fig. S3A) indicated that in QCCs, cell cycle-related genes were significantly downregulated (Fig. 6B, Fig. S3A), validating the quiescent state characteristics of these cell populations. Meanwhile, chemotherapy resistance-related genes were significantly upregulated (Fig. 6C, Fig. S3B), suggesting a higher resistance potential of QCCs during treatment. Additionally, hypoxia and glycolysis-related genes were significantly upregulated (Fig. 6D, Fig. S3C, D), implying the presence of a hypoxic environment in the QCC region and the dependence of these cells on glycolysis as the main energy source.

Furthermore, to gain deep insights into the pathways and roles of the DEGs, we conducted KEGG and GO enrichment analysis. The results revealed that in QCCs, downregulated genes were primarily involved in cell cycle-related pathways and functions, while upregulated genes were mainly associated with glycolysis and hypoxia-related pathways (Fig. 6E-H). HIF1A promotes GC immune suppression through inducing glycolysis

To identify key genes that regulate the biological functions of QCCs, we imported the proteins corresponding to the top 100 upregulated genes in QCCs into the String database and constructed a PPI network (Fig. 7A). We found that the hypoxia-inducible factor HIF1A occupied a central position in the network, followed by the glycolysis-related protein ALDOA (Fig. 7B). To confirm the hypoxic microenvironment in the QCC area, we conducted immunofluorescence staining for Pimonidazole and CD3 levels in tumor tissue of the QCC region. Pimonidazole was used to mark the hypoxic regions of the tumor tissue. We observed that the Pimonidazole-positive areas co-localized with the QCC clusters and showed a negative correlation with the T cell-enriched regions (Fig. 7C). This indicates that the QCC area is under a hypoxic microenvironment, leading to inhibited T cell infiltration.

HIF1A, as a major transcription factor in the hypoxic response, can activate downstream genes in related pathways (Bao and Wong, 2021). Through online prediction using the Jaspar database (https://jaspar.elixir.no/), we identified binding sites of HIF1A in the promoter regions of glycolysis-related genes ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 (Table S3). To verify the regulatory relationship between HIF1A and ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2, we first silenced HIF1A in QCCs. The RT-qPCR data revealed a notable decline in HIF1A expression in QCCs after HIF1A knockdown, with better silencing effect observed with sh-HIF1A-1 (Fig. 7D). A substantial decrease was detected in the expression levels of ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 upon HIF1A silencing (Fig. 7E). Dual-luciferase reporter assays illustrated a prominent drop in the promoter activity of ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 after HIF1A silencing, while their mutated counterparts showed no significant changes (Fig. 7F-G). ChIP experiments further revealed a significant reduction in HIF1A enrichment on the promoter regions of ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 after HIF1A silencing (Fig. 7H). These findings indicate that HIF1A promotes the transcription of ALDOA, LDHA, and HK2 by binding to their promoter regions.

Given the discoveries outlined above, it is hypothesized that HIF1A may contribute to the formation of an immunosuppressive environment by promoting the transcription of glycolysis-related genes, thereby resisting T cells and promoting resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors in GC.

Effects of HIF1A silencing on t cell activity and sensitivity to immune checkpoint inhibitors in QCCs

To further investigate the influence of HIF1A expression in QCCs on T cell activity in the context of immune checkpoint inhibitor treatment, we silenced HIF1A in QCCs and co-cultured them with T cells. Additionally, we introduced the immune checkpoint inhibitor anti-PD1 into the co-culture system (Fig. 8A). Flow cytometry data exhibited a substantial upsurge in apoptosis levels in silenced HIF1A QCCs. Importantly, the effect of anti-PD1 in the co-culture system became more pronounced after HIF1A silencing (Fig. 8B). Moreover, the proliferation capacity of T cells (Fig. 8C-D) and their activity (Fig. 8E) were significantly enhanced after HIF1A silencing in the co-culture system. These findings suggest that silencing HIF1A in QCCs promotes T cell proliferation and activation, as well as apoptosis in QCCs, and enhances the sensitivity of QCCs to immune checkpoint inhibitor drugs.

Silencing HIF1A suppresses gc growth and metastasis

Finally, the effects of HIF1A on the immune microenvironment, tumor proliferation, and metastasis in GC were explored by creating subcutaneous and orthotopic tumor models in nude mice (Fig. 9A). The silencing of HIF1A in QCCs notably hindered the proliferation of subcutaneous and orthotopic tumors in nude mice, as evidenced by a significant decrease in tumor volume and weight after HIF1A silencing (Fig. 9B-D), as well as a reduction in fluorescent signal intensity in orthotopic tumors (Fig. 9E). Additionally, we found a decrease in lymph node metastasis of GC after silencing HIF1A (Fig. 9F), further supporting the inhibitory effect of HIF1A silencing on tumor growth and lymph node metastasis.

Moreover, flow cytometry analysis of T cell markers in GC orthotopic tumor tissues revealed that HIF1A silencing led to a notable rise in T cell infiltration and a significant upregulation of CD8 expression (Fig. 9G). In summary, the silencing of HIF1A may inhibit immune evasion, thereby suppressing GC growth and lymph node metastasis, potentially by fostering T cell proliferation in the TME.

Discussion

GC is a major contributor to cancer-related mortality globally and often develops resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (Li et al. 2021b; Tabernero et al., 2021; Kang et al. 2022). However, the emergence of immune checkpoint inhibitors has brought new hope for the treatment of GC (Joshi and Badgwell 2021; Janjigian et al. 2021a, b). Therefore, it is crucial to explore the mechanisms of immune therapy resistance in GC and to find new treatment strategies (Malfertheiner et al. 2023; Liu et al. 2022; Zhong et al. 2022). The primary focus of this investigation is to elucidate the function of QCCs and HIF1A in the formation of an immune-inhibitory environment, offering a more profound insight into the resistance mechanisms of immunotherapy in GC and guiding the design of novel treatment strategies.

Previous studies have uncovered mechanisms of immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance, such as an increase in myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) and TAMs, and impaired T-cell function (Salewski et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2022). The TME presents a significant obstacle to the infiltration of T lymphocytes, thereby inhibiting their functionality (Verma et al. 2022). The TME comprises various cell types and cytokines that participate in tumor progression, potentially leading to dysfunction of T cells (Xia et al., 2019). Within the TME, tumor cells and immunosuppressive cells produce highly active soluble oxygen and toxic metabolites that hinder T cell responses (Maimela et al. 2018). MDSCs are essential for tumor progression by promoting immune suppression activities (Ostrand-Rosenberg 2010), while TAMs in the TME often display characteristics similar to M2 phenotype, contributing to immune suppression and tumor development (Cassetta et al. 2016). Cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) are acknowledged as the primary stromal cell population in the TME, secreting immune regulatory factors and participating in the suppression of T cell immunity (Zhen et al. 2017). Precisely defining the relative contributions of these potential factors within the TME to T cell functionality and differentiation poses a challenging task. This study distinguishes itself from previous research by uncovering QCCs and their crucial role in resistance formation. Through an in-depth investigation of QCCs and HIF1A, our comprehension regarding the mechanisms contributing to the establishment of an immune-inhibitory environment has been expanded.

QCCs, as one of the main drivers of immune therapy resistance, possess the following characteristics: firstly, QCCs have lower proliferation capacity compared to other types of cancer cells (Baldominos et al. 2022; Francescangeli et al. 2023). This makes them less susceptible to immune attack (Baldominos et al. 2022; Francescangeli et al. 2023). Secondly, QCCs exhibit stronger immune evasion ability, enabling them to rapidly adapt to immune pressure and reorganize their morphology, thereby weakening T-cell attacks against them (Francescangeli et al. 2023). This suggests that the presence of QCCs alters the surrounding immune environment and contributes to the formation of an immune-inhibitory environment (Francescangeli et al. 2023). Additionally, the region-specific microenvironment of QCCs influences T-cell immune activation and macrophage metabolic functions, further complicating treatment (Lu et al. 2023). In our analysis, it was observed that apart from the decrease in T cell numbers in the QCCs regional microenvironment, there was a significant increase in the nember of macrophages, particularly with a marked increase in M2 macrophages. Studies have shown that the enrichment of TAMs can promote the immune escape of tumors (Varricchi et al. 2019; Li et al. 2024). NK cells may also be deceived by cunning tumor cells. Research by the Massague laboratory demonstrated that following treatment with the WNT inhibitor DKK1, the drastic decrease in ULBP (a ligand binding to surface receptors on NK cells and NK1.1 + cells) levels led to tumor cells entering a state of dormancy to evade attack by NK cells (Malladi et al. 2016). The extracellular matrix (ECM) plays a pivotal role in the TME, with CAFs being the primary source of ECM production in the TME. Through various mechanisms such as abnormal secretion of collagen, fibronectin, and ECM-degrading enzymes, generation of vascular endothelial growth factor, as well as production of inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, CAFs influence the progression and drug resistance of tumors (LeBleu and Kalluri 2018). Liu et al. discovered that CAFs can enhance the tumorigenicity of quiescent cancer stem cells through the Notch3-LSD1 signaling pathway (Liu et al. 2018). Additionally, Pilar Baldominos et al. found an enrichment of inhibitory-type CAFs in the QCCs regional microenvironment (Baldominos et al. 2022). Therefore, this study has primarily focused on exploring T cells, and in the future, we will attempt to investigate the vital roles other immune cells play in the QCCs regional microenvironment. Furthermore, this study concentrates on the immediate interactions between tumor cells and immune cells, while the long-term therapeutic effects and potential side effects remain unclear.

Grounded on the exhaustive examinations, we can provisionally ascertain (Fig. 10) that high expression of HIF1A in QCCs can establish an immune-suppressive environment by promoting the transcription of glycolysis-related genes, thereby resisting T cell attacks and facilitating GC immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance. This study elucidates the contribution of QCCs in shaping the immune-suppressive environment and highlights the central role of HIF1A in this process. The presence of hypoxia in most solid tumors serves as a distinctive feature of the TME, correlating with cancer progression and metastasis (Chaturvedi et al. 2014; Rankin and Giaccia 2016). Abundant evidence from mouse models indicates that hypoxia facilitates tumor evasion of immune surveillance and immunotherapy (Lukashev et al. 2006; Townsend et al. 2013; Zheng et al. 2019; Jayaprakash et al. 2018). Moreover, there is an increasing amount of clinical evidence pointing to the importance of tumor hypoxia in hampering immune cell penetration into tumors and hindering the efficacy of ICB treatment (Gide et al. 2019). Research findings reveal that in triple-negative breast cancer patients, hypoxia induces HIF1α-dependent epigenetic regulation, leading to immune effector dysfunction and resistance to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy (Ma et al. 2022). Additionally, targeting HIF-1α can eliminate immune evasion mediated by PD-L1 in the TME while promoting tissue tolerance (Bailey et al. 2022). Moreover, by promoting the transcription of glycolysis-related genes, HIF1A may represent one of the mechanisms for creating an immune-suppressive environment to resist T cell attacks (Liu et al. 2024), offering new clues for further exploring the biological functions of QCCs. These findings deepen our understanding of the TME and immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance mechanisms, potentially providing new insights for GC treatment strategies. While this study highlights the role of HIF1A, the regulation of the TME is a complex interplay of multiple factors and signaling pathways, likely involving various cell types and molecules. Subsequent investigations should systematically and comprehensively explore the distribution characteristics and correlations of different cell populations within the tumor tissue.

Molecular mechanism of immune checkpoint inhibitor resistance in GC promoted by QCCs forming an immune-suppressive microenvironment

This study primarily utilized a murine model to elucidate the roles of QCCs and HIF1A in the formation of an immunosuppressive environment in GC. Nonetheless, significant differences exist between animal models and human clinical settings, presenting challenges to the translation of research findings. Firstly, this study was solely based on murine models and cell experiments. Laboratory-derived cell lines, due to their loss of polarity, do not serve as optimal models for studying QCCs. Discrepancies in the composition and function of the immune systems between mice and humans may result in differing immune responses and drug efficacy. Additionally, variations in TME and metabolic pathways across different species could impact the mechanisms of drug action and treatment outcomes. Despite the crucial value of murine models in studying tumor biology and treatment strategies, further preclinical research is essential to validate the feasibility and effectiveness of these findings in future studies. Moreover, patient heterogeneity and individual differences in clinical trials must be accounted for in the translation process to ensure the reliability and effectiveness of research results in clinical applications. It is imperative to emphasize the collection and analysis of clinical samples to validate the importance of QCCs and HIF1A in human GC and explore the potential use of this information in predicting responses and resistance to immunosuppressive therapies. Furthermore, while this study specifically elucidated the role of HIF1A in forming an immunosuppressive environment, we also identified other DEGs in the sequencing data, such as ALDOA. Literature reports have linked ALDOA expression to disease progression and prognosis in lung cancer, as well as its close association with TME immune infiltration (Lu et al. 2021), highlighting the pivotal role of ALDOA in glycolysis as a prognostic marker for cancer and the extent of macrophage and T cell immune infiltration (Tian et al. 2022). Further research is warranted to investigate whether DEGs and pathways are involved in the regulation of QCCs.

The findings of this study underscore the importance of considering patient stratification and personalized treatment in GC immunotherapy. Due to significant biological and genetic differences among patients, personalized treatment strategies can enhance therapeutic efficacy and reduce adverse reactions. Through genomic analysis and biomarker detection, patients with a higher probability of positive response to specific treatments can be identified, enabling the development of more precise treatment plans. Additionally, understanding the expression levels of QCCs and HIF1A in different patient populations can guide personalized treatment, optimize treatment strategies, and improve patients' clinical outcomes. To overcome immunotherapy resistance, this study reveals the crucial roles of QCCs and HIF1A in the immunosuppressive environment of GC. However, a single treatment approach may not be sufficient to completely overcome resistance, making the exploration of emerging therapies and combination treatment methods crucial.

Recent studies suggest that targeting metabolic pathways with drugs, such as glycolysis inhibitors, may overcome immune suppression in the TME by weakening the metabolic adaptability of tumor cells. Combining this treatment strategy with immune checkpoint inhibitors may significantly enhance treatment effectiveness. Furthermore, other approaches like metabolic reprogramming agents, immune metabolism modulators, and even personalized immunotherapy strategies show promise in overcoming immune resistance. Additionally, cell therapy (such as CAR-T cell therapy) in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, or co-administration with HIF1A inhibitors, may provide a more comprehensive treatment approach by simultaneously boosting the attacking power of immune cells and weakening tumor cell defense mechanisms. Future research should further explore the practical outcomes of these combination therapies, particularly through clinical trials, to validate their effectiveness in overcoming GC immunoresistance. These emerging treatment methods offer new hope for improving the success rate of immunotherapy and the survival rates of patients.
