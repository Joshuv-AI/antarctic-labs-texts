Elucidation of DPP‐4 involvement in systemic distribution and renal reabsorption of linagliptin by PBPK modeling with a cluster Gauss–Newton method
Authors: Ryo Nakamura, Takashi Yoshikado, Yasunori Aoki, Yuichi Sugiyama, Koji Chiba
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP‐4) inhibitor linagliptin (LNG) exhibits target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) in clinical settings, characterized by saturable binding to plasma soluble DPP‐4 (sDPP‐4) and tissue transmembrane DPP‐4 (tDPP‐4). Previous studies have indicated that saturable renal reabsorption of LNG contributes to its nonlinear urinary excretion observed in humans and wild‐type mice, but not in Dpp‐4 knockout mice. To elucidate the mechanisms underlying these complex phenomena, including DPP‐4‐related renal reabsorption of LNG, we employed physiologically‐based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling combined with a cluster Gauss–Newton method (CGNM). The CGNM facilitated the exploration of parameters in rat and human PBPK models for LNG and the determination of parameter identifiability. Through PBPK–CGNM analysis using reported autoradiography data ([14C]‐LNG) in wild‐type and Dpp‐4‐deficient rats, DPP‐4‐specific distributions of LNG in various tissues were clearly differentiated from nonspecific parts. By fitting to human plasma concentrations and urinary and fecal excretions of LNG after intravenous and oral administrations, multiple unknown PBPK parameters were simultaneously estimated by the CGNM. Notably, the amount of tDPP‐4 and the reabsorption clearance for LNG–DPP‐4 complexes were identifiable, indicating their critical role in explaining the complex nonlinear pharmacokinetics of LNG. Compared with previous PBPK analyses, the CGNM allowed us to incorporate greater model complexity (e.g., consideration of tDPP‐4 expressions and in vitro binding kinetics), ultimately resulting in a more accurate reproduction of LNG's TMDD. In conclusion, by considering LNG as a high‐affinity probe for DPP‐4, comprehensive PBPK–CGNM analyses suggested a dynamic whole‐body distribution of DPP‐4, including its involvement in the renal reabsorption of LNG.

INTRODUCTION

A DPP‐4 inhibitor, linagliptin (LNG), is extensively utilized in clinical scenarios for managing type 2 diabetes. 1 , 2 LNG exhibits a heightened binding affinity for DPP‐4 compared with other DPP‐4 inhibitors 3 and demonstrates target‐mediated drug disposition (TMDD) due to its saturable binding to DPP‐4. Consequently, it displays nonlinear pharmacokinetics, as evidenced by the plasma concentration and urinary excretion profiles in humans. 4 , 5 Renal clearance calculated from the plasma unbound LNG concentrations (CLR,u) markedly increases at higher doses. Notably, nonlinear urinary excretion is also observed in wild‐type mice, with the fraction of urinary LNG excretion increasing dose‐dependently. This phenomenon is absent in Dpp‐4 knockout mice. 6

Dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 (DPP‐4), also known as CD26, serves as the target of DPP‐4 inhibitors. 7 DPP‐4 exists in two forms: soluble DPP‐4 in plasma (sDPP‐4) and transmembrane DPP‐4 in tissues/organs (tDPP‐4). 8 , 9 The predominant form is tDPP‐4, with widespread whole‐body expression, particularly high expression on the brush border membrane of renal proximal tubules. 10 DPP‐4 functions enzymatically by cleaving various peptides, such as glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) and glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP). Additionally, DPP‐4 exhibits non‐enzymatic activities, including signal transduction related to inflammation and fibrosis. 11 Moreover, DPP‐4 possesses a distinctive peptide sequence on its transmembrane domain that potentially facilitates translocation across cell membranes, 12 a feature supporting its uptake and recycling via endocytosis in Caco‐2 cells. 13

In previous research, the potential presence of a DPP‐4‐related renal reabsorption mechanism of LNG was demonstrated through a series of PBPK model analyses using human clinical trial data, which included nonlinear plasma and urinary pharmacokinetics. 5 However, the mathematical complexity of the PBPK model made it challenging to rule out the possibility of finding only a “local minimum” due to initial parameter dependencies, which could reproduce the observations superficially. Overcoming the limitations of the PBPK model analyses could verify the presence of an appropriate balance in the model structure while emphasizing the plausibility of the DPP‐4‐related renal reabsorption mechanism.

Cluster Gauss–Newton method (CGNM) 14 is a novel parameter estimation algorithm enabling the optimization from multiple initial parameter combinations simultaneously. This approach is particularly advantageous in avoiding the identification of only a parameter combination that contingently reproduces observations (i.e., a local minimum) in overparameterized models like the PBPK model. Additionally, the CGNM facilitates the rapid approximation of profile likelihood, aiding in the assessment of parameter identifiability. 15 Recent studies have reported the application of PBPK–CGNM analyses to complex pharmacokinetic phenomena involving numerous unknown parameters, such as TMDD and an endogenous transporter substrate. 16 , 17 , 18

In this study, we developed novel rat and refined human PBPK models of LNG incorporating plasma sDPP‐4 and tissue tDPP‐4. The study by Sarashina et al. 5 had limitations in terms of the reproducibility of plasma concentration profiles and in the mechanistic description of the PBPK model (e.g., minimal consideration of tDPP‐4 expression in tissues and a significant discrepancy between the estimated K d values and in vitro data). Breaking through these limitations was the primary objective of the present study. While the expression pattern of DPP‐4 throughout the human body remains uncertain, efforts were made to mitigate uncertainties in the PBPK model by assuming similarity to the expression pattern estimated from [14C]‐LNG autoradiography data in rats. Comprehensive PBPK–CGNM analyses of human clinical trial data, comprising nonlinear plasma and urinary profiles of LNG, which could be regarded as a high‐affinity probe for DPP‐4, clarified the involvement of DPP‐4 in the renal reabsorption of LNG, along with its reabsorption clearance and whole‐body amounts of sDPP‐4 and tDPP‐4 in humans.

METHODS

Evaluation of LNG's nonlinear pharmacokinetics

Prior to conducting PBPK model analyses, a non‐compartmental analysis of plasma LNG concentration in humans was performed to elucidate the relationship between nonlinear pharmacokinetics and saturable plasma protein binding. This analysis involved calculating the dose‐normalized unbound AUC (AUCu/Dose) and unbound renal clearance (CLR,u/Dose) based on the unbound plasma concentration profile of LNG, derived from plasma concentration profiles 5 and the dose‐dependent fraction unbound. 6 Further details regarding the consideration of unbound concentration are provided in the Data S1.

Overview of the PBPK model construction

PBPK models for rats and humans were developed to characterize autoradiography data and plasma concentration–time profiles in rats, as well as the human clinical trial data. Additional information (data sets, comparison of model assumptions, and analyzed data between the previous 5 and present studies) is also available in the Data S1.

Parameter estimation by the CGNM

Parameter estimation was conducted utilizing the R implementation of CGNM version 0.6.5 (https://cran.r‐project.org/web/packages/CGNM/index.html) on R 4.3.2. This approach helps to avoid excessive optimization toward mathematical optima, preventing deviations of estimated parameters from physiologically plausible values.

Evaluation of parameter estimates obtained by the CGNM

After running the CGNM, parameter combinations that better reproduced observed data were selected using a combination of a χ2‐test and an Elbow method based on SSR (or SSR').

To visualize the distribution of parameter estimates, violin plots were employed to illustrate distributions of initial and estimated parameters. Parameter identifiability was confirmed through the use of the profile likelihood 15 plot, which displays the −2 log‐likelihood against each parameter value. In this study, a parameter was deemed identifiable when it visually exhibited minima in the profile likelihood plot.

RESULTS

As outlined in the Methods section, our analyses comprised two primary components. First is the evaluation of DPP‐4‐dependent and ‐independent tissue distribution of LNG, estimating the amount of tDPP4 and the K p values in tissues, through the PBPK–CGNM analysis of the rat autoradiography data. Second is the human PBPK–CGNM analysis utilizing the estimated tDPP‐4 amount and the K p values to elucidate parameter combinations explaining unique human TMDD data of LNG, including its nonlinear urinary excretion. Consequently, we successfully estimated essential parameters, including total amounts of tDPP‐4 and sDPP‐4 (AtDPP4 and AsDPP4, respectively) and DPP‐4‐related renal reabsorption clearance (CLreab,DPP4) in humans, which should be associated with in vivo amount of DPP‐4 expression and clearance of DPP‐4 internalization.

Non‐compartmental analysis for the human clinical trial data of LNG

Prior to the PBPK model analyses, the relationship between saturable protein binding and nonlinear pharmacokinetics in humans was examined. The dose‐normalized AUCu (AUCu/Dose) for 0.5, 2.5, 5, and 10 mg oral doses were calculated to be 5.44, 5.41, 7.41, and 6.67 (10−3 nmol*h/L/mg), respectively (Table S6). Subsequently, CLR,u were calculated to be 0.510, 3.28, 2.96, and 3.46 (104 L/h), respectively (Table S6).

The estimated parameters are summarized in Table 1. The estimated parameter combinations well reproduced the tissue concentration–time profiles (Figure 1c) and the dose‐normalized tissue concentrations (Figure 1d) of LNG in both wild‐type and Dpp‐4‐deficient rats. Fecal and urinary excretion profiles, which were added to describe the mass balance, were also roughly reproduced (Figure 1b).

Comparison of the amounts of DPP‐4 between rats and humans

Species differences in DPP‐4 amounts were evaluated by comparing estimates from PBPK–CGNM analyses of rat plasma concentration data (Table 1) and human clinical trial data (Table 2). The amounts of DPP‐4 in rats and humans were normalized by body weight, which was assumed to be 0.25 and 70 kg, respectively. The total amount of Dpp‐4 (ADPP4) estimated directly from the analysis of the rat plasma concentration data was subsequently divided into the AsDPP4 and the AtDPP4 using the fraction of expression estimated from the analysis of the autoradiography data. As shown in Table S7, the species differences of AsDPP4 and AtDPP4 between rats and humans were 3.55 and 1.69‐fold, respectively.

Confirmation of the necessity of the DPP‐4‐related reabsorption of LNG and sensitivity analyses of the key parameters reflecting the human data

To elucidate the indispensability of DPP‐4‐associated renal reabsorption clearance (CLreab,DPP4) of the LNG‐DPP4 complex, a sequence of rat‐to‐human analyses was conducted under two supplementary scenarios: one involving a model where CLreab,DPP4 was constrained to zero, and the other where renal reabsorption clearance was solely applied to unbound LNG (CLreab,u). The outcomes revealed that neither the model with CLreab,DPP4 fixed at zero (Figure S4b) nor with CLreab,u (Figure S4c) adequately described the observed urinary excretion profiles, while our novel PBPK model incorporating CLreab,DPP4 has successfully described the observed nonlinear urinary excretion profiles (Figure S4a).

Next, to assess the impact on the primary mechanisms influencing plasma concentration and urinary/fecal excretion profiles, including nonlinearity, sensitivity analyses were conducted for the parameters estimated by the PBPK–CGNM analysis of humans. Using the parameter set with the lowest SSR as a reference, simulations were performed by varying five essential parameters estimated in this study (AtDPP4, AsDPP4, Kd, koff, CLreab,DPP4) by factors of 0.1 and 10. The results are presented in Figure S5. The fold changes of these results are described in the Supplementary Text AX4 in Data S1. Among them, AsDPP4 and K d had a significant impact on plasma concentration, AtDPP4 had a considerable effect on urinary excretion, and AtDPP4 also strongly influenced fecal excretion.

DISCUSSION

This study undertook a comprehensive investigation employing PBPK–CGNM analyses of TMDD of LNG in both rats and humans. The primary objective was to elucidate the expression levels of DPP‐4 and the DPP‐4‐related renal reabsorption. The human PBPK–CGNM analyses supported by the information from rat PBPK–CGNM analyses regarding whole‐body DPP‐4 distribution, and the penalized (middle‐out) parameter approach based on multiple in vitro experiments, we obtained 193 parameter combinations describing the nonlinear plasma concentrations, renal and fecal excretions of LNG. The distribution and profile likelihood of parameter estimates revealed that 14 out of 16 parameters were identifiable (Figure 4), including AsDPP4, AtDPP4 and CLreab,DPP4, deemed essential for capturing the complicated TMDD phenomena of LNG. As a result, this led to a significant improvement in the reproducibility of clinical data compared with the analysis by Sarashina et al. 5 (Figure 5).

Non‐compartmental analysis elucidated the relationship between nonlinear pharmacokinetics and saturable protein binding of LNG. While AUCu displayed linearity, CLR,u exhibited dose‐dependent escalation (Table S6), suggesting that the nonlinearity of plasma LNG concentration could be attributed to the saturable binding of plasma sDPP‐4. Conversely, nonlinear urinary excretion would be mainly attributed to the renal tDPP‐4 rather than plasma sDPP‐4, underscoring the crucial role of renal tDPP‐4 in this phenomenon. Notably, the nonlinear urinary excretion of LNG is also observed in wild‐type mice, but not in Dpp‐4 knockout mice 6 . These findings suggest that renal tDPP‐4 would play a key role in the nonlinearity of urinary LNG excretion.

The rat PBPK–CGNM analysis using the autoradiography ([14C]‐LNG) data for blood and 7–9 tissues/organs, elucidated 25 unknown parameters (Table S3). Among them, the AsDPP4 in plasma, the AtDPP4 and the K p values in several tissues were identifiable (Figure S1). As shown in Figure 1d, in the wild‐type rats, the distribution into tissues is primarily mediated by Dpp‐4 at low doses, which becomes saturated at higher doses, as the dose‐normalized tissue concentration approaches the values observed in the Dpp‐4‐deficient rats (i.e., it becomes mainly explained by nonspecific binding). Such dose‐dependent phenomena due to the saturation of tDPP‐4 should have supported the identifiability of the AtDPP4 and the K p values in tissues. The tissue LNG concentration–time profiles (Figure 1c) also indicated different characteristics in the wild‐type and Dpp‐4‐deficient rats, although the difference was not as clear as the dose‐dependent data. Consequently, the profile likelihood of the Dpp‐4 amount in reproductive organs (AtDPP4reprod) lacking dose‐dependent data, showed a slightly indistinct minimum compared with the AtDPP4 in other tissues (Figure S1). After analyzing autoradiography data, the analyses of plasma concentration–time profiles successfully estimated ADPP4 which was optimized to reproduce the nonlinear plasma concentration–time profiles in wild‐type and Dpp‐4‐deficient rats. The amounts of tDPP‐4 and sDPP‐4 (Table S7) were posteriorly calculated and compared with those in humans later.

The human PBPK–CGNM analyses successfully identified 14 parameters including the AsDPP4, AtDPP4, and CLreab,DPP4 that should be the determinant of the whole‐body DPP‐4 distributions and the renal tDPP‐4 dynamics in humans, as shown in the profile likelihood plots (Figure 4b). The median values for estimates of AsDPP4 and AtDPP4 were 31.0 nmol and 2.61 × 103 nmol, respectively (Table 2). By using AsDPP4 and AtDPP4, we posteriorly calculated the concentration of sDPP‐4 in the plasma and the density of tDPP‐4 on the renal tubule (4.29 and 516 nmol/L kidney, respectively). Those obtained values were within 1.5‐fold range of our previous report, 5 suggesting consistency between our previous and present analyses.

Notably, as shown in Table S7, a comparison of the estimated DPP‐4 amounts normalized by body weight in rats and humans (63.1 and 37.4 nmol/kg for AtDPP4, 0.125 and 0.444 nmol/kg for AsDPP4) demonstrated some inter‐species differences within 1.69‐ and 3.55‐fold, respectively. Given the pivotal role of DPP‐4 in modulating various peptide hormones including incretins, it is unsurprising that there exists variation across species. To clarify these inter‐species differences, further investigations integrating quantitative proteomics and an exploration of the variances in DPP‐4 functionality among different species are imperative.

The median value for the estimates of CLreab,DPP4 was 0.0228 L/h. Upon comparison with the CLreab,DPP4 estimated in our previous study, the findings exhibited remarkable similarity, falling within a narrow range of 1.4‐fold difference. It is plausible that our previous study might have found “one of the best” parameter combinations that could reproduce the clinical data. In our present study, an exhaustive exploration conducted via CGNM yielded a multitude of parameter combinations, encompassing those akin to our previous findings, 5 thereby affirming that the identification of the “one of the best” parameter set was not serendipitous but rather a discernible outcome. The concurrence of findings between our previous and present studies further strengthened the credibility of our observations suggesting DPP‐4‐mediated renal reabsorption. Given the abundant expression of DPP‐4 on the brush border membrane of the renal proximal tubule 19 and its reported internalization via endocytosis in Caco‐2 cells, 13 it can be postulated that these transmembrane DPP‐4 form complexes with drugs, facilitating their translocation across the membrane. However, elucidating the specific mechanism underlying DPP‐4‐related reabsorption necessitates further investigation, including in vitro experimentation.

Multiple PBPK–CGNM analyses with varying model structures underscored the indispensability of considering DPP‐4‐related renal reabsorption for accurately reproducing the nonlinearity of urinary excretion. Each analysis was conducted within the same series of PBPK–CGNM analyses for rats and humans, wherein the assumption regarding the mechanism of renal reabsorption was altered. As depicted in Figure S4, an analysis where CLreab,DPP4 was held constant at zero failed to capture the nonlinear pattern of urinary excretion, indicative of the necessity for a non‐zero value of CLreab,DPP4. Furthermore, an analysis incorporating CLreab,u at the renal tubule also proved unsuccessful in reproducing nonlinear urinary excretion, thereby suggesting the improbability of the involvement of transporters typically responsible for the transport of unbound drugs. Although LNG has been identified as a substrate for P‐gp and OCT2, 20 no other transporters implicated in renal reabsorption have been reported. Moreover, the absence of nonlinearity of renal excretions in DPP‐4 knockout mice 6 implies that this nonlinearity will not be attributed to unidentified transporters, and the findings in this study further support these hypotheses.

The pivotal parameters identified in the present model (AtDPP4, AsDPP4, and CLreab,DPP4), particularly the amount of DPP‐4, are anticipated to exhibit similarities to those obtained through analyses of the nonlinear pharmacokinetics of other DPP‐4 inhibitors. For instance, sitagliptin demonstrates a marginal escalation in renal excretion from 70.6% to 88.7% as oral doses ranging from 12.5 to 400 mg are administered, 21 implying the involvement of a mechanism similar to that observed with LNG. However, given the predominance of renal clearance in sitagliptin, renal DPP‐4 is likely to become rapidly saturated, potentially obscuring the manifestation of DPP‐4‐related reabsorption as conspicuously as with LNG. We propose that the initial stage of reabsorption involves the formation of a drug‐DPP4 complex in the urine or on the canalicular membrane facing the urine, a process explicable through the binding and dissociation kinetics of drugs. Nevertheless, the drug‐dependent aspects of subsequent processes following complex formation, such as internalization or complex dissociation within organelles, remain elusive. Further experimental endeavors are warranted to elucidate the drug‐dependent dynamics of DPP‐4.

Compared with the analysis by Sarashina et al., 5 our study showed an improvement in the reproducibility of the observed LNG plasma concentrations (Figure 5). In the process of investigating parameters related to physiological processes that were not captured in the previous model, we focused on the K d value for the following reasons. In the present study, the K d was estimated as 0.0259 nmol/L, approximately half of the experimental value 6 (Table 2, Figure 4). While it is notable that such a deviation was observed despite the penalizing, it could potentially be attributed to the measurement accuracy limitations inherent in surface plasmon resonance. 3 In contrast, the previous study 5 estimated markedly lower K d (0.00252 nmol/L). The results of sensitivity analyses for the estimated parameters (Figure S5) demonstrated a clear increase in plasma concentration when the K d was adjusted downward. Therefore, the underestimation of the K d in the previous study had led to the overestimation of plasma concentrations (Figure S6b), which was improved in the present study (Figure S6a) for a more accurate representation of the physiological processes.

The differences in the estimated K d values between the previous and present studies could be due to the improved consideration of the tDPP‐4 distribution in the present study. In other words, our previous model, which barely considered tDPP‐4, may have estimated a smaller K d , by substituting the tDPP‐4 binding with the tightened binding. The current model exhibits a notable enhancement in the predictability of the distribution phase of the plasma concentration–time profile. Specifically, when scrutinizing plasma concentration profiles from 0 to 6 h, our model more accurately reproduced the distinctive distribution phase of LNG (Figure S6) compared with our previous model. 5 This unique distribution phase observed in humans is presumed to originate from the time‐dependent distribution of LNG to tDPP‐4 in each organ, a phenomenon precisely described by the present PBPK model adequately incorporating tDPP‐4 distribution in each organ/tissue. The reasons Sarashina et al. 5 were unable to incorporate AtDPP4 can be ascribable to two factors: the need to avoid model complexity (as over‐parameterization can prevent convergence) and the uncertainty regarding tDPP‐4 distribution in humans. Our study was able to overcome these challenges through the robust parameter estimation provided by the CGNM and the bridging approach from rats to humans.

A simulation of the tDPP‐4 occupancy on renal tubules was conducted to delineate the relation between the nonlinear urinary excretion of LNG and the presence of tDPP‐4 on renal tubules. The simulation predicted that tDPP‐4 occupancy would reach 100% at an LNG dose of 2.5 mg when varying doses of 1, 2.5, 5, and 10 mg were orally administered (Figure S7). Consistent with the simulation, the observed reduction in unbound renal clearance of LNG (CLR,u) at lower doses (Table S6) could be attributed to saturable tDPP‐4‐related reabsorption on renal tubules.

In conclusion, leveraging the notably high affinity of LNG, we have effectively revealed the whole‐body distribution and renal dynamics of DPP‐4 in humans through a translational PBPK analysis of rat‐to‐human. This endeavor was substantiated by corroborative evidence from animal experiments, in vitro studies, and the comprehensive parameter search of CGNM. This study will propose the potential of PBPK–CGNM approaches in unraveling complex pharmacokinetic phenomena such as TMDD.
