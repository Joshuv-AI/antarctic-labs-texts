Measuring the potential risk of re-identification of imaging research participants from open-source automated face recognition software
Authors: Carl M Prakaashana, Marios Savvides, Jeffrey L Gunter, Matthew L Senjem, Prashanthi Vemuri, Kejal Kantarci, Johnanthan Graff-Radford, Ronald C Petersen, Clifford R Jack, Christopher G Schwarz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years facial recognition software has gone from an area of research to widespread adoption and broad public availability. Open-source face recognition packages are freely available on the internet for anyone to download, and several public websites allow users to run facial recognition on photos without needing any technical knowledge or equipment beyond internet access, making facial recognition accessible for anyone to use for any purpose. Previous research has demonstrated the ability of commercial software to identify a person based on facial content in brain imaging. In this study we tested two commercial facial recognition programs and a variety of popular open-source computer vision and facial recognition software packages to measure how accurately they could be used for reidentification of research participants in brain imaging studies. We tested a “population to sample” threat model, measuring the rates of success for which face recognition software selected the correct MRI-based face reconstruction from a set of 182 participants as its top-scoring match for input facial photographs. We found that the freely available open-source software packages we tested can reidentify a research participant with up to 59 % accuracy. This was less than the commercial packages, which were able to achieve much higher accuracies in the ranges of 92 % and 98 % in identical testing scenarios, but it demonstrates the feasibility of re-identifying faces in research MRI even by individuals with access to only freely available software. As the trust and confidence of potential participants is essential to brain imaging research, especially with widespread and mandated data-sharing of brain scans, this further supports the need to replace identifiable face imagery in brain images to protect the privacy of research participants.Keywords: De-facing, De-identification, Face recognition, Data sharing

Background

It has been demonstrated that research participants can potentially be re-identified from de-identified MRI, CT, and PET brain scans with up to 98 % accuracy using Microsoft Azure’s cloud-based commercial facial recognition software (Schwarz et al., 2022). This shows the need to “de-face” publicly shared research brain scans to ensure participant privacy. Subsequently, Microsoft has begun restricting its face recognition services, intending to prevent misuse(Farley et al., 2022). More recently, Steeg et al. have shown that research participants with self-reported social media use and who had photos present in a Google search can be re-identified using readily available, user-friendly, software and websites, requiring no special technical knowledge (Steeg et al., 2024). Our current study tests a variety of popular open-source computer vision and facial recognition software packages to measure the re-identification risk posed by potential malicious actors with access to only unrestricted free software, and it performs a direct comparison with two closed-source commercial facial recognition packages.

Methods

This study used brain MRI from Siemens 3T scanners and face photographs from 182 participants (ages 34–93, mean=63.2, median=65, SD=15.5, stratified by sex and age-decade) in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (MCSA), a population representative study of cognitively unimpaired older adults from Olmsted County, Minnesota, USA. Additional details of this dataset have been previously published (Schwarz et al., 2022). All participants provided informed consent under the Mayo Clinic IRB for the present study (18–009120) and for brain imaging in the MCSA (14–004401). Each participant had photos taken with an iPad, which were manually cropped loosely around the head and converted to grayscale. Each 3D T2-FLAIR MRI was used to automatically create a 3D reconstruction by using Otsu’s method to determine a binary threshold separating head from background, smoothing, and creating a surface mesh from the result using the isosurface function in Matlab, saving the result as a .gii (“gifti”) file. Using Surf Ice (Rorden, 2021), a total of 81 renders (2D reconstruction images; Fig. 1) were made from each 3D reconstruction by looping across a range of orientation and lighting source positions and saving a 2D .png file for each one.

An example MRI-based face reconstruction (render) automatically created using Surf Ice software. This example is from an average of 202 participants’ MRIs, not an individual participant.

We used the above data to test a “population to sample” threat model, where an actor seeks to identify a specific target (from the population) within the dataset (sample), e.g. “Which of these people is Waldo?”. This differs from a “sample to population” threat model where an actor seeks to identify individuals from a sample from potentially the entire world population, e.g. “Who is this person? I have no idea…”. Our dataset of 182 individuals cannot reasonably be considered a “population”, and our participants did not consent to uploading their imagery for searching public face recognition websites with population-level databases. Therefore, we focus on the population to sample scenario, measuring percentage success rates of how often someone could automatically match photographs to the correct MRI (represented by the 81 2D renders from the 3D reconstruction) from among 182 potential matches, across each of our 182 participants. We counted a correct match when face recognition software selected the correct MRI as its top-scoring match for input facial photographs. In other words, if someone expected that “Waldo” was one of the 182 individuals, how often would they be able to correctly and automatically find Waldo?

Several open-source Python libraries, namely OpenCV’s (Bradski, 2000) ORB (OpenCV Team, 2025a) and SIFT (OpenCV Team, 2025b), face_recognition (Geitgey, 2022), and DeepFace (Serengil and Ozpinar, 2024), as well as two closed-source commercial facial recognition programs, Microsoft Azure (Farley et al., 2024) and Ultron-v0, were evaluated for their ability to correctly match photos to renders of their respective participants. We describe each method and our steps for implementation and testing in the following sections.

Our goal was to measure the risk levels posed by the availability of these software in aggregate and compare it with our previous work that used only Microsoft Azure. Azure and other commercial software built using exceptionally large databases are typically not available to low resource individuals, unlike free open-source software that can be downloaded by anyone. In testing the open-source software, we measure the potential for re-identification by an individual with sufficient knowledge to obtain a dataset of MRIs, open them with research-standard speciality software to produce face renders, and use popular pre-trained open source Python face recognition software by installing them and adapting their examples to use the target data. This testing was performed by a single individual with a B.S. in Computational Physics (C.M.P.) working in a MRI research lab, and he performed an exhaustive comparison of many methods using a high-performance computing environment, but installing a single package and using it out-of-the-box would require far less knowledge and resources. By testing a range of open-source software, we estimate the range of performance that a low resource individual might obtain by using a single face recognition package.

As we do not want to provide a blueprint for how potential attackers might maliciously re-identify medical research data, we have chosen to de-identify the tested algorithms. These algorithms generally divide their task into two steps: 1) detecting facial regions in images (face detection), and 2) determining the best match in a database for those detected faces by extracting and comparing facial features (face recognition). Some packages also allow these components to be mixed and matched and independently configured. To prevent potential misuse of our results, we re-labeled each face recognition model with letters A-J, and each face detection model with numbers 1–8. SIFT

Scale-Invariant Feature Transform (SIFT) (OpenCV Team, 2025b) is a general-purpose computer vision algorithm to detect and describe point-level “features” in images that are designed to be stable and invariant (or tolerant) to scale and rotation. It was not designed specifically for face recognition, but we tested it because a previous publication reported good results using this approach (Provenzano et al., 2022). Each feature keypoint is associated with a 128-dimensional integer vector acting as its feature descriptor (OpenCV Team, 2025b). We used Open CV’s implementation of SIFT, creating a SIFT object with default settings and using it for computing keypoints and descriptors, storing them in a dictionary for later reference.

SIFT was tested with OpenCV’s implementations of brute-force (OpenCV Team, 2025c) and Fast Library for Approximate Nearest Neighbors (FLANN) (OpenCV Team, 2025d) matching algorithms with a workflow based on a Datahacker.rs tutorial on use of OpenCV (Zivkovic, 2021).

OpenCV’s Brute-Force matcher was tested with OpenCV’s three compatible norms, cv.NORM_L1, cv.NORM_L2, and cv.NORM_L2SQR (OpenCV Team, 2025e) as distance metrics, but otherwise used default settings. OpenCV’s Brute-Force matcher (OpenCV Team, 2025c) compares two sets of descriptors, a set for the photo being matched and a second set for a render. The Brute-Force matcher compares each descriptor from the photo to each descriptor from the render, returning the best matches based on which pair are the closest as determined by a specified norm FLANN matching used parameters suggested by Datahacker.rs (algorithm = FLAN_INDEX_KDTREE, trees = 5, checks = 50) (Zivkovic, 2021) but otherwise used default settings.

The “best match” for each photo was tested in 5 different ways. In all cases, image-level matches were based on distances of point-level matches to find which pairs of photos and renders had the “closest” keypoints and descriptors. If the participant for the photo and the render matched, it was counted as correct. The first test took the mean of all the point-level matches in the image, which accounts for variations in the number of keypoints for different photos and renders to get the mean. The second test standardized the number of matches to a sorted subset of the best matches with the size of the subset determined by the photo/render that had the smallest number of keypoints. For example, if the smallest number of keypoints for any render in the test was 204 and the smallest number of keypoints for any photo in the test was 254, the subset included the 204 shortest distance matches. To check if the shortest distance matches were disproportionally good predictors, the third, fourth, and fifth tests further reduced this subset to the ten, three, and the single shortest distance matches, respectively.

ORB

Like SIFT, ORB (OpenCV Team, 2025a) is a general-purpose object matching algorithm based on detection and matching of salient keypoints in each image, building point-level image descriptors to be used for matching across images. It was designed to be an open-source alternative to SIFT (OpenCV Team, 2025f). Using OpenCV’s implementation of ORB, our testing procedures mirrored those we used for SIFT. ORB detector objects were created with OpenCV’s default settings and used to detect keypoints and compute descriptors for each of the photos and renders, saving them in a dictionary for quick reference. ORB calculates descriptors as 32-dimensional integer vectors.

face_recognition and deepface

We tested two open-source Python libraries, face_recognition and DeepFace, which, unlike ORB and SIFT, are designed specifically for facial recognition. They follow a typical face recognition workflow of loading an image, locating the faces in the image with a pre-made model, normalizing the faces (to account for scale and rotation), then generating an embedding vector of the face using another pre-trained feature extraction model with the encodings being represented as a vector. The face_recognition package uses Dlib’s implementations of two face-locating (i.e. detection) models, Histogram of Oriented Gradients (HOG) as the default, and Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) (Dlib Team, 2018) as an option. Also included are two face encoding models, “large” and “small”; “large” is the default value, and “small” returns fewer face landmarks but runs faster. The documentation for Dlib states that CNN is more accurate than HOG but requires more processing power (Dlib Team, 2018). The face_recognition library was tested with all four combinations of locating and encoding models. The DeepFace library included six unique face locating models (ssd (Ajad, 2024), opencv (OpenCV Team, 2025g), RetinaFace from InsightFace (Guo et al., 2023), MTCNN (Centeno, 2024), Dlib, and MediaPipe (Kariat et al., 2025)) and 9 unique face encoding modules (VGG-Face from University of Oxford’s Visual Geometry Group (Cao et al., 2018), Facenet and Facenet512 (Esler, 2023), OpenFace (Baltrusaitis, 2019), DeepFace, DeepID, Dlib (King, 2009), ArcFace from insightface (Guo et al., 2023), SFace (Yaoyao, 2021)). Both packages included several optional parameters which we tuned empirically during preliminary analyses. For each parameter we found that one option was either an obvious best choice which was used for all later trials, or that the parameter had little effect, in which case the default value was used.

Matching for these packages was performed in two ways, first with a locally implemented brute-force matcher and second with the Support Vector Machines (SVM) library from scikit-learn (Pedregosa et al., 2011). Brute-force matching used DeepFace’s cosine distance metric with every photo embedding vector being compared to every render embedding vector and the render producing the closest match being taken as the “best match.” Matching was also attempted using the mean embedding vector of the renders for a given participant, where the participant whose mean embedding vector was closest to the embedding vector of the photo was the “best match,” but this didn’t perform better than the simple brute-force matching and was excluded after initial tests.

Scikit-learn’s implementation of SVM includes three models of support vector classification (SVC): “SVC”, “NuSVC”, and “LinearSVC.” The SVM objects are trained with a labeled list of render encodings and any optional parameters; after training, the SVM will then return a predicted match when called with a photo encoding. Each of the SVCs allows a plethora of optional input parameters. Because trying every possible combination was not feasible, after an initial test with default parameters, we tuned them empirically as follows. A broad parameter crawl was conducted with each of the SVCs using one pair of DeepFace face location and encoding models that performed well in brute-force testing to establish a standard parameter space for further SVM testing, initially trying every combination of discrete-option parameters and a handful of different values for the numeric parameters, eliminating options which had no impact on matching performance or selecting option which always showed superior results. From this reduced parameter space, a larger range of numeric values were tested. From this, a standard parameter range for each SVC was selected with a range of numeric values centered around the best performing values from this test with the intention of having a great enough range to include the best performing values for other combinations of face locating and encoding models while keeping runtimes manageable. With these parameters established, all three of the SVCs were tested with every combination of face locating and encoding models for both face_recogntion and DeepFace.

Microsoft azure

We also tested using Microsoft Azure’s Face API (Farley et al., 2024), using identical methods we have previously described and published (Schwarz et al., 2021, 2019). Briefly, a training set of 10 labeled renders for each participant was uploaded to Azure, in a secure private Mayo Clinic Azure instance. This set was randomly chosen for each image from the 81 images, because past preliminary testing found diminishing accuracy benefits with increased runtime when more were used. From this training set, Azure creates classifiers for each participant using Microsoft’s proprietary internal pre-trained face detector (Farley et al., 2025a) and embedding encoder (Farley et al., 2025b) models. At time of writing, these are still the latest model versions available for each. After this training, photos of each participant were submitted to Azure, which returns a ranked list of up to 50 closest matches participants based on the render-trained classifiers with confidence scores ranging from 0–1. The confidence scores were summed for each participant’s five photos, from which the matched participant with the higher summed confidence score was taken to be the best match. Unlike the other methods, the raw face feature encoding embedding vectors are not made available to end users, so we were unable to test with SVM-based matching.

Ultron

Ultron-v0, a commercial facial recognition program developed by Marios Savvides et al. of Carnegie Mellon University, was provided for testing in a closed-source Docker installation. The same collection of photos and MRI surface renders used with the other software were imported into the Docker environment and a run using a modified version of a Python script provided with the software. Ultron generated feature set encodings which were used to train its internal matching model. The photos were then encoded by the same process and queried to the matching model, which returned the participant which most closely matched the photo. The feature set embedding encodings for the photos and renders are structured as 512-dimentional floating-point vectors, similar in structure to the encodings produced by face_recognition and DeepFace. These embedding encodings were exported from the Docker environment and also tested with SVMs in the same manner as face_recognition and DeepFace.

Feature matching with SIFT and ORB (both from OpenCV) did not outperform random guessing despite a prior publication reporting moderate success (Provenzano et al., 2022). As general computer vision algorithms SIFT and ORB were not developed specifically for facial recognition and rely on corner and edge detection to find sets of keypoints for matching across images. During initial testing, manual review of keypoints showed that SIFT disproportionately identified areas at edges around eyeglass frames, hair, including facial hair, and the border between the face and the background of the images, elements unrelated to the face itself and invisible in MRI. A subset of participant photos was selected containing the 85 participants whose photos did not have eyeglasses and did not have facial hair. These photos were then cropped to include only the face itself, excluding the background and hair other than eyelashes and eyebrows to try to improve SIFT and ORB’s matching. This variant also failed to outperform random chance. Because neither SIFT nor ORB performed better than random guess, testing beyond default settings was not attempted.

The best match rates for the open-source libraries were 58–59 % accuracy using models from face_recognition or DeepFace with support vector machines (SVMs). Without SVMs, these packages performed modestly worse (51 %–55 %). Microsoft Azure was the top performing method with 178/182 (98 %) correct matches. The other commercial application, Ultron-v0, correctly matched 90 % using its feature-matching functions and 92 % using our SVM matching of its feature sets.

Discussion

In summary, the commercial face recognition packages achieved the best results (98 % and 92 %), but the open-source face recognition libraries also performed well (up to 59 %), and the general-purpose keypoint-based algorithms (SIFT and ORB) had poor results (no better than random chance).

The high performance of Microsoft Azure was expected based on our previously published results for this task in the same dataset (Schwarz et al., 2023, 2022, 2021, 2019), and its leading performance in large-scale comparisons of non-medical face recognition tasks (Grother et al., 2019). Ultron-v0’s performance was also very high, while SIFT and ORB performed poorly. We expected relatively poor performance because these are general object-matching methods that are not designed specifically for face recognition; however, we expected performance to be at least better than chance based on a previous study using them with brain CT images (Provenzano et al., 2022). Our results with SIFT did not concur with those from Provenzano et al. (Provenzano et al., 2022). We contacted Dr. Provenzano to discuss potential reasons for our discrepant findings, and she suggested we try renders made from CT images and volumetric renders (displays all voxels in a 3D volume with partial transparency, showing some features from below the surface), rather than surface renders (displays only the outer-most layer of suprathreshold voxels) (Provenzano, 2023). CT images were not available for these participants, but we performed preliminary testing on a few images using surface rendering with MRIcroGL, and these did not appreciably improve matching nor keypoint detection, so we did not explore these changes further.

This study addresses a lack of published data comparing multiple face recognition packages according to their potential to re-identify participants in brain imaging research studies. Its strengths include a thorough examination of the potential of a knowledgeable, but minimally resourced, individual to leverage freely available open-source software to potentially re-identify brain research participants in research databases. While not exhaustive of every open-source facial recognition model, the numerous models included in the DeepFace library as well as face_recognition make it clear that there are at least three open-source models available that can match research participants to their photos with 50–59 % accuracy and at least two commercial packages which exceed 90 % accuracy. We have also shown the ability of Support Vector Machines to modestly improve the results of facial recognition generally.

Our study’s limitations include the limited number of packages tested, with free and open-source packages such as Exadel CompreFace (Dapšauskas et al., 2023) and OpenCV’s facial recognition not being tested. We also did not thoroughly test all of the distance measures included with DeepFace (Cosine, Euclidean, and Euclidean_L2). Cosine and Euclidean_L2 performed similarly well in initial testing, and both performed better than Euclidean, but Cosine was faster than Euclidean. Based on this, it was decided to use only cosine for brute force testing for face_recognition and DeepFace. All the testing involved pre-trained models, and no attempt was made to train a model specifically for MRI-based face renders. Also, facial recognition is a computationally intensive task. The computer used for this research was a multiuser interactive server with 2 Intel Gold 5218 CPU at 2.3Ghz for 64 total (logical) cores, 768GB RAM, and an Nvidia Tesla T4 (16GB), and some tests required days to complete even with the limited pool of 182 potential matches. While all the tests performed can, in principle, be run on a common desktop computer, the testing environment is not something that is available to a hypothetical minimally resourced individual. The empirical tuning of the face encoding models and the SVMs were never tested on a separate dataset and may have been overfitted. To our knowledge, no additional data with matched MRI and face photographs is available from any other source, so we cannot form an independent validation set. This study tests a “population-to-sample” matching paradigm, measuring the rates at which an attacker would be successful in identifying a target individual within a dataset of brain MRI with 182 participants. It does not examine the inverse “sample-to-population” problem in which an attacker attempts to re-identify a brain MRI from the entire world population, which is inherently a much more difficult problem. However, one recent study examined population-to-sample by querying a public, closed-source face recognition website with MRI-based face reconstructions, and they found that individuals without domain expertise were easily able to correctly re-identify 2 out of 4 participants (50 %) (i.e., photos of the correct person were returned as results), demonstrating that re-identification is a concern even for sample-to-population attacks (Steeg et al., 2024). We also acknowledge that pre-trained face recognition models are typically trained using data scraped from Internet sources, which over-represents some population groups relative to others. Our sample contains equal numbers of men and women and equal numbers across age-decades 30–90+, but its large majority identify with European ancestry, so our data may not reflect performance in under-represented populations.

Conclusion

While the highest match rates obtained by widely available open-source packages (59 %) are much lower than those of a limited-access commercial products, (Ultron-v0 with 92 % and Microsoft Azure, with 98 %), they show that malicious actors with modest technical ability and no-cost readily available software could still potentially re-identify a research participant from a brain scan most of the time. This further highlights the need for image refacing software, such as mri_reface (Schwarz et al., 2021) (https://www.nitrc.org/projects/mri_reface/), to protect the privacy and identifies of research volunteers. When de-facing is not a feasible solution to provide direct protection from re-identification (e.g. when the research purpose requires original face imagery), a reasonable alternative is sharing images only under strict, enforceable legal agreements that safeguard participant privacy, although such agreements add significant barriers and time delays to data sharing (Jwa et al., 2025).

Future work:

This work shows a range of performance across a large but finite set of tested methods. Future work might continue to enlarge and update the set of tested methods. It also might compare the effects of various de-facing methods at preventing re-identification, similar to our previous testing with Microsoft Azure only (Schwarz et al., 2022, 2021). Standardized benchmarks could be developed and updated regularly for face recognition performance in the neuroimaging domain, analogous to the NIST Facial Recognition Vendor Test (Grother et al., 2019). The risk of re-identification could also be measured more in context, either compared against or together with the risk posed by other frequently shared research data types, such as demographic, regional, and genomic data. These and future findings could lead to more specific policies or recommendations for safeguarding privacy in neuroimaging data sharing by governments, IRBs, or research funders.
