Sulphate resistance of geopolymer mortars incorporating waste clay brick powder
Authors: Shaila Sharmin, Prabir K. Sarker, Wahidul K. Biswas, Nusrat Jahan Mim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The high carbon emissions and environmental impact associated with ordinary Portland cement have driven the search for sustainable alternative binders. In this context, waste clay brick powder, generated in large quantities from construction and demolition waste, offers a promising aluminosilicate source for geopolymer production. This study investigates the long-term durability performance of geopolymer mortars incorporating waste clay brick powder in terms of resistance to aggressive sulphate exposure, highlighting the potential of waste clay brick powder geopolymer as a potential sustainable building material. Mortars with 0–60% waste clay brick powder content were immersed in 5% Na₂SO₄ solution for up to 180 days to assess the chemical resistance, dimensional stability, and mechanical performance. Microstructural and phase analyses using X-ray diffraction and scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive spectroscopy revealed a cohesive matrix with limited microcracking and minimal sulphur ingress, indicating strong chemical and structural stability. Moderate waste clay brick powder incorporation enhanced matrix durability, with compressive strength peaking at approximately 69 MPa after 180 days, compared to 63 MPa for the control with a decline in strength at higher waste clay brick powder contents. After 180 days of sulphate exposure, compressive strength reduction ranged from 10 to 19% across all mixes. Mixture W40 showed the lowest expansion of 0.0516%, compared to 0.0632% in the control and 0.0757% in W60. The mass gain was 1.91% for mix W40 compared to 2.8% for the control, indicating reduced susceptibility to sulphate ingress. These results indicate the potential of waste clay brick powder as a sustainable binder for durable building materials, contributing to enhanced performance in aggressive environments.

Introduction

The global cement and construction industries continue to seek sustainable alternatives to ordinary Portland cement (OPC) due to increasing concerns regarding high carbon emissions, resource depletion, and long-term environmental impact1–3. Alkali-activated materials and geopolymers have emerged as promising alternative cementitious binders with potential for reduced carbon emissions, depending on the activator system and mixture design, due to their ability to form a stable, cross-linked aluminosilicate network using industrial by-products and construction wastes4,5. Numerous studies have demonstrated their favourable mechanical performance, reduced embodied carbon, and enhanced resistance to certain chemical environments6,7. However, despite these advantages, the long-term durability of geopolymers particularly under aggressive sulphate-rich conditions remains insufficiently understood, limiting their widespread adoption in critical infrastructure applications8.

Sulphate attack is a major durability challenge faced by cementitious materials exposed to seawater, groundwater, wastewater, and sulphate-bearing soils9. In OPC-based systems, sulphate ions initiate expansive reactions with calcium-containing hydration products, forming gypsum, ettringite, and thomasite, which lead to cracking, softening, and substantial loss in mechanical strength10. In contrast, geopolymers generally contain low calcium and exhibit fundamentally different degradation mechanisms11. Instead of forming significant expansive products, sulphate ingress may induce ion exchange processes, disrupt the aluminosilicate gel structure, or generate internal crystallisation pressures linked to sodium- or magnesium-sulphate interactions12. The severity of deterioration is strongly dependent on precursor chemistry, pore structure, alkali content, curing regime, exposure conditions, and the interaction between residual unreacted phases and sulphate ions10,11. Although several studies have explored sulphate resistance in fly ash (FA), ground granulated blast furnace slag (GGBFS), and metakaolin based geopolymers, the findings remain inconsistent and highly sensitive to material characteristics, which indicates the need for more precursor-specific investigations10,13.

The choice of precursor material plays a crucial role in determining sulphate resistance14–26. Fly ash-based geopolymers display varied responses, with high-alkali systems generally exhibiting greater vulnerability due to increased ion mobility and gel instability under sulphate exposure27–29. GGBFS-rich binders, despite their superior early-age strength, may undergo decalcification and destabilisation of C–A–S–H or C–(N)–A–S–H gels when exposed to sulphates, resulting in progressive strength loss30,31. Metakaolin systems often perform better due to their dense N–A–S–H gel network, although ion exchange and structural changes can still occur32. These variations demonstrate that precursor mineralogy and microstructural evolution hold significant influence over long-term chemical stability. However, alternative aluminosilicate sources particularly construction and demolition wastes have received far less attention in sulphate durability studies4,30,33–35.

OPC systems are susceptible to sulphate attack due to their high calcium content, leading to the formation of expansive phases such as gypsum and ettringite, whereas geopolymers, with lower calcium and a stable aluminosilicate gel, generally exhibit improved resistance depending on mix design36. In parallel, the use of recycled powders, including recycled cement, GGBS-based waste, and waste clay brick powder, has gained increasing attention in cementitious materials for enhancing durability through filler effects and pore refinement37–39. Additionally, modification approaches such as nano-silica incorporation have been shown to further improve microstructural and mechanical performance in recycled material-based systems40.

Among the emerging waste-based materials, waste clay brick powder (WCBP) has recently attracted interest as a potentially sustainable and readily available precursor for geopolymer production7,39. Generated in large quantities from construction and demolition activities, WCBP primarily consists of partially calcined aluminosilicate phases, quartz, and feldspathic minerals41. Past research has shown that WCBP exhibits measurable pozzolanic activity and can contribute to blended binder systems42. Earlier work of the authors demonstrated that WCBP effectively enhances mechanical strength in ternary WCBP–FA–GGBFS binders and influences gel composition, reaction pathways, and pore network development41,42.

However, the long-term durability of WCBP as a potentially sustainable geopolymer precursor under aggressive environments need to be understood. Previous studies have investigated WCBP-based geopolymers in terms of water absorption, sorptivity, drying shrinkage, freeze–thaw resistance, fire resistance, acid exposure, abrasion, and performance after prolonged water immersion or cyclic wetting–drying39,43–48. While these studies provide understanding into general mechanical and durability behaviour, sulphate-induced degradation in two-part (alkaline solution-activated) WCBP geopolymers, where reaction kinetics, gel chemistry, pore structure, and ion transport differ substantially remains largely unexplored. Consequently, the combined effects of WCBP content, activator chemistry, pore structure, and sulphate ingress on mechanical and microstructural stability represent a critical knowledge gap for the reliable application of these materials in sulphate-rich environments.

In response to this knowledge gap, the present study investigates the sulphate attack resistance of WCBP geopolymer mortar through a comprehensive experimental and microstructural analysis. Mortar specimens incorporating different proportions of WCBP were exposed to sulphate environments, and deterioration was assessed through mass change, visual inspection, dimensional variations, and compressive strength development. Complementary microstructural techniques including X-ray diffraction (XRD), and scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive spectroscopy (SEM–EDS) were employed to capture sulphate-induced changes in gel chemistry, crystalline phases, and pore network evolution. By linking macro-scale deterioration with underlying chemical and microstructural transformations, this study provides new insights into the durability of WCBP geopolymer mortar and contributes to the development of reliable, waste-derived, low-carbon binders suitable for sulphate-exposed environments.

Materials and methods

Raw materials and geopolymer paste preparation

Based on geopolymer binders previously reported by the authors, three formulations were selected for the development of geopolymer mortars42. To further evaluate the influence of WCBP, an additional mixture containing a higher proportion of WCBP was also designed to determine whether increasing its content could provide superior performance compared with the previously identified optimal composition. The primary objective of this study is to assess the durability characteristics of these mortars, with particular emphasis on sulphate resistance. Detailed descriptions of binder synthesis, the optimisation strategy, and the associated chemical, mineralogical, and microstructural characteristics have been documented in earlier publications by the authors41,42. The optimised systems include a binary binder composed of fly ash and GGBFS, which serves as the reference mixture, along with three ternary blends where WCBP is incorporated alongside FA and GGBFS at varying proportions.

The ternary geopolymer binders were formulated using WCBP, FA, and GGBFS as the precursor materials. The WCBP was produced from discarded clay bricks obtained from a nearby brick recycling facility. Initially, the bricks were crushed into fragments ranging between 10 and 20 mm. These fragments were subsequently washed, dried in an oven at 105 °C for a duration of 24 h, and then milled in a laboratory ball mill for approximately four hours to achieve a particle size distribution comparable to that of FA and GGBFS. During the milling process, a charge-to-brick mass ratio of 5:1 was maintained. The grinding media consisted of stainless-steel balls, comprising 60% with a diameter of 50 mm and 40% with a diameter of 25 mm. The milling drum operated at a rotational speed of 60 rpm throughout the grinding period. After processing, the resulting powder was preserved in a dry storage environment, and its mean apparent density was determined to be 2395 kg/m³ following the procedure outlined in AS114149.

The physicochemical and microstructural characteristics of WCBP, including XRF, XRD, and morphology, have been comprehensively reported in the authors’ previous work41,42, indicating that WCBP consists primarily of aluminosilicate phases with significant quartz content and exhibits measurable pozzolanic reactivity, which plays a key role in governing the performance of the blended geopolymer mortars. FA sourced from Gladstone in Queensland and GGBFS supplied locally in Western Australia were used as received without additional processing. The specific gravities of FA and GGBFS were 2.34 and 2.90, respectively. The alkaline activating solution was prepared by blending sodium silicate (Na₂SiO₃) with a sodium hydroxide (NaOH) solution. The sodium silicate solution contained 14.7% Na2O, 29.4% SiO2, and 55.9% water, with a specific gravity of 1.52 and a pH ranging between 11 and 13. The NaOH solution was produced by dissolving analytical-grade pellets (~ 99% purity) in water to obtain a concentration of 12 M. Both activator components were combined approximately three hours before the mortar preparation stage to ensure proper dissolution and chemical equilibrium of the solution. For the fine aggregate, locally available concrete sand was used, having a fineness modulus of 1.95 and a specific gravity of 2.60. Prior to mixing, the sand was conditioned to a saturated surface-dry state in accordance with ASTM C12850.

All specimens were initially cured under controlled ambient conditions and sealed by plastic wrapping to minimise moisture loss, which served as the reference curing condition. Accordingly, all reported strength reductions are calculated relative to these ambient-cured control specimens. A separate water-exposed control was not included in this study; therefore, the results represent the combined effects of ongoing geopolymerisation and sulphate exposure. The mix design parameters, including activator ratio, sodium silicate to sodium hydroxide ratio, and curing conditions, were kept constant across all mixtures, with WCBP content as the primary variable. However, it is acknowledged that interactions between mix chemistry, pore structure, and reaction kinetics may also influence the observed performance (Table 1).

Mortar formulations were produced by partially substituting FA with WCBP while maintaining a constant GGBFS content of 15% within the binder system. Four mixtures were prepared for the investigation, consisting of a control sample without WCBP (CM) and three additional mixtures containing 20%, 40%, and 60% WCBP, designated as W20, W40, and W60, respectively. The ratio of alkaline activator solution to binder was fixed at 0.45 for all mixtures. Additionally, the proportion of sodium silicate to sodium hydroxide in the activator solution was maintained at 2, while the concentration of the sodium hydroxide solution remained constant at 12 M across all mixes.

For mortar preparation, the dry precursor materials and sand were initially blended in a Hobart mixer for approximately three minutes to ensure uniform distribution. The previously prepared alkaline activator solution was then slowly added to the dry mixture. Mixing continued for three minutes at low speed, followed by an additional minute at high speed to obtain a well-dispersed and uniform geopolymer mortar. The freshly prepared mortar was subsequently cast into moulds for further mechanical testing and microstructural characterisation.

Experimental procedures

Specimen casting and conditioning

Specimens were cast in two different geometries to assess compressive strength, dimensional stability, and changes in mass: cylindrical samples measuring 50 mm in diameter and 100 mm in height, and prismatic samples with dimensions of 25 mm × 25 mm × 285 mm (Fig. 1). For each test condition, a minimum of three specimens was prepared to ensure reliability of the results. During casting, the moulds were filled in two layers, and each layer was carefully compacted to eliminate entrapped air and minimize the formation of internal voids.

Immediately following casting, the moulded specimens were sealed with plastic sheets and positioned on elevated supports within airtight containers to limit moisture evaporation. The sealed containers were subsequently placed in an oven maintained at a temperature of 35 ± 3 °C for approximately 24 h. After removal from the moulds, the samples were transferred to controlled curing conditions and allowed to harden for at least seven days, by which time they had developed an average compressive strength of roughly 20 MPa.

Sulphate exposure, and mechanical testing

After the initial curing stage, the specimens were immersed in a 5% sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄) solution prepared 24 h in advance. The solution pH was maintained between 6 and 8 in accordance with the requirements of ASTM C101251. To minimise evaporation and external contamination, the containers were tightly sealed, and the temperature of the solution was controlled at 23 ± 2 °C during the entire exposure period. To maintain a stable chemical environment, the sulphate solution was periodically replenished and replaced whenever specimens were temporarily removed for measurements or testing.

The mass variation and expansion behaviour of the mortar bars were recorded at specified intervals of 7, 14, 28, 56, 90, 150, and 180 days. Compressive strength tests on the cylindrical specimens were performed at curing ages of 7, 28, 56, 120, and 180 days using a Shimadzu Universal Testing Machine. The loading rate was maintained within the range of 900–1800 N/s in accordance with ASTM C057952. Prior to testing, the specimen surfaces were lightly dried, and any loose debris was removed. For each testing condition, three specimens were evaluated, and the reported values correspond to the average results.

The dimensional stability of the mortar bars under sulphate exposure was assessed by monitoring length changes (Fig. 2). The specimens remained submerged in the 5% Na2SO4 solution during the testing period. Before each measurement, the bars were carefully wiped to remove excess surface moisture, after which their lengths were determined using a length comparator. Expansion was calculated as the percentage change relative to the original gauge length of 285 mm. Measurements were carried out at regular intervals, and the mean value obtained from three specimens was reported for each testing age.

Microstructure and phase analysis

Microstructural features and elemental composition were investigated using scanning electron microscopy coupled with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM–EDS). Analyses were conducted on specimens subjected to sulphate exposure for 28 and 180 days. Two specimen configurations were prepared for this purpose. Surface samples were obtained from the external regions of the hardened mortar to assess morphological characteristics, pore structure, and any deterioration associated with sulphate interaction. In addition, cross-sectional samples were extracted from the inner portion of the specimens to evaluate the internal microstructure, the distribution of geopolymeric gel phases, and the occurrence of microcracking. Small fragments measuring approximately 10 mm × 10 mm were cut using a ceramic cutting saw and subsequently mounted onto aluminium stubs using conductive carbon adhesive tape. To enhance electrical conductivity during imaging, the specimens were coated with a thin carbon film of approximately 20 nm thickness using a 208 h sputter coater. Microstructural imaging was performed with a Tescan Mira scanning electron microscope equipped with both secondary electron (SE) and backscattered electron (BSE) detectors. Elemental composition and atomic ratios were obtained using the EDS system. SEM observations were carried out at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV, whereas EDS spectra were acquired at 15 kV.

The mineralogical characteristics of the mortar mixtures were determined through X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis using a Bruker D8 diffractometer fitted with an automatic sample changer. The measurements employed Cu Kα radiation with a wavelength of 1.5406 Å, operating at 40 mA and 35 kV. Phase identification was achieved by matching the obtained diffraction patterns with reference data from the PDF-5 database using DIFFRAC EVA software. For semi-quantitative phase determination, each powdered sample was mixed with 10 wt% fluorite (CaF2), which served as an internal standard. Semi-quantitative phase analysis was subsequently performed using Topas software.

Results and discussion

Visual appearance

All mortar specimens were closely examined throughout the sulphate exposure period to monitor surface deterioration, including the development of microcracks, porosity, and spalling. The visual appearance of the samples at 90, 120, and 180 days are presented in Fig. 3.

At 90 days, the mortar samples mostly retained their original surface integrity. Minor porosity was noticeable, especially in the W60 specimens, which exhibited slightly rougher surfaces and small localized damages compared to the other samples. Microcracks were barely visible at this stage. W20 and W40 samples, as well as the control, showed smoother surfaces with minimal defects, indicating better early-stage resistance to sulphate-induced damage.

Fig. 3Surface condition of geopolymer mortar specimens at different curing ages: (A) 90 days, (B) 120 days, and (C) 180 days.

Surface condition of geopolymer mortar specimens at different curing ages: (A) 90 days, (B) 120 days, and (C) 180 days.

By 120 days, surface deterioration became more apparent. Microcracks began to form, primarily along edges and corners. The W60 specimens showed an increase in surface roughness and more evidence of cracking, suggesting that higher WCBP content contributed to accelerated degradation. In contrast, the W20 and W40 specimens exhibited only minor microcracks. Among them, the W40 samples maintained sharper edges and smoother surfaces, demonstrating comparatively better resistance to the initial stages of sulphate attack. The control specimens also showed some microcracking, but the severity was less pronounced than in the W60 samples.

At 180 days, the effects of prolonged sulphate exposure were most pronounced. The W60 specimens exhibited widespread microcracking and a significantly porous surface, reflecting comparatively extensive degradation. The control and W20 samples experienced moderate cracking and minor surface erosion, with edges showing some rounding and localized damages. The W40 specimens, however, remained mostly intact, with only minor microcracks with limited edge erosion observed. These visual observations indicate that increasing WCBP content beyond a moderate level (40%) reduces durability under sulphate attack, while moderate replacement levels provide the best combination of structural integrity and resistance to surface deterioration over long-term exposure. However, these observations are qualitative and should be interpreted together with mechanical results. A comparable outcome was also observed in the prior work53.

Compressive strength evolution under sulphate exposure

The compressive strength of the mortar specimens exhibited distinct evolution trends over the 180-day sulphate exposure period, reflecting the combined effects of WCBP content, binder reactivity, and prolonged chemical interaction with the sulphate environment (Fig. 4). The experimental setup employed for compressive strength testing of the geopolymer mortar specimens, W20 and W60, is presented in Fig. 5. Percentage reduction in compressive strength is calculated relative to the corresponding non-exposed (sealed ambient cured) control specimens at the same age (Fig. 6). At early ages, all mixes showed steady strength gains due to ongoing geopolymerization and sufficient moisture availability. At 7 days, the control mix (CM) and the 20% WCBP mix (W20) achieved higher early-age strengths of 35.3 MPa and 37.2 MPa, respectively, compared with the higher WCBP content mixes (W40: 25.3 MPa; W60: 25.4 MPa), indicating faster initial reaction kinetics in binders with lower WCBP replacement. Correspondingly, the early percentage reduction in strength due to sulphate exposure was relatively low for CM (≈ 5.3%) and W20 (≈ 2.4%), while W40 experienced a higher early reduction (≈ 10.3%), reflecting the initially slower reaction of high-WCBP binders (Fig. 6).

By 28 days, the W40 mix exhibited the most pronounced strength gain, reaching 75.2 MPa and surpassing all other mixtures, while W20 and CM attained strengths of 71.4 MPa and 63.8 MPa, respectively. W60 remained comparatively weaker at 56.4 MPa. The corresponding percentage reductions at 28 days were 3.5% for W40, 1.3% for W20, 3.0% for CM, and 1.0% for W60, indicating that sulphate exposure had a minimal effect during the period of active geopolymerization. This behaviour suggests that a 40% WCBP content optimally balances reactive and less reactive phases, promoting an optimised matrix densification while initially being more sensitive to chemical ingress.

Peak compressive strength was generally observed at around 56 days. The W20 mix recorded the highest strength (74.0 MPa), followed by W40 (69.3 MPa), CM (67.3 MPa), and W60 (57.8 MPa). Beyond this age, the percentage reduction under sulphate exposure increased notably for all mixes, reflecting progressive microstructural alteration: CM (≈ 6.6%), W20 (≈ 10.9%), W40 (≈ 14.4%), and W60 (≈ 8.4%). By 180 days, the long-term reductions reached 10.0%, 19.2%, 18.0%, and 14.4% for CM, W20, W40, and W60, respectively. These results indicate that mixes with lower WCBP content (CM, W20) experienced moderate sulphate-induced degradation after peak strength, while W40, despite higher early relative reductions, retained superior absolute strength compared to most mixes, demonstrating enhanced long-term durability. Mix W60, while exhibiting the lowest overall strength, showed a more gradual reduction, indicating a relatively stable matrix under extended chemical attack. The increased surface cracking observed in W60 is primarily associated with a less dense and more heterogeneous matrix structure at higher WCBP content, although minor chemical effects from sulphate interaction such as limited formation of secondary products below XRD/SEM detection limits cannot be fully ruled out. These trends reflect the combined influence of matrix development and sulphate exposure under the applied curing regime.

Analysis of the failure patterns reveals that mixtures CM, W20 and W40 exhibited similar, relatively controlled failure under compressive loading, reflecting their high compressive strengths and the ability of the matrix to sustain stress redistribution even after prolonged sulphate exposure (Fig. 5). In contrast, W60 displayed a more gradual, less abrupt failure, consistent with its lower compressive strength and reduced stiffness (Fig. 5).

Overall, the results highlight the interplay between early-age strength development, long-term durability, and failure behaviour under prolonged sulphate exposure. Mixes with higher early reactivity developed strength rapidly but experienced more pronounced reductions over time, while mixes with slower initial geopolymerization maintained more stable performance under extended exposure. The optimal mix achieved most balanced performance in terms of strength and durability within the 180-day exposure period. Previous studies report similar behaviour for geopolymer mortars under sulphate exposure32,54. Strength evolution often shows initial stability or slight gain followed by gradual reduction, with durability strongly influenced by binder composition and phase development32,55. These observations align with the present results, where WCBP content governed compressive strength retention and microstructural stability under prolonged sulphate attack. In particular, studies specifically focusing on geopolymer systems containing waste clay brick powder have reported similar trends, where an optimum replacement level around 30–40% provides the best balance between reactivity and filler-induced densification, leading to improved compressive strength and durability performance32,54. The present findings are consistent with this reported optimum range, further confirming the role of WCBP content in governing matrix development and long-term performance under sulphate exposure32,54.

Mass change

The mass changes of mortar specimens during 180 days of sulphate exposure are shown in Fig. 7. All mixes exhibited slight initial mass loss due to surface drying, with 7-day reductions of 0.35, 0.31, 0.28, and 0.33% for CM, W20, W40, and W60, respectively. Between 14 and 21 days, a minor recovery (0.06–0.09%) was observed, reflecting initial water absorption and the onset of sulphate reactions.

After 28 days, a more pronounced mass gain occurred in all specimens, attributed to the formation of attributed to possible sulphate-related reaction products or ion accumulation within the pore structure, which were not clearly identified by XRD or SEM–EDS within detection limits, which refine the pore structure and absorb water. This behaviour is also be influenced by capillary water absorption and moisture ingress within the porous matrix. Between 28 and 56 days, CM, W20, W40, and W60 recorded mass gains of 0.05, 0.05, 0.02, and − 0.01%, respectively, indicating early-stage development of sulphate reaction products within the pore network.

Over the long term, mass continued to increase, with CM showing the highest gain of 2.8%, while W20, W40, and W60 exhibited lower gains of 2.35, 1.91, and 2.22%, respectively, demonstrating that partial replacement with WCBP reduces susceptibility to sulphate-induced mass changes. Interestingly, although higher WCBP content mitigated early mass loss, W60 showed slightly higher long-term gain than W40, suggesting that optimal sulphate resistance depends on the balance between pore refinement and formation of any expansion product. Mass variation reflects combined effects of pore filling, continued reaction, and limited sulphate ingress.

These results align with previous studies, where supplementary cementitious materials such as fly ash and silica fume enhance mass stability under sulphate attack by densifying the matrix and reducing permeability, whereas materials like GGBFS may accelerate mass changes due to increased ettringite formation and altered pore characteristics55,56.

Length change

The dimensional stability of the mortar specimens during 180 days of sulphate exposure was assessed by monitoring length changes, and the results are presented in Fig. 8. The results reveal pronounced differences in expansion behaviour depending on the WCBP content. For the control mix (CM), length increased rapidly during early exposure, reaching a peak expansion of 0.1088% at 21 days, followed by a slight decrease to 0.0947% at 28 days. Over the longer term, expansion fluctuated, with values of 0.0786%, 0.0807%, and 0.0632% recorded at 56, 120, and 180 days, respectively, indicating cyclic expansion and minor contraction throughout the exposure period.

The W20 mix exhibited moderate early expansion, peaking at 0.0997% at 21 days, followed by a reduction to 0.0596% at 28 days and a gradual increase to 0.0702% at 180 days. This trend suggests that partial replacement of fly ash with WCBP mitigates early-stage sulphate-induced expansion while allowing limited dimensional changes over the long term. In the W40 mix, early expansion was slightly lower than in W20, with a maximum of 0.0903% at 21 days, followed by a steady decline to 0.0516% at 180 days. The reduced expansion indicates that increasing WCBP content enhances resistance to the formation of expansive reaction products, improving long-term dimensional stability. The W60 specimen displayed the smallest early expansion, peaking at 0.0587% at 21 days, with minor fluctuations over time, ending at 0.0757% at 180 days. This behaviour reflects the ability of higher WCBP content to limit early dimensional changes while maintaining stability over prolonged sulphate exposure.

Overall, the results indicate a clear trend of reduced sulphate-induced expansion with increasing WCBP content. Moderate WCBP content stabilizes early expansion, while higher replacement enhances long-term dimensional stability. These findings demonstrate that WCBP contributes to reducing sulphate-induced expansion, particularly at moderate replacement levels.

Microstructural analysis

X-ray diffraction analysis and phase evolution

The X-ray diffraction patterns of geopolymer mortar specimens after 28 and 180 days of sulphate exposure are presented in Figs. 9 and 10, highlighting the crystalline phases present in the matrices. The primary phases identified include quartz, mullite, hematite, and calcite. Semi-quantitative analysis, shown in Fig. 11, further indicates the relative proportions of these crystalline phases along with a significant internal amorphous fraction, corresponding to the reactive geopolymer gel. The results demonstrate that the crystalline phase composition evolves slightly over time, while the amorphous content remains substantial, reflecting ongoing geopolymerization indicating that no clear crystalline sulphate phases were detected within the resolution limits of XRD, suggesting relatively stable phase composition under the tested conditions of sulphate exposure.

Effect of waste clay brick powder substitution

At 28 days, the quartz content increased progressively from 24.15% in the CM to 35.2% in W60, reflecting the high quartz content inherent in WCBP. Mullite content showed a slight decrease from 12.32% in CM to 9.99% in W60, which is likely due to dilution of fly ash by WCBP, which contains less mullite. Hematite content remained low across all mixes at 28 days (0.26–0.4%), while calcite decreased slightly with higher WCBP content. The internal amorphous content remained high (42.75–51.2%), consistent with early-stage geopolymer gel formation.

Phase evolution over time

After 180 days, several notable changes occurred in the phase composition. Quartz content increased slightly for lower WCBP contents (from 24.6% in CM to 31.6% in W40) but remained almost constant in W60 at 35%. Mullite content increased across all mixes (11.4 to 14.5%), indicating progressive stabilization or slow crystallization during the curing period. Hematite content increased markedly (2.6–3.7%), which is likely due to oxidation of iron-containing species within the WCBP and fly ash. Interestingly, anorthite was not detected at 180 days in any mix, suggesting its partial dissolution, transformation, or incorporation into other aluminosilicate phases over long-term curing. Calcite content increased slightly (2.8–3.8%), indicating minor carbonation during the sulphate exposure.

The internal amorphous content decreased significantly, particularly in high WCBP mixes (from 42.75% in W60 at 28 days to 33% at 180 days), reflecting continued geopolymerization and conversion of reactive gel into more stable crystalline phases. However, the reduction in amorphous content, particularly in W60, may also partially reflect localized depolymerization or dissolution of the geopolymer gel under prolonged sulphate exposure. This interpretation is consistent with the SEM–EDS results presented in section “Scanning electron micrograph with elemental analysis”, which show no evidence of sulphate-related crystalline products or significant chemical degradation.

Implications for mechanical properties and durability

The observed phase evolution indicates that WCBP content significantly modifies the long-term microstructure of geopolymer mortars. Increases in quartz and mullite content over time contribute to the structural framework and mechanical reinforcement. The diminishing presence of anorthite by 180 days suggests partial transformation into more stable aluminosilicate or amorphous-crystalline hybrid phases, which is likely to support the matrix densification and long-term strength retention. Concurrently, the reduction in amorphous content reflects ongoing geopolymerization, leading to a denser gel network and enhanced chemical stability, improving resistance to sulphate attack.

The compressive strength trends (section “ Compressive strength evolution under sulphate exposure”) correlate closely with these XRD observations. The superior performance of W40 can be attributed to an optimal balance between reactive amorphous phases and stabilizing crystalline phases, yielding a dense and durable matrix. In contrast, CM and W20, with higher initial amorphous content and lower proportions of reinforcing crystalline phases, achieved rapid early strength but suffered greater long-term degradation. Mixture W60, despite slower early strength development, demonstrated more gradual strength loss over time, reflecting the stabilizing influence of its higher crystalline content. Collectively, these results indicate that the interplay between amorphous and crystalline components governs both strength development and long-term performance of WCBP-based geopolymer mortars under sulphate exposure. Similar XRD phase evolution has been reported in fly ash and blended systems (e.g., fly ash–calcined clay geopolymers), where quartz and mullite remain as stable crystalline phases, amorphous gel densifies over time, and minor phase transformations occur during long-term curing, supporting enhanced strength and durability57,58.

Cross-sectional SEM-EDS images (Fig. 17) illustrate the internal microstructure of all samples after 180 days. The matrix remains compact with evenly distributed geopolymer gel, and minor microcracks are present. No significant deterioration or new phase formation is observed, indicating no severe microstructural deterioration within SEM resolution. Surface SEM images of CM (Fig. 18) and W40 (Fig. 19) further reveal detailed morphology: the CM sample retains a relatively porous surface, whereas W40 appears comparatively denser with reduced visible porosity, reflecting the beneficial effect of higher WCBP content.

The observed differences in surface porosity between CM and W40 are directly related to their macroscopic performance. The denser and less porous microstructure of W40 indicates a more continuous geopolymer gel network with reduced pore connectivity, which contributes to improved compressive strength retention and enhanced resistance to sulphate-induced expansion. In contrast, the relatively porous structure of the control mix suggests higher permeability and a less compact gel matrix, which facilitates greater ingress of sulphate ions and results in comparatively lower durability performance. These observations show a strong correlation between microstructural densification and the long-term mechanical and dimensional stability of geopolymer mortars. It is noted that though the microstructural compactness is qualitative, it supports the corresponding trends in compressive strength and expansion behaviour.

Overall, the microstructural analysis demonstrates that the geopolymer mortars maintain their internal and surface integrity over time. At 28 days, no significant changes were observed, while at 180 days, the internal matrix remains compact, W40 shows improved surface compactness, and only a very low sulphur signal is detected in EDS mapping. These observations are consistent with previous studies, where geopolymer mortars exposed to sulphate solutions exhibited dense gel networks and stable crystalline phases (quartz, mullite) that limited sulphate ingress, minimized microcracking, and prevented formation of expansive products, thereby preserving microstructural integrity and strength59,60. No new phases or chemical compounds are formed, suggesting relatively stable microstructural integrity under the tested conditions. Although quantitative pore structure characterization was not performed, the combined trends in mass change, expansion behaviour, compressive strength retention, and SEM observations collectively suggest differences in matrix compactness and transport pathways among the mixes. Future studies are recommended to include a quantitative pore structure characterization to enable direct validation of transport-related mechanisms.

In conclusion, geopolymer mortars containing WCBP, particularly at the rates of 20–40%, exhibited sound durability and structural stability under 180 days sodium sulphate exposure, providing a promising route for sustainable construction materials that combine waste valorisation with enhanced durability under the test conditions of sulphate environment. This study is limited to sodium sulphate (Na₂SO₄) exposure. It is acknowledged that a set of parallel water-immersed control specimens would help isolate the sulphate-induced effects from any effect of water on ongoing geopolymerisation. Future research may consider other aggressive environments, including magnesium sulphate and mixed sulphate systems, to better represent field conditions and improve the generalisability of sulphate resistance. Although the study considers exposure up to 180 days, longer-term investigations may be required to fully capture degradation mechanisms relevant to field conditions. In addition, a comparison with parallel water-exposed samples would comprehend the differentiation of sulphate-induced effects from natural strength and microstructural development over time. Furthermore, a quantitative pore structure characterization would benefit the direct validation of transport-related mechanisms discussed in this study.
