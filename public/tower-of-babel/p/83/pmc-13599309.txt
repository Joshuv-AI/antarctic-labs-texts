Interpretable SHAP-based machine learning framework for patient satisfaction prediction: a case study in Thammasat University Hospital
Authors: Tanatorn Tanantong, Warut Pannakkong, Nittaya Chemkomnerd, Prachya Boonkwan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient satisfaction is a critical indicator of healthcare service quality, particularly in specialized outpatient departments where service processes are complex and resources are constrained. This study aims to develop an explainable artificial intelligence framework to predict patient satisfaction and to identify the key factors influencing satisfaction in a public ophthalmology outpatient department.MethodsSurvey data were collected and patient satisfaction was transformed into both binary and three-class settings. Multiple machine learning models, including Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, Extreme Gradient Boosting, Categorical Boosting, and Light Gradient Boosting, were evaluated using a nested stratified cross-validation with 5 outer folds and 5 inner GridSearchCV folds. To address class imbalance, SMOTE-NC and G-SMOTENC were applied only within the training folds. Model performance was assessed to identify the best-performing predictive approach, which was subsequently analyzed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsAmong the evaluated models, Gradient Boosting with G-SMOTENC achieved the highest performance in the binary setting, while Random Forest performed best in the three-class setting. SHAP analysis showed that length of stay contributed most strongly to satisfaction predictions in both settings, followed by demographic and visit-related variables.ConclusionThe results suggest that combining predictive modeling with SHAP-based interpretation can support transparent, context-specific assessment of patient satisfaction in the studied outpatient setting. The proposed approach provides interpretable, context-specific insights that may support data-driven resource management and future service improvement in specialized outpatient settings.Keywords: Patient satisfaction, Explainable artificial intelligence, Machine learning, Ophthalmology outpatient department, Decision support systems

Introduction

Hospitals are essential components of public service systems in every society, as illness and functional decline are inevitable human experiences [1]. Within hospital systems, outpatient departments (OPDs), particularly those providing specialized care, are inherently complex, as they must manage diverse patient conditions, varying service pathways, and highly specialized resources. This complexity makes performance assessment and operational planning in specialty OPDs especially challenging, as traditional efficiency measures often fail to capture patient experience and service heterogeneity. Among specialized OPDs, the ophthalmology department plays a crucial role in maintaining visual health across all age groups [2]. However, many ophthalmology department, especially in public hospitals, face significant resource constraints, including shortages of ophthalmologists, dependence on highly specialized equipment, and limitations in service space and operating hours [3–5]. In addition, population aging further intensifies these challenges, as advanced age is a major risk factor for sight-threatening diseases, resulting in a growing number of patients with visual impairment and increasing demand for specialized ophthalmic services [6]. These pressures are further worsen during public health crises, such as pandemics, when resource availability, service capacity, and care pathways are significantly disrupted, making service management more complex and directly affecting patient satisfaction [7]. Consequently, there is an urgent need for advanced analytical tools and intelligent systems that can effectively evaluate patient satisfaction and support informed resource allocation decisions under conditions of both demographic change and operational uncertainty, particularly in the context of Thailand’s rapidly aging population.

In the context of healthcare delivery, patient satisfaction is a vital indicator of service quality, institutional trust, and overall patient experience [8]. Satisfaction not only influences treatment adherence but also affects the future utilization of healthcare services. In ophthalmology departments, which serve diverse patient groups in large numbers, understanding satisfaction determinants is fundamental to improving care quality and operational sustainability [9, 10]. As healthcare environments change, survey-based evaluations often do not capture the dynamics in real time, leading to delayed or suboptimal managerial responses [11, 12]. Several existing studies have examined patient satisfaction within the context of hospital resource management using traditional statistical techniques, such as regression-based and survey-driven analytical methods, to quantify the relationship between service factors and satisfaction outcomes [13–15]. In these circumstances, the ability to predict patient satisfaction becomes essential. Although numerous studies have employed traditional statistical techniques to examine relationships between service factors and patient satisfaction, such approaches are often limited in their ability to capture the complex and nonlinear interactions inherent in real healthcare systems [16, 17]. In addition, regression-based models tend to lack flexibility when facing contextual shifts, such as demographic changes associated with an aging society or sudden resource constraints during pandemics, both of which can substantially alter service dynamics. These limitations restrict the usefulness of conventional methods for anticipating patient satisfaction under rapidly changing and uncertain conditions.

In response to these challenges, artificial intelligence (AI) has been increasingly applied in healthcare analytics due to its strong capability to model nonlinear relationships and interactions that traditional approaches fail to capture [18]. Recent studies have summarized the development of AI techniques and current trends in their application across different domains [19], while analytical reviews of machine learning techniques have highlighted the strengths of different algorithms for data-driven prediction tasks [20]. AI-based predictive models have been widely used to improve operational performance and service quality by forecasting outcomes such as waiting time, length of stay, clinical risks, and patient satisfaction [21, 22]. For example, deep learning models have been successfully applied to skin disease classification, supporting dermatologists by accurately predicting disease types from medical images while accounting for computational and resource constraints in clinical practice [23]. These advances demonstrate the growing potential of AI to support healthcare decision-making across diverse clinical and operational contexts. However, while AI enables more accurate prediction of healthcare outcomes, predictive accuracy alone is insufficient for effective service management. Beyond knowing satisfaction levels in advance, hospital administrators must also understand which underlying factors drive patient satisfaction, as patient perceptions are influenced by a combination of operational, demographic, and experiential factors. Consequently, there remains a research gap in predictive approaches that not only estimate patient satisfaction under complex and evolving conditions but also provide transparent and interpretable insights to support informed resource allocation and service improvement.

To meet this need, predictive models must be complemented by explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) techniques that make model behavior transparent and interpretable. XAI enables decision-makers to understand how different input variables contribute to predicted outcomes, thereby supporting evidence-based and accountable decision-making [24–26]. Building on this perspective, the present study adopts a multi-model comparative framework to evaluate several state-of-the-art machine learning algorithms commonly used in healthcare analytics [27], including Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Categorical Boosting (CatBoost), and Light Gradient Boosting (LightGBM). After systematically comparing model performance, the best-performing model is selected for predicting patient satisfaction in the outpatient setting.

To further address the limitations of black-box prediction, the selected model is analyzed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), which provide both global and local interpretations of model predictions. SHAP enables the identification of key operational and patient-related factors that influence satisfaction outcomes. By integrating predictive modeling with explainable analytics, this research demonstrates the practical use of established machine learning and SHAP-based interpretation for patient satisfaction analysis in a real-world outpatient context. The proposed applied ML–XAI workflow may help hospitals identify important drivers of patient satisfaction and support evidence-informed operational decision-making. Rather than proposing a new machine learning or explainability method, this study contributes a context-specific case study showing how interpretable predictive modeling can be applied to support healthcare operations in a public ophthalmology outpatient department. This practical focus is relevant to hospitals operating under resource constraints and supports broader objectives of patient-centered and sustainable healthcare delivery aligned with Sustainable Development Goal (SDG) 3: Good Health and Well-Being and SDG 10: Reduced Inequalities.

Literature review

Predicting patient satisfaction

Patient satisfaction is a multidimensional concept deeply influenced by both the efficiency and fairness of healthcare resource allocation. Numerous studies have emphasized that equitable distribution of medical resources directly enhances patient satisfaction and health outcomes. For example, redistribution of medical resources from tertiary hospitals to lower-tier facilities has been shown to improve patient satisfaction by increasing access to quality care and reducing treatment costs [28]. Equitable and efficient allocation of medical resources, including staff–patient assignments, has been achieved using mixed-integer linear programming models, leading to improved care quality and enhanced patient satisfaction [29].

Beyond regional analyses, Predictive management frameworks have been developed to align hospital operations with patient demand, enabling a balance between service efficiency and operational cost [30]. Machine-learning-based scheduling approaches have been applied to improve fairness in appointment allocation, contributing to higher patient satisfaction through more timely and equitable service delivery [31]. In addition, the integration of forecasting and optimization models has been used to synchronize hospital capacity with fluctuating patient demand, alleviating overcrowding and improving overall satisfaction levels [32]. Personalized communication systems between physicians and patients further strengthen trust and service quality [33, 34].

From a conceptual perspective, prior studies have shown that patient satisfaction is a multidimensional construct, shaped by a combination of clinical, operational, and contextual factors rather than a single aspect of care. Commonly identified determinants include service quality, patient–provider interaction, treatment outcomes, operational efficiency, patient-centered care, and external influences such as socioeconomic context [16, 35, 36]. In outpatient settings, factors related to operational efficiency, particularly waiting time and length of stay, are repeatedly reported as dominant drivers of patient satisfaction, as they directly affect patient experience and perceived service responsiveness [16, 37]. At the same time, demographic characteristics and visit-related attributes can moderate satisfaction perceptions, leading to heterogeneous responses across patient groups [36, 38].

Most existing studies rely on descriptive analyses or regression-based models that focus on identifying associations rather than enabling forward-looking prediction. Such approaches offer limited support for proactive service planning, particularly in complex outpatient environments where demand, resources, and patient composition change dynamically. Moreover, few studies simultaneously address predictive accuracy and interpretability, leaving hospital managers without clear guidance on which operational levers should be prioritized to improve satisfaction. Consequently, there remains a research gap in methodologies that can not only predict patient satisfaction under realistic service conditions, but also transparently identify the most influential operational and patient-related factors, enabling actionable and evidence-based decision-making in outpatient healthcare systems.

Artificial intelligence in healthcare prediction

The increasing complexity of healthcare systems and the rapidly growing volume of operational data have accelerated the adoption of AI for predictive analytics. These techniques allow hospitals to model nonlinear interactions, uncover hidden patient behavior patterns, and generate operational insights not easily captured by traditional statistical approaches [39]. Recent studies demonstrate that AI has been widely applied across healthcare domains to enhance prediction, monitoring, and decision-making under complex and uncertain conditions. AI-based predictive models have been used to forecast hospitalization rates during epidemics such as COVID-19, supporting healthcare sustainability by enabling early planning under limited data availability and rapidly changing external factors, including policies, locations, and public sentiment [40]. Beyond epidemic forecasting, AI has been increasingly integrated into clinical practice through intelligent diagnostic systems, medical imaging, robotic-assisted surgery, and continuous patient monitoring, contributing to improved efficiency, reduced clinical risk, and support for healthcare professionals facing increasing workloads [41, 42]. In emergency and real-time care contexts, AI combined with Internet of Things technologies has enabled rapid accident detection, false alarm reduction, and automated rescue coordination, demonstrating its potential to improve response time and patient outcomes in time-critical situations [43, 44]. AI has also shown promise in disease diagnosis and classification, particularly in resource-constrained settings where annotated medical datasets are scarce [45–47]. Furthermore, machine learning methods have been applied to analyze large-scale behavioral and sensor data, enabling real-time interpretation of complex health signals and supporting clinical decision-making [48, 49]. In ophthalmology, machine learning has been widely applied to diagnostic and image-based tasks, particularly for glaucoma detection and retinal fundus image classification. For example, prior studies have developed automated glaucoma classification frameworks using retinal fundus images [50] and evaluated machine learning techniques for glaucoma detection based on textural and intensity features [51]. These studies demonstrate the growing role of AI in ophthalmology; however, most existing work focuses on clinical diagnosis or image interpretation, while fewer studies examine patient satisfaction and outpatient service experience.

The literature has explored the use of AI models to predict patient satisfaction. Direct satisfaction labels are often difficult to collect in real time. Therefore, many researchers rely on implicit operational indicators, such as waiting time, LOS, and visit duration. For example, supervised learning approaches have been applied to predict waiting times, showing that reductions in waiting time can indirectly improve patient satisfaction [52]. Gradient Boosting and Random Forest models have been employed to minimize length of stay, based on the assumption that shorter service durations correspond to improved patient experience [53]. A multi-modal predictive framework combining structured data and unstructured clinical text has been introduced to forecast emergency department outcomes within 24 hours, demonstrating the potential of AI to support short-term operational planning [54]. In addition, patient satisfaction has been explicitly modeled by capturing key determinants of patient experience, illustrating that AI techniques are capable of learning and predicting complex subjective outcomes [55].

Within the field of predictive analytics, several AI models have gained attraction due to their ability to handle nonlinear relationships, large feature spaces, and data imbalance. Ensemble tree-based methods such as Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, LightGBM, and CatBoost have been widely applied in healthcare due to their robustness and flexibility. XGBoost, introduced by Chen et al. [56], has become a benchmark model across industries because of its scalability and regularized boosting structure. Its variants, LightGBM and CatBoost, further improve training speed, memory efficiency, and handling of categorical variables—making them ideal for mixed operational–survey datasets. For instance, XGBoost has been applied to predict emergency department overcrowding [57], while gradient boosting techniques have been used to model outpatient satisfaction [58]. These studies show that boosted-tree algorithms often outperform traditional ML approaches.

A recurring challenge across all these models is class imbalance, especially in satisfaction studies where dissatisfied cases may represent a minority. Techniques such as Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) have been used to balance training data and improve model sensitivity, but results vary [59]. While some studies report performance gains, others observe reduced generalizability after oversampling. The importance of evaluating classification models using metrics such as the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC), which more accurately reflects performance under class imbalance than accuracy or F1-score, has been emphasized [60].

Despite the advances of AI in healthcare prediction, clinical adoption remains limited due to the black-box nature of many algorithms. As a response, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) has emerged as a critical research area enabling transparent and accountable prediction systems. SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), introduced by Lundberg et al. [61], is now one of the most widely used interpretability frameworks. SHAP uniquely quantifies the contribution of each input feature to a model’s prediction using cooperative game theory, supporting both global (population-level) and local (case-level) explanations. Several healthcare studies, such as heart failure risk prediction [62, 63], and hospital readmission analysis [64, 65], demonstrate SHAP’s value in providing actionable insights that align with clinical understanding. Outside healthcare, the interpretability of SHAP has improved policy trust in domains, reinforcing its generalizability and practical relevance. However, despite its strengths, few studies integrate SHAP into patient satisfaction evaluation frameworks. Most XAI applications interpret predictions derived from retrospective datasets only, without connecting those insights to dynamic policy changes such as adjusting physician allocation, modifying scheduling rules, or evaluating crisis scenarios. The lack of integration between satisfaction factors, AI prediction, and XAI represents a major gap in current healthcare analytics research.

Methods

This study develops an explainable machine learning framework for predicting patient satisfaction using exclusively survey-based data collected from actual outpatient encounters. The overall research methodology is illustrated in Fig. 1. The process begins with data collection, followed by data preprocessing to prepare survey variables for analysis. The cleaned dataset is then evaluated using a nested stratified cross-validation framework, where the outer folds are used for performance evaluation and the inner folds for model tuning. Both binary (2-class) and multi-class (3-class) formulations of patient satisfaction are considered. To address class imbalance, models are trained and compared under multiple configurations, including the original imbalanced data and balanced data generated using SMOTE-NC and G-SMOTENC. Multiple AI models are subsequently developed and evaluated, and their predictive performance is compared using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC). Based on this comparison, the best-performing model is selected and further analyzed using SHAP-based explainable AI, leading to the identification of key factors influencing patient satisfaction. Each stage is described in detail in the following subsections.

Framework of the research

Data collection

All input data used in this study were obtained from a structured patient survey administered in the Ophthalmology Outpatient Department of Thammasat University Hospital as part of our previous research [14]. The survey was conducted over a one-month data collection period and yielded a total of 464 patient responses. It was designed to capture essential predictors of patient experience and included four major types of information: patient demographics, symptom complexity, LOS, and patient satisfaction scores.

Demographic variables covered age, gender, and visit type. Symptom-related items asked patients to report the names or descriptions of their eye symptoms. Because some reported symptoms were highly specific and occurred infrequently, they were categorized into three broader symptom-complexity levels based on the classification system described by [66]: Easy, Varied, and Hard. The Easy category refers to symptoms that are not severe and may recover without intensive treatment, while the Hard category refers to severe symptoms that require immediate treatment. The Varied category includes symptoms whose severity and treatment requirements depend on the underlying cause. For example, an eye stye was categorized as Easy because it typically resolves by itself within approximately two weeks [67]. In contrast, glaucoma was categorized as Hard because untreated cases can lead to vision loss [67]. LOS was recorded in minutes from registration to completion of care and served as one of the primary operational predictors in the dataset. The outcome variable, patient satisfaction, was measured on a five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied.

The questionnaire used in this study was developed to collect patient characteristics, service-related information, symptom complexity, LOS, and patient satisfaction outcomes relevant to the ophthalmology outpatient context. The questionnaire items were reviewed by experts to assess their alignment with the study objectives using the Index of Item-Objective Congruence (IOC). The instrument passed the IOC assessment before data collection, indicating that the items were considered appropriate for measuring the intended constructs. In addition, the study protocol and questionnaire were reviewed and approved by the relevant ethics committee before data collection. Participation in the survey was voluntary, and all responses were anonymized before analysis. During the survey process, the research team provided explanations or assistance when necessary, particularly because some ophthalmology patients had vision-related difficulties that limited their ability to complete the questionnaire independently. The research team provided only clarification or reading assistance and did not guide or influence patients’ answers.

Data collection in the ophthalmology outpatient department was practically challenging because many patients experienced vision-related problems and could not complete the questionnaire independently. Therefore, the research team provided explanations and assistance during questionnaire completion when necessary. This process increased the time required for each response and limited the number of responses that could be collected during the one-month data collection period. The department serves approximately 300 patients per day, corresponding to approximately 6,000 patients per month. Based on the finite population sample size calculation at a 95% confidence level and a 5% margin of error [68], the required sample size is approximately 361 responses for a population of 6,000. Although the final analytic sample of 342 patients after excluding neutral responses is slightly below this threshold, it remains close to the recommended sample size for survey-based estimation.

Preprocessing

Before model development, the survey dataset underwent a structured preprocessing procedure to ensure consistency and analytical rigor. Input features were selected based on their relevance to patient satisfaction in the ophthalmology outpatient context and their availability from the survey data. The final predictors included demographic characteristics, visit-related variables, symptom complexity, and LOS. These variables were retained because they are clinically and operationally meaningful for understanding patient satisfaction in outpatient service delivery. All categorical variables, such as gender, visit type, and symptom category, were transformed using one-hot encoding to make them compatible with the machine learning algorithms applied in the study [69]. Numerical variables were examined for completeness, and missing values in the LOS variable were imputed using the median, a method chosen to minimize the influence of extreme observations while preserving the central tendency of service durations. No dimensionality reduction technique was applied because the number of predictors was limited and preserving feature interpretability was important for the subsequent SHAP analysis. In addition, feature removal based on multicollinearity was not performed because the models used in this study were tree-based ensemble methods, which are generally less sensitive to correlated predictors than linear models.

The primary outcome of interest, patient satisfaction, was originally measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = very dissatisfied to 5 = very satisfied. In the primary binary classification analysis, the satisfaction scores were recoded into two classes to distinguish clearly dissatisfied and satisfied patients. Specifically, scores of 1 and 2 were recoded as “not satisfied,” while scores of 4 and 5 were recoded as “satisfied.” Responses with a score of 3, representing a neutral or indifferent experience, were excluded from the binary classification dataset to reduce ambiguity in the target variable and ensure a clearly defined binary learning task. This binary formulation has been used in prior satisfaction modeling studies because it can simplify interpretation and support clearer managerial decision-making [70].

However, neutral responses may represent a meaningful middle group in satisfaction research and may be important for hospital administrators, as these patients may be more responsive to service improvement efforts. Excluding this group may reduce the representativeness of the analytic sample and may artificially sharpen the distinction between satisfaction classes. Therefore, to address this concern and evaluate the robustness of the binary classification approach, this study also conducted an additional three-class sensitivity analysis by retaining the neutral group. In the three-class setting, scores of 1 and 2 were classified as “not satisfied,” score 3 was classified as “neutral/uncertain,” and scores of 4 and 5 were classified as “satisfied.” Accordingly, this study presents both the binary classification and the three-class sensitivity analysis, allowing comparison between a polarized satisfaction prediction setting and a more inclusive classification setting that better reflects the full patient population.

Model development

The study employed five machine learning algorithms to develop prediction models for patient satisfaction: Random Forest (RF), Gradient Boosting (GB), Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM), and Categorical Boosting (CatBoost). These algorithms were selected because they are well established in healthcare analytics, particularly for their ability to model nonlinear relationships, handle heterogeneous survey data, and capture complex interaction effects among predictors [71]. Each model was trained using the same set of features derived from the survey dataset, ensuring consistency across experimental conditions and enabling a fair comparison of algorithmic performance.

To improve the robustness of model evaluation, this study adopted a nested cross-validation framework. The outer loop used five-fold stratified cross-validation to evaluate model performance, while the inner loop used five-fold stratified GridSearchCV for hyperparameter tuning. In this design, hyperparameter selection was conducted only within the training portion of each outer fold, and the selected model was then evaluated on the corresponding unseen outer validation fold. This procedure helps reduce optimistic bias in model evaluation and provides a more reliable estimate of model performance under limited sample conditions.

To ensure optimal performance for each machine learning model, a systematic hyperparameter tuning procedure was conducted using grid search within the inner cross-validation loop. The grid search explored a predefined range of values for key hyperparameters relevant to each algorithm. The complete set of hyperparameter ranges evaluated for each model is presented in Tables 1, 2, 3, 4 and 5, which summarizes all candidate values used during the tuning process. Final model performance was reported as the average across the five outer folds, together with variability estimates, to reflect the stability of each model across different validation subsets.

Data imbalance handling

The patient satisfaction dataset exhibited class imbalance between the “satisfy” and “not satisfy” categories, which can bias machine learning models toward the majority class and lead to misleading performance evaluation [72]. To address this issue and to examine the robustness of model performance under different oversampling strategies, two oversampling approaches were implemented using SMOTE-NC (SMOTE for Nominal and Continuous) and G-SMOTENC (Geometric SMOTE-NC), which is appropriate for datasets containing both categorical and continuous variables [73].

In the first approach, SMOTE-NC was applied only to the training portion of each outer cross-validation fold, while the corresponding outer validation fold remained unchanged. The minority class in the training set was synthetically oversampled until both classes reached the same level, while the test dataset remained unchanged [74]. This strategy preserves the original data distribution in the test set and reflects real-world deployment conditions, allowing model evaluation on unbiased data.

In the second approach, G-SMOTENC was applied as an alternative training-data oversampling method to address class imbalance. G-SMOTENC was selected because the original study [75] proposing this method reported that it can improve upon conventional SMOTE-NC for datasets containing both categorical and continuous variables by generating synthetic samples in a more geometrically informed manner. This configuration allowed the study to examine whether an alternative mixed-data oversampling strategy could improve minority-class learning while maintaining a valid evaluation protocol based on unseen, naturally distributed validation data.

Model comparison

To identify the most reliable prediction model for patient satisfaction, all trained machine learning models were systematically compared using the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) as the primary evaluation metric. MCC was selected because it provides a balanced assessment of classification performance by simultaneously considering true positives, true negatives, false positives, and false negatives. Unlike accuracy or F1-score, which can be misleading in the presence of class imbalance, MCC remains informative even when the class distribution is skewed, making it particularly suitable for healthcare satisfaction data [76].

Each model was evaluated under three conditions: original imbalanced training data, SMOTE-NC applied only within training folds, and G-SMOTENC applied only within training folds. No oversampling was applied to the outer validation folds. Performance was reported as mean ± SD across the five outer folds. The model with the highest mean MCC across outer validation folds was selected for SHAP analysis.

Explainable AI interpretability

After identifying the optimal prediction model based on the MCC, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were employed to interpret the model’s predictions and to identify the most influential attributes affecting patient satisfaction. SHAP is an explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) technique grounded in cooperative game theory that assigns each feature a contribution value representing its impact on the model’s output [77, 78]. By decomposing individual predictions into additive feature effects, SHAP enables both global and local interpretability while preserving consistency with the underlying predictive model.

In this study, SHAP analysis was applied exclusively to the best-performing model to ensure that interpretability was derived from the most reliable classifier. Global SHAP importance values were used to rank input attribute, such as patient demographics, symptom severity, and LOS, according to their overall influence on satisfaction predictions across the dataset. In addition, SHAP summary plots were generated to examine the directionality of each attribute’s effect, revealing whether higher or lower feature values increased the likelihood of patient satisfaction. The resulting explanations were assessed in terms of faithfulness to the predictive model and consistency with established healthcare knowledge. SHAP explanations are grounded in game-theoretic principles and satisfy properties such as local accuracy and consistency, providing theoretical guarantees for explanation reliability.

Statistical results of variables

Following the data cleaning process, the final dataset reflected an aging outpatient population, with a substantial proportion of older adults. Patients aged 60 years and above constituted the largest group (30.6%), followed by those aged 45–59 years (29.2%) and 30–44 years (23.3%), while younger patients aged 18–29 years accounted for 16.9% of the sample. This age distribution highlights the increasing demand for ophthalmic services among older populations, consistent with demographic aging trends. The gender distribution was slightly skewed toward females (57%) compared with males (43%).

Most respondents had prior experience with the hospital, with 88.6% previously treated at Thammasat University Hospital and 79.5% having prior visits to the ophthalmology department. In addition, 82.7% of patients were scheduled appointments rather than walk-in cases. Symptom severity was relatively evenly distributed across categories, with 34.2% classified as easy, 34.9% as varied, and 30.9% as hard, indicating a balanced representation of clinical complexity.

With respect to the target variable, the cleaned dataset exhibited a moderate class imbalance, with 71.9% of patients classified as “satisfy” and 28.1% as “not satisfy.” This imbalance reflects real-world outpatient satisfaction patterns and motivated the subsequent application of oversampling techniques and MCC-based model evaluation. Overall, the cleaned dataset provides a realistic and well-structured foundation for analyzing patient satisfaction using machine learning methods.

Experimental setting

All experiments were conducted under a consistent and controlled experimental setting to ensure fair comparison across models. After data cleaning and preprocessing, model evaluation was performed using a nested cross-validation framework. Specifically, a five-fold stratified cross-validation procedure was used as the outer loop to estimate model performance, while a five-fold stratified GridSearchCV procedure was used as the inner loop for hyperparameter tuning. In each outer fold, hyperparameter tuning was conducted only on the training portion, and the optimized model was then evaluated on the corresponding unseen validation fold. Final performance was reported as the mean and standard deviation across the five outer folds.

All machine learning models were implemented in Python, using widely adopted open-source libraries to ensure reproducibility. Tree-based models, including Random Forest, Gradient Boosting, XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM, were developed using the scikit-learn, XGBoost, CatBoost, and LightGBM libraries. The imbalanced-learn library was used to implement SMOTE-NC and G-SMOTENC for handling class imbalance. Model training, hyperparameter tuning via grid search, and performance evaluation were conducted within this unified Python environment.

Model performance

This section presents the performance of the evaluated machine learning models for predicting patient satisfaction under both binary (2-class) and multi-class (3-class) settings. Model evaluation was conducted using nested stratified cross-validation, and the reported results represent the mean and standard deviation across the five outer folds to ensure robustness and reduce evaluation bias. Performance was assessed using MCC on the test sets, given its suitability for imbalanced data. The comparison includes results obtained from the original dataset as well as configurations incorporating SMOTE-NC and G-SMOTENC.

Table 7 presents the performance comparison of the five machine learning models for binary patient satisfaction prediction under the original, SMOTE-NC, and G-SMOTENC conditions. Overall, the models demonstrated comparable predictive performance, and the ANOVA results indicated no statistically significant difference among model performances. However, because SHAP analysis requires a single final model for interpretation, the model selection was based on the highest mean cross-validated MCC across the outer validation folds. To interpret the predictions of the selected Gradient Boosting with the G-SMOTENC binary model, SHAP was applied to quantify the contribution of each input attribute to the classification of patient satisfaction. SHAP analysis provides both global and local explanations, allowing the model’s use of survey-based factors to be examined when classifying patients as “satisfy” or “not satisfy.” In the SHAP summary plot, features are ranked according to their mean absolute SHAP values, and the direction of each feature’s contribution is shown by the distribution of SHAP values across individual observations.

In Fig. 6, the SHAP results show that LOS had the highest SHAP importance and contributed most strongly to the model’s predictions. Higher LOS values were associated with negative SHAP values, corresponding to lower predicted satisfaction, while lower LOS values were associated with positive SHAP values, corresponding to higher predicted satisfaction. The SHAP global feature importance bar chart further confirms that LOS ranked highest by a substantial margin, whereas gender, age range, visit-related variables, symptom level, and appointment status showed smaller average SHAP values.

Discussion

Class imbalance handling under limited survey data

This study evaluated multiple ensemble-based machine learning models with and without the application of SMOTE-based techniques (SMOTE-NC and G-SMOTENC) to address class imbalance in patient satisfaction data. In the binary classification setting, the Gradient Boosting model with G-SMOTENC achieved the highest MCC, indicating that oversampling can provide performance gains in specific model configurations. However, these improvements were relatively small and not statistically significant when compared across models, and similar gains were not consistently observed in other algorithms. In contrast, in the multi-class setting, oversampling generally resulted in reduced performance. These findings suggest that while oversampling may enhance model performance in certain cases, it does not provide a consistent or robust advantage across different models and problem settings. One possible explanation is that the original dataset already contained sufficient patterns for distinguishing satisfaction classes, and generating synthetic samples in overlapping regions of the feature space may have blurred class boundaries. Furthermore, in structured survey data with limited feature diversity, synthetic observations created by interpolation may not reflect realistic patient profiles, thereby reducing the model’s ability to generalize to unseen data. These findings are consistent with prior studies indicating that oversampling may be less effective when class distributions overlap or when the dataset size is relatively small.

From an operational perspective, survey-based data collection in hospital environments is inherently challenging. In the studied ophthalmology outpatient department, approximately 300 patients are served per day, implying a potential population of nearly 6,000 patients per month. However, practical constraints such as patient availability, response fatigue, ethical considerations, and vision-related difficulties among ophthalmology patients significantly limit the achievable survey sample size. In many cases, patients could not complete the questionnaire independently, and the research team needed to provide explanations or assistance during questionnaire completion. This process increased the time required for each response and limited the number of responses that could be collected during the one-month study period. Previous healthcare machine learning studies have also been conducted using limited real-world clinical datasets, reflecting the practical challenges of collecting patient-level data in clinical settings [79, 80]. After excluding neutral responses, the final analytic sample included 342 patients. Based on a standard finite population sample size calculation, this sample size is close to the recommended sample size for survey-based estimation from a monthly patient population of approximately 6,000. However, it remains limited for training and comparing multiple machine learning models.

In this context, SMOTE-based methods can still be viewed as exploratory tools for assessing how models behave under balanced class conditions, rather than as techniques that inherently improve predictive performance. The results suggest that oversampling was less effective because it replicated or interpolated existing patterns without increasing the diversity of informative samples, particularly for categorical features handled by SMOTE-NC. In addition, for the multi-class setting, generating synthetic samples for intermediate classes (e.g., “uncertain”) may have further increased ambiguity in class representation, making it more difficult for the model to learn distinct decision boundaries. As a result, the benefits of balancing class frequencies were offset by reduced data quality and increased noise. These findings highlight that oversampling should be applied cautiously in healthcare survey datasets, where data are limited and feature relationships are complex, and reinforce the importance of collecting larger and more representative datasets to support robust model development.

Managerial interpretation

Beyond predictive accuracy, the applied value of this study lies in using SHAP to translate model outputs into interpretable managerial insights within the studied outpatient context. Rahman et al. [81] addressed a research problem similar to this work, as it also applied machine learning models combined with explainable techniques to predict and interpret patient satisfaction outcomes in a hospital setting. While their study identified LightGBM as the optimal model in a private-hospital context in Bangladesh, the present study selected Gradient Boosting with G-SMOTENC as the representative model in the binary setting and Random Forest as the representative model in the three-class setting. This difference is not contradictory but instead reflects variations in datasets, service environments, and explanatory variables. Specifically, Rahman et al. incorporated factors such as treatment plans, medication explanations, and physician–patient interactions, whereas this study focused on operational and demographic variables relevant to a public ophthalmology outpatient department. These contextual and structural differences naturally lead to different optimal model choices, while still suggesting that explainable machine learning can be useful for context-specific patient satisfaction analysis. Therefore, the predictive model and SHAP patterns obtained in this study may be most applicable to similar large public hospital OPDs with comparable service structures, patient volumes, and resource constraints. For other hospitals, departments, or specialties, the generalizable contribution is primarily the methodological workflow rather than the trained model itself; external validation and local recalibration would be required before implementation in different settings.

Despite the variation in best-performing algorithms, both studies converge on a critical insight which is operational efficiency strongly influences patient satisfaction. The SHAP analysis in this study clearly identifies LOS as the most influential factor affecting satisfaction predictions, exceeding the impact of demographic and visit-related variables. The role of waiting time as a key driver of patient dissatisfaction has been emphasized in prior studies [81].

From a management perspective, these findings suggest that LOS and related service-flow conditions may be important areas for further operational review. In practice, hospital administrators can use the SHAP results as a screening tool to identify which operational factors should be examined first, then combine these insights with process mapping, queue analysis, staff consultation, or simulation experiments to identify specific bottlenecks and evaluate potential interventions. Resource allocation strategies such as optimizing physician schedules, improving patient flow, and enhancing appointment systems may be relevant options for hospital administrators to investigate, particularly because LOS is an operational indicator that can be monitored and managed in practice. However, the SHAP results should not be interpreted as causal evidence that reducing LOS alone will necessarily improve patient satisfaction. Instead, the applied ML–XAI workflow presented in this study may support hospital administrators in identifying predictive factors that warrant closer examination when planning service improvements. SHAP analysis does not replace causal evaluation, prospective validation, or operational simulation, but it provides an interpretable indication of which variables are most strongly associated with predicted satisfaction outcomes in this dataset. This interpretability may increase the practical usefulness of predictive modeling by helping decision-makers understand why certain outcomes are predicted and by supporting more transparent discussion of potential policy adjustments.

Overall, this study demonstrates that combining established ensemble learning models with SHAP-based interpretation can provide context-specific predictive and explanatory insights for patient satisfaction analysis in the studied public ophthalmology outpatient setting. By identifying LOS as the most important predictor in the trained model, the results suggest that service-flow efficiency is a relevant area for managerial attention in large public OPD settings with similar operational characteristics. However, these findings should be interpreted as model-based associations rather than causal evidence of intervention effects. Moreover, although the proposed workflow can be adapted to other healthcare settings, the generalizability of the trained model to other hospitals or medical specialties remains limited and should be evaluated through external validation using multi-hospital and multi-specialty datasets. Future studies should combine predictive modeling with causal inference, prospective validation, or simulation-based intervention analysis to examine whether changes in LOS or related operational processes lead to measurable improvements in patient satisfaction. Within this applied case-study scope, the study contributes to patient-centered healthcare decision support and aligns with broader health system goals such as SDG 3 (Good Health and Well-Being) and SDG 10 (Reduced Inequalities).

Binary and three-class satisfaction prediction

This study presents both binary and three-class classification results. The binary setting focuses on clearly polarized responses by classifying patients as either “not satisfied” or “satisfied,” while the three-class setting retains the neutral group and classifies patients as “not satisfied,” “neutral/uncertain,” and “satisfied.” This additional analysis provides a more inclusive evaluation of model performance and better reflects the full distribution of patient satisfaction responses.

Overall, the binary classification models achieved higher performance than the three-class models across all evaluation metrics. In the binary setting, the best-performing models generally achieved accuracy values above 0.85, Macro F1 scores around 0.89–0.92, MCC values around 0.65–0.72, and ROC-AUC values above 0.90. In contrast, the three-class models showed lower performance, with accuracy values mostly around 0.66–0.72, Macro F1 scores around 0.60–0.66, MCC values around 0.46–0.54, and ROC-AUC values around 0.81–0.85. This performance reduction is expected because the three-class problem is more complex than the binary problem. By retaining neutral responses, the model must distinguish patients with middle-level satisfaction from both dissatisfied and satisfied patients, which may involve greater overlap in patient characteristics and service experience. The comparison also highlights the different practical meanings of the two prediction settings. The binary model is useful when the objective is to identify clearly dissatisfied and satisfied patients, which can support broad screening or classification of polarized satisfaction outcomes. However, the three-class model provides additional information by retaining patients with neutral or uncertain satisfaction. This group is important from a hospital management perspective because neutral patients may represent a transitional group whose satisfaction could improve or decline depending on service experience and operational changes.

In summary, the binary and three-class analyses should be interpreted as complementary rather than competing approaches. The binary setting provides clearer class separation and stronger predictive performance, while the three-class setting better preserves the structure of the original satisfaction scale and offers a more realistic representation of the full patient population. Therefore, the binary results are useful for understanding polarized satisfaction patterns, whereas the three-class results provide broader insight into the complexity of satisfaction prediction in real outpatient settings.

Conclusions

Patient satisfaction is a critical indicator of healthcare service quality and operational effectiveness, particularly in public hospital outpatient departments where demand is high and resources are limited. Traditional survey-based assessments provide valuable feedback but are often retrospective and lack the ability to support timely, data-driven decision-making. Motivated by these challenges, this study was conducted to explore whether explainable machine learning could be used to predict patient satisfaction and to identify the key operational factors that hospitals can actively manage to improve patient experience.

This study proposed an explainable machine learning framework for predicting patient satisfaction in a public ophthalmology outpatient department using survey-based data. Multiple ensemble learning models were systematically compared under different imbalance-handling strategies, and both binary and three-class satisfaction prediction settings were examined to reflect different formulations of the original Likert-scale outcome. In the binary classification setting, Gradient Boosting with G-SMOTENC achieved the highest MCC and was selected for SHAP analysis, although the ANOVA results indicated no statistically significant performance difference among models. In the three-class setting, Random Forest achieved the highest MCC, but overall performance was lower than in the binary setting, reflecting the greater difficulty of distinguishing “not satisfy,” “uncertain,” and “satisfy” classes.

The results suggest that binary classification provides clearer separation between dissatisfied and satisfied patients, while the three-class setting captures a more nuanced but more challenging representation of satisfaction. SMOTE-based methods showed context-dependent effects. G-SMOTENC improved the best binary model, whereas oversampling did not provide consistent benefits across models and was less effective in the three-class setting. SHAP analysis further showed that LOS contributed most strongly to model predictions in both settings, followed by demographic and visit-related variables. Overall, the findings demonstrate the practical usefulness of combining tabular machine learning with SHAP-based interpretability as a decision-support approach for understanding patient satisfaction in the studied outpatient context.

Despite these contributions, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this study relied on survey-based data collected over a one-month period from a single ophthalmology outpatient department, which constrained the sample size relative to the total outpatient population served by the department. Although the department serves approximately 300 patients per day, corresponding to around 6,000 patients per month, survey data collection was practically challenging because many patients experienced vision-related problems and could not complete the questionnaire independently. Therefore, the research team often needed to provide explanations or assistance during questionnaire completion, increasing the time required for each response. Together with practical constraints such as patient availability, response burden, and ethical considerations, these factors limited the number of completed surveys. Although the final analytic sample was close to the recommended sample size for survey-based estimation, it remains limited for training and comparing multiple machine learning models. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted as preliminary and context-specific rather than broadly generalizable. As a result, while oversampling techniques were explored to assess robustness and visualize system-level patterns, it did not provide consistent predictive benefits across models and settings and may have introduced noise or synthetic bias, particularly in the three-class setting. However, because the present model was developed using data from a single public ophthalmology outpatient department, its performance and explanatory patterns should be interpreted within this specific context. The findings may be relevant to similar large public hospital OPDs with comparable patient volumes, service structures, and resource constraints. Nevertheless, for other hospitals, departments, or specialties, the trained model should not be directly applied without external validation and local recalibration. In this regard, the broader generalizable contribution of this study lies in the applied machine learning and explainable AI workflow rather than the specific trained model.

Future research may extend this work by integrating the proposed ML–XAI framework into simulation-based or digital twin environments and advancing it toward adaptive or real-time satisfaction prediction systems. By coupling satisfaction prediction with discrete-event simulation or digital twins of outpatient operations, hospitals could virtually evaluate alternative resource allocation strategies, such as physician staffing, appointment scheduling, and patient flow redesign, under varying demand scenarios before real-world implementation. In parallel, incorporating near–real-time operational data (e.g., dynamic waiting times or queue conditions) would enable continuous monitoring of patient experience and early detection of emerging dissatisfaction. Future studies should also use larger and more diverse datasets with broader clinical, operational, and service-quality variables. Systematic feature selection, dimensionality reduction, and multicollinearity assessment should also be considered to improve model robustness while preserving interpretability. Together, these extensions would transform the framework from retrospective analysis into a proactive, explainable decision-support system that supports patient-centered, sustainable healthcare management.
