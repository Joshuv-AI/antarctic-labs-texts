Glycine-modulating Slc6a20a-ASO restores NMDA receptor function in SHANK2 and SHANK3-mutant mice and cortical organoids
Authors: Junyeop Daniel Roh, Mihyun Bae, Yusang Oh, Yeji Yang, Suho Lee, Woo-Chang Hwang, Esther Yang, Hyeonji Kim, Hyunjee Jang, Hyung-Wook Choi, Hyun Kim, Jin Young Kim, Eunjoon Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Suppressed NMDA receptor (NMDAR) function contributes to multiple brain disorders, including schizophrenia, autism spectrum disorder (ASD), and NMDAR encephalitis. Previous attempts to restore NMDAR activity by increasing ambient glycine, a critical co-agonist, through GlyT1 inhibition have yielded mixed outcomes, partly due to GlyT1’s extensive expression in essential brainstem regions. Slc6a20a, a glycine transporter widely expressed in cognition-relevant regions such as the cortex and hippocampus, offers a targeted alternative. Here we show that antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)-mediated Slc6a20a inhibition (Slc6a20a-ASO) normalizes ASD-related phenotypes in male Shank2- and Shank3-mutant mice, with model-dependent rescue profiles. Slc6a20a-ASO rescues NMDAR hypofunction and synaptic phospho-proteomic profiles in the prefrontal cortex. Furthermore, ASO targeting human SLC6A20 rescues suppressed NMDAR function in cortical organoids harboring SHANK2 or SHANK3 mutations. These findings underscore the potential and limitations of Slc6a20a/SLC6A20-ASO for treating disorders characterized by NMDAR hypofunction.

Introduction

NMDA receptors (NMDARs) play critical roles in the regulation of brain development and function1,2. NMDAR dysfunctions have been associated with various neurological, neurodevelopmental, and psychiatric disorders1,3. Examples of disorders involving NMDAR hypofunction include schizophrenia4–6, autism spectrum disorders (ASD)3,7–13, intellectual disability14–16, NMDAR encephalitis17,18, and Phelan-McDermid syndrome (PMS)19,20. To date, efforts to modulate NMDAR activity in brain disorders have primarily focused on pharmacological agents3,11. However, clinical trials using NMDAR modulators have reported mixed effects, partly due to the heterogeneity of spatiotemporal NMDAR expression3,11,21,22. This underscores the need for safer and more precise approaches to NMDAR modulation.

An approach to normalize NMDAR hypofunction involves targeting glycine transporters, which regulate brain levels of glycine, a co-agonist required for NMDAR activation alongside glutamate2,11,23. A key glycine transporter, GlyT1, mediates the reuptake of glycine into nearby cells, including glial cells24,25. Inhibition of GlyT1 using compounds like sarcosine has been shown to increase ambient glycine levels and enhance NMDAR function in animal models exhibiting NMDAR hypofunction25,26. Nonetheless, human clinical trials with GlyT1 inhibitors for the treatment of schizophrenia have demonstrated limited efficacy and notable side effects26–30. The adverse effects are thought to arise partly because GlyT1 is highly expressed in brainstem regions25,31, where its inhibition leads to abnormal glycine receptor activation that disrupts respiratory and motor regulation32,33.

A recent study identified Slc6a20a as a novel glycine transporter whose upregulation reduces ambient glycine levels, leading to NMDAR hypofunction34. Inhibition of Slc6a20a with antisense oligonucleotides (Slc6a20a-ASO) was shown to increase ambient glycine concentrations and restore NMDAR function in these mice34. Genetic deletion of Slc6a20a in mice similarly results in elevated extracellular glycine levels, enhanced NMDAR currents, and behavioral abnormalities34,35. Importantly, Slc6a20a is expressed primarily in cortical and hippocampal regions that are more directly involved in cognitive functions, as well as in choroid plexus and meningeal regions that may regulate glycine flux between the brain and peripheral tissues34. This regional specificity suggests that Slc6a20a-ASO could provide a more targeted and effective approach to alleviating NMDAR hypofunction for various neurodevelopmental disorders associated with NMDAR hypofunction.

In the present study, we investigated whether Slc6a20a-ASO could normalize suppressed NMDAR function in two key ASD/PMS models, Shank2 and Shank3-mutant mice36–39, and in human cortical organoids carrying corresponding SHANK2 or SHANK3 mutations. Our findings show that Slc6a20a-ASO significantly restores NMDAR function in both mouse models and human cortical organoids. Furthermore, phospho-proteomic analyses identified alterations in synaptic and NMDAR-related pathways in Shank2 and Shank3-mutant mice, many of which were normalized following Slc6a20a-ASO treatment, underscoring the mechanistic basis by which Slc6a20a-ASO corrects NMDAR hypofunction. These results suggest the therapeutic potential of Slc6a20a-ASO as a targeted strategy to correct NMDAR hypofunction in ASD and other neurodevelopmental disorders.

Results

Slc6a20a-ASO rescues NMDAR function and social interaction and communication deficits in Shank2-KO mice

We previously reported that Slc6a20a, primarily identified as a proline transporter, can also mediate glycine transport34. Inhibiting Slc6a20a expression with an ASO restores ambient glycine levels and normalizes NMDAR function in Pten-mutant mice34. These findings suggest the potential utility of Slc6a20a-ASO in regulating NMDAR hypofunction in various mouse models of brain disorders.

To investigate this possibility, we delivered Slc6a20a-ASO into the lateral ventricles of Shank2-mutant mice40 and Shank3-mutant mice41 via intracerebroventricular (ICV) injection, aiming for widespread downregulation of Slc6a20a expression in the brain (Fig. 1a). Four weeks post-injection, mice underwent a series of behavioral tests assessing behaviors, including locomotion, social interaction, and repetitive behaviors, followed by electrophysiological and proteomic analyses. The injected Slc6a20a-ASO effectively reduced Slc6a20a mRNA levels without inducing cellular toxicity (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). In Shank2-KO mice, Slc6a20a-ASO restored NMDAR currents in layer 2/3 pyramidal neurons within the prelimbic region of the medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC), as shown by restored ratios of NMDAR and AMPA receptor (AMPAR)-mediated evoked excitatory postsynaptic currents (eEPSCs; NMDA/AMPA ratio) (Fig. 1b). From this point on, we referred to WT-injected with vehicle and ASO as WT-veh, and WT-aso, and the same for the mutants (i.e. KO-veh, KO-aso). Notably, AMPAR-mediated synaptic transmission was unaffected by Slc6a20a-ASO treatment, as shown by miniature excitatory postsynaptic currents (mEPSCs) (Supplementary Fig. 2a).

Behaviorally, Slc6a20a-ASO rescued social-interaction deficits in Shank2-KO mice, as demonstrated in the three-chamber test (Fig. 1c; Supplementary Fig. 2b; Supplementary Data 1). Social novelty recognition was unaffected in Shank2-KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 2c). Ultrasonic vocalization (USV) during courtship, representing a social communication, was almost completely abolished in adult Shank2-KO mice and partially rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO; while KO-ASO USVs are significantly improved relative to KO-vehicle USVs (in call number although not in total duration), KO-ASO USV levels remained significantly different from those of WT-vehicle (Supplementary Fig. 2d). In addition, social approach of a mouse in home cages with a transparent plastic apparatus with a stranger mouse40 was decreased under baseline conditions and was rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO (Supplementary Fig. 2e). However, the open-field hyperactivity and suppressed marble burying in the mutant mice were not rescued by the ASO (Supplementary Fig. 2f, g). Baseline differences in the long-term movements measured in the LABORAS test could not be assessed owing to the lack of two-way ANOVA interactions (Supplementary Fig. 2h–k). These results collectively suggest that Slc6a20a-ASO rescues NMDAR function and normalizes behavioral deficits, including social-interaction deficits (three-chamber social interaction and home-cage social approach) and social-communication deficits (courtship USV; partial), in Shank2-KO mice.

Rescue of synaptic phospho-proteome in Slc6a20a-ASO-treated Shank2-KO mice

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the Slc6a20a-ASO-mediated rescue of synaptic and behavioral phenotypes in Shank2-KO mice, we conducted total and phospho-proteomic analyses. These analyses focused on the frontal cortex, a brain region implicated in cognitive functions and autism spectrum disorder (ASD).

In our phospho-proteomic analysis, phosphorylated peptides were enriched using immobilized metal affinity chromatography (IMAC), followed by mass spectrometry. This method identified a total of 77,651 phospho-peptides and 23,513 phospho-sites, providing a comprehensive dataset for phosphorylation profiling (Supplementary Data 2).

A baseline comparison of vehicle-treated WT mice and vehicle-treated Shank2-KO mice revealed both up and downregulated protein phosphorylations (723 up and 679 downregulated differentially expressed phospho-peptides; termed DEPPs) (Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Metascape analysis of these DEPPs associated them with biological functions, including synapses, neuronal projections, and small GTPases (Supplementary Fig. 3c). Additionally, SynGO analysis42 indicated that a large portion of the DEPPs (31.4% for CC, 26.2 % for BP) are synaptic proteins and have specific synaptic localizations and functions (Supplementary Fig. 3d). In addition, these DEPPs formed physical connections (protein-protein interactions; PPIs) that are enriched for functions associated with synapse (maintenance of postsynaptic specialization structure), synaptic signaling (calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase complex), and actin (positive modulation of Arp2/3-mediated actin nucleation) (Supplementary Fig. 4a).

Next, we analyzed differentially expressed phospho-proteins (DEPPs) that were initially altered by the Shank2 mutation but normalized to WT-like levels following Slc6a20a-ASO treatment. This analysis combined baseline differences with the rescued DEPPs that no longer show significant differences, identifying 513 upregulated-and-normalized DEPPs and 486 downregulated-and-normalized DEPPs (Fig. 1d).

Among the top-ranked upregulated-normalized DEPPs were Stk11 (Serine/Threonine Kinase 11), Dop1b (DOP1 Leucine Zipper Like Protein B), Lnpk (Lunapark, ER Junction Formation Factor), Huwe1 (HECT, UBA, and WWE Domain Containing E3 Ubiquitin Protein Ligase 1), Gpr158 (G Protein-Coupled Receptor 158), Lsp1 (Lymphocyte Specific Protein 1), Syngap1 (Synaptic Ras GTPase Activating Protein 1), Pcdh8 (Protocadherin 8), Cds1 (CDP-Diacylglycerol Synthase 1), and Mapk8ip2 (Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase 8 Interacting Protein 2; also known as JIP2) (Fig. 1e).

Similarly, top-ranked downregulated-normalized DEPPs included Dlgap1 (PSD-95/SAP90 Binding Protein 1, also known as GKAP), Scg2 (Secretogranin 2), Ncor1 (Nuclear Receptor Corepressor 1), Aatk (Apoptosis-Associated Tyrosine Kinase), Glud1 (Glutamate Dehydrogenase 1), Gabbr2 (Gamma-Aminobutyric Acid B Receptor 2), Mfsd6 (Major Facilitator Superfamily Domain Containing 6), Akap12 (A-Kinase Anchoring Protein 12), Rtn2 (Reticulon 2), and Ptk2b (Protein Tyrosine Kinase 2 Beta; also known as Pyk2) (Fig. 1e). Interestingly, many of the top-ranked rescued phospho-proteins were associated with synaptic or NMDAR functions. These included Syngap1, Dlgap1, Scg2, Glud1, Rtn2, Ptk2b, Gabbr2, Gpr158, Akap12, Huwe1, and Mapk8ip2/JIP2.

DAVID analysis of the ASO-rescued DEPPs revealed enrichment in processes related to glutamatergic synapses, postsynaptic density, and other synaptic signaling pathways (Fig. 1f). Notably, many of the biological functions identified by KEGG and Reactome pathway analyses at baseline (vehicle-treated WT mice vs. vehicle-treated Shank2-KO mice) were also present among the ASO-rescued pathways (indicated in bold). When clustered according to functional similarity, these ASO-rescued terms grouped into categories such as axon and neurodevelopment, synapse and postsynaptic processes, membrane trafficking and junction organization, Rho GTPase signaling, intracellular signaling pathways, and cytoplasmic/protein kinase functions (Fig. 1g). These results suggest that Slc6a20a-ASO treatment not only rescues individual protein functions but also restores their coordinated biological functions. SynGO analysis further indicated that approximately 30% and 25 % of the rescued DEPPs were associated with synaptic proteins and exhibited specific synaptic localizations and functions, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 4b). Moreover, these ASO-rescued DEPPs formed PPIs that are enriched for functions, including synapse (postsynaptic density organization) and synaptic signaling (calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinase complex) (Supplementary Fig. 4c).

To assess whether changes in the DEPPs were due to phosphorylation levels or total protein abundance, mass spectrometry analysis of total protein levels was performed. This analysis identified 9,492 differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) reflecting baseline differences between vehicle-treated WT and vehicle-treated Shank2 mice (Supplementary Data 3).

DAVID analysis of these DEPs indicated that their associated biological functions were minimally linked to neuronal synapses, with enrichment in processes such as RNA splicing (Supplementary Fig. 5a). This finding contrasts with the synapse-related functions observed in the DEPPs. Similarly, SynGO analysis revealed that only a small fraction (<10%) of the DEPs corresponded to synaptic proteins (Supplementary Fig. 5b), in contrast to the strong synaptic enrichment observed in the DEPPs (~25–30%).

Additionally, ASO-rescued DEPs displayed smaller magnitudes of changes (up to 3.5-fold) as compared with those in ASO-rescued DEPPs (up to 109-fold) (Supplementary Fig. 5c, d). Moreover, DAVID and SynGO analyses indicated that ASO-rescued DEPs were minimally associated with synapse-related functions, being moderately enriched for chromatin and nucleus-related processes (Supplementary Fig. 5e, f).

These findings collectively suggest that Slc6a20a-ASO treatment restores phospho-proteome patterns associated with excitatory synaptic and signaling pathways, while having minimal effects on the total proteome. This highlights phosphorylation changes, rather than protein abundance, as the primary driver of the observed effects.

Slc6a20a-ASO rescues NMDAR function, social and repetitive behaviors, and synaptic phospho-proteome in Shank3-HM mice

Another well-characterized mouse model of ASD with NMDAR dysfunction is the Shank3-mutant mouse, which has also been linked to other neurodevelopmental disorders, including Phelan-McDermid syndrome, schizophrenia, intellectual disability, and epilepsy36–39. Shank3-mutant mice carrying a deletion of exon 21 (referred to as Shank3-ΔC) exhibit NMDAR hypofunction and show ASD-like social and cognitive deficits and repetitive behaviors41,43,44.

In this study, we utilized both homozygous mutant (Shank3-HM) and heterozygous mutant (Shank3-HT) mice. While Shank3-HM mice have been extensively studied in prior research, Shank3-HT mice may better represent clinical scenarios, as heterozygous mutations are more commonly observed in human conditions.

ASO-treated Shank3-HM mice showed rescued NMDAR function, as shown by NMDA/AMPA ratio, without affecting AMPAR-mediated mEPSCs in prefrontal pyramidal neurons (Fig. 2a; Supplementary Fig. 6a). Behaviorally, Slc6a20a-ASO improved self-grooming in both home and LABORAS cages (Fig. 2b, c). Social interaction was abnormally increased in Shank3-HM mice through aberrant object aversion and rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO, although baseline social novelty recognition was unaltered in Shank3-HM mice (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). Adult courtship USV was reduced in Shank3-HM mice and rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Similarly, home-cage social approach was reduced in Shank3-HM mice and rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO, whereas marble burying, decreased in Shank3-HM mice, was not (Supplementary Fig. 6e, f). In phospho-proteomic analysis, DEPPs from Shank3-HM mice for baseline changes (WT-veh and HM-veh) were associated with synapse, dendrite, and microtubule-related functions (Metascape analysis) (Supplementary Fig. 7a–c; Supplementary Data 4). These DEPPs were also strongly enriched for SynGO proteins and synaptic localizations/functions (Supplementary Fig. 7d). These DEPPs formed PPIs enriched for functions associated with synapse (postsynaptic density organization) (Supplementary Fig. 8a).

An analysis of ASO-rescued DEPPs from Shank3-HM mice identified many proteins (285 up and 219 down) with synaptic functions such as Shisa6, Lrrc7/densin-180, Slc32a1/Vgat/Viaat, Nos1ap, Srcin1, Itsn1, and Kctd8 (Fig. 2d, e). These DEPPs were associated with glutamatergic synapse, postsynaptic density, actin, and small GTPase-related functions (DAVID) and synaptic proteins/localizations/functions (SynGO) (Fig. 2f, Supplementary Fig. 8b). Consistent with the Shank2 results, many baseline KEGG/Reactome pathway alterations (vehicle-treated WT vs. vehicle-treated Shank3-HM) were normalized by ASO (indicated in bold; Fig. 2f). Functionally related, ASO-rescued terms clustered into membrane trafficking & junction organization, synapse & postsynaptic processes, intracellular signaling pathways, Rho-family pathways, axon & neurodevelopment, and cytoplasmic/protein-kinase categories (Fig. 2g). In addition, ASO-rescued DEPPs formed PPI networks enriched for synaptic processes, including postsynaptic density organization (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

In contrast to the phospho-proteomic results, ASO-rescued total proteomic DEPs from WT and Shank3-HM mice (baseline difference) were minimally associated with synapse-related biological functions (DAVID analysis) and synaptic protein/localization/function (SynGO analysis) (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b; Supplementary Data 5). Additionally, ASO-rescued DEPs from Shank3-HM mice were not associated with synaptic functions, as shown by volcano plot, top-DEP, DAVID and SynGO analysis results (Supplementary Fig. 9c–f). These results indicate that Slc6a20a-ASO rescues NMDAR function, social approach, social communication (USV), self-grooming, and synaptic phospho-proteome in Shank3-HM mice.

Slc6a20a-ASO rescues NMDAR function, self-grooming, and synaptic phospho-proteome in Shank3-HT mice

Given the greater clinical relevance of heterozygous Shank3 mutations, we next sought to restore NMDAR function in Shank3-HT mice. Slc6a20a-ASO rescued NMDAR function (NMDA/AMPA ratio) in prefrontal pyramidal neurons without affecting AMPAR-mediated mEPSCs in Shank3-HT mice (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 10a). In phospho-proteomic analysis, the baseline DEPPs (WT-veh and HT-veh) were associated with synapse-related functions (Metascape analyses) and synaptic proteins/localizations/functions (SynGO analyses) (Supplementary Fig. 10b–e; Supplementary Data 6). These DEPPs formed PPI networks enriched for synaptic processes, including maintenance of postsynaptic specialization structures (Supplementary Fig. 11a). ASO-rescued DEPPs in Shank3-HT mice included multiple synaptic proteins, including Pclo, Eif4g3, Tsc1, Braf, Ppp3cb, and Iqsec2 (Fig. 3c, d). Accordingly, ASO-rescued DEPPs were associated with synapse-related functions (glutamatergic synapse, postsynaptic density, actin, and small GTPases) in DAVID analyses, with many baseline alterations normalized following ASO treatment (Fig. 3e). Functionally related, ASO-rescued terms clustered into membrane trafficking & junction organization, axon & neurodevelopment, synapse & postsynaptic processes, intracellular signaling pathways, Rho-related pathways, and cytoplasmic/protein-kinase categories (Fig. 3f). The ASO-rescued DEPPs also formed PPI networks enriched for synaptic functions, including maintenance of postsynaptic specialization structures (Supplementary Fig. 11b). Intriguingly, many of the ASO-rescued DEPPs from Shank3-HT mice overlapped with those from Shank3-HM mice, and were associated with synapse-related functions (NMDA receptor, synaptic junction and transmission, actin, and synaptic vesicle) (Fig. 3g).

Total proteomic analyses from Shank3-HT mice revealed that the baseline differences in differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) showed only weak associations with synapse-related functions, as determined by both DAVID and SynGO analyses (Supplementary Fig. 12a, b; Supplementary Data 7). Moreover, the ASO-rescued DEPs were minimally associated with synaptic functions, as further supported by volcano plot data, top-DEP analyses, and additional DAVID and SynGO assessments (Supplementary Fig. 12c–f). These results indicate that Slc6a20a-ASO restores NMDAR function, ameliorates behavioral deficits, and normalizes phospho-proteomic profiles in Shank3-HT mice.

Lastly, when KEGG functions enriched in ASO-rescued DEPPs from Shank2-KO, Shank3-HM, and Shank3-HT were compared, three functions commonly found in the three top-10 lists included glutamatergic, endocytosis, and oxytocin pathways (Supplementary Data 8). Within these pathways, altered and rescued components were marked by distinct star symbols for each mouse line. Such marking highlighted the following proteins in each pathway: glutamatergic (PSD-95/Dlg4, PP2B/protein phosphatase 2B also known as calcineurin, AC/adenylate cyclase), oxytocin (VGCC/voltage-gated calcium channel, ADCY/adenylate cyclase, MLC/myosin light chain kinase, and CALN/calcineurin), and endocytic (dynamin and Arp2/3 [actin modulator]) (Supplementary Figs. 13–15), suggesting that these proteins may play important roles in regulating these pathways.

Slc6a20a-ASO has long-lasting and no harmful effects

The abovementioned effects of Slc6a20a-ASO on Shank2/3 mouse phenotypes were measured four weeks after ASO injection. When tested for longitudinal effects of Slc6a20a-ASO, it had long-lasting effects of suppressing target gene expression that are observed not only at 4 weeks but also at 8 weeks after ASO injection (Supplementary Fig. 16a). Slc6a20a-ASO also had minimal effects on body weights of all three mutant mouse lines measured at 4 and 8 weeks after ASO injection (Supplementary Fig. 16b), indicating that it does not exert harmful effects over an extended period of time.

Importantly, Slc6a20a-ASO could rescue social interaction in Shank2-KO mice at 8 weeks after ASO injection (Supplementary Fig. 16c), similar to its effect at 4 weeks after injection. In the LABORAS test, Slc6a20a-ASO treatment, after 8 weeks, did not rescue hyperactivity in Shank2-KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 16d). Notably, climbing was significantly increased in Shank2-KO mice (Supplementary Fig. 16e), deviating from the unaltered climbing at 4 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 2j), and this increase was rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO, indicating that climbing phenotype is developmental stage-dependent and can also be rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO. Self-grooming and rearing at 8 weeks could not be assessed owing to the lack of two-way ANOVA interactions (Supplementary Fig. 16f, g), similar to 4 weeks.

In Shank3-HM mice, 8 weeks after injection, climbing, but not locomotion or rearing, was decreased and was not rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO (Supplementary Fig. 16h–j). Notably, the increased self-grooming, similar to the results at 4 weeks (Fig. 2c), was partially rescued by Slc6a20a-ASO (in duration but not in frequency) during light-off periods (Supplementary Fig. 16k), partly similar to the complete ASO-dependent rescue at 4 weeks.

These results collectively suggest that Slc6a20a-ASO produces sustained molecular and behavioral effects without causing adverse outcomes during prolonged treatment. Specifically, the ASO produced durable knockdown with no adverse effects, restoring social interaction in Shank2-KO mice while leaving locomotion largely unchanged; it also normalized the late-emerging climbing phenotype in Shank2-KO mice and partially alleviated excessive self-grooming in Shank3-HM mice.

To generate cortical organoids, embryonic stem cells (H1) were first cultured and subsequently subjected to SHANK2 and SHANK3 mutations using the CRISPR approach. H1 cells were electroporated with SHANK2/SHANK3-targeting CRISPR gRNAs, resulting in homozygous frame-shifting a one base-pair insertion and four base-pair deletion in the exon 16 of SHANK2 and the exon 21 of SHANK3 genes, respectively (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Fig. 17a, b). Successful knockout of SHANK2 and SHANK3 was confirmed by RT-qPCR analysis using primers that differentiate between WT and mutant transcripts (Fig. 4b). The proper differentiation of WT and SHANK2/SHANK3-KO cells into cortical organoids was validated by immunostaining of organoid slices for Nestin and NeuN, markers of neural precursor cells and mature neurons, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 17c). Further validation of cortical organoids was performed using single-cell transcriptomic analysis of cortical organoids, which confirmed the presence of expected cell types, including corticofugal projection neurons, callosal projection neurons, and cortical glutamatergic neurons (Supplementary Fig. 17d).

SHANK2- and SHANK3-KO cortical organoids were treated with SLC6A20-ASO, which targets the human SLC6A20 gene, leading to ASO dosage-dependent reductions in SLC6A20 mRNA levels, as assessed by qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 4c). To visualize human neurons, AAV-hSyn-EGFP infection was combined with SLC6A20-ASO treatment (Supplementary Fig. 17e). For electrophysiological experiments, SLC6A20-ASO-treated cortical organoids were sliced, and EGFP-positive SHANK2/SHANK3-KO neurons were then examined for NMDAR-dependent mEPSCs (Fig. 4d). NMDAR-mEPSCs were pharmacologically isolated in the presence of NBQX (AMPAR blocker), picrotoxin (GABAA receptor blocker), and tetrodotoxin (TTX, sodium channel blocker).

These experiments demonstrated that EGFP-positive SHANK2- and SHANK3-mutant neurons exhibited reduced peak amplitudes of NMDAR-mediated mEPSCs compared to WT neurons (Fig. 4e). Notably, treatment with SLC6A20-ASO restored NMDAR-mEPSC amplitudes to WT levels. Collectively, these findings indicate that human SHANK2- and SHANK3-KO neurons exhibit NMDAR hypofunction and that SLC6A20-ASO rescues NMDAR function in SHANK2/SHANK3-KO human neurons, consistent with observations in mutant mice.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that Slc6a20a-ASO effectively restores NMDAR function and alleviates behavioral deficits in Shank2 and Shank3 mouse models of ASD/PMS. Phospho-proteomic analyses confirmed reversal in aberrant phosphorylation patterns associated with synaptic and NMDAR-related proteins. Importantly, comparable restoration of NMDAR function was also observed in human cortical organoids carrying SHANK2 and SHANK3 mutations, emphasizing the translational potential of SLC6A20-ASO as a therapeutic strategy for disorders linked to NMDAR hypofunction (summarized in Supplementary Fig. 18).

An advantage of Slc6a20a-ASO over broader GlyT1 inhibitors is its potential for fewer side effects due to region-specific expression. GlyT1 inhibitors exhibit limited efficacy and adverse effects, notably due to high expression in brainstem regions involved in essential respiratory and motor functions31–33. In contrast, Slc6a20a is prominently expressed in cortical and hippocampal regions relevant to cognition, thereby potentially enhancing therapeutic effects while minimizing unwanted side effects. Additionally, meningeal expression of Slc6a20a suggests a role in regulating glycine between brain and peripheral tissues, further minimizing off-target effects.

Our findings broaden the therapeutic applications of Slc6a20a-ASO beyond initial demonstrations in Pten-mutant mice, supporting its utility across diverse ASD models. This is reinforced by improvements in social interactions and communications, and repetitive behaviors in Shank2 and Shank3 mice. Notably, these behaviors were differentially affected and rescued, likely reflecting the distinct synaptic functions of Shank2 and Shank3. However, certain phenotypes, such as hyperactivity in Shank2-KO mice, were not rescued, possibly because hyperactivity is an NMDAR-independent phenotype, consistent with the notion that not all Shank2-related deficits are rescued by NMDAR activation40. This suggests that specific behavioral domains are more readily normalized by NMDAR restoration than others. Mechanistically, phospho-proteomic analyses identified key synaptic proteins—such as Syngap1, Dlgap1, and Pyk2—whose phosphorylation was reversed by ASO treatment, underscoring their relevance to synaptic and NMDAR functions. Pathway analyses further identified convergent pathways (e.g., glutamatergic synapse, endocytosis, and oxytocin signaling) consistently normalized across different models, highlighting central mechanisms underlying therapeutic benefits.

Furthermore, our restoration of NMDAR function in SHANK2- and SHANK3-mutant human cortical organoids strengthens the translational relevance of SLC6A20-ASO as a glycine-site–targeted therapeutic approach. Prior human iPSC–based studies using SHANK2/SHANK3 mutant neurons and organoids have consistently reported pathological synaptic and network phenotypes—including alterations in excitatory synaptic density, transmission, and signaling and neuronal morphology and excitability—supporting convergent postsynaptic mechanisms in these disorders45–54. To our knowledge, our work uniquely demonstrates electrophysiological normalization of NMDAR function in human-derived SHANK2/3 models through targeted modulation of glycine availability, highlighting a tractable path to mechanism-guided intervention.

Alternative disease-modifying strategies for ASD, such as gene re-expression to restore SHANK dosage or receptor/channel modulation to rebalance excitability, have been proposed and explored in human iPSC-derived systems45,48,50,52,54. These provide useful comparators to our SLC6A20-ASO strategy, which leverages glycine-site modulation of NMDARs downstream of SHANK perturbations. Unlike gene re-expression, which must recreate the correct isoform abundance, subcellular targeting, and developmental timing (and carries risks of overexpression or long-lived vector exposure), ASO-mediated knockdown offers dose-tunable, reversible engagement with predictable pharmacology and readily measurable target engagement (SLC6A20 transcript reduction and NMDAR-mediated electrophysiological readouts). Compared with receptor/channel modulation using small molecules, our approach targets a convergent synaptic co-agonist gate that can normalize NMDAR function without overexpressing synaptic scaffolds or globally potentiating excitability. Together, these considerations position SLC6A20-ASO as a mechanistically specific yet isoform-agnostic intervention with built-in titration and clear pharmacodynamic biomarkers, complementary to gene restoration or receptor-centric strategies.

Despite the promising outcomes, several hurdles remain before broad clinical translation. NMDAR heterogeneity in abundance, subunit composition, and co-agonist control (glycine vs. D-serine) varies across brain regions, cell types, and developmental stages, making dose selection and targeting non-trivial. In parallel, clinical heterogeneity in ASD and related conditions (genetic background, cognitive and language profiles, and comorbid conditions) complicates generalizability and may dilute treatment signals.

In conclusion, our data underscore Slc6a20a/SLC6A20-ASO as a promising targeted approach for correcting NMDAR hypofunction in ASD and related disorders. By circumventing limitations of current pharmacological treatments, this method represents a novel, safe, and potentially broadly applicable therapeutic option.

Methods

Animals

Shank2−/− mice (Mus musculus) lacking the exons 6 and 7 of the Shank2 gene maintained in the genetic background of C57BL/6N have been previously reported40. Shank3tm1.1Pfw/J (Shank3ΔC, Shank3Δ ex21) mice (Mus musculus) were purchased from Jackson Lab (Stock No: 018398) and maintained in the C57BL/6J background. For both lines, experimental mice were generated by crossing heterozygotes. Mice were bred and maintained at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST) mouse facility in accordance with KAIST’s Animal Research Requirements. Only male mice were used. All experimental procedures adhered to the guidelines for the Protection of Animals used for Scientific Purposes (Laboratory Animal Act Law 19918) and the KAIST Animal Welfare Guidelines, and were approved by the KAIST Committee of Animal Research and Ethics (KA2023-093-v1) and IBS (IBS-2025-033).

Mice were provided food and water ad libitum and maintained on a 12 h light/dark cycle (lights on 07:00, lights off 19:00), at an ambient temperature of 22 ± 1 °C and relative humidity of 50 ± 10%. Pups were weaned at 21 days of age and subsequently housed in groups of three to five per cage. For behavioral assays, age- and sex-matched mice were randomly selected. PCR genotyping of Shank2-mutant mice was performed using two sets of oligonucleotide primers targeting exons 6–7. For the wild-type (WT) allele (522 bp product), the forward primer was 5’-CCT TTG TGT GGT GTT GGC TAG C-3’. For the mutant allele, the forward primer was 5’-GCA TCT GCG TGT TCG AAT TCG C-3’, paired with a common reverse primer 5’-CTC CAC CTG TGT GTG ATT TCT GAC-3’. For genotyping Shank3 mice, the following primer pairs were used: a common forward primer 5’-GTG TCC CCT CAT TGA TGT TG-3’, a WT reverse primer 5’-ATG TCC TGT TGC AGG TAG GG-3’, and a mutant reverse primer 5’-CTC TGC CAC CTT CTG CCT AC-3’.

Ethics statement

All experimental procedures using human cells and derived organoids adhered to the guidelines and were approved by the KAIST IRB (KAISTIRB-2025-117). All experimental procedures using animals adhered to guidelines and were approved by KAIST Committee of Animal Research and Ethics (KA2023-093-v1) and IBS (IBS-2025-033).

Behavioral tests

All adult behavioral tests were performed with age-matched male WT, Shank2-KO, Shank3-HT, and Shank3-HM mice (3-7 months). All behavioral experiments were carried out during light-off periods, except for LABORAS tests. More detailed methods are available in the Supplementary Information.

Antisense oligonucleotide (ASO)

Synthesis and purification of all chemically modified ASO was performed as follows (IDT, CA, USA): All antisense oligonucleotides were 25 bp in length, included five 2’-O-methoxyethyl (2’-MOE)-modified nucleotides with 5-methylcytosine modifications at each end of the oligonucleotide, with 15 DNA nucleotides in the center, and were phosphorothioate modified in all positions, and CpG were 5′-methylcytosines, as described previously55. The sequence of the ASO was 5′-CACTG AGGCC GTGCC TTCTC CATGT-3′ directed against mouse Slc6a20a (NM_139142.2; confirmed unique in mouse with BLASTN), a nucleotide sequence that has been used previously to knockdown Slc6a20a expression34,56. 5′-CAC AGC GGC CGC GCT TTC TCC ATG G-3′ directed against human SLC6A20 (NM_020208.4; confirmed unique in human with BLASTN).

Statistics and reproducibility

Electrophysiological recordings were replicated across ≥3 mice per group; behavioral findings were replicated in independent cohorts; proteomics experiments used ≥3 biological replicates per group; and organoid electrophysiology used ≥3 independent organoid preparations per genotype/treatment. All attempted replications were successful; no experiments failed to reproduce.
