Hypoxia drives cervical cancer progression via OCT4/ORAI3-dependent glycolysis and Ca2+ signaling
Authors: Lingjia Lu, Fenfen Wang, Ting Wang, Ziyu Xing, Peiyue Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCervical cancer is a common cancer among women worldwide. It has been revealed that hypoxia contributes to the progression of cervical cancer. In our study, we discovered that hypoxia indeed promoted the malignant phenotypes of cervical cancer cells by enhancing glycolysis. Loss-of-function experiments showed that hypoxia treatment upregulated the octamer-binding transcriptional factor 4 (OCT4) expression via glycolysis. Through the RNA-sequence and enrichment analyses, we found that hypoxia induced the enrichment of the calcium signaling pathway and upregulation of Calcium Release-Activated Calcium Modulator 3 (ORAI3), which could be abrogated by silencing OCT4. Notably, overexpressing ORAI3 has similar effects on the malignant phenotypes of HeLa and SiHa cells as those of hypoxia. Furthermore, silencing ORAI3 or inactivating calcium signals significantly reversed OCT4-induced malignant progression of cervical cancer both in vitro and in vivo. ChIP and dual-luciferase reporter results confirmed that OCT4 contributed to the transcription of ORAI3. Mechanically, hypoxia upregulated OCT4 expression by facilitating glycolysis, and OCT4 overexpression enhanced the transcription of ORAI3, activating the calcium signaling pathway and ultimately promoting the malignant progression of cervical cancer. Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a critical global health burden [1], ranking as the fourth most common cancer among women worldwide and the second most prevalent in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), accounting for 6.9% of global female cancer cases and 23.3% of female cancers in LMICs [2, 3]. In the 1980 s, the German virologist Harold Zur Hausen specified the link between cervical cancer and human papillomavirus (HPV) infections. The treatments for cervical cancer consist of surgery (early stages) and the combination of brachytherapy and chemoradiation (advanced stages) [4]. HPV infection is now well-established as the primary etiological factor driving cervical carcinogenesis [5], with high-risk HPV types (notably HPV16/18) inducing malignant transformation through persistent expression of oncoproteins E6/E7 that inactivate tumor suppressors p53/Rb and dysregulate host epigenetic machinery [6, 7]. Despite > 90% of cases being preventable via HPV vaccination and evidence-based screening programs, cervical cancer persists as a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in low-resource settings due to barriers in healthcare access and vaccine inequity [8].

Emerging evidence highlights the critical role of ion channels in HPV-driven cervical cancer malignancy [9]. Viral oncoproteins actively mediate these transmembrane proteins to disrupt host cell balance, fostering uncontrolled proliferation, survival, and metastasis. For instance, HPV oncogenes (e.g., E6/E7) directly induce upregulation and activation of the KATP channel complex via SUR1, a regulatory subunit of KATP channels [10], causing cervical cancer cell proliferation by contributing to MAPK/AP-1 signalling. Wang et al. found that TRPV1, being a widely expressed superfamily of ion channels that regulate intracellular Ca2+ homeostasis and signal transduction [11], is downregulated in cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CSCC) and inversely correlates with cancer risk. It regulates tumor differentiation, ferroptosis, inflammation, metabolism, and immune modulation (positively with T cells, negatively with macrophages), positioning it as a potential diagnostic biomarker and therapeutic target [12]. Additionally, natural product-derived compounds are demonstrating significant preclinical efficacy against cervical cancer through multi-target mechanisms [13], including the plant-derived compound α-mangostin [14], shikonin [15], and resveratrol [16, 17] et al.. Although surgical intervention combined with chemoradiation, brachytherapy, and emerging natural extracts constitutes the therapeutic cornerstone, the patients always suffer severe side effects after treatment, which negatively affect living quality [18]. This unmet clinical need underscores that elucidating the underlying pathogenesis and developing more efficient therapies for cervical cancer is a strong need.

The reprogramming of glycolysis has been shown to regulate the progression of tumors. For instance, tumor-derived exosomes containing ENO2 can reprogram glycolysis to modulate the polarization of tumor-associated macrophages, thereby promoting the progression of diffuse large B-cell lymphoma [19]. Additionally, circROBO1 has been found to accelerate glycolysis, which in turn promotes the growth of prostate cancer and resistance to enzalutamide [20]. Furthermore, metformin exerts its anti-leukemic effects by modulating lactate metabolism and overcoming imatinib resistance in chronic myeloid leukemia [21]. These results highlight the crucial role of glycolysis reprogramming in tumor development and provide potential targets for therapeutic intervention. For most solid tumors, hypoxia serves as a typical feature in the microenvironment due to uncontrolled and rapid cell proliferation, which results in the limitation of oxygen availability [22]. Undoubtedly, the clinical significance of hypoxia in anti-tumor therapy was established, as oxygen is extremely vital for driving cellular bioenergetics [23]. The diminished availability of oxygen triggers glycolysis for those highly proliferating cells, including cancerous and embryonic cells, to metabolize glucose, thereby securing the energy supply. Mounting studies have shown that tumor malignancy is linked to this kind of metabolic reprogramming process [24]. Hypoxia-mediated de novo angiogenesis, the drug efflux proteins overexpression, apoptosis, autophagy induction, and inhibition of DNA damage contribute to the malignant progression of cancers [25]. Importantly, it has been clearly stated that hypoxia in tumor tissues of cervical cancer patients is closely associated with poor prognosis, and this is independent of treatment modality [26]. Notably, emerging insights reveal complex interactions between hypoxia and the oncogenic machinery driven by high-risk HPV, where viral oncoproteins E6 and E7 cooperatively dysregulate oxygen-sensing pathways, thereby amplifying hypoxia’s tumor-promoting effects [27–30]. Therefore, handling hypoxia is significantly important, and the therapeutic strategies to control hypoxia and hypoxia-derived biological processes are highly needed.

As the major second messenger, calcium is essential for all higher organisms, regardless of fate or survival [31]. It has been reported that the up-regulation of intracellular calcium levels is an important response of multiple cell types that suffer hypoxia [32]. The purpose of this study is to investigate the role of hypoxia in the malignant progression of cervical cancer. Specifically, we aim to confirm whether hypoxia contributes to the development and progression of cervical cancer and to elucidate the underlying mechanisms involved. Our focus will be on the relationship between hypoxia, glycolysis, and the expression of Octamer-binding transcriptional factor 4 (OCT4), as well as the impact of OCT4 on the transcription of Calcium Release-Activated Calcium Modulator 3 (ORAI3) and its involvement in the activation of the calcium signaling pathway. By understanding these processes, we hope to gain insights into potential targets for therapeutic intervention in cervical cancer.

Results

Hypoxia promoted the malignant phenotype of cervical cancer cells

It is well-known that oxygen serves an indispensable role in normal cellular biological processes, including cell proliferation, cell metabolism, immune response, and so on. In solid malignancies, the oxygen tension is always lower compared with that in normal tissues because of a mismatch between the supply and consumption of oxygen; as such, hypoxia is closely related to the malignant progression of various tumor types, especially solid malignancies [33]. In this study, we explored the role of hypoxia in cervical cancer. After analyzing the clinical data of cervical cancer obtained from the UALCAN database, we found that the overall survival of cervical cancer was negatively correlated with the mRNA level of the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α), indicating that hypoxia is significantly related to the malignant progression of cervical cancer (Fig. 1A). Indeed, hypoxia treatment significantly promoted the cell migration and invasion abilities of HeLa and SiHa cells (Fig. 1B, Figure S1A, **P < 0.01, *P < 0.05, as below), with an increase in the expression of N-cadherin and Vimentin, and a decrease on that of E-cadherin (Fig. 1C, Figure S1B), indicating that hypoxia promoted the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT). Besides, hypoxia also promoted the sphere-forming ability (Fig. 1D, Figure S1C), and enhanced the cell proliferation of the two cervical cancer cell lines (Fig. 1E, Figure S1D), with significant promotion on the expression levels of OCT4 and Nanog, which are the cancer stemness-related biomarkers, both at protein and mRNA levels (Fig. 1F&G, Figure S1E&F). The results from flow cytometry further illustrated that the number of CD133/CD44-positive HeLa and SiHa cells was significantly increased after hypoxia treatment (Fig. 1H, Figure S1G), revealing that hypoxia promoted the stemness and contributed to the self-renewal of cervical cancer cells.

Given that the cells can enhance glycolysis as their metabolic strategy for maintenance of ATP generation, we detected the contents of glucose, adenosine triphosphate (ATP), lactic acid, and PKM2, and the results showed that the glucose in the supernatant of the two cell lines cultured under hypoxia condition was significantly decreased, indicating that the ability of cells to take up glucose was enhanced. Besides, the content of ATP, lactic acid, and PKM2 in those hypoxia-treated cells was all increased compared with that in the normoxia-treated cells (Fig. 1, Figure S1H). These results provided evidence that the negative effects of hypoxia in the progression of cervical cancer were related to glycolysis, and this was further confirmed by the WB and RT-qPCR experiments, which showed us that hypoxia statistically up-regulated the expression of (hexokinase 2) HK2 and (glucose transporter type 1) GLUT1 expressed in HeLa and SiHa cells (Fig. 1J&K, Figure S1 I&J).

Glycolysis Inhibition by 2-DG attenuated hypoxia-induced malignant progression through OCT4 downregulation in cervical cancer

To explore the role of glycolysis in the hypoxia-promoted malignant progression of cervical cancer, 2-DG, the inhibitor of glycolysis, was used to perform rescue experiments. HeLa and SiHa cells were treated with various concentrations of 2-DG to determine the sensitivity of the two cell lines and then determine the appropriate concentration for the following experiments. As shown in Fig. 2A, the two cell lines were similarly sensitive to 2-DG, with IC50 = 13.22 mM for HeLa cells and 14.16 mM for SiHa cells. After integrating cytotoxicity and inhibitory effects, 2.5 mM of 2-DG was used for the following exploration. Interestingly, the application of 2-DG significantly reversed hypoxia-enhanced cell migration, invasion, and self-renewal capabilities of HeLa and SiHa cells (Fig. 2B&C). At the protein level, the hypoxia-upregulated OCT4, HK2, GLUT1, N-cadherin, and Vimentin, while inhibited E-cadherin expression, were all recovered after 2-DG treatment, indicating that hypoxia facilitated cervical cancer via glycolysis. Next, we overexpressed OCT4 for further validation. Not surprisingly, OCT4 overexpression indeed reversed the 2-DG-inhibited cell migration, invasion, and sphere-forming capacities of HeLa and SiHa cells (Figure S2 A&B), with a similar reversion in the effects of 2-DG on the expression of OCT4 and EMT-related proteins (Figure S2 C&D). Taken together, we preliminarily considered that hypoxia facilitated the glycolysis, and then promoted the expression of OCT4 in the cervical cancer cells to contribute to the malignant progression of cervical cancer.

OCT4 mediated calcium signaling pathway via up-regulating ORAI3

To explore the underlying mechanism by which the hypoxia-promoted OCT4 facilitates the malignant progression of cervical cancer, we knocked down OCT4 using siRNAs. As shown in Fig. 3A, HeLa and SiHa cells were transfected with three siRNAs against OCT4 and a corresponding control siRNA, and the silencing efficiency of siR-OCT4-2 was the best. Subsequently, combined with hypoxia-treated or not, we further confirmed that the expression of OCT4 could be promoted by hypoxia (Fig. 3B). RNA-seq analysis revealed distinct transcriptional landscapes mediated by OCT4 and hypoxia (Fig. 3C) in HeLa cells. Specifically, OCT4 silenced alone significantly altered the expression of 103 genes (58 up/45 down), while hypoxia alone modulated 772 transcripts (601 up/171 down). Strikingly, the combinatorial intervention (hypoxia + OCT4 silenced) demonstrated non-additive effects, regulating only 136 genes (76 up/60 down), suggesting OCT4 mediates a substantial fraction of hypoxia-responsive transcription in cervical cancer cells. Notably, 31% of hypoxia-induced DEGs were reversed by OCT4 depletion, positioning OCT4 as a master regulator of hypoxic adaptation. More importantly, the GO and KEGG enrichment analyses showed that these DEGs are mainly enriched in the calcium signaling pathway (Fig. 4A&B). For further exploration, we selected the top five DEGs (folding changes greater than 2 ×) to perform RT-qPCR experiments, and surprisingly, we found that hypoxia significantly up-regulated ORAI3, yet this could be abrogated by silencing OCT4 (Figure S3, Fig. 4C), which was consistent with the sequencing results, and this was also confirmed at the protein level (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, OCT4 knockdown also recovered the hypoxia-activated STAT3 and calcium signaling pathways (Fig. 5A).

Subsequently, we constructed ORAI3-overexpressing HeLa and SiHa cell lines to validate the role of ORAI3. After the validation of overexpression efficiency (Fig. 5B), the ORAI3-overexpressed HeLa and SiHa cells and their corresponding control cells were used for the transwell assay. The results showed that both cell migration and invasion abilities of the ORAI3-overexpressing cervical cancer cells were better than those of the normal cervical cancer cells (Fig. 5C). Although OCT4 enhanced the cell migration and invasion, ORAI3 knockdown significantly abrogated those results (Fig. 6A); and OCT4-activated EMT and calcium signaling pathways were also reversed by knocking ORAI3 down (Fig. 6B). More importantly, the application of R24571, the inhibitor of the calcium signaling pathway, could also reverse the OCT4-promoted cell motility of cervical cancer cells (Figure S4A), and OCT4-promoted ORAI3 expression and EMT process, as well as the OCT4-activated signaling pathway, were all recovered by R24571 at the protein level (Figure S4B). These results strongly suggest that the activation of the calcium signal OCT4 regulates the activation of the calcium signaling pathway by up-regulating ORAI3. What’s more, this was further proved in vivo, since the overexpression of OCT4 significantly promoted the volume and weight of the xenograft tumors, with an increased ORAI3 protein expression in the tumor tissues, yet all of which were reversed when ORAI3 was knocked down (Fig. 7A). Besides, we found that OCT4 overexpression could promote the pulmonary metastasis of cervical cancer, as the surface metastases in the isolated lungs derived from HeLa cells (OCT4-overexpressed)-injected mice were significantly increased, while this was also abrogated by knocking ORAI3 down (Fig. 7E&F).

OCT4 up-regulated ORAI3 expression by promoting ORAI3 transcription

As shown in Fig. 8A&B, OCT4 overexpression induced the up-regulation of ORAI3 mRNA and protein levels, and we wondered about the underlying mechanism by which OCT4 regulates ORAI3 expression. The JASPAR database predicted that there were two potential binding sites of OCT4 on the ORAI3 promoter (−1000 ~ 200 bp) (Fig. 8C). The following ChIP experiments further confirmed that the OCT4 protein exhibited enrichment at site 1 (Fig. 8D), indicating that OCT4 could bind to the ORAI3 promoter at site 1. Consistently, the outcomes from luciferase reporter also demonstrated that, although OCT4 was overexpressed, the luciferase activity at site 1 was significantly lower at site 1-mutated ORAI3 promoter compared with that at the wild type of ORAI3 promoter, suggesting that OCT4 overexpression contributed to the transcription of ORAI3. As such, hypoxia up-regulated OCT4 level via glycolysis, and the accumulated OCT4 mediated the activation of the calcium signaling pathway by promoting the transcription of ORAI3, eventually, facilitating the malignant progression of cervical cancer.

Discussion

In this research, we revealed novel molecular mechanisms by which hypoxia induces the progression of cervical cancer. Hypoxia significantly promotes the expression of OCT4 by boosting glycolysis, a metabolic process that provides energy for cancer cell growth and metastasis. This upregulation of OCT4 directly binds to the ORAI3 promoter, enhancing ORAI3 transcription. Subsequently, this leads to the activation of the calcium signaling pathway, which plays a crucial role in various cellular processes involved in cancer development. Ultimately, these mechanisms contribute to the promotion of cervical cancer. Our findings provide new insights into the complex interplay between metabolic pathways and transcription factors in cancer progression and highlight potential targets for therapeutic intervention in cervical cancer treatment.

The energy metabolism is the basis of all biological activities; the constant production of ATP from oxidative phosphorylation requires a constant O2 supply. However, hypoxia is a typical characteristic of almost all solid tumors. In response to the severely reduced content of oxygen, HIFs are always activated to regulate the metabolic adaptive responses to hypoxic stress in tumor cells [34], and they function via recognizing and binding to the hypoxia response elements [35]. Accumulating studies proved that HIFs are engaged in angiogenesis, cell proliferation, survival, death, adhesion, as well as energy metabolism via activating the downstream genes transcriptionally [35]. Therefore, the HIF pathway, especially HIF-1, is extremely essential for tumor growth. Clinically, the expression level of HIF-1α is positively correlated with the patient mortality of various cancers [36]. Importantly, we also confirmed that the survival rate of CESC patients with high HIF-1α mRNA expression was significantly poorer than that of CESC patients with low HIF-1α mRNA expression, revealing that hypoxia has negative effects on the progression of cervical cancer. Indeed, hypoxia promoted the cell migration, invasion, sphere-forming, and proliferation abilities of both HeLa and SiHa cells, accompanied by glycolysis, which were also validated at the protein levels. Furthermore, we confirmed that hypoxia induced the up-regulation of OCT4 both at mRNA and protein levels, while OCT4 overexpression could reverse the effects of interrupting glycolysis. These results were significant, as OCT4 is one of the pluripotency proteins, which is closely related to cancer stemness. Far from being merely a marker of cellular origin or dedifferentiation, OCT4 appears to act as an active participant in oncogenesis. Existing research positioned OCT4 as a critical mediator in the transformative process initiated by high-risk HPV infection [37, 38]. The established oncogenic function of OCT4 explains cervical tumor stem-like populations, indicating that pluripotency acquisition directly drives malignancy; thus, targeting the OCT4 axis is promising, but characterizing its downstream effectors, particularly within HPV-driven networks, is needed to develop disruptive strategies. It has been proven that glycolysis serves an essential role in maintaining pluripotency [39]. The link between OCT4 and glycolysis has been demonstrated previously that human embryonic stem cells have a promoted glycolytic rate and are significantly more pluripotent, with an increased expression level of OCT4, SOX2, as well as Nanog [40]. Christensen et al. have proved that this phenomenon is triggered by GLUT3 and PKM2 [41], and PKM2 was shown to that it can promote the transcription of target genes via interacting with HIF-1α [42, 43]. But interestingly, the interaction between PKM2 and OCT4 was controversial, as one study pointed out that the function of OCT4 was inhibited by PKM2 [44], and another one indicated the reverse results [45]. Our study indeed found hypoxia promoted the expression of OCT4, and this might be addressed through glycolysis. However, the underlying mechanism needs further exploration.

It is also worth noting that the upregulation of ORAI3 and the activation of the calcium signaling pathway occurred in hypoxia-treated cervical cells. Furthermore, we observed STAT3 phosphorylation, a key hyperactivation event in HPV-driven cervical carcinogenesis [46], in hypoxic cervical cancer, sustained by distinct signaling modalities. Research reported that Intrinsic virus-driven Rac1-NFκB-IL-6 autocrine loops directly activate STAT3 in HPV positive cancer cells [47], whereas extrinsic tumor-associated macrophages-derived Oncostatin M OSM, a pleiotropic cytokine, epigenetically reprograms transcriptional programs via STAT3 to promote metastasis and poor prognosis [48]. Future efforts will focus on targeting STAT3, either through IL-6 blockade or selective degradation to disrupt these pro-oncogenic networks, offering a high-impact therapeutic strategy for advanced cervical cancer. Here, through the gain and loss-function experiments, we confirmed that the expression of ORAI3 was promoted by OCT4. As the major second messenger in cells, calcium is involved in the regulation of the fate of all higher organisms. The concentration of calcium varies between inside and outside of cells and is also different in different organelles. This was regulated by an elaborate system, consisting of the calcium channels, transporters, pumps, exchangers, as well as binding/buffering proteins [49]. The propagation of calcium is fundamental to the intracellular transmission of biological information and essential biological processes, such as gene transcription, protein modification, neurotransmission, cell proliferation, cell death, and so on [50]. Interestingly, it has been reported that cytosolic calcium increases quickly in human 143B osteosarcoma cells that are maintained under hypoxic conditions, and this triggers AMP-activated protein kinase, which is also known as the “fuel gauge” of cells. Mechanically, the influx of calcium was addressed by the Ca2+ release-activated Ca2+ (CRAC) channels, which are mediated by hypoxia-activated reactive oxygen species signal [51]. ORAI3 belongs to the ORAI family, which contributes to the CRAC channel [52]. It has been revealed that ORAI3 is always overexpressed in multiple neoplastic cells in which the CRAC channel is involved in supporting various cellular activities; as such, leading to the malignant progression of cancers. For example, in breast cancer, Ca2+ influx through the ORAI3 channel regulates p53 expression via activating the PI3K/Sgk-1 signaling pathway, which further induces the ubiquitinated degradation of p53, thereby conferring anti-apoptotic capacity, and ORAI3 was also engaged in the resistance to chemotherapeutic drugs [53]. Zhu et al. indicated that the up-regulation of ORAI3 in colon cancer xenograft models and patients was dependent on HIF-1/2α activation [54]. Our outcomes found that there is a binding site of OCT4 on the ORAI3 promoter, and the transcription of ORAI3 was regulated by OCT4. This has not been previously reported.

The hypoxic microenvironment within tumors has significant implications for the development of new treatment strategies for cervical cancer. Jiang et al. revealed that MicroRNA-519d-3p inhibits proliferation and promotes apoptosis in cervical cancer under hypoxic conditions by targeting HIF-2α, suggesting that targeting HIFs or their downstream pathways could be a potential strategy to disrupt tumor adaptation and progression [55]. Milica et al. defined a clear mechanism explaining why hypoxic HPV-positive cancer cells are exceptionally sensitive to the pro-senescent effects of B-MYB repression due to their failure to induce compensatory A-MYB. Exploiting this specificity offers a promising therapeutic window for eliminating the hypoxic, therapy-resistant fraction of tumors [56]. Additionally, since hypoxic tumors often respond by promoting angiogenesis, treatments that inhibit the formation of new blood vessels could starve the tumor of the oxygen and nutrients necessary for its growth [57, 58]. Some drugs are specifically activated under low oxygen conditions. These hypoxic cytotoxins, such as tirapazamine, are designed to target cells in the hypoxic regions of tumors and could be used in combination with other treatments to enhance their efficacy [59]. Various observations suggest that a deeper understanding of the molecular mechanisms involved in hypoxia-mediated adaptation and survival of cervical cancer cells is crucial for designing more effective and targeted therapies.

Conclusion

Here, we demonstrated that hypoxia has negative effects on the progression of cervical cancer. Fundamentally, hypoxia up-regulated OCT4 expression via promoting glycolysis, and OCT4 promoted the transcription of ORAI3 and then mediated the activation of the calcium signaling pathway, thereby, the interruption of calcium homeostasis resulting in the malignant progression of cervical cancer, providing new avenues for developing novel treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Data source

The HIF-1 A mRNA expression dataset from human cervical squamous cell carcinoma (CESC) was obtained from the University of UALCAN (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html).

Drugs

Calmidazolium chloride (R24571, HY-103319) and 2-Deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG, HY-13966) were purchased from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China).

Cells and hypoxia incubation

HeLa (RRID: CVCL_0030, CL-0101) and SiHa (RRID: CVCL_0032, CL-0210) cell lines were purchased from Procell Bio(Wuhan, China) and maintained in the DMEM medium (high-glucose, Gibco, Grand Island, USA, 02-5062EJ) with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, AUS, 10099141 C), 100 U/ml penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, New York, USA, 15140122), and glutamine (1×) (Gibco, Grand Island, USA, A2916801). Normoxic condition was addressed by maintaining the cells at a normoxic incubator (Thermo Fisher, USA. 21% O2, 5% CO2, 74% N2), and hypoxia condition was addressed by maintaining the cells at a thin oxygen concentration (Thermo Fisher, USA. 1% O2, 5% CO2, 94% N2). Up to 80% confluence (4 × 105 cells per dish), the cells were trypsinized with 0.025% trypsin (Gibco, R001100). All cell lines used are listed using the official cell line name and its Research Resource Identifier as available in the ExPASy Cellosaurus database. All experiments were performed with mycoplasma-free cells. All cells that have been authenticated using STR profiling within the last three years have been included and were cultured according to ATCC standards. All experiments were performed using mycoplasma-free cells.

Transwell migration and invasion assay

HeLa and SiHa cells after indicated treatment were seeded into the 24-well transwell (2 × 104 cells per well) (Millipore, Massachusetts, USA, CLS3396) inserted with an 8 μm PET membrane (Millipore, CLS3464), and the lower chamber was filled with 10% FBS-containing DMEM medium. The cells were allowed to migrate, and 24 h later, paraformaldehyde fixation (4%, Aladdin, Beijing, China, C104190) and crystal violet staining (0.5%, Aladdin, C110702) were performed. The migrated cells were numbered based on three random fields. For the cell invasion ability, the inserted PET membrane needed to be pre-coated with Matri-gel (Corning, New York, USA, 354234) one day in advance, and the following steps were similar to those of the migration assay.

Sphere formation

Cells were suspended using freshly prepared medium (formulated as Table 1), and the cells were seeded into a 6-well plate (low attachment, 3000 cells per well) (Corning, New York, USA, CLS3471) and maintained for 2 weeks. The forming spheres were observed and recorded by a microscope (Olympus Corp, Tokyo, Japan, Olympus IX73, magnification: 200×). FGF and EGF were purchased from Aladin, and the item numbers were rp155946 and rp145392, respectively; insulin was purchased from MedChemExpress (New Jersey, USA, HY-P73243); B-27 was purchased from Thermo Fisher (Grand Island, New York, USA, 17504044).

MTT proliferation assay

The cell proliferation of HeLa and SiHa cells with hypoxia-treated or not was measured using MTT assays. Cells were seeded at a density of 3000 cells per well and allowed to attach to the 96-well plates. Subsequently, the cells maintained for 0, 24, 48, 72, and 96 h respectively were collected and 1 mg/ml of MTT solution (Sigma, Saint Louis, USA, M2128, 50 µl per well) was added to co-incubate for 3 h, and then 150 µl Dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO, Merck, New Zee burn, USA, D2650-5 × 5ML) were applied to dissolve the forming purple crystals. Ultimately, the absorbance at 570 nm of the final solution was determined using a microplate reader (Molecular Device, California, USA), and the curve of cell proliferation was generated using GraphPad Prism 6.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

MTT survival assay

HeLa and SiHa cells were seeded at a density of 4000 cells per well and treated with various concentrations of 2-DG (0, 2.5, 5, 10, 20 mM, 3 duplications per concentration) in 96-well plates for 24 h. Then, 50 µl MTT solution was added, and the subsequent steps were the same as those of the proliferation assay.

CD44+/CD133+ determination

The CD44 and CD133 profiles in HeLa and SiHa cells with hypoxia-treated or not were determined using flow cytometry. Briefly, cells were adjusted at a density of 1 × 106−2 × 108 cells/ml and cultured in 100 µl of PBS solution with 1% BSA (maintained on ice). Then, the cells were labeled with BB515-conjugated anti-CD44 (BD, New Jersey, USA, 564587) and PE-conjugated anti-CD133 (Miltenyi, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany, 130-090-853) for 30 min in the dark. Finally, the labeled cells were suspended using PBS, and CD44 and CD133 levels were analyzed using a flow cytometer (Thermo, AttuneNxT, SG, A24858).

Glucose determination

The supernatant of the cervical cancer cells was collected and centrifuged. Subsequently, the glucose level in the supernatant was measured using a Glucose Detection Kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China, BC2505) according to the instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay experiments.

The levels of ATP, lactic acid (LA), and pyruvate kinase muscle isozyme M2 (PKM2) in HeLa and SiHa cells with hypoxia treated or not were detected using ATP Detection Kit (MEIMIAN, Jiangsu, China, MM14432H2), LA Detection Kit (MEIMIAN, MM-12907H2), and PKM2 Detection Kit (MEIMIAN, MM-13234H2) according to the instructions.

Plasmid transfection and lentivirus infection

The OCT4-silenced and ORAI3-overexpressed HeLa and SiHa cell lines were constructed by transient transfection. Shortly, the human ORAI3 cDNA was PCR-amplified and subcloned into the pCDH vector (YouBio, Hunan, China, VT8070); the siRNAs against OCT4 (siR-OCT4) and corresponding control siRNA (siR-NC) were purchased from Hippo Bio (Huzhou, China). siR-OCT4, siR-NC, the empty pCDH vector, and the ORAI3-overexpressed plasmids were respectively transfected into the two cervical cancer cells with the help of Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, Waltham, USA, 11668-019) as the manufacturer’s.

The stable over-expression of OCT4 and stable knockdown of ORAI3 in the HeLa and SiHa cells were addressed by lentivirus infection. The OCT4-overexpressed lentivirus (Lenti-OCT4), the ORAI3-silenced lentivirus (Lenti-sh-ORAI3), and their corresponding control lentivirus (Lenti-con) were purchased from GUANNAN Bio (Hangzhou, China). Cells were infected with the above lentivirus (1 × 108 Tu ml−1), and 1 µg/ml of puromycin (Aladdin, P408017) was used to screen the positive cells. Moreover, the transfection and infection efficiencies were determined by WB and RT-qPCR experiments. All sequences used in this section are exhibited in Table 4.

Xenograft models

Eighteen Balb/c nude mice (Female, 4 weeks old, about 20 g) were purchased from SLAC (Shanghai, China), housed in a specific pathogen-free (SPF) environment, maintained with proper ventilation and a 12-hour light/dark cycle, and provided with ad libitum access to food and water before euthanasia. All of them were randomly grouped into three (n = 6) and injected subcutaneously with HeLa cells infected with Lenti-con, HeLa cells infected with Lenti-OCT4, and HeLa cells infected with Lenti-OCT4 and Lenti-sh-ORAI3, respectively. The concentration of injection was 4 × 106 in each mouse. 4 weeks later, the mice were sacrificed by cervical dislocation (as below), and we tried our best to minimize the pain. Xenograft tumors were kindly isolated, and the volume and weight were recorded. All animal experiments in this study follow the Guiding Principles in the Care and Use of Animals (China) and are approved by the Laboratory Animal Ethics Committee of Women’s Hospital, Medicine School of Zhejiang University (Approval No.: IRB-20200313-R).

Metastasis assay in vivo

Eighteen nude mice (Female, 4 weeks old, about 20 g) were divided into three groups (n = 6), and the mice in the three groups were injected with HeLa cells infected with Lenti-con, HeLa cells infected with Lenti-OCT4, and HeLa cells infected with Lenti-OCT4 and Lenti-sh-ORAI3, respectively (1 × 106 each mouse) through the tail vein. 4 weeks later, the mice were killed, and the lungs were isolated for the count of the surface metastases. Besides, the lungs were paraffin-embedded, and then they were hematoxylin and eosin (HE) stained according to this description [60, 61].

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

The IHC experiments were performed as described previously [62] with the following primary antibodies: Ki67 (CST, 9449 S, dilution ratio: 1:300), OCT4 (proteintech, 11263-1-AP, dilution ratio: 1:200), ORAI3 (proteintech, 25766-1-AP, dilution ratio: 1:200). The qualification of IHC results was addressed using Image J software at × 200 magnification according to the mean optical density (MOD) [63]. The criteria for the test results were evaluated by assessing the intensity of the positive stain, the proportion of positive cells to the total number of cells, and the distribution of positive cells in the tissue.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation assay kit (ChIP)

ChIP experiments were performed based on a SimpleChIP® Enzymatic Chromatin IP Kit (Agarose Beads) (CST, Boston, USA, #9003) in line with the manufacturer’s description. Briefly, after cell lysis, crosslinking, and chromatin shearing, the supernatant was collected. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with OCT4 antibody (Abcam, Cambridge, MA, USA, ab181557), and the rabbit IgG (Abcam, ab46540) worked as the internal control. Besides, the collected supernatant was the Input control. Then, the immune complexes were harvested, followed by elution and decrosslinking. After the purification and enrichment of the DNA fragments, RT-qPCR experiments were performed to examine the expression of the ORAI3 promoter region. The primers were as follows:

Site 1: Forward: 5’-CATGGGACTCCTCCAGCCT-3’, Reverse: 5’-CCTGGGCAAATGGGATACA-3’;

Site 1: Forward: 5’-CATGGGACTCCTCCAGCCT-3’, Reverse: 5’-CCTGGGCAAATGGGATACA-3’;

Site 2: Forward: 5’-GCCCAGGGAACTGCCACTC-3’, Reverse: 5’-ATCGGGAGGTGTCCGTTGG-3’.

Site 2: Forward: 5’-GCCCAGGGAACTGCCACTC-3’, Reverse: 5’-ATCGGGAGGTGTCCGTTGG-3’.

Dual-luciferase experiment

The ORAI3 WT (wild type), ORAI3 MUT (mutant) luciferase plasmids, and corresponding empty pGL3 vector were purchased from GUANNAN Bio (Hangzhou, China). Combined with the Renilla, these luciferase plasmids were transfected into HeLa and SiHa cells, respectively, using Lipofectamine 2000 for 24 h. Finally, a microplate reader was used to measure the luciferase activity, and the Firefly luciferase activity/the Renilla luciferase activity = the final relative luciferase activity.

RNA sequencing (RNA-seq)

To elucidate the effect of OCT4, the RNA-seq was performed (LC-BIO Hangzhou, China). Briefly, the OCT4-silenced and the corresponding control HeLa cells were cultured under hypoxic conditions or not (HeLa cells were treated with hypoxia for 12 h). These conditions included: (i) control siRNA-expressing HeLa cells cultured under normoxic conditions; (ii) control siRNA-expressing HeLa cells subjected to hypoxia for 12 h; (iii) OCT4-specific siRNA-expressing HeLa cells cultured under normoxic conditions; and (iv) OCT4-specific siRNA-expressing HeLa cells subjected to hypoxia for 12 h. Following these treatments, the cells were collected, and RNA-seq was performed to analyze the OCT4-related differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in each group. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) and Gene Ontology (GO) were used to analyze the enrichment pathways of DEGs.

Statistical analysis

The student’s two-tailed t-test and one-way ANOVA were used for the statistical tests in GraphPad Prism 6.0, and Tukey’s test was applied for all one-way ANOVAs; the results represent mean ± standard deviation (SD). A p-value less than 0.05 means statistically significant.
