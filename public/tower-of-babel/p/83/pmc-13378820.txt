Community-based care for patients with severe mental illness: A comparative study in Iran and selected countries
Authors: Zahra Mohammadi, Kourosh Zarea, Simin Jahani, Maryam Rassouli, Marziyeh Asadizaker, Samira Beiranvand
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The shift from institutional care to community-based mental health services is critical for improving the outcomes of patients with severe mental illness. This study compared community-based mental health service models in Iran with those in Canada, Australia, Italy, Turkey, and South Korea to identify the best practices for improving mental health care in Iran.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This descriptive-comparative study examined nine dimensions: policies and strategies, legal frameworks, service models, financing, workforce development, psychosocial interventions, information systems, civil society engagement, and research. Data were collected through a literature review of national and international databases.RESULTS:The findings revealed robust community-based strategies that demonstrated effective service delivery and integration practices in Canada, Australia, and Italy. In contrast, community mental health services in Iran are still developing and facing challenges such as limited resources, workforce shortages, and implementation barriers.CONCLUSION:To achieve effective community-based mental healthcare, Iran needs to strengthen its policies, increase funding, and develop its workforce. This study highlights the importance of adapting successful approaches from the countries reviewed, while addressing local challenges, improving mental health outcomes, and reducing stigma.

Introduction

Mental disorders range from minor disturbances in thought and behavior to severe mental illness (SMI) such as schizophrenia, bipolar disorder, and depression with psychotic features.[1] Studies have shown that people with serious mental illness (SMI) continue to experience high rates of disability, poverty, food insecurity, homelessness, stigma, discrimination, human rights violations, poor physical health, and premature death.[23] Psychiatric hospitals were the primary setting for the care of people with serious mental illness (SMI) in the 19th and early 20th centuries. The deinstitutionalization movement grew out of these institutions’ failure to care for people with SMI.[4] Deinstitutionalization in this area includes the closure or curtailment of psychiatric hospitals and development of smaller community mental health centers.[5] Integrating mental health services into the community not only increases access to and affordability of services but also raises awareness of mental health and reduces stigma and discrimination. Therefore, international programs and guidelines emphasize community mental health care.[6]

Different countries have different mental health approaches that are changing and tailored to their existing situations, goals, and policies. Simultaneously, with the development of community-based mental health worldwide, Iran was trying to change traditional and inefficient mental health services and adopt a community-based approach.[7] One of the main approaches is the integration of mental health services into Primary Health Care (PHC), and the implementation of this plan through district medical health networks in villages has been successful; however, in large cities, the desired outcome has not been observed.[8] The results of the last mental health survey in Iran showed that one in four people suffers from psychiatric disorders, and with a prevalence of 23.6%, it is on an increasing trend.[9] Additionally, the burden of mental disorders in Iran over the past 3 decades (1990–2019) at 77% has been higher than the general increase worldwide, which is 55%.[10] However, it has been found that two-thirds of people with psychiatric disorders in Iran do not use health services, and most of the services lack the desired quality.[11] In addition, urban demographic trends indicate that mental health services in cities must be delivered through a community-based approach.[12] Recently, patterns for community-based mental health services have been proposed, one of which is the provision of comprehensive services in a Community Mental Health Center (CMHC).[13]

The first CMHC system was established in Tehran in 2010. Owing to the effectiveness of these centers in providing care and patient satisfaction, in 2015, the Ministry of Health and Medical Education included the CMHC service model as a technical outpatient service model in the SERAJ plan, which is a comprehensive mental and social plan for Iranian communities to create a national model for providing prevention, treatment, and rehabilitation services. This plan was implemented as a pilot project for three cities. Subsequently, more cities were covered by this plan. In the SERAJ program, three mental health service packages were considered: basic mental health services in primary health centers, technical services through CMHCs with support from psychiatric units of general hospitals (for psychiatric hospitalization), and community action packages.[814]

Despite the development of these centers to expand rehabilitation services for patients with SMI, the number of centers is very small, existing only in a few cities. The population covered is limited (almost a ratio of 1 to 200,000), and there are challenges in providing services, such as a lack of workforce, trained staff, and financial resources to continue providing services.[14] Moreover, in most medium and large cities, much of the mental health care is provided outside the primary care network and in an uncoordinated manner by hospital centers and clinics affiliated with the Ministry of Health and Medical Education, as well as other government institutions and the private and charitable sectors, such as the Welfare Organization.[14] This shows the weak participation of the private and public sectors and the lack of a regulated mental health plan.[12] It should be noted that comparative studies appear to offer valuable insights into mental health service systems across various countries globally. Through similarities and differences, these studies can lead to numerous innovations and achievements for Iranian society. These innovations and achievements can include identifying strengths in the system and enhancing them, revealing weaknesses and suggesting solutions, transferring knowledge for effective mental health service models, recognizing effective policies for protecting the rights of individuals with mental illnesses, and creating similar laws. Additionally, using new technologies in mental health services, developing evaluation indicators, improving training for professionals, and designing awareness campaigns can help reduce stigma. These achievements can lead to improved mental health in Iranian society and enhance the quality of life for people with mental illnesses. Therefore, the current study aimed to describe and compare community-based services by investigating the structure of services in Italy, Canada, Australia, Turkey, South Korea, and Iran.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

This study employed a descriptive-comparative design to analyze and compare community-based mental health service models for patients with SMI across six countries: Italy, Australia, Canada, Turkey, South Korea, and Iran. A comparative approach based on the Brady and Hilker method was used, including description, interpretation, juxtaposition, and comparison.[15] According to this method, the first stage involves recording research phenomena based on evidence and gathering findings for the next stage. The second stage examines and analyzes this information. In the third stage, the information is classified to create a framework for comparing similarities and differences. Last, the comparison stage analyzes and compares the research problem regarding the details of similarities and differences, answering the research questions.

Study participants and sampling

The choice of countries was purposively made based on specific development criteria, including the maturity of mental health policies, the availability of community services, and overall integration within public health frameworks. Accordingly, Italy, a leader in community-based mental health services since 1978, closed all psychiatric hospitals by 2000 and has over 40 years of experience in this field, providing excellent care without using hospitals,[16] Canada, from the Americas region, has made very rapid progress in providing community-based mental health services since 2012.[17] Australia, from the Asia-Pacific region, has been a leader in this field since the start of this type of service in 1995.[18] Turkey, being in the same region as Iran in terms of geography and the proximity of social, economic, cultural, and religious conditions, and South Korea, from the Southeast Asian region, are both successful in providing this type of service.[19]

Statistical analysis method

The analysis focuses on nine key dimensions to facilitate a comprehensive comparison. These dimensions were taken from the World Health Organization’s Guidance on Community Mental Health Services: Promoting person-centered and rights-based approaches. These include Policies and Strategies, Legal Frameworks, Service Models, Financing, Workforce, Socio-Psychological Interventions, Information and Data Systems, Civil Society Engagement, and Research. The model is chosen for its comprehensive view of community-based mental health services from the World Health Organization, adapted to the country’s needs and challenges.

Results

In the current study, community-based mental health services in Iran and selected countries (Canada, Australia, Italy, South Korea, and Turkey) were compared, and the differences and similarities between each in this area were described.

Policies and strategies for mental health

Community-based mental health services in Canada are characterized by a decentralized approach, with provinces and territories largely responsible for service delivery. The country has taken important steps to develop comprehensive mental health strategies, including the Mental Health Commission of Canada and the first national mental health strategy, “Changing Directions, Changing Lives,” in 2012. Mental health strategies emphasize recovery-oriented approaches.[17]

Australia is known for developing comprehensive mental health strategies with a focus on integrating services into primary care. Australia is at the forefront of community-based mental health reform, with initiatives such as the National Mental Health Strategy and the Fifth National Mental Health and Suicide Prevention Plan. Its features include the integration of primary care through better access, a strong focus on early intervention, and innovative e-mental health services to reach remote populations.[20]

Italy is known as a pioneer in the field of deinstitutionalization, following the adoption of Law 180 in 1978, which led to the closure of psychiatric hospitals and the development of community-based services. Its main features include an extensive network of community mental health centers and a strong emphasis on social inclusion and recovery.[21]

Turkey has made significant progress in developing community-based mental health services through the implementation of the National Mental Health Action Plan, including the rapid expansion of Community Mental Health Centers (CMHCs) and the integration of mental health services into primary care.[22]

South Korea has made efforts to strengthen community-based mental health services, particularly since the enactment of the Mental Health Act in 1995. South Korea’s 5-year National Mental Health Plan aims to strengthen community-based services, including the expansion of community-based mental health care centers and a strong emphasis on suicide prevention strategies.[23]

Iran, located in the eastern Mediterranean, is a leader in primary healthcare and has developed a national policy for mental health services. Although the country has integrated these services into its primary care system, community-based services for severe mental disorders are still in their infancy.[24]

Law reform

Legal frameworks that support community mental health services vary significantly from country to country. Canada and Australia have strong legal protection for the rights of patients with mental health problems. These laws address issues such as the concepts of mental health and disability, mental health services, treatment and efficacy of psychiatric medications, consent to psychiatric treatment, limitations on involuntary admission, involuntary hospitalization and treatment, and privacy and confidentiality of psychiatric health records.[2526]

Italy’s Law 180 remains a landmark in the mental health legislation. It emphasizes the principle of patients’ freedom to choose treatment options, voluntary treatment, and involuntary admission only under specific conditions (including the need for emergency intervention, the patient’s refusal of treatment, and the lack of alternative treatment in the community) and, for people with acute conditions, hospitalization only in psychiatric wards of public hospitals (maximum 15 beds).[27]

Turkey has recently implemented legal reforms. The new draft of the Mental Health Law consists of 24 new laws related to various aspects, including respect and protection of the human rights of people with mental disorders, voluntary and involuntary hospitalization, treatment in facilities, rights of people with mental illness, and rights of service providers, regional laws, and mental health promotion, but implementation challenges remain.[28]

South Korea’s Mental Health Law was revised in 2016 to better protect patient rights. The main goal of the revised law was to support deinstitutionalization by shortening the length of stay, promoting discharge, and ultimately helping patients return to their lives in the community and preventing readmission to ensure patients’ human rights and autonomy.[23]

Moreover, a review of the existing laws in Iran showed that although there is no independent law in the field of mental health, various aspects of the rights of people with mental disorders have been pointed out in the body of laws of the country, such as civil law, family law, and Islamic punishment. The draft of the mental health law has been developed into six chapters and 50 articles with headings on definitions, hospitalization and compulsory treatment, special treatment methods, criminal patients, special groups, and other cases. However, this draft has not yet been approved by Parliament.[29]

Service model and providing community-based mental health services

In this dimension, reviews found that in four countries (Italy, Australia, Canada, and South Korea), different types of services and models exist in the community mental health sector, and hospitalizations are in day hospitals or psychiatric units in general hospitals. In addition, in Canada, hospital-based services (specialized hospitals and psychiatric units in general hospitals and emergency care) and community-based services (community-based MHA clinics, day or night care centers, residential settings, hospital outpatient care, and outpatient clinics) were included.[25] Australia has general psychiatric hospitals and general hospitals with a psychiatric department or unit, as well as private hospitals, residential mental health care centers, and community mental health care centers.[30]

Community Mental Health Centers (CMHC), Day Care Facilities (DCF), General Hospital Psychiatric Units (GHPU), and Residential Facilities (RF) are the most common service models in Italy.[27] In South Korea, the three divisions of mental health institutions (mental health hospitals, psychiatric clinics (public and private), and mental health rehabilitation facilities) include community-based services in various centers (e.g., daycare centers, group homes, residential rehabilitation centers), short-term/long-term, vocational rehabilitation centers, and community mental health welfare centers (case management for people with SMI, online/telephone crisis intervention programs).[31]

However, in Turkey, hospital-based service models are most prevalent in the mental health sector. According to the country’s National Mental Health Action Plan, a balanced care plan is being implemented before the full reform of mental health policy to a community-based model due to a lack of human resources. The plan includes the establishment of psychiatric clinics in general hospitals to provide treatment and rehabilitation for people with SMI.[32] In Iran, mental health services are also provided by health centers in rural areas and hospitals (private and public) in urban areas. In addition, government organizations that are not affiliated with the Ministry of Health, such as the Welfare Organization, provide services to people with SMI in day and night centers (providing long-term services) and day centers. In addition, in line with the promotion of urban mental health, community-based mental health centers have been established in Tehran and other cities.[15]

Financing

Another important aspect of community-based care is the provision of financial resources, which, based on studies conducted in selected countries, shows that the share allocated to mental health services is a smaller proportion of the total health budget.[33] Despite the lower budget allocation, the financing policy for many mental health services in these countries is to include mental health services under public insurance (free or with reduced insurance) and consider subsidies for psychotropic drugs.[20] Canada allocates 7% of its budget to mental health, has Medicare insurance coverage for citizens and permanent residents, and includes physician-provided mental health care under Medicare, publicly funds home care, or through government P/T contracts with provider agencies, services, or through government grants to patients to purchase services lacking insurance coverage for private psychologists and to provide long-term services in non-hospital centers and at the community level.[34]

Italy is covered by public health insurance for all citizens and permanent residents; free primary care, inpatient care, health screening, home care, residential care, and long-term semi-residential care are covered by this insurance, with no role in basic mental health care. Fees for residential services vary by region and are based on patient income.[34] Australia has universal public health insurance (Medicare), government subsidies through Medicare rebates for private–public services (e.g., psychiatry or allied health services [e.g., psychology]), and subsidies for psychotropic medications.[20]

South Korea: Mandatory health insurance system that determines the cost of outpatient psychiatric treatment daily, regardless of the type of treatment, with payment of 10% of the total cost by people with severe psychiatric illness (people with diagnosis codes in category (20TH)).[35]

The Turkish healthcare system is a combination of national and private health insurance components that finance mental health services, including tax-based financing and social security costs.[36]

The share of Iran’s mental health budget in the total government health budget is very small at around (3%). One of the most effective measures of the Iranian public health insurance system is mandatory insurance coverage (government, private, and supplementary) for all outpatient and inpatient psychiatric services from the beginning of the summer of 2016. It should be noted that there is a limit to the payment of inpatient treatment by insurance companies, and only up to 56 days of hospitalization per year are covered.[37]

Workforce development and training

The survey found that there are a wide variety of roles in the mental health workforce, including family physicians, social workers, psychologists, psychiatrists, nurses, and occupational therapists. The Canadian College of Psychiatrists and the Canadian Psychological Association play key roles in the education and credentialing of professionals. The association (CPA) provides educational resources and opportunities through conferences, workshops, and training programs for mental health professionals, aiming to improve clinical skills and knowledge.[38]

Australia benefits from a diverse mental healthcare workforce, including informal carers, which is highlighted in the Fifth National Mental Health Plan.[31] Mental health professionals are trained through undergraduate and postgraduate programs and overseen by professional bodies such as the Australian and New Zealand College of Psychiatrists and the Australian Psychological Society.[39]

Italy also has psychiatric rehabilitation specialists on staff, which other countries do not have.[40] In addition, Turkey has a smaller number of CMHC centers, which are below the standards of the Organization for Economic Cooperation and Development (OECD), and suffer from a lack of workforce.[41]

In South Korea, the main difference in mental health services is the level of training required for professionals compared with nonprofessionals. Nurses and social workers require at least 1 year of training, whereas clinical psychologists require at least 3 years of training and a master’s degree in clinical psychology. In South Korea, service users and their families are instrumental in improving community self-help services.[31]

In Iran, a group of workers, including psychiatrists, general practitioners, psychologists, and health workers, work in various centers, from health centers to psychiatric hospitals and clinics, whose number is small concerning the population. Iran is facing a shortage of mental health professionals. In community-based mental health service centers, a professional team is responsible for caring for 30 patients referred to the center each month. The team included a psychiatrist, a general practitioner, three clinical psychologists, a social worker, two nurses, an occupational therapist, two therapist liaisons, and a service provider. All clinical psychologists, social workers, nurses, and occupational therapists must have at least a bachelor’s degree and experience with psychiatric patients.[1124]

Sociopsychological interventions and psychotherapeutic medication

It is evident that a comprehensive range of services is accessible across all nations. These services encompass medication management, family interventions, Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT), social skills training, psychoeducational interventions for both families and individuals, cognitive modification techniques, problem-solving strategies, relaxation methods, counseling services, occupational therapy, lifestyle modification programs, and supported employment initiatives, among others.[2542] The nature of interventions and their continuity varies according to the social, cultural, and economic contexts of each nation. In Iran, specialized outpatient services for prevalent mental health disorders, including depression and anxiety, are provided through a collaborative framework. This approach incorporates daily rehabilitation and post-discharge care, which includes home visits and telephone follow-ups[14] [Table 1].

Information and data systems

A comparison of these cases shows that the countries under review have data collection systems and analyze and review them accordingly. For example, the Italian Ministry of Health collected information on mental health from relevant departments in each region.[21] In Canada, multiple databases include information on home care, prescription drugs, and the healthcare workforce.[43] In Australia, databases cover community mental healthcare and hospital data.[44] However, specific information on data-collection systems has not been found in South Korea. However, Turkey has a National Health Information System with subsets for mental health and addiction.[45] In Iran, the Integrated Health System (SIB) has been in place since 2015 to collect and register information on households and citizens online. It focuses on mental health services in health and community centers, allowing access to doctors and specialists through individuals’ electronic health records.[46]

Civil society, people, and communities

This aspect emphasizes the role of civil society in influencing political and social decisions and in addressing harmful attitudes and stigmas. In Canada, this involves engaging and uniting various stakeholders, given its federal structure and the fact that provinces and regions bear the primary responsibility for healthcare. This diversity of interest groups creates a platform for exchanging perspectives, exploring values and priorities, and tackling key issues and tensions to achieve a balanced national approach to mental health policies.[47] Italy has seen significant legal reforms regarding mental health care, notably through the Basilica Law, which is part of a broader social movement aimed at enhancing civil and social rights. This initiative seeks to fully restore citizenship in individuals with mental disorders by minimizing the use of involuntary care.[16] Australia encourages the involvement of individuals and key stakeholders, including indigenous communities and representatives of those affected by mental health issues and their caregivers, in the redesign and reform of the mental health development system at both the local and national levels.[48] Turkey has ratified the Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities (CRPD); however, individuals with mental health issues continue to encounter significant civil rights limitations as well as stigma and discrimination. Unfortunately, the efforts of nongovernmental organizations in this field have largely gone unrecognized.[28]

In South Korea, destigmatization efforts are initiated through program revisions and implementation of the mental health improvement and welfare services protection act. These initiatives encompass nationwide public education, a 5-year national mental health plan, and a comprehensive suicide prevention strategy.[49]

In Iran, policy documents for improving community mental health emphasize destigmatization as a key focus. This priority is important for redesigning mental health programs and educating policymakers and senior leaders.[50] The SERAJ program involves community action by creating a cooperation agreement among various departments, establishing public participation houses with over 20 community representatives, and forming self-help units for clients from basic and specialized centers. This promotes mental and social health awareness, reduces stigma, and increases health literacy.[11]

Research

Examination of this dimension shows that in all the countries studied, various agencies and institutions, including the Ministry of Health, the National Research Institute, the local research council, and universities, are responsible for mental health research, and most countries, including Canada, emphasize the need for evidence-based research in their mental health policies.[25] South Korea has also emphasized the development of translational research.[51] In 2013, Iran established a mental health research information bank at the National Medical Research Centre. It has now been transformed into a Psychiatric Research Center at the University of Tehran. This database contains research articles on the basic neuropsychological sciences, psychological sciences, social and cultural aspects of mental health, epidemiology of mental disorders, clinical sciences, and mental health services.[52]

Discussion

This study examined and compared nine dimensions of community mental health services in Iran and selected countries (Canada, Australia, Italy, Turkey, and South Korea). The landscape of community-based mental health services has evolved significantly across countries, reflecting diverse approaches tailored to meet the unique needs of their populations. This discussion synthesizes findings from Canada, Australia, Italy, Turkey, South Korea, and Iran, highlighting key themes and implications for the future of mental healthcare.

In terms of policy and strategy for mental health, the results showed that each of the selected countries adopted different approaches and strategies to provide community-based mental health services to people with SMI. These approaches include decentralization and integration of mental health services into primary healthcare, as well as the formulation and implementation of national strategies. These efforts have resulted in significant improvements in the mental health of the community. Other notable initiatives include pioneering the phasing out of institutional care and developing networks for community-based service providers. Iran, on the other hand, is making progress in developing these types of services, although it still has a long way to go compared to successful models, such as those in Italy and Canada. To improve the state of mental health services in Iran, it is necessary to adopt similar approaches and invest in mental health infrastructure.

Taban et al.’s[53] study in Iran indicates that there is a notable difference between what is ideally expected from mental health services and the actual results of current programs. Additionally, the failure to update or improve existing programs further hampers service delivery. On the other hand, Zeeman et al.[18] found that healthcare professionals and policymakers focus on reviewing mental health policies, training programs for specialist and nonspecialized health professionals, and testing new models.

Adopting the national mental health policy leads to the coordination of essential activities and services and ensures that the target group receives appropriate care and prevents gaps and inefficiency in the mental healthcare system.[54] Therefore, reviewing current policies considering social context and challenges in Iran is important. Policymakers need to offer suitable solutions to ensure the effective implementation of the program nationwide. Regarding the legal dimension, community-based mental health services varied significantly across countries. Some countries have strong laws protecting the rights of mental health patients, addressing issues such as the freedom to choose treatment, limitations on involuntary hospitalization, and privacy protection.[55] In others, new legal reforms are being implemented that focus on human rights and voluntary versus involuntary hospitalization, but they face implementation challenges.[2356] Additionally, in some countries, especially Iran, there is no independent legislation on mental health, and the rights of patients are primarily recognized within general laws, although new drafts of legislation are in development.[29] The United Nations Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities states that all persons with disabilities, including those with mental health problems, should have full access to human rights and fundamental freedoms.[55] On the other hand, a study by Shojaei et al.[57] in Iran showed that patients with psychiatric disorders face rights violations and lack authority from healthcare providers. Considering that the draft mental health law in Iran is in accordance with the cultural aspects and social structures of the country and emphasizes human dignity and respect for rights, it is important to promptly implement these laws to protect the rights of individuals with mental illnesses.

Regarding the type and model of service provision, mental health services can be provided in a variety of settings such as hospitals, homes, and communities.[58] Our review highlighted different types of services, including community mental health, integration with primary care, informal care, general hospital services, and reduced reliance on psychiatric hospitals. The WHO emphasizes that a key aspect of mental health services is their collaboration with social sector services, especially in times of crisis, and for ongoing treatment and support.[59] Also, numerous studies show that continuity of care for severe mental illnesses can reduce mortality, prevent suicide, and improve quality of life.[60] Iran has also attempted to address the comprehensiveness, continuity, and availability of services and their alignment with social services by integrating the CMHC model into the SERAJ program and creating three service packages.[11]

Community-based care relies on financial resources, but studies have shown that funding for mental health services is often a small fraction of the overall health budget in different countries.[43] For example, this study determined that leading countries such as Canada, Australia, and Italy allocate relatively small budgets to mental health services, although these services are often covered by universal insurance and may include subsidies for medications.[20] In Iran, the limited budget for mental health has created a significant gap between what is intended and what is implemented. Insufficient funding from the Ministry of Health limits the ability of Community Mental Health Centers (CMHC) to provide specialized social and support services.[53] Although insurance coverage for mental health services is mandatory, insurance companies often avoid providing comprehensive coverage due to concerns about low-income potential and high out-of-pocket costs, creating difficulties for patients in need of mental health care.[37] Integrating CMHCs into the national healthcare system may help establish a more sustainable budget and improve insurance coverage.[14]

Community health cannot be achieved without experienced or trained staff.[61] The WHO states that interprofessional education is essential in healthcare, allowing individuals to learn from each other and understand their roles and responsibilities.[61] This study found that all countries have a team of professionals working together to provide comprehensive and personalized care to patients with severe mental disorders.[2143] In Iran, CMHC centers have hired staff with different skills and specific roles.[11] However, the main challenges for these centers are finding qualified staff, managing resources, and securing funding.[14] In this regard, Lakshmana et al.[62] identified human resource issues and the lack of a multidisciplinary team as barriers to accessing mental health services. Countries at all income levels face challenges in training, deploying, retaining, and performing their healthcare workforce.[63] Therefore, maintaining an adequate health workforce requires effective strategies. The main strategies include financing, clear roles and responsibilities, increasing salaries and benefits, fair distribution of labor, effective supervision, continuous education and training, recognition for health workers, a welcoming work environment, community engagement and collaboration, effective teamwork, and proper use of information technology.[64] In addition, in the current study, in Australia and South Korea, the involvement of people with lived experiences, such as those with psychiatric disorders or their families, helps to improve self-help services.[3031] In this regard, it has emphasized the creation of educational content and the organization of training courses led by people with lived experience to improve attitudes and strengthen the workforce in the social and healthcare sectors.[59] Policymakers can consider including people with lived experiences in human resource development to improve mental health programs in the country.

International standards emphasize the need for human rights-based and evidence-based interventions.[59] Research shows that community-based services use a variety of social, psychological, and psychotherapeutic approaches.[42] When using these interventions, it is important to consider their limitations and the potential negative effects. The final decision should be based on an individual’s wishes, preferences, and informed consent.[59] Barlati et al.[65] stated that several different interventions and treatment programs are currently available (e.g., social skills training, psychoeducation, family interventions, cognitive behavioral therapy, physical exercise, and lifestyle interventions), but only interventions with proven effectiveness should be recommended for use in rehabilitation programs in clinical practice. Therefore, finding the best intervention for each individual and appropriate to the stage of the disease is the best approach. Additionally, combining different interventions can produce beneficial effects and can be an effective strategy if resources permit. In Iran, community-based mental health centers provide a combination of evidence-based services that have been shown to be effective in previous studies.[14]

The results of the surveys in this study showed that leading countries such as Canada and Australia have benefited from the participation of civil society, individuals, and advocates in the development and revision of their mental health policies.[4748] One of the benefits of this partnership has been to increase public awareness of mental disorders and to destigmatize patients with mental disorders.[48] Stigma has been identified as the most important and greatest obstacle to treatment-seeking, treatment retention, and rehabilitation processes.[66] In the Alissa’s study, stigma was identified as a potential barrier to seeking help for a mental health issue.[67] Because of the importance of this issue, many countries, including Iran, have destigmatized mental disorders as one of the goals and values of their healthcare systems.[68] In Iran, redesigning mental health programs and implementing an educational program to improve the awareness and attitudes of policymakers and leaders in the country,[50] as well as the community action package in the SERAJ program, can be considered measures to reduce stigma.[11] In addition, reforming structures and policies, developing effective strategies based on research, providing adequate funding to reduce stigma, training healthcare providers, and raising public awareness through education and campaigns can be effective approaches.[69]

Online data systems and databases are essential to government services. They help decision-makers identify problems, track progress, evaluate interventions, and make informed decisions about health policies and resource allocation.[46] In the current study, the reviewed countries benefited from information registration systems, and Iran also has different health information systems and databases, such as SIB, disease registration programs, and electronic health records. Currently, data from these systems are mainly used locally for management reporting, and there is no centralized analysis.[54] Due to the high cost of data management, integration, and use, these data should be prioritized by stakeholders.[46] Also, by using new techniques and reviewing past cases, country officials can design and implement more effective systems of planning, reporting, and accountability for mental health.[70]

In the field of research, the present study showed that various organizations and institutions are active in this area. Research is defined as the process of solving unsolved problems to expand knowledge.[71] To improve mental health research, it is essential to prioritize the agenda based on the country’s needs and resources and align research policies with the national mental health policy.[72] One study conducted in Iran showed that there is a growing trend in the field of mental health research. In this direction, and to facilitate access to research data in this field, the Mental Health Information Bank has been established, which can be used to make progress in the field of mental health.[52]

Limitations and recommendations

The study’s limitations include a potentially nonrepresentative sample of countries, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to other contexts. Variability in data collection methods and definitions across countries can lead to inconsistencies, while cultural, social, and economic differences may influence the implementation and perception of mental health services. Additionally, differences in the timing of data collection can introduce temporal biases, and qualitative assessments may be subject to personal biases. Limited access to comprehensive and current data, alongside a focus on specific aspects of mental health services, may overlook important factors and challenges in the practical implementation of community-based care.

For future research, investigating available ways to attract funding from other sources, developing and localizing clinical guidelines for providing comprehensive care to patients with serious mental illness (SMI), designing national programs and guidelines for destigmatization and raising public awareness, and designing and developing short-term training programs and in-service training for service providers in the field of mental health services can be considered.

Conclusion

The findings of the comparative study indicate that Iran’s community-based mental health care system was specifically designed and adapted to enhance access to mental health services by emulating models from other countries. This initiative was driven by the notable prevalence of mental health disorders, particularly in urban areas. However, this strategy faces challenges related to both financial and human resources as it is being implemented as a pilot plan. The number of these centers is quite limited, serving a relatively small population. Integrating this model into the ministry of health could potentially enable distinct funding opportunities. By utilizing the resources available at both public and private medical universities across the country, we can improve coverage and expand the number of these centers. Additionally, improving the home care infrastructure by designating certain hospital facilities as home care centers would be beneficial. The privatization of home care centers, as well as facilities dedicated to daily rehabilitation, alongside the engagement of private companies for investment, can significantly enhance the quality and comprehensiveness of services provided to patients with severe and persistent mental disorders. Additionally, leveraging the involvement of nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) and charitable entities to augment the budget may prove beneficial in delivering these essential services.
