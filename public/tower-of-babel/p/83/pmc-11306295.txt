Risk and mediation analyses of hemoglobin glycation index and survival prognosis in patients with sepsis
Authors: Aifeng He, Juanli Liu, Jinxin Qiu, Xiaojie Zhu, Lulu Zhang, Leiming Xu, Jianyong Xu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
An increasing number of studies have reported the close relation of the hemoglobin glycation index (HGI) with metabolism, inflammation, and disease prognosis. However, the prognostic relationship between the HGI and patients with sepsis remains unclear. Thus, this study aimed to analyze the association between the HGI and all-cause mortality in patients with sepsis using data from the MIMIC-IV database. In this study, 2605 patients with sepsis were retrospectively analyzed. The linear regression equation was established by incorporating glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and fasting plasma glucose levels. Subsequently, the HGI was calculated based on the difference between the predicted and observed HbA1c levels. Furthermore, the HGI was divided into the following three groups using X-tile software: Q1 (HGI ≤ − 0.50%), Q2 (− 0.49% ≤ HGI ≤ 1.18%), and Q3 (HGI ≥ 1.19%). Kaplan–Meier survival curves were further plotted to analyze the differences in 28-day and 365-day mortality among patients with sepsis patients in these HGI groups. Multivariate corrected Cox proportional risk model and restricted cubic spline (RCS) were used. Lastly, mediation analysis was performed to assess the factors through which HGI affects sepsis prognosis. This study included 2605 patients with sepsis, and the 28-day and 365-day mortality rates were 19.7% and 38.9%, respectively. The Q3 group had the highest mortality risk at 28 days (HR = 2.55, 95% CI: 1.89–3.44, p < 0.001) and 365 days (HR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.29–1.97, p < 0.001). In the fully adjusted multivariate Cox proportional hazards model, patients in the Q3 group still displayed the highest mortality rates at 28 days (HR = 2.02, 95% CI: 1.45–2.80, p < 0.001) and 365 days (HR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.08–1.56, p < 0.001). The RCS analysis revealed that HGI was positively associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Finally, the mediation effect analysis demonstrated that the HGI might influence patient survival prognosis via multiple indicators related to the SOFA and SAPS II scores. There was a significant association between HGI and all-cause mortality in patients with sepsis, and patients with higher HGI values had a higher risk of death. Therefore, HGI can be used as a potential indicator to assess the prognostic risk of death in patients with sepsis.

Introduction

Sepsis refers to the imbalance of systemic inflammatory response caused by bacteria, fungi, viruses, and other infections, resulting in serious life-threatening organ dysfunction. Recent epidemiological studies have estimated that sepsis is prevalent in approximately 48.9 million people worldwide, and septicemia-related deaths account for 19.7% of total deaths each year [1]. Although remarkable advances have been achieved in emergency/critical care medicine, sepsis remains the leading cause of death for severely ill patients, with an overall mortality rate of up to 40% [2]. The mortality rate of sepsis is also extremely high, emphasizing the need for early mortality risk prediction and more targeted triage of patients with sepsis to reduce mortality. The current clinical treatment for sepsis primarily includes antibiotics, infection removal, blood volume maintenance, vasoconstriction drugs, and mechanical ventilation. Despite so many treatment options, patients with sepsis have a poor prognosis. Early identification of sepsis is therefore critical, underscoring the requirement for biomarkers that can aid clinicians in diagnosing sepsis more quickly and assessing patient prognosis. Moreover, sepsis is reported to be associated with elevated inflammatory indicators and organ dysfunction. A few promising biomarkers for sepsis prognosis have been previously identified, including PCT [3], presepsin [4], IL-6 [5], suPAR [6], and MR-proADM [7].

Glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) is formed by the non-enzymatic reaction of intracellular HbA1 with glucose, exhibiting certain differences between its measured and predicted levels [8]. Due to factors related to glucose metabolism, the relationship between HbA1c and FPG may vary significantly between individuals. The HGI quantifies this alteration in HbA1c levels. The hemoglobin glycation index (HGI), first proposed by Hempe et al. in 2002 [8], reflects the difference between the measured and predicted HbA1c values and is calculated using a linear regression equations based on average blood sugar levels, thereby overcoming the disadvantages of HbA1c tests. Numerous studies have established that the HGI is independently and positively correlated with the risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases as well as metabolism and inflammation [9–11].

Since there is no clear study on the relationship between HGI and sepsis now, the purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between HGI and all-cause mortality in sepsis patients.

Methods

Study data

This study used data from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care (MIMIC)-IV 2.0 database. MIMIC is a large, freely available database for clinical research in emergency and critical care medicine. These data are sourced mainly from the intensive care system of the Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center between 2008 and 2019, comprising basic patient information, vital signs, laboratory indicators, surgical information, medication records, various scores, and survival prognosis [12]. A member of our research team has been granted access to the database after training and is responsible for data extraction (certification number: 44274909). Given that MIMIC is an anonymous public database conforming to the review board protocol, all personal information was de-identified, using only random codes to identify specific patients. As a result, the Ethics Committee of Binhai County People’s Hospital waived its requirements for informed consent and ethical approval.

Study population

We used the International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 and -10 codes (99,592, 19, A419, R6521, R6520, and A4159) to enroll intensive care unit (ICUs) patients admitted with sepsis. The patient exclusion criteria were as follows (Fig. 1): (1) age < 18 years; (2) missing information on laboratory indicators; (3) missing Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II) score; or (4) lacking information on previous diseases such as hypertension and diabetes.Fig. 1Flow chart of the patient selection process in this study

Flow chart of the patient selection process in this study

Data extraction

The study data were extracted and managed using structured query language (SQL), PostgreSQL tool (version 15), and STATA 17.0 (64-bit). Various data, including admission number, age, gender, disease history, laboratory indices on initial ICU admission, length of stay, and date of death, SOFA score, SAPS II score, were extracted based on the structured code. The extracted data were then processed using STATA software. Previous diseases primarily included hypertension, diabetes, stroke, and heart failure. Laboratory indicators mainly consisted of white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet (PLT) count, lactic acid, alanine transaminase, aspartate transaminase, electrolyte, glucose, HbA1c, albumin, urea nitrogen, and blood creatinine levels, along with the 28-day and 365-day mortality data for our study analysis. Only the first recorded value of each variable within 24 h after admission was included in the analysis.

Definitions of exposure variables and outcome events

Linear regression models of fasting blood glucose (FPG) and HbA1c levels were established based on data from all study patients. With HbA1c as the dependent variable and FPG as the independent variable, the linear regression equation was established: predicted HbA1c = 0.17 × FPG + 4.98 (r = 0.609, p < 0.001). The difference between the observed and predicted values of HbA1c is then calculated as HGI [13]. The HGI was further divided via X-tile software into three groups (Fig. 2): Q1 (≤ − 0.50), Q2 (− 0.49 ≤ HGI ≤ 0.18), and Q3 (≥ 1.19). The results relevant to this study were patients with sepsis as well as 28-day and 365-day mortality, with sepsis being defined by a SOFA score of 2 according to the third international consensus definition (Sepsis-3.0) [14].Fig. 2X-tile analysis demonstrating the optimal cut points for the HGI. Statistical analysis

Using the HGI, we divided the baseline characteristics of all study patients into three groups. Continuous variables were shown as mean ± standard deviation. We used ANOVA (analysis of variance) to assess inter-group differences, followed by Tukey’s HSD test for specific comparisons. Categorical variables were reported as percentages. We used Chi-square tests for inter-group comparisons. For significant differences found initially, we applied Bonferroni correction as a post hoc analysis to adjust for multiple comparisons.

Kaplan–Meier plots and log-rank test were used for survival analysis. The relationship between the HGI and the incidence of clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis was investigated by applying multiple Cox proportional hazards models. In addition, a more comprehensive assessment of continuous variable HGI and clinical outcomes was performed using restricted cubic spline analysis. Moreover, a mediation effect analysis was conducted to determine whether the HGI mediates the survival of patients with sepsis through other variables. In this analysis, a dose–response relationship was posited between the HGI (X) and survival (Y), which was mediated by an intermediate variable (M). Furthermore, the total effect of the HGI was split into a direct effect (DE) and an indirect effect (IE) on survival. According to the criteria set by Baron and Kenny [15], the mediation effect of M is confirmed when the associations between X and M and between M and Y are statistically significant. Finally, subgroup analyses were performed based on previous medical conditions, including the presence or absence of the following: hypertension, diabetes, acute kidney injury, heart failure, and stroke.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.3.0) and STATA 17.0 (64 bits) software and R packages such as “mediation” and “rms,” while graphical visualizations were generated using the R package “ggplot2.” The threshold of statistical significance was set at a bilateral p-value of < 0.05.

Kaplan–Meier analysis for cumulative risk of 28-day and 365-day mortality

As illustrated in Fig. 3, a comprehensive Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis was meticulously conducted to compare the incidence of primary outcomes among the three distinct HGI groups. The results of this detailed analysis indicated that the 28-day mortality rate in the Q1 group was significantly lower than that observed in the Q2 and Q3 groups, with these differences being statistically significant as evidenced by the log-rank test (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3A). Furthermore, when examining the 365-day mortality rates, it was found that the Q3 group exhibited a markedly higher mortality rate compared to the other two groups, with these differences also being statistically significant on the log-rank test (log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3B).Fig. 3Kaplan–Meier survival analysis curve for comparing A: 28-day all-cause mortality and B: 365-day all-cause mortality between the three HGI groups

Correlation analysis of the HGI with outcome events

Using Q1 as a reference, we constructed multiple Cox proportional risk analysis models to examine the relationship between HGI and 28-day and 365-day mortality (Table 2). In the unadjusted model, the analysis showed that both Q2 and Q3 groups were significantly associated with an increased risk of 28-day mortality. The Q2 group had a hazard ratio of 1.41 (95% confidence interval: 1.10–1.82, p = 0.008), while the Q3 group had a hazard ratio of 2.55 (95% confidence interval: 1.89–3.44, p < 0.001). The Q3 group exhibited the highest risk. Subsequent analysis for 365-day mortality revealed that while the Q2 group was linked to an increased mortality risk, this association was not statistically significant. However, the Q3 group was significantly associated with higher 365-day mortality (hazard ratio = 1.59, 95% confidence interval: 1.29–1.97, p < 0.001). In Model 1 (adjusted for age and sex), both Q3 and Q2 groups were still significantly associated with an elevated risk of 28-day mortality, with Q3 having the highest risk (hazard ratio = 2.57, 95% confidence interval: 1.91–3.47, p = 0.129). Additionally, this elevated association with the risk of 28-day mortality was slightly reduced compared to that observed in the unadjusted model. In the case of the risk relationship with 365-day mortality, the analysis did not demonstrate any association with the Q2 group, while the Q3 group was significantly associated with 365-day mortality risk (hazard ratio = 1.61, 95% confidence interval: 1.31–1.99, p < 0.001). After further adjustments in model 2 (including for comorbidities such as hypertension, heart failure, and stroke), the Q2 group was found to have no associations with 28-day and 365-day mortality risk. In contrast, the Q3 group remained associated with an increased mortality risk at 28 and 365 days. However, the extent of this relationship continued to decline (28 -day mortality risk: hazard ratio = 2.34, 95% confidence interval: 1.69–3.22, p < 0.001; 365-day mortality risk: hazard ratio = 1.51, 95% confidence interval: 1.20–1.89, p < 0.001). In model 3, which was additionally adjusted for SOFA score, SAPS II score, WBC count, PLT count, and HGB, albumin, sodium, potassium, blood urea nitrogen, and serum creatinine levels, the associations of the Q3 group with 28-day and 365-day mortality risk remained stable (hazard ratio = 2.02, 95% confidence interval: 1.45–2.80, p < 0.001 and hazard ratio = 1.28, 95% confidence interval: 1.08–1.56, p = 0.033, respectively).Table 2Association between the HGI and posterior survival in patients with sepsisHGI groupsUnadjusted modelAdjusted model 1Adjusted model 2Adjusted model 3HR (95% CI)P valueHR (95% CI)P valueHR (95% CI)P valueHR (95% CI)P value28-day all-cause mortalityHGI (≤ − 0.50)RefRefRefRefHGI (− 0.49, 1.18)1.41 (1.10, 1.82)0.0081.37 (1.06, 1.77)0.0151.26 (0.95, 1.67)0.1121.18 (0.89, 1.56)0.249HGI (≥ 0.19)2.55 (1.89, 3.44) < 0.0012.57 (1.91, 3.47) < 0.0012.34 (1.69, 3.22) < 0.0012.02 (1.45, 2.80) < 0.001HGI group trend < 0.001 < 0.001 < 0.001 < 0.001365-day all-cause mortalityHGI (≤ − 0.50)RefRefRefRefHGI (− 0.49, 1.18)1.13 (0.96, 1.33)0.1291.08 (0.92, 1.28)0.331.03 (0.85, 1.24)0.7720.96 (0.79, 1.15)0.612HGI (≥ 0.19)1.59 (1.29, 1.97) < 0.0011.61 (1.31, 1.99) < 0.0011.51 (1.20, 1.89) < 0.0011.28 (1.08, 1.56)0.033HGI group trend0.001 < 0.001 < 0.001 < 0.001Unadjusted model: no variables were adjustedModel 1: Adjusted for age and genderModel 2: Adjusted for age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, acute kidney injury, and strokeModel 3: Adjusted for age, gender, hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, acute kidney injury, stroke, SOFA score, SAPS II score, WBC count, PLT count, and HGB, albumin, sodium, potassium, urea nitrogen, and creatinine levelsHGI Hemoglobin glycation index; HR Hazard Ratio; SOFA Sequential Organ Failure Assessment; SAPSII Simplified Acute Physiology Score II; WBC White blood cell; PLT Platelet; HGB Hemoglobin

Analysis of mediating factors for the effect of HGI on 28-day and 365-day mortality rates

The impact of the HGI on mortality among patients with sepsis was further investigated by conducting a mediation analysis, as depicted in Fig. 5. The findings showed that the HGI potentially influenced mortality risk in patients with sepsis through its interaction with the SOFA and SAPS II scores. In particular, the SOFA score mediated 20.7% of the association between the HGI and 28-day mortality (IE = 0.004, 95% CI: 0.003–0.010; DE = 0.017, 95% CI: 0.008–0.030, Fig. 5A) and 31.9% of the relationship with 365-day mortality (IE = 0.005, 95% CI: 0.003–0.010; DE = 0.010, 95% CI: 0.008–0.020, Fig. 5C). Moreover, mediation by the SAPS II score accounted for 24.5% of the correlation between the HGI and 28-day mortality (IE = 0.005, 95% CI: 0.003–0.010; DE = 0.016, 95% CI: 0.007–0.030, Fig. 5B) and 50.5% of the association with 365-day mortality (IE = 0.007, 95% CI: 0.005–0.010; DE = 0.007, 95% CI: 0.006–0.020, Fig. 5D). Thus, the SOFA and SAPS II scores are comprehensive indicators with metrics for evaluating the circulatory, respiratory, urinary, and hematological systems. Although initial analyses of contrasting indicators such as WBC and PLT counts demonstrated no significant differences, significant disparities were evident in the comprehensive assessments provided by the SOFA and SAPS II scores. In the case of patients with sepsis but no other chronic diseases, our analysis indicated an elevated mortality risk in the Q3 group, consistent with the previously mentioned results. Discussion

Sepsis refers to the imbalance of systemic inflammatory response caused by bacterial, fungal, viral, and other infections, which can progress to septic shock, multiple organ failure, and even death if not diagnosed and treated early. Furthermore, sepsis often presents with non-specific clinical symptoms, and its diagnosis and severity assessment can be challenging. Thus, assessing biomarkers in patients with sepsis may provide a valuable reference for clinical treatment decisions and disease prognosis. It has been consistently reported that high HGI is associated with an increased risk of hypoglycemia in people with diabetes and an elevated risk of chronic vascular disease in people with and without diabetes [16]. Several previous studies have also demonstrated a close relationship between the HGI and adverse cardiovascular events, kidney damage, and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [9–11, 17]. At present, there is no clear study on the relationship between HGI and sepsis. The purpose of this study was to investigate the relationship between HGI and all-cause mortality in sepsis patients using data from the MIMIC-IV database. Our findings show a significant association between HGI and all-cause mortality in this patient population.

WBC is the most commonly used indicator to determine whether there is infection, and it is also one of the common indicators in the early diagnosis and prognosis evaluation of sepsis. When the body is infected and tissues are damaged, the WBC rapidly increases the accumulation and devours of invading pathogens. However, WBC has certain limitations in the diagnosis of sepsis due to individual differences and easy to be affected by factors such as exercise and surrounding environment [18]. FENG et al. [19] demonstrated that low platelet counts increase the risk of sepsis in patients with acute pancreatitis. Inflammatory factors negatively regulate hematopoietic function, inhibit bone marrow hematopoietic function, reduce the number of mature megakaryocytes, and lead to a decrease in the number of platelets. SCHUPP et al. have demonstrated that platelets are independent predictors of 30-day all-cause mortality in patients with sepsis and septic shock [20]. Albumin can be used as an independent risk factor for mortality prediction [21], and the decrease of albumin is closely related to inflammatory response [22]. Decreased albumin and elevated inflammatory markers are predictors of infection and organ dysfunction. In this study, WBC in Q3 group was significantly higher than those in Q1 and Q2 groups, while albumin and were platelets significantly lower in Q3 than in Q1 and Q2.

SOFA and SAPS II scores in Q3 group were significantly higher than those in Q1 and Q2 groups. In 2016, the American Society of Critical Care Medicine and the European Society of Critical Care Medicine jointly released the latest definition of sepsis (Sepsis-3.0) [23]. The evaluation criteria for detecting sepsis involve the SOFA score, which refers to an acute infection-related change in the total SOFA score of ≥ 2 points. Moreover, the SAPS II score includes seventeen variables, encompassing twelve physiological variables, age, hospitalization type, and three chronic diseases (including acquired immune deficiency syndrome, metastatic cancer, and hematologic malignancies). The SAPS II, which is based on a large international sample of patients, provides an estimate of the mortality risk [24]. The higher the SOFA score, the more serious the situation. SOFA score has a certain value in the diagnosis and prognosis of sepsis [25, 26]. Compared with SOFA score, SAPSII improved the ability to identify, calibrate, and predict mortality in patients with sepsis [27]. Mediation analysis showed that the HGI might influence mortality risk in patients with sepsis through interactions with the SOFA and SAPS II scores. Specifically, the SOFA score mediated 20.7% of the association between the HGI and 28-day mortality and 31.9% of the relationship with 365-day mortality. The mediation by the SAPS II score accounted for 24.5% of the correlation between the HGI and 28-day mortality and 50.5% of the association with 365-day mortality. Thus, the SOFA and SAPS II scores are comprehensive indicators of sepsis prognosis. The results of survival curve analysis in this study showed that the higher the HGI, the higher the 28-day and 365-day sepsis mortality. Moreover, the Cox proportional risk analysis suggested that the Q3 group was still significantly associated with an increased risk of death at 28 and 365 days. Therefore, the higher the HGI, the worse the prognosis of sepsis.

While baseline analysis showed significantly higher numbers of patients with hypertension, diabetes, heart failure, and stroke in group Q2 than in groups Q1 and Q3, both relatively high and low HGI values were associated with an increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events [28, 29]. Another study found that a low HGI was significantly associated with the risk of death in patients with critical coronary artery disease in a fully adjusted Cox proportional risk model [11]. This finding may be ascribed to the stress-related increase in blood sugar levels among patients with severe coronary artery disease. Elevated HGI values can also increase stroke risk [30] and may be a novel risk factor for chronic kidney disease [17]. Another recent investigation highlighted the potential association of telomere attrition with high HGI, possibly via the mediating effect of TNF-α [31]. Advanced glycation end products (AGEs) are covalent complexes consisting of non-enzymatic reactions between amino acids and reducing sugars or oxidized lipids [32]. Hence, the role of the HGI in reflecting AGE levels is partially obvious. In addition, AGEs can cause tissue damage by promoting the production of reactive oxygen species [33]. Considering all these findings, we postulate that the influence of the HGI on sepsis spans a range of metrics, underlining the requirement for a complex systemic approach.

The higher the HGI, the higher the mortality. But in our subgroup analysis with 28-day mortality as the endpoint, the mortality risk in patients with sepsis and accompanying chronic conditions such as hypertension and diabetes was closely associated with the Q2 and Q3 groups. In the case of patients with sepsis and without chronic diseases, an elevated 28-day mortality risk was linked only to the Q3 group. Therefore, the ability of the HGI to predict short-term mortality may differ between patients having sepsis with or without comorbid chronic disease.

Our study is the first investigation to use data from the MIMIC-IV database to combine the risk factors for sepsis and HGI. It is also suggested that HGI may affect the survival prognosis of patients via multiple indicators related to SOFA and SAPS II scores. Nevertheless, this study has a few limitations that should be considered. Due to the limitations of the included study data, our study could not distinguish between the different grades of chronic disease, thus preventing the evaluation of the association between the HGI and all-cause mortality in patients with sepsis and chronic diseases of varying severity.

Conclusions

Our study findings show that the impact of the HGI on sepsis involves multiple indicators, thereby emphasizing the need for a multifaceted systemic strategy. Furthermore, this study revealed the association between HGI and all-cause mortality in sepsis. Sepsis patients with higher HGI levels have a poorer prognosis. Therefore, the HGI may serve as a potential indicator to assess prognostic mortality risk in this patient population. Finally, the ability of HGI to predict short-term mortality from sepsis may vary in patients with or without chronic disease. This is worth exploring further.
