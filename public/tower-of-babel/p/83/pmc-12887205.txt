Who Is That Cardiology Fellow? A Cluster Trial Examining the Impact of Cardiology Fellows' Biographical Information on Patient Perceptions of Patient‐Centered Care
Authors: Zachary Force, Caroline D. Martin, Zeba Hashmath, Robin Fernandes, Mazen Iqbal, Ateet Kosaraju, Ahmed Qavi, Dishita Pandya, Sara Samaan, Chetan Singh, Kyaw Khant, Kesavan Sankaramangalam, Kunjan Udani, Avinash Singh, Rajasekhar Nekkanti, Samuel F. Sears
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe American College of Cardiology has called for greater emphasis on patient‐centered care, but methods to accomplish this goal have not yet been elucidated, particularly in training settings. This study examined the effect of providing fellow biographical information versus medical clinic information on perceptions of patient‐centered care, patient–provider relationships, and medical visit engagement in a large, rural‐serving cardiology training clinic.MethodsParticipating patients were assigned to receive either a biography about their cardiology fellow provider or information about the clinic facility. Participants completed questionnaires assessing their perceptions of patient‐centered care and their relationship with their provider following the clinic visit.ResultsParticipants (N=198; mean (M) age 62.72; 47.5% Black; 54.5% male) who received biographies perceived fellows as engaging in significantly higher levels of patient‐centered care behavior (M: 74.54 versus 69.83, respectively, P=0.003). Moreover, the provider biography group reported significantly greater relationship depth with their providers (M: 26.72 versus 23.75, respectively, P=0.015) compared with controls, and were more likely to ask a question during their visit (96.9% versus 87%, respectively).ConclusionsPatients reported improved perceptions of patient‐centered care and asked more questions when provided with fellow biographies. Biographical information about cardiology fellows may be a viable and low‐cost method of improving perceptions of patient‐centered care with minimal provider burden.

Patient‐centered care (PCC) has been defined as a method of health care delivery that values the whole person and includes 5 conceptual dimensions: utilizing a biopsychosocial perspective, humanizing both patients and providers, developing a therapeutic alliance, and distributing equitable power and responsibility for decision‐making. 1 Recent reviews have reported that PCC was associated with improvements in patient quality of life including improved psychological health, physical health, perceptions of their illness and associated burden, health knowledge, self‐care and health management skills, and overall satisfaction. 2 Furthermore, PCC was associated with reduced hospital admissions, length of hospital stays, and overall health care utilization. 2

The American College of Cardiology has highlighted the need for PCC within cardiology, emphasizing the importance of bidirectional communication between providers and patients, including shared decision‐making and collaborative goal setting. 3 Previous work has demonstrated that increased perceptions of PCC in patients with chronic heart failure were associated with reduced symptom burden, improved quality of life, and increased patient engagement during medical visits. 4 Despite PCC's demonstrated importance and impact on patient outcomes, there remains a disconnect between policy and practice. Barriers to delivering PCC include inconsistency in how PCC is taught, evaluated, and modeled, 5 and time constraints due to financial and productivity demands placed on physicians. 6 Unsurprisingly, concrete tactics and strategies to enhance perceptions of PCC in cardiology settings are unknown. However, increasing familiarity to enhance the patient–provider relationship (PPR) has been suggested as a potential, standardized, low‐burden, cost‐effective method to improve perceptions of PCC.

The purpose of the present study was to determine the impact of offering biographical information about cardiology fellows versus medical clinic information on perceptions of PCC and PPR in a cardiology fellow training clinic.

METHODS

Following approval from the institutional review board at East Carolina University, recruitment for the present study began. One hundred ninety‐eight participants were recruited by the principal investigator (ZF) during their regularly scheduled cardiology appointments at the East Carolina Heart Institute, a rural‐serving outpatient cardiology clinic at East Carolina University in Greenville, North Carolina. Patients were eligible for participation if they were 18 years of age or older and were proficient in reading and speaking English. A power analysis conducted using G*Power indicated a sample size of 102 would be sufficient for a medium effect size. Therefore, a sample of 102 was estimated as sufficient for detecting potential between‐group differences. Because this was a novel study, a larger sample size was sought to reduce the chances of false‐negative effects of the intervention. Recruitment began in March 2023 and ended in April 2023, upon achieving a sufficient sample size. All patients who agreed to participate during this period were enrolled. The present author (ZF), at the time a doctoral student in clinical health psychology, provided patients with information about the study and obtained informed consent before patient participation in the study. To maintain patient confidentiality, all data were coded and deidentified upon collection. Data may be made available upon reasonable request by contacting the corresponding author.

Procedure

Participants were assigned to either the intervention or control group. Intervention group participants received a handout with biographical information about their respective fellows (Figure 1). Control group participants received a handout with basic information about the East Carolina Heart Institute clinic facility (Figure 1). Typical standard of care at East Carolina Heart Institute is that patients receive no informational handouts before their medical visits. To control for group assignment contamination, participants were grouped based on the week of their appointment. For example, patients with appointments during the first and third week of the month would be assigned to the intervention group, while those with appointments during the second and fourth week of the month would be assigned to the control group. This was done to prevent the biographical information from being shared in the waiting room or study materials being left behind and found by the other group. Cardiology fellows were not made aware of which group was being seen.

Materials

Provider Biography

Provider biographies were created for each participating fellow. Fellows were supplied with a list of 30 questions, both personal and professional, and were instructed to only answer questions that they felt comfortable conveying to their patients. The answers were then compiled to create a single‐page biography for each fellow (see Figure 2 for a de‐identified example biography). The fellows were consulted for final approval of their respective biographies.

Provider biography and control information handouts.

Control Information

During control weeks, participants were given a single‐page handout with basic structural information about the clinic facility. This information was taken from the institution's website and included the institute's mission statement and information regarding the range of services available (Figure 2). This control information handout was provided to control for the act of giving information.

Measures

Demographics

Participant demographic information was collected from their medical records including age, sex, ethnicity, and past medical history. Information about medical comorbidities was collected to calculate a Charlson Comorbidity Index score for each participant. Clinic history information was also collected including whether this was the patient's first appointment with their respective fellow, and if not, how many visits had occurred.

Cardiology fellow demographic information was collected via self‐report including sex and fellowship training year.

Participants Measures

Participants completed the following measures during their visit:

Patient–Professional Interaction Questionnaire

The Patient–Professional Interaction Questionnaire assesses PCC from the patient's perspective. 7 The Patient–Professional Interaction Questionnaire was adapted from the Provider–Patient Relationship Questionnaire, which was designed to assess PCC behavior from a provider perspective in a hospital setting. 8 The Patient–Professional Interaction Questionnaire is a 16‐item measure that utilizes a 5‐point Likert scale and comprises 4 subscales: Effective Communication, Interest in Patient's Agenda, Empathy, and Patient Involvement in Care. The subscale scores can be summed for a global score of overall provider PCC behavior with a minimum score of 16 and a maximum score of 80. The scale has demonstrated good internal consistency for the combined scale (α=0.95) and for each subscale: EC (α=0.86), IPA (α=0.92), E (α=0.76), and PIC (α=0.85). The scale has demonstrated sensitivity over multiple visits with the same provider. 7

Patient–Doctor Depth of Relationship Tool

The Patient–Doctor Depth of Relationship Tool assesses the depth of the PPR, or “PPR Quality.” 9 The scale consists of a single‐factor solution containing 8 items that utilize a 5‐point Likert scale and has been found to have high reliability (α=0.93) and good test–retest reliability (α=0.87). 9

Provider Measures

To minimize intrusion on patient care, fellows were asked about patient behavior following the medical encounter. Specifically, fellows were asked to report whether patients had asked a question. Examples of questions asked of fellows by their patients include “I love Asheville, too. What are some of your favorite places to go there?” and “How does this medication (referencing atorvastatin) actually lower my cholesterol?”

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were conducted in SPSS 28. 10 Data were cleaned and screened before proceeding with analysis, including the identification of outliers and normality. Independent samples t tests were performed to determine whether the type of information provided impacted perceptions of PCC and PPR. A χ2 test of independence was performed to determine whether patients asked a question differed based on the type of information provided.

RESULTS

Provider Demographics

All institutional cardiology fellows (N=12: 33% women) in the 3‐year fellowship participated, with 4 fellows in each fellowship year. Fellows were evenly distributed among the 3 groups: first‐year fellows (30%), second‐year fellows (34.5%), and third‐year fellows (34.5%). Fellows were racially diverse; of our 12 fellows, 66.7% were Asian Indian, 16.7% were Asian Pakistani, 8.3% were Arabic, and 8.3% were Asian Myanmar.

Patient Information

During recruitment, 259 patients were approached to participate in the study; 23% of patients (N=61) either declined to participate or did not have enough time to read their respective handouts before their appointment. A total of 198 patients (98 intervention and 100 control participants) were included in the study (see Table 1 for patient demographic information). Significant ethnicity differences existed between groups, with all Hispanic patients being assigned to the control group. However, ethnicity was not used as a covariate in the analyses. Additionally, 100% of patients in the study reported reading their respective study material (fellow biography or clinic facility information).

DISCUSSION

The current study demonstrated that patients who were given biographical information about their cardiology fellows perceived them as engaging in higher levels of patient‐centered care, reported higher levels of relationship depth with their providers, and asked more questions during their visit. These results are encouraging for improving perceptions of PCC and patient engagement with a cost‐effective approach such as provider biographies. These results are consistent with prior evidence demonstrating that a history of prior visits with a medical provider significantly influences PCC behavior. 7 Perhaps the perceptions of familiarity provided by the biography may mimic familiarity that comes from having a prior visit with a provider. Methods to increase familiarity may be particularly important for the first clinical encounter between patient and provider as a means of building rapport and decreasing patient apprehension. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14

As noted above, our provider sample was particularly diverse, with 33% of the cardiology fellows being female, compared with a national average of 21.5% as of 2016. 15 Furthermore, our sample of fellows was racially diverse, particularly when considering the United States national average of 40.5% White cardiology fellows as of 2016. 15 The generalizability of these findings could be idiosyncratic to the samples involved here, but the principle of providing biographical information is promising. Current evidence regarding the impact of provider–patient race concordance on perceptions of PCC is mixed and deserving of further research. 16

This study extends the literature related to PCC and the training of cardiology fellows in that, to our knowledge, this is the first study to examine perceptions of PCC in a sample of cardiology fellowship trainees. Interpersonal strengths and opportunities to improve PCC can enhance cardiology fellowship training and career success. Importantly, the offering of a fellow biography appeared to cause little to no clinic workflow disruption. Fellowship poses an excellent opportunity to heed the call of the American College of Cardiology and demonstrate the value of PCC; this foundational practice base may have cascading effects as fellows become independent practitioners.

This study illustrates the ease and benefit of improving perceptions of PCC within a cardiology setting using provider biographies. Continued refinement of these biographies may help optimize these effects while minimizing patient and provider burden. Future research may explore structural approaches to improving perceptions of PPC by educating patients on their fellow's qualifications and explaining the role of the attending provider to aid in the continuity of care across multiple cardiology fellows. In addition, further information emphasizing the importance of training clinics in shaping the next generation of cardiologists could prove beneficial. Future studies may benefit from exploring potential benefits to providers of improving PCC and PPR, such as reducing burnout or increasing job satisfaction.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, administrative and logistical factors, such as when the appointment began, whether on time or late, and visit length may have influenced the patient's perceptions of their cardiology fellow, and therefore, the results, because these factors were not controlled for. In addition, potential unknown confounders such as personal characteristics of the provider, previous clinical experiences with other providers, and patient characteristics were not controlled for. Prior visits with a provider may play a larger role in PPR in cardiology settings, given that patients can be seen in both inpatient and outpatient settings with differing levels of acuteness. Patient knowledge of their provider may be important to examine broadly, but in a clinical training setting, both a cardiology fellow and an attending are always present. This may provide some blunting of the effect, given that participants learned only about the cardiology fellow in this study. The study was not fully blinded, because fellows could have been able to determine the treatment group of their patients. This may have impacted study results via the Hawthorne effect, where an individual's behavior changes because of the knowledge that they are being studied. 17 Finally, East Carolina Heart Institute is a rural‐serving clinic. Although this factor makes the study unique, it may also impact the generalizability of these data to other training settings.

CONCLUSIONS

PCC remains an important objective for cardiology care for both fellowship trainees and attending cardiologists. The current study demonstrated that providing patients with literature‐informed, biographical information about their cardiology fellow significantly improved both perceptions of their fellow's PPC behaviors and PPR, and increased patient engagement during their medical visit. This study offers a feasible and cost‐effective method to improve perceptions of PCC with minimal provider burden that can be implemented at any clinic.
