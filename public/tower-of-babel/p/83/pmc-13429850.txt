Obesity in Adults with Type 1 Diabetes Mellitus
Authors: Adnan Batman, Cem Sulu, Dilek Yazıcı, Luke Boyle, Barbara McGowan, Volkan Yumuk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The prevalence of overweight and obesity in individuals with type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) has increased significantly due to multifactorial causes, paralleling global trends. Beyond known causes of obesity, pathophysiological and biopsychosocial causes specific to T1DM also have a role in the development of obesity. Obesity in T1DM may arise from treatment-related factors (intensive insulin therapy and defensive carbohydrate intake), behavioral aspects (reduced physical activity due to fear of hypoglycemia), and pathophysiological mechanisms such as adipose tissue dysfunction. Clinically, obesity in T1DM is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, nephropathy, retinopathy, and psychosocial burden. Weight gain and increased insulin dependence have created a vicious cycle in the management of obesity in T1DM. Recent clinical studies have shown that incretin-based therapies, particularly glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists and dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP)/GLP-1 agonists, can reduce body weight and insulin requirements without significant safety concerns. Similarly, sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors offer metabolic benefits but carry a risk of diabetic ketoacidosis. The lack of regulatory approval for obesity treatment agents in these patients highlights the importance of further research. In this review, we address the pathophysiology of obesity in T1DM, its clinical implications, and therapeutic approaches using an evidence-based approach.

INTRODUCTION

Type 1 diabetes mellitus (T1DM) has traditionally been viewed as a disease affecting lean individuals, but the global obesity epidemic has not spared this population. Overweight and obesity are increasingly common among people with T1DM, creating a dual burden that amplifies health risks.1 This convergence of T1DM with excess adiposity has profound clinical implications. Excess weight in T1DM increases the risk of both diabetes-related and obesity-related complications such as cardiovascular (CV) disease, stroke, and certain cancers.2 Moreover, individuals with T1DM and obesity often exhibit features of insulin resistance (IR) akin to type 2 diabetes (T2DM), compounding their metabolic burden.3 The combination of absolute insulin deficiency and IR presents unique management challenges, as intensive insulin therapy to control glycemia can itself promote weight gain, creating a vicious cycle.4 Given the rising prevalence and numerous health consequences of obesity in T1DM, there is a pressing need to understand the scope of this problem and to develop strategies for weight management tailored to this population. This narrative review provides an updated, comprehensive overview of the epidemiology, pathophysiology, and clinical management of obesity in individuals with T1DM, with an emphasis on recent evidence and emerging therapies.

EPIDEMIOLOGY

Over the past two decades, the prevalence of overweight and obesity in individuals with T1DM has risen steadily, closely following global trends in the general population.5 In many regions, people with T1DM are now just as likely to have excess weight as those without diabetes.6,7 In a recent U.S. cohort, roughly one-third of adults with T1DM were overweight, and a further 28% were living with obesity, figures that mirror rates in the general population.8 Similar patterns have been documented in large European datasets, including both a Belgian registry of nearly 90,000 individuals with T1DM and an Austrian national cohort that showed overweight and obesity rates comparable to their peers without diabetes.9,10 Regional differences should be kept in mind in interpreting these figures. In countries with exceptionally high background obesity rates, patients with T1DM may show a lower prevalence of obesity. For example, Mexico’s Registro Nacional de Pacientes con Diabetes Tipo 1 (RENACED-T1), a national registry of individuals with T1DM, reported obesity in just over 8% of adults with T1DM, a substantially lower rate than in the general population.11 Despite such variation, long-term data from the U.S. reveal a striking upward trajectory: obesity prevalence among people with T1DM increased from about 3% in the mid-1980s to 23% by the early 2000s, a rise that outpaced the general population.12

Similar trends are evident in pediatric populations. In the U.S. SEARCH for Diabetes in Youth Study (SEARCH), the prevalence of overweight was higher in children and adolescents with T1DM compared to their peers without diabetes (22% vs. 16%). In contrast, obesity prevalence was not higher (approximately 12%–13% vs. 17% in peers without diabetes).13

RISK FACTORS

Insulin therapy and weight gain

Obesity in T1DM often results from a combination of treatment-related factors and behavioral adaptations. The most significant contributor is intensive insulin therapy. The Diabetes Control and Complications Trial (DCCT) provided robust evidence: adults randomized to intensive insulin therapy gained about 5 kg in the first year, more than double the approximately 2.4 kg seen with conventional therapy.4 In DCCT, individuals with higher baseline glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c) and greater reductions in glucose experienced the largest weight gain.

Several mechanisms explain this effect. Mechanistically, improved glycemic control reduces glycosuria, so that more of the ingested calories are stored rather than lost in urine.14 In addition, subcutaneous insulin delivery leads to peripheral hyperinsulinemia, which favors fat deposition in adipose tissue.15,16 As injected insulin bypasses the liver’s first-pass metabolism, its action is disproportionately greater in muscle and fat than in hepatic tissue, promoting weight gain. Over time, this can lead to higher insulin requirements as weight increases and IR develops, creating a vicious cycle.17 The same hormone that sustains life in T1DM can, paradoxically, fuel progressive weight gain. Breaking this insulin–weight spiral remains a key challenge in contemporary T1DM management.

Lifestyle

Beyond insulin’s biological effects, behavioral responses to the risk of hypoglycemia also play a major role in weight gain. Intensive insulin therapy increases the risk of hypoglycemia episodes, and people respond with “defensive” carbohydrate intake. Frequent snacking to treat or prevent hypoglycemia can add substantial excess calories. In the DCCT, participants on intensive therapy had triple the rate of severe hypoglycemia compared with those on conventional therapy, and weight gain was more often linked to these episodes, likely reflecting the caloric impact of repeated corrective eating.4 Anticipation of hypoglycemia can also lead to chronic overcompensation, where daily energy intake is intentionally kept higher just in case.

Physical activity is another important aspect. Fear of exercise-induced hypoglycemia can discourage regular activity.18 Studies suggest that people with T1DM may be less active than the general population, partly due to the challenges of exercising with circulating insulin and the need to prevent post-exercise glucose drops.18,19 Without careful insulin adjustment or carbohydrate timing, vigorous activity can precipitate hypoglycemia. To avoid this, some patients run their glucose levels higher or consume extra carbohydrates before workouts, which, if not matched by sufficient energy expenditure, can promote weight gain.

Other risk factors

Other factors also contribute to weight gain in T1DM. Biological predisposition plays a role. Individuals with T1DM who have a family history of T2DM might be more susceptible to weight gain.20 Longer disease duration, cumulative insulin exposure, and ageing are likewise associated with gradual increases in body weight. Genetic factors have also been demonstrated to play a role in the development of obesity in T1DM. The fat mass and obesity-associated (FTO) gene has been demonstrated to predict obesity in T1DM.7 A genome-wide association study among patients with non-autoimmune T1DM (type 1b) identified 13 novel loci, nine of which were linked to obesity.21

Psychosocial influences are also important. The ongoing demands of carbohydrate counting, insulin dosing, and weight monitoring can create stress and frustration, which in turn may foster unhealthy eating patterns. Disordered eating behaviors, including both under- and overeating, are more common in adolescents with T1DM than in their peers without diabetes.22 Depression, which occurs more frequently in T1DM, can further contribute to weight gain through emotional overeating and reduced motivation for physical activity.23,24

Socioeconomic factors also mirror trends in the general population. Data from a U.S. adolescent T1DM registry show that female sex, older age, lower household income, and lower parental education are all linked to higher rates of overweight and obesity.25 These findings emphasize that weight gain in T1DM is not solely the result of insulin therapy, but also arises from complex interactions among biological susceptibility, psychosocial pressures, and environmental disadvantage.

PATHOPHYSIOLOGY

Excess adiposity adds a distinct layer of metabolic dysfunction in T1DM by driving IR. As a potent anabolic hormone, insulin facilitates lipid storage, thus increasing the doses required to overcome IR often promote weight gain. This bidirectional relationship, where weight gain worsens IR, which in turn demands higher insulin doses, creates a self-perpetuating double diabetes phenotype, blending autoimmune β-cell failure with features of metabolic syndrome.26 Clinically, these patients frequently present with hypertension, dyslipidemia, and central obesity alongside autoimmune disease. Breaking this cycle is crucial, as IR in T1DM is consistently associated with poorer long-term outcomes including higher rates of microvascular and macrovascular complications and increased allcause mortality.27-30 In individuals with T1DM, conventional indices such as the homeostatic model assessment of IR are generally not appropriate, as fasting insulin concentrations reflect exogenous insulin administration rather than endogenous secretion. Therefore, epidemiologic and clinical studies in T1DM commonly use validated surrogate measures such as the estimated glucose disposal rate and estimated insulin sensitivity equations, which have been validated against the euglycemic–hyperinsulinemic clamp and have been shown to correlate with vascular complications and CV risk.31-33

Excess adipose tissue in obesity is metabolically active, functioning as an endocrine organ that promotes chronic low-grade inflammation. In T1DM, this added inflammatory burden destabilizes an already vulnerable metabolic balance.7,34 Enlarged adipocytes release pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as tumor necrosis factor-α, interleukin 6 (IL-6), and IL-1β, and attract immune cells into adipose depots. These signals disrupt insulin action, particularly through cytokine-mediated phosphorylation of insulin receptor substrates, worsening IR in muscle and the liver.28,35-37 According to the “accelerator hypothesis,” such inflammation may also hasten autoimmune β-cell destruction in genetically predisposed individuals.38

When adipose tissue storage capacity is exceeded, excess lipids accumulate in ectopic sites, including the liver, skeletal muscle, and pancreatic islets. This lipid spillover elevates circulating free fatty acids, which impair insulin signaling, impair mitochondrial function, and trigger endoplasmic reticulum stress in β-cells, leading to apoptosis.39-41 In T1DM, sustained exposure to elevated fatty acids can further destabilize glycemia and erode residual β-cell function.6

Hyperglycemia amplifies this injury by driving oxidative stress. Excess reactive oxygen species damage endothelial cells, β-cells, and other tissues, further promoting inflammation and IR.41 The combined effects of chronic inflammation, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and metabolic dysfunction accelerate the onset of microvascular and macrovascular complications. Indeed, patients with T1DM and obesity have higher rates of nephropathy and cardiomyopathy than their lean counterparts, reflecting these pathophysiologic insults.28,29

In summary, obesity in T1DM emerges from a convergence of insulin therapy-driven weight gain, adiposity-induced IR, and inflammatory-lipotoxic processes (Fig. 1). These mechanisms create a self-reinforcing cycle that progressively worsens metabolic control and increases the risk of complications.

CLINICAL IMPLICATIONS OF OBESITY IN TYPE 1 DIABETES MELLITUS

As discussed, obesity in T1DM drives IR, increases insulin requirements, and undermines glycemic control.42-44 Beyond these effects, excess adiposity further disrupts metabolic regulation, elevates CV risk, and contributes to adverse psychosocial outcomes. In addition, complications in the natural course of obesity are similar in the T1DM population.

Cardiometabolic risks

Obesity adds substantial CV burden in people with T1DM. Excess adiposity promotes hypertension, atherogenic dyslipidemia, and persistent low-grade inflammation, compounding the baseline risks of the disease.45 In the Coronary Artery Calcification in Type 1 Diabetes (CACTI) study, obesity independently predicted the progression of subclinical atherosclerosis. Over roughly 6 years, T1DM participants with obesity had significantly greater increases in coronary artery calcium scores than their lean counterparts, even after adjusting for blood pressure, lipid levels, and inflammatory markers.28 Other population data similarly linked higher body mass index (BMI) in T1DM with increased prevalence of hypertension and dyslipidemia, both major drivers of CV disease.2 CV disease risk and all-cause mortality were also associated with high IR in T1DM cases in relation to obesity.46

Microvascular complications

The vascular impact of obesity extends beyond large arteries. IR, hypertension, and dyslipidemia can accelerate microvascular complications. In a UK national T1DM registry, patients with diabetic kidney disease were nearly twice as likely to be living with obesity as those with preserved renal function.47 In T1DM cases, increased BMI and associated IR are related to all microvascular complications.48

Taken together, these findings identify a high-risk double diabetes T1DM/T2DM phenotype in which obesity magnifies the likelihood of CV disease, metabolic syndrome, and both micro- and macrovascular complications.

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease

The rising prevalence of obesity in T1DM has also brought increased attention to metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). In T1DM, MASLD is often underdiagnosed but can progress silently to fibrosis, cirrhosis, and heightened CV risk.49,50 A meta-analysis of 3,901 patients found a 22% prevalence of non-alcoholic fatty liver disease in adult T1DM patients.51 Screening with liver enzymes and imaging in at-risk patients, along with weight management and improved glycemic control, is critical to preventing disease progression. Awareness of MASLD underscores the need to view T1DM not only as an autoimmune condition but also as one increasingly influenced by metabolic comorbidities.

Obstructive sleep apnea

Obesity in people with T1DM substantially increases the risk of obstructive sleep apnea (OSA), a condition characterized by repetitive upper airway obstruction during sleep.52,53 OSA contributes to poor sleep quality, daytime fatigue, and impaired concentration, which can further complicate self-management of T1DM. Importantly, intermittent hypoxia and sleep fragmentation caused by OSA may worsen IR, blood pressure, and CV risk, factors already amplified in obesity.

Psychosocial and quality of life consequences

The combination of T1DM and obesity can profoundly affect mental health and daily well-being. Managing T1DM is already an intensive, round-the-clock task; adding weight management challenges and obesity-related stigma further compounds the psychological burden.54 T1DM already carries a higher baseline risk of depression and anxiety compared to the general population, and obesity can worsen these mental health challenges.55,56 If left unaddressed, depression and anxiety can worsen self-management, impair glycemic control, and perpetuate a cycle of distress and poor outcomes.57 It is worth highlighting that excess weight can impair daily functioning and diminish health-related quality of life in people with T1DM.58 Many patients describe the “push–pull” dynamic between intensive insulin therapy, which promotes weight gain, and lifestyle measures aimed at weight loss, which increase the risk of hypoglycemia. This ongoing tension can contribute to burnout and frustration, undermining self-care engagement.

MANAGEMENT STRATEGIES

Lifestyle interventions

Lifestyle modification is a cornerstone of obesity management and is universally recommended as the first-line strategy across all forms of diabetes. However, the application of lifestyle interventions in individuals with T1DM is uniquely complex due to the need to maintain strict glycemic control, avoid hypoglycemia, and balance insulin requirements with caloric restriction and physical activity.59 Despite these challenges, structured and individualized lifestyle approaches can be effective in promoting weight loss and improving metabolic outcomes in this population.

Nutrition therapy in T1DM with obesity

Calorie restriction plays a key role in obesity management. A calorie deficit of 500 to 750 kcal based on measured or estimated resting energy expenditure may be recommended for weight loss.60 Medical nutrition therapy (MNT) is the core element of treatment for obesity among individuals with T1DM. For MNT to be effective, it requires individualized calculation of insulin-to-carbohydrate ratios and close follow-up to prevent glycemic variability, with an end-goal of promoting weight loss while maintaining glycemic stability and minimizing the risk of hypoglycemia or diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA). The Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics concluded that macronutrient composition and energy intake have an insignificant contribution in adults with T1DM. No discernible effect on HbA1c and CV risk factors was observed with varying carbohydrate (39% to 57% energy) and fat (27% to 40% energy) intake.61 In a small retrospective study of children with T1DM, a low-carbohydrate diet was associated with a 3.0 kg (4%) reduction in body weight, a 3.5% reduction in BMI, and a 1.3% decrease in HbA1c levels after 3 months.62

The literature on the effects of the ketogenic diet on T1DM is limited. A number of preliminary studies have indicated a decline in HbA1c; however, there are concomitant safety concerns, including increased hypoglycemia, increased risk of DKA, and elevated lowdensity lipoprotein cholesterol levels. Capillary β-hydroxybutyrate is used to monitor DKA; maintaining ketone levels between 1.5 and 3.0 mmol/L is imperative, although interpretation is challenging in the context of a ketogenic diet. While evidence suggests that a ketogenic diet may improve glycemic control in T1DM, further randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are required to assess its safety and efficacy.

Physical activity: role and challenges in T1DM

Physical activity increases insulin sensitivity, reduces visceral fat, and improves CV health in individuals with diabetes. However, individuals with T1DM may be resistant to exercise due to fear of hypoglycemia, a lack of knowledge about the effects of different exercise types on glucose variability, and difficulty adjusting carbohydrate/insulin ratios. The interaction between different types of exercise and glucose levels varies. Light to moderate-intensity endurance exercise (e.g., running and cycling) increases the risk of hypoglycemia soon after exercise, while moderate-to-vigorous-intensity exercise lasting >45 minutes increases the risk of nocturnal hypoglycemia. Consuming carbohydrates before exercise to achieve a glucose level of 145 mg/dL is a more optimal approach than reducing insulin dosage. On the other hand, resistance exercise (e.g., weight training) slightly increases glucose levels, especially in the morning; this effect is reduced with fewer repetitions and heavier weights. Performing resistance exercise before endurance exercise may reduce the risk of hypoglycemia in T1DM. High-intensity intermittent (HIIT) exercise has a mild effect on glycemia risk; however, if performed late in the day, there may be a risk of nocturnal hypoglycemia. Hypoglycemia-like symptoms during exercise or post-exercise hyperglycemia may also occur. To reduce the risk of hypoglycemia, the pre-exercise bolus of insulin should be reduced by 25%–75%, basal insulin should be adjusted for long-term exercise, and/or 10– 20 g of carbohydrates should be consumed before and after exercise, depending on blood glucose levels.63,64 Exercise recommendations should be individualized based on the patient’s glucose level, lifestyle, medical history, and type/duration of exercise.65,66

Behavioral interventions

Distress due to chronic diseases such as T1DM and insulin therapy management, including meal content (e.g., carbohydrate counting), is the main reason for targeting glycemic and weight regulation with behavioral therapy. Behavioral therapy includes assessing and managing psychological and behavioral factors to manage lifestyle changes, increase adherence to treatment options, and identify barriers.67 The prevalence of fear of hypoglycemia is common in T1DM, and occurs in 30% to 60% of patients. Behaviors that may cause weight gain, such as overeating, increased depression, anxiety, avoidance of exercise, decreased quality of life, and poor sleep quality, may be observed as a result of fear of hypoglycemia.68 Continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems or automatic insulin devices have been shown to reduce fear of hypoglycemia and should be recommended, especially in patients with T1DM who are concerned.69 People with T1DM, especially those who complain of weight gain, should be routinely screened for the presence of eating disorders commonly associated with obesity, such as night eating syndrome and binge eating.68 If there are concerns related to disordered eating, individuals should be referred for psychological evaluation and support. In particular, excessive bolus doses and improper carbohydrate-counting techniques may increase the fear of hypoglycemia or lead to excess calorie intake. Multidisciplinary evaluation of patients and education on diabetes self-management should be recommended.70

Pharmacotherapy

Pharmacotherapy in the management of obesity in patients with T1DM is an increasingly important but still controversial area. Despite successful results in T2DM, many pharmacological agents are not approved for T1DM due to the risk of DKA and hypoglycemia, and because of differences in pathophysiology. However, their use as adjuvant therapies is increasing. Studies will primarily focus on glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitor (SGLT2i), and their combinations, given their beneficial effects on glycemic control, weight loss, and overall cardiometabolic parameters.6

Metformin

Metformin treatment, one of the main therapies for T2DM, has a short-term effect on weight loss and glycemic regulation in T1DM patients, but this benefit disappears over time.33 A systematic review of nine studies found that adding metformin reduced total daily insulin dose and HbA1c by 0.6% to 0.9% and facilitated weight loss.71 It has a protective effect on IR and total insulin dose reduction, lipid parameters, and CV parameters. It may be considered a metabolic benefit in patients with T1DM because it does not increase the risk of hypoglycemia or DKA.72 However, metformin is not routinely recommended for glycemic management in T1DM. In REducing with MetfOrmin Vascular Adverse Lesions (REMOVAL), the largest long-term randomized study in adults with T1DM, the initial HbA1c reduction was not sustained, although modest reductions in weight and insulin dose were observed.73 Current guidelines do not therefore support routine use, although National Institute for Health and Care Excellence suggests that metformin may be considered in adults with T1DM and BMI ≥25 kg/m2 who wish to reduce insulin dose, with careful patient selection.74,75

GLP-1RAs and emerging incretin-based therapies

The use of incretin-based therapies, particularly GLP-1RAs and dual GLP-1RA/glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) receptor agonist, has changed obesity treatment algorithms in the management of T2DM and has been investigated in the management of T1DM over recent years. In addition to promoting weight loss, these agents offer multiple systemic benefits, including increased satiety, delayed gastric emptying, and suppression of glucagon secretion. It has been thought that these mechanisms may be particularly beneficial in individuals with obesity and T1DM.6,70,76

Although GLP-1RAs are not currently officially approved for the treatment of T1DM, results from clinical trials and real-world studies suggest potential benefits when used in combination with an individualized treatment approach, close monitoring, and appropriate safety precautions. Liraglutide is one of the most studied agents in patients with T1DM. Many studies have been conducted over the last decade, including the ADJUNCT ONE (n=1,398) and ADJUNCT TWO (n=835) trials, which were pivotal double-blind, placebo-controlled studies with large numbers and long durations. Both studies showed significant reductions in BMI, HbA1c, and insulin doses without increasing the risk of hypoglycemia.76,77 In a randomized, doubleblind, placebo-controlled study called the Lira pump trial, patients with obesity and T1DM receiving continuous subcutaneous insulin infusion were examined, and HbA1c (5%), total daily insulin dose (–8 units/day), and body weight (–6.8 kg) were significantly reduced without increasing the risk of hypoglycemia.79

Semaglutide, a once-weekly, long-acting GLP-1RA used in patients with T2DM and obesity, has a greater weight-loss effect than liraglutide. Its use in T1DM is still being studied. In a retrospective study, the semaglutide reduced HbA1c, BMI, and glucose variability by 0.66%, 7.9%, and 2.5%, respectively, after 1 year of use, with no reports of severe ketoacidosis or hypoglycemia.84 In a recent double-blind trial of patients with T1DM who were treated with an automated insulin delivery system and had a BMI >30 kg/m2, a 0.3% reduction in HbA1c and weight loss of 8.8 kg were observed. There were no differences in hypoglycemia or DKA.86

Tirzepatide is the first novel dual GIP/GLP-1RA. In a pilot study by Karakus et al.,43 a 7% increase in time in range (TIR; 70–180 mg/dL), a 0.5% decrease in HbA1c, and a 9% decrease in weight were observed at the 8-month follow-up. In a single-center, retrospective observational study by Akturk et al.,88 the efficacy of tirzepatide treatment was evaluated in 26 T1DM patients over an 8-month observation period. In that study, HbA1c decreased by 0.6%, body weight decreased by 10.1%, and TIR increased by 12.6%. Only one patient had severe hypoglycemia, and no DKA was observed. A significant reduction in daily insulin requirements (36%), improved glucose control (0.9% decrease in HbA1c), and significant weight loss (8.5%) were observed over 8 months without any serious side effects (including severe hypoglycemia and DKA) in another study as well.42

In a meta-analysis of 24 studies (n=3,377) examining liraglutide, exenatide, and albiglutide, for liraglutide HbA1c reductions were −0.28% (95% confidence interval [CI], −0.38 to −0.19) for 1.8 mg daily, −0.21% (95% CI, −0.30 to −0.12) for 1.2 mg daily, −0.17% (95% CI, −0.26 to −0.07) for 0.6 to 0.9 mg, and for exenatide −0.17% (95% CI, −0.28 to 0.02). For liraglutide, weight reduction was −4.89 kg (95% CI, −5.33 to −4.45) for 1.8 mg, −3.77 kg (95% CI, −4.24 to −3.30) for 1.2 mg, −2.27 kg (95% CI, −2.75 to −1.79) for 0.6 to 0.9 mg, and for exenatide −4.06 kg (95% CI, −5.33 to −2.79). In all studies, the risk of hypoglycemia was 1.43 (95% CI, 1.13 to 1.80) and that of DKA was 1.34 (95% CI, 1.04 to 1.79). Although GLP‑1RAs, particularly semaglutide and liraglutide, frequently cause gastrointestinal intolerance in patients with T1DM (12.6%), these side effects are generally benign, transient, and manageable.90 In another meta-analysis (n=378) investigating CGM metrics with GLP-1RAs, a significant decrease in HbA1c (mean difference, −0.21%; 95% CI, −0.36 to −0.06; P=0.007) was observed, but no significant decrease was observed in CGM metrics.91

Sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors

SGLT2i have been shown to improve CV outcomes and hyperglycemia in people with T2DM. They improve hyperglycemia by increasing urinary glucose excretion, as well as having positive cardiometabolic effects through weight loss, reduced oxidative stress, and systolic blood pressure-lowering effects. There are extensive studies on SGLT2i in T1DM, primarily dapagliflozin and empagliflozin, as well as canagliflozin and the dual SGLT1/2i sotagliflozin.33 There have been several large-scale RCTs on the effects of SGLT2i on T1DM, most notably the Dapagliflozin Evaluation in Patients with Inadequately Controlled Type 1 Diabetes (DEPICT), Empagliflozin as Adjunctive to inSulin thErapy (EASE), and TANDEM trials (Supplementary Table 1). In a meta-analysis of 16 RCTs, SGLT2, and dual SGLT1/2i showed promising effects on glycated hemoglobin and weight loss in people with T1DM without increasing the risk of hypoglycemia, but they were shown to increase the risk of DKA compared to placebo.92

A real-world study of 115 patients evaluated GLP-1RAs (76 patients) or SGLT2 inhibitors (39 patients). The reduction in HbA1c was similar, but GLP-1RA recipients experienced greater weight loss, while those using SGLT2i had a higher risk of DKA (12.8% vs. 3.9%).93 In a meta-analysis of 2,310 patients from eight RCTs, the use of SGLT2i was associated with a significant increase in TIR and a significant increase in the risk of DKA without causing hypoglycemia.94 As a real-world study, the efficacy, safety, and cardio-renal outcomes at 5 years were analyzed in patients who used GLP-1RA (1,822 patients) and SGLT2i (992 patients) for at least 6 months. A decrease in HbA1c of 0.2% was observed with SGLT2i and of 0.5% with GLP-1RA. In addition, patients treated with SGLT2i had a higher risk of DKA and urinary tract infections than those treated with GLP-1RA. In the SGLT2i group, more favorable cardio-renal benefits were observed over the 5-year follow-up period compared to the GLP-1RA group, including better preservation of glomerular filtration rate, lower risk of heart failure, and lower hospitalization rates for any cause.95 Another meta-analysis of 33 studies (9,234 patients) examined the effects of all alternative pharmacological treatment options (GLP-1RA, SGLT2i, dipeptidyl peptidase-4 inhibitor, metformin, and lifestyle interventions [diet and exercise]) in patients with T1DM. Liraglutide 0.6 to 1.8 mg (−2.22 to −4.85 kg), empagliflozin 2.5 to 25 mg (−1.47 to −3.06 kg), and sotagliflozin 200 to 400 mg (−2.40 to −3.23 kg) produced significant weight loss, but no significant effects on weight loss were observed with the other treatment options.96

Pramlintide

Being an analogue of amylin, pramlintide is the only drug approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration for use in T1DM other than insulin. It suppresses postprandial glucagon levels by delaying gastric emptying/intestinal glucose absorption, thereby providing a feeling of fullness. In T1DM, the addition of pramlintide to insulin has been observed to reduce postprandial hyperglycemia by 60%, body weight by 1.3 kg, and daily insulin dose by 28%.97 It is an injectable treatment that can be taken with meals. Multiple daily injections, minimal effect on weight, increased risk of hypoglycemia, high cost, and high gastrointestinal side effects limit its use.70

Technology-assisted approaches

Technological advances in the management of T1DM have become widespread in recent years. In particular, insulin pump systems including CGM systems and advanced hybrid closed-loop (AHCL) technologies have been shown to significantly improve glycemic control. Insulin pump systems were observed to reduce HbA1c and daily insulin requirements by 23% and 20%, respectively, compared to multiple daily insulin injections.69 Insulin pumps, especially sensor-assisted pumps or automated insulin delivery systems integrated with CGM, allow basal and bolus rates to be individualized according to individual needs. The use of these systems reduces total insulin dose, which in turn reduces glycemic variability and improves both hyperglycemia-induced lipogenesis and hypoglycemia-induced overeating behaviors. CGM use in overweight and T1DM individuals is associated with increased time in target range and decreased glycemic variability, facilitating safe calorie restriction and exercise, which are important for weight loss.98 The Advanced Hybrid Closed Loop Study in Adult Population with Type 1 Diabetes (ADAPT) study demonstrated reduced total insulin dose, increased time in target range, and greater user experience among AHCL users with T1DM, allowing them to increase physical activity by reducing fear of hypoglycemia.99 CGM and AHCL platforms are increasingly integrated with mobile apps that offer nutritional tracking, behavioral alerts, and automated coaching. Mobile apps and digital health platforms that provide personalized nutrition and physical activity tracking along with behavioral support improve outcomes in diabetes care and calorie intake. In T1DM populations, digital platforms (such as mySugr, Glooko, or Tidepool) have been shown to improve glycemic control and may have potential impacts on weight management.100

Bariatric surgery

Metabolic/bariatric surgery is the most effective and sustainable weight-loss intervention for the treatment of obesity.70,101 Although bariatric surgery has been well elucidated for the treatment of T2DM (usually resulting in remission via enhanced incretin responses, e.g., GLP-1 and peptide YY [PYY]), the knowledge about its role in T1DM is limited.102

Current studies of metabolic/bariatric surgery in T1DM are mainly retrospective and variable in design. The prominent finding in studies conducted was a reduction in insulin requirements and weight loss after surgery.103 In a study by Lannoo et al.104 involving 22 T1DM patients (Roux-en-Y gastric bypass=16, sleeve gastrectomy= 6), significant weight loss and decreased insulin requirements were observed at the 37-week follow-up, while glycemic control did not change significantly.104 In the study conducted by Vilarrasa et al.,105 33 patients with T1DM were followed for 4.6±2.6 years after bariatric surgery. Total weight-loss percentage was 30.4%±9.2% in the first year of surgery and decreased to 28.1%±11.5% in the fifth year (P=0.02). Although glycemic regulation was significant in the first year, no difference was observed on long-term followup.105 The lack of sufficient studies and safety risks, such as serious perioperative complications, including DKA and hypoglycemia, limit the use of this treatment option. The current international guidelines are based on BMI and comorbidity rather than diabetes type, and they recommend surgery for people with a BMI of 35 kg/m² and for those with a BMI of 30 to 34.9 kg/m² when metabolic disease is inadequately controlled.106,107 Nevertheless, there is a dearth of high-quality data in T1DM, and diabetes-specific guideline recommendations mostly target T2DM.102,106,107 Because of the dangers of DKA and severe hypoglycemia, bariatric surgery in people with T1DM should be addressed on an individual basis within a multidisciplinary environment, with appropriate perioperative glycemic control.

CONCLUSION

Although obesity in T1DM has long been underestimated, its increased CV, microvascular, and psychosocial risks are now increasingly important in treatment management. Breaking the vicious cycle of weight gain and increased insulin requirements requires a paradigm shift from a purely glucose-centered strategy to a glucose-and-weight-centered approach. Promising advances in adjunctive pharmacotherapies and diabetes technologies highlight the potential of integrated interventions; however, off-label use of pharmacotherapy, safety concerns, and lack of long-term data warrant further investigation. Bariatric surgery, while offering glycemic and weight-loss benefits, is limited due to its risk of complications. As the need for mechanistic studies and personalized medicine approaches continues, obesity management remains a fundamental therapeutic challenge requiring multidisciplinary, individualized, and evidence-based approaches.
