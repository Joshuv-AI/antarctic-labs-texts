A Challenging Presentation of Mycoplasma pneumoniae : Mild Hepatic Dysfunction With Persistent Leukocytosis in a Pediatric Patient
Authors: Robert J Heins, Sierra Scott, Stephen Mock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACT Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is a common pediatric respiratory pathogen that can rarely present with extrapulmonary manifestations such as hepatic involvement. Recognition of hepatic dysfunction in this context is important for accurate diagnosis and timely therapy. A five‐year‐old female with asthma presented with fever, cough, fatigue, pruritus, and scleral icterus. Laboratory evaluation revealed leukocytosis, elevated C‐reactive protein, mild transaminitis, direct hyperbilirubinemia, elevated PT/PTT, and normocytic anemia. Infectious and hepatic workups were initially unrevealing. The patient showed gradual clinical improvement while receiving empiric ceftriaxone but had persistent leukocytosis. On hospital Day 7, a positive MP IgM prompted azithromycin initiation, which corresponded to the normalization of hepatic indices. This case highlights a rare extrapulmonary manifestation of MP infection with mild hepatic dysfunction and transient hematologic changes, emphasizing the importance of recognizing atypical presentations of MP to facilitate timely diagnosis, avoid unnecessary interventions, and prevent prolonged hospitalization.Keywords: acute medicine, infectious diseases, pediatrics and adolescent medicine, respiratory medicine

Introduction

Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) is one of the leading causes of community‐acquired pneumonia in children requiring hospitalization worldwide [1]. Clinical manifestations are often confined to the respiratory tract, including persistent dry cough, fever, and fatigue. However, extrapulmonary involvement occurs in up to 25% of pediatric cases and may include gastrointestinal symptoms such as abdominal pain, nausea, and vomiting, as well as less common complications such as hepatitis, hemolytic anemia, dermatologic eruptions, and myocarditis [2, 3, 4]. These atypical manifestations can obscure the underlying diagnosis, as the clinical picture frequently overlaps with typical infectious or inflammatory processes, including viral hepatitis, autoimmune hepatitis, and biliary tract infection. This case report describes a diagnostically challenging presentation of MP infection in a young female with a history of recurrent asthma exacerbations who presented with cholestasis, transaminitis, and leukocytosis. The case underscores the importance of maintaining a broad differential diagnosis and recognizing extrapulmonary features that may signal MP infection despite an initially nonspecific clinical presentation.

Case History/Examination

A five‐year‐old African American female presented with a two‐week history of intermittent dry cough and a two‐day history of episodic subjective fevers. Her mother additionally reported that the patient had scleral yellowing, pruritus of the extremities, and decreased energy over the preceding week. In the emergency department, the patient developed mild epigastric discomfort but denied rash, sore throat, gastrointestinal, or urinary symptoms. Vital signs were notable for a fever of 103.3°F, tachycardia, and tachypnea, with normal oxygen saturation. She appeared nontoxic on examination. Physical examination revealed bilateral scleral icterus with faint jaundice best appreciated on the soles of the feet. Lung examination was clear, and the abdomen was soft and non‐tender without hepatosplenomegaly. Her medical history was significant for poorly controlled asthma with multiple exacerbations over the preceding year requiring emergency department visits, including one prior hospitalization requiring intensive care unit admission.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations and Treatment

Initial laboratory evaluation demonstrated normocytic anemia, neutrophilic leukocytosis with eosinophilia, and thrombocytosis (Table 1). Chemistry studies showed hyponatremia, hypochloremia, mild transaminitis with ALT greater than AST, and direct hyperbilirubinemia. Inflammatory markers and PT/PTT were elevated. Urinalysis revealed positive leukocyte esterase without nitrites. Chest radiography showed no acute findings by radiology interpretation, though subtle perihilar opacities were noted on independent review (Figure 1). Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated no biliary obstruction or acute hepatobiliary pathology, though mild hepatic heterogeneity was later noted after a second review with the attending radiologist (Figure 1). Further evaluation excluded alternative causes of hepatic dysfunction. Viral hepatitis panel (A, B, C), EBV, CMV, HIV, and leptospirosis testing were negative, and autoimmune evaluation including ANA and anti‐smooth muscle antibody was also negative (Table 1). The patient was admitted for further evaluation.

On hospital Day 0, the patient received treatment in the emergency department for asthma symptoms, including inhaled ipratropium‐albuterol and a single dose of systemic corticosteroids prior to admission. She was subsequently started on intravenous fluids for electrolyte abnormalities and empiric ceftriaxone for suspected bacterial infection in the setting of high fever, leukocytosis, elevated inflammatory markers, and an abnormal urinalysis. Although the chest radiograph was interpreted as having no acute findings, subtle perihilar opacities were noted on independent review, raising concern for early or atypical pneumonia. Given the patient's persistent fever and laboratory abnormalities, vancomycin was added to provide broader empiric coverage while awaiting culture results. By hospital Day 1, electrolytes normalized, though fevers and leukocytosis persisted. Hematology/oncology was consulted on Day 2 for anemia and coagulation abnormalities. Iron studies were recommended, and hemolysis was excluded by normal lactate dehydrogenase and haptoglobin levels. Direct bilirubin remained elevated but was down trending.

On hospital Day 3, an infectious disease consultation was obtained due to persistent fever, leukocytosis, transaminitis, and elevated inflammatory markers despite antibiotic therapy. Incomplete Kawasaki disease was considered, though physical examination and echocardiography were unremarkable at this time. Vancomycin was discontinued after a repeat urinalysis was unremarkable, and extensive infectious and autoimmune testing was initiated at this time (Table 1).

By hospital Day 5, the patient had been afebrile for 24 h with improving inflammatory markers and normalization of bilirubin, though leukocytosis persisted above 20 × 109/L. Ceftriaxone was continued, and additional testing was expanded to include MP, leptospirosis, strongyloides, parvovirus B19, enterovirus, hepatitis E, and HIV. By hospital Day 6, transaminases and coagulation parameters had largely normalized, and autoimmune testing was negative. The patient remained clinically well with no reported symptoms.

On hospital Day 7, MP IgM returned positive. Azithromycin was initiated, and ceftriaxone was continued to complete a ten‐day course. Leukocytosis persisted despite clinical stability and improving inflammatory markers. Persistent leukocytosis in this patient was likely multifactorial in the setting of recent infection and systemic inflammation, with potential contribution from the patient's history of poorly controlled asthma and recurrent exacerbations.

Outcome and Follow‐up

The patient was discharged on hospital Day 10 with a diagnosis of MPinfection, in stable condition with improving laboratory markers, completion of antibiotic therapy, and an optimized asthma maintenance regimen. Although the diagnosis was supported by a positive MPIgM result, confirmatory testing such as PCR or paired IgG serology was considered but not obtained during hospitalization. Given the patient's clinical improvement and normalization of hepatic indices following azithromycin therapy, the decision was made to discharge her with outpatient follow‐up rather than pursue additional inpatient testing. She was instructed to follow closely with pediatrics, hematology/oncology, and infectious disease in the outpatient setting for continued clinical and laboratory monitoring.

Discussion

This case highlights the need for a broad differential diagnosis and the diagnostic complexity encountered in a pediatric patient with persistent fever and leukocytosis despite broad‐spectrum antibiotic therapy. The initial presentation was complicated by overlapping respiratory symptoms with elevated transaminases and direct hyperbilirubinemia, obscuring whether the underlying process was primarily hepatic, infectious, pulmonary, or multifactorial, including a potential contribution from her uncontrolled asthma. Although the chest radiograph in this case was unremarkable, atypical pathogens such as MP can produce pneumonia with clear or ambiguous radiographic findings, as demonstrated by Kitazawa et al. [5]. Persistent leukocytosis in this patient was likely multifactorial, influenced by uncontrolled asthma‐associated systemic inflammation [6].

Extrapulmonary manifestations of MP infection have been reported, including gastrointestinal involvement such as hepatitis, acute acalculous cholecystitis, and pancreatitis, although many reports are in adults rather than pediatric populations [3, 4]. In a study by Kim et al. [7], Korean children with MP infection were examined for features and prevalence of acute hepatitis. The study found that 7.7% of children had features of hepatitis complicating the clinical course. These patients demonstrated elevated AST and ALT that normalized within an average of 7.5 days without complications. Similarly, the present case demonstrated mildly elevated transaminases that resolved spontaneously after approximately 6 days in the hospital. Chang et al. [8] similarly reported a pediatric case of MP‐associated hepatitis with transient depression of multiple coagulation factors that resolved spontaneously, consistent with the present case. In addition, the patient received ceftriaxone for 10 days. This antibiotic can induce biliary stasis by binding calcium in the bile, leading to sludge formation in the ducts, a phenomenon known as pseudolithiasis [9]. This mechanism may have contributed to the patient's transient hepatic and biliary abnormalities.

Large‐scale comparable data from North America are limited, despite the increasing number of pediatric MP cases seen in recent years [10]. A recent systematic review by Poddighe [11] found mild liver dysfunction to be relatively common in pediatric MP cases after retrospective review, although acute severe hepatitis remains rare. The exact mechanism of hepatic involvement in MP infection is not fully elucidated but is thought to be immune‐mediated. Proposed pathways include molecular mimicry with cross‐reacting antibodies, circulating immune complex deposition, and cytokine‐driven activation of hepatic macrophages leading to hepatocellular inflammation [12, 13]. These mechanisms may explain transient transaminase elevations and occasional cholestatic features in the absence of biliary obstruction.

Conclusions

This case underscores the challenges in pediatric infectious disease diagnosis when multiple overlapping factors including uncontrolled asthma, transient iatrogenic hematologic changes, and nonspecific laboratory abnormalities obscure clinical interpretation. Clinicians should maintain a broad differential and consider atypical presentations of MP, including mild hepatic involvement. Timely recognition supports targeted therapy, rational antibiotic use, and avoidance of unnecessary interventions or prolonged hospitalization, while emphasizing the value of multidisciplinary collaboration in complex pediatric cases.
