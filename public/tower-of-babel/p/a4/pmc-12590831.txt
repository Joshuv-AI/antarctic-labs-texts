Factors associated with accidental exposure to infectious agent among health care providers in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of data published from 2014 to 2023
Authors: Shambel Dessale Asmamaw
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHealth care providers (HCPs) are at a higher risk of being accidentally exposed to infectious agents while providing care to their clients and patients. Despite the presence of individual studies, there is limited evidence on the overall burden and predictors of accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs in Ethiopia. The present comprehensive meta-analysis aimed to highlight the pooled prevalence of accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs in Ethiopia.MethodsThis review includes articles published between January 4, 2014, and April 29, 2023, using electronic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, SCOPUS, Cochrane Library, and Google Scholar). Studies that explore blood and/or sharp device exposure among HCPs in Ethiopia were included, and the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist was utilized to assess the quality of included studies. The data was extracted and analyzed using Excel and STATA version 14, respectively. The association between each variable is explained using Odds ratio (OR). The Cochrane Q test and I2 test statistics assessed study heterogeneity, and a random effects model was used.ResultsThe final analysis included 34 observational studies with a total of 10,859 study participants, which revealed that the pooled prevalence of accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs in Ethiopia was 11.48% (95% CI: 2.6, 20.35). Not taking training (OR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.03, 3.43), being male (OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.03, 2.06), inconsistent use of personal protective equipment (OR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.06, 2.68), and recapping needles after use (OR = 4.64; 95% CI: 2.46, 8.76) were positively associated with accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs.ConclusionsThese findings suggest that accidental blood and sharp device exposure among HCPs remain a significant public health concern in Ethiopia, and interventions should be implemented to address the identified risk factors, such as proper training on infection prevention and control measures and discouraging the recapping of needles, and, moreover, strategies to improve the consistent use of personal protective equipment should be implemented to reduce exposure.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01716-y.Keywords: Accidental exposure, Exposure to infectious agent, Healthcare provider, Systematic review, Meta-analysis, Ethiopia

Introduction

Healthcare workers face a higher likelihood of infections associated with their occupation and their working environment. Healthcare workers provide patient care in environments that are considered to be one of the most unsafe occupational settings. This poses a significant risk for the transmission of infection, as they are frequently exposed to body fluids and clinical practices during their day-to-day activities. Previous evidence demonstrated that exposure to blood and body fluids and needle stick injuries in an occupational setting not only jeopardizes the safety and well-being of healthcare workers but also compromises the quality of healthcare delivery [1–4].

HCPs faced daily a variety of injuries and exposure to infections, and infections contaminated medical equipment [5]. Divers’ pathogens (disease-causing microorganisms) have been reported to cause infections in HCPs following sharp injuries and exposures. This includes bacteria, viruses, fungi, and parasites from sources such as blast mycosis, crypto mycosis, diphtheria, Ebola, gonorrhea, hepatitis virus infections, HIV/AIDS, tetanus, herpes, tuberculosis, toxoplasmosis, leptospirosis, staphylococcal infection, streptococcal infection, and others [5–8].

Several factors have been found to increase the risk of infections among HCPs while caring for their clients/patients. Previous evidence has shown that these factors include recapping needles after use, being overworked and working for more than 40 h per week, noncompliance with standard precautions, lack of training, gender, work experience, and inconsistent use of personal protective equipment. These factors have been identified as being associated with an increased risk of infection among HCPs in Ethiopia [2, 9–18].

Although there are some studies on the risk factors and burden of accidental blood and sharp device exposure among HCPs in Ethiopia [2, 9–41], a comprehensive understanding of the overall burden and common risk factors has not been established, and this comprehensive meta-analysis was conducted to determine the overall burden of accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs, as well as their associated factors, in Ethiopia.

Methods

The study was conducted based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines [42]. This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered in PROSPERO and assigned CRD number 42,020,149,328.

Study design and search strategy

Potential articles were identified by searching PubMed, SCOPUS, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, Google Scholar, and related databases. The search used a combination of key terms, including “burden,” “magnitude,” “prevalence,” “epidemiology,” “risk,” “determinants,” “predictors,” “associated factors,” “risk factors,” “factors,” “facility-acquired infection,” “nosocomial infection,” “hospital-acquired infection,” “care-related infection,” “bloodstream infection,” “surgical site infection,” “skin and soft tissue infection,” “exposure to HIV,” “bloody and bloody fluid,” “hepatitis B virus,” “Needle stick injury,” “pneumonia,” “ventilator-associated pneumonia,” “healthcare professionals,” “healthcare providers,” “Midwives,” “Nurses,” “Physicians,” “Health officers,” “Laboratory technologist,” and Ethiopia. Boolean operators “AND” or “OR” were utilized to combine each word as a searching strategy (Supplementary Material 1).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This review encompasses all published studies conducted between January 4, 2014 and April 29, 2023, and includes all published observational studies in the English language. However, systematic reviews, review articles, conference abstracts, studies with poor methodological quality, qualitative studies, case reports, and case series were excluded from this review.

Operational definition

Healthcare-associated infection

an infection as the invasion and multiplication of pathogenic microorganisms in body tissues, which can lead to disease.

Accidental exposure to infectious devices (agent)

a percutaneous injury (e.g., needle stick or cut with a sharp object), contact of eye, mouth, or other mucous membrane or non-intact skin (e.g., exposed skin that is chapped, abraded, or with dermatitis) with blood, saliva, tissue, or other body fluids that are potentially infectious.

Study selection, quality appraisal, and data extraction

All articles retrieved from the selected databases were exported to Endnote X8, and duplicate files were eliminated. The author screened the articles and abstracts that met the inclusion criteria for full-text appraisal. The quality of articles that met the inclusion criteria was assessed using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal checklist [43]. Data extraction was performed using Microsoft Excel 2019. The data extraction tool included information such as the author’s name, year of publication, region of study, study area, participants, study period, type of infection, main outcome, sample size, number of participants develop an outcome, prevalence, and OR (including lower and upper Confidence Interval (CI)).

Statistical methods and analysis

The data were analyzed using STATA version 14. The pooled estimate of the prevalence of HCP and its predicators were estimated with OR with a 95% CI. The degree of heterogeneity was assessed using I2 statistics, with a random-effects model used for I2 values greater than 50%. Subgroup analyses were performed to explore the possibility of heterogeneity. Furthermore, Egger’s test was conducted to assess the presence of publication bias [44].

Results

Study selection

This review includes only published articles on factors associated with accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs in Ethiopian healthcare workers from January 4, 2014, and April 29, 2023. As depicted in Fig. 1, a comprehensive search of electronic databases yielded a total of 1,243 articles, with an additional 25 articles identified through the references of cited lists. After removing duplicated articles, 678 remained, and the remaining 622 articles were further excluded based on their titles and abstracts. A total of 56 articles were assessed for eligibility. Among these, 22 full-text articles were excluded, resulting in a total of 34 articles that met the inclusion criteria and were included in the review.

Of the included studies, 14 were conducted in the Amhara region, 5 in the Oromia region, 4 in Addis Ababa, and the remaining 11 studies were conducted in other regions or in combination with other regions. The total number of study participants considered in this review was 10,859, as indicated in the details presented in Table 1.

Prevalence of accidental exposure to infectious agent among health care providers in Ethiopia

The overall pooled estimate of accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs in Ethiopia was found to be 34.64% (95% CI: 26.59, 42.68), with a high level of heterogeneity observed among the studies (I2 = 99.4%, p-value = 0.000) (Fig. 2). However, the funnel plot showed a non-symmetrical appearance (refer to Supplementary Material 2), and the Egger’s regression asymmetry test indicated significant publication bias with a p-value of 0.00, thus suggesting the presence of publication bias. To address this, the ‘trim and fill’ analysis method was performed to estimate the number of missing studies as a potential source of publication bias (Supplementary Material 3). Therefore, the final adjusted pooled prevalence of infections among HCPs in Ethiopia was 11.48% (95% CI: 2.6, 20.35). Both the lowest and highest prevalence were reported in Amhara regions, with the magnitude of 1.4% and 88.9% [22, 41].

Sub-group analysis

Subgroup analyses were conducted to identify potential sources of heterogeneity based on various study-level characteristics, such as the year of study (before 2019 and 2019 & above), regions (Amhara, Oromia, Addis Ababa, and other regions), infection developed (health care associated infection or not), and exposure type (sharp injury or not). The prevalence of infection among HCPs in studies conducted before 2019 was 39.3 (95% CI: 26.06, 53.59), while after 2019, it was 29.93 (95% CI: 20.86, 39.99). In terms of region, the highest prevalence was observed in the Amhara region; it was 37.95 (95% CI: 23.84, 52.06), as illustrated in Table 2.

Sensitivity analysis

The findings of a sensitivity analysis revealed that no single study had a value that deviated from the total estimate’s 95% confidence interval (see supplementary material 4).

Factors associated with accidental exposure to infectious agent among health care providers in Ethiopia

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to identify potential associations between several variables, including infection prevention training, gender of healthcare professionals, utilization status of personal protective equipment (PPE), recap needle usage, working hours per week, and compliance status regarding standard precautions, and their respective impact on accidental exposure to infectious devices in Ethiopia.

The results of this study showed that HCPs who had not received infection prevention training were almost two times more likely to be exposed to infectious devices compared to those who had undergone such training. This association was statistically significant with an odds ratio of 1.88 and a 95% CI of 1.03 to 3.43. However, there was high heterogeneity noted in this analysis with an I2 value of 92.8% and a p-value of 0.001, as illustrated in Fig. 3a.

Pooled odds ratio (OR) for accidental exposure to infectious agents (devices) among health care providers in Ethiopia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of data published from 2014 to 2023: not taking training (a), Male health care providers (b), inconsistent use of Personal Protective Equipment PPE (c), needle recap after use (d), Working hour ≥ 40 per week (e), and no compliance on standard precautions (f)

Furthermore, male HCPs were found to be 1.46 times more likely to expose to infectious devices than female HCPs. This association was found to be statistically significant with an odds ratio of 1.46 and 95% CI of 1.03 to 2.06. However, the analysis showed moderate heterogeneity with an I2 value of 73.3% and p-value of 0.001, as shown in Fig. 3b.

In addition, the association between the utilization of PPE and accidentally exposure to infectious devices among HCPs was assessed using eight studies. The overall pooled meta-analysis revealed that HCPs who utilized PPE inconsistently were 1.68 times more likely to prone to accidentally exposed to infectious devices than those who used PPE consistently. This association was statistically significant with an odds ratio of 1.68 and 95% CI of 1.06 to 2.68, as displayed in Fig. 3c.

Moreover, the association between needle recap after use and accidental exposure to infectious devices among HCPs was evaluated using data from twelve studies. The final pooled analysis revealed that HCPs who recapped needles after use were 4.64 times more likely to accidentally be exposed to infectious devices than those who did not recap needles. This association was statistically significant with an odds ratio of 4.64 and a 95% CI of 2.46 to 8.76, as shown in Fig. 3d.

Similarly, the association between the number of working hours per week and compliance with standard precautions in relation to accidental expose to infectious devices among HCPs was evaluated. The overall results indicated that neither of these factors had a significant effect accidentally expose to infectious devices among HCPs. The odds ratios were 2.32 with a 95% CI of 0.83 to 6.46 and 2.2 with a 95% CI of 0.99 to 4.88, as shown in Fig. 3e and f, respectively. These findings suggest that the number of working hours per week and compliance with standard precautions haven’t an association with accidentally exposed to infectious agents.

Discussion

Accidental exposure to infectious agents can pose serious risks to healthcare workers and has become a growing concern globally, particularly in resource-limited countries. The pooled prevalence of accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs in Ethiopia was found to be 11.48% (95% CI: 2.6, 20.35). This figure is consistent with the point prevalence report of low-income and middle-income countries, which ranged from 5.7 to 19.1% [45], In sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence of HAIs ranged between 1.6% and 28.7%, while the national report of Ethiopia is 9–35.8% [46, 47]. This high prevalence may be attributed to the inadequate implementation of infection prevention and control measures in healthcare facilities in resource-limited countries. According to an assessment by the World Health Organization of infection prevention measures in 30,000 clinics and hospitals in Africa, only 16% had assessment scores above 75%. Moreover, many health institutions lack the necessary infrastructure to implement key infection prevention measures or to prevent overcrowding [48].

This systematic review and meta-analysis also analyzed the pooled prevalence of accidental exposure to infectious agents by taking into account the year of study, region, exposure type, and infection developed. The pooled prevalence of accidental exposure to infectious agents in a study conducted before 2019 and after 2019 was 39.3% and 29.93%, respectively. The pooled prevalence of accidental exposure among HCPs is 37.95% in Amhara, 32.74% in Oromia, 31.87% in Addis Ababa, and 31.8% in other regions. This review had also tried to show the subgroup analysis by taking exposure type (infectious agent type) and then 38.63% for sharp devices and 30.09% for fluid and other infectious agents. In this subgroup analysis, the pooled prevalence of accidental exposure to infectious agents (devices) has no difference by region, study year, or exposure type.

Additionally, subgroup analysis was done by taking into account whether infection developed or not among HCPs. The pooled prevalence among healthcare providers exposed to and developing infection was 3.68%, and only exposure to the infectious agent was 41.82%. Healthcare-associated infection among healthcare professionals in Ethiopia was 3.68 (1.75, 5.61). It is lower than the previous meta-analysis conducted in Ethiopia, which was 7.4% [49]. The possible explanation is the variation in the time of the study period and the number of studies included. But it is in line with the study conducted in 62 countries, which was 2.3% [50]. The possible explanation is that low-resource countries are prone to imperfect vaccine policies, including partial coverage of eligible individuals, and the involvement of stakeholders in infection prevention decreases [51, 52].

The present meta-analysis revealed that not taking training (OR = 1.88; 95% CI: 1.03, 3.43), being male (OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.03, 2.06), inconsistent use of personal protective equipment (OR = 1.68; 95% CI: 1.06, 2.68), and recapping needles after use (OR = 4.64; 95% CI: 2.46, 8.76) were statistically associated with accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs in Ethiopia.

Healthcare providers who did not receive infection prevention training were almost twice as likely to accidentally be exposed to infectious agents compared to those who did receive training. This finding is consistent with research conducted in sub-Saharan Africa [53]. The observed association between infection prevention training and reduced risk of infection may be attributed to the fact that such training enhances the knowledge, attitudes, and practices of HCPs. Training can be an effective means of acquiring the necessary knowledge and skills to implement safety precautions and qualified HCPs are better equipped to put theoretical or training-based knowledge into practice. Consequently, they are better able to protect themselves against workplace infections [54, 55].

Furthermore, our study found that male HCPs were at a higher risk of infectious agents compared to female health care professionals (OR = 1.46; 95% CI: 1.03, 2.06). This may be due to the fact that men are less likely than women to adhere to universal precautions, which could explain this finding [32].

Likewise, the utilization of PPE was also found to be associated with accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs. HCPs who inconsistently utilized PPE were 1.7 times more likely to acquire infections compared to those who consistently utilized PPE. This finding underscores the importance of adhering to universal precautions, which can help prevent work or procedure-related injuries. In addition, this study revealed that accidental exposure to infectious agents was more than four times higher among healthcare providers who recapped needles after use compared to their counterparts. This may be attributed to the fact that recapping needles can lead to self-injury or accidental punctures. According to the World Health Organization’s infection prevention guidelines, any used needles and sharp materials should be disposed of in a safety box without recapping. Recapping sharp materials after use contradicts this standard. Lastly, our systematic review and meta-analysis indicated that there was no statistical association between accidental exposure to infectious agents among HCPs and their working hours per week or compliance with standard precautions.

Conclusion

These studies found that accidental exposure to infectious agents was prevalent among HCPs in Ethiopia, with approximately one in every ten professionals being susceptible to accidental exposure to infectious Agents. This finding is consistent with national reports on the matter. Not having undergone infection prevention training, being male, inconsistent use of personal protective equipment, and recapping needle usage after use increased the odds of accidental exposure to infectious agents among healthcare professionals in Ethiopia. Therefore, implementing and consistently adhering to standard infection prevention and control practices and following a multi-pronged approach are expected to alleviate the existing condition.

Limitation of the study

This review tried to address the pooled prevalence and factors associated with accidental exposure to infectious agents among health care providers in Ethiopia, but it is not without limitation. The first thing is that this study includes observational studies, which don’t estimate cause-and-effect relationships. Secondly, subgroup analysis, meta-regression, and sensitivity analysis were done to identify heterogeneity, but heterogeneity still exists.
