An Unhealthy Start: The Nutritional Composition, Processing, and on‐Pack Claims of Commercial Foods for Infants and Toddlers in Thailand
Authors: Abhirat Supthanasup, Nisachol Cetthakrikul, Andini Pramono, Matthew Kelly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNutrition during the first 1000 days of life is vital for fostering child health. Commercial foods for infants and toddlers are now increasingly prevalent, yet their nutritional quality and insufficiently regulated marketing are a global concern. This cross‐sectional audit comprehensively assessed the nutritional composition, processing levels, on‐pack claims, and compliance with the WHO Nutrient and Promotion Profile Model (NPPM) nutrient composition requirements of 216 products available in Thailand. Our findings revealed that ultra‐processed products dominated the market (59.7%), with 43.5% of all foods containing added free sugars. Snacks and finger foods were the largest and most concerning category, 77.7% of these were ultra‐processed, 67.0% contained added free sugar, and 25.9% had added salt. Nutritionally, this category was high in energy, sugar, and sodium but deficient in micronutrients. Compliance with WHO NPPM nutrient composition was highly variable and particularly low for snacks concerning added free sugars. The prevalence of on‐pack claims was high, with composition and nutrition (96.8%), health (58.8%), and marketing (88.4%) claims. Therefore, urgent policy interventions are required to address the prevalence of nutritionally inadequate products and misleading claims.Keywords: child nutrition, claims, commercial foods for infants and toddlers, food and nutrition policy, food marketing, nutrient profiling, processing, Thai

Introduction

Nutrition during the first 1000 days of life is a pivotal determinant of an individual's lifelong health and well‐being (Saavedra and Dattilo 2022). Complementary feeding is a vital component of this developmental window, playing a role in preventing child malnutrition and mitigating the long‐term risk of non‐communicable diseases (NCDs) (Adair 2012). Recognizing this, global health authorities recommend introducing nutritious and safe complementary foods from 6 months of age, while advising against the consumption of foods containing added sugar and salt (Binns et al. 2020; Fewtrell et al. 2017; United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2020). Despite these guidelines, evolving food systems, and social shifts have led to a significant global rise in the availability and consumption of commercial foods for infants and toddlers. This shift is underscored by the significant growth trajectory of the global food market. For example, the sector had a market value at $105.11 billion in 2024 and a forecast of $122.02 billion by 2030 (Research and Markets 2025). This trend is not confined to high‐income countries but is increasingly prevalent in low‐ and middle‐income countries (LMICs), including those in Southeast Asia (Green et al. 2019; Hinnouho et al. 2023; Pries et al. 2017). In Thailand, while the milk formula market faces challenges from government policies promoting breastfeeding, the prepared and dried baby food segments are showing positive growth (Euromonitor International 2025). Prepared baby food recorded the highest growth within the sector in 2025, growing by 6.9% in current retail value. Dried baby food also showed consistent growth with a 2.9% current value compound annual growth rate (CAGR) from 2020 to 2025, and it is forecast to grow at a 3.3% constant value CAGR from 2025 to 2030 (Euromonitor International 2025). This shift is partly driven by the needs of modern parents, who may find commercial products to be a more convenient and nutritious option (Heller et al. 2019; Supthanasup et al. 2021a).

The proliferation of commercial foods for infants and toddlers has raised significant public health issues. These products are industrially processed foods, which are typically formulated with high levels of refined carbohydrates, sugar, fat, sodium, and food additives (Santos et al. 2022). Furthermore, these food products often have low nutrient density, failing to provide the essential micronutrients. This is a significant gap given that the South East Asian Nutrition Surveys II (SEANUTS II) found the risks of inadequate micronutrient intakes were high among young children (Pongcharoen et al. 2024). Specifically, the risk of inadequate iron intake for those aged 0.5–0.9 years was 66.5%, while the risk of inadequate vitamin A intake for children aged 1.0–3.9 years was 17.1%. This study also revealed a high anemia prevalence of 56.6% in Thai infants and 35.2% in toddlers (Pongcharoen et al. 2024). While information on the iron content of commercial products in Thailand is sparse, the SEANUTS II study's finding of high inadequate dietary iron intake highlights a potential nutritional gap these products may fail to address. Also, this market exemplifies the dynamics of the Commercial Determinants of Health (CDoH), where industry practices directly shape consumer preferences and public health outcomes (Dumbili et al. 2025). A major concern is that regulations for infant and toddler foods lag behind those for breast‐milk substitutes, particularly regarding on‐pack claims, marketing terminology, and mandated nutritional standards (Bridge et al. 2021). This regulatory gap enables marketing strategies that use claims to mislead caregivers about a product's true nutritional quality, thereby creating a health halo effect. Such misleading marketing presents a key challenge to advancing childhood nutrition, as it can influence parental choices that favor less nutritious products (Dixon et al. 2024).

Existing regulatory frameworks, including the Codex Alimentarius and European Union standards guiding nutrient composition of foods for infants and young children, are outdated and inadequate. This has allowed unsuitable, sweet products to dominate the market as they fail to regulate added sugar or require label declaration (Leszczyńska et al. 2025). In response to these global concerns, the World Health Organization (WHO) Regional Office for Europe developed the Nutrient and Promotion Profile Model (NPPM) to guide the regulation of commercial foods for infants and toddlers aged 6–36 months (World Health Organization 2022). Recent evaluations using the NPPM in countries such as the United States (Coyle et al. 2024), Australia (Chung et al. 2024), and Spain (Leszczyńska et al. 2025) have revealed a consistent pattern of non‐compliance, with a large proportion of products failing nutritional standards and virtually none meeting the criteria for appropriate promotion. While these findings point to a widespread global issue, the level of compliance and specific market characteristics vary, making local assessments essential for informing targeted policy interventions. Therefore, this study aims to conduct a comprehensive cross‐sectional audit of commercial foods for infants and toddlers in Thailand. The objective is to assess the nutritional composition, level of processing, on‐pack claims, and the compliance with the WHO NPPM criteria. The findings will provide critical evidence to support the development of policy interventions required to address the prevalence of nutritionally inadequate products and misleading claims in Thailand.

Methods

This study employed a cross‐sectional design to audit the market for commercial foods for infants and toddlers in Thailand.

Data Source

Data on commercial infant and toddler food products were collected between May and August 2025 through a comprehensive review of the official websites of four major Thai food retailers. These retailers were selected as they represent the leading players in Thai retail food sector. According to the USDA (2024) report, the hypermarket segment is dominated by Lotus and Big C, while Tops and Gourmet Market are key leaders in the supermarket segment. Products were identified from the “baby and toddler food” category on each retailer's website and subsequently cross‐referenced with manufacturer websites to compile a comprehensive list of all available commercial foods for infants and toddlers.

Product Identification & Distinction

Products were included in the sample if they were explicitly marketed as suitable for feeding infants and young children up to 36 months of age. Product suitability was determined based on explicit age recommendations provided on the packaging (United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2023; World Health Organization WHO Regional Office for Europe 2019a). To avoid duplication, different package sizes or types of the same item were treated as a single product. However, products were considered distinct if they differed by flavor, brand, or manufacturer (Bassetti et al. 2022).

This study excluded infant formula and other breastmilk substitutes, products lacking clear age recommendations, packaged snacks intended for general consumption, street foods, and any products where the ingredient list and nutritional information were not available in Thai or English (Bassetti et al. 2022; Khosravi et al. 2023; Moumin et al. 2020; Sugimoto et al. 2023; United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2023).

Data Analysis

Product Characteristics, Processing Levels, and Food Additive Usage

The product name, country of manufacturer and the full ingredient list from their labels were extracted into a database. Following data extraction, we classified products into categories according to the WHO NPPM classification system: cereals, dairy, processed fruit and vegetables, meals, snacks and finger foods, ingredients, confectionery, and drinks (World Health Organization 2022). However, the ingredients category was excluded from analysis as the scope of the study focused on food products requiring minimal preparation or ready‐to‐eat products. Additionally, no products in the dairy, confectionery, and drinks categories suitable for this age group were identified in the surveyed market. Thus, these categories were not included in the final analysis.

To assess the level of food processing and the use of food additives, the study adopted the methodology from a previous study utilizing the NOVA classification system (Pries et al. 2024). Based on an analysis of their ingredient lists, all products were categorized into unprocessed/minimally processed, processed or ultra‐processed. Unprocessed/minimally processed products were defined as those containing only unprocessed foods, which may include added fortified vitamins and minerals. Processed foods were identified by the inclusion of processed culinary ingredients such as sugars, salts, or oils. Ultra‐processed foods were identified by the presence of specific ingredient markers (e.g., inverted sugar, maltodextrin, protein isolates, hydrogenated oils, artificial flavors) or any cosmetic additives used to enhance flavor, color, or texture.

As part of the ingredient assessment, added free sugars were defined and identified in accordance with the WHO NPPM guideline. This definition included (1) all mono‐ and disaccharides (including sugars derived from fruits, sugarcane, palms or root vegetables), (2) all syrups, nectars and honey, and (3) fruit juices or concentrated/powdered fruit juice (excluding lemon or lime juice) (World Health Organization 2022). While the WHO NPPM combines these with non‐sugar sweeteners for the overall compliance assessment, this ingredient analysis evaluated added free sugar as a standalone category to provide more detail at the types of ingredients being used.

Nutritional Profile and Claims

Nutritional data (serving size, nutrient values per serving and per 100 g) were extracted. For declared values using a less than symbol (e.g., < 20 mg), the stated threshold value was used (Alexy et al. 2022). Fields with missing label information were recorded as missing. We extracted all textual and symbolic content in English or Thai to identify on‐pack claims. Claims were classified as either nutrition or health claims following WHO NPPM definitions (World Health Organization 2022). Claims not referring to nutrition or health were coded as marketing claims and categorized into six themes (1) high quality (2) convenience/lifestyle, (3) optimum feeding, (4) ideal texture, (5) ideal taste, and (6) endorsement.

Compliance With the WHO NPPM Nutrient Composition Requirements

To evaluate compliance with the WHO NPPM for nutrient composition, this study utilized the official NPPM data entry template, which was obtained from the babyfoodnppm.org website. For each commercial product, relevant information was transcribed from the product packaging into the NPPM data entry template. The completed data entry form was then uploaded to the NPPM assessment tool hosted on the babyfoodnppm.org website. The tool automatically processed the data to generate a results page, which detailed the product's performance and compliance with WHO recommendations.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. We calculated frequencies and percentages for categorical data (e.g., product characteristics, presence of concerning ingredients, food additives, processing levels, and on‐pack claims). The Chi‐square test was used to examine the associations between these characteristics and the product categories. For continuous nutritional data, which were not normally distributed, values were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR). The Kruskal–Wallis H test was employed to compare the median values of energy and nutrients (protein, fat, carbohydrates, total sugar, sodium, iron, and Vitamin A) across the different infant and toddler food categories. Where significant differences were detected, pairwise multiple comparisons with a Bonferroni adjustment were performed.

Ethics Statement

This study involved the assessment of commercially available food products and their packaging information. As the research did not involve human participants, animal subjects, or sensitive personal data, ethical approval or waiver from an Institutional Review Board (IRB) was not required.

Results

Product Characteristics, Processing Levels, and Food Additive Usage

Of the 216 commercial foods for infants and toddlers analyzed, 75.0% were produced in Thailand. All products were intended for children age 6 months and older, with no products reported a minimum age under 6 months. Specifically, 47.2% had a manufacturer‐recommended minimum age of 6 months, 27.3% were for 7–11 months, and 25.5% were for children aged 12 months and over. Overall, nearly 60% of all products were classified as ultra‐processed, with 43.5% having added free sugars and 15.7% having added salt. Food additives were presented in approximately one‐third of all products, with 31.0% of the total sample containing at least one cosmetic additive.

Product characteristics differed significantly across categories (p < 0.001). Snacks and finger foods were the most common category and contained the highest prevalence of concerning ingredients. The majority of these products were ultra‐processed (77.7%), while two‐thirds (67.0%) contained added free sugar and over a quarter (25.9%) contained added salt. For these products, the most frequent minimum age recommendation was 12 months and over, followed by 6 months. In contrast, cereals were less processed, with 55.0% classified as minimally processed. Although commonly recommended at 6 months (75.0%), nearly a quarter (22.5%) of these products contained added free sugar. Processed fruit and vegetable products and meals demonstrated varied ingredient and processing characteristics. While the majority of fruit and vegetable products were ultra‐processed (68.8%), they were the least likely to contain added salt (3.1%) or cosmetic additives (3.1%). Meals were predominantly processed and had the lowest prevalence of both added free sugar (9.4%) and other additives (9.4%). Further details are given in Table 1.

Nutritional Profile

The nutritional analysis of 216 commercial foods for infants and toddlers revealed statistically significant differences in energy and nutrient content across the four product categories (p < 0.05). The nutritional profiles of the product categories fell into two distinct groups relative to one another based on significant differences in nutrient density. There were high‐energy, high‐carbohydrate products and low‐energy products. Cereals and snacks and finger foods were both characterized as high‐energy, high‐carbohydrate products, with an identical median energy content of 400.0 kcal/100 g. They also had the highest median carbohydrate content at 78.6 g/100 g and 83.2 g/100 g, respectively. However, these two categories differed in other key nutrients. Cereals contained significantly higher median protein (10.0 g/100 g) and median fat (2.4 g/100 g) compared to others. In contrast, snacks and finger foods were higher in median total sugar (10.0 g/100 g) and contained the highest median sodium of all categories (116.0 mg/100 g). Furthermore, snacks were notable for their lack of micronutrients, with a median value of 0.0 mg/100 g for iron and 0.0 µg RAE/100 g for Vitamin A. However, these micronutrient values reflect limited reporting, as only 48.2% of snacks labeled iron content and 59.8% labeled vitamin A.

Conversely, processed fruit and vegetable products and meals were low‐energy products, with median values of 63.6 kcal/100 g and 53.0 kcal/100 g, respectively. Processed fruit & vegetable products had the lowest median protein (1.0 g/100 g) and sodium (9.7 mg/100 g) content, but a high median total sugar content (10.2 g/100 g) comparable to those of snacks. Although meals contained moderate protein (2.4 g/100 g) and sodium (48.6 mg/100 g). Meals also had a low total sugar (1.1 g/100 g) similar to cereals. Further details are given in Table 2.

Compliance With the WHO NPPM Nutrient Composition Requirements

Table 3 indicates high variability in the compliance of 216 commercial foods for infants and toddlers with the WHO NPPM nutritional criteria. While the products generally met the standard for total fat, many failed to meet the criteria for energy density, sodium, and added free sugar. Among cereals, there was a clear distinction between those with and without added milk. Dry cereals without milk completely failed to meet the energy density criterion. However, this subgroup had high compliance rates for the sodium (88.9%) and added free sugar (88.9%) criteria. In contrast, 84.6% of dry cereals containing milk passed the energy density criterion but showed lower compliance with the sodium (60.0%) and added free sugar (53.8%) criteria. Processed fruit and vegetable products also showed high compliance in energy density (over 65.0%) and with the criteria for sodium and added free sugar (over 80.0%). Most meal subcategories showed high compliance with total fat and added free sugar, yet the compliance with energy density and sodium was typically low. Lastly, while snacks and finger foods showed relatively high compliance for sodium and total fat (around 70% and higher), compliance with the added free sugar criterion was notably low in the two largest subcategories, specifically snacks with dairy (18.8%) and snacks without dairy (28.7%).

Composition, Nutrition, and Marketing Claims

Composition and nutrition claims were the most prevalent type of marketing message, appearing on 96.8% of all products (Table 4). The prevalence of these claims was 100% for cereals, processed fruits and vegetables, and meals. They were also high for snacks (93.8%). The most common subtypes were “free from” claims (84.3%) and claims about the “natural or healthful nature of ingredients” (81.5%). Within the “free from” category, the most frequent statements were “no preservative” (54.2%), “allergen free” (50.5%), and “no added sugar” (48.1%). However, the use of these specific claims differed significantly across product categories. For instance, claims of being “allergen free” were most common on snacks and finger foods (67.9%), whereas “no added sugar” claims appeared most frequently on meals (75.0%) and processed fruit and vegetables (68.8%). Similarly, “no added salt” claims were significantly more likely to be found on meals (68.8%) than on any other category. Finally, claims promoting “beneficial ingredients,” such as whole grains, were most prominent on cereal foods (40.0%) and snacks (36.6%).

Health claims were present on over half of all products (58.8%), though their use varied significantly across the different food categories (p < 0.001). This claim type was highly used in cereals (97.5%) followed by snacks and finger foods (67.0%). However, they were used less frequently on processed fruit and vegetable (31.3%) and meals (9.4%). Notably, claims related to “health and development were commonly used for cereals (62.5%) and snacks and finger foods (55.4%),” while they were rare on meals (9.4%) and absent from processed fruit and vegetable products. Additionally, marketing claims were also very common, identified on 88.4% of all products. The most frequent marketing subtype was an appeal to “high quality,” which appeared on 58.8% of products. Specific marketing messages were clearly targeted at different product types. Claims emphasizing “convenience or lifestyle” were most prominent on meals (87.5%) and cereals (60.0%) but were significantly less common on snacks (21.4%). In contrast, claims about “ideal texture” were a predominant feature on snacks and finger foods (54.5%). However, they were rare on processed fruit and vegetable products and meals, at less than 10%.

Discussion

This study comprehensively assessed commercial foods for infants and toddlers in Thailand market by analyzing product processing levels, nutritional content, on‐pack claims, and compliance with the WHO Nutrient and Promotion Profile Model (NPPM) nutrient composition requirements according to product categories. The results reveal that the Thai market is dominated by ultra‐processed snacks and finger foods, which frequently contain added free sugars and sodium. Despite these nutritional shortfalls, nearly all products carried promotional claims, and compliance with WHO nutrient standards was highly variable. The composition of the market for commercial infant and toddler foods appears to vary significantly across different geographical contexts, with notable differences between Thai market and those in high‐income countries. Our findings highlight the Thai landscape dominated by snacks and finger foods, which account for 51.9% of all products. Our finding reinforces a previous survey in 2021 in Thailand that also identified this category as dominant, comprising 42.2% of products (Consortium for Improving Complementary Foods in Southeast Asia COMMIT 2023). This composition diverges from market compositions reported in high‐income countries. For instance, snacks were one of the smallest market categories in Spain (Leszczyńska et al. 2025). While in Australia and the UK, markets were dominated by processed fruits and vegetables and meals, with snacks accounting for 21% and 16% of all products, respectively (Bozkir et al. 2025; Scully et al. 2024). We hypothesize that this dominance of snacks may be driven by the increasing demand for convenience among modern Thai parents, where commercial snacks fill a specific niche as on‐the‐go foods or finger foods for motor development, whereas main meals are still largely prepared at home. The practice of snacking in early childhood warrants careful consideration, as it significantly influences overall diet quality (Kachurak et al. 2019; Nuru and Mamang 2015). Although details on timing may differ, the fundamental principle across national guidelines is the critical importance of food quality. For example, recommendations from both Thailand's Department of Health and the UK's NHS exclusively endorse simple, nutrient‐dense, and unprocessed foods (Department of Health Ministry of Public Health 2025; National Health Service 2024). These recommendations point out the concern in Thai market which is dominated by ultra‐processed snack products and relatively poor nutrient content.

The increasing prevalence of commercially processed foods in the diets of infants and young children is a global public health concern (World Health Organization Regional Office for Europe 2019b). This dietary shift is problematic as many of these products are ultra‐processed and formulated with various cosmetic additives, which may have adverse implications for child health and development. Our findings underscore this concern, revealing that more than half (59.7%) of commercial foods for infants and toddlers in Thailand market were ultra‐processed. This prevalence is higher than in previous regional studies. A study using 2021 data from Southeast Asia reported a 47.7% prevalence of ultra‐processed products (Pries et al. 2024). While an earlier analysis of the European market between 2017 and 2021 found the rate of 29.2% (Grammatikaki et al. 2021). This comparison highlights a possible increasing trend in the availability of ultra‐processed options for infants and toddlers. The extensive use of food additives is also a key feature of these products. As shown in our results, cosmetic additives were present in 31.0% of all sample, which was consistent with a previous study in Southeast Asia (Pries et al. 2024). Recent research suggests that food additives in ultra‐processed foods are a primary mechanism for gut microbiome disruption (Rondinella et al. 2025). The consumption of these foods during early life poses a significant risk, as this is a crucial period for establishing the gut microbiome and any disruption could compromise healthy growth and development (Fontaine et al. 2023).

The high prevalence of added free sugars and salt in commercial foods for infants and toddlers is a global public health concern that contradicts recommendations from organizations including UNICEF, the European Society for Pediatric Gastroenterology, Hepatology, and Nutrition (ESPGHAN), and the Asia Pacific Academic Consortium for Public Health (APACPH) (Binns et al. 2020; Fewtrell et al. 2017; United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2020; World Health Organization 2023). Our analysis found that 43.5% of all products contained added free sugar. This issue was particularly concerning in snacks where two‐third of products contained added sugar. This is a part of a global trend, with similarly high rates of added sugar found in snacks in the United States (73.8%) and Australia (51.0%) (Coyle et al. 2024; Scully et al. 2024). These nutritional issues in Thai market are part of a regional trend, as a recent study found high sugar content in similar products across Southeast Asia based on WHO Europe criteria (Bassetti et al. 2022). The addition of free sugars is a primary nutritional concern, as it increases the risk of excess weight gain, poor dental health, and the development of a preference for sweet foods (Beckerman et al. 2017; World Health Organization 2015). The presence of added salt in 15.7% of all audited samples, and 25.9% of snacks, further underscores the divergence of commercial product formulations from established pediatric nutrition guidelines. Our analysis further highlights the poor nutritional quality of commercial foods for infants and toddlers in Thailand market. The poor quality of these products is exemplified by snacks which are typically empty calories, as they are high in energy, sugar, and sodium while being deficient in iron. After 6 months of age, infants' depleted neonatal reserves make dietary iron from complementary foods essential for continued growth, immune function, and neurological development (United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2020). Insufficient intake can lead to iron deficiency and subsequent anemia. These conditions are known to permanently impair long‐term cognitive and psychomotor development (Aboussaleh and Bikri 2022; Mitterstiller et al. 2022; Pivina et al. 2019).

The poor nutritional quality of the commercial foods for infants and toddlers in this study raises the contentious issue of fortification. While fortification is often proposed as a strategy to address hidden hunger and nutrient deficiencies, more research is required to determine if these products lead to the displacement of other nutrient‐dense foods or result in excessive nutrient intake (Csoelle et al. 2022). Furthermore, fortifying nutritionally poor, ultra‐processed products may inadvertently promote the consumption of empty calorie vehicles. Recent literature suggests that adding micronutrients to ultra‐processed foods can create a health halo effect, leading caregivers to overestimate the nutritional quality of products that are otherwise high in sugar, salt, or fat (Kroker‐Labos et al. 2022). This may mislead parents into perceiving high sugar or high sodium snacks as healthy options. Consequently, the fortification of nutritionally poor products potentially undermines efforts to promote a diverse, whole food‐based diet.

Our assessment of WHO NPPM nutrient composition compliance reveals category‐specific nutritional shortcomings. For instance, dry cereals prepared without milk showed zero compliance with the energy density criterion. Our result aligns with Australian findings but contrasts with high compliance rates in the United States and Spain (Coyle et al. 2024; Leszczyńska et al. 2025; Scully et al. 2024). This difference may also reflect variations in product formulation. Thai cereals are predominantly simple rice‐based products which are naturally lower in energy density. This pattern of category‐specific issues extended to other nutrients. While the compliance of snacks with added free sugar was exceptionally low, meals consistently failed to meet the sodium standard. Specifically, our study found that fewer than 30% of snacks met the “no added sugar/sweetener” criterion, a rate lower than the 39% compliance reported in a previous Thai survey in 2021 (Consortium for Improving Complementary Foods in Southeast Asia COMMIT 2023). The low compliance for sodium in savory meals mirrors findings from Australia and Spain, where this category also performed poorly compared to others (Leszczyńska et al. 2025; Scully et al. 2024). This highlights a significant challenge for Thai caregivers seeking to make healthy choices and underscores the urgent need for national regulation of the commercial infant and toddler food industry. This conclusion is strongly echoed by the authors of the multi‐country Southeast Asian study, who similarly concluded that the majority of commercial baby foods were not nutritionally suitable and called for new policies and stronger regulations to limit the promotion of inappropriate commercial foods for infants and toddlers in the region. This argument is consistent with a broader regional study across Southeast Asia, which similarly identified most commercial baby foods as nutritionally inappropriate and advocated for stronger protective policies (Bassetti et al. 2022).

The use of on‐pack claims is a ubiquitous and highly strategic practice in Thai market for commercial infant and toddler foods. Our audit found that nearly all products feature composition and nutrition or marketing claims with health claims also present on over half. This strategic use of claims in the Thai market reflects a global trend, particularly concerning “free‐from” statements. Our finding that 54.2% of all products claim “no preservatives” is comparable to the 67% in Australia (Dunford et al. 2024), but is nearly five times higher than the 11.5% reported in the US (Coyle et al. 2024). The difference is also pronounced for “allergen free” claims, which were more prevalent in our study (50.5%) than in the US sample (19.0%) (Coyle et al. 2024). Our findings suggest a strong marketing focus on consumer anxieties around safety and purity. This marketing strategy leverages real parental fears, as Thai caregivers have shown significant concern over allergenic ingredients (Supthanasup et al. 2021b). Free‐from claims regarding undesirable ingredients have been shown to be particularly influential in parental purchasing decisions for their toddlers (Dixon et al. 2024). Such strategies targeting parental concern are not unique to infant and toddler foods and have also been observed in the marketing of infant formula (World Health Organization & United Nations Children's Fund UNICEF 2022). This marketing strategy is problematic because it aligns with consumer anxiety but conflicts with current scientific consensus. Recent evidence suggests that delaying allergen introduction may increase allergy risk, while early introduction promotes tolerance (Ferrante et al. 2020; Larson et al. 2017). Moreover, the “no added sugar” claims on commercial foods for infants and toddlers is also a well‐documented global marketing trend. High prevalence rates (around 60%–70%) have been reported in many markets, including Portugal, the UK, and Australia (Brunacci et al. 2023; Garcia et al. 2022; Santos et al. 2022). Our findings align with this trend, with nearly half of all products featuring a “no added sugar” claim. This marketing is highly effective, as the “no added sugar” claim has been shown to be a powerful motivator for parents' purchasing choices (McCann et al. 2022).

Our analysis reveals a significant paradox in the marketing of commercial infant and toddler foods, where health claims are most heavily applied to some of the least nutritionally appropriate product categories. While over half of all products featured a health claim, they were overwhelmingly concentrated on cereals and snacks. The targeting of cereals is consistent with a study in Taiwan (Koo et al. 2018). However, the central problem remains the application of these claims to the snack category, which our data reveal to be empty calories. The strategic use of positive health messages on these nutritionally inadequate products creates a “health halo” effect. When products feature health‐related claims, consumers may perceive them as being healthier (Pulker et al. 2018). Misleading marketing and labeling of commercial infant and toddler foods present a key challenge to advancing nutrition in the early years, which is fundamental for lifelong health.

Marketing claims were also highly strategic and targeted. The emphasis on “convenience” for meal products in our study (87.5%) appears to be a direct response to a primary purchasing motivation for parents (Awoke et al. 2025; Isaacs et al. 2022). Interestingly, this theme was less pronounced in New Zealand, where only 39% of products featured convenience claims (Mackay et al. 2025). In contrast, the appeal to “high quality” is a more consistent international strategy. Our finding of 58.8% was almost identical to that in New Zealand (57%). Similarly, while 54.5% of snacks in our study claimed “ideal texture,” this was the most common marketing claim in New Zealand at 85% (Mackay et al. 2025). The widespread use of nutrition, health and marketing claims on products indicates a significant regulatory gap in the marketing of commercial foods for infants and toddlers.

The primary strength of this study is its comprehensive sample of commercial foods for infants and toddlers, gathered from four major Thai retailers and cross‐referenced with manufacturer websites. However, the study is limited by its dependence on on‐pack nutritional information without laboratory validation. This is a significant consideration, as nutrients in trace amounts can be legally reported as zero. Furthermore, due to its cross‐sectional design, our findings only reflect the market from May to August 2025 and do not capture later product changes. Additionally, this study focused on the leading retail chains and did not include smaller grocery stores, which generally stock a limited subset of high‐turnover brands already found in these major hypermarkets. Future research should therefore focus on analytically verifying the nutrient content of these products against their on‐pack claims.

Conclusion

This comprehensive cross‐sectional audit reveals a concerning landscape for early life nutrition in Thai market for commercial infant and toddler foods. Our findings demonstrate a market dominated by ultra‐processed products, with the snacks and finger foods category being the largest and most problematic due to high rates of added sugar and salt, coupled with a deficiency in essential micronutrients. Unregulated and pervasive on‐pack marketing worsens the poor nutritional quality of these products. The high frequency of on‐pack claims, especially on the least healthy products, creates a misleading health halo. This effect can confuse caregivers and undermine appropriate feeding practices. The results of this study necessitate immediate and strong policy measures designed to support caregivers and protect the long‐term health of Thai children.
