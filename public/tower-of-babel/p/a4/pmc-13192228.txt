Perceptions, health practices, and wildlife interactions among the Kaleung ethnic group in Sakon Nakhon, Thailand: implications for COVID-19 and zoonotic disease prevention
Authors: Kanokwan Suwannarong, Nisachon Bubpa, Thanomsin Ponlap, Kannika Thammasutti, Phitsanuruk Kanthawee, Paisit Boonyakawee, Worakamon Thongkan, Chutarat Saengkul, Chatchai Suesirisawad, Kangsadal Suwannarong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to determine perceptions, practices, health conditions, including COVID-19, and wildlife contact characteristics among the Kaleung ethnic group in Kut Bak district, Sakon Nakhon province, from June to July 2023.MethodsThis qualitative study carried out 4 in-depth interviews (IDIs), 5 key informant interviews (KIIs), and 3 focus group discussions (FGDs), with a total of 27 Kaleung and non-Kaleung (Thai) individuals, including males (16, 59.26%) and females (11, 40.74%).ResultsThe study revealed that the Kaleung communities maintained a deep connection with ancestral spirits and followed a traditional lifestyle intertwined with nature. Most of the Kaleung community members still relied on folk healers, hunted and consumed wild animals daily. They have continued to uphold their cultural practices, including forest hunting for food. Some Kaleung individuals understood the principle of COVID-19 prevention and employed herbal remedies to maintain their health condition. Despite their understanding of COVID-19, there was a notable lack of awareness regarding zoonotic diseases and the risks associated with consuming raw wild animals.Conclusions and recommendationsThe Kaleung community respects their elders and the guardian ancestor spirit, maintaining traditional practices despite modern influences, with shamans supporting community survival. Living near the forest, they use its resources sustainably, but hunting and consuming wild meat often involve poor hygiene and raw consumption, raising zoonotic disease risks. These findings could highlight the need for a One Health approach, integrating human, animal, and environmental health. Collaboration among health professionals, authorities, community leaders, and village health volunteers is essential to promote safe food practices, hygiene, and disease prevention. Incorporating One Health principles into training for villagers and local health personnel can strengthen surveillance and preventive strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-026-27224-4.Keywords: COVID-19, Kaleung ethnic group, Perception, Practices, Thailand, Wildlife

Introduction

The Kaleung ethnic group initially settled in the Mueng Kham Kerd and Mueng Khammouane subdistricts of the Lao People's Democratic Republic (Lao PDR) [25, 33]. During the late nineteenth century, they faced significant challenges due to the Haw Wars, which took place between the Haw people (also called Hor), Chinese-speaking groups from southern China, and various local forces in the region [33]. During this period, the Kaleung people faced hardships and dangers due to the instability caused by the wars [33]. As a result, many of them migrated to new areas, including Sakon Nakhon province (in Muang and Kut Bak Districts) and Nakhon Phanom, Mukdahan, and Nong Khai provinces in Thailand [25, 33]. This migration occurred during the Rattanakosin period, particularly under the reign of King Rama V, who ruled from 1868 to 1910, marking a significant historical movement and settlement phase for the Kaleung people [33]. The Kaleung ethnic group established their communities along the edges of Nong Han and the Phu Phan Mountain range [33]. They have a lifestyle closely connected to nature and live in highland areas along mountainsides, selecting places to live based on their livelihood needs, culture, and beliefs [33]. They also maintain a distinct spoken language, characterized by unique accents and phrases used within their group [33].

The recent pandemic has increased global attention on zoonotic diseases, which are illnesses transmitted from animals to humans [1, 11]. This focus has also brought attention to the consumption and trade of wildlife and practices that are often crucial for the livelihoods and cultural traditions of indigenous peoples and ethnic groups [1, 11]. Recent pandemics, such as COVID-19, have emphasized the importance of the One Health (OH) approach, which highlights the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health. The OH approach is particularly relevant in addressing emerging infectious diseases that often arise at the interfaces between these domains [10, 29]. The COVID-19 pandemic also posed a significant threat to global health, causing significant social, economic, and political disruptions [36]. By understanding the interconnectedness of ecosystems and the factors contributing to the emergence and spread of zoonotic diseases, the OH approach provides a framework for improving disease surveillance, response, and prevention strategies [31].

Additionally, proper nutrition is crucial for maintaining overall health and well-being [24]. Forests play a significant role in human nutrition by providing goods and services directly or indirectly, especially for indigenous communities [43]. These forests also support ancient cultures that value social, economic, environmental, and spiritual interdependence [26]. Cultural practices, including religion, dress, food, customs, and music, play a crucial role in connecting individuals and communities, fostering unity and shared identity, and emphasizing the importance of considering these practices in promoting health and nutrition [28].

Although the general public is aware of the COVID-19 pandemic that relates to health and economic effects, the pandemic's broader impact remains unknown [23, 44]. However, there were limited published studies on attitudes toward wildlife consumption during the COVID-19 and SARS pandemics that could indicate insufficient scientific attention to global conservation and safety attitudes [23, 44], especially among different ethnic groups. In addition, the contribution of wildlife to the global epidemiology of COVID-19 appeared minimal, even though wildlife may evolve into a potential reservoir for COVID-19 over time and could subsequently transmit the virus to humans [5].

Wildlife consumption also became a subject of interest during the COVID-19 pandemic because the virus is believed to have originated from animals, with early reports suggesting transmission to humans may have occurred through the trade and consumption of wildlife [4, 48]. This connection raised public health concerns about the risks associated with consuming wild animals and highlighted the need to better understand people's attitudes and behaviors regarding wildlife consumption in the context of emerging infectious diseases, such as COVID-19. Incorporating OH principles into public health initiatives can enhance the effectiveness of disease control measures by addressing the root causes of health issues and promoting sustainable practices that benefit human and animal populations and ecosystems [36]. Individual behaviors largely determine the risk of virus transmission and adherence to preventive measures. Recognizing the complex relationship between ethnicity and COVID-19 is critical for developing effective public health interventions and policies [39].

Consequently, our team intended to study various ethnic groups situated in different locations within Thailand. Each group possesses distinct cultural contexts and spiritual beliefs inherited from its ancestors. Accordingly, this study was carried out to determine perceptions, practices, health conditions, including COVID-19, and wildlife contact characteristics among the Kaleung ethnic group. The findings of this study are expected to be used by Thailand's Ministry of Public Health, local health offices, local administrations, and other stakeholders involved in One Health, facilitating the promotion of proper literacy in appropriate wildlife contact characteristics among this ethnic group. These would represent a significant aspect of the public health contribution and implications.

Methods

Study design and study period

This qualitative study was one of a series of ethnicity studies in Thailand focusing on wildlife contact characteristics [9, 40]. It was carried out from August to September 2023. In-depth interviews (IDIs), key informant interviews (KIIs), and focus group discussions (FGDs) were utilized to gather information in order to determine the perception and practices of the Kaleung ethnic group related to wildlife and the COVID-19 pandemic in Sakhon Nakhon province of Thailand.

Study locations

The study was carried out in Kut Had subdistrict, Kut Bak district, Sakon Nakhon province (Fig. 1). Kut Bak district is located in the northeastern region of Thailand and is bordered by the Phu Phan Mountain range [27]. It is known for its diverse indigenous ethnic groups, including Kaleung, and rich natural resources, such as forests, wildlife, and mountains, supporting local livelihoods [27]. The Kut Had subdistrict was selected due to its significant Kaleung population, which has a long history and unique socio-cultural dynamics, such as a rich cultural heritage, strong community cohesion, traditional knowledge and practices, spiritual beliefs, and intergenerational relationships [32, 35].

Map of Kut Bak district in Sakon Nakhon province

In addition, the Kut Had subdistrict had a total population of 6,106 [18]. The Kaleung people were initially located on the left bank of the Mekong River in the Lao PDR. They fled the war and were subsequently relocated to Thailand, specifically to Sakon Nakhon and Nakhon Phanom, Mukdahan, and Nong Khai provinces [32].

This study was carried out because this ethnic group lives in valleys surrounded by mountains. There are a few flat areas and limited agricultural land, so they rely on forest resources for a living. They use the mountainous areas to farm and raise cattle and buffalo. This lifestyle of hunting wild animals and gathering forest products from the mountains can be accomplished by walking, as the distance to the forest edge is approximately one kilometer. Other ethnic groups, such as Phu Thai, however, live closer to the city.

Study respondents

The study respondents were purposively selected, consisting of Kaleung individuals who had resided in the Kut Bak district for a minimum of 12 months prior to the data collection and non-Kaleung (Thai) individuals who were each a community leader, VHV, a health professional, and a Subdistrict Municipality Mayor. Non-Kaleung respondents were interviewed because of the roles and responsibilities that have impacted daily life and relevant policies regulating Kaleung practices. They were willing to participate in the study and possessed comprehensive information about the communities. Additionally, they were referred to for being interviewed due to their occupations or activities associated with wildlife. The selection was based on referrals from health and community authorities, as well as information from a gatekeeper indicating that the potential respondents have interacted with wildlife and possess extensive knowledge of Kaleung histories.

KIIs were conducted to gather data from the village head, an assistant of the village head, the subdistrict municipality mayor, a health professional from a health-promoting hospital, and VHVs. IDIs were employed to interview the Kaleung community members. The FGD respondents comprised the Kaleung people, representing diverse social groups, including a housewife, an agriculture merchant, an electrical appliance repairer, and farmers (Table 1). This is noted that the study sample size was determined based on data saturation [34]. Saturation was assessed alongside data collection and preliminary analysis. After each IDI, KII, and FGD, the research team conducted debriefing sessions to review key points, emerging codes, and themes. During fieldwork, the research team reviewed and coded transcripts step by step and checked whether new themes related to wildlife interactions, health practices, and COVID-19 perceptions appeared. In the final interviews and focus group discussions, no new themes appeared, and the data repeated earlier findings. After a team-based review of all coded data, the research team agreed that additional interviews would not provide new information, indicating that data saturation had been reached.

Study tools

The semi-structured discussion guides (DGs) for the KII, IDI, and FGD were adapted from a previous study [9, 40], which was a series of ethnicity studies in Thailand (supplement material no. 1). They covered a wide range of topics, including socio-demographic profiles, cultural and belief information, perceptions and practices toward wildlife interaction characteristics and COVID-19, health concerns, and accessibility to healthcare services. The modified DGs were pretested with a small number of individuals who were not the actual study respondents and were refined based on the pretested.

Data collection

The IDIs, KIIs, and FGDs were conducted in familiar, quiet, comfortable, and private locations that the respondents chose, such as community meeting locations and health centers. Our qualitative study team members also prioritized respondents' confidentiality and cultural sensitivity by ensuring respectful interactions with the respondents. Each session lasted between 30 and 90 min and was audio-recorded. Interviews and discussions were conducted in the local northeastern dialect to facilitate clear communication and nuanced responses. All interviews and discussions were audio-recorded.

Data analysis

The audio recordings were transcribed verbatim from the local dialect to the Thai central language and reviewed by the facilitators and note-takers for the precision of the information before further analysis. A thematic analysis approach was employed to identify, organize, and interpret patterns and themes within the data to ensure data completeness and reduce bias [13, 14]. The data were identified, coded, and grouped into major themes: socio-demographic respondent profiles, cultural practices, wildlife interactions, and health perceptions, particularly regarding zoonoses and COVID-19. These involved iterative coding and categorization and a deeper exploration of thematic relationships to derive meaningful insights [22].

For the triangulation process [12, 22], the research team included experts in qualitative research approach, cultural anthropology, and public health, specializing in Southeast Asian and One Health-related studies, to review and determine conclusions and recommendations of this study. They reviewed and helped refine the thematic categories and ensured a comprehensive understanding of the Kaleung community's experiences and practices before further analysis using NVivo qualitative analysis software.

It is worth noting that the preliminary results were analyzed in fields shared with the study respondents, community members, and relevant stakeholders at the study locations to validate the results and obtain their critical feedback to enhance the analysis's accuracy and cultural sensitivity. This rigorous process aimed to minimize bias and ensure that the analysis accurately represented the diverse perspectives and practices of the Kaleung community and adhered to established standards of qualitative trustworthiness, including credibility, transferability, dependability, and confirmability [22].

Results

Profiles of the study respondents

This study involved a total of 27 respondents, including 23 Kaleung and 4 non-Kaleung (Thai) respondents. Of these, 16 were males (59.26%), and 11 were females (40.74%). Three focus group discussions (FGDs) with 18 FGD respondents, 5 informant interviews (KIIs), and 4 in-depth interviews (IDIs) were carried out (Table 1 and 2). Their average age was 52.44 years. Eighteen of them (66.67%) were farmers, two were community leaders (7.41%), two were village health volunteers (VHVs) (7.41%), and one each was a housewife (3.70%), an electrical appliance repair worker (3.70%), an agriculture merchant (3.70%), a health professional (3.70%), and a Subdistrict Municipality Mayor (3.70%) (Table loyment worker2). We selected individuals who are not Kaleung participating in this study, as their roles and responsibilities might influence practices, laws, regulations, and policies related to daily activities, healthcare services, and wildlife interaction practices of Kaleung people in the communities. We did not include non-Kaleung general villagers in this study because we focused on perceptions of the Kaleung people.

Profiles of study respondents

The Kaleung's cultures and rituals

The Kaleung ethnic group's way of life is centered around the worship of the "Chao Pu Ta" spirits (the spirit of the grandfather), considered supreme spirits and an ancestor who settled in Paeng Muang village (Fig. 2). This spirit is believed to have protected the forest and the community for generations. An important annual tradition of the Kaleung ethnic group is the "Raising the Spirit of Grandfather" ceremony, held to bring prosperity, good health, happiness, and agricultural success. During the ceremony, Kaleung villagers believed this ceremony would keep their families free from illness and their livestock safe from diseases.

Guardian ancestors “Chao Pu Ta” spirit house in the community forest

"During the third month, raising the spirit of the grandfather is a crucial ceremony for Kaleung people; he (the spirit of the grandfather) safeguards both livestock and family members."

(A Subdistrict Municipality Mayor, SK_KII_M_001)

The "Raising the Spirit of Grandfather" ceremony includes therapeutic rituals for the sick and tying a thread on village seniors' arms. Following prayers led by the mediator, "Chao Cham," a communal meal was shared in the forest. Most respondents highlighted the deep-rooted cultural beliefs and practices of the Kaleung ethnic group, particularly about their health and well-being. These beliefs significantly influenced their healthcare decisions and daily activities because the ethnic group people in spirits, including ancestral spirits, house spirits, field spirits, and forest spirits. For instance, when an individual becomes sick, the community will seek the counsel of a shaman, who engages with spirits, to determine what is causing the illness. It may be due to illness or the influence of spirits. Treatment must be administered appropriately, as the patient may have "offended the spirits" by entering the forest, speaking disrespectfully, cutting down trees, or killing animals without permission. Improper behavior during these daily activities may result in illness. The belief in spirits has resulted in the establishment of rituals within the community.

The wildlife interaction characteristics

Wildlife as food

Most respondents reported that the Kaleung people maintained their ancestral lifestyle and relationship with wildlife. They relied on wild animals for sustenance and income, hunting in forests or purchasing from markets. This included a variety of animals, such as squirrels, wild boars, birds, and insects like beetles and cicadas. Additionally, they harvested amphibians such as tiny frogs, frogs, and bullfrogs (Fig. 3). Some respondents revealed a long tradition of wildlife consumption among the Kaleung people, which was a substantial part of their cultural identity and passed down through generations.

Red ant eggs and small frogs for sale at the market

"I've consumed wild animals since childhood, and our family has consumed them (wild animals) for generations. There aren't many other reasons; it's just our traditional dietary practice. "

(An electrical appliance repair worker, SK_IDI_M_002)

The Kaleung people had unique culinary customs for enjoying wild animals, which varied by season depending on availability. In winter, wildlife meats likely include birds, rats, red ant eggs, and snakes, which were prized for their rich flavors, while tiny frogs were appreciated for their tasty dry meat (Fig. 3), while wild boars and barking deer were trapped for consumption year-round. In addition, they focus on fruits and plants during the rainy season.

"When food is present, I eat everything without discrimination. I don't selectively choose. Usually, in winter, heavy rainfall brings an abundance of rats and other animals."

(A community leader, SK_KII_M_004)

"Birds and rats are not limited to special occasions; they are enjoyed anytime and taste delicious. Eating them during this period is important, as their flavor remains consistent year-round."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_F_004)

"People hunt animals like birds, rats, and snakes for food and to sell."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_F_004)

Several respondents indicated that wild animal hunting comprised groups of 3–5 individuals per trip in the forest, generally aged 40–50 years. Forming a group of 3 to 5 individuals is advantageous in mitigating losses during forest hunting. They would call contacts before entering the forest to obtain information in order to avoid forest officials. The way they got into the forest depended on each group's readiness; larger groups drove personal cars, while others walked up the mountains. Hunters brought provisions for a two- or three-night stay, along with firearms, knives, and trap snares for catching boars and deer. After capturing a boar, they immediately dissected it in the forest, dividing the meat evenly and wrapping it for transport. This was done to make the meat easier to transport and to prevent spoilage during the journey back. Smaller animals, like squirrels, were brought home intact for dissection. Hunters decided whether to share or sell the animals, often prioritizing sharing with neighbors before selling to trusted individuals to avoid scrutiny from the Royal Forest Department.

"When hunting as a group and catching a large animal like a wild boar, it's shared among members. The animal is dissected in the forest."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_F_004)

Some respondents reported that some hunters entered the forest alone and mainly hunted for food consumption and sale, depending on the number of animals caught. Some respondents mentioned that family members assisted in preparing the wildlife they caught for cooking. This involved cleaning, cooking, and singeing the skin. They used water to cleanse and soften the skin. They noted that even if the skin was charred by fire, washing it with water could restore its cleanliness and brightness.

"Water is employed to cleanse and soften the skin (of animals). Even when charred by fire, washing it with water renews its cleanliness and brightness."

(A farmer, SK_FGD_F_003)

Some respondents reported no injuries from dissecting with bare hands, believing that wild animals pose no risks. One respondent mentioned that they had never observed any sickness from this practice, attributing it to a belief that wild animals do not pose risks because wildlife primarily consumes chemical-free fruits.

"I've never encountered any injuries while dissecting with bare hands. We believe wild animals don't pose any risks since they mainly eat chemical-free fruits."

(A farmer, SK_FGD_F_003)

Wild animals like wild boar, muntjac (deer), and jungle fowl were once butchered or finely chopped and mixed with pig's blood, chili peppers, roasted rice, monosodium glutamate, lime juice, and shallots to create a raw dish known as "Koy Dib" (raw meat salad). This dish was consumed raw under the belief that these animals were clean, as they primarily fed on plants, fruits, roots, and cassava. They also mentioned that the food menu from pangolins did not have a strong fishy smell because they consumed ants and termites. Their meat was not odorous and could be used to cook delicious dishes like Kaeng Om (herbal, meat, and vegetable soup). This was in contrast to squirrels, flying squirrels, and bamboo rats, which had a strong, unpleasant odor and could not be eaten raw. The meat of these animals had to be cooked thoroughly in dishes that used herbs to mask the smell, such as meat with Kaeng Om, Hor Mok (steamed curry in banana leaves), and larb (a spicy minced meat salad).

"It's okay to eat them (wild boar meat) raw. These wild boars are clean. Wild boars and deer, …sometimes, they eat fruits, roots, and tubers."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_M_001)

"Long-tailed giant rats (Rattan rats) are cooked in ways similar to chipmunks and squirrels, commonly used in dishes like larb (spicy minced meat salad), curry, and grilled preparations. Among these, Long-tailed giant rat Kaeng Om (herbal, meat, and vegetable soup) is often considered the most delicious."

(An electrical appliance repair worker, SK_IDI_M_002)

"The most flavorful choices are barking deer spicy salad, wild boar, squirrel, and civet, though they're currently rare. Each delicacy has a unique taste and aroma specific to its type."

(A farmer, SK_FGD_003)

"Wild chickens and boars are used in preparing raw meat salad, adding sourness, aroma, roasted chili, and rice. Adding blood is vital for barking deer, making it a personal favorite."

(Farmer and agriculture merchant, SK_FGD_001)

Most respondents mentioned that wild animals, such as lizards and rodents, needed to be thoroughly cooked before consumption. The meat was grilled until entirely done, then finely chopped for dishes like meat salad. Bats were available at the local village market, where they could be made into soup. The meat was first grilled over a fire, then shredded and seasoned with spring onions and chili. Finally, it was roasted with vegetable paste and boiled before being served with steamed rice. Chameleons are grilled until cooked, minced, and combined with raw mangoes to make a lump dish.

"I'll cook the rodent in different styles: soft curry, grilled, and roasted, resulting in tender meat, particularly in the soft curry. Bats are only eaten by some people, not everyone, and can also be grilled."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_F_004)

Some individuals consume only wild animal meat, preferring its taste over farmed meat and appreciating its natural flavor without added enhancers. In addition, some reported that wild animal meat provided protein benefits and was considered chemical-free compared to market meat.

"A small protein intake is essential to prevent protein deficiency resulting from not consuming meat. "

(A farmer, SK_KII_M_005)

However, the respondents also revealed awareness of the potential health risks associated with consuming raw wild animal meat. They mentioned that blindness and fatalities occurred among people who ate raw wild boar meat in the communities.

Beliefs related to wildlife

The Kaleung people's beliefs and practices surrounding wildlife reflected a deep cultural connection with their environment, incorporating elements of spirituality, tradition, and community support. Evidence was related to using different parts of black pigs (native or local breed pigs) in ghost transformation rituals during wedding ceremonies. This ritual involved dissecting the pig and selecting specific parts like the head, feet, tail, nose tip, and ears, underscoring the ceremonial importance of these animals.

"Instead of directly using a wild animal, particularly a pig or black pig, for the ghost transformation ceremony, the practice entails dissecting and choosing specific parts like the head, four feet, tail, nose tip, and ears."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_M_001)

Additionally, the tradition of seeking permission from the spirit or owner (spirit of the house) when capturing wild boars emphasized a spiritual reverence for wildlife. This practice involved offering a portion of wild boar to the spirit, dividing and sharing the meat, including all parts, such as the skin, liver, kidneys, and belly. This ritual respected the community's spiritual beliefs and ensured equitable distribution among community members.

"In the past, when capturing wild boars, tradition required seeking permission from the owner (spirit of the house) or spirit by offering a portion. This involved dividing every part of the boar and sharing it accordingly, including the skin, liver, kidneys, and belly."

(A farmer, SK_FGD_002)

Interestingly, different breeds of pigs were used in the rituals. They occasionally used local breed pigs, black pigs, or wild boars, but on different occasions.

Most respondents indicated that if someone caught or hunted a wild animal, they would share the meat with siblings and nearby friends. The practice of sharing wild meat, such as squirrels, chipmunks, and insects like dung beetles and cockchafers, was rooted in communal kindness and support (Fig. 4). This tradition ensured that those who hunted or gathered wildlife distributed the meat among neighbors and relatives, reflecting principles of fairness and mutual aid. Pregnant women, for example, received double portions to ensure the rights and well-being of their unborn children, illustrating the community's emphasis on care and equity.

Chipmunk found for sale at the market

"Upon his (a hunter) searching (for wild animals), he often returns and shares findings with his siblings, also extending the sharing to friends, following the tradition of communal food sharing."

(An electrical appliance repair worker, SK_IDI_M_002)

Sharing the catch of wild animals was part of a mutual protection agreement within the community. This practice fostered a sense of solidarity and ensured that meat was shared during times of scarcity, with the expectation that others would reciprocate in the future. Initially, this sharing was publicly signaled and presented to a community leader like Chao Cham, but now it often happens discreetly to avoid legal repercussions from forest officials.

"It's customary to hunt various animals, then distribute them among close relatives. If siblings pass by during hunting, the catch must be shared among those present. Sharing increases the chances of receiving a share in the future."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_M_001)

However, some respondents mentioned no direct spiritual significance attached to wild animals among the Kaleung villagers; they consumed wild meat primarily for sustenance and to reduce expenses.

Even though it was expected to eat wild meat, some people doubted whether COVID-19 came from bats. Some respondents expressed that there was news indicating the virus originated from bats, but most respondents were uncertain about the validity of the news.

"I doubt that the pathogens (of COVID-19) came from bats since our ancestors traditionally consumed fruits, not bats. However, reports are indicating the virus originated from bats, but I'm uncertain about their validity."

(A housewife, SK_IDI_F_003)

Wildlife as traditional medicine

Most of the study respondents reported that the Kaleung people still followed the ancestral practice of eating wild animals for nutritious meals and believed it boosted their health; for example, they used geckos to treat asthma. Seniors believed geckos could aid in treating jaundice in children. The gecko was prepared by burning, skinning, decapitating, cleaning, grilling, chopping, and cooking with chili and roasted rice for dishes like "Koi" (raw meat salad) or "larb" (spicy minced meat salad).

They also reported that traditional remedies were used to treat various injuries and ailments. For instance, in the case of a cobra bite, cobra blood was mixed with 40-degree white liquor to create a healing concoction. Similarly, fresh duck blood mixed with white liquor was used to treat injuries resulting from falls from trees, car accidents, or any other incidents that caused bruising. As described by a farmer, this practice reflected the local reliance on traditional and natural remedies for managing health issues.

"For a cobra bite, he (a community member) used cobra blood mixed with white liquor. Fresh duck blood mixed with white liquor is used for falls from trees, car accidents, or any bruising accidents."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_M_001)

Wildlife conservation

They reported that the Kut Had village formed a forest conservation group to ban hunting in community forest areas in 2003. This was due to the villagers' desire to conserve the wild animals in their community. Hunting has been a traditional practice since the ancestors' era of our ancestors, making it challenging to separate people from the forest. The village committee limited wildlife hunting, imposing a 2,000 THB (approximately 60 USD) fine on violators. Compliance increased after this policy was implemented, with no further hunting incidents. If hunting or firearm use was detected in the park, Forestry Department officials apprehended and transferred offenders to the police for legal action, where fines were imposed according to regulations, and offenders faced charges in court.

They also mentioned that the community forest's regulations prohibit hunting or capturing wild animals as part of its commitment to forest conservation. However, they find it difficult to separate themselves from the forest because they have lived and relied on forest products. These perspectives originate from the traditional forest-dwelling lifestyle passed down from parents and ancestors.

"As a community committed to forest conservation, regulations within this community forest prohibit hunting or capturing wild animals……However, it's difficult to separate people from the forest and prevent them from disrupting it."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_M_001)

Health status, perceptions, and practices toward COVID-19

Health problems

The Kaleung people used to have better physical health because of their access to clean water. The consumption of wild meat, as well as the use of chemicals in meat production, has been linked to a variety of illnesses, including diarrhea, gout, and high blood pressure.

"The increase in chemical use in meat production and wild meat consumption has resulted in more cases of diarrhea, gout, and high blood pressure among the Kaleung people."

(A health professional, SK_KII_M_003)

Liver fluke disease was common in the village in the past because the villagers' knowledge about parasitic diseases was limited. They often ate fish with egg parasites without properly cooking them. The respondents reported that in the northern region of Thailand, diseases from consuming raw wild animals could cause severe outcomes like sudden death, deafness, or blindness.

"Once, when he had liver fluke disease, he didn't know enough about parasites. Consequently, he ate leafy and stem vegetables and raw fish without scalding them properly, unknowingly ingesting egg worms."

(A Subdistrict Municipality Mayor, SK_KII_M_001)

Chronic diseases, such as liver disease and diabetes, were prevalent among Kaleung people, along with contagious illnesses such as hepatitis and COVID-19. Medical expenses vary based on travel distance, and local assistance is often sought for transportation. Some respondents reported that villagers supported each other by pooling resources for petrol to travel to health centers.

Perceptions and practices toward health conditions and COVID-19

Most of the Kaleung people still used traditional healing methods passed down from their ancestors as their primary choice, and used modern healthcare as an alternative option. The decision to visit doctors or traditional healers to maintain health depended on each person's faith and beliefs. In cases where individuals remained unwell despite hospital treatment, especially if the cause was unknown, traditional beliefs, particularly among the older generation, suggest arranging a ceremony with a fortune teller or "Shaman." Three to four seniors, led by Chao Cham, typically oversee the ritual. Acting as intermediaries between the villagers and the spirit of ancestor "Chao Pu Ta," they recited magical incantations over holy water, seeking Chao Pu Ta's aid in protecting and healing the afflicted. This included addressing ailments such as children's nighttime cries or discomfort caused by fish bones stuck in the throat.

"When sick, I bring flowers, incense, and candles to Don Pu Ta (the sacred area of ​​the village), seeking guidance. Then, I call upon my ancestors to aid the sick children and bring healing."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_M_001)

However, some respondents mentioned a significant generational shift in attitudes towards traditional practices, driven by increased access to modern healthcare and changing risk perceptions. Unlike their parents, who adhered to these practices due to a lack of alternatives, the respondents chose to seek medical assistance, leading to a decreased value placed on traditional methods. This shift indicated a broader trend of modernization and evolving cultural values within the community.

Mor Yao ceremonies, also known as the "Dance of the Worship to the Ghost", are performed as a last resort if illness persists despite hospital treatment. These ceremonies involve the patient providing raw chicken eggs, incense sticks, and covering the treatment costs. During the ritual, the healer and their team perform a dance and communicate with spirits to identify the root cause of the illness and seek guidance for healing. This practice is seen as a way to boost the patient's morale and provide emotional support.

"If hospital visits don't help, some turn to herbal remedies and cultural beliefs; we will seek guidance from ancestral spirits and traditional healers."

(Farmer and agriculture merchant, SK_FGD_001)

Modham ceremonies involve mystical chants, divination, and exorcisms to banish malevolent spirits or ward off bad luck. These ceremonies include specific actions like sitting in designated areas or breaking sticks to dispel negativity. Although these practices are less common today, they are still considered by some, especially when facing serious illnesses.

"Modham, in addition to predicting future events, also offers guidance on avoiding misfortune and inviting good fortune. While it aims to raise awareness, not all its beliefs are necessarily accurate."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_F_004)

Mor Pao, or folk healers, use techniques involving downward dispelling from the head to treat animal bites and broken limbs, and offer massages. They also reported conducting ceremonies to invoke blessings from entities like the Naga. Although their availability is currently restricted, they focus on treating bone-related ailments and aiding healing, especially for surgical wounds.

"Doctors must be meticulous in treating falls leading to broken bones, ensuring wounds are properly set for effective healing post-surgery. Their careful attention to wounds aims to facilitate recovery while providing essential encouragement to uplift spirits, a crucial aspect in the healing journey."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_F_004)

Healthcare expenses for services from a Modham, Mor Yao, or Chao Cham typically cost 6 to 12 THB (approximately 0.18 to 0.36 USD), covering services and offerings. Any additional amount, usually 100 to 200 THB (approximately 3 to 6 USD), was a gesture of generosity. Traditional healers were primarily sought for their services and helped care for bedridden patients.

The herbal medicine network "Inpang" distributed aloe vera oil, advocating its use for treating flu. This group collected traditional Thai herbal medicine to encourage self-care, relying on community wisdom and utilizing local herbs. When conventional medicine failed, divination sessions were sought. Upon improvement, a ritual was conducted to bind the patient's arms, providing emotional support alongside medical treatment.

Some respondents indicated that COVID-19 was believed to have originated from a laboratory leak in Wuhan, China. The idea of the virus jumping from animals to humans was considered unlikely due to its perceived man-made. There were also suspicions regarding its potential connection to the development of biological weapons.

"I don't think the pathogens (COVID-19) causing infection come from animals anymore. I've dismissed that idea because I'm convinced it's not natural. Instead, I believe it leaked from a lab."

(A farmer, SK_FGD_003)

Healthcare in Kaleung communities was managed by government health staff, with chronic disease appointments arranged through community coordination centers and village health volunteers (VHVs). Persistent symptoms required referral to district hospitals for further treatment.

Communication channels during COVID-19

Most respondents revealed that most individuals obtain information about COVID-19 from various sources, such as television (TV), radio, and online platforms such as Facebook, YouTube, and LINE (communication platforms). Village announcements by leaders and health volunteers, along with official guidelines from the Ministry of Public Health (MOPH), also played a vital role in spreading awareness. Government public relations materials and NGO training sessions further contribute to knowledge dissemination. Many trusted government-provided information sources are available for direct community communication.

However, some respondents were careful about trusting the information they received, believing that only about half of it was reliable. They would consult community leaders or VHVs since they are considered trustworthy resources. This was because the community leaders and VHVs obtained information from government sources.

"I generally trust about half of what I hear, recognizing that sometimes it may not be entirely dependable. Both community leaders and VHVs are equally reliable to me since they receive similar information. "

(An electrical appliance repair worker, SK_IDI_M_002)

Perceptions and practices, including surveillance systems during COVID-19

Most respondents compared COVID-19 symptoms to those of a common cold, including runny nose, breathing difficulties, chest tightness, diarrhea, loss of taste and smell, and body aches. Herbal remedies like Andrographis paniculata, lemon, ginger, galangal, lemongrass, and kaffir lime leaves were commonly used for the treatment. Kaleung community members reported sharing herbs within and outside their community, reducing pandemic-related worries about COVID-19.

In addition, most respondents used rapid antigen tests (ATK) for detection and self-quarantined if positive, notifying VHVs who coordinated further treatment with hospitals. In some cases, hospitals prescribe Favipiravir to manage illness.

Some respondents reported that some individuals hesitated about vaccination and preferred alternative methods like herbal remedies and avoiding crowded places. Others, especially those who traveled frequently or attended gatherings, chose to get 2–3 vaccine doses.

"I have doubts about it. I heard about someone who refused vaccination and died, as reported in the news. To stay safe, I kept my distance from others and drank boiled medicine with hot water."

(A farmer, SK_FGD_002)

There was noticeable hesitancy regarding vaccination, necessitating ongoing educational efforts to dispel misconceptions, especially among seniors.

"Villagers often brand those who reject vaccination as stubborn or set in their ways. This resistance, particularly among older individuals, raises concerns. It instills fear in some villagers, leading them to be extra cautious."

(A Subdistrict Municipality Mayor, SK_KII_M_001)

Some also reported that surveillance and prevention measures included creating municipal waiting centers, identifying some patients, and setting up temperature screening points managed by VHVs. During gatherings like funerals, official documents emphasized mask-wearing, social distancing, and avoiding large crowds.

"Village leaders strive to spread early morning announcements, yet enforcing restrictions poses challenges. Despite rules against drinking during the epidemic, some in the community persist with their usual drinking habits."

(A farmer, SK_IDI_M_001)

Conclusions and discussion

The study revealed that the Kaleung ethnic community upheld values of respect, compliance, and modesty towards their seniors, continuing to revere the guardian ancestor spirit, "Chao Pu Ta," as a sacred force protecting their village and surrounding forests. Their lifestyle revolved around living in harmony with nature, using the forests for sustenance, raising livestock, and relying on natural resources for medicinal purposes. Cultural traditions and ceremonies, such as the "Raising the Spirit of Grandfather" ceremony and healthcare rituals like Mor Yao and Modham, are deeply intertwined with healthcare practices and significantly influence daily activities. Despite modern influences, the Kaleung people demonstrated a strong commitment to their traditional practices. This was evident in the extensive knowledge held by seniors, encompassing diverse rituals and belief systems. In addition, the Kaleung people have consistently placed their trust in local shamans for healthcare, further highlighting their adherence to cultural practices, which aligns with findings from other studies on indigenous health practices. The preservation of the traditions of Kaleung in these study locations aligned with the results of a study by Thipchai et al. that explored the fusion of Thai Lao and Kaleung cultures and emphasized the persistence of Kaleung practices despite external influences [8].

Additionally, local shamans have played a crucial role in maintaining the well-being of the Kaleung people, a tradition passed down through generations. Mor Yao practices entail addressing illnesses that are believed to stem from ancestral spirits or manifest as unusual symptoms such as depression or delusions. This method has remained an enduring means of preserving the health of the Kaleung people, consistent with the research findings of Alongkorn Itthiphon [16] on the concept of "Hiaak Kwan" in Mor Yao healing rituals, as the Kaleung people's use of shamans involved addressing health issues believed to be caused by ancestral spirits or manifesting as psychological symptoms like depression or delusions [16]. According to previous studies in Thailand, Mor Yao performed rituals to summon spirits and heal patients by fostering morale and offering encouragement, thereby empowering patients to confront their illnesses with newfound vigor and resilience [15, 16].

The Kaleung people have lived close to the forest for a long time, using its resources sustainably and conserving them. They have gathered forest products and hunted mainly for subsistence. However, their consumption practices regarding wild animal meat are reported to lack hygiene and food safety measures, and they have little awareness during the butchering process of animals like barking deer, wild boar, and wild chicken. Such meat was often consumed raw, posing potential health risks from zoonotic diseases.

Moreover, this current study found that the Kaleung people have continued their traditional hunting and consuming wild animals despite declining wildlife populations. This ongoing connection between humans and animals among the Kaleung is similar to the findings of the study of the Mani ethnic group in southern Thailand [9]. Despite modern influences and rapid societal changes, the Kaleung people's traditional beliefs about eating wild animals remained strong.

We also found that the Kaleung ethnic group's response to COVID-19 involved intense community surveillance that was set by the local health-promoting hospital and local administration authorities, following health guidelines, and getting information from various sources, including social media platforms. This approach was similar to those seen in Thailand and other countries like Singapore and Vietnam [30, 37, 45]. They acted quickly by implementing widespread testing and strict quarantine measures, which helped control the spread of the virus [37, 42, 45]. On the other hand, countries like Indonesia and the Philippines faced challenges due to weaker healthcare systems and less coordinated efforts, showing how crucial robust health systems and community engagement are [37, 45]. The Kaleung community's behaviors, such as emphasizing collective action and following guidance from health professionals, align with effective strategies reported in other regions of Thailand, Singapore, and Vietnam, emphasizing the essential role of community participation in managing public health challenges [37, 42, 45].

This study also revealed that some Kaleung believed that consuming bats did not lead to COVID-19 or other infectious diseases, as the animals resided in forests and consumed fruit, thereby reducing the risk of chemical contamination. This perception is similar to findings by Suwannarong et al. [41], where some rural communities in Thailand also believed that consuming bats posed minimal health risks due to the natural diet and habitat of bats [41]. However, the studies by Li et al. [20] and Leroy et al. [19] identified bats as natural reservoirs of SARS-like coronaviruses and Ebola virus, respectively [19, 20].

In addition, previous studies suggested culturally specific public health education to bridge the gap between local beliefs and scientific evidence to reduce disease transmission risks [19, 20, 41].

The Thai government promoted self-reliance through herbal medicine during the COVID-19 pandemic. Similarly, the Kaleung community shared herbs both within and outside of their community, reducing COVID-19 pandemic-related concerns. This promotes mutual assistance and self-sufficiency, aligning with studies on ethnic groups like the Urak Lawoi, who combine modern and traditional medical care, and it was recommended that traditional herbs can be used alone or as complementary or alternative medicines to combat COVID-19 infections [3, 6, 7, 40]. Similarly, herbal medicines were widely used to treat COVID-19, especially in China and other Asian countries, such as Japan, Korea, Vietnam, India, and Indonesia [21]. In contrast, the Bajau ethnic group in Malaysia showed a marked reliance on modern medicine rather than traditional remedies during the COVID-19 pandemic; the Bajau turned to government healthcare services due to a perception of greater efficacy in combating COVID-19 [2, 17, 46].

In conclusion, the Kaleung ethnic group's traditional practices, especially in healthcare and wildlife interactions, showed the strong cultural beliefs that influenced their way of life. Their response to the COVID-19 pandemic, marked by a blend of traditional and modern practices, highlights their resilience and adaptability. Wildlife could become a potential reservoir for COVID-19 over time and may further transmit the virus to humans [5]. Although this study is primarily descriptive, it is guided by a public health and One Health perspective, emphasizing how culturally embedded practices and human–wildlife interactions shape health behaviors and zoonotic disease risks in specific community contexts [47].

Through a detailed qualitative approach, this study provided valuable baseline insights into the Kaleung community's cultural and health practices related to wildlife contact and healthcare related to COVID-19. This study provided in-depth qualitative insights into the Kaleung community’s cultural practices, health behaviors, and wildlife interactions. Despite the small sample size, data saturation was achieved, with no new themes emerging in the later interviews [34].

Moreover, the findings of the current study may assist local health offices and administrations in designing communication interventions for the ethnic community to enhance their health literacy regarding interactions with wild animals by integrating traditional beliefs and practices into feasible approaches.

Further studies should be planned and implemented to understand the long-term impacts of integrating traditional and modern healthcare practices on the health outcomes of ethnic groups like the Kaleung by focusing on implementing special educational measures on various ethnic groups or cultural practices at the consumer level, which entail unique risks that might be attributed to emerging diseases [38].

Recommendations

To effectively prevent zoonotic disease transmissions and safeguard public health within the Kaleung ethnic group in the Kut Bak district, Sakon Nakhon province, health professionals, government agencies, community leaders, and VHVs need to collaborate in raising awareness on understanding zoonotic diseases, promoting good hygiene, safe livestock and pet handling, and minimizing wildlife interactions. This can be achieved through targeted health campaigns, training sessions, monitoring wildlife interactions, and educating them on safe food consumption and zoonotic disease prevention. Effective collaboration among health professionals, government agencies, community leaders, village health volunteers, and other relevant stakeholders is critical for promoting safe food consumption, disease prevention, and hygienic practices within the Kaleung ethnic group, thereby contributing to broader public health implications.

Furthermore, it is recommended to incorporate One Health principles into the training provided to all villagers, including local health personnel and Village Health Volunteers, to improve disease surveillance and prevention strategies for the Kaleung ethnic group. These individuals are regarded as frontline workers due to their close relationship with community members and their familiarity with local contexts. By understanding the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, these efforts can contribute to more comprehensive and sustainable public health interventions and risk communication implications.
