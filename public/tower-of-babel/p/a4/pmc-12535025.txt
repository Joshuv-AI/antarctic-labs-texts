Rare advanced complication of an infusion port with pocket skin necrosis successfully preserve the port : a case report
Authors: Min Li, Lanyan He, Qinqin Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe totally implantable venous access port (TIVAP) is widely recommended for patients requiring chemotherapy, total parenteral nutrition, or long-term antibiotic therapy. Nevertheless, pocket skin necrosis, albeit infrequent, represents a potential complication that may progress to more severe adverse outcomes.Case presentationA 55-year-old female patient with a history of breast cancer developed localized skin thinning at the infusion port site six months after completing adjuvant chemotherapy. Over the subsequent seven months, conservative management was implemented; however, the patient later presented with skin necrosis and exposure at the site, accompanied by localized pain. To address this, a new pocket was created adjacent to the original site, and the puncture hub was relocated within the new pocket. Following this intervention, the patient successfully completed her treatment course.ConclusionProactive preparation of the local pocket skin may help mitigate the risk of pocket skin necrosis. In cases where necrosis occurs, creating an adjacent new pocket for hub relocation constitutes an effective approach for management and preservation of treatment continuity.Keywords: Infusion port, Complications, Pocket skin necrosis, Case report

Introduction

The totally implantable venous infusion port (TIVAP) is composed of a puncture reservoir connected to a catheter, with the entire assembly being completely implanted beneath the skin. It has been extensively adopted owing to its relatively low complication rate when compared to other frequently utilized infusion catheters [1]. Complications arising during catheter placement or within 30 days post-insertion are classified as early complications, encompassing postoperative hemorrhage, pocket hematoma, incision dehiscence, and port body inversion. In contrast, late complications predominantly include infection, thrombosis, and skin necrosis [2]. Notably, skin necrosis represents a rare late complication with an incidence ranging from 0.04% to 0.15%. It is characterized by localized cutaneous discoloration (typically blackening), pain, and functional impairment, often stemming from inadequate local tissue perfusion or thin skin flaps. Such pathological conditions may result from factors including progressive subcutaneous tissue atrophy, premature port utilization during administration of VEGF inhibitors, local cutaneous radiotherapy, and other therapeutic interventions [3]. Despite its low occurrence rate, skin necrosis can lead to port exposure and catheter-associated infections, thereby significantly compromising the continuity of patient treatment. Currently, the mainstay management strategies for skin necrosis involve debridement combined with skin grafting or device extraction [4, 5]. However, successful preservation of the TIVAP in the setting of skin necrosis remains uncommon. Accordingly, we present a case of successfully managing TIVAP-associated skin necrosis.

Case presentation

In March 2022, a 55-year-old female breast cancer patient with no history of allergies was admitted to our department. In the same month, a totally implantable venous access port (TIVAP) was inserted via the internal jugular vein, with chest radiography confirming the catheter tip position at the level of the 9th thoracic vertebra (T9) (Fig. 1). The patient initiated the AC-THP chemotherapy regimen on the day following port placement and successfully completed six cycles of treatment. In August 2022, the patient reported localized skin thinning and pruritus at the port pocket site, accompanied by significant anxiety. Upon further questioning, she disclosed a history of frequent chest expansion exercises and wearing tight undergarments. Intervention measures included advising discontinuation of these activities, topical application of epidermal growth factor, local coverage with sterile gauze, and emotional support to alleviate her distress. During her seventh cycle of chemotherapy, the patient noted progressive skin thinning at the site, though she denied resuming the aforementioned activities. Given the absence of port exposure, a multidisciplinary team—comprising specialist nurses, departmental directors, and oncology nursing staff—was convened to assess potential etiologies, including port-related infection and port body rupture with consequent drug extravasation. Following evaluation, a palliative management strategy was implemented. By September 2022, the patient presented with overt exposure of the infusion port, accompanied by localized pain (Fig. 2), and exhibited marked fear. Mindfulness-based interventions were introduced to address her anxiety. The patient declined port removal or transition to peripheral venous access. Considering the poor prognosis associated with Her-2 positivity in this patient [6], the clinical team deemed port retention necessary for subsequent treatment. Additionally, the exposed area was small, with no evidence of infection or drug extravasation. A catheter specialist therefore created a new pocket adjacent to the original site, oriented at an obtuse angle relative to the longitudinal axis of the body (rather than perpendicular to the original pocket) to preserve catheter integrity. Intraoperatively, the port body was inspected and found to be intact. The puncture hub was then relocated to the new pocket, with reinforcement of the pocket aperture. The existing non-coring needle was retained, and flushing and locking procedures were performed successfully. The incision of the original pocket was also reinforced. Postoperatively, topical epidermal growth factor was administered. Dressing changes (sterile gauze and dressing) were performed at 24 h, revealing satisfactory wound healing without signs of infection. Chest radiography confirmed upward migration of the catheter tip by two vertebral bodies, aligning with the optimal position (Fig. 3). Subsequently, the patient completed all planned treatments without recurrence of skin thinning or exposure at the port site over the following two years. The port was explanted in October 2024.

The TIVAP serves as a critical infusion conduit for oncology patients. Due to its fully subcutaneous implantation, it is associated with a lower incidence of infection-related complications compared to other venous access devices [7, 8]. Nevertheless, skin necrosis—albeit rare—may occur, typically presenting with localized cutaneous pain and functional impairment [3].

Discussion

Numerous studies have implicated skin necrosis in association with the administration of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) inhibitors such as bevacizumab, alongside factors including impaired local cutaneous barrier function, thin tissue flaps [4], inadequate subcutaneous tissue volume, and suboptimal selection of port type [5]. In the present case, the port was implanted on the contralateral (non-affected) side with intact local skin prior to placement. Additionally, while some research indicates that extravasation of chemotherapeutic agents can induce necrosis, this typically presents as immediate localized severe pain, edema, and erythema [9], which were not observed in this patient.

The patient’s body mass index (BMI) was 25.5 kg/m² at the time of port insertion and 24.9 kg/m² at the onset of port exposure, reflecting a minimal change. A appropriately sized port was selected, and sufficient subcutaneous tissue was present at the time of implantation, with the port positioned 3–5 mm beneath the thoracic fascia—consistent with adequate depth. Despite these precautions, long-term port placement was complicated by progressive cutaneous changes culminating in necrosis. The patient initially reported localized dryness and pruritus, which may indicate early impairment of the skin barrier [4]. Given the patient’s age, age-related decline in skin barrier integrity [10] may have contributed. Furthermore, the patient infrequently wore a brassiere during chemotherapy, and her breast tissue was relatively full. Had the pocket been oriented perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the body, movement-related tension could have further compromised skin integrity over time, potentially precipitating necrosis.

Although our interventions did not reverse established skin necrosis, vigilant monitoring enabled early detection of cutaneous deterioration. In the absence of catheter-related infection, timely intervention—including preservation of the original port during the early stages of necrosis, suturing of the original pocket, and topical application of epidermal growth factor—successfully protected the affected area. To prevent dehiscence of the new pocket, proactive skin care education was provided at the time of implantation, including recommendations for moisturizer use to maintain stratum corneum integrity. Recognizing that skin regenerative capacity is inherently limited by age and chemotherapy exposure, our strategy focused on enhancing protective factors, with an emphasis on prevention [11]. Patients were advised to avoid excessive movement of the affected shoulder. Following wound healing, daily moisturizer application [12] was recommended to reduce friction between shoulder straps and the obliquely positioned pocket, thereby minimizing further damage to the local epidermis.

Notably, the catheter specialist’s decision to angle the new pocket (rather than align it perpendicularly) reduced local tension, enabling sustained port functionality.

Throughout the clinical course, the patient experienced anxiety, cutaneous deterioration, and necrosis—factors that could have impaired treatment continuity and adherence. However, comprehensive psychological support facilitated active patient engagement with the management plan, including adherence to shoulder movement restrictions, topical moisturizer use to prevent compression-related injury, and pocket repositioning to minimize secondary damage.

Conclusion

We present a case of localized skin necrosis associated with a TIVAP, wherein successful port preservation was achieved through multidisciplinary collaboration and timely psychological intervention, thereby alleviating the patient’s economic burden. This case introduces an alternative management strategy for pocket necrosis, complementing existing approaches such as port replacement or flap transposition. Key unresolved questions include the role of supportive undergarments, the lack of clinical guidelines for such scenarios, and the potential utility of transverse incisions to optimize pocket positioning in patients with fuller breast tissue [13]. Ultimately, this case underscores the critical importance of multidisciplinary care in TIVAP management.​.

Further clinical investigations are warranted to evaluate the impact of pocket incision orientation and location in patients with plump breast tissue.
