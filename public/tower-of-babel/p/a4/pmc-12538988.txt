The role of surgical helmet systems in reducing infection rate: a systematic review
Authors: Tan Qing Hang, Amirzeb Aurangzeb, Omar Mohamed Hussein El Dishish, Charles Kon Kam King, Moo Ing How
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a severe complication of arthroplasty. Surgical helmet system (SHS) was introduced to reduce airborne contamination however their efficacy remains controversial. This systematic review evaluates on the role of SHS in reduction PJI, explores contamination mechanisms and the impact of design and operational factors.MethodsA systematic review was performed according to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines across PubMed, Medline and Cochrane Library databases from 1950 to 2024. Studies were included if they investigated SHS in arthroplasty and reported on infection rates, contamination or SHS performance. Data extraction focused on study design, SHS type, infection outcomes and contamination mechanisms and whether paper is supporting SHS in reducing PJI. Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP) quality tool was used to assess methodological quality of each included papers.ResultsTwenty studies met inclusion criteria. Five large registry studies evaluated SHS effectiveness in reducing PJI, with conflicting outcomes: one showed significantly lower infection rates with SHS, while others found no difference or a potential increase in risk. Ten studies assessed contamination pathways, with several reporting higher microbial emission and wound contamination associated with SHS use. Design and operational factors significantly influenced contamination, with delayed fan activation and use of sealed or zippered gowns associated with reduced contamination levels.ConclusionEvidence on SHS effectiveness in reducing PJI remains inconclusive. While modern systems may offer benefits, improper use can increase contamination. Optimized protocols including delayed fan activation, appropriate gowning techniques, and regular helmet decontamination are essential. SHS should complement, not replace, established infection prevention strategies. High-quality prospective studies are needed to define best practices for SHS implementation.Keywords: Surgical helmet, Prosthetic joint infection, Arthroplasty, Joint replacement

Background

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) is a devastating complication of arthroplasty surgery, complicating 1–2% of hip and knee arthroplasties, with intraoperative contamination rates ranging from 12 to 63% [1–3]. Treatment of PJI poses a significant economic burden, projected to exceed $1.85 billion by 2030 with increased morbidity and mortality in elderly patients [4, 5]. Contamination sources range from sterility and implant handling techniques, operating theatre environment to surgical attire. To mitigate against the risks of PJI, a multitude of strategies are adopted, ranging from laminar airflow, prophylactic antibiotics, type of dressing choice to the use of specialized surgical attire [6–9]. The modernization of the original body exhaust system introduced by John Charnley for hip arthroplasty surgery has resulted in the evolution of a modern positive pressure surgical helmet system (SHS) with the primary goal of mitigating airborne contamination [10]. A large study involving 16,000 arthroplasties reported that 17% of the surgeries utilize SHS [11] to reduce infection rate. Bacteria shedding from the surgeon onto the surgical site is a major source of contamination [12]. Thus, with the design of an enclosed hoods system, wound contamination rate could be potentially reduced. The Charnley system was originally designed as a negative pressure system created by inflow and outflow tubes that draws the air away from the surgical site [13]. The Charnley body exhaust suit has been shown to be effective in reducing PJI while the overall effectiveness of positive pressure SHS in reducing PJI in arthroplasty surgery remains disputed [8]. The cumbersome nature of inflow-outflow tubes of the suit prompted the development of a more convenient and self-contained modern positive pressure SHS. Typically, the modern positive pressure SHS has a fan component to blow fresh air into the hood creating positive pressure that prevents particles and fluids from entering, thus acting as a barrier to shield the surgeon from potential contamination from the surgical field. This systematic review aims to evaluate the effectiveness of SHS in reducing PJI, explore contamination mechanisms, and examine design and operational factors affecting SHS performance.

Methods

Literature search

This systematic review was conducted following guidelines outlined in Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA). A comprehensive primary search was conducted across three databases from PubMed, Medline and Cochrane Library (1950 to 2024), using the keywords: “Spacesuit" OR "Space suit" OR "Surgical Helmet" OR "Personal Protective Equipment" OR "Head Protective Devices" AND "Arthroplasty. Initial screening yielded 280 citations, with 17 studies included after meeting inclusion criteria, removal of duplicates and full-text review. Additional 3 studies were included in our study during citation searching.

Study selection

The studies were assessed by two independent reviewers, who screened the abstracts/titles according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Any difference in opinion between the reviewers was resolved by consensus discussion. Studies were included if they investigated the use of SHS in arthroplasty surgery, reported on infection rates, contamination mechanisms or evaluated design and operational factors for SHS. Studies were also only included if they were published in the English language. Studies were excluded from the study if they focused on non-orthopaedic surgeries, lack quantitative data, full text unavailability or if the study has an unclear methodology.

Data extraction

Data was analyzed and extracted from the full text of each study, including the study design, type of SHS, sample size, study methodology and key findings. The extracted data was then categorized into 3 different main themes: effectiveness of the SHS in reducing PJI, contamination mechanisms and evaluation of design and operational factors. As the role of SHS is to reduce contamination and prosthetic joint infection rate, each study was assessed as to whether the conclusion is in favour of the helmet system. The heterogeneity of the collected data precluded a meta-analysis.

Results

A total of 280 citations were retrieved from the initial database search (PubMed, Medline (Ovid) and Cochrane Library). After removal of 10 duplicates, 270 records underwent title and abstract screening. Of these, 250 studies were excluded for not meeting predefined inclusion criteria. 2 records were excluded due to unavailable full text. 1 study was then excluded as it mainly focused on Charnley Exhaust-type bodysuits. Additional 3 studies from citation search were assessed for eligibility and included in the review. A total of 20 studies were included in our systematic review as shown in Fig. 1. Quality appraisal of the studies was conducted using the Effective Public Health Practice Project (EPHPP) as seen in Appendix 1. EPHPP is a quality assessment tool for quantitative studies, developed by McMaster University, Ontario, Canada and hence was used to assess the methodological quality and risk of bias. Each selected study was rated individually for selection bias, study design, confounders, blinding, data collection and withdrawals. All the ratings were combined and concluded into 3 individual scores: weak, moderate and strong.

Effectiveness of SHS in reducing PJI

Table 2 below shows 5 studies that compare the PJI rate between SHS and conventional surgical gowning (SG). Rahardja et al. [14] conducted an observational study involving 19,322 primary total knee arthroplasties (TKRs) and reported lower PJI rates in surgeries performed with SHS in multivariate analysis, with significant reductions at both 90 days (adjusted odds ratio [OR] = 0.50, p = 0.008) and 6 months (adjusted OR = 0.55, p = 0.022) postoperatively. Contrastingly, Tayton et al. [15] analyzed 64,566 TKRs and found significantly higher risk of infection with SHS use at 6 months in multivariate analysis (OR = 1.53, p = 0.052). However, there was no association between helmet system and revision for infection at 12 months. In univariate analysis, SHS is a risk factor for PJI at 6 months (OR = 2.07, p = <0.001) but not at 12 months (OR = 1.29, p = 0.103). So et al. [16] studied 260 total ankle arthroplasties and found no significant difference in infection rates between SHS and conventional gowning (p = 0.45). 2 large studies by Namba et al. in 2012 and 2013 studied the relationship between deep surgical site infection and SHS use in total hip replacement (THRs) and TKR surgeries respectively. Namba et al. [17] reported SHS used does not increase risk of deep surgical site infection rate in THR in univariate analysis (Hazard ratio = 1.55, p = 0.046) while Namba et al. [18] reported no association in lowering risk of infection after TKR in univariate and multivariate analysis (Hazard ratio = 0.91, p = 0.401 in univariate, hazard ratio = 0.87, p = 0.258 in multivariate) [17, 18].

Contamination Mechanism and Sources

10 studies assessed contamination mechanisms as seen in Table 3. Nine of these studies evaluated airborne contamination as a factor whereas one of their paper studies on intraoperative wound contamination, Shirley et al. [27] compared intraoperative wound contamination between SHS and standard gowns, reporting positive wound cultures in 10% of cases with SHS use compared to 0% with standard gowns, though the difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.16). Taping the gown-glove interface did not show association with higher contamination rates compared to without taping (p = 0.18). Vijaysegaran et al. [20] reported higher microbial emission and particle rates with SHS compared to conventional gowns across various particle sizes at 24 and 48 hours (3.25 x 10*-5 vs 6.51*10-6 colony forming unit/sec, p = 0.002 at 24 hours) (2.3 x 10*-5 vs 5.7x10*-6 colony forming unit/sec, p = 0.008 at 48 hours. Young et al. [21] observed greater contamination at the dominant hand's flexor surface in SHS users, with a mean powder migration score of 3.75. Nakajima et al. [22] found increased chest particle concentration during surgeon motion when SHS was used (1053 vs. 34 N/F3, p = 0.0032). Fraser et al. [23] reported 100% contamination at the gown-glove interface during simulated arthroplasty using SHS. Thaler et al. [33] reported helmet contamination in 12.2% and glove contamination in 18.3% of intraoperative swabs. Tarabichi et al. [25] reported 63.6% helmet contamination rates prior to decontamination, which reduced to 18.8% following hypochlorite spray treatment. Kearns et al. [26] found 49% overall contamination in SHS components sampled postoperatively in 102 primary arthroplasty cases. In contrast, McGovern et al. [19] reported lower airborne particle counts with the Toga system compared to conventional hood and gown setups [42 vs 328 (p=0.037), 42 vs 1178 (p=0.007)]. James [24] reported no statistically significant difference in airborne bacterial contamination between SHS and conventional gowns (Mean colony forming unit 24.81 vs 23.77, p = 0.785).

Design and operational factors affecting SHS performance

Table 4 shows 5 studies that evaluate the influence of design and operational practices in SHS. Konopitski et al. [28] found that zippered gowns and the use of a surgical vest significantly reduced microbial particle settlement rates by 49% (p = 0.011) and 45% (p = 0.018) compared to traditional gowns. Premature activation of SHS fans before gowning resulted in major particle exhaust through gown seams. Kang et al. [29] demonstrated that late activation of airflow after gowning minimized contamination at the shoulder and forearm regions. Vermeiren et al. [30] compared single-fan and double-fan SHS designs, reporting no overall difference in contamination scores (median 2.5 vs. 1, p = 0.082), although greater axillary contamination was observed with single-fan systems (p = 0.01). Hanselman et al. [31] found that airflow activation before gowning significantly increased contamination (6/6 vs 0/6, p = 0.014), with particle spread extending over a five-foot diameter. McAleese et al. [32] compared 2 different donning techniques between surgeons donning by a scrubbed colleague or by non-scrubbed colleague, reporting no significant difference in microbial contamination rates of the operating surgeon's glove. McAleese et al. also reported positive cultures over 12% of SHS hoods immediately after SHS donned.

Discussion

SHS have become increasingly prevalent in arthroplasty procedures, with registry data indicating adoption rates ranging from 34% in the New Zealand Joint Registry to 75-77% in the Kaiser Permanente [14, 21]. While these systems were designed to reduce airborne contamination and subsequent PJIs, our systematic review of 20 studies reveals significant heterogeneity in their reported efficacy. The conflicting evidence underscores the complex interplay between device design, operational protocols, and surgical environment that ultimately determines clinical outcomes.

Effectiveness in reducing PJI

5 key studies included in this review specifically addressed the impact of SHS on PJI rates. Rahardja et al.'s analysis of 19,322 primary TKRs in 2022 demonstrated statistically significant reduction in infection rates at both 90 days (adjusted OR = 0.50, p = 0.008) and 6 months (adjusted OR = 0.55, p = 0.022) compared to conventional gowning, supporting the use of SHS in arthroplasty surgery [14]. This large-scale registry study evaluated modern SHS models (2013–2018 data) that likely incorporated changes in gowning practice of SHS such as the usage of sealant tape over the gown-glove interface [14]. Other possibilities for the data above may be due to underestimation during the time of earlier studies. A study of the rate of PJI in the New Zealand Joint Registry reported a sensitivity of 63% in capturing re-operation rate for PJI between 2006 and 2008 [34]. Similarly, Swedish and Danish Hip Arthroplasty Registries reported that the true rate of PJI can be up to 40% higher than recorded [35, 36]. In contrast, Tayton et al.'s [15] analysis of an earlier cohort (1999–2012) from the same registry found a concerning trend toward increased infection risk with SHS use (OR = 1.53, p = 0.052). Potential reasons include increased bacterial shedding due to excess heat and airflow within the suit, disruption of laminar flow systems both by the up flow of warm air dispelled from below the gown and SHS, and loss of surgeons’ dexterity leading to being more easily to contaminate themselves. Hooper et al. performed a large sample study that investigated laminar flow and the use of space suits in reducing early PJI, reporting that SHS increases need for early revision for deep infection after total hip and knee replacement surgery in univariate analysis (0.243%, p = < 0.001). Tayton et al. 's 2016 study utilized the same set of data as Hooper et al. but included more variables and performed a multivariate analysis, ensuring robustness of their findings [15, 37]. The temporal difference between these studies suggests the amendment in the growing practice of SHS such as taping of gown-glove interface and activation of SHS after gowning to reduce shedding of bacteria in the wound. So et al.'s 2022 study of 260 ankle arthroplasties found no significant difference in infection rates between SHS and conventional gowning (p = 0.45) though this may reflect the smaller sample size and lower baseline infection rates in ankle procedures [16]. 2 large scale sample studies done by Namba et al. in 2012 and 2013 reported no association between SHS and deep surgical site infection in TKRs and THRs [17, 18]. This may be due to multiple variables being included in the study such as patient factors, surgical factors, hospital factors in relation to deep surgical site infection which affect the pureness of association between SHS and deep surgical site infection. Together, these studies illustrate that while SHS may reduce infection risk under specific conditions, particularly with certain gowning protocols e.g. taping of gown-glove interface, delayed activation of SHS, there remains no definitive consensus, and infection outcomes likely depend on multiple interacting variables.

Contamination mechanisms and sources

The shedding of bacteria from the skin of the surgical team has been known as a major potential source of infection [38]. This precipitated the use of an enclosed system, with McGovern et al. noting reduced particle count in the use of the Toga system (hood combined with an integral gown). This is especially when air was expelled through filtered exhaust ports and away from the sterile field without any leakage of air at the gown-hood interface region [19]. These findings suggest that specific design features can mitigate contamination risks. On the other hand, Shirley et al. directly assessed intraoperative wound contamination, reporting a higher, though not statistically significant, rate of positive cultures in the SHS group compared to standard gowns [27]. Similarly, Vijaysegaran et al. found that SHS emitted significantly more microbial particles compared to standard gowns across various particle sizes and time intervals [20]. Additionally, this can be explained by a randomized controlled study on contamination risk of the modern surgical helmet system by Eggers et al. [39] showed increasing risk of bacterial deposition in the surgical field due to positive pressure mechanisms that inadvertently trap and aerosolize contaminants. These findings were corroborated by Young et al. and Nakajima et al. who identified specific high-risk zones including the glove-gown interface, chest region, and dominant hand surfaces. Young et al. [21] reported a mean powder migration score of 3.75 at the flexor surface of the surgeon's dominant hand, while Nakajima et al. [22] documented a 31-fold increase in particle concentration at the chest during motion (1053 vs 34 particles, p = 0.0032) [21, 22]. The gown-glove interface hence commonly carries a higher contamination rate, likely due to the positive pressure within the suit with more movement during surgical procedures [21]. Despite these potential causes identified and pre-emptive measures such as tapping of the glove-gown interface being implemented, there was no significant difference in contamination as compared to without taped [27]. However, it is worth noting that delayed activation of the surgical helmet system after gowning the inner glove showed minimal contamination over the gown-glove interface [29]. This is useful in reducing contamination risk. Another possible contamination source is the surgical helmet, as described in Thaler et al. and Tarabichi et al. [25, 33]. It is postulated that vertical large air flows from the laminar system which pushes the air and debris from the ceiling to the floor. These flows pass the head and upper body of the surgeon and can potentially cause wound contamination. Anti-septic spray such as hypochlorite spray can aid in reducing the contamination as mentioned in Tarabichi et al. [25]. Hooper [37] noted that SHS may paradoxically increase the risk of contamination due to several factors: reduced spatial awareness in the surgeon, a false sense of security while wearing the suit, and the possibility of patient contact contaminating the hood during surgery. Additionally, the fan system may act as a concentrated source of airborne debris and bacteria, potentially increasing microbial shedding within the suit itself. It is important to acknowledge the limitations of the current body of evidence. Wound infections are multifactorial in nature, and the precise relationship between intraoperative contamination and the development of deep infections remains unclear. Furthermore, it is not well established whether there is a linear relationship or a contamination threshold that significantly increases infection risk. These uncertainties limit the generalizability of findings from experimental studies to real-world clinical settings.

Design and operational factors

Five studies addressed the optimisation of SHS through design and operational modifications. Konopitski et al. demonstrated that zippered gown styles and the use of a vest underneath the surgical gown significantly reduced microbial particle spread by 49% (p = 0.011) compared to traditional tie-back gowns [28]. This is likely because many standard surgical gowns have unsealed rear seam, allowing helmet exhaust to escape near the waist, an area near the sterile field. Similarly, Kang et al. and Hanselman et al. showed that activating SHS airflow after donning the gown significantly reduces contamination across multiple body regions. Contrastingly, early activation can generate outward airflow that entrains surrounding airborne particles and redistributes them onto the gown and gloves [29, 31]. These findings underscore the need for clear institutional protocols that specify the proper sequence of gowning and helmet activation. Additionally, Kang et al. [29] found that contamination levels of a gowned and gloved scrub nurse who put the SHS hood on the surgeon was higher as compared to delayed SHS fan activation after the surgeon has donned their gown and gloves. This indicates that optimal donning technique should include non-sterile members applying sterile SHS hood before activation of SHS fan but after the sterile team member has donned their gown and gloves. Vermeiren et al. [30] compared single-fan and double-fan systems, finding no significant difference in overall contamination (median scores 2.5 vs 1, p = 0.082) but noting increased axillary contamination with single-fan designs (3.67 vs 2.33, p = 0.01). This is possibly due to the fact that the single-fan helmet system potentially creates a larger pressure gradient inside the suit. It is also worth noting that while the double-fan system design is theoretically advantageous by reducing bacteria shedding through the exhaustion of used air from back of the helmet via second fan, there is possible air leak especially over the gown-glove interface, thus leading to a more pronounced contamination in both systems (mean contamination score 3 vs 3, p = 0.56). McAleese et al. conducted a study in 2023 to compare the bacterial contamination of surgeon’s gloves with 2 different donning techniques—donning with sterile team members and non-sterile team members. The study was performed under laminar airflow with delayed fan activation after the surgeon had donned his gown and gloves. It found that there is no difference in contamination of the surgeon’s gloves in these 2 different donning techniques (p = 0.99) [32]. Hence, team members need to be careful in donning SHS on surgeons to prevent contamination. The study also demonstrated that SHS hoods have some baseline degree of contamination and thus should not be presumed to be sterile. Similarly, Kearn et al. [26] reported 22% contamination over the SHS hood at time zero after donning.

Clinical recommendation

The collective evidence from these 20 studies suggests several key considerations for clinical practice. First, not all SHS designs are equivalent—newer systems with improved airflow control and sealing mechanisms appear more effective than earlier models. Second, operational protocols are critical—a proper gowning sequence and activation timing significantly impact contamination risk. Evidence supports the use of delayed fan activation (after gowning and gloving) and the avoidance of early airflow initiation, which may otherwise disperse airborne contaminants. The use of zippered gowns integrated surgical vests, and strict donning sequences—preferably involving non-sterile staff fitting the helmet before activation, may also minimize contamination risk. Third, SHS should be viewed as one component of a comprehensive infection prevention strategy rather than a standalone solution. Additional measures, such as routine decontamination of helmet components via the use of hypochlorite spray, should be implemented to reduce baseline contamination [25]. Institutions adopting SHS should provide formal training and enforce standardized protocols to ensure optimal use. Taken together, these strategies form a multi-layered solution to help mitigate infection risks and maximize the protective benefits of SHS in arthroplasty surgery.

Limitations

We acknowledge some limitations in our study. Registry studies, while valuable for assessing real-world outcomes, may underreport infections that don't require revision surgery [35, 40]. While laboratory studies offer important mechanistic insights, they are usually unable to fully replicate the complexity of real-world surgical environments. There is also significant heterogeneity in outcome measures across studies, making direct comparisons challenging. These factors underscore the need for high-quality, prospective studies with standardized protocols to better understand the clinical impact of SHS use.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this systematic review demonstrates that the efficacy of surgical helmet systems in arthroplasty depends critically on device design, operational protocols, and surgical context. While modern systems show promise in reducing PJI rates when properly implemented, improper use can paradoxically increase contamination risk. Institutions should critically evaluate their SHS protocols and consider adopting evidence-based best practices regarding gown selection, activation timing, and staff training to maximize potential benefits while mitigating risks. Further randomized controlled trials are essential to establish definitive guidelines for SHS use in arthroplasty surgery.
