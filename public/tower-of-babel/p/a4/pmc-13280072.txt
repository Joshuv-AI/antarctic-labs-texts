The Importance of Achieving High Treatment Targets in Atopic Dermatitis: A Narrative Review
Authors: Antonio Costanzo, Giulia De Martino, Sara Di Fino, Luca Stingeni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAtopic dermatitis (AD) is associated with a significant physical, psychological, social and economic burden and a decreased quality of life. There is a relationship between disease burden and the level of disease severity and control. The burden of AD largely depends on skin lesions, itch and sleep disturbances, although other aspects also play a role. Even though treat-to-target approaches have been proposed for AD in recent years, studies showed that many patients with AD continue to perceive disease burden despite the achievement of the established treatment targets. This prompted the conception of the Aiming High in Eczema/Atopic Dermatitis (AHEAD) recommendations, which included novel optimal targets for both clinician- and patient-reported outcomes, the achievement of which is defined as minimal disease activity or MDA. This narrative review appraises studies that show that the achievement of elevated treatment targets results in multiple benefits. Aiming high helps to achieve more in patients with AD.Keywords: Atopic dermatitis, Optimal treatment targets, Optimal treatment outcomes

Key Summary Points

Atopic dermatitis (AD) causes considerable physical, psychological, social and economic burdens, affecting the quality of life of patients with AD.A relationship exists between disease burden and its severity and control.Setting ambitious goals has been shown to improve outcomes in patients with AD.

Introduction

Atopic dermatitis (AD) is the most common inflammatory skin disorder, affecting up to one-fifth of the global population [1], with over 200 million people currently suffering from it [2]. Amongst skin conditions, AD tops the statistics of non-fatal years lived with disability [3] with an increasing prevalence, especially in high-income countries [4, 5]. In a cross-sectional study, the point prevalence of AD in the adult population in the EU was 4.4% [6]. A systematic review of incidence and prevalence data in Europe suggested that, compared to the twentieth century, there has been an increase in both parameters in the twenty-first century; however, this was confirmed by several but not all studies [7].

AD is a heterogeneous condition that can manifest itself with varying degrees of severity, ranging from mild to severe. More than 60 scoring systems have been developed to assess the severity of the disease. The assessment is based on the intensity and extent of lesions and symptoms (e.g. itch). Scoring atopic dermatitis (SCORAD), Investigator Global Assessment (IGA) and Eczema Area and Severity Index (EASI) are the most commonly used scoring systems in AD [8]. The same scores are used to assess the response to treatment, and in clinical trials, EASI improvement is the preferred clinician-reported outcome (CRO) measure. The subjective burden of AD is better reflected by patient-reported outcomes (PRO) including Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM), Patient-Oriented Scoring Atopic Dermatitis (PO-SCORAD), numerical rating scale (NRS)-itch, numerical rating scale (NRS)-sleep and quality of life scores such as Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI). Mental health is frequently assessed through the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS) [8, 9].

The care plan for adults affected by AD is stepwise and depends on the severity of the disease [10]. The emergence of new innovative treatments in the past 10 years has resulted in improved treatment outcomes.

This article reviews the burden of disease in AD and explores the concept that the achievement of high treatment targets can result in important benefits for the patient.

Materials and Methods

We performed a non-systematic literature search using the advanced search builder in PubMed for either “atopic dermatitis” or “atopic eczema”, combined with the following terms: “burden”, “treat-to-target”, “minimal disease activity”, and “EASI90” in the Title/Abstract field, as well as “itch” in the Title field only. The searches were limited to the 2015–2025 decade and only records in English were considered. Both original articles and reviews were considered. Further relevant references were identified through both bibliographic mining and forward citation searching.

Ethical Approval

Ethical approval was not applicable as this article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors.

Disease Burden

AD is associated with a significant physical, psychological, social and economic burden and a decreased quality of life [11–16].

From the physical point of view, the burdensomeness of AD largely depends on skin manifestations of the disease, such as skin redness, eczematous lesions, flaking/scaling and related symptoms (itch and pain) [16–18]. In a recent study of 88 patients with AD, over 75% of them experienced itch, skin redness, and dry and flaky skin every 1–3 days [17]. Disease flares worsen the burden of AD, and severe disease is associated with a higher number of flares than mild AD [14]. Patients experiencing both itch and skin pain had more body areas affected and were more bothered by their symptoms than those without; they also reported worse daily work impairment, sleep disruption and diminished quality of life [18]. Sleep disturbances, due to itching, are an important part of disease burden in AD and the most common sleep problems in adults with AD are difficulty in falling asleep, frequent nighttime awakenings and consequently daytime sleepiness [19, 20]. The relationship between AD and sleep is bidirectional as a vicious circle exists [19]: patients with more severe disease take longer to fall asleep and report a higher number nights out of previous 7 nights with sleep disturbances than individuals with mild disease [14]. Using appropriate moisturisers, cleansing routines and fabric selection can impact the burden of skin symptoms [21, 22].

Physical burden of AD is also linked to the fact that the disease is associated with multiple, atopic and non-atopic, comorbidities. Amongst others, the atopic comorbidities include asthma, rhinitis, conjunctivitis and food allergy, whereas non-atopic comorbidities such as autoimmune, cardiovascular, endocrine, infectious, ocular, psychiatric diseases and certain cancers have been reported [23]. The comorbidities and their pathophysiological relationship with AD are reviewed elsewhere [23, 24].

Psychology-wise, patients with AD, especially those with inadequately controlled disease, have higher levels of anxiety and depression than matched controls from the general population as shown by a study conducted on 1860 patients with AD in Europe [25]. Almost 80% of patients with AD suffered from mood changes and this is percentage was higher (94%) at worst point of AD, whereas mood changes were associated with time spent managing AD and the severity of AD [13]. More severe disease was associated with lower mental health scores in the short form (SF)-12 health survey (which consists of 12 questions covering physical functioning and social functioning, emotional and mental health [26]) and lower HADS scores [14, 16]. Several mechanisms affect the link between AD and mental health, such as poorly controlled AD, sleep disturbances, itch, inflammation and coping patterns [27]. Relevant associations between depressive symptoms, fatigue and work productivity highlight the disease burden caused by the psychological components of AD. In fact, productivity loss was strongly associated with depressive symptoms (r = 0.68) and fatigue (r = 0.60) [28]. In addition, about 25% of patients with moderate-to-severe AD are affected by alcohol use disorder [29]. This again can create a vicious circle, as smoking and excessive alcohol consumption negatively impact AD [30].

Regarding the social aspects of the disease, inclusive of relationships and sex life, there are no conclusive data. Some studies suggested that the relationships were not affected, one stated that sexual dysfunction remained little explored, and another study found that AD disrupted sexual function, and lowered libido and reproductive desire of women [31–34]. The disease affects other family members and patients and family members deal with time-consuming treatment regimens, dietary requirements and household changes [35, 36]. There is a dearth of data on the families of adults with AD as most studies were conducted in paediatric patients and their families [35]. In a population-based cross-sectional study, AD was shown to limit lifestyle (51.3%), lead to avoidance of social interaction (39.1%) and impact activities (43.3%) [16]. Even patients with mild AD reported lifestyle limitations and avoidance of social interactions, although more severe disease results in a higher level of activity impairment [14, 16]. As a result, a significantly higher proportion of patients with AD are very dissatisfied with life (crude OR 2.22 (1.45–3.40); p = 0.0006) [16].

Regarding the economic burden, a systematic review of 22 unique studies showed that total costs of AD reach over €20,000 per person per year in patients with uncontrolled moderate-to-severe disease [37]. Both direct (expenditures on prescription medicines, hospitalisations, and visits to healthcare providers) and indirect (absenteeism and presenteeism, career modification, and reduced quality of life) costs contribute to these spendings [37, 38]. In Europe, moderate-to-severe AD resulted in significant indirect (up to €14,000 per patient per year) and direct (up to €7000 per patient per year) costs as documented by a study in France, Germany, Italy, Spain and the UK [39]. Regarding the healthcare utilisation, one study showed that 21.6% of patients with AD and 37.9% of patients with inadequately controlled AD reported at least one emergency department visit in the previous 6 months versus 16.5% of controls without AD, and 93.1% of patients with AD versus 84.2% of those without AD had at least one clinician visit (both p < 0.001) [40]. Similarly, another study showed that patients with severe disease (determined by the EASI score) required more healthcare or acute care visits than patients with clear skin in the previous 6 months (6.6 vs. 3.7) [14]. Together, these data show that disease control is a way of reducing healthcare utilisation. Also in a study that analysed Medicare insured adults, patients with AD showed greater utilisation of outpatient services, outpatient pharmacy services and short-term disability benefits than individuals in the control group without AD [41].

AD has a negative impact on work productivity and can be linked to bulling and discrimination [14, 28, 42, 43]. On average, estimated absenteeism and presenteeism in workers with AD were 10.9 days/year and 13.1 days/year respectively [43]. Stratifying the Work Productivity and Activity Impairment (WPAI) scores according to the EASI score severity strata, Eyerich et al. showed that compared to mild disease, severe AD was associated with higher percentages of absenteeism (4.3% vs. 15.6%), presenteeism (19.8% vs. 40.6%), overall productivity impairment (22.5% vs. 44.9%) and hours missed from work (1.5 h vs. 6.1 h) (all p < 0.0001) with values for moderate AD falling between those seen in mild and severe disease [14]. Absenteeism and presenteeism were more pronounced in individuals who experienced discrimination, with 33.9% of workers with AD feeling discriminated against due to their disease [43]. Patients with AD and high levels of embarrassment and those on systemic therapy miss approximately 1.5 more days at work than those who perceive no embarrassment [44]. Some people may need to change type of work because of their AD and jobs in healthcare, catering, cleaning, hairdressing and car repair should be avoided [35].

Thus, as mentioned for the individual domains, there is a relationship between disease burden and the level of disease severity and control. Indeed, adults with AD report a substantial burden in multiple domains that are significantly higher in individuals with severe disease and poorly controlled [14, 40, 45]. Also, the severity of AD and the use of topical treatments, the frequency and the amount of time spent managing symptoms showed strong associations with the burden of the disease and decreased quality of life [13, 46]. Interestingly, the highest disease burden was reported in geographic clusters including those with the lowest use of systemic therapies [14]. Many patients feel that physicians, mainly the non-dermatologists, underestimate the burden of their disease and erroneously treat AD as a skin condition only [17]. Individual disease burden should be gauged to deliver the most appropriate treatments that fulfil the patient’s needs and expectations [47].

Taken together, we can state that AD poses a considerable burden that extends beyond physical symptoms, significantly impacting patients’ emotional wellbeing, social interactions and economic stability. Addressing this burden requires comprehensive strategies that encompass clinical management, psychological support and economic considerations [37].

Benefits of Elevated Treatment Targets

The identification of AD’s “treatable traits” in 2020 by the European Task Force on Atopic Dermatitis was important for the future implementation of the treat-to-target approach in AD. Treatable traits must be clinically relevant, identifiable and measurable. The Task Force survey established that itch, skin lesions and sleep problems were the top-ranking treatable traits in AD [48]. Subsequently, the International Steering Committee produced, through an eDelphi process, a treat-to-target framework for shared decision-making on systemic treatment continuation, modification or discontinuation in AD. Three-month and 6-month treatment target goals were proposed for five disease scores: EASI (EASI50 at 3 months and EASI75 or EASI ≤ 7 at 6 months), SCORAD (SCORAD50 at 3 months and SCORAD75 or SCORAD ≤ 24 at 6 months), Worst-Pruritus-NRS (WP-NRS) (WP-NRS reduction of at least 3 points at 3 months and absolute score ≤ 4 at 6 months), DLQI (a reduction of at least 4 points at 3 months with an absolute DLQI ≤ 5) and POEM (absolute POEM ≤ 7 at 6 months). Moreover, 3- and 6-month targets for the Patient self-reported Global Assessment of disease severity (PtGA) were introduced to assess the patient’s broad perception of AD activity. According to this algorithm, if the PtGA target plus at least one disease domain target are achieved, treatment can be continued. Otherwise, treatment modification should be taken into consideration [45]. This framework was later adapted for local settings, e.g. Italy or Canada [49, 50].

Despite the advancements in defining treatment targets, a post hoc analysis of the MEASURE-AD study showed that approximately 45% of 1434 adults with AD who were candidates/received systemic therapy and met more than one 6-month treatment target (as defined by [45]) continued to have a moderate or severe disease activity, in terms of EASI, SCORAD, WP-NRS, POEM and DLQI [51]. This analysis suggested that the treatment targets mentioned above may not be high enough to provide a real benefit for the patients and prompted the conception of the Aiming High in Eczema/Atopic Dermatitis (AHEAD) recommendations that established a novel approach to AD management combining shared decision-making with the concept of minimal disease activity (MDA) [52]. The AHEAD recommendations introduced moderate and optimal targets for CROs (EASI, SCORAD, IGA and BGA) and PROs (NRS, POEM, HADS, DLQI). MDA was defined as the simultaneous achievement of at least one CRO and one PRO optimal target (see Fig. 1 for optimal targets) [52].

Interestingly, a post hoc analysis of three clinical trials (i.e. Measure Up 1, Measure Up 2, and AD Up) showed that greater degrees of clinical responses were linked to more robust improvements in multiple dimensions impacted by AD, such as skin pain, sleep, anxiety, depression and DLQI, in a stepwise pattern. Indeed, improvements and response rates for both CROs and PROs were higher in the subgroup of patients who simultaneously achieved EASI90 and WP-NRS 0/1, i.e. the MDA, compared to patients achieving EASI90 alone or WP-NRS 0/1 alone. For example, the percentage of patients achieving DLQI 0–1, which means no impact on quality of life, was 18% for those who reached EASI90 but not WP-NRS 0/1, 40% for those who reached WP-NRS 0/1 but not EASI90, and 73% for those who reached both EASI90 and WP-NRS 0/1 [53]. It should be underlined that this combined endpoint was first used in the Level Up study, which confirmed superiority of upadacitinib over dupilumab in the treatment of moderate-to-severe AD measured as simultaneous achievement of EASI90 and WP-NRS 0/1 at week 16 [54]. Moreover, patients who achieved optimal treatment targets, compared to individuals who achieved moderate or no treatment targets, had greater improvements in health-related quality of life. Also, patients who achieved MDA more frequently reported improvements in health-related quality of life than patient who achieved only optimal CRO targets or optimal PRO targets [55].

The analysis of the TARGET-DERM AD registry data demonstrating that achieving optimal treatment targets for both itch resolution and skin clearance markedly enhances patient-reported AD outcomes further substantiates the importance of achieving higher outcomes. In that study of 1920 patients with AD, the odds ratios of achieving ideal PROs (POEM 0–2, Sleep-NRS 0/1, and Pain-NRS 0/1, DLQI 0/1) were the highest in patients who simultaneously achieved optimal targets for skin clearance (IGA 0/1) and itch resolution (WP-NRS 0/1) [56]. Thus, the study confirmed the validity of the AHEAD recommendations and the importance of aiming for higher treatment targets.

Conclusions

Considerable progress has been made in understanding the pathophysiology of AD, and these insights have led to the development of innovative therapies. Despite these advances, patients still do not experience complete benefit from treatment. Increasing evidence suggests that achieving higher treatment goals yields greater advantages for patients. Achieving near or total clearance of skin lesions and relief from itching, i.e. the MDA, is linked to significant improvement across all aspects of the disease, including sleep disturbances, skin pain and overall quality of life. This comprehensive burden reduction is an outcome all dermatologists should aim for. Nevertheless, achieving MDA in routine clinical practice remains challenging, as access to both prescription and over-the-counter therapies varies across countries of residence. Such disparities frequently persist irrespective of medication cost or reimbursement eligibility.
