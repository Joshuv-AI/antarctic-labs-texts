Left Main Spontaneous Coronary Artery Dissection Complicated by Cardiogenic Shock

Spontaneous coronary artery dissection (SCAD) involves a spontaneous intimal tear and hemorrhage of the vasa vasorum that forms an intramural hematoma, creating a false lumen and impairing coronary perfusion. SCAD often affects young women without preexisting cardiovascular disease. It is associated with pregnancy and vasculopathies, including fibromuscular dysplasia, and is identified in more than 80% of cases. It is also associated with connective tissue disease, including Marfan syndrome and Ehler Danlos syndrome as the most identified. Rarely, SCAD can be associated with autoimmune conditions and emotional stress. SCAD has a wide range of clinical manifestations, which are dependent on the affected coronary territory. The left anterior descending artery is the most common artery affected, involved in 70% of cases. If the affected artery does not carry a high-risk lesion and the patients are hemodynamically stable, they are often managed in absence of revascularization, as most lesions heal within 6 months.1 This approach greatly changes if the affected artery involves high-risk anatomy such as in left main involvement, which is associated with greater mortality given the larger myocardial territory that is affected.

Case Summary

A 58-year-old woman with hypertension, former smoking history, and no obvious connective tissue disease features presented to an outside hospital for acute severe chest pain radiating to the back, with diaphoresis and dyspnea. She received 325 mg of aspirin and was hypoxic on arrival, requiring a non-rebreather oxygen mask. Electrocardiogram revealed sinus rhythm with first-degree atrioventricular block, incomplete right bundle branch block, minimal ST-segment elevations in leads AVR, AVL, and V1-V2, and inferolateral ST-segment depressions (Figure 1). Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) of 25%, hypokinesis of the basal and mid anterior walls, akinesis of apical cap, and mild to moderate mitral regurgitation.

The patient experienced respiratory failure and pulseless ventricular tachycardia and fibrillation 4 hours after symptoms started. She required intubation, 3 rounds of defibrillation, epinephrine, and amiodarone, after which she had return of spontaneous circulation. Angiogram revealed a 99% left main coronary artery occlusion due to dissection (Figure 2). She had persistent hemodynamic instability with evidence of cardiogenic shock, therefore an Impella was placed. She received a successful drug-eluting stent to the left main coronary artery (Figure 3). Afterward, she received 180 mg of ticagrelor.

She initially had lactic acidosis with level of 9.7 mmol/L, fifth-generation cardiac troponin T level of 47,000 ng/L, kidney injury with creatinine up to 1.57 mg/dL, and transaminitis with alanine aminotransferase 162 IU/L and aspartate aminotransferase 644 IU/L. She continued amiodarone, in addition to vasopressors with epinephrine, norepinephrine, and vasopressin, as well as inotropy with milrinone and initial Impella support level of P6. The patient was transferred to our institution for management of cardiogenic shock.

Pulmonary artery catheter placement revealed pulmonary artery saturation of 49%, cardiac output of 2.9 L/min, cardiac index of 1.7 L/min/m2, and systemic vascular resistance of 2,147. She continued anticoagulation with heparin and dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin and ticagrelor. She subsequently had significant improvement in organ perfusion: troponin level halved, kidney function normalized, and she had resolution of transaminitis, acidosis, and ectopy. She had an improved pulmonary artery saturation of 69%, cardiac output of 4.5 L/min, and cardiac index of 2.7 L/min/m2. She was taken off amiodarone and vasoactive, inotropic support and was extubated on the second day of her hospitalization at her full neurologic baseline. Repeat transthoracic echocardiography revealed an improvement in LVEF to 43% on the second day. Given her improvement, her Impella and pulmonary artery catheter were removed on the third day. She was considered for an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator as secondary prevention given the ventricular tachycardia arrest but was planned to reassess in the outpatient setting. She was started on guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) with sacubitril-valsartan, metoprolol succinate, empagliflozin, and spironolactone. Rheumatology was consulted to evaluate for autoimmune, connective tissue disease, and vasculopathies. She had normal renal arterial and carotid duplexes. Serologic testing was overall unremarkable, apart from an initial hypocomplementemia with C3 of 45 mg/dL and C4 of 13 mg/dL, which resolved after a few days. This was attributed to initial liver injury, although the true etiology was not determined. On follow-up testing, she had a mildly reduced immunoglobulin G subclass 1 level of 227 mg/dL, of unknown significance. The patient was evaluated to have idiopathic SCAD, with no autoimmune, connective tissue disease, or vasculopathies identified. On discharge, she continued dual antiplatelet therapy and GDMT and will follow up with rheumatology for further work-up.

Procedural Steps

Left main SCAD (LMSCAD) is life threatening and exceedingly rare, with 4% of SCAD cases involving the left main coronary artery. It often involves more severe features, with more than 90% presenting with acute coronary syndrome, 20% with cardiogenic shock (>2 times greater compared to SCAD of other arteries), 13% with recurrent myocardial infarction at 120 days (30% greater), 8% with arrhythmias, and 9% with all-cause mortality at 120 days.2 Early identification is crucial to optimize outcomes, with coronary angiography being the gold standard. In SCAD, the American Heart Association recommends urgent revascularization via percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or coronary artery bypass graft for patients with left main involvement, persistent ischemia, ventricular arrhythmia, or hemodynamic instability, because early revascularization is associated with decreased mortality and all-cause death when compared to medical therapy.3 Conversely, hemodynamically stable patients with low-risk lesions are often managed conservatively with beta-blockers in the absence of revascularization, as they are associated with reduced recurrence according to numerous observational studies, although there is still a lack of definitive evidence from randomized trials.4 Hypertension control has also been associated with reduced recurrence, with blood pressure targets <130/80 mm Hg, although there remains a need for more studies to further elucidate this. Long-term surveillance of LMSCAD involves monitoring for recurrence, reassessing cardiac function recovery, and screening for associated vasculopathies.

Given the fragility of the dissected vessel, anticoagulation is generally avoided in LMSCAD unless there is mechanical support involvement, which requires anticoagulation to lessen thrombotic risk. The general strategy involves using the lowest therapeutic dosage of anticoagulation for the shortest duration possible while on mechanical support, and once hemodynamics stabilize, the mechanical device should be removed and anticoagulation should be stopped. Some studies show that intermediate-range activated partial thromboplastin time (40-60 seconds) can reduce major bleeding events (5.9% vs 47.1%) without increasing thrombotic events.5 Impella use in LMSCAD complicated by cardiogenic shock should be considered when hemodynamic support is needed as a bridge to recovery with revascularization. Placement prior to PCI is associated with improved survival, and the general strategy involves a slow wean for intensive care unit patients by reducing support level every 4 hours while monitoring for maintenance of hemodynamic stability. A patient is deemed ready for device removal when hemodynamics have remained stable, with cardiac index >2.2 L/min/m2, mean arterial pressure >65 mm Hg, central venous pressure <12 mm Hg, LVEF recovery >25%, and adequate end-organ perfusion maintained.6 In patients who experience cardiac arrest in LMSCAD, implantable cardioverter-defibrillator placement is reasonable 40 to 90 days postarrest if LVEF remains <35% to 40%, although it requires an individualized approach, because the risk-benefit ratio in SCAD is uncertain owing to a likely reversible ischemic trigger and expected myocardial recovery from spontaneous native artery healing.7

In SCAD cases managed in absence of revascularization, 4 weeks of dual antiplatelet therapy followed by aspirin monotherapy has been used. However, this remains controversial. In the presence of drug-eluting stent placement, dual antiplatelet therapy for at least 12 months is recommended to prevent in-stent thrombosis, followed by aspirin monotherapy, however this requires further research given a lack of randomized trials analyzing anticoagulation and antiplatelet therapy in LMSCAD. Patients can also receive GDMT in the presence of left ventricular dysfunction, although there is no consensus regarding optimal medical therapy in LMSCAD.8

Potential Pitfalls

PCI in LMSCAD can be procedurally demanding, with high risks of complications given the fragility of the affected artery, predisposing to extension of hematoma, false lumen guidewire entry, and stent malposition due to resorption of intramural hematomas. Because a large portion of SCAD cases involve significant dissection lengths, they often require long stents, which increase the risk of in-stent thrombosis and restenosis over time.9 For entry, radial access is associated with improved outcomes compared to femoral access owing to lower rates of vascular complications, bleeding, and similar rates of procedural success.10 Sheaths should be small to minimize risk of iatrogenic dissection. Guide catheters should not be large bore, and the technique to minimize vessel injury involves non-coaxial positioning for careful vessel manipulation, as well as avoiding both forceful contrast injection and deep engagement. The guidewires should be flexible and have a soft tip to minimize trauma.

Conclusions

LMSCAD is a unique cause of acute coronary syndrome in young women. It has a several-fold increased risk of mortality, cardiogenic shock, and sudden cardiac death compared to SCAD of other coronaries. Urgent revascularization is crucial, although PCI can be procedurally challenging. Optimal medical therapy for LMSCAD still requires further research to be fully elucidated, but current management consists of antiplatelet therapy, beta-blockers, and GDMT in the presence of left ventricular dysfunction. Overall, LMSCAD requires high clinical suspicion, prompt diagnosis, and individualized management for every patient to optimize outcomes.
