Foster Care Entry and Later Academic Achievement Among Infants Involved With Child Protective Services
Authors: Kierra Sattler, Sarah Font, Carlomagno Panlilio
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTTiming of foster care placement, especially early in life, may have important implications for children's later academic functioning. Given racial disparities in placement decisions, examining associations between age at foster care entry and school outcomes by race is warranted. To address this gap, linked, longitudinal administrative data were used for a cohort of infants involved with child protective services by age one (n = 8795; 47% White, 21% Black, 16% Hispanic, 16% other race/ethnicity; 52% male). Results suggest foster care placement in the first 6 months is related to higher odds of basic reading test scores and lower odds of absenteeism, with associations stronger for Black children. These results have significant implications for the child welfare system.

Children under age one face the highest risk of maltreatment, especially neglect (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services [U.S. DHHS] 2024). Early childhood maltreatment is associated with significant delays in social and cognitive development (Sylvestre et al. 2016; Toth and Manly 2018). The child protective services (CPS) system is tasked with responding to maltreatment and preventing its recurrence. Most children with substantiated maltreatment reports remain with their parents (U.S. DHHS, 2024), and CPS is responsible for providing services, such as substance use treatment or parenting classes, to rectify the conditions that led to maltreatment. A small share of children referred to CPS enter foster care, disproportionately infants, where they remain until CPS determines it is safe for them to return home or they are placed in other permanent arrangements (e.g., adoption or guardianship).

Debate over the impacts of foster care has persisted for many years, with most recent studies arguing that foster care improves academic performance for children at the margin of removal—cases where removal is a possible but not certain outcome (Bald et al. 2022; Gross and Baron 2022)—and perhaps has no effect on the average removed child (Berger et al. 2009, 2015). Yet, little attention has been paid to the timing of children's entry into foster care, and most studies (for exception, see Bald et al. 2022) focus on children who encounter CPS at school age or older (Berger et al. 2009, 2015; Doyle Jr. 2007; Font et al. 2023; Gross and Baron 2022). This is a significant omission in the literature, given that infants have the highest removal rates and roughly half of all foster care entries involve children under the age of five (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2023). Moreover, because early childhood (especially infancy) is a highly sensitive period for cognitive and social development that affects later school performance (Cunha et al. 2006), the change in environment provided by foster care—whether positive or negative—may be more impactful at younger ages. Thus, this study examines the association of foster care entry and its timing with the academic outcomes of children reported to CPS for suspected abuse or neglect victimization in their first year of life. This study also explores racial disparities in foster care outcomes, heeding calls within social science broadly (Boyd et al. 2020) and child welfare specifically (Jones et al. 2023) to disaggregate by race and to explore structural contributors to disparate outcomes across racial groups.

Early Maltreatment and Academic Outcomes

Infancy and toddlerhood represent sensitive periods of development (Marvin and Britner 2008) involving rapid and substantial growth due to neural plasticity as children learn and adapt in response to their experiences and environments (McLaughlin and Gabard‐Durnam 2022). Within the context of supportive environments (i.e., responsive caregiving), plasticity enables positive developmental trajectories for children (Bornstein 2019). However, within adverse environments, children may adapt in ways that increase vulnerabilities for later negative outcomes. Early life adversity, defined as “experiences that are likely to require significant adaptation by an average child and that represent a deviation from the expectable environment” (McLaughlin 2016, 364), is particularly relevant given experiences that represent either the absence of expected environmental input (e.g., neglect, deprivation) or the presence of unexpected environmental input (e.g., abuse, exposure to violence, threat).

Prolonged exposure to maltreatment (i.e., deviation from expected environments) during sensitive periods of development results in stabilized and enduring effects on children's developmental outcomes that are important for academic success (Jones Harden et al. 2016; Kaplow and Widom 2007; McLaughlin and Gabard‐Durnam 2022; Widom 2014). Further, exposure to maltreatment can undermine several domains of school readiness (e.g., cognition, language and literacy, social and emotional, motor and physical, and approaches to learning) that are important for academic success during the transition to formal schooling (Bell et al. 2018; Lipscomb et al. 2019; Panlilio et al. 2018). Educational gaps that emerge early in school tend to persist or grow over time (Michelmore and Dynarski 2017), such that early educational performance is a strong signal of later educational skills and attainment. The enduring consequences of maltreatment on school readiness domains are evident in children's later academic challenges, which include increased absenteeism, higher rates of expulsion or suspension, and lower scores on standardized test scores in reading and math (Armfield et al. 2020; Berger et al. 2015; Font et al. 2023; Mullins and Panlilio 2021; Ryan et al. 2018).

Despite these early consequences of maltreatment, modification of brain architecture remains ongoing. However, such changes require increased effort at later ages through experience‐dependent learning: facilitated learning across the lifespan that capitalizes on neural plasticity (McLaughlin and Gabard‐Durnam 2022). Prior studies have demonstrated that the adverse effects of extreme emotional and physical neglect on developmental outcomes are more difficult to reverse after the age of 6 months (Humphreys et al. 2015; Sonuga‐Barke et al. 2017). This means that providing a safe and enriching environment earlier in life, especially during early infancy, can mitigate the negative cascading effects of early life adversity on developmental domains associated with learning and school adjustment (Cicchetti et al. 2010; Green et al. 2018). Therefore, foster care as an experience‐dependent developmental context may, depending on the potential counterfactual environment, either support or inhibit the cognitive and social development needed for subsequent academic success.

Foster Care in Early Childhood

Compared with older children, infants may be disproportionately impacted by positive and negative environments within foster care because infancy is a period of rapid growth, and the impact of adversity becomes more difficult to reverse as children age (Humphreys et al. 2015; Sonuga‐Barke et al. 2017). Children removed during infancy often enter foster care at birth or after their first CPS report (Jones et al. 2024), which may mean that their experience‐expectant learning occurs in foster care. In contrast, children removed at later ages have an average of four CPS reports before entering foster care (Jones et al. 2024), suggesting that their sensitive period of development occurred in a context of chronic maltreatment. Indeed, toddlers and preschoolers are over twice as likely to have developmental delays at foster care entry than infants (Jee et al. 2010). Thus, for the typical infant, adequate foster care may prevent the onset of developmental delays; for older children, care must be compensatory to reverse existing delays.

Whether foster care provides an adequate or compensatory environment for early development is unclear. Foster care varies substantially in quality, duration, stability, and other relevant factors (Font and Gershoff 2020b). Screening policies typically preclude prospective foster families with histories of perpetrating maltreatment or criminality, and approved foster families receive ongoing monitoring (Child Welfare Information Gateway 2014), but these processes vary across jurisdictions and do not ensure quality. Children are also impacted by their post‐foster care environments, often reunification or adoption, which also vary widely. The distribution of risks and benefits of foster care likely depends on a comparison of the foster care environment to counterfactual environments (Font and Kennedy 2022; Wakefield and Wildeman 2022). Thus, we discuss how foster care could support or harm children's development and later educational outcomes.

Foster Care Supports

An adequate foster care system ensures children have a safe, stable environment during and after placement. Research suggests that young children involved with CPS are less likely to experience maltreatment in foster care than at home (S. A. Font 2015). Children may also experience less cumulative maltreatment and safer, more supportive environments in the long term if parents receive effective services and make meaningful changes prior to reunification (Gross and Baron 2022) or if children exit to permanency with a relative or adoptive family. Research suggests that, among families involved with CPS, child foster care placement increases parental uptake of substance use treatment (Goldstein and Font 2025; Grimon 2023) and decreases encounters with the criminal legal system (Baron and Gross 2022), both of which could improve the safety and stability of children's environments following reunification.

Children in foster care may also receive higher‐quality health and social services that facilitate well‐being. Despite similar health and developmental needs as children who remain at home following maltreatment (Raman and Sahu 2014), children in foster care receive more preventive and treatment services (Ringeisen et al. 2009; Smith et al. 2024), perhaps because states impose special requirements for screening and services among children in foster care and monitor their services more closely. If earlier treatment services support developmental catch‐up, or ensure that children receive needed supports and accommodations at school entry, foster care early in life may improve later academic achievement (Hill and Koester 2015).

Foster Care Risks

Foster care also confers inherent risks to children's well‐being, even if placements are safe and stable. Placement in a new, potentially unfamiliar environment can evoke anxiety and fear, especially in the short term (Folman 1998). Infants in foster care miss important bonding opportunities with their biological parents, and families may face significant readjustment challenges if reunified (Font and Palmer 2022; Goldsmith et al. 2004). Further, agencies sometimes place children in restrictive institutional environments or with caregivers who are poorly vetted, trained, and supported to meet children's needs (Font and Gershoff 2020a). However, these insufficiencies tend to disproportionately affect adolescents (Covington et al. 2023; Konijn et al. 2019; Sattler et al. 2018; Wulczyn 2020). Adolescents are more likely to have accumulated adverse experiences and corresponding psychosocial issues that require specialized caregivers (Jones et al. 2024; Palmer et al. 2022), and agencies tend to have relatively few placement options for higher levels of care (U.S. Government Accountability Office 2018). Thus, unfavorable foster care experiences that dominate public discourse and legislative attention are heavily concentrated among adolescents, including group or institutional care (Covington et al. 2023), multiple placement moves (Konijn et al. 2019), disrupted adoptions and guardianships (Sattler et al. 2018), and aging out (Wulczyn 2020). Infants and young children are not exempt from negative foster care experiences, but they are substantially more likely to experience environments that are stable, permanent, and non‐institutionalized than adolescents (Covington et al. 2023; Konijn et al. 2019; Sattler et al. 2018; Wulczyn 2020)—all attributes that are more conducive to positive social and cognitive development.

Foster Care Counterfactuals

It is important to consider the alternative environments children would experience if not placed in foster care. After CPS is contacted, the alternatives to removal typically include no response, informal services, or monitored in‐home services (often court‐ordered; Jonson‐Reid et al. 2017). Children may also enter a diversion placement (placement with a relative outside of the formal foster care system) (Annie E. Casey Foundation 2024), but these are not tracked or easily identified in existing data. These contexts may confer different advantages and disadvantages relative to foster care.

Research has established that the home environments of children involved with CPS typically include challenges related to substance use, mental illness, domestic violence, or abuse (Goldstein and Font 2025; Palmer et al. 2024), as well as substantial socioeconomic disadvantage (Dolan et al. 2011). In turn, these contexts undermine parental engagement and educational outcomes for children (Duncan et al. 2017; Hatzis et al. 2017; Reeves and Howard 2013). Although CPS often provides services when children remain at home, limited data are available on the type, dosage, and duration of services provided to families, and services are typically not designed to improve families' longer‐term economic stability (Jonson‐Reid et al. 2017; Slack and Berger 2020). Moreover, reviews find that dropout rates for substance use treatment and home visiting programs exceed 30%, and dropout rates are higher for the most socially disadvantaged individuals (Lappan et al. 2019; Latimore et al. 2017). Consequently, CPS involvement may induce little improvement in home environments following suspected maltreatment in infancy.

Race and Foster Care

Nationally, Black children are more than twice as likely to experience foster care as White children (Yi et al. 2020), though the probability of foster care entry is roughly equivalent among children who encounter CPS (Drake et al. 2023). Disproportionality in rates of foster care is well‐established, yet questions remain about whether foster care has similar associations with education and well‐being for Black and White children (Kennedy et al. 2022). Disparate associations of foster care for Black and White children may emerge due to different experiences within foster care (e.g., disparities in placement experiences or support services) or different counterfactuals to foster care (e.g., disparities in in‐home services).

Evidence on racial disparities in foster care experiences is difficult to summarize since they often emerge across states or are found in only some localities (S. Font 2023). In Wisconsin (the site of this study) research found that Black children were less likely to achieve permanency (i.e., exit to reunification, adoption, or guardianship) within 18 months than White children (49.8% vs. 53.9%), and less likely to achieve permanency within 3 years (77.7% vs. 83.4%; S. Font 2023). Whether delayed permanency has adverse implications for child outcomes depends on whether the permanency environment is similarly safe, stable, and supportive as the foster care environment. Evidence suggests young children who reunify from foster care are rereferred to CPS with new maltreatment allegations at high rates (Jones and Jonson‐Reid 2023; Palmer et al. 2023) and exhibit worse well‐being outcomes (Font et al. 2018, 2021; Taussig et al. 2001) than children who remain in care or exit to other forms of permanency.

The concept of different counterfactuals to foster care refers to disparate conditions under which Black and White children would live if not placed in foster care. These differences can emerge from two sources: first, extensive racist policies that induced segregation and inhibited economic opportunities for Black families for decades, and currently shape structural conditions and environments of Black and White families (Boyd et al. 2020). For example, Milwaukee, Wisconsin's largest metro area, consistently ranks among the most segregated areas in the U.S. due to historic redlining and other discriminatory practices, and is a place where Black families experience persistently high rates of poverty, unemployment, incarceration, and other social problems (Levine 2020). Although our study is focused on children who encounter CPS as infants—a comparatively disadvantaged group—Black children in our sample may still come from more challenging socioeconomic and neighborhood circumstances due to structural factors. Thus, foster care may improve Black children's educational outcomes to a greater extent than White children if Black children previously experienced more resource‐restricted environments.

Second, disparate counterfactuals to foster care by race can emerge if Black and White children are removed or reunified at different thresholds of risk. If, due to bias, agencies removed Black children at lower harm thresholds than White children or required Black parents to meet more stringent reunification standards than White parents, there would be (1) more White children remaining in (or reunified to) high‐risk environments; or (2) more Black children removed, potentially permanently, from low‐risk environments. This possibility continues to be debated. A recent study in Michigan found disparate removal rates for Black and White children who were on the margin of removal (i.e., neither the highest nor lowest risk children) following a CPS report (Baron et al. 2023). They determined that the disparity reflects decisions to leave White children in homes with significant risk of harm (and not decisions to remove Black children from low‐risk environments; Baron et al. 2023). However, national studies have found no evidence of differential thresholds for removal, suggesting that the presence, nature, and direction of such bias are dependent on place, time, and risk level (Drake et al. 2023).

The Current Study

Young children, especially infants, may be more susceptible to positive and negative foster care experiences because they are changing environments during a period of rapid growth and opportunity for developmental progress. Whether early foster care has positive, negative, or null associations with later educational outcomes (i.e., math and reading test scores, punitive school discipline, and chronic absenteeism) is not established. Therefore, we examine two primary research questions: (1) Compared with CPS‐involved children who never experienced foster care by age 5, how is foster care entry before age 6 months, at 6–24 months, and at 2–4 years associated with Grade 3 academic outcomes (math and reading test scores, punitive school discipline, and chronic absenteeism)?; (2) Are associations between the timing of foster care entry and academic outcomes consistent among White and Black children? We hypothesize positive associations between early foster care placement and children's academic outcomes for reasons discussed in detail in earlier sections. Also, we hypothesize that foster care during infancy will be more strongly associated with educational outcomes than later foster care entry, and that these associations will be particularly strong among the highest risk children—where the family was deemed in need of services. We explore racial differences in the associations between foster care and educational outcomes, but pose no a priori hypotheses due to the dearth of prior research and plausible reasons to suspect both weaker and stronger associations for Black children relative to White children. Given that neither in‐home services nor foster care are well‐defined or homogenous intervention conditions (Font and Kennedy 2022) and that particular forms of foster care or non‐removal interventions may have unique associations with academic outcomes, we also explore whether associations are consistent across different conditions (e.g., court‐ordered services) and across different types of foster care experiences (e.g., setting, duration, and reunification) in supplementary analyses. Thus, the first research question is confirmatory, and the second research question and supplemental analyses are exploratory.

Method

Data and Sample

We use the 2019 Wisconsin Administrative Data Core (WADC), a longitudinal linked administrative database that includes data from a range of government programs and services. The primary agency databases used for this study were the Department of Children and Families (CPS and foster care cases), Department of Public Instruction (enrollment, attendance, test scores, disciplinary records, and district‐level demographics), birth records, and welfare records.

Our target sample was all children born from 2005 to 2010 who were the alleged victims of maltreatment in a CPS investigation within their first year of life (N = 13,115). We then restricted the sample to children who were enrolled in a WI public school in Grade 3 (10,082) and who took the Grade 3 statewide standardized test (N = 8806). The latter exclusion means that the sample does not include children with severe intellectual disabilities or other conditions who do not take the test, as well as children absent on the day of the test. The sample was limited to children who had information on sex and race/ethnicity (N = 8795).

Measures

The primary dependent variables are basic or above reading skills and basic or above math skills, based on the state standardized tests taken in Grade 3, as well as punitive school discipline and absenteeism in Grade 3. Children's standardized test scores (in reading and mathematics) are categorized as below basic, basic, proficient, or advanced. We combine basic, proficient, and advanced due to changes over time in the threshold for proficiency and the small proportion of children in our cohort who achieved proficiency or advanced. Punitive school discipline included whether children ever experienced suspension or expulsion during 3rd grade. Chronic absenteeism is based on the average attendance rate in Grade 3 and recoded as a dichotomous variable (0 = 90% or higher attendance rate and 1 = below 90% attendance rate).

The primary independent variable is age at foster care entry, which is categorized into four groups: (1) Early infancy (i.e., prior to 6 months old); (2) Toddlerhood (i.e., 6–24 months old); (3) Preschool (i.e., 2–4 years); and (4) never experienced foster care prior to age 5. Among the subset of children who experienced foster care, we also examine variation by care experiences, including exit type (reunification vs. other permanency), placement setting (kin vs. other), number of placements (count), and time spent in foster care before discharge (more than 1 year vs. 1 year or less).

We included covariates at the child, family, case, and school district levels. Child covariates were sex (female, male), race/ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, Other race/ethnicity), receipt of Supplemental Security Income (SSI; proxy for disability, 1 if yes, 0 otherwise) by age 1, and birth year. Family covariates were mother's age at child's birth (under 18 years old, 18–20 years old, over 20 years old), maternal receipt of cash assistance through Temporary Assistance for Needy Families (TANF; 1 if yes, 0 otherwise), and living arrangement at the time of the initial investigation (two parents, single parent, other arrangement). Case‐level covariates pertain to the child's first investigation. Physical abuse (1 if the report included alleged physical abuse, 0 otherwise) is the only allegation variable because nearly all other investigations were for neglect alone (sexual abuse is very rare in the first year of life). Other case‐level variables were emergency referral (1 if the report was assigned for “immediate” response, and 0 if next day or up to 72 h), alleged perpetrator's relationship to the child (not a biological parent, mother only, father only, both parents) and whether Milwaukee County (the largest in the state) was responsible for the case. Lastly, two school district‐level covariates were included because districts vary in their composition, resources, and average performance. These measures were the percentage of White students and the percentage of students receiving free and reduced lunch.

Analytic Approach

All analyses were conducted in Stata version 18 (StataCorp 2023). First, we estimated several logistic regression models in which our outcomes (reading, mathematics, punitive school discipline, and absenteeism) were regressed on age of first removal and all covariates. We note that although regression with controls can result in biased estimates due to omitted confounders, it is important to understand that omitted information about the family of origin environment or the maltreatment children experienced tends to bias findings toward unfavorable estimates of the effect of foster care (Berger et al. 2009). That is, because foster care is an intervention of last resort, reserved for the most threatening situations, children who are removed from their homes are typically from higher‐risk environments than children who remain in their homes following a CPS report. The false identification of positive associations between early foster care entry and children's educational outcomes would require that children predisposed to favorable educational outcomes were disproportionately selected for removal. Next, we produce within‐race models separately for Black and White children to examine if findings are consistent.

In our supplemental analyses, we estimated the same logistic regression models among a subsample of children who received case services. Further, we also examined whether our results were consistent for children in Milwaukee County only. Next, we examined whether foster care experiences predicted children's outcomes (reading, mathematics, punitive school discipline, and absenteeism) while controlling for age at first out‐ofof‐home placement and our covariates among children who experienced foster care prior to age 5.

Race Subgroup Results

We then considered the associations between age at first foster care placement and our outcomes within race (White only; Black only) given the disparate findings in our outcomes and the larger discussion on racism within the child welfare system. Supplemental Analyses

Based on our initial findings documenting the significance of age of removal and disparities across racial groups, we conducted a series of supplemental analyses. First, there are likely systematic differences in the risk level of children who enter foster care compared with children who are investigated but remain home—and especially those who were found to require no ongoing services from CPS. Estimated coefficients of foster care entry may be biased (toward identification of harms) if the children in the comparison condition were not maltreated and faced low risk of future harm in their homes. Unfortunately, research consistently shows that substantiation is a poor indicator of whether maltreatment occurred or whether the child is at high risk of future victimization. Thus, our supplemental analysis constrains the comparison group to children who had a CPS case opened for in‐home services by age 5—these are children for whom the agency determined that ongoing agency, and often family court, involvement was necessary to avoid foster care. The OR and confidence intervals from the logistic regression models among the subsample of children who received case services can be found in Table 5. The general pattern of findings is consistent with the full sample models presented in Table 2. Compared to children who never entered foster care by age 5, children who entered care during early infancy (OR = 1.34, p < 0.01) or toddlerhood (OR = 1.41, p < 0.001) had higher odds of demonstrating basic or above reading skills in Grade 3. Children placed in foster care during early infancy (OR = 0.46, p < 0.001), toddlerhood (OR = 0.62, p < 0.001), or preschool (OR = 0.55, p < 0.05) all had lower odds of chronic absenteeism compared to children who were never placed in foster care before age 5. Results are fairly consistent in the subgroup models by race. Specifically, we find that foster care placement is significantly associated with lower odds of chronic absenteeism among White and Black children, but foster care placement during infancy or toddlerhood is significantly associated with higher odds of basic or above reading skills among Black children only.

Given that children have different experiences in foster care following removal, which can influence their academic and social outcomes (Sattler et al. 2023), we also examined associations between foster care contexts and academic outcomes among the subsample of children with foster care placement by age 5 (see Appendix A, Table A1). Specifically, we investigated whether reunification, ever experiencing kinship care, number of placements, experiencing foster care for more than a year, and age at first foster care entry were associated with basic or above reading or mathematics, school discipline, and absenteeism. Age of foster care removal was not associated with any academic outcomes among the sample of children who experienced foster care by age 5. Reunification, compared with adoption, guardianship, or remaining in foster care at the end of the observation period, is associated with higher odds of absenteeism (OR = 2.13, p < 0.001). Among White children who experienced foster care by age 5, ever being placed with kin was associated with higher odds of demonstrating basic or above mathematics skills (OR = 1.39, p < 0.05). Among Black children, reunification (OR = 1.91, p < 0.05) and ever being placed with kin (OR = 1.65, p < 0.05) are both associated with higher odds of chronic absenteeism. Additionally, Black children who experienced foster care in infancy had higher odds of basic or above reading skills compared to Black children who entered foster care during preschool.

Lastly, we sought to disentangle whether our findings for race heterogeneity are explained by geography, given the history of racial segregation in Wisconsin. The majority of Black children who are placed in foster care in Wisconsin live in Milwaukee County (the largest county in Wisconsin), whereas the majority of White children entering foster care live elsewhere. It is possible that the observed patterns are driven by the unique context of Milwaukee County, which is highly racially and economically segregated (Levine 2020). To differentiate race and place, we present supplemental models (Appendix B, Table B1) that show (1) models for Milwaukee County only; (2) all non‐Milwaukee County cases; (3) Black children in Milwaukee County; and (4) Black children in all other counties. Across these specifications, results are generally consistent with the main models. One exception is that, for Black children outside of Milwaukee County, foster care during infancy was significantly associated with all outcomes (higher odds of reading and mathematics skills and lower odds of punitive school discipline and attendance). We caution against overinterpretation, however, given the small sample size (n = 487).

Discussion

Young children, especially infants, face the highest risks for CPS involvement and foster care entry; they are also highly sensitive to abusive or neglectful caregiving. Thus, foster care placement early in life may be particularly important for the development of children in high‐risk family contexts. At the same time, given the importance of early attachment relationships and the uneven quality of foster care, such placements also carry significant risks for children's development. We contribute to this debate by estimating how the timing of foster care entry (in early infancy, toddler years, or preschool, compared with never or after age 5) is associated with children's third‐grade reading and math skills, exposure to punitive school discipline, and chronic absenteeism among children reported to CPS in the first year of life. For this vulnerable population, we find some evidence that early foster care placement, compared to later or no foster care entry, has the potential to support early academic skills and reduce chronic absenteeism, but there are nuances in these associations.

Our first major finding is that foster care intervention during early infancy (ages 0–6 months) is associated with significantly higher odds of basic or above reading skills and lower odds of chronic absenteeism, compared to no foster care intervention before starting school. Our results extend beyond prior literature documenting increased academic performance following foster care placement for children on the margin of removal (Bald et al. 2022; Gross and Baron 2022), to illustrate that early foster care has the potential to support reading skills for infants who come into contact with CPS. This finding is consistent with understandings of sensitive periods of development and how chronic maltreatment early in life undermines healthy brain development. Our findings highlight the need for agencies to assess and support cognitive stimulation and normative development in this sensitive period, regardless of whether children remain in their homes or enter foster care.

CPS agencies are increasingly preserving children in their familial homes following early life maltreatment. In fact, the rate of foster care entry has declined by 30% in the past two decades (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2006, 2023). Yet, current CPS practice for children remaining in the home largely focuses on support and treatment services to parents (Berger & S. A. Font 2015), and these services may be insufficient to help children overcome early life adversity. Under federal law, states are supposed to refer all substantiated victims of child maltreatment to Early Intervention services to facilitate developmental assessment and treatment where needed (Child Abuse Prevention and Treatment Act (Amended) 2003). However, the vast majority of children encountering CPS are not substantiated as victims, despite facing similar risks for adverse outcomes throughout childhood (Font and Maguire‐Jack 2020; Ryan et al. 2018). Moreover, few substantiated victims ultimately engage in Early Intervention services (Moxley et al. 2012). Enhanced efforts to engage vulnerable families in relevant services can promote children's positive developmental trajectories in cognitive, physical, and social domains (McManus et al. 2012), as well as prevent further maltreatment (Reynolds et al. 2009). Given that CPS caseworkers are responsible for service plans, this requires educating the workforce about the importance of supporting socioemotional development post‐referral (Edwards and Panlilio 2024).

Our second major finding is that foster care may reduce chronic absenteeism. Children are unlikely to attain basic skills if they are not attending a substantial share of lessons (Gottfried 2009), and absenteeism is a strong predictor of later school dropout. Children of teen, single, unemployed, or low‐income parents are more likely to miss school (Romero and Lee 2008). On the whole, these conditions are very common among CPS‐involved families and less common among foster families (Font and Potter 2019). Illness is also a direct cause of absenteeism (Allen et al. 2018) and children reported to CPS early in life have elevated rates of physical and other health problems (Leslie et al. 2005). However, foster care comes with additional surveillance—by caseworkers, courts, and schools—that may increase the likelihood that absenteeism is noticed and addressed. Further research is needed to uncover the reasons for chronic absenteeism in this population and to develop solutions.

Lastly, our findings indicate that Black children benefited more from early foster care placement than White children. These findings require replication in other contexts and samples, and we caution against overinterpretation of these findings. Importantly, baseline differences in math and reading scores in our sample were already very large, as White children were almost twice as likely as Black children to demonstrate at least basic mathematics or reading skills. Although racial gaps in academic outcomes have been consistently documented in the general population (Kuhfeld et al. 2021; Reardon and Portilla 2016), it was somewhat surprising that gaps remained large in the context of a highly disadvantaged sample of children—those reported to CPS in their first year of life. However, there were significant differences in the family environments of Black and White children referred to CPS in infancy in our sample. Black children were substantially more likely to be born to teen mothers, reside in a single‐parent household, receive cash assistance, and attend an economically disadvantaged school district. These factors—family structure, family poverty, and neighborhood/school poverty—are strongly linked to children's developmental outcomes (Duncan and Magnuson 2012; Kearney 2023; Manduca and Sampson 2019). Yet, even after controlling for these differences, we found that the benefits of foster care were concentrated among Black children. As such, it may be that White families are provided more services when they remain at home, thereby reducing the impact of foster care on academic outcomes. Alternatively, it may be that Black families do not receive adequate, high‐quality, or appropriate resources when children remain at home following an investigation. Given widespread discussions about systemic racism within and outside of CPS, our findings suggest that systems may want to specifically examine if there are racial disparities in child well‐being outcomes across different types of interventions, and if the nature, accessibility, quality, and fit of services provided to children who remain in home are equitable across racial groups.

Importantly, our findings need to be considered in light of several limitations. First, we are not able to assess the quality of services received by different families. It may be that White families receive different types, quality, or quantity of in‐home services or engage differently with service providers (Font 2013), such that White children remaining in home are better supported than Black children who remain at home. Efforts to improve the quality of services data within administrative systems are needed. Future research should consider augmenting administrative data with parent‐report or foster caregiver‐report questionnaires to provide a more holistic view of CPS involvement and foster care experiences. Second, we are only able to capture district characteristics, and there may be unobserved disparities within a district in the schools that children attend. Lastly, there are likely to be unobserved variables that affect our estimates. Specifically, we lack detailed information on the nature or severity of maltreatment that children experienced. Because foster care is an intervention of last resort, children who are removed likely faced more severe harms or threats of harm in their home environments than children who were maintained in the home. If so, we may be underestimating the benefits of foster care by failing to account for greater baseline disadvantages in the removed subsample. In addition, there are a range of potential mechanisms that we cannot measure that may provide insight into our findings. For example, differential uptake of child care could contribute to children's academic achievement. Prior work has demonstrated that only 30% of children involved in CPS who remain in home are likely to experience child care; the majority of whom are in center‐based care (Sattler 2023). Participation in any child care has been documented to promote cognitive and language outcomes for children impacted by CPS (Dinehart et al. 2012; Klein 2016; Merritt and Klein 2015; Sattler 2023). However, there is limited evidence on participation rates in child care and resulting developmental outcomes among children placed in foster care. Future work should consider whether children in in‐home or out‐of‐home care differentially participate in child care and whether child care is protective for both placement types following experiences of abuse and neglect.

Despite these limitations, our study adds to a growing body of literature highlighting improved outcomes for children who enter foster care versus children who remain in home following suspected maltreatment. To be clear, the use of foster care should be based on child safety needs: our findings do not speak to the appropriate levels of foster care placement or promote increases in the use of foster care. Agencies have a special obligation to children in foster care due to their custodial status, but our findings highlight that children who remain in home experience poorer developmental outcomes and thus may benefit from enhanced supports. The Family First Prevention Services Act of 2018 (FFPSA) sought to improve the rate and quality of services provided to families with children at risk of entering foster care. Yet, in 2023, states served a total of 18,324 cases with these federal funds (U.S. Children's Bureau 2024)—far fewer than the approximately 600,000 substantiated child victims or the more than three million children experiencing an investigation (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2024). Numerous factors inhibit the expansion of high‐quality services, including provider shortages and turnover, insufficient population size to implement an appropriate array of interventions simultaneously, and failure to engage families with maltreatment‐related risks in services, either in or out of the child welfare system (Child Welfare League of America 2024; Putnam‐Hornstein et al. 2021). Moreover, many of the services funded by the FFPSA have not been proven effective within the child welfare population, nor have they been shown to improve children's safety or normative development (U.S. Department of Health and Human Services 2025). There is no evidence available to date that FFPSA has improved the safety or well‐being of children who remain in the home. Further research is needed to understand and address the low uptake of evidence‐based services to support positive early development, including home visiting and Early Intervention, among CPS‐involved families.
