Association between the oxidative balance score and depressive symptoms in adults with metabolic syndrome: a cross-sectional study from NHANES
Authors: Jinshun You, Jiaqi Wu, Lingfang Zheng, Jingyi Pei, Shujie Xia, Shijie Qiao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study investigated the association between the Oxidative Balance Score (OBS) and the odds of depressive symptoms in adults with metabolic syndrome (MetS) using National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data (2007–2018), and employed machine learning to enhance predictive insights.MethodsWe analyzed 6,244 U.S. adults with MetS. Multivariable logistic regression assessed the relationship between the OBS and the odds of depressive symptoms in U.S. adults with MetS, with subgroup and Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analyses. Four machine learning models (Decision Tree, k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)) were developed and interpreted using Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP).ResultsAmong the participants, 1052 individuals (16.85%) were identified as having depressive symptoms. Significant differences were observed between participants with MetS with depressive symptoms and those with MetS alone in terms of age, gender, race, poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), smoking status, educational level, marital status, hypertension, diabetes, and OBS. No significant difference was found in abdominal obesity. In the unadjusted model, partially adjusted models, and the fully adjusted model, higher OBS were consistently associated with reduced odds of depressive symptoms with MetS. Subgroup analyses revealed no significant differences in the association between OBS and depressive symptoms across the examined strata among adults with MetS. RCS indicated a linear negative correlation. Machine learning models demonstrated strong performance, with Random Forest achieving the highest AUC (0.914). SHAP analysis identified educational level, marital status, and gender as the top predictors.ConclusionA higher OBS is associated with reduced odds of depressive symptoms in U.S. adults with MetS, highlighting the significant role of socioeconomic factors. These findings align with existing evidence and warrant prospective validation.Keywords: Depressive symptoms, Metabolic syndrome, Oxidative balance score, NHANES, Diet

Introduction

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a cluster of metabolic disorders characterized by dyslipidemia, obesity, insulin resistance, and impaired glucose homeostasis [1]. Driven largely by sedentary lifestyles and dietary changes, the global prevalence of MetS has been rising, establishing it as a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases, type 2 diabetes, and other multi-metabolic disorders [2]. Depression, on the other hand, is a psychiatric condition marked by symptoms such as low mood or anhedonia, loss of energy or fatigue, sleep disturbances, psychomotor retardation, pessimism, feelings of guilt, low self-esteem, and suicidal tendencies [3]. The World Health Organization has projected that depression will become one of the leading causes of the global disease burden by 2030 [4]. An increasing number of studies suggest a significant association between MetS and depression. For instance, a 7-year follow-up study reported a depression prevalence of 37.02% among middle-aged and older adults with MetS, with this prevalence increasing over time [5]. Furthermore, research by Jeong-Kyung Ko et al. suggested that metabolic dysregulations, such as elevated triglyceride and blood glucose levels, may contribute to an increased risk of suicidal behavior and depression in adults [6].

The Oxidative Balance Score (OBS) is a composite measure that ranks an individual’s antioxidant status by assessing both the pro-oxidant and antioxidant components derived from dietary and lifestyle factors. A higher OBS generally indicates a predominance of antioxidant exposures [7]. The OBS framework is thought to enhance the body’s overall antioxidant capacity and has been associated with a reduction in circulating levels of inflammatory markers, such as C-reactive protein. This relationship underscores the critical role of diet in modulating oxidative stress(OS) and its potential impact on health outcomes [8].

The pathogenesis of MetS is closely associated with insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, ectopic lipid deposition, and adipose tissue remodeling [2]. OS serves as a core driver in this process, whereby inflammatory cytokines induce the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which in turn amplify the inflammatory response [9]. Dietary factors play a crucial role in the development of MetS. Diets rich in grains, fish, fruits, and vegetables have been shown to reduce the risk of MetS, while diets high in meat and fried foods—typical of a Western dietary pattern—increase the risk of MetS [10]. Such unhealthy eating habits trigger adverse biological effects that promote the onset and progression of MetS. For example, high-fat diets contribute to lipid accumulation, disrupt energy metabolism, adversely affect adipose tissue function, and ultimately lead to obesity [11]. Additionally, a sedentary lifestyle has been demonstrated to impair metabolic function and weaken endothelial integrity, resulting in increased OS [12]. A cross-sectional study further indicated that a higher OBS is associated with a reduced incidence of MetS, highlighting the important role of antioxidant-rich diets and lifestyles in the prevention and management of MetS [13].

Although the pathological mechanisms underlying depression are not yet fully understood, current research indicates a close relationship between depression and OS [14]. Studies have shown that an imbalance between pro-oxidants and antioxidants can increase the production of ROS, leading to OS, which may be involved in the pathogenesis of depresssion [15]. Foods rich in various vitamins possess anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties; they can also protect against free radical damage and exert neuroprotective functions [16]. Cross-sectional studies have demonstrated that a higher OBS is associated with a reduced incidence of depression [17]. Therefore, it is evident that antioxidant-rich dietary patterns and lifestyles may influence both MetS and depressive symptoms.

Unlike traditional statistical methods, machine learning algorithms are capable of uncovering complex relationships and nonlinear patterns, thereby enabling the construction of predictive models that reveal underlying structures in large datasets [18]. These methods enhance precision in individual-level risk stratification, improve computational efficiency through dimensionality reduction, accurately identify high-risk cohorts, and provide robust, scalable frameworks for analyzing complex, high-dimensional datasets [19]. In depression research, machine learning has demonstrated effectiveness in identifying a broad range of factors associated with depression risk. Techniques such as k-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) [20], Random Forest [21], and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) [20]have enabled researchers to develop powerful predictive models and evaluate both individual and combined effects on depression. However, it remains unclear whether the OBS is associated with reduced odds of depressive symptoms in adults with MetS. To address this gap, we utilized data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) between 2007 and 2018 to examine the relationship between OBS and the odds of depressive symptoms in U.S. adults with MetS. Furthermore, we developed machine learning models to explore more complex patterns and enhance the predictive capability for depressive symptoms in this population, thereby providing deeper insights into the potential metabolic contributors to depressive symptoms in adults with MetS.

Methods

Data source and study population

The data for this study were derived from the NHANES, a nationally representative survey conducted by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). The survey protocols were approved by the Research Ethics Review Board of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS). The NCHS protocol details are available at: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/erb.html. All participants provided written informed consent at the time of enrollment. The NHANES datasets are publicly accessible at: https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/.

Our study utilized pre-pandemic data from the NHANES (2007–2018). The initial cohort consisted of 59,842 participants. This analysis focused specifically on respondents aged 20 years and older with MetS. Individuals with missing Patient Health Questionnaire (PHQ-9) data for depressive symptoms were excluded, as were participants who did not provide dietary information, since they could not be assessed for the calculation of the OBS. A complete-case analysis yielded a final sample of 6,244 participants. The participant selection process is detailed in Fig. 1.

Definition of oxidative balance score

The OBS was calculated by aggregating pro-oxidant and antioxidant components derived from dietary and lifestyle factors [22]. Dietary components included 16 nutrients assessed through 24-hour dietary recall interviews. To enhance the reliability of self-reported intake, data from the first and second dietary interviews were averaged. The lifestyle-based OBS components included physical activity, body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption, and smoking status. To determine the physical activity score, we followed the NHANES guidelines and calculated the Metabolic Equivalent (MET) score based on methods outlined in published articles. This score included work-related activities, leisure-time physical activities, and walking or cycling for transportation. The score is calculated as the product of the MET value, weekly activity frequency, and activity duration. It is then divided into gender-specific tertiles and assigned scores (within each gender, from the lowest to highest tertile: 0/1/2) [23]. The smoking status was assessed using serum cotinine, the primary metabolite of nicotine, as an objective biomarker. Serum cotinine levels were divided into gender-specific tertiles, and reverse scoring was applied (within each gender, from the lowest to the highest tertile: 2/1/0). According to previous literature, alcohol consumption was categorized into three groups: non-drinkers, nonheavy drinkers(0 to 15 g/d for female and 0 to 30 g/d for male) and heavy drinkers(≥ 15 g/d for female and ≥ 30 g/d for male). The scores were assigned as follows: 2 points for non-drinkers, 1 point for nonheavy drinkers, and 0 points for heavy drinkers [24].

Definition of depressive symptoms in participants with MetS

NHANES uses the PHQ-9 to assess depressive symptoms, which evaluates the presence of depressive symptoms in participants over the past two weeks. The total score ranges from 0 to 27. Participants with a score of 10 or higher are classified as having depressive symptoms [25]. This study will analyze the odds of depressive symptoms, defined as a PHQ-9 total score of 10 or higher, as the outcome variable.

MetS is assessed based on five key criteria: according to the ATP III guidelines, a diagnosis of MetS is made if three or more of the key features are present. We referred to relevant research literature for the extraction of MetS criteria [26].

Covariates

The covariates in this study, including gender, age, race, family poverty-to-income ratio (PIR), waist circumference, hypertension, diabetes, education level, and marital status, were sourced from various sections of the NHANES database, such as demographic data, physical examination records, the diabetes questionnaire, and the smoking questionnaire. A comprehensive description of all variables and the data can be found on the official NHANES website.

Statistical analysis

Multivariable logistic regression models were used to examine the independent association between OBS and depressive symptoms in participants with MetS. The OBS levels were converted from a continuous variable to a categorical variable (based on tertiles), and trend tests were conducted to assess the linear relationship between OBS and the odss of depressive symptoms in this population. Three regression models were constructed: Model 1: Unadjusted. Model 2: Adjusted for age, gender, race, and PIR. Model 3: Further adjusted for smoking status, educational level, marital status, hypertension, and diabetes. Restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression was applied to explore potential nonlinear relationships between OBS and depressive symptoms in this population. Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the association across strata defined by age, gender, race, PIR, education level, smoking status, marital status, diabetes and hypertension. Four machine learning algorithms—Decision Tree, KNN, Random Forest, and XGBoost—were implemented for predictive modeling, selected for their compatibility with subsequent Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) interpretability analysis. The model performance was evaluated using the average area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). The best-performing model was selected according to the highest AUROC in each dataset and subsequently interpreted using SHAP analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1). A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association between OBS and depressive symptoms in participants with MetS

Table 3 shows the association between OBS and depressive symptoms in participants with MetS. The OBS was categorized into tertiles (T1: 1–15, T2: 16–23, T3: 24–35) and analyzed using logistic regression. Compared to the T1 group, higher OBS tertiles were associated with reduced odds of depressive symptoms in MetS participants. Specifically, the T2 group had an odds ratio (OR) of 0.75 (95% CI: 0.64–0.88, p < 0.001), and the T3 group had an OR of 0.59 (95% CI: 0.50–0.69, p < 0.001). In the unadjusted model, partially adjusted models, and the fully adjusted model, higher OBS was consistently associated with decreased odds of depressive symptoms with MetS.

Machine learning analysis

Statistically significant covariates identified from the baseline characteristics, along with the OBS, were used to develop four machine learning models for predicting depressive symptoms in adults with MetS. To maximize data utilization, the entire dataset was used without splitting into separate training and validation sets. Instead, cross-validation was employed to assess model generalizability. Given the pronounced class imbalance in the data, the AUC-ROC was selected as the sole performance metric. All models were trained using the caret package in R, with optimal hyperparameters identified via grid search. The specific configurations were as follows:

Decision tree: The CART algorithm was implemented with strict structural constraints to mitigate overfitting: maximum tree depth of 16, minimum samples per split of 22, minimum samples per leaf of 7, and maximum number of terminal nodes of 60.

Random forest: Tuning focused on the number of randomly selected predictors considered at each split (mtry), with the search space spanning from 2 to the total number of features and 5 evenly spaced candidate values evaluated.

XGBoost: A tree-based gradient boosting algorithm was used, with SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) applied during training to address class imbalance.

KNN: All predictor variables were centered and scaled prior to modeling. The number of neighbors (k) was tuned over the range 3 to 21 with a step size of 2.

All models underwent 5-fold repeated cross-validation with 3 repetitions. The resulting average AUC values were as follows: Decision Tree = 0.72, Random Forest = 0.914, KNN = 0.763, and XGBoost = 0.852. The corresponding ROC curves are presented in Fig. 4.

SHAP visualization of model contributions

A notable strength of the Random Forest model is its inherent capability to provide internal measures of relative feature importance for predictions. This model is generally suitable for various types of problems, regardless of scale, and performs robustly even with imbalanced or incomplete data [27]. Furthermore, it enables the analysis of variable importance, which may explain its strong predictive performance for depressive symptoms in individuals with MetS. Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), a popular feature-based interpretability method, was applied to enhance the transparency and trustworthiness of the model predictions. This approach is particularly effective for interpreting complex tree-based models, offering high computational efficiency and stability. The visualization of SHAP analysis illustrates the global contributions of variables to the Random Forest model (Figure 5). The top seven influential predictors for depressive symptoms in MetS were, in descending order: educational level, marital status, gender, age, diabetes, race, and the OBS.

Discussion

This study revealed significant differences between participants with MetS and depressive symptoms and those with MetS alone in terms of age, gender, race, PIR, smoking status, educational attainment, marital status, hypertension, diabetes, and OBS. Specifically, female sex, lower educational level, unmarried status, current smoking, older age, lower income, hypertension, and diabetes were associated with increased odds of comorbid depressive symptoms, findings largely consistent with previous studies [28]. Although no significant difference was observed in abdominal obesity, the prominent features of hypertension, diabetes, and altered oxidative balance strongly suggest that MetS with depressive symptoms is a multifactorial condition. These results underscore the importance of comprehensive clinical assessment focusing on metabolic health parameters and oxidative status abnormalities in identifying and managing depressive symptoms in participants with MetS.

In this cross-sectional study, our findings demonstrated a negative association between the OBS and depressive symptoms among participants with MetS. This inverse relationship persisted even after adjustment for potential confounding covariates. RCS analysis further revealed a linear relationship between OBS and depressive symptoms in individuals with MetS, with the association remaining statistically significant both before and after covariate adjustment. Research suggests that metabolic dysregulation may influence depression development through pro-inflammatory and OS pathways [29]. OS represents one of the key mechanisms linking MetS to depression, where increased ROS can lead to neuronal damage and inflammation, promoting neurodegeneration and exacerbating depressive symptoms [30]. Elevated ROS production and depleted antioxidant defenses contribute to cellular damage and redox imbalance, leading to irreversible accumulation of oxidative products, insulin resistance, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and ultimately, MetS [31]. Dietary factors can either exacerbate or ameliorate OS by modulating the intake of pro-oxidant or antioxidant compounds [32]. Similarly, lifestyle behaviors such as excessive alcohol consumption, long-term smoking, and physical inactivity can further increase oxidative burden [33]. The present study’s finding of a significant association between OBS—which incorporates both pro-oxidant and antioxidant components from diet and lifestyle—and depressive symptoms in MetS provides supportive evidence that imbalance in OS may represent an important underlying mechanism in this comorbidity.

To the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have specifically examined the relationship between OBS and depressive symptoms in individuals with MetS. Addressing this gap, our study found that higher OBS scores were inversely associated with the odds of depressive symptoms in this population. Subgroup analyses revealed no significant differences across strata of gender, race, education, poverty level, age, smoking status, marital status, hypertension, or diabetes. As noted in the literature, subgroup differences are considered statistically meaningful only when the interaction term reaches p < 0.05 [34], suggesting that the effect of OBS on depressive symptoms did not vary across these subgroups.

We developed four machine learning models to predict depressive symptoms in adults with MetS, among which the Random Forest model demonstrated strong predictive performance (AUC = 0.914). SHAP visualization was applied to interpret variable importance within the model. Machine learning algorithms have shown impressive predictive capabilities across various domains and are increasingly utilized in depressive symptoms prediction. For instance, a cross-sectional study conducted in Kenya incorporated adverse childhood experiences, poverty, and stress as social predictors of depressive symptoms, achieving an AUC of 0.83 [35]. While previous machine learning studies have focused on predicting either depressive symptoms or MetS independently, few have addressed their comorbidity. Our study applied interpretable machine learning techniques to explore the role of OBS and social factors in predicting depressive symptoms among adults with MetS. SHAP analysis identified education, marital status, and gender as the top three contributors, underscoring the significant influence of social determinants—a finding consistent with prior cross-sectional studies emphasizing their dominant role in depressive symptoms [36].

This study has several notable strengths. First, to our knowledge, it is one of the few investigations specifically examining the relationship between the OBS and depressive symptoms in participants with MetS. Second, the study was based on a large, nationally representative dataset from the NHANES, which enhances the generalizability and external validity of the findings to the U.S. adults population. Third, rigorous statistical approaches were employed, including adjustment for potential confounding variables, RCS regression to explore potential nonlinear associations, and subgroup analyses to evaluate the consistency of the relationship between OBS and depressive symptoms in MetS participants across various strata.

However, our study also has several limitations. First, due to the cross-sectional study design, we were unable to establish a causal relationship between OBS and depressive symptoms in participants with MetS. Therefore, large-scale randomized controlled trials are needed to better understand the causal relationships between these variables. Second, the dietary data in this study were based on self-reported recall. Although we reduced random error by averaging data from two surveys, reliance on self-reported dietary intake still makes it difficult to completely avoid recall bias, which could introduce information bias. Additionally, missing dietary data may lead to an underestimation of the OBS. Third, although we employed repeated cross-validation for internal validation rather than external validation on an independent cohort, cross-validation remains a statistically rigorous method for performance estimation. However, the absence of an external test set limits the generalizability of our findings to broader populations or different datasets. Moreover, the observed class imbalance in the dataset, while mitigated through resampling techniques (such as SMOTE) within the cross-validation framework to reduce bias, may still influence model sensitivity, particularly in detecting the minority class (depressive symptoms with MetS). These factors should be considered when interpreting the predictive performance of the models and their potential clinical applicability. Additionally, model calibration was not assessed in this study. While AUC-ROC values indicate good discriminative performance, the reliability of predicted probabilities remains unevaluated and should be examined in future work. Finally, because the study sample mainly consisted of U.S. adults, the generalizability of our findings to other populations may be limited.

Conclusion

This study found that higher OBS in participants with MetS was associated with reduced odds of depressive symptoms. Machine learning analyses further highlighted the important role of social determinants in the development of comorbid depressive symptoms among adults with MetS. However, depressive symptoms in the context of MetS is a multifactorial condition. Therefore, comprehensive and personalized approaches are needed to address the underlying complexity of this comorbidity.

Since this study is based on a secondary analysis of publicly available anonymized NHANES data, no additional ethical approval was required.
