Effects of football training on patients with hypertension: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Liu Wangyang, Jiang Fangfang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHypertension and metabolic disorders remain major global health burdens, yet the specific benefits of football training for adults with these conditions are not fully characterized. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the effects of recreational football on cardiovascular, metabolic, and fitness outcomes, addressing current gaps in evidence.MethodsWe systematically reviewed randomized and matched controlled trials published in English and Chinese, including 20 studies and 1,225 adult participants. Primary outcomes were blood pressure, heart rate, body composition, peak oxygen uptake (VO₂max), muscle strength, and cardiometabolic biomarkers. Random-effects models were used for pooled analyses, with subgroup and sensitivity analyses. This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered in the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD420251143230).ResultsFootball training significantly reduced systolic blood pressure (MD = − 6.67 mmHg, 95% CI: − 8.95 to − 4.39; p < 0.00001), resting heart rate (SMD = − 2.26, 95% CI: − 3.71 to − 0.81; p = 0.002), and diastolic pressure in both normotensive (SMD = − 2.44, 95% CI: − 3.41 to − 1.47) and hypertensive groups (SMD = − 0.92, 95% CI: − 1.40 to − 0.43). VO₂max improved (SMD = 3.96, 95% CI: 1.62 to 6.31; p = 0.0009), as did ejection fraction, E/A ratio, and LV mass. Benefits were observed for LDL (SMD = − 1.64), triglycerides (SMD = − 3.42), total cholesterol (SMD = − 2.44), and HDL (SMD = 2.91). Body fat mass declined in males (SMD = − 0.74), muscle strength increased (SMD = 2.14), and insulin, HbA1c, and FGF-21 improved significantly.ConclusionRecreational football is an effective intervention for improving cardiovascular and metabolic health in adults, particularly those with hypertension. These findings highlight football training as a practical adjunct to conventional exercise therapy.Keywords: Body Composition, Exercise Therapy, Heart Rate, Hypertension, Physical Fitness, VO₂max

Introduction

Hypertension, operationally defined as a persistent elevation in arterial blood pressure above 140/90 mmHg, remains the foremost modifiable risk factor for cardiovascular morbidity and premature mortality globally [10, 11, 54]. Epidemiological estimates suggest over 1.3 billion adults are affected by hypertension, with the disease burden disproportionately increasing in low- and middle-income nations driven by urbanisation, nutritional transitions, and sedentary lifestyles [10, 47]. Suboptimal blood pressure control is linked not only to elevated risks of myocardial infarction, stroke, and chronic kidney disease, but also to reduced quality of life and heightened healthcare expenditures [8, 9, 16]. In light of these escalating public health costs, current international guidelines endorse lifestyle modification, including physical activity, as a foundational, non-pharmacological strategy for both the prevention and management of hypertension [16, 38, 43].

Among the spectrum of exercise interventions, recreational football (soccer) training has emerged as a particularly promising, multidimensional modality for cardiometabolic health promotion [20, 40]. Unlike conventional aerobic protocols such as walking or cycling, football training integrates continuous and intermittent high-intensity activities, such as sprinting, cutting, dribbling, and rapid positional shifts, within the context of small-sided, team-based play. This complex physiological stimulus simultaneously targets aerobic and anaerobic energy systems, promotes neuromuscular adaptation, and is often associated with higher participant enjoyment and adherence due to its engaging, socially interactive structure [23, 34]. Several mechanistic hypotheses support football’s unique efficacy: the repeated bursts of high-intensity activity enhance endothelial function and arterial compliance, while the varied motor demands improve muscle strength and coordination [34, 40]. Furthermore, the inherently social and competitive elements of football foster motivation, persistence, and long-term exercise adherence factors linked to sustained blood pressure control [13, 34]. High-risk men have been shown to engage more effectively with football club–based programs, highlighting the motivational potential of football as a vehicle for health promotion [21].

A growing body of RCTs and systematic reviews supports the role of football in improving cardiovascular risk profiles, yet critical uncertainties persist regarding its efficacy for blood pressure reduction relative to other exercise modalities [51, 55]. Zhu et al. [55] demonstrated that football interventions led to significantly greater reductions in both systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to yoga or walking. However, their analysis revealed considerable heterogeneity among included trials. Teixeira et al. [51] further emphasised the importance of rigorous meta-analytic methods for accounting for this heterogeneity, recommending subgroup and meta-regression analyses to clarify sources of between-study variation. Complementary findings by Li et al. [30] and Deng et al. [15] suggest that high-intensity interval training (HIIT) and continuous aerobic exercise improve vascular function and autonomic regulation. Still, the evidence base remains limited by a predominant focus on surrogate outcomes (e.g., heart rate variability) and a lack of direct head-to-head comparisons with football training. Notably, meta-analyses by de Barcelos et al. [14] identified age and intervention duration as significant moderators of aerobic exercise efficacy, underscoring the importance of individualised, context-specific intervention design.

Additional research has interrogated the specificity of football training’s impact, with studies such as Clemente et al. [12], Schrader et al. [45], and Mohr et al. [37] demonstrating large effect sizes for blood pressure and metabolic markers, but simultaneously reporting high statistical heterogeneity (I2 > 70%), small sample sizes, and gender-restricted samples, limiting external validity. For instance, Mohr et al. [33] documented marked benefits of football training in elderly populations but highlighted a need for greater cultural and ethnic diversity in trial populations. Krustrup et al. [28] reported significant improvements in blood pressure among middle-aged hypertensive men following a 24-week intervention, yet also noted challenges with participant adherence and retention, common issues in real-world implementation.

The broader exercise science literature consistently supports exercise-induced blood pressure reductions. Hao [19] and Luo et al. [31] documented substantial systolic and diastolic blood pressure drops following structured exercise, but the intervention types were not always specified or standardised. Abdel Fattah et al. [1] reported rapid improvements with walking but encountered methodological flaws affecting reliability. Pedralli et al. [39] linked aerobic training to improved endothelial function but noted substantial between-study heterogeneity and inconsistent outcome measures. Football training, in contrast, may offer both greater physiological stimulus and stronger social reinforcement, though robust meta-analytic synthesis has been lacking.

Despite encouraging findings, several knowledge gaps limit the translation of current evidence into clinical and policy recommendations. First, most meta-analyses do not adequately explore the sources and impact of heterogeneity, such as differences in intervention intensity, participant characteristics (age, sex, comorbidities), or comparator group protocols [14, 51]. Second, studies often focus on short-term interventions with limited follow-up, and few stratify results by gender or baseline hypertension status. Third, theoretical models integrating physiological, behavioural, and social mechanisms are underutilised, hindering understanding of how football training exerts its effects. Finally, the literature rarely addresses why football should be prioritised over more accessible, lower-intensity activities such as walking or cycling,this study argues that football’s unique combination of physiological demands and high adherence potential may offer additive benefits [23, 34].

Objectives

The present systematic review and meta-analysis aims to (1) estimate the pooled effects of football training on systolic and diastolic blood pressure in adults with hypertension; (2) quantify statistical heterogeneity and its moderators across included trials; and (3) explore the relative influence of intervention duration, intensity, participant demographics, and study design using subgroup and meta-regression analyses. By addressing these aims, this study seeks to clarify the clinical value of football training as a targeted intervention for blood pressure management and to advance the conceptual understanding of exercise–hypertension relationships.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and adhered to the 2020 PRISMA guidelines. This systematic review and meta-analysis were registered in the PROSPERO international prospective register of systematic reviews (CRD420251143230).

Information sources and search strategy

We systematically searched six databases: PubMed, Web of Science, Scopus, SPORTDiscus, CENTRAL, and CINAHL from inception through February 2025. The search terms were structured using Boolean logic as follows: (“football” OR “soccer” OR “recreational soccer” OR “small-sided games”) AND (“hypertension” OR “blood pressure”) AND (“exercise” OR “training”) AND (“controlled trial” OR “RCT”). Reference lists of eligible studies and prior reviews were manually screened to identify additional sources. The search was not restricted by age, allowing the inclusion of adolescent and pediatric populations, provided the intervention met the design and outcome criteria.

Study selection process

Following de-duplication in EndNote X9 (EndNote Software, USA), two reviewers independently screened (i) titles and abstracts and (ii) full-text articles for eligibility. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or consultation with a third reviewer. Agreement was quantified using Cohen’s κ, which yielded 0.91 for title/abstract screening and 0.89 for full-text screening. Full-text authors were contacted for clarification where data were missing or eligibility was ambiguous. A detailed PRISMA flow diagram illustrating the selection process is provided in Fig. 1.

PRISMA Flowchart of Identification of Studies

Data extraction

Two reviewers independently extracted data in accordance with Cochrane guidelines. Each eligible study was reviewed for the following items: (i) study identification details including author, year, country, and design; (ii) participant characteristics including age, sex, sample size, and baseline blood pressure status; (iii) intervention components including football type, session frequency, duration, intensity, heart rate zones, and field structure; (iv) control group description including passive, usual-care, or alternative-exercise comparators; and (v) outcome variables including mean and standard deviation for pre- and post-intervention values. Additional data were recorded on adherence levels, medication modifications, dropout rates, and adverse events. Where data were available only in graphical form, values were extracted using DigitizeIt (v2.5). If standard deviations were missing, they were back-calculated from standard errors, confidence intervals, or p-values; a pre-post correlation of r = 0.5 was assumed in accordance with Bacon et al. [7].

Risk of bias and quality assessment

The risk of bias of included randomised controlled trials was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias 2 (RoB 2) tool, following Cochrane Handbook guidelines [49]. Two reviewers independently evaluated risk of bias, achieving interrater agreement of κ = 0.94. Additionally, we used the Physiotherapy Evidence Database (PEDro) scale to assess methodological quality across 11 criteria, assigning each study a total score ranging from 0 to 10. Studies scoring ≥ 6 were considered high quality. Two reviewers independently evaluated risk of bias, achieving interrater agreement of κ = 0.94. To assess the overall certainty of the evidence base, we applied the GRADE approach, accounting for study limitations, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias across outcomes.

Data synthesis

Meta-analyses were conducted using the random-effects model (DerSimonian and Laird) in RevMan (Cochrane Collaboration). Effect sizes were expressed as (i) standardised mean differences (SMD) for outcomes measured on different scales and (ii) mean differences (MD) for outcomes measured on uniform units, each with 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was quantified using I2 statistics, τ2 estimates, and Cochran’s Q test, with I2 interpreted as low (< 25%), moderate (25–75%), or high (> 75%). Publication bias was evaluated visually using funnel plots and statistically via Egger’s test (p < 0.10). Where evidence of asymmetry was present, the trim-and-fill procedure was applied. Prespecified moderators for subgroup analyses included: (i) participant sex; (ii) age group classified as children (10–12), adolescents (13–17), or adults (≥ 18); (iii) intervention duration (≤ 12 vs > 12 weeks); (iv) training frequency (≤ 2 vs > 2 sessions per week); and (v) baseline systolic blood pressure (hypertensive vs normotensive). Meta-regression was performed to identify contributors to heterogeneity. Effect magnitudes were interpreted as trivial (< 0.2), small (0.2–0.6), moderate (0.6–1.2), large (1.2–2.0), very large (2.0–4.0), and extremely large (> 4.0).

The initial database search yielded a total of 875 records: PubMed (n = 212), Web of Science (n = 185), Scopus (n = 198), SPORTDiscus (n = 124), CENTRAL (n = 92), and CINAHL (n = 64). After de-duplication and title/abstract screening, 69 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility (see Fig. 1). Ultimately, 20 studies met our inclusion criteria and were used in the analyses. The included studies (Table 3) covered diverse populations, from untrained adults and the elderly to individuals with type 2 diabetes, hypertension, prostate cancer, or obesity. Most participants were male, with studies [3, 5, 6, 18, 22, 26, 27, 52, 53] focusing on males, while others [8, 9, 24, 25, 28, 35] targeted hypertensive or sedentary women. Ages ranged from adolescents [18, 46] to those aged 65–75 [44]. Sundstrup et al. [50] and Randers et al. [42] included socially vulnerable groups.

Football-based interventions typically involved small-sided games (4v4–6v6) or recreational football, conducted 2–3 times weekly for 30–60 min. Intensity ranged from 78%–85% HRmax, with some exceeding 90% [26, 35]. Durations varied from 8 weeks [18] to 52 weeks [28, 44]. Controls usually received no structured activity, though some studies used active comparators. These protocols consistently improved blood pressure, body composition, VO₂max, and mobility (e.g., CMJ, 1RM), highlighting football’s broad health benefits across diverse ages and clinical populations.

Description of interventions used

The interventions across the included studies varied in format, frequency, and intensity but predominantly centred on structured recreational football or small-sided games (SSG). Hammami et al. [18] implemented a 12-week program consisting of two weekly sessions comprising four 6-min SSG bouts with 3-min passive rest intervals, targeting 80–90% of maximum heart rate (HRmax). Seabra (2012) designed a 10-week protocol involving two 60-min sessions per week that combined skill-based drills and 5v5 competitive games conducted at moderate-to-high intensity (65–85% HRmax). Milanović et al., [32] applied a 12-week intervention consisting of three weekly 60-min football sessions organised into interval-based SSG interspersed with technical exercises. Krustrup et al. [27] emphasised a community club-based approach with twice-weekly training sessions lasting 45–60 min over 24 weeks, progressing from 3v3 to 7v7 formats and consistently maintaining heart rates above 80% HRmax. Other studies (e.g., [44], Mohr et al., [36]) incorporated structured warm-ups, dynamic movement drills, match simulations, and cool-down phases, with many protocols employing telemetry to monitor cardiovascular load. Although program durations ranged from 8 to 24 weeks, nearly all ensured progressive overload, peer interaction, and football-specific stimulus conducive to improving cardiovascular health. Reported attendance across studies exceeded 75%, supporting the acceptability and feasibility of these interventions in untrained or sedentary populations.

Subgroup stratification and participant characteristics

The included studies exhibited broad variability in participant demographics, with reported ages ranging from 10.0 to 72.5 years. This necessitated a redefinition of population eligibility to encompass adolescents through older adults. Seabra (2012) examined obese boys aged 10.0–10.7 years, while Hammami et al. [18] studied adolescent males around 16 years old. In contrast, Krustrup et al. [27] and Milanović et al. [32] recruited hypertensive adults aged between 40 and 70 years. Most studies included predominantly male participants, although three trials (e.g., [44]) enrolled mixed-gender cohorts. In terms of health status, several trials incorporated individuals with comorbidities such as obesity, prehypertension, metabolic syndrome, or type 2 diabetes, whereas others focused exclusively on normotensive sedentary populations. To manage this heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were pre-specified according to age category (≤ 18, 19–45, 46–65, > 65 years), sex, and presence of comorbid conditions. This stratification framework enabled clearer interpretation of training effects across different subpopulations, offering insights into differential blood pressure responses and adaptation trajectories. The broad inclusion criteria further enhanced ecological validity and the translational relevance of the pooled findings to real-world primary prevention settings.

Risk of bias

Overall risk of bias was moderate (Fig. 3). Most studies showed low risk for randomisation and outcome assessment, but had high or unclear risk for allocation concealment and participant blinding. Selective reporting and attrition bias were present in some studies. While key domains were generally sound, potential biases were considered when interpreting pooled effect estimates.

Risk of bias assessment for individual studies across all domains

Overall, most studies demonstrated low risk of bias across the assessed domains (Fig. 2). However, some concerns were identified, particularly in allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, and selective reporting, where several studies were judged to have unclear or high risk of bias (Fig. 3).

Effects on resting heart rate

Our meta-analysis, involving 164 participants, revealed that football interventions significantly reduced resting heart rate (RHR), with a pooled standardised mean difference of − 2.26 bpm (95% CI: − 3.71 to − 0.81; Z = 3.05, p = 0.002), indicating a beneficial effect (see Fig. 4). However, substantial heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 92%), likely reflecting variation in study populations and intervention protocols.

Forest plot of the effect of football interventions on resting heart rate (RHR) compared to control groups

Effects on maximal oxygen uptake (vo₂max)

Five studies (n = 142) demonstrated a significant improvement in VO₂max following football training, with a pooled effect size of SMD = 3.96 [1.62, 6.31]; p = 0.0009 (see Fig. 5). Heterogeneity was high (I2 = 94%), reflecting substantial variability across study populations and intervention protocols. These results suggest that football training leads to a measurable increase in VO₂max.

Effects on left ventricular mass/function (echocardiography)

The pooled analysis (n = 345) showed significant improvements in cardiac function following football training (see Fig. 6). Ejection fraction improved (SMD = 2.41 [1.16, 3.66], I2 = 84%), as did the E/A ratio (SMD = 2.88 [0.86, 4.90], I2 = 92%). Left ventricular mass index showed a smaller but significant effect (SMD = 0.46 [0.09, 0.83], I2 = 0%). The overall pooled effect was significant (SMD = 1.87 [1.02, 2.71], p < 0.0001; I2 = 90%). These findings indicate that football training leads to measurable improvements in key indicators of cardiac function.

Forest plot of the effect of football training versus control on left ventricular (LV) function indicators ejection fraction (%), E/A ratio, and left ventricular mass index (g/m.2)

The analysis (n = 320) examined 10 comparisons of metabolic outcomes (see Fig. 7). Football training significantly reduced LDL (SMD = − 1.64 [− 2.11, − 1.18], p < 0.00001; I2 = 0%). This indicates a consistent beneficial effect on lowering LDL across studies. HDL was significantly increased (SMD = 2.91 [0.98, 4.84], p = 0.003; I2 = 87%). This suggests football training improves protective lipoprotein concentrations, though heterogeneity was high. Triglycerides were significantly reduced (SMD = − 3.42 [− 4.40, − 2.44], p < 0.00001; I2 = 40%). The consistent negative effect reflects robust evidence for triglyceride lowering.

Forest plot for changes in metabolic outcomes (LDL, HDL, triglycerides, and total cholesterol) following football training compared to control. Lipid outcomes: negative SMD favours football for LDL/TG/total cholesterol, positive SMD favours football for HDL

Total cholesterol was significantly reduced (SMD = − 2.44 [− 3.41, − 1.47], p < 0.00001; I2 = 57%). This demonstrates an apparent reduction in overall cholesterol levels. The overall pooled effect was non-significant (SMD = − 1.20 [− 2.83, 0.44], p = 0.15; I2 = 97%) with significant subgroup differences (I2 = 91.5%). This suggests that while individual lipid parameters improved, high variability across studies limits the generalizability of the pooled metabolic effect.

Effects on body fat mass

Body fat mass was analysed across six studies (n = 228). In males (k = 3, n = 59), football training significantly reduced body fat (SMD = − 0.74 [− 1.17, − 0.30], I2 = 0%, Z = 3.34, p = 0.0008), indicating a consistent effect (see Fig. 8). In females (k = 3, n = 69), the reduction was smaller and non-significant (SMD = − 0.25 [− 0.95, 0.44], I2 = 73%, Z = 0.71, p = 0.48), reflecting heterogeneity and variability between studies. The overall pooled analysis showed a significant reduction in body fat mass (SMD = − 0.48 [− 0.92, − 0.05], I2 = 59%, Z = 2.20, p = 0.03). Subgroup differences were not significant (Chi2 = 1.34, p = 0.25, I2 = 25.5%), suggesting consistent overall benefits.

Isometric muscle strength

The analysis (football n = 81, control n = 74) evaluated isometric strength across the quadriceps and hamstrings (see Fig. 9). For quadriceps, football training produced a significant improvement (SMD = 2.82 [1.33, 4.32], Z = 3.70, p = 0.0002; I2 = 82%), though with substantial heterogeneity. For hamstrings, the pooled effect was smaller and borderline significant (SMD = 1.48 [− 0.01, 2.97], Z = 1.94, p = 0.05; I2 = 81%), indicating variability across studies. The overall pooled effect demonstrated a significant improvement in isometric muscle strength in favour of football (SMD = 2.14 [1.21, 3.07], Z = 4.50, p < 0.00001; I2 = 78%). Subgroup differences were not significant (Chi2 = 1.55, p = 0.21; I2 = 35.5%), suggesting consistent benefits across both quadriceps and hamstrings.

Subgroup analyses

Intervention-based subgroup analyses demonstrated consistent blood pressure reductions across all formats, with varying effect magnitudes (see Table 4). Recreational football overall yielded a significant pooled effect (SMD = − 1.84), while small-sided formats (≤ 5v5) showed an even larger reduction with moderate heterogeneity (I2 = 66%). Community club interventions had a slightly lower effect size (SMD = − 1.28) but remained statistically significant. Interventions lasting more than 12 weeks and those delivered more than twice per week produced notably greater reductions (SMDs = − 2.03 and − 2.21, respectively), highlighting the dose–response relationship between exposure duration/frequency and cardiovascular benefit.

Publication bias and sensitivity analyses

Publication bias showed moderate asymmetry, with one study far to the right of the mean effect and others unevenly scattered. Egger’s test showed no significant asymmetry (p > 0.05), indicating no strong evidence of bias. The trim-and-fill method imputed no studies, and the pooled effect remained stable. Sensitivity analyses showed consistent results when excluding individual studies, suggesting that the intervention effects were robust and not driven by bias or outliers.

GRADE summary showed overall evidence quality for outcomes in the football training meta-analysis. Four outcomes were rated high-quality, indicating strong confidence in effects on aerobic capacity, muscular strength, and body composition. Five were rated moderate-quality, limited by imprecision or inconsistency. Four outcomes had low-quality ratings due to high heterogeneity or small samples, and one was rated very low-quality, reflecting limited confidence in the effect estimate.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to evaluate the cardiometabolic, hemodynamic, and neuromuscular effects of recreational football training across diverse populations, with particular focus on statistical heterogeneity and subgroup-specific responses. The pooled findings demonstrated significant reductions in resting heart rate, cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength, and glycemic regulation. Subgroup analyses revealed that greater blood pressure reductions were observed in participants aged 46–65 years, those training more than twice per week, and individuals with existing comorbidities such as obesity, metabolic syndrome, or type 2 diabetes. Despite the presence of high heterogeneity, the consistency of large effect sizes across most intervention types and subpopulations affirms the robustness of the evidence. Collectively, these findings support recreational football as a viable, socially engaging intervention for non-pharmacological prevention and management of cardiovascular and metabolic disorders.

Recreational football training resulted in a clinically significant improvement in blood pressure, particularly among individuals with mild hypertension, with mean reductions of − 6.67 mmHg in systolic and − 1.20 mmHg in diastolic values (both p < 0.001). These magnitudes are comparable to, and in some cases exceed, reductions typically achieved through monotherapy with antihypertensive agents, thereby reinforcing the role of exercise as a first-line intervention. Notably, subgroup findings revealed that the most pronounced SBP decreases occurred in participants aged 46–65 years and those engaging in more than two sessions per week, suggesting a dose–response gradient in line with prior aerobic training literature [39]. Conversely, normotensive individuals exhibited minor SBP elevations, potentially indicating physiological autoregulation rather than adverse response, and underscoring the need for personalised programming. Heart rate reductions were also significant (SMD = − 2.26, p = 0.002), consistent with findings by Mohr et al. [35] and Krustrup et al. [26], which documented 6–8 bpm decreases following 12–16 weeks of training. These effects likely reflect enhanced vagal tone and autonomic balance induced by the intermittent cardiovascular stress inherent in small-sided games. Such adaptations are particularly beneficial in older or comorbid populations, where improved autonomic function may reduce cardiovascular event risk and contribute to mortality attenuation.

Body composition changes following recreational football training were heterogeneous across sexes and outcome domains. While mean body weight change was not statistically significant (− 0.06 kg, p = 0.83), fat mass declined markedly in male participants (SMD = − 0.74, p = 0.0008), with a smaller but still significant overall reduction (SMD = − 0.48, p = 0.03), likely reflecting the high-intensity intermittent format of sessions sustained above 80% of maximum heart rate. Lean body mass increased modestly in males (SMD = − 0.38, p = 0.05). Still, this effect was not observed in females, possibly due to differences in baseline muscle mass, anabolic hormone levels, or training response. These sex-specific trends emphasise the need to stratify body composition results when assessing exercise efficacy. In terms of glycemic outcomes, the training elicited significant reductions in glucose (SMD = − 0.73, p = 0.02), insulin (SMD = − 2.03, p < 0.001), and glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c: SMD = − 1.39, p < 0.001), indicating improved insulin sensitivity and long-term glucose regulation. These effects corroborate prior findings by Andersen, T. R., Schmidt, J. F., Thomassen, M., et al. [6], who reported concurrent VO₂peak and fat mass gains in individuals with type 2 diabetes. Additionally, fibroblast growth factor 21 (FGF-21), a hepatokine linked with metabolic stress, significantly decreased (SMD = − 2.01, p < 0.001), suggesting broader systemic improvements. By contrast, no meaningful changes were detected for endothelin-1 or oxidised LDL, potentially due to short intervention durations or baseline variability. Collectively, these results support football training as an effective modality for cardiometabolic modulation, particularly in overweight or metabolically at-risk groups.

Recreational football training was associated with a marked improvement in cardiorespiratory fitness, as evidenced by a large effect size for VO₂max (SMD = 3.96, p = 0.008). This magnitude aligns with increases of 10–15% reported in previous controlled trials [17, 29, 35, 44] following 12–16 weeks of training. Physiologically, the intermittent high-intensity nature of football likely stimulates central and peripheral adaptations, including enhanced stroke volume, mitochondrial enzyme density, and capillary perfusion. These benefits may accrue more efficiently than with continuous aerobic training and are particularly advantageous for populations with limited capacity or time, such as older adults or individuals with chronic conditions. In parallel, neuromuscular outcomes showed substantial gains, with significant improvements in countermovement jump performance (SMD = 0.85, p < 0.001) and isometric muscle strength (SMD = 2.36, p = 0.002), replicating effects observed in earlier studies of older cohorts [32, 48, 50, 51]. The eccentric loading, rapid directional shifts, and frequent decelerations inherent to small-sided football likely activate type II muscle fibres, enhancing both explosive strength and endurance. These adaptations are functionally relevant for reducing fall risk and maintaining mobility in ageing populations. However, the observed heterogeneity (I2 > 80%) suggests variability in exercise dose, participant fitness level, and intervention standardisation across studies, warranting caution in extrapolating strength outcomes to broader populations.

Football training induced notable improvements in cardiac morphology and function, reflected by significant increases in ejection fraction (SMD = 2.41, p = 0.002) and E/A ratio (SMD = 2.88, p < 0.00001). These results are consistent with prior studies [2, 4, 48], supporting the capacity of regular football training to elicit favourable myocardial remodelling across sex and age strata. Mechanistically, repeated elevation in cardiac preload and afterload during play likely stimulates myocardial hypertrophy and diastolic function improvement, leading to enhanced stroke volume and contractility. Despite the high heterogeneity observed, these structural adaptations suggest that football offers cardioprotective benefits comparable to endurance training modalities. Pulmonary improvements were more selective,maximal voluntary ventilation (MVV) improved significantly (SMD = 1.31, p < 0.0001), while static lung function markers, such as FEV₁ and FVC, remained unchanged. This pattern aligns with findings from previous interventions [24, 25, 31, 46], suggesting enhanced respiratory muscle endurance and thoracic compliance rather than lung volume changes. These adaptations may support improved exertional capacity, especially in sedentary or elderly adults with limited baseline reserve. Inflammatory and endothelial biomarkers revealed mixed outcomes. ICAM-1 declined significantly (SMD = 0.76, p = 0.02), indicating improved endothelial health, while CRP remained statistically unchanged (SMD = 0.47, p = 0.13). This divergence may reflect short intervention durations, limited inflammatory load at baseline, or inter-individual metabolic variability. Nonetheless, the observed endothelial enhancement supports mechanisms involving increased nitric oxide bioavailability and vascular compliance, reinforcing the plausibility of observed blood pressure reductions.

Subgroup analyses revealed that blood pressure and cardiorespiratory gains varied substantially by intervention format, duration, and participant characteristics (see Tables 4 and 5). Larger systolic reductions were observed with training durations exceeding 12 weeks (SMD = − 2.03, I2 = 83%) and higher session frequency (> 2 sessions/week, SMD = − 2.21), suggesting a dose-dependent benefit linked to sustained cardiovascular loading and autonomic adaptation cycles. Similar findings were reported by Mohr et al. [35], who used a 16-week randomised trial to demonstrate greater SBP and VO₂max improvements in longer interventions among hypertensive men. Small-sided formats (≤ 5v5) also yielded robust effects (SMD = − 2.11), consistent with physiological profiles reported by Randers et al. [41], who observed heart rate > 85% HRmax for > 60% of match time in 4v4 games, promoting cardiovascular overload. Age-stratified analyses identified the 46–65 age group as showing the strongest blood pressure response (SMD = − 2.12), in line with findings by Krustrup et al. [27], who noted maximal VO₂ and BP benefits in mid-aged untrained men. Males exhibited greater reductions than mixed-gender cohorts, possibly due to higher baseline risk or sex-specific cardiovascular responsiveness, as supported by Milanović et al. [32], who reported greater fat loss and aerobic gains in male cohorts. Participants with comorbidities experienced stronger improvements (SMD = − 2.11 vs. −1.37), consistent with studies like Andersen, T. R., Schmidt, J. F., Nielsen, J. J., et al. [5], which found amplified VO₂peak and glycemic responses in diabetic men. These subgroup-specific patterns suggest that heterogeneity reflects meaningful biological and contextual differences rather than inconsistency, affirming the generalizability and clinical utility of recreational football across populations.

Theoretical and practical implications

The observed benefits of recreational football align with established physiological frameworks and reinforce its translational potential as a multicomponent exercise modality. The reductions in blood pressure and improvements in autonomic function support the application of the baroreflex resetting theory, where repeated moderate-to-high intensity bouts recalibrate cardiovascular control mechanisms, enhancing parasympathetic tone and reducing sympathetic drive. This mechanism is particularly relevant in hypertensive or ageing populations with impaired autonomic regulation. Concurrently, the significant increases in VO₂max, MVV, and cardiac output parameters (ejection fraction and E/A ratio) reflect adaptations consistent with the central cardiovascular training model, in which stroke volume, left ventricular mass, and diastolic filling improve with progressive endurance loading. The neuromuscular gains observed, especially in CMJ and isometric strength, are best explained by the neuromuscular overload and functional adaptation model, wherein eccentric and multidirectional movements stimulate muscle recruitment and joint stabilisation beyond what is achieved in linear aerobic modalities. Clinically, these combined adaptations suggest that football-based interventions offer not only aerobic conditioning but also strength and functional mobility benefits, meeting multiple rehabilitation and public health goals simultaneously. Compared to traditional gym-based or structured aerobic programs, the social, competitive, and gamified elements of football may enhance long-term adherence, addressing a common barrier in lifestyle interventions. These findings support the inclusion of recreational football in exercise prescriptions for hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and age-related functional decline, particularly in resource-limited settings where infrastructure for supervised training is scarce but informal play is accessible.

Limitations and future research

A key limitation of this meta-analysis is the limited representation of female participants, which restricts sex-specific inferences regarding physiological adaptation and training responsiveness. Additionally, very few studies incorporated objective fidelity measures such as heart rate telemetry or attendance logs to verify training intensity and compliance, limiting mechanistic interpretation. Future research should prioritise sex-balanced recruitment, incorporate fidelity monitoring using wearable technologies, and evaluate longitudinal effects beyond the intervention window to assess sustainability. Trials comparing football training with pharmacological or lifestyle interventions using head-to-head designs are also warranted to guide integrated care strategies for cardiometabolic risk reduction.

Conclusion

This meta-analysis provides robust evidence that recreational football training offers a comprehensive, multimodal intervention with substantial cardiovascular, metabolic, and neuromuscular benefits. Beyond physiological improvement, the inherently social, engaging, and adaptable nature of football enhances participant motivation and long-term adherence factors often lacking in traditional exercise prescriptions. Its capacity to accommodate diverse age groups and comorbidity profiles positions football as a valuable tool in preventive and rehabilitative care, particularly in community and primary healthcare settings. Moreover, its alignment with global physical activity promotion goals supports its integration into public health policies targeting noncommunicable diseases. Future clinical trials should explore implementation models that ensure accessibility, monitor safety in high-risk individuals, and examine how training variations affect outcomes across different demographic strata. Football should be considered not merely as a sport, but as a structured health intervention capable of addressing pressing global health challenges.
