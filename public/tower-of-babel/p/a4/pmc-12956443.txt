Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy in a Patient With Miliary Tuberculosis: Differential Diagnosis From Tuberculoma
Authors: Jong-Gyu Baek, Hee Jung Kwon, Hyun Min Chae, Doo Hyuk Kwon
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

The human-specific pathogen John Cunningham polyomavirus (JCV) is an established etiological agent of progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML), a frequently fatal demyelinating disease of the brain.1 Recently, the spectrum of conditions predisposing to PML has expanded, with rising reports among patients receiving immunomodulatory therapies for autoimmune diseases as well as among those with hematologic malignancies, organ transplantation, and other chronic immunosuppressive states.123 In PML, JCV replication causes the lytic injury of oligodendrocytes and induces astrocytic changes, resulting in gradual and widespread degeneration of white matter in the central nervous system. Infection of oligodendrocytes, the main targets of JCV, generally results in productive viral replication. These infected oligodendrocytes show nuclear enlargement with basophilic intranuclear inclusions, which lead to impaired myelin synthesis and eventual extensive white matter demyelination.1 However, astrocytes infected by JCV show a more variable pattern.2 Some cells contain late viral genes and are subsequently destroyed, whereas others do not support complete replication and instead develop atypical transformation-like features.3 Infected astrocytes often show notable changes, commonly exhibiting a varied morphology with unusually enlarged nuclei and bizarre forms. PML produces multifocal areas of demyelination, accompanied by reactive gliosis and giant, atypical multinucleated astrocytes within the affected regions. Clinical symptoms, including motor deficits, visual disturbances, cognitive impairment, altered mental status, and personality changes, vary with the site of demyelination. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) is the optimal modality for diagnosing PML. On T2-weighted and fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) MRI sequences, classical PML lesions show hyperintense signals and are asymmetric, lack contrast enhancement or mass effect, and typically spare the periventricular white matter.456 However, atypical features such as ring-enhancing or tumefactive lesions have also been observed, particularly in immune reconstitution inflammatory syndrome, thereby complicating the differential diagnosis due to overlap with infectious or neoplastic pathologies.678

Here, we encountered an 85-year-old man who had been receiving antituberculous therapy for 1 month and had recently been diagnosed with miliary tuberculosis confirmed through chest computed tomography (Fig. 1A) and antituberculous tests. The patient had a medical history of ischemic heart disease, dyslipidemia, and hypertension. He presented with progressive dysarthria, left facial weakness, and hemiparesis. Brain MRI revealed a lesion located in the right superior frontal gyrus and centrum semiovale, exhibiting hyperintensity on T2-weighted and FLAIR sequences but no significant enhancement on contrast-enhanced T1 images (Fig. 1B-D). Based on the suspicion of active tuberculosis and the imaging characteristics, we assessed for both tuberculoma and PML. Consequently, surgical removal with biopsy was conducted to obtain a definitive diagnosis. During the hospitalization, several serum laboratory tests were also conducted, along with cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) studies including JCV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test. CSF analysis demonstrated normal opening pressure, cell count, and cytology and no oligoclonal bands. The JCV PCR test of the CSF yielded a positive result. No other infections associated with HIV, bacteria, or autoimmune causes, such as aquaporin-4 and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein, were identified. Histopathological findings revealed atypical and bizarre astrocytes (Fig. 1E, green arrows), macrophage infiltration (Fig. 1E), and enlarged oligodendroglial nuclei (Fig. 1F, blue arrow) but no granulomatous inflammation. Immunohistochemistry confirmed SV40 positivity (Fig. 1G), which reflects cross-reactivity with the JC virus T-antigen and serves as a surrogate marker of JCV infection, supporting the diagnosis of PML. The diagnostic procedure was complicated by the immunocompromised state resulting from the interplay of advanced age and disseminated tuberculosis. However, a diagnosis of PML was confirmed in the final stages. The patient subsequently received only supportive care during follow-up, with no meaningful neurological improvement observed.

Tuberculomas are typically characterized by enhancing parenchymal lesions often ring-shaped and surrounded by edema, but signal intensities vary based on caseation stage.9 However, intracranial tuberculomas have inconsistent enhancement patterns and may change substantially according to the extent of caseation and lesion evolution.910 Despite the classical nonenhancing nature of PML, it may exhibit atypical enhancement or mass-like characteristics, particularly when immune modulation is present. Therefore, radiological differentiation is rendered unreliable by these overlapping manifestations. Among HIV-negative individuals, PML remains a rare but increasingly recognized complication of chronic immunosuppression, with only sporadic cases reported in association with disseminated tuberculosis. Our case highlights the need for a broad differential diagnosis in immunocompromised patients having atypical or mass-like brain lesions, emphasizing that PML should be considered even in those with active tuberculosis. Finally, establishing early suspicion, integrating clinical context, and confirming the diagnosis through CSF JCV PCR or tissue biopsy are crucial in preventing the misdiagnosis of PML in such patient populations.
