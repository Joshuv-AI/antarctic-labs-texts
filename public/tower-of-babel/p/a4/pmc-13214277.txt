Navigating chylous pericardial effusion: a case study and evidence-based review of management options
Authors: Zheng Yang Donavan Phey, Marco Lizwan, Cynthia Ming Li Chia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractChylopericardium is an uncommon condition characterised by chyle accumulation within the pericardial space, arising idiopathically or secondary to trauma, malignancy, or thoracic surgery. Owing to its rarity, management strategies remain non-standardised. We report a 34-year-old woman with biopsy-proven primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma complicated by superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction who developed progressively enlarging pericardial effusion. Serial echocardiography demonstrated gradual fluid accumulation without initial haemodynamic compromise. High-risk pericardiocentesis was eventually performed, yielding 480 mL of milky fluid with biochemical confirmation of chyle. She was managed conservatively with a low-fat, medium-chain triglyceride diet, subcutaneous octreotide, and oral midodrine. Drain output declined markedly, and the effusion resolved without recurrence. The patient remained well on follow-up. This case underscores the pathophysiologic interplay between SVC obstruction, impaired lymphatic drainage, and chyle accumulation. Chylopericardium remains diagnostically challenging because of its variable presentation and limited evidence base. Our experience supports that appropriately selected patients may achieve resolution with medical management alone, avoiding operative morbidity. Further studies are warranted to refine treatment algorithms and identify predictors for recurrence or surgical intervention. Conservative therapy – particularly dietary modification and pharmacologic reduction of lymphatic flow – can be effective in selected patients. Introduction

Chylopericardium refers to the presence of chyle within the pericardial cavity. Idiopathic cases account for more than half of reported presentations, followed by postoperative and neoplastic etiologies [1]. Although many patients remain asymptomatic, cardiac tamponade develops in up to one-third of cases, and invasive intervention may be required when conservative therapy fails [2]. Given the absence of established guidelines, management is often individualised and influenced by institutional experience and patient-specific factors [3].

We describe a case of chylopericardium secondary to primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma with SVC obstruction, successfully treated using a non-operative multimodal regimen. We also review current therapeutic options and propose a pragmatic, stepwise approach to management based on the available literature.

Case presentation

A 34-year-old woman with newly diagnosed primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma complicated by superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction was undergoing chemotherapy. Her past medical history was notable for a solitary left kidney, possible bicornuate uterus, and thalassemia. A surveillance transthoracic echocardiogram (TTE) initially demonstrated a small pericardial effusion, which was thought to be reactive (Fig. 1A). Serial imaging demonstrated fluctuating effusion size over subsequent months.

B) During admission, when the pericardial effusion was noted to be moderate. C). A few days later, the pericardial effusion is now large, with the largest dimension 30 mm in the subcostal view

Two months later, she developed type I respiratory failure due to Pneumocystis jirovecii pneumonia, during which a small, non-occlusive SVC thrombus was detected, and the patient was started on subcutaneous clexane 40 mg twice daily. Thoracic computed tomography confirmed a small pericardial effusion. Following clinical recovery, repeat imaging demonstrated resolution of the SVC thrombus without progression of the effusion.

A month later, persistent tachycardia prompted repeat TTE, which demonstrated a moderate-to-large pericardial effusion with echographic features of early tamponade, including right ventricular diastolic collapse and significant respiratory variation in mitral inflow, but without inferior vena cava dilatation or hemodynamic compromise (Fig. 1B). Pericardiocentesis was deferred as she remained haemodynamically stable. One week later, repeat TTE demonstrated further effusion enlargement (Fig. 1C), and cardiothoracic consultation was sought.

High-risk image-guided percutaneous pericardiocentesis was performed via a subcostal approach using a standard pericardiocentesis kit with an 8.3 F pigtail catheter. Approximately 480 mL of milky fluid was aspirated (Fig. 2A), and biochemical analysis confirmed chylopericardium.

a) Clinical photograph of milky white output from the pericardial drain that was inserted, biochemical analysis subsequently revealed the diagnosis of chylopericardium. b)Line chart showing the drain output trend with time

She was commenced on a low-fat, medium-chain triglyceride (MCT) diet, subcutaneous octreotide (100 µg three times daily), and oral midodrine (10 mg twice daily). Drain output decreased significantly (Fig. 2B), allowing removal after stabilization. Follow-up TTE demonstrated only minimal residual fluid. She was discharged well and reviewed at 1 and 3 months in clinic. Follow-up TTE showed that the effusion remained small. The patient was given an open-date follow-up.

Discussion

Chylopericardium is a rare clinical entity that may arise spontaneously or secondary to malignancy, trauma, or cardiothoracic surgery [4]. In the present case, mediastinal lymphoma and altered venous hemodynamics likely disrupted lymphatic drainage. Intrinsically, the patient had a small thrombus in the SVC, which did not occlude flow. Extrinsically, the patient also had occlusion of her left brachiocephalic vein; however, there was collateral flow into the SVC via the azygous vein. There were prominent to enlarged mediastinal, supraclavicular, and left axillary nodes however they did not appear to compress on the SVC. Elevated central venous pressure can impede thoracic duct emptying, leading to retrograde chyle leakage into the pericardial space [5]. Lymphatic infiltration by tumor deposits may further compromise flow [6].

Given the rarity of chylopericardium, no universally accepted management algorithm existsl. Treatment decisions should depend on etiology, hemodynamic status, and chyle output. Conservative therapy is appropriate in hemodynamically stable patients, whereas surgical intervention is reserved for refractory, recurrent or high-output cases. Medical management

Dietary therapy forms the foundation of conservative management. Restricting long-chain triglycerides and supplementing MCT reduces chyle production by diverting lipid absorption away from the lymphatic system. Although non-invasive and inexpensive, dietary compliance may be challenging, and prolonged restriction risks nutritional deficiency. Reported success rates approach 60% [16].

Octreotide, a somatostatin analogue, further reduces lymphatic flow by decreasing splanchnic blood flow and gastrointestinal secretions. Doses of 50–200 µg subcutaneously three times daily have achieved resolution in approximately 80% of cases when combined with dietary restriction [7, 8]. Our patient responded favorably to this regimen.

Midodrine, an oral α₁-adrenergic agonist, promotes vasoconstriction within lymphatic channels, reducing chyle leakage [9]. Though evidence is limited to case reports, adjunctive use with octreotide and dietary modification has yielded promising results in selected cases, including ours.

Interventional and surgical strategies

Pericardiocentesis offers diagnostic confirmation and immediate relief in tamponade but carries the risk of recurrence if the lymphatic leak persists. Image-guided drainage was diagnostic and therapeutic in our case.

Pericardial window creation enables continuous drainage into adjacent cavities and is indicated for recurrent or loculated effusions [12, 20]. Pericardiectomy is reserved for refractory or constrictive cases but carries significant morbidity and mortality [10, 13, 14,15].

Thoracic duct ligation provides definitive treatment for high-output or recurrent chylopericardium. When combined with pericardial window creation, recurrence rates are reported below 5% [4].

Radiotherapy or chemotherapy may be beneficial in malignancy-associated cases, particularly for radiosensitive tumors such as lymphoma, where a reduction in mass effect restores lymphatic flow [11, 18, 19].

Management considerations in this case

Surgical intervention was contemplated owing to persistent drainage, but the patient remained asymptomatic without hemodynamic compromise. The decision to continue conservative therapy was supported by her improving lymphoma status, elevated surgical risk due to prior SVC obstruction, and the potential for spontaneous resolution.

Conclusion

Chylopericardium is an infrequent but important cause of pericardial effusion. Diagnosis relies on biochemical confirmation of chyle, and management should be individualized. This case highlights that conservative therapy integrating dietary modification, octreotide, and midodrine can achieve durable resolution in malignancy-associated chylopericardium. A multidisciplinary and stepwise approach remains essential, with surgical intervention reserved for refractory or recurrent disease. Further research is warranted to define standardized treatment pathways and prognostic indicators.
