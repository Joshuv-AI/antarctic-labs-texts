Right Ventricular Outflow Tract Stenting in a Premature Infant With Tetralogy of Fallot
Authors: Connie Choi, Troy Johnston, Agustin Rubio, Brian Morray
Published: 2025

Palliation with right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) stent placement in patients with tetralogy of Fallot (TOF)/pulmonary stenosis (PS) has become an acceptable approach, particularly in premature infants and those with comorbidities.1,2 Known rare complications of these procedures include outflow tract perforation, tricuspid valve injury, and RVOT stent embolism.3 More than 1 stent in the RVOT at the time of TOF repair has been shown to increased cardiopulmonary bypass time.4 We present a case showing that a single stent that can be dilated to a larger diameter can be safely used for RVOT stent placement in a patient weighing <2 kg with TOF/PS.

Case Summary

The patient was a twin born at 33 4/7 weeks at 1.74 kg with growth restriction and a prenatal diagnosis of TOF/PS with possible absent patent ductus arteriosus (PDA). Prostaglandin E (PGE) was initiated shortly after birth, and the patient was transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit. His saturations were >90% on room air. The initial echocardiogram confirmed the diagnosis of TOF/PS with no flow acceleration across his RVOT. Additionally, there was a small PDA with left-to-right flow.

On day 2 of life, the decision was made to try stopping PGE. His saturations remained >80% off PGE until day 8 of life. He required supplemental oxygen, and propranolol was started. A repeat echocardiogram showed no PDA as well as subvalvular pulmonary stenosis and valvular pulmonary stenosis. The RVOT velocity by Doppler was 3.3 m/s. Within 48 hours, he required increasing respiratory support from nasal cannula to noninvasive ventilation and higher Fio2. PGE was restarted and titrated up to 0.05 μg/kg/min, but the PDA remained closed.

Over the following 24 hours, he continued to have progressive hypoxia requiring intubation, initiation of nitric oxide, and paralysis. He then required Fio2 of 100% and high amounts of vasoactive support to maintain saturations in the 60s to 70s, so the decision was made to pursue an RVOT stent.

Procedural Steps

A 4-F 7-cm Prelude (Merit) short sheath was placed in the left femoral vein using ultrasound guidance. A 4-F Glidecatheter (Terumo) was positioned in the right ventricle (RV) with the assistance of an 0.018-inch Hi-Torque wire (Abbott). An RV angiogram demonstrated a hypertrophied RV with severe RVOT obstruction and main pulmonary artery measuring 3.6 mm (Video 1).

A 2.7-F Progreat microcatheter system (Terumo) through the 4-F Glidecatheter was used to cross the RVOT and obtain the microcatheter position in the right pulmonary artery. An 0.018-inch Hi-Torque wire was positioned in the right pulmonary artery as a rail. The microcatheter and Glidecatheter were removed, and the 4-F short sheath was then exchanged with a 5-F CHB long sheath (Cook) over the wire. A 5 × 16 mm Formula 418 stent (Cook) was prepared and advanced over the wire and positioned across the RVOT. Angiograms were obtained through the long sheath, and small adjustments were made to ensure that the multiple levels of obstruction were fully covered by the stent (Videos 2 and 3). The balloon was inflated to 8 atm with full expansion achieved, and no significant residual waist was noted (Video 4). The balloon catheter was removed without difficulty. The long sheath was used to obtain another RV angiogram, which showed no residual RVOT obstruction (Video 5).

The patient's Fio2 was weaned in the catheterization laboratory to 30% with saturations >80%. The patient was transferred back to the cardiac intensive care unit without any complications. Over the following 48 hours, he was weaned off nitric oxide and paralytic and vasoactive medications. Post-procedural echocardiogram showed a stent spanning the length of the RVOT with a peak velocity of 3.2 m/s. There was trivial tricuspid regurgitation and normal biventricular systolic function.

The infant remained an inpatient until 33 day of life for feeding intolerance due to his prematurity. He was discharged at that time on room air with saturations in the 80s to 90s. He was readmitted 3 months following RVOT stent placement for hypoxia in the setting of a viral upper respiratory infection. Owing to persistent hypoxia and interval somatic growth (weight 3.9 kg), he returned to the catheterization laboratory 1 week into the admission. Angiography showed good interval growth of his branch pulmonary arteries with both measuring 8 mm in diameter. His branch pulmonary arteries had measured 3 mm before RVOT stent placement (Video 6). His previously placed stent was serially dilated to 8 mm (Video 7). He was discharged home 3 days following the procedure on room air.

At 5 months of age (weight 5.25 kg), he underwent a transannular repair with complete removal of the RVOT stent. He was discharged on postoperative day 7. Echocardiogram obtained at discharge showed trivial flow acceleration across the RVOT (peak velocity of 1.5 m/s) with normal RV systolic function.

Potential Pitfalls

Stent selection

This case demonstrates that RVOT stent placement with a noncoronary stent can be performed without complications in a patient weighing <2 kg without a PDA. Importantly, the choice of a stent that can dilate to a significantly larger size (noncoronary stent) allowed for the patient to be palliated in the catheterization laboratory to the goal weight of >5 kg with a single stent. Coronary stents have a smaller profile and often track more easily, which is particularly useful in patients <2 kg. However, they do not expand much larger than their original intended diameter, and a 5-mm coronary stent would not have been able to be dilated to the 8 mm this patient needed to reach the goal weight of >5 kg. In addition, coronary stents are often difficult to fracture, and those that can be fractured require additional stent placement. The 5 × 16 mm Formula 418 stent is a bare metal stent that has a larger profile and requires an 0.018-inch wire for a rail. This case demonstrates that this stent can be safely deployed in this patient size. This method may be considered for palliation of a premature or very-low-weight infant with TOF/PS.

Long sheath

Long sheaths can be difficult to manipulate in a small infant and can sometimes cause hemodynamic instability if it props open the tricuspid valve. However, it allows for angiograms to be obtained while positioning the stent to increase the likelihood that the whole length of the RVOT is covered by a single stent.

Conclusions

RVOT stents are an acceptable palliative strategy for infants with TOF/PS and prematurity and/or additional comorbidities. This report describes successful RVOT stent placement with a noncoronary stent in a premature, low-weight infant with TOF/PS.
