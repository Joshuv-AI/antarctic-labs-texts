Gastric Melatonin
Authors: Violet Kayamba
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

This rebuttal is written in response to comments on the manuscript HSR‐2024‐10‐3048 [1]. The topic raised pertains to an important and actively evolving area of gastrointestinal (GI) physiology, in which new evidence continues to expand current understanding. The role of melatonin in GI health and disease is an area of growing interest, and this formed the basis for our investigation into the association between gastric juice melatonin levels and disease states.

Although melatonin was historically considered to be produced exclusively by the pineal gland, substantial evidence now supports significant extrapineal synthesis. As reviewed by Jaworek et al., indoleamines are widely distributed in extrapineal tissues, including the gastrointestinal tract, with particularly high concentrations reported in the stomach [2]. These findings are well established and supported by multiple experimental studies. Therefore, the suggestion that the detection of melatonin in gastric juice is inherently erroneous is not supported by the current literature.

In addition, the precursor of melatonin, L‐tryptophan, is readily available in the gastrointestinal lumen during protein digestion and has been previously demonstrated in the gut using immunochemical and radioimmunoassay methods. This provides a biologically plausible substrate for local melatonin synthesis within the GI tract. The role of GI microbiome is another aspect of GI melatonin production that was beyong the scope of our manuscript [3].

Experimental studies have further demonstrated that pinealectomy does not eliminate gastrointestinal melatonin, indicating that GI melatonin production occurs independently of the pineal gland [2]. While these data are primarily derived from animal models, they provide important mechanistic insight and suggest relevance to human physiology. We acknowledge that interspecies differences in melatonin localization within the gut exist; however, this remains an area for further investigation rather than a limitation of the current findings. Moreover, luminal melatonin has been reported by other investigators, supporting the biological plausibility of our observations.

With respect to the ELISA assay, the kit used in this study was validated by the manufacturer, and the use of validated commercial assays is standard practice when protocols are followed rigorously. Wuhan Fine Biotech Co. Ltd. is a recognized supplier of immunoassay reagents. Gastric pH was measured for all samples, and prior to analysis, we consulted the manufacturer regarding sample preparation and interpretation under acidic conditions. Hypochlorhydria was also included as a variable in the final data analysis.

We acknowledge that extending measurements beyond the stated assay range of 500 pg/mL represents a methodological limitation, and this has been explicitly mentioned in the discussion.

In summary, while the role of melatonin in gastric physiology and disease is not yet fully defined, existing evidence supports its presence and potential relevance within the gastrointestinal tract. We view our findings as contributing to this emerging field and intend to pursue further studies to elucidate the mechanisms underlying gastrointestinal melatonin production and its role in gastric disease.
