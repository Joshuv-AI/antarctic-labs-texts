Cerebral phosphorus metabolite as imaging biomarkers in Alzheimer’s disease: a 31P magnetic resonance spectroscopy study
Authors: Shuai Zhao, Yanjun Dong, Baiyan Jiang, Yan Bai, Wei Wei, Yaping Wu, Chenxu Li, Jieshang Chen, Meiyun Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•31P-MRSI can detect early metabolic changes in AD.•PCr/Pi ratios are significantly reduced in the hippocampus of AD patients.•The hippocampus exhibited progressively elevated PME/PDE ratios across the HC–aMCI–AD continuum, which were associated with cognitive decline.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common cause of progressive and irreversible neurodegeneration, accounting for approximately 60–70 % of all cases of dementia worldwide(Dementia, n.d.). Amnestic mild cognitive impairment (aMCI) represents a transitional stage between healthy aging and AD, with an annual conversion rate to AD of about 10–15 % (Shukla et al., 2021). The clinical manifestations of AD often begin with memory impairment and progress to profound deficits in a range of cognitive functions, including executive processing, decision-making and planning (Marshall et al., 2012). While the pathological hallmarks of AD are the accumulation of amyloid beta (Aβ) plaques and neurofibrillary tangles composed of hyperphosphorylated tau in the brain (Braak and Braak, 1991). However, increasing evidence suggests that both mitochondrial dysfunction and disturbances in phospholipid metabolism play important roles in the early pathogenesis of AD (Lynn et al., 2010, Nitsch et al., 1992). Impaired mitochondrial function weakens neuronal energy supply, increases neuronal vulnerability, and leads to synaptic dysfunction, thereby accelerating cognitive decline (Atamna and Frey, 2007, Das et al., 2021, Sharma et al., 2021). Meanwhile, phospholipids are components of neuronal and synaptic membranes, and their homeostasis is essential for maintaining membrane fluidity, signal transduction, and synaptic plasticity. Disturbances in phospholipid metabolism not only compromise membrane structural integrity but may also accelerate disease progression by altering cellular energy metabolism (Piomelli et al., 2007).

Phosphorus-31 magnetic resonance spectroscopic imaging (31P-MRSI) is a non-invasive imaging technique that enables in vivo assessment of cerebral energy metabolism and membrane metabolites by detecting high-energy phosphate compounds, such as phosphocreatine (PCr), inorganic phosphate (Pi), adenosine triphosphate (ATP), phosphocholine (PC), phosphoethanolamine (PE),glycerophosphocholine (GPC) and glycerophosphoethanolamine (GPE) (Bottomley et al., 1992, Chaumeil et al., 2009, Lopez et al., 2024a). Phosphomonoesters (PME) signals primarily arise from phospholipid precursors such as PC and PE, whereas phosphodiesters (PDE) signals reflect phospholipid degradation products including GPC and GPE (Mandal and Akolkar, 2011). In recent years, advances in multi-voxel 31P-MRSI have made it possible to spatially resolve metabolic abnormalities across different brain regions, providing new insights into early biochemical alterations associated with neurodegenerative diseases (Jett et al., 2023, Payne et al., 2024).

Evidence from 31P-MRSI studies indicates that brains affected by AD often show altered PCr and Pi levels, reflecting impaired mitochondrial energy homeostasis (Rijpma et al., 2018). Concurrently, abnormal alterations in PME and PDE suggest a disruption of the dynamic balance between membrane phospholipid synthesis and degradation, potentially compromising synaptic membrane integrity and neuronal plasticity (Pettegrew et al., 2001). Moreover, disturbances in the Pi signaling pathway are associated with intracellular Ca2+ dysregulation, Tau hyperphosphorylation, and Aβ aggregation (Jett et al., 2023). Therefore, we propose that phospholipid metabolic defects may drive AD-related neurodegeneration through a synergistic cascade of energy imbalance, membrane disruption, and aberrant signaling, ultimately leading to synaptic dysfunction and cognitive decline.

Although previous studies have reported abnormalities in phosphorus metabolism in patients with AD (Mecheri et al., 1997, Rijpma et al., 2018, Smith et al., 1995), most 31P-MRSI investigations to date have focused on a single brain region or on gray matter areas with high neuronal density and metabolic activity (Derflinger et al., 2011, Frisoni et al., 2002, Mandal et al., 2012, Shi et al., 2009). In contrast, white matter regions have received comparatively limited attention, despite increasing evidence from conventional MRI studies indicating that white matter abnormalities are more prevalent in individuals with AD than in cognitively healthy controls (Eloyan et al., 2023, Peterson et al., 2025). These structural alterations may reflect underlying metabolic disturbances within white matter (Garnier-Crussard et al., 2022). To date, no study has systematically applied 31P-MRSI to assess metabolic alterations in both cortical and white matter regions within the same anatomical area in AD patients. The prefrontal cortex and hippocampus are critically involved in executive function, attention, and working memory—domains commonly impaired in AD (Buckner, 2004, Diamond, 2013). Therefore, simultaneous evaluation of phosphorus-containing metabolites in these regions may provide novel insights into region-specific metabolic disruptions and improve our understanding of the neurochemical basis of AD pathology.

This study aims to investigate the utility of 31P-MRSI as a noninvasive tool for detecting early alterations in cerebral energy metabolism in AD. Specifically, we compared regional metabolic profiles among HC, aMCI, and patients with AD, focusing on three key brain regions: the hippocampus, prefrontal gray matter, and prefrontal white matter. These findings may facilitate the identification of metabolic biomarkers associated with Alzheimer’s disease progression and further support the potential clinical utility of 31P-MRSI in early diagnosis and longitudinal monitoring.

Materials and methods

Participants

A total of 81 subjects were enrolled in this study. The participants were predominantly right-handed and included 25 HC participants, 26 aMCI patients, and 30 patients with mild to moderate AD. The diagnosis of AD was based on the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Diseases and Stroke/Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) criteria for the clinical diagnosis of probable AD (McKhann et al., 1984). AD patients were classified as mild-stage or moderate-stage based on cognitive assessment scores, including Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE), Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), and Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), in line with common practice in clinical and neuroimaging studies (Folstein et al., 1975, Morris, 1993). Patients were recruited according to the following inclusion criteria: (1) older than 50 years, (2) symptoms of cognitive impairment were assessed using the MMSE and the MoCA, (3) no evidence of other dementia types aside from AD, and (4) no evidence of any neurological or psychiatric disorders potentially responsible for cognitive decline or motor deficits (e.g., Parkinson’s disease, cerebrovascular disease). Patients who did not fulfill these criteria were excluded from the study. The diagnostic criteria for aMCI were based on Petersen et al. (Petersen, 2004), requiring subjective memory complaints, objective memory impairment, preserved global cognitive function, and intact daily activities. The HC participants were age- and gender-matched to AD patients and had no cognitive decline complaints, with the MMSE score ≥27.

Medical history, MoCA and MMSE scores, as well as demographic information including date of birth, sex and education level, were collected for all participants. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Henan Provincial People’s Hospital, China. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians prior to study initiation.

MRI examination

31P-MRSI scans were acquired using a 3.0 T MRI system (ElitionX, Philips Healthcare, Best, Netherlands) equipped with a transmit-receive, dual-tuned 1H/31P birdcage quadrature head coil (Rapid Biomedical).Spectral-spatial localization was achieved using two-dimensional chemical shift imaging (2D-MRSI) in combination with image-selected in vivo spectroscopy (ISIS). Two separate 2D-MRSI sequences were acquired, targeting the hippocampal and prefrontal regions respectively. Acquisition parameters for the decoupled 2D-MRSI spectra were as follows: repetition time = 4000 ms, echo time = 0.22 ms, number of signal averages = 8, sampling points = 2048, spectral bandwidth = 3000 Hz, flip angle = 90°, slice thickness = 30 mm, field of view = 225 mm2, acquired voxel sizes = 37.5 × 37.5 × 30 mm3 and, following k-space filtering and zero filling using a reconstruction matrix of 15 × 15, reconstructed voxel size was 15 × 15 × 30 mm3. Data acquisition followed the MRSinMRS reporting guidelines(Lin et al., 2021), and details are provided in the MRSinMRS checklist table (Supplementary Table 1).

A three-dimensional (3D) T1-weighted inversion-recovery volumetric image was acquired and co-registered with each 2D-MRSI dataset to enable precise anatomical localization. Imaging parameters were as follows: repetition time = 6.0 ms, echo time = 3.0 ms, field of view = 240 mm2, flip angle = 8°, inversion time = 1000 ms, slice thickness = 1 mm, acquired voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3, reconstruction matrix = 240 × 240 mm2, reconstructed voxel size = 1 × 1 × 1 mm3. MRS data processing and quantification

For each participant, six ROIs were selected for subsequent analysis(Fig. 1). Each ROI comprised two adjacent voxels, which were averaged to obtain metabolite values. The six ROIs corresponded to the left and right hippocampus, the left and right prefrontal gray matter, and the left and right prefrontal white matter. For data from both hemispheres, the corresponding ROIs in the left and right hemispheres were first selected, and the spectra from these regions were then jointly fitted to obtain an combined result.

Spectral post-processing and quantification were performed using pyAMARES (Xu et al., 2024) and matlab-based (Matlab 2022a, The Mathworks Inc., Natick, MA) CSIgui toolbox (CSIgui v2.2). CSIgui was used to perform manual phasing and applying an apodization of 5 Hz, the baseline of the spectrum was corrected by fitting a second-order spline, no zero-filling was used. The phased, apodized and baseline-corrected MRS data were then quantified using the advanced method for accurate, robust, and efficient spectral fitting (AMARES) algorithm implemented by pyAMARES software. The complete protocol is summarized in the MRSinMRS checklist according to current reporting consensus(Lin et al., 2021) (Supplementary Table S1).

Prior knowledge of the AMARES fitting include the following metabolite peaks: β-ATP, γ-ATP, α-ATP, PCr, GPC, GPE, PI, PC, and PE. The initial chemical shift and linewidth parameters were adapted from Ren et al. (Ren et al., 2015). The β-ATP was modeled as a 1:2:1 triplet with the J-coupling constant of 15 Hz, while α- and γ-ATP were modeled as doublets with J-coupling constant of 16 Hz. The phases of all peaks were constrained to be the same.

Data quality was assessed using fitting residuals and Cramér-Rao Lower Bounds (CRLB) provided by pyAMARES software. Only peak amplitudes with CRLB <30 % were included in our analysis, and all spectral fits quality were visually inspected. To evaluate cerebral mitochondrial function and energy metabolism, six metabolite ratios were calculated for each voxel: (1) ATP/Pi: commonly regarded as an indicator of cellular energy state, reflecting the balance between ATP availability and Pi; (2) PCr/ATP: reflects the capacity of the phosphocreatine system to buffer ATP levels, often interpreted as an energy reserve marker; (3) PCr/Pi: widely used as a sensitive marker of bioenergetic stress and energy homeostasis; (4) PME/PDE: represents the relative balance of phospholipid precursors and breakdown products, providing insight into membrane phospholipid metabolism; (5) PME/ATP: reflects cell membrane synthesis activity; and (6) PDE/ATP: reflects cell membrane degradation activity(Lopez et al., 2024b).

An in-house script was used within MATLAB for segmentation and co-registration of T1-weighted structural images.T1-weighted images were segmented using Statistical Parametric Mapping toolbox (SPM12, https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/software/spm12/) and co-registered to the 2D-MRSI voxel of interest using Gannett (https://www.gabamrs.com/). For each analyzed spectroscopic voxel, the partial brain fraction—defined as the proportion of gray and white matter relative to the total voxel volume—was calculated according to the method described by Sassani M et al. (Sassani et al., 2020). To assess whether observed changes in spectroscopic parameters could be attributed to partial volume effects, this partial brain fraction was included as an additional covariate in multiple regression models.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of general demographic data and 31P-MRSI metabolite ratios, while Levene’s test was applied to evaluate the homogeneity of variances. Group differences in gender distribution among HC, aMCI, and AD patients were analyzed using the chi-square (χ2) test. Group comparisons of 31P-MRSI derived mitochondrial function ratios, as well as demographic and clinical characteristics, were performed using one-way ANOVA or the Kruskal-Wallis H test, followed by Bonferroni or Tamhane’s T2 post hoc tests as appropriate. To account for potential variability in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) content within each voxel, the brain tissue fraction (1−CSF fraction) was included as a covariate in the regression analyses. For results showing significant group differences, multiple linear regression models were further applied, sequentially adjusting for brain tissue fraction, sex, and years of education. Finally, associations between clinical scale scores and 31P-MRSI metabolite ratios were examined using linear regression analysis. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics version 27.0.1 and GraphPad Prism version 10.

Association between PME/PDE ratios and cognitive

Among participants, higher PME/PDE ratios in the hippocampus were significantly associated with lower cognitive performance, as reflected by MMSE scores (R = –0.480, 95 % CI: –0.632 to –0.292, P < 0.001) and MoCA scores (R = –0.482, 95 % CI: –0.634 to –0.295, P < 0.001). Similarly, in the frontal gray matter, higher PME/PDE ratios were also associated with lower MMSE (R = –0.413, 95 % CI: –0.579 to –0.213, P < 0.001) and lower MoCA (R = –0.364, 95 % CI: –0.540 to –0.158, P < 0.001) (Fig. 4). Group differences in tissue fractions within voxels

Brain tissue fraction (1 − CSF fraction) analysis revealed significant group differences within hippocampal voxels, with mean values of 0.92 ± 0.04 in healthy controls, 0.89 ± 0.07 in aMCI, and 0.83 ± 0.08 in AD (P < 0.001). Similarly, significant differences were observed in frontal gray matter voxels, with values of 0.93 ± 0.04, 0.88 ± 0.06, and 0.82 ± 0.08 for the HC, aMCI, and AD groups, respectively (P < 0.001).

Discussion

The PCr/Pi ratio is a well-established marker of cellular energy status, where a reduced PCr/Pi ratio indicates increased energy demand and may reflect underlying metabolic crisis (Valkovič et al., 2019). Previous studies, ranging from early findings in the parietal-temporal regions (Brown et al., 1989) to recent confirmations in the temporal cortex using high-field MRSI (Das et al., 2021),have consistently reported reduced PCr/Pi ratios in the brains of AD patients. This study further extends this finding to the hippocampus: a significant decrease in the PCr/Pi ratio was observed in the hippocampal region of AD patients. This metric not only effectively distinguishes AD from HC group but also highlights the particular vulnerability of the hippocampus to energy metabolism abnormalities during the mild and moderate pathological stages of AD. However, the lack of a significant difference in this ratio between the aMCI and HC groups suggests that pronounced mitochondrial dysfunction and consequent energy metabolism abnormalities may primarily emerge during the critical transition from aMCI to AD dementia. Further analysis revealed elevated PCr/γ-ATP and reduced γ-ATP/Pi ratios in AD patients, indicating increased levels of both PCr and Pi. Therefore, the decrease in the PCr/Pi ratio is primarily attributable to a relatively greater accumulation of Pi. This indicates that the energy metabolism imbalance in AD patients is more likely rooted in impaired clearance of ATP hydrolysis products and decreased energy utilization efficiency (Zhu et al., 2006). The abnormal accumulation of Pi may not only exacerbate intracellular energy crisis but could also directly impair mitochondrial function, thereby promoting neuronal damage and cognitive decline (Carvalho and Moreira, 2023). In summary, although mitochondrial dysfunction may already be present during the prodromal phase of AD (such as in the aMCI stage), its pathologically significant consequences are predominantly concentrated in the early to middle stages of AD. During this phase, it becomes a key driver of metabolic homeostasis disruption, neuronal loss, and cognitive deterioration. Furthermore, although metabolic alterations such as elevated PCr/γ-ATP and reduced γ-ATP/Pi were observed in the prefrontal gray matter in this study, the PCr/Pi ratio itself showed no significant intergroup differences, further emphasizing the distinctly region-specific pattern of energy metabolism abnormalities in the AD brain. As an early site of vulnerability, the hippocampus likely exhibits metabolic abnormalities before other regions such as the frontal lobe, suggesting that it may play a pivotal role in initiating subsequent widespread cascades of metabolic dysregulation (Cunnane et al., 2020, Mosconi et al., 2009).

As critical components of membrane phospholipid metabolism, PME and PDE are recognized as key biomarkers of cellular membrane dynamics. Alterations in lipid levels and lipid metabolism have been reported during normal aging and in AD (Cuenoud et al., 2020, Forester et al., 2010, Kao et al., 2020). There is inconsistency in the literature regarding PME/PDE ratios in AD. While one study demonstrated significantly elevated PME/PDE ratios in the left prefrontal cortex of AD patients compared to healthy controls (Forlenza et al., 2005), another found no significant differences in HC, aMCI, and AD groups (Das et al., 2021), reflecting heterogeneity across studies. In the present study, we observed a progressive increase in PME/PDE ratios in the hippocampus and frontal gray matter along the continuum from HC to aMCI and AD, although the difference between the aMCI and HC groups did not reach statistical significance in the frontal gray matter, indicating that disruptions in membrane phospholipid metabolism become increasingly pronounced as the clinical progression of AD advances. Further analysis revealed significant group differences in the PME/γ-ATP ratio, whereas PDE/γ-ATP ratios remained largely unchanged, confirming that the elevation in PME/PDE is primarily driven by abnormal accumulation of PME, the membrane phospholipid synthesis precursor, rather than by reductions in PDE, the phospholipid degradation product. This pattern suggests an early imbalance between phospholipid synthesis and degradation in AD (Farooqui and Horrocks, 2006). Elevated PME levels may represent compensatory enhancement of phospholipid synthesis in response to neuronal membrane damage. This imbalance likely reflects reduced phospholipid turnover rate, serving as a hallmark of abnormal membrane dynamics in neurodegenerative diseases (Jing et al., 2024, Rijpma et al., 2017). Furthermore, neuroinflammation and oxidative stress in AD may further disrupt membrane stability and aggravate metabolic disturbances, collectively contributing to the increased PME/PDE ratio (Leng and Edison, 2021). Smith et al. (Smith et al., 1995) used 31P-MRSI to investigate phosphate-containing metabolite alterations in the frontal lobe of AD patients, reporting a reduced PME/PDE ratio predominantly in female patients. To control for potential confounding factors, the present study incorporated brain fraction, sex, and years of education as covariates in the statistical analysis. The results demonstrated that adjusting for these variables did not substantially alter the pattern of group differences in the PME/PDE ratio, suggesting that they are not major drivers of the observed metabolic changes. Instead, heterogeneity across studies is more likely attributable to factors such as ROI selection, sample size, and methodological variations. Therefore, future investigations with larger sample sizes, multi-regional assessments, and broader coverage across disease stages are warranted to further validate the trajectory of membrane phospholipid metabolic abnormalities.

Several studies have tentatively explored the association between 31P-MRSI parameters and the severity of cognitive decline and dementia in AD. Smith et al. (Smith et al., 1995) reported a negative correlation between the PCr/Pi ratio, which was reduced in AD patients, and dementia severity, as measured by the Dementia Rating Scale. Meanwhile, Forlenza et al. (Forlenza et al., 2005) reported that PME levels were elevated in the early stages of AD and show a negative correlation with memory performance, whereas PDE levels increase with disease progression. In the present study, we observed that elevated PME/PDE ratios in the hippocampus and frontal gray matter were significantly associated with cognitive decline, as reflected by lower MMSE and MoCA scores. Although the correlation was moderate, these findings further support the close relationship between membrane phospholipid metabolism abnormalities and cognitive impairment. This observation is consistent with previous reports and underscores the critical vulnerability of the hippocampus to early pathological damage in AD. Collectively, our results reinforce the notion of dynamic alterations in membrane phospholipid metabolism throughout the progression of AD and highlight the potential utility of the PME/PDE ratio as a metabolic biomarker for early diagnosis and monitoring of disease progression.

Analysis of brain tissue fraction (1−CSF fraction) revealed significant differences among groups in both the hippocampus and frontal gray matter. Specifically, healthy controls exhibited the highest brain tissue fraction, AD patients the lowest, and aMCI participants were intermediate. This trend suggests a gradual reduction in brain tissue content with worsening cognitive function, likely reflecting AD-related neuronal and synaptic loss leading to brain atrophy and increased cerebrospinal fluid volume. Previous studies investigating 31P-MRSI in AD have primarily placed the voxels within cortical gray matter regions, where metabolic alterations are more readily detectable due to the high density of neuronal and synaptic activity. In contrast, our study uniquely focused on frontal white matter, aiming to explore whether similar phospholipid metabolic changes could be identified in regions typically considered less metabolically active. However, we did not observe any significant differences in PME/PDE, or other phosphorus metabolite ratios in white matter between diagnostic groups. This lack of detectable change may reflect a relatively lower sensitivity of white matter tissue to early neurochemical alterations in AD. Moreover, pathological studies have suggested that gray matter abnormalities typically precede those in white matter (Weston et al., 2015), which may further explain the absence of significant white matter metabolic alterations in our cohort.

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, especially within the heterogeneous aMCI subgroup, limited the ability to detect subtle metabolic alterations and may affect the generalizability of the results. Second, the incomplete data on core Alzheimer’s disease biomarkers (e.g., amyloid status and APOE genotype) and potential imbalances in their distribution between groups preclude a comprehensive interpretation of the findings. Finally, the analysis did not include other relevant brain regions, such as the posterior cingulate cortex and precuneus. Future large-scale longitudinal studies that incorporate complete biomarker data and a broader neuroanatomical coverage are warranted to validate these findings, delineate the temporal dynamics of PME/PDE alterations, and evaluate their clinical biomarker potential.

Conclusion

In this study, we found that the hippocampal PCr/Pi ratio was significantly lower in AD patients compared to HCs. Moreover, along the continuum from HC to aMCI to AD, PME/PDE ratios in the hippocampus and frontal gray matter exhibited a progressive increase, although the difference between the aMCI and HC groups did not reach statistical significance in the frontal gray matter. Further analysis showed that PME/PDE ratios in the hippocampus and frontal gray matter were negatively correlated with MMSE and MoCA scores, suggesting that disturbances in energy metabolism and membrane phospholipid metabolism may jointly contribute to the pathological progression of AD and are associated with cognitive impairment. However, no significant differences were observed in membrane phospholipid or other phosphorus metabolite ratios in frontal white matter across the diagnostic groups.
