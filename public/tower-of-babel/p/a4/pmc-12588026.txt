Predicting disease progression in people living with HIV using machine learning and a nomogram: a 10-year cohort study based in Xinjiang, China
Authors: Qianru Mao, Qian He, Yongkang Ni, Zhen Ni, Changyu Zeng, Jiliang Wang, Xiaoyan He, Xinhuan Feng, Mingjian Ni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesCurrent prediction models for disease progression to AIDS in people living with HIV primarily rely on traditional statistical methods. This study aimed to develop and compare four machine learning models and to create a clinically applicable nomogram for identifying risk factors associated with AIDS progression.DesignA retrospective cohort study conducted from January 2013 to December 2022.SettingYining City, Xinjiang, China.ParticipantsNewly diagnosed HIV-infected patients (aged 18–60 years) who received antiretroviral therapy and had not progressed to AIDS at baseline.Primary outcome measuresProgression from HIV infection to AIDS, as defined by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention criteria.ResultsAmong the 2305 patients included, 652 progressed to AIDS. The cohort was predominantly male, with a mean baseline CD4 cell count of 384 cells/μL. Four machine learning models—Support Vector Machine, Random Forest, Logistic Regression and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost)—were developed. The XGBoost model demonstrated the best predictive performance (area under the curve, AUC: 0.877). Its calibration curve, after bias correction, showed good agreement with the ideal curve, and decision curve analysis indicated potential clinical utility.ConclusionsIn this cohort, the XGBoost model showed superior performance for predicting AIDS progression. The proposed nomogram may serve as a practical tool to facilitate rapid risk assessment in similar clinical settings. These findings suggest that enhanced monitoring and regular follow-up might be beneficial for patients with low CD4 counts for timely intervention and to improve outcomes.

Introduction

Since the beginning of the HIV epidemic in the early 1980s, over 85 million people have been infected globally. As of December 2023, an estimated 39.9 million individuals were living with HIV worldwide.1 A prominent hallmark of HIV infection is the progressive depletion of CD4+ T lymphocytes. In the early stages of the disease, most individuals can maintain relatively high CD4 levels; however, as the disease progresses, the number of CD4 cells gradually decreases, which in turn results in AIDS-defining conditions.2 The time it takes for individuals to progress from HIV infection to AIDS varies among individuals, with most cases taking 10–15 years.3 When patients enter the end stage of HIV infection, namely the AIDS stage, their clinical manifestations mostly include HIV-related symptoms, signs, opportunistic infections and tumours.4 5 Antiretroviral therapy (ART) plays a crucial role in impeding the progression of the disease through potent suppression of viral replication.6 7 With sustained ART adherence, life expectancy for people living with HIV (PLWH) now approaches 77.8 years, comparable to HIV-negative populations.8 The HIV clinical latency period typically ranges from 7 to 10 years, exhibiting substantial interindividual variability. The progression of the disease is influenced by a multitude of factors, including the host’s immune status, comorbidities and individual specificity. Understanding the characteristics of disease progression among PLWH facilitates the identification of high-risk individuals who are more likely to progress rapidly to AIDS in the early phases of the disease.

With the advancement of artificial intelligence technology, machine learning has demonstrated unique advantages in the medical field, particularly in handling complex medical data, identifying disease progression patterns and supporting clinical decision-making.9 Compared with traditional statistical methods, machine learning can handle medical datasets that are high-dimensional, nonlinear and noisy more effectively, thereby generating more accurate prediction models with stronger generalisation ability.1012 Logistic regression (LR) is a widely used benchmark method and ‘gold standard’ in clinical prediction modelling; support vector machines (SVM) excel at handling complex nonlinear relationships in high-dimensional spaces, while random forests (RF) and eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) are powerful ensemble learning algorithms that can effectively capture complex interaction effects among predictor variables and often perform well in various prediction tasks. In recent years, many scholars have used machine learning technology to conduct HIV prediction research, and all the machine learning models used have demonstrated high performance capabilities.1315 A nomogram integrates multiple prognostic indicators into a single planar diagram according to their relative contributions, offering an intuitive visualisation of the interrelationships among predictor variables. It assists in optimising clinical treatment strategies and predicting disease progression, thereby providing valuable decision-making support for both clinicians and researchers. This tool assigns scores to different values of each predictive factor in the model, and the summation of these scores yields a total point value, which corresponds to an individual’s risk of a specific outcome. Furthermore, the total score can be converted into a precise predictive probability for the occurrence of the outcome event, enabling an effective transition from factor analysis to risk quantification.

The aim of this study is to construct a predictive model for HIV disease progression that is both accurate and practical. By integrating the advantages of machine learning techniques and nomograms. By comparing multiple machine learning models—SVM, RF, LR and XGBoost—we aim to identify the most appropriate model for predicting HIV disease progression. The application of machine learning models in the investigation of disease progression among PLWH could lead to a better understanding of the pathogenic mechanisms underlying HIV infection and offer clinicians a powerful decision-support tool. These contributions may support the advancement of HIV prevention and treatment strategies.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This retrospective cohort study included 2305 individuals newly diagnosed with HIV who received ART in Yining City, China, from 1 January 2013 to 31 December 2022. The total observation period spanned 10 years, from January 2013 to December 2022, with the start of observation for each individual being the date of their initial diagnosis and entry into the database. Data on PLWH were obtained from both the national surveillance system and the national treatment databases. All information has been deidentified to maintain the confidentiality of sensitive details. Eligible participants were adults (aged 18–60 years) with laboratory-confirmed HIV infection but without AIDS. All participants were receiving ART and had at least one documented paired measurement of viral load (VL) and CD4 cell count during the enrolment period.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the progression from HIV infection (PLWH without AIDS) to AIDS (PLWH with AIDS) during the observation period. AIDS-defining events were based on the diagnostic criteria issued by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention,4 including CD4 cell counts <200 cells/mm³ after HIV infection, or development of at least one AIDS-related clinical manifestation, such as unexplained irregular fever (>38°C) persisting for >1 month, pneumocystis pneumonia, oropharyngeal candidiasis, cerebral toxoplasmosis. The determination of this outcome (progression to AIDS) is strictly based on new clinical events or laboratory test results occurring after the baseline assessment. No variables measured at baseline were used to define the outcome.

Data collection

At enrolment, demographic and clinical characteristics were extracted from the electronic database. ‘Baseline’ refers to the time point at which the first clinical examination was conducted on the participant’s initial diagnosis and entry into the medical surveillance system. This included demographic data such as sex, age, marital status, education level and occupation, as well as clinical characteristics like baseline CD4 cell counts (measured at initial diagnosis), WHO clinical stage and HIV transmission route. Annual follow-up laboratory parameters were obtained from the treatment database of designated treatment hospitals, which comprised complete blood count (including white cell count, platelets and haemoglobin), renal function (serum creatinine), metabolic profile (triglycerides, total cholesterol and fasting glucose) and measures of liver function, including alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and total bilirubin levels.

Data management

To ensure the quality of the model’s input data, we implemented rigorous data preprocessing and feature engineering pipelines. First, a coding dictionary was constructed for all categorical variables, which were then converted into numerical formats suitable for algorithmic processing. For key study variables with missing values representing less than 5% of the data, the corresponding cases were excluded from the analysis. Potential outliers were controlled through source data quality control procedures, including a double-data entry system for verification and the identification of laboratory values falling outside physiologically plausible ranges. Subsequently, the baseline information database and the annual follow-up laboratory database were integrated using each patient’s unique ART code.

Establishment of machine learning models and nomogram

For model development, we randomly partitioned the dataset into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets using a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility. Four machine learning approaches were implemented: (1) SVM using the e1071 package, (2) RF via the RF package with hyperparameter optimisation through caret, (3) XGBoost gradient boosting implemented with the xgboost package and (4) LR using the glm function from the stats package. Model performance was rigorously assessed through fivefold cross-validation.

The importance of features in the XGBoost model is quantified using the gain metric. Gain measures the contribution of each feature to the model by calculating the total reduction in the loss function (such as Gini impurity or entropy) caused by all splits based on that feature. A higher gain value indicates that the corresponding feature is more critical to improving node purity and the predictive performance of the model in all subtrees.

The RF implementation included comprehensive hyperparameter tuning using caret to maximise predictive accuracy, while maintaining methodological consistency across all modelling approaches.

For the RF model, hyperparameter optimisation was performed using a grid search approach within a fivefold cross-validation framework. The number of features randomly sampled as candidates at each split (mtry) was tuned over the values (2, 4, 6), and the number of trees in the forest (ntree) was optimised over the values (100, 200, 300). For the SVM model with a radial basis function kernel, a grid search was similarly employed to optimise the kernel coefficient (sigma) across the values (0.1, 1, 10) and the regularisation parameter (C) across the values (0.1, 1, 10). The XGBoost model was tuned by adjusting multiple key hyperparameters: the learning rate (eta) was set to 0.1, the maximum depth of a tree (max_depth) to 3, the minimum loss reduction required to make a further partition on a leaf node (gamma) to 0.1, the subsample ratio of features (colsample_bytree) to 0.8, the minimum sum of instance weight needed in a child (min_child_weight) to 1 and the subsample ratio of the training instances (subsample) to 0.8. The optimal model was selected based on the highest area under the curve (AUC) value, with additional evaluation using accuracy, precision, recall and F1 score.

A clinically applicable nomogram was developed incorporating significant variables identified through univariate and multivariate LR analyses. Model performance was comprehensively assessed through ROC analysis, calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA).

Statistical analyses

Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean±SD (M±SD), while non-normally distributed data were expressed as median with IQR. Categorical variables were described using frequencies or percentages. For comparative analyses between the training and validation cohorts, independent samples t-tests or Wilcoxon rank-sum tests were employed for continuous variables, while χ² tests or Fisher’s exact tests were used for categorical variables. All statistical tests were two-sided, and a p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Model performance was evaluated on a held-out validation set that was not used during model training. Predictions were generated using the trained model via the predict() function in R. Specifically, the feature data from the validation set was passed as the newdata argument to the final fitted model. The function automatically applied the model to produce prediction outcomes for each sample. All analyses and visualisations were performed in R software (V.4.4.0).

Patient and public involvement

No members of the public were involved in the design or implementation of this study.

Results

Patient characteristics

From 1 January 2013 to 31 December 31, 2022, 2528 PLWH were enrolled in this study, of whom 223 were excluded because they did not meet the criteria (figure 1). A total of 2305 PLWH were included in this study, with 652 progressing to AIDS and 1653 patients who did not progress to AIDS. The cohort was divided into a training set (n=1611) and a testing set (n=652). All baseline characteristics showed no statistically significant differences between the training and testing sets (table 1), confirming successful dataset partitioning and balanced distribution. Among the 2305 patients, 63.3% were male. The mean initial CD4 cell count was 384 cells/µL, and the average age was 37 years. Labour professionals or farmers constituted the largest occupational group. Marital status distribution was as follows: 22.2% single, 47.3% married or living with partners, and 30.6% divorced or widowed. Sexual transmission was the predominant route of HIV infection. Development and validation of machine learning models

Model construction and parameter evaluation

The dataset was split into training and testing sets at a 7:3 ratio. Four machine learning models—LR, RF, SVM and XGBoost—were developed using 18 feature variables. All four models demonstrated strong predictive capabilities for HIV disease progression, with XGBoost achieving the highest AUC (0.877), followed by RF (AUC=0.863), LR (AUC=0.854), and SVM (AUC=0.831). Feature importance ranking

To assess the predictive value of clinical features, we quantified feature importance using the Gain value, which reflects the contribution of each variable to model purity improvement in gradient boosting frameworks. According to our analysis, CD4 cell count was the top-ranked predictor of HIV disease progression. The top 15 influential features were: CD4 cell count, platelet count, white cell count, haemoglobin, total bilirubin, triglyceride, ALT, creatinine, total cholesterol, plasma glucose, AST, WHO clinical stage, age, route of HIV transmission and occupation (table 4 and figure 3).

Development of the nomogram

Univariate LR identified WHO clinical stage, marital status, route of HIV transmission, age, CD4 cell count, white cell count, total bilirubin, platelet count, haemoglobin and total cholesterol as significant predictors of disease progression (all p<0.05). Multivariate LR retained WHO clinical stage, CD4 cell count, route of HIV transmission, platelet count and haemoglobin (all p<0.05) as independent variables. The final nomogram included these five variables for predicting disease progression in PLWH (table 5 and figure 4).

Discussion

This study used machine learning models to identify the factors influencing disease progression in PLWH and developed a predictive model for disease progression risk. Among the evaluated models, XGBoost exhibited the highest predictive accuracy. By integrating clinical, virological and immunological data, the study accurately identified fifteen characteristic variables associated with disease progression in PLWH, including CD4 cell count, platelet count, white cell count, haemoglobin, total bilirubin, triglyceride, ALT, creatinine, total cholesterol, plasma glucose, AST, WHO clinical stages, age, route of HIV transmission and occupation, with CD4 cell count consistently being the strongest indicator. Additionally, the study introduced a nomogram to aid in the visual assessment of disease progression. The nomogram, based on LR, retained five key indicators (route of HIV transmission, WHO clinical stages, CD4 cell count, platelet count and haemoglobin) through feature selection, which showed a high degree of overlap with the high-importance features in the XGBoost model, further confirming their critical role in HIV disease progression. The XGBoost model can provide refined risk stratification, and the simplified nomogram offers a practical tool for rapid risk prediction and assessment in clinical settings, aiding primary healthcare institutions in quick screening.

Based on the gain (Gain) metric, CD4 cell count was identified as the most influential predictor of HIV disease progression in our model. The top 15 most influential features, ranked in descending order of importance, are as follows: CD4 cell count, platelet count, white cell count, haemoglobin, total bilirubin, triglycerides, ALT, creatinine, total cholesterol, plasma glucose, AST, WHO clinical stage, age, HIV transmission route and occupation. During the disease progression of PLWH, the baseline CD4 cell count serves as a valuable indicator for assessing immune status and aids in predicting disease progression. Machine learning models in this study confirmed that CD4 cell count contributes to predicting disease progression among PLWH. Clinically, CD4 cell count, as a readily measurable indicator, can be applied to forecast HIV disease progression.16 17 During the rapid disease progression phase of HIV, patients exhibit relatively low CD4 cell counts. Therefore, initiating ART as early as possible is particularly critical.7 18 Evidence suggests that higher ART adherence is positively correlated with elevated CD4 cell counts, while lower CD4 cell counts predict accelerated disease progression and poorer prognosis.19 Therefore, for PLWH with lower CD4 cell counts, pretreatment counselling and support should be intensified to enhance disease awareness and facilitate improved health management.

The study found that older PLWH exhibited a relatively higher risk of disease progression, which is consistent with the findings reported in several previous studies.20 Specifically, the median survival time of PLWH aged 45–54 years is significantly shorter than that of those aged 15–24 years.21 This observation could be attributed to the elderly’s increased susceptibility to comorbid conditions and their comparatively poorer immune recovery relative to younger individuals.20 22 23 However, some studies propose that pre-existing immunity acquired through repeated mucosal exposure prior to viral infection in older individuals may contribute to delayed disease progression.24 Although females exhibit lower baseline VL than males after HIV infection, studies have shown accelerated progression to AIDS in females, which may be associated with accelerated CD4 cell count depletion driven by postinfection inflammatory responses.25 These findings highlight that HIV progression is not governed by a single pathway but by a constellation of factors. In populations identified as high risk for progression, early ART initiation may be warranted, given its potential to control VL and delay the disease course.

PLWH infected through various transmission routes exhibit notable differences in the rates and timelines of disease progression. These differences are likely influenced by factors such as racial disparities, socioeconomic status and the unequal distribution of healthcare resources. Research suggests that individuals who inject drugs are more likely to seek medical care and receive treatment later, resulting in a faster progression to AIDS or death compared with those infected through sexual contact.2628 Heterosexual contact (HC) is linked to a higher risk of progression to AIDS compared with injecting drug use (IDU) and MSM.29 This could be due to delayed HIV testing in HC populations, leading to later treatment initiation and poorer outcomes. MSM often have lower disease progression risk, likely because of more frequent HIV testing and earlier intervention. Individuals who inject drugs face significant socio-economic and behavioural challenges that heighten HIV risks. These include needle sharing, which drives HIV transmission, and issues like unemployment, limited education, incarceration and lack of social support. Such factors hinder healthcare access and treatment adherence. Given the distinct disparities among MSM, IDU and HC populations in terms of HIV acquisition risk, disease progression patterns and healthcare access, implementing population-tailored prevention, testing and treatment strategies is of great importance for effective HIV epidemic control.

Haematological abnormalities and thrombocytopaenia are both associated with CD4 levels and are considered surrogate indicators for monitoring disease progression.30 Thrombocytopaenia is a common complication among PLWH.31 Following HIV infection, thrombocytopaenia resulting from antibody-mediated immune destruction of platelets can lead to bleeding symptoms and complicate the treatment of other comorbidities.32 In the present study, platelets were identified as a significant factor influencing HIV disease progression. This finding aligns with previous research indicating that platelets in PLWH are associated with reduced CD4 counts and accelerated progression to AIDS,33 with thrombocytopaenia occurring more frequently in the advanced stages of the disease. Furthermore, thrombocytopaenia is linked to HIV prognosis and contributes to increased mortality among PLWH. It has been demonstrated that timely initiation of ART reduces the prevalence of thrombocytopaenia,34 thereby improving disease progression rates and underscoring the importance of comprehensive ART implementation.

Machine learning has demonstrated considerable potential in the medical field in recent years, particularly in enhancing diagnostic accuracy and predicting prognosis.35 XGBoost constructs a robust predictive model by integrating multiple weak predictors, capable of processing large-scale datasets and leveraging powerful parallel computing capabilities. In medical applications, XGBoost has been used to rapidly analyse diverse physiological indicators and demographic data of patients, aiding clinicians in diagnostic decision-making.3638 Furthermore, it can inform personalised treatment recommendations through analysis of individual patient heterogeneity.39 40 ML methodologies have become increasingly diversified, with each algorithm exhibiting unique advantages. Through comparative multimodel studies and validation using multiple evaluation metrics, our findings revealed that XGBoost demonstrated superior performance, aligning with observations from other researchers.4143 Compared with traditional LR models, XGBoost not only achieved higher AUC values on ROC curves but also demonstrated favourable clinical utility in DCA. These studies suggest that XGBoost, as a robust ML algorithm, holds promise for broader application prospects in healthcare and may offer performance benefits for specific clinical tasks.

Our analytical approach integrated the interpretability of conventional statistical methods with the high predictive accuracy of modern machine learning algorithms, enabling a comprehensive comparison of their utility in this specific clinical context. Clinically, the developed nomogram offers a user-friendly, visual tool that translates complex model predictions into an actionable risk score, facilitating point-of-care assessment. This is particularly valuable for resource-limited settings like Xinjiang, where sophisticated computing resources may not be readily available. Our findings provide valuable regional insights into AIDS progression and demonstrate the potential of predictive modelling to inform targeted intervention strategies, which could contribute to improved HIV care precision in underserved areas. However, the study has several limitations. First, the data originate from a single medical centre in Yining, Xinjiang, China, which may limit generalisability. Second, the limited dataset size in this study precluded the inclusion of deep learning models for comparison. Finally, our cohort exclusively included patients receiving ART, which may introduce selection bias and limit the model’s applicability to untreated individuals living with HIV. Future research could focus on multicentre, prospective external validation studies that incorporate more granular clinical and treatment data for the development of more robust and universally applicable predictive tools.

Conclusions

This study employed machine learning algorithms to develop predictive tools for AIDS progression in PLWH, aiming to address limitations associated with traditional methods. Key clinical markers such as WHO stages, CD4 count and haematological parameters were identified as important predictors. The XGBoost model demonstrated comparatively higher performance among the models evaluated, highlighting the potential value of machine learning in healthcare applications. The approach may facilitate risk stratification, and a simplified nomogram is provided to support possible clinical risk assessment. Regular follow-up is suggested for patients with low CD4 counts to help improve health outcomes.
