Can Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids improve bone mineral density?: Evidence from a Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Xusheng Gou, Rui Zhang, Mi Li, Renxin Xu, Dapeng Zhang, Tao Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Previous studies have reported controversial associations between n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFA) and bone mineral density (BMD). To clarify whether a causal relationship exists, this study employed a 2-sample Mendelian randomization analysis using single-nucleotide polymorphisms significantly associated with n-3 PUFA as instrumental variables; data for both exposure (n-3 PUFA) and outcome (BMD) were obtained from genome-wide association studies. The inverse variance weighting method served as the primary analytical approach, supplemented by multiple sensitivity analyses to assess the robustness of the findings. Results from the 2-sample Mendelian randomization analysis provided strong genomic evidence for a negative causal association between n-3 PUFA and BMD (inverse variance weighting: odds ratio = 0.94, 95% confidence interval: 0.90–0.98, P = .01). These findings indicate that higher n-3 PUFA is associated with an increased risk of reduced BMD, suggesting that elevated n-3 PUFA may be a risk factor for low BMD and potentially osteoporosis.

1. Introduction

As a prevalent orthopedic disease, osteoporosis (OP) is a chronic metabolic bone disorder exhibiting significant variations across ethnic groups and regions. Currently, the global prevalence of OP is 18.3%. With the ongoing aging of the world’s population, its prevalence is projected to rise further.[1–3] The most prominent features of OP are decreased bone mineral density (BMD) and deterioration of bone microstructure.[4–6] Therefore, investigating the factors contributing to reduced BMD is crucial for the diagnosis, treatment, and prevention of OP. Studies have identified various risk factors closely associated with decreased BMD, such as aging, estrogen deficiency, and lipid metabolism disorders.[7–9]

In recent years, the association between fatty acid (FA) metabolism and bone metabolism has received increasing attention, especially n-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (n-3 PUFA). Many observational studies have shown positive effects of n-3 PUFA on bone[10–12]; however, results remain inconsistent. Furthermore, as most evidence derives from observational designs, inherent limitations, including potential reverse causality and residual confounding, preclude robust causal inference.

To address these issues, we employed Mendelian randomization (MR), an epidemiological approach using genetic variants (e.g., single-nucleotide polymorphisms [SNPs]) as instrumental variables (IVs) to infer causality between exposures and outcomes.[13–15] Specifically, we utilized SNPs associated with n-3 PUFA levels as IVs to investigate the causal effect of n-3 PUFA on BMD. Unlike observational studies, MR minimizes confounding from environmental/behavioral factors and inherently satisfies temporality (genetic variants precede disease onset), strengthening causal plausibility.

2.2. IV selection

In this study, SNPs meeting the following criteria were selected as IVs.[13–15] SNPs highly associated with n-3 PUFA (P < 5 × 10−8). To avoid linkage disequilibrium (LD) caused by n-3 PUfA-associated SNPs, LD must satisfy r2 < 0.001 and kb = 10,000. To ensure a strong relationship between IV and n-3 PUFA, we chose an F-statistic > 10 to avoid bias caused by weak IV, and the F-statistic = (β/SE)2, where SE is standard error.[18] To ensure that genetic variants were independent of the second MR hypothesis of potential confounders, we conducted queries in the PhenoScanner database to exclude IVs associated with known confounders[19,20] (e.g., gender, lifestyle, advanced age, steroid therapy, low body mass index, hormonal dysregulation, and some chronic diseases). The number of IVs that met the above 3 assumptions was 6.

2.3. MR analysis

In this 2-sample MR design, we used the inverse variance weighting (IVW) method, weighted median method, simple median method, weighted median estimator method, and MR-Egger regression to calculate the causal association between n-3 PUFA genetic variation and BMD.[21–23] Among them, the IVW method was used to calculate odds ratios as the main method, and the slope of the weighted regression of the outcome effect on the exposure effect represented the outcome estimate (with an intercept restricted to zero). In addition, the other 4 methods were used as tests for the robustness of the primary outcome.

2.4. Sensitivity analysis

We used different methods to rule out potential effects to ensure that IV was independent of outcomes other than exposure. First, pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) was applied to test and calibrate the outliers of horizontal pleiotropy, and the outliers in IV were removed.[21] We use MR-Egger regression to explain horizontal pleiotropy, and when the P-value of the intercept is >.05, there is no horizontal pleiotropy.[24] A funnel plot is drawn to test for horizontal pleiotropy. If the funnel plot has a symmetrical shape, this usually means that there is no significant pleiotropy.

Heterogeneity of individual estimates of genetic variation was assessed using Cochran Q test. If the P-value of Cochran Q test was >.05, it meant that there was no significant heterogeneity among the SNPs, and either the random effect model or the fixed effect model could be used, because the results obtained by the 2 methods were the same.[25] If the P-value of Cochran Q test is <.05, it means that there is heterogeneity among the SNPs, and the random effect model must be used for MR analysis. The P-value of Cochran Q test in that study was <.05; therefore, in this study, we used the random effects model for MR analysis.[26,27] Leave-one-out sensitivity test is used to observe whether the results change significantly after the removal of specific SNPs. If any SNPs are removed one by one, the results remain relatively stable, indicating that the overall error line change range is small after the removal of any SNP. Specifically, each SNP was removed one by one, and then, the meta-effects of the remaining SNPs were calculated, in which the influence of each SNP was separately evaluated by IVW analysis and represented by forest plot. All statistical analyses were performed using R Studio (version 4.3.1), 2-sample MR (version 0.5.7), and MR-Presso (version 1.0) software packages.

4. Discussion

The association between n-3 PUFA and BMD remains controversial. Conventional perspectives suggest that n-3 PUFA may positively influence bone remodeling through multiple mechanisms, such as inhibiting osteoclast activity and promoting osteoblast function, thereby enhancing BMD.[28–30] However, most supporting evidence derives from observational studies reliant on retrospective questionnaires to estimate FA intake.[31] It has always been limited to the effects of certain types of FAs on bone health or on certain subpopulations of people, such as postmenopausal women and the elderly. Several recent studies have reported conflicting and inconsistent conclusions regarding the association. A meta-analysis of 28 randomized controlled trials with a total of 7288 participants showed that increasing intake of n-3 PUFA might have a modest effect on BMD at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, but the evidence was of low grade[32]; and in further analysis, it was found that an increase in total PUFA intake may have little effect on BMD.[32] Orchard et al[33] found that higher intake of n-3 PUFA was associated with a higher risk of total fracture and that higher intake of n-3 PUFA may lead to bone loss, which may further lead to the development of OP. This is consistent with the results of our study. In a single-center study of postmenopausal Spanish women, high levels of plasma n-3 PUFA may be an independent risk factor for bone health.[34]

Our 2-sample MR study suggests a negative causal relationship between n-3 PUFA and BMD, with higher n-3 PUFA being associated with an increased risk of decreased BMD, and higher n-3 PUFA may be a risk factor for low BMD and even OP. We performed multiple sensitivity analyses to distinguish between true negative results and results that lacked validity, ensuring that the study met the 3 MR assumptions. Given our application of the above methods to demonstrate the consistency of the findings, we are confident in the validity of the results of this MR analysis.

Our study has several strengths. First, we applied the 2-sample MR approach for the first time to investigate the causal relationship between n-3 PUFA and BMD, avoiding potential confounding factors and reverse causality. Second, we employed the largest available genome-wide association study sample to identify FA instruments and assess causality, thereby minimizing the biases of small sample sizes and traditional observational study methods.

However, this 2-sample MR study still has some limitations. First, the database used in this study was based on the populations of European ancestry, and it is unclear whether the results would apply to populations of non-European ancestry. Therefore, confirmation of these findings in other populations is warranted. Second, our study only explored the relationship between n-3 PUFA and BMD and did not investigate the effect of n-3 PUFA on BMD in different bone parts of the body; future studies should further investigate the effect of n-3 PUFA on BMD in different bone parts of the body. Finally, unobserved pleiotropy is a major limitation of MR studies, which may influence conclusions that assess the genetically predicted association between n-3 PUFA and BMD risk.

5. Conclusion

This 2-sample MR analysis generates strong new genomic evidence of inverse causality between n-3 PUFA and BMD; that is, higher n-3 PUFA is associated with an increased risk of decreased BMD, and higher n-3 PUFA may be a risk factor for low BMD and even OP.
