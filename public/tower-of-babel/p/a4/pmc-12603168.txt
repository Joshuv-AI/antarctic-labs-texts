Magnetostrictive mechanical frequency combs
Authors: Guanqi Ye, Ruitong Sun, Junning Zhao, Fusheng Ma
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMagnetostriction plays a pivotal role in magneto-mechanical systems. Here, we propose and experimentally demonstrate a mechanical frequency comb solely requiring the fundamental mode f0 of a magnetostrictive mm-scale macroresonator. Both integer-harmonic combs and half-integer-harmonic combs are observed in kHz regime with Hz resolution by near-resonant pumping fp ≈ f0 and parametrical stiffness modulation fs ≪ f0. The tooth spacing of both combs is determined by fs, which can be continuously tuned by changing fs from Hz to kHz. Moreover, the half-integer-harmonic combs can be purposely switched with frequency shifting half a tooth spacing via suppressing period-doubling bifurcation. The experimentally observed formation, evolution, and switching of combs can be well understood by introducing the bias magnetical force and the parametrically modulated stiffness into the Duffing equation. Our findings could provide a magneto-mechanical platform with tunability for potential applications in the acoustic frequency range such as non-invasive/contactless sensor and wireless antenna.Subject terms: Acoustics, Mechanical engineering

Introduction

Frequency comb is a spectrum composed of equidistant narrow lines1. Optical frequency combs (OFCs) have been widely utilized for frequency metrology, optical clocks, spectroscopy, optical ranging, astronomical spectrograph calibration, and optical frequency synthesis2–7. Inspired by the achievements of OFCs, mechanical frequency combs (MFCs)8,9, also called acoustic or phononic frequency combs, are attracting increasing attention and have been realized in electro- and opto-mechanical systems. For electro-mechanical systems, a mechanical mixer has been reported using a strongly driven nanomechanical resonator of silicon-on-insulator wafers10. Based on the theory of Fermi-Pasta-Ulam α chains, the MFCs were theoretically proposed11 and experimentally demonstrated using a piezo-electrically driven micromechanical resonator12. Since then, MFCs have been widely studied in electro-mechanical systems on nanometer and micrometer scales via N-wave mixing, bifurcation, internal resonance, and parametric resonance12–22. MFCs have also been realized in nonlinear opto-mechanical systems through the parametric phonon-photon coupling induced mechanical mode lasing8,23–28 and Kerr nonlinearity29,30. Alternatively, magneto-mechanical systems are emerging as a platform for mechanical applications benefiting from the advantages of non-invasive and contactless control31, flexible manipulation32, and ambient operation environment33. Magneto-mechanical resonators have been extensively explored as nerve stimulators34, magnetoelectric sensors35,36, logic-in-memory devices37, and magnetometers38,39. Recently, magnonic frequency combs have been achieved from the nonlinear oscillation of magnons induced by the magneto-mechanical interaction40,41. MFCs based on magneto-mechanical systems are expected but not yet approached.

Most of the reported MFCs are working in the megahertz and gigahertz range8,12,20,23. Actually, the Hz to kilohertz (kHz) range is crucial to many applications of magnetic field detection42, geological survey43, magnetoencephalography44, and so forth. Specially, MFCs working in kHz are highly anticipated in underwater ranging and communication in view of the weak attenuation and the low dispersion in liquid45,46. Based on the magneto-mechanical coupling, the mechanical resonators made of magnetostrictive materials have been utilized in the frequency range from Hz to kHz as wireless neural stimulator34,47 and antenna48. Magnetostrictive resonators are promising for investigating MFCs in kHz range without requiring high-cost manufacturing and measuring technologies. On the one hand, the magnetostrictive resonators for kHz applications are on the centimeter to millimeter scale, which can be simply prepared from amorphous alloy ribbon by laser cutting. On the other hand, the mechanical vibrations of magnetostrictive resonator can be measured contactlessly and precisely by detecting the deformation-induced magnetization via electromagnetic induction with a coil.

From the application point of view, the spectral bandwidth and tooth spacing of frequency comb are the most significant two characteristics in sensing and metrology49. The tunability of tooth spacing is essential for precision measurements and comb self-referencing. For the reported MFCs based on mode coupling and competition, the tooth spacing can only be switched non-continuously since it is determined by discrete eigenfrequencies17,23,24. Recently, it has been demonstrated that the tooth spacing can be tuned continuously by changing modulating frequency in electro-mechanical systems16,50. Additionally, the comb centers, in principle, can only locate at the eigenfrequencies of resonators and their high-order integer-harmonics. Whereas, achieving MFCs with simultaneous tunability of both tooth spacing and comb center remains a significant challenge.

In this work, we propose a scenario of tunable MFCs comprised of integer-harmonics combs (IHCs) and half-integer-harmonics combs (HIHCs), which results from parametrical stiffness (PS) modulation and period-doubling (PD) bifurcation. Interestingly, the HIHCs can be purposely switched with frequency shifting half a tooth spacing by suppressing PD bifurcation via PS modulation. We experimentally demonstrate the proposed scenario in the kHz regime by driving a magnetostrictive resonator on millimeter scale with two-tone, i.e., near-resonant (NR) pumping fp ≈ f0 and PS modulation fs ≪ f0. The tooth spacing of both IHCs and HIHCs is determined by fs, which can be continuously tuned from 10 to 5000 Hz. While the centers of IHCs and HIHCs are located at integer- and half-integer-harmonics of fp, i.e., lfp and (2n − 1)fp/2 (l, n = 1, 2, 3, …), respectively. Furthermore, the switching of HIHCs is realized by changing the PS modulation amplitude Vs. By modifying the Duffing equation through introducing the bias magnetical force and parametrically modulated stiffness, the theoretical calculations quantitatively reproduce the experimentally observed formation, evolution, and switching of IHCs and HIHCs. Our magnetostrictive mechanical frequency combs could pave the way of exploring magneto-mechanics for non-contacting and high precision metrology especially in acoustic frequency range.

Results

Scenario of generating IHCs and HIHCs

Considering a mechanical resonator with the fundamental mode f0, it would be driven into the nonlinear dynamic regime and generate MFCs by two-tone pumping fields, i.e., a NR pumping Vnp = Vpcos(2πfpt) and a PS modulation Vps = Vscos(2πfst), as schematically shown in Fig. 1. Here, the two pumping frequencies satisfy the condition of fp ≈ f0 and fs ≪ f0. Keeping the NR pumping amplitude Vp as a constant, there will be a threshold Vp0 corresponding to the PD bifurcation point and varying with the PS modulation amplitude Vs. For Vs = 0 and Vp < Vp0, the forced vibration mode and its higher harmonics lfp (l = 1, 2, 3, …) are excited. For Vs = 0 and Vp > Vp0, the resonator will transit to the PD bifurcation state, which is manifested by the appearance of the extra PD vibration modes (2n − 1)fp/2 (n = 1, 2, 3, …) as shown in Fig. 1a. For Vs > 0 and Vp > Vp0, both IHCs and HIHCs are generated via PS modulation as shown in Fig. 1b. The IHCs and HIHCs can be described in the manner of lfp ± mfs and (2n − 1)fp/2 ± mfs, respectively, where m = 1, 2, 3, … represents the mth comb tooth. The bandwidth of both IHCs and HIHCs grows steadily with increasing Vs as shown in Fig. 1b–e. Moreover, the HIHCs will switch between distinct states with Vs. Firstly, the HIHCs switch from (2n − 1)fp/2 ± mfs to (2n − 1)fp/2 + fs/2 ± mfs when Vp0 crosses Vp as shown in Fig. 1b, d. To be noted that the switching can be approached by either superposition or non-comb for Vp0 around Vp. As an instance, Fig. 1c exhibits the superposition of (2n − 1)fp/2 ± mfs and (2n − 1)fp/2 + fs/2 ± mfs. Secondly, the HIHCs will be gradually suppressed and finally disappear when Vs further increases as shown in Fig. 1e. For the sake of simplicity, Fig. 1 only shows the l = 1 and n = 1 case.

Magneto-mechanical system

In order to experimentally demonstrate the proposed scenario of generating tunable MFCs, we build up an experimental setup as schematically shown in Fig. 2a. Two transmission (Tx) coils are utilized to excite the magnetostrictive resonator by providing alternating magnetic fields: Hp = hpcos(2πfpt) from Tx1 for NR pumping; and Hs = hscos(2πfst) from Tx2 for PS modulation. The Hp and Hs are proportional to the applied voltage Vp and Vs, respectively. While the bias magnetic field Hdc is provided by an electromagnet, which can be alternatively replaced by permanent magnets. Based on electromagnetic induction and inverse magnetostrictive effect, the deformation-induced magnetization at frequency f is detected and converted to electrical signal Vin(f) by the receiving coil. It should be noted that the measured IHCs and HIHCs are only attributed to the magnetostrictive resonator, and the effects induced by three coils can be completely ignored (see Supplementary Notes 1, 2).

Here, we prepare the magnetostrictive resonator from the amorphous alloy ribbon (Metglas AYFY-N). The fundamental mechanical mode f0 of the used resonator is determined by Young’s modulus, which can be reversibly determined by Hdc. The measured dependences of f0 and df0/dHdc on Hdc are shown in Fig. 2b, where the f0 is extracted from the S11 spectra. The f0 can be manipulated from MHz to kHz by scaling up the resonator from millimeter to decimeter (see Supplementary Note 3). The left inset in Fig. 2b presents the mode profile of f0 simulated by using the finite element method. The right inset in Fig. 2b shows the measured S11 spectrum with Hdc = 14.3 Oe, at which the resonator has the highest sensitivity to external magnetic fields. To make sure the sensitivity of the resonator, the Hdc used in this work is fixed at 14.3 Oe. The f0 = 150.4 kHz and the mechanical quality factor Q = 192.8 are extracted from the S11 spectrum. The damping rate γ = 4900 s−1 is calculated from 2πf0/Q.

PD bifurcation

We firstly study the effect of Vp on the vibration state of the resonator with fp = 150 kHz and Vs = 0. Fig. 2c shows the color plots of measured Vin(f) spectra as a function of Vp, which can be divided into three regimes. For the NR pumping amplitude Vp < 9.5 V, the resonator is forced into the periodic vibration state with frequency f = fp = 150 kHz. For 9.5 V < Vp < 21.1 V, two new tones f = 0.5fp = 75 kHz and f = 1.5fp = 225 kHz are observed, which indicates the appearance of the PD bifurcation of fp. For Vp > 21.1 V, there is no obvious resonance, i.e., broadly distributed spectrum, which indicates that the vibration bifurcates on the period-doubling route to chaos. Three typical spectra corresponding to three distinct states of period, PD bifurcation, and chaos are clearly shown in Fig. 2d with Vp = 5, 18, and 25 V, respectively.

Formation and evolution of IHCs and HIHCs

We further study the effect of Vs, Vp, and fp on the PD bifurcation states of the resonator. Fig. 3a shows the measured Vin(f) spectra for various Vs with fp = 150 kHz, Vp = 20 V, and fs = 200 Hz. The IHCs locate at fp ± mfs with l = 1 are observed around 150 kHz. It is also found that the comb bandwidth grows steadily by increasing Vs and can approach 20 kHz with the number of comb teeth exceeding 100 for Vs = 750 mV, (see Supplementary Note 6). For the reported MFCs based on mode coupling, the tooth spacing is determined by the discrete eigenfrequencies of the resonator17,23,24. In contrast, the tooth spacing in our work can be continuously tuned by changing fs. Simultaneously, a new type of comb HIHCs is observed at fp/2 ± mfs with n = 1 around 75 kHz. Interestingly, it is observed that the HIHCs multiply switch between mode M1 (fp/2 ± mfs) and mode M2 (fp/2 + fs/2 ± mfs) by increasing Vs. As shown in Fig. 3a, the mode M1 located at 75 kHz is observed for Vs = 150 and 300 mV, while the mode M2 located at 75.1 kHz is observed for Vs = 240 mV. During the transition between modes M1 and M2, there is a non-comb mode for Vs = 230 mV as well as a mixed comb mode (fp/2 ± mfs/2) as the superposition of modes M1 and M2 for Vs = 260 mV. The evolution of HIHCs can be clearly observed from Fig. 3b, which plots the measured Vin(f) spectra as a function of Vs. Fig. 3c shows the amplitude of three comb teeth (Δ = f - fp/2 = −0.1, 0, and 0.1 kHz), and it is found that the HIHCs switch between modes M1 and M2 six times when Vs increases from 0 to 750 mV. The observed asymmetry of HIHCs, larger number and amplitude of comb teeth on high-frequency side, is attributed to a red-shift detuning fp < f0. Similar phenomenon has also been observed in electro-mechanical system using a μm-scale MEMS resonator of silicon50.

The evolution of HIHCs is also highly dependent on near-resonant pumping amplitude Vp. In comparison with Vp = 20 V, the number of comb teeth decreases and the HIHCs switch four times for Vp = 10 V when Vs increases from 0 to 580 mV as shown in Fig. 3d, e. It should be noted that the HIHCs finally disappear for Vs > 580 mV in Fig. 3d and for further increasing Vs in Fig. 3b. To study the effect of detuning fp - f0 on HIHCs, we also measured Vin(f) spectra as a function of fp with a step size of 0.1 kHz for Vp = 10 V and Vs = 200 mV as shown in Fig. 3f. The HIHCs are observed for fp - f0 ranging from −1.9 to 4.2 kHz. This is further confirmed by analyzing the amplitude of the comb teeth Δ = −0.1, 0, and 0.1 kHz as shown in Fig. 3g. Moreover, the tooth spacing of HIHCs can be continuously tuned from Hz to kHz by changing fs (see Supplementary Note 7).

Discussion

The fundamental mode of our magnetostrictive resonator can be reversibly tuned by changing the Young’s modulus via the bias magnetic field. Alternatively, the Young’s modulus can also be thermally modulated through either annealing treatment52 or measuring temperature53. Here, the observed IHCs and HIHCs are generated by applying two-tone pumping magnetic fields, and other pumping manners are also possible, such as inverse piezoelectric effect54, acoustic waves55, electrostatic force56, radiation-pressure23,24, and so on. The mechanical vibrations of our resonator are measured by solenoid coil based on electromagnetic induction and inverse magnetostrictive effect. Our home-made coils can be further minimized by replacing them with commercial medical coils. Instead, the observed phenomena can also be approached by other instruments, such as the laser Doppler vibrometer8,18,57. The dynamic range is a critical factor from the application point of view, since it characterizes the linear response of the resonator to external driving force58,59. Future work will carry out to investigate the dynamic range of our magneto-mechanical system for increasing the signal-to-noise ratio and reliability.

Alternating magnetic fields have been widely used for biotechnology and bioengineering, such as wireless neural stimulator34,47, programming of mammalian transgene expression60, and wireless power for implants61. Meanwhile, ultrasonic waves have also been extensively used for neuroscience, for instance, enhancing vascular permeability in the brain62 and neural stem cell therapy of traumatic brain injury63. The mechanical motion in our magnetostrictive resonator simultaneously results in extra alternating magnetic fields and ultrasonic waves of various frequencies corresponding to that of generated MFCs. Therefore, our magneto-mechanical system could potentially provide more effective dual-functional therapy, which benefits from the cooperative effect of alternating magnetic fields and ultrasonic waves.

In summary, we experimentally demonstrated two types of MFCs, i.e., IHCs and HIHCs, using magneto-mechanical system based on PS modulation and PD bifurcation. The magnetostrictive resonator on mm-scale is magnetically driven by two-tone pumping, i.e., NR pumping fp and PS modulation fs. The bandwidth of first IHC fp + mfs can approach 20 kHz with the number of comb teeth ~100. Interestingly, the HIHCs can switch between mode M1 (2n − 1)fp/2 ± mfs and mode M2 (2n − 1)fp/2 + fs/2 ± mfs, which results from the shift of bifurcation point. The formation and evolution of the observed MFCs can be quantitatively explained by modifying Duffing equation with the bias magnetical force and the parametrically modulated stiffness. This work contributes significantly to the nonlinear dynamics of mechanical systems utilizing magneto-mechanical macrosystems.

Methods

The magnetostrictive macroresonator adopted in this work is fabricated from an amorphous alloy ribbon (Metglas AYFA-N) by fiber laser. The dimensions of used resonator for length, width, and height are 15 mm, 4 mm, and 25 μm, respectively. Figure 2a schematically shows our home-built experimental setup for the measurement of mechanical vibrations. Three coils are coaxially arranged for providing alternating magnetic fields and detecting deformation-induced magnetization. The Tx1 coil is utilized to apply NR pumping Hp = hpcos(2πfpt) by Vnp = Vpcos(2πfpt) via a signal generator (DG5000, RIGOL). The Tx2 coil is utilized to apply PS modulation Hs = hscos(2πfst) by Vps = Vscos(2πfst) via lock-in amplifier (UHF 600 MHz, Zurich) and power amplifier (ATA−1372A, Aigtek). Besides, the bias magnetic field Hdc is provided by an electromagnet (P9060, East Changing). Based on electromagnetic induction and inverse magnetostrictive effect, the deformation-induced magnetization at frequency f is detected via the receiving coil, and the converted electrical signal Vin(f) is measured by vector network analyzer (Ceyear VNA 3657B) or lock-in amplifier. The Vin(f) measured by the lock-in amplifier is post-processed in the time and frequency domains, respectively.
