Structurally tuned g-C3N4 nanosheets via thermal exfoliation for superior photocatalytic oxidative degradation of industrial food dye pollutants
Authors: Silpa Sunil, Badal Kumar Mandal, Srinivasan Latha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGraphitic carbon nitride (g-C3N4), an attractive metal-free photocatalyst, has received significant interest for the treatment of wastewater due to its high chemical stability and visible light responsiveness. The photocatalytic effectiveness of pure g-C3N4 (GCN) is limited due to its low surface area a3nd fast recombination of charge carriers generated by photons. In this work, the physicochemical properties of bulk GCN were systematically modified by direct thermal exfoliation to the thermally exfoliated GCN. The photocatalytic efficiency of the synthesized samples was assessed through the degradation of food dyes Carmoisine (CM) and Indigo Carmine (IC) under UV, visible, and natural sunlight exposure. Structural and morphological analysis (i.e., FTIR, XRD, FE-SEM, EDAX, BET, and UV-DRS) revealed the improved surface properties and optical characteristics of the material. BET surface area measurements significantly increased from 5. 03 m²/g (bulk GCN) to 26.2 m²/g for exfoliated GCN (GCN 550 ˚C). Radical trapping experiments revealed that the superoxide radicals (•O2−) exerted more significant effects on degradation than hydroxyl radicals (•OH) and photoinduced holes (h+) did. The photoluminescence (PL) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) results indicated that exfoliated GCN had lower charge carrier recombination and higher interfacial charge transfer. Moreover, the catalyst displayed remarkable recyclability with only a slight decrease in activity from 92% to 86% across five cycles. The obtained results demonstrate that the exfoliated graphitic carbon nitride is an efficient, stable, and sustainable photocatalyst for wastewater treatment applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s11671-026-04505-w.Keywords: Graphitic carbon nitride, Carmoisine, Indigo Carmine, Photocatalyst, Waste water treatment

Introduction

Food dyes are widely used in the global food industry to enhance the aesthetic appeal and visual attractiveness of a diverse range of food products. These synthetic compounds not only contribute to product appearance but also significantly influence consumer behavior, perception, and marketability [1–3]. Over the years, the dye and pigment industry has experienced substantial expansion, with production volumes nearing 700,000 tons globally and encompassing thousands of dye varieties [4–7]. The market value had already surpassed USD 11 billion by 2008, reflecting its growing demand and industrial relevance (Renita et al.., 2023). Despite their widespread use, the production and application of synthetic food dyes pose serious environmental concerns [8–12]. A considerable fraction of these dyes, approximately 1–2% during manufacturing and an additional 15–25% during processing, are discharged into wastewater streams [13–16]. These losses result in the introduction of persistent, chemically stable, and potentially toxic compounds into natural water systems, thereby threatening the health of aquatic life and ecosystems. Consequently, the urgent need to mitigate dye pollution has driven research toward sustainable and efficient removal technologies, especially photocatalytic approaches under visible-light irradiation. Photocatalysts generate reactive species through photoexcited charge carriers, enabling efficient degradation of complex organic pollutants [17]. While metal-based catalysts have shown good performance, concerns over cost, toxicity, and secondary pollution have accelerated the search for sustainable, metal-free alternatives. This has positioned graphitic carbon nitride (GCN) as a promising photocatalyst due to its visible-light activity, stability, and environmental compatibility [18–23].

As a promising metal-free semiconductor, GCN exhibits unique physicochemical properties along with good sustainability and cost-effectiveness [24]. GCN has gained increasing attention as a photocatalyst due to its visible-light responsiveness, structural stability, and suitable band structure for redox reactions [25–27]. Upon irradiation, GCN generates photoinduced charge carriers capable of degrading toxic pollutants into non-toxic end products like CO2 and H2O. However, the photocatalytic performance of bulk GCN is often limited by its low surface area and the fast recombination of electron–hole pairs, which restricts the generation of reactive species [28–32]. To address these limitations, various modification strategies such as compositing with metal oxides [33], quantum dots, or constructing heterojunctions have been explored [34–37]. Despite numerous reports on thermally exfoliated GCN for the photocatalytic degradation of organic dyes, most studies primarily focus on generic azo dyes or employ bulk-like GCN systems without systematic optimization of key reaction parameters such as solution pH, catalyst loading, and light intensity. Although exfoliated g-C3N4 exhibits enhanced surface area and improved charge separation compared to bulk material, its application has largely remained limited to commonly studied model dyes. Reports addressing the photocatalytic degradation of food dyes such as Carmoisine and Indigo Carmine using thermally exfoliated, bulk-derived GCN are notably scarce in the existing literature. In contrast, the present work employs controlled thermal exfoliation to enhance photocatalytic activity and demonstrates improved degradation of specific food dyes, namely Carmoisine and Indigo Carmine, under optimized conditions, thereby offering a clear methodological distinction and extending the application of thermally exfoliated GCN to food dye degradation.

This study demonstrates a simple thermal exfoliation approach for producing GCN with enhanced surface properties for photocatalytic applications. The material was evaluated for the degradation of food colours CM and IC under UV radiation. Radical scavenging tests and band potential calculations were used to identify active species in the degradation pathway. The study highlights the potential of thermally exfoliated GCN as an inexpensive, metal-free photocatalyst for effective elimination of dyes from aqueous systems, a field that is currently underexplored in the literature.

Experimental section

Materials and methods

High-purity melamine was procured from Alfa Aesar (Ward Hill, MA, USA) and used as the precursor for GCN synthesis. Ethanol (analytical grade) was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) and served as a solvent during the synthesis and washing procedures. Food dyes, including Carmoisine (96% purity) and Indigo Carmine, were supplied by QReC® (Asia) Sdn. Bhd., Malaysia, and were selected as model organic pollutants for photocatalytic degradation studies. All other reagents used in the experiments were of analytical reagent grade and employed without any further purification. Ultrapure water, produced using a Milli-Q water purification system, was used throughout the experimental process for solution preparation, catalyst washing, and dilution of dye solutions. The consistency and reliability of the chemical sources ensured reproducibility and accuracy of the photocatalytic experiments.

Synthesis of bulk GCN nanomaterials

Bulk GCN was prepared following a previously reported method using melamine as the precursor [38]. Briefly, 10 g of melamine were placed in a covered alumina crucible. The crucible was then heated at a rate of 5 °C per minute up to 550 °C under static air conditions. The temperature was maintained for 4 h to allow complete thermal condensation. After natural cooling, the yellowish agglomerates formed were collected and subsequently ground into a fine powder using an agate mortar.

Synthesis of exfoliated GCN nanomaterials

Exfoliated GCN samples were prepared by thermally treating Bulk GCN in an open alumina crucible at different temperatures. The heating was carried out at 450 °C, 500 °C, and 550 °C for a duration of 2 h, with a ramping rate of 5 °C per minute. The resulting materials were designated as GCN 450 °C, GCN 500 °C, and GCN 550 °C, corresponding to their respective calcination temperatures.

Characterisation of synthesised photocatalysts

The structural analysis of the synthesized samples was carried out using X-ray diffraction (XRD) on a Bruker D8-ADVANCE diffractometer, equipped with Cu-Kα radiation (λ = 0.1546 nm). Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectra were recorded using a Perkin Elmer Spectrum 1000 in the 350–4000 cm−1 range, with the samples prepared as KBr pellets. UV–Visible diffuse reflectance spectroscopy (UV-DRS) was conducted using a Specord 210 spectrophotometer (Analytik Jena) to evaluate the optical properties of the materials. The surface morphology of GCN nanomaterials was examined using Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) with an FEI-Tecnai G2 20 Twin microscope. High-Resolution Transmission Electron Microscopy (HR-TEM) was employed to observe the particle size, distribution, and detailed structural features of the GCN materials. Photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy was conducted on a Hitachi F7000 spectrofluorometer to evaluate the charge carrier recombination behavior (e−/h+ pairs) of the synthesized photocatalysts. The specific surface area and porosity were determined using nitrogen adsorption–desorption isotherms measured on a BELSORP-max system via the Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) method. Zeta potential measurements were performed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS to assess the colloidal stability of the photocatalysts in aqueous media. The electrochemical properties were studied using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation (CH Instruments, USA). Degradation efficiency over time was monitored by Ultra-Performance Liquid Chromatography (UPLC) using the WATERS SM-FTN ACQUITY H-CLASS system equipped with a PDA detector. To identify intermediate and final degradation products formed during photocatalysis, Liquid Chromatography coupled with High-Resolution Mass Spectrometry (LC-HRMS) was performed using the WATERS XEVO G2-XS-QToF instrument.

Photocatalytic degradation of food dye experiment

The degradation of the anionic dyes Carmoisine (CM) and Indigo carmine (IC) in simulated wastewater under visible light illumination was used to examine the photocatalytic efficiency of GCN nanocomposites (NCs). To attain the highest degradation efficiency, experimental factors such as solution pH, initial concentration of dye, dosage, and light source characteristics were carefully adjusted. To avoid photodecomposition, 10 mg of dye was dissolved in 100 mL of double-distilled water (DDW) to create a stock solution of CM and IC (100 ppm), which was then left in the dark at room temperature. Appropriate serial dilutions were then used to create working solutions with lower concentrations.

A series of operational variables, including the pH of the solution (3–9), initial dye concentration (10–50 ppm), photocatalyst dosage (0.2–1.0 mg/mL), and irradiation source (UV lamp, 250 W at 365 nm; visible tungsten lamp, 300 W; and direct solar light) were optimized to assess their influence on performance. Scavenger method

Scavengers were used to determine the reactive species responsible for the photocatalytic degradation of CM/IC food dye. Several scavengers, including ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), isopropyl alcohol (IPA), p-benzoquinone (PBQ), and silver nitrate (AgNO3), were used to inhibit the activity of particular reactive species, including electrons, hydroxyl radicals, holes, and superoxide radicals, during the degradation process. Before the dye solution was exposed to UV light in each experiment, 0.2 mg/mL of GCN catalyst and 2.0 mmol/L of the corresponding scavenger were added separately. The major reactive species responsible for dye decomposition were carefully determined by examining the accompanying changes in photocatalytic degradation efficiency.

Electrochemical study

The catalyst’s charge transfer properties were assessed using electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) in an electrolyte solution that contained 0.1 M KCl and 5 mM [Fe (CN)6]3−/4−. The GCN catalyst (10 mg/mL) was homogeneously dispersed in the solvent for 30 min using a bath sonicator to prepare the electrode. Eight microliters of the evenly distributed suspension were then drop-cast onto a screen-printed carbon electrode (SPCE) and left to dry at room temperature naturally.

Result and discussion

XRD analysis

XRD analysis was performed to investigate the crystallographic properties of the prepared GCN samples (Fig. 1). All samples showed distinctive diffraction peaks corresponding to the standard JCPDS card No. 87-1526, indicating the formation of GCN [41]. Two main peaks were identified: a prominent peak at approximately 27°, attributed to the (002) plane, which represents the interlayer stacking of aromatic structures. Another peak near ~ 12° assigned to the (100) plane), shows the structural packing of tri-s-triazine pattern [42]. Notably, thermally exfoliated GCN showed broader and less intense peaks compared to the bulk material, implying a decrease in crystallinity and reduced stacking due to exfoliation. This structural alteration suggests increased porosity and surface area, both advantageous for photocatalytic performance. FT-IR

Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was used to detect the functional groups and chemical bonding in the synthesized samples, as illustrated in (Fig. 2a). The absorption peak at 806 cm−1 represents the breathing vibration of the tri-s-triazine framework, an essential component of GCN. The g-C3N4 framework exhibits a broad absorption band between 3000 and 3500 cm−1 due to stretching vibrations of hydroxyl (O-H) and amine (N-H) groups. This band could be caused by surface-adsorbed molecules of water or terminal amino groups. Furthermore, the stretching vibrations of C–N and C = N heterocycles are linked to the absorption bands between 1200 and 1650 cm−1 [43]. These similarity in vibrational features across all samples indicates that thermal exfoliation does not significantly alter the fundamental chemical structure of GCN.

Optical properties

UV-Vis- diffuse reflectance spectroscopy was used to analyze the optical absorption properties of thermally treated GCN samples. As shown in Fig. 2b, the absorption edge of GCN progressively shifted toward longer wavelengths for exfoliated GCN with increasing calcination temperature, indicative of enhanced light absorption. The corresponding band gap energies were estimated using Tauc plots (Fig. 2c), showing band gap energies of 2.77, 2.76, and 2.66 eV for exfoliated CGN samples treated at 450 °C, 500 °C, and 550 °C. The band gap narrows with increasing temperature, suggesting a thermally induced alteration in the electronic structure, which may facilitate improved photocatalytic performance under UV light irradiation. Electrochemical studies

Photoluminescence (PL) analysis of the synthesized photocatalysts has been used to investigate the charge carrier recombination rate. Figure 3a illustrates the PL spectra of GCN bulk and thermally exfoliated GCN samples excited at 300 nm. Among the samples, the material exfoliated GCN at 550 °C shows a lower intensity of PL than the others, which indicates the significant suppression of charge carrier recombination. This enhanced charge separation is expected to contribute to improved photocatalytic performance. Therefore, the exfoliated GCN 550°C sample demonstrates superior photocatalytic activity compared to bulk GCN, highlighting its potential as a more efficient photocatalyst.

a PL analysis of Bulk GCN and GCN 550 ˚C, b EIS analysis of Bulk GCN and GCN 550 ˚C

Electrochemical characterization offers valuable insights into charge transport behavior, separating the efficiency of charge carriers created by light in photocatalysts. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was used to determine the interfacial charge transfer resistance and the efficacy of charge carrier separation. Nyquist plots of bulk GCN and thermally processed GCN 550 at frequencies ranging from 0.1 Hz to 1 MHz (Fig. 3b). The semicircular arc for GCN 550 is substantially narrower than that of bulk GCN, indicating a substantial reduction in charge transfer resistance. The reduction in arc diameter demonstrates increased carrier mobility and reduced electron-hole recombination, resulting in enhanced photocatalytic performance for GCN 550.

These results indicate that the recombination rate of charge light-induced carriers is significantly reduced, according to the photoluminescence (PL) results. Additionally, the enhanced electron and hole separation and transfer are confirmed by the Nyquist analysis. Since the catalyst successfully encourages charge carrier separation while reducing recombination losses, two essential components in reaching higher photocatalytic performance, such as excellent charge dynamics, are essential for increasing the photocatalytic activity.

Morphology analysis

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM) and field Emission Scanning Electron Microscopy (FE-SEM) were employed to investigate the surface morphology of the synthesized photocatalysts at different magnification levels. The corresponding micrographs, which reveal distinct morphological features, are displayed in Fig. 4. They represent the morphological characteristics of the synthesized materials, emphasizing how thermal treatment temperature influences their exfoliation. The pristine bulk GCN showed dense (Fig. 4a), stacked sheets with a smooth, compact structure and minimal porosity. In contrast, samples subjected to higher exfoliation temperatures revealed progressively thinner and more fragmented nanosheets, along with increased surface roughness and layer separation, as evident in (Fig. 4b-d). These structural changes are consistent with typical exfoliation behaviour observed in similar thermally treated carbon nitride systems. The FESEM morphology of the exfoliated sample obtained shows (Fig. 4g-h) thinly layered thin nanosheets along having an additional porous and crumpled surface, while bulk GCN shows (Fig. 4e) thickly stacked, irregular block-like structures. This change attests to the material’s effective exfoliation and enhanced surface exposure.

Chemical composition and oxidation states

X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to determine the elemental composition and chemical states of the produced catalyst. The survey XPS spectrum (Fig. 6a) clearly verifies the existence of carbon (C) and nitrogen (N) elements within the GCN framework. Figure 6b shows that the high-resolution C 1s spectra of bulk GCN are separated into two peaks at binding energies of 288.2 eV and 284.9 eV. The other peak at 288.2 eV is the sp²-hybridized carbon (N-C = N) within the aromatic triazine pattern of GCN, while the peak at 284.9 eV is connected with the sp2 C = C and C-NH2 surface groups. The N 1s spectra of Bulk GCN (Fig. 6c) show peaks at 398.7 eV and 400.15 eV, corresponding to the sp²-hybridized nitrogen atoms in the C = N-C configuration of the triazine ring and tertiary nitrogen (N-(C)3) species. Figure 6d and e show the XPS spectra obtained from the 550 sample in the C 1s and N 1s region. The C 1s shows two major peaks with binding energy values of 288.34 eV and 285.34 eV, representing the (N-C = N) and (C = C) bonding surroundings, respectively. The N 1s spectra show two distinct peaks at 398.86 eV and 401.1 eV, indicating triazine rings and hybridized nitrogen atoms are bound to carbon inside aromatic networks. The results show that GCN 550 kept its chemical structure even after undergoing heat treatment.

Surface area analysis

The synthesized photocatalyst’s specific surface area was examined utilizing the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) technique for nitrogen physisorption. The materials exhibit Type IV isotherms with H3 hysteresis loops, indicating the presence of mesoporous structures, which is consistent with typical characteristics reported in mesoporous solids. These patterns suggest that adsorption occurs through both monolayer and multilayer mechanisms. As the thermal exfoliation temperature increased, a notable enhancement in surface area and porosity was observed. The measured BET surface areas for GCN Bulk, GCN 450 °C, GCN 500 °C, and GCN 550 °C were 9.035 m2/g, 10.189 m2/g, 13.13 m2/g, and 26.2 m2/g, respectively (Fig. 7a–d). This improvement in surface area at higher exfoliation temperatures is likely due to increased surface roughness, as seen in FE-SEM images, and correlates well with the crystallinity changes observed in XRD patterns.

Photocatalytic performance of bulk GCN towards Carmoisine and Indigo Carmine dyes

The degradation efficacy of Bulk and exfoliated GCN samples was investigated under the irradiation of different sources of light at ambient temperatures. Carmoisine and Indigo Carmine dyes possess distinct physicochemical characteristics that influence their degradation efficiency under UV light irradiation. In order to maximize the photocatalytic conditions for the effective degradation of each dye, a number of tests were carried out using both bulk GCN and thermally exfoliated GCN samples. The optimization parameters were tailored separately for each dye system. Degradation progress was monitored at regular time intervals using UV–Vis spectrophotometry. A 250 W/cm² UV light source was utilised to irradiate a suspension of 0.1 g photocatalyst, and 10 ppm of CM and IC dyes with a pH of 3 for CM and a pH of 5 for IC, which were examined independently. To assess the level of self-degradation, control tests were carried out in which the dye solutions were exposed to UV light in the absence of any catalyst. The exfoliated samples exhibited improved adsorption capacities over the bulk material, which can be attributed to their increased surface area, as confirmed by BET analysis. The overall photocatalytic efficiency followed the trend: bulk < 450 < 500 < 550, showing the best degradation results, achieving 93.2%, 83.5%, 79.1%, and 74.2% for CM dye, and 77.8%, 79.9%, 84.5%, and 95.5% for IC dye. According to preliminary findings, exfoliated GCN 550 has substantially higher photocatalytic activity for both dyes than bulk GCN. This is due to its enormous surface area, better charge separation, and higher capacity to absorb visible light. Due to its superior photocatalytic efficiency, GCN 550 was chosen for further studies. The pH of the solutions was systematically varied from 3 to 9, and the samples were irradiated under UV light for approximately 140 min. The pH of a solution is directly related to its surface charge and has a significant impact on deterioration [45–47]. Reaction and reduction occur when the catalyst’s interactions with the analyte grow. In this study, the impact of pH was evaluated in the range 3–9 at 10 ppm and 1 mg/mL doses of catalyst for CM and IC dye degradation. Figures 9a and 10a show the kinetic study of Bulk GCN for the degradation of CM and IC. At pH 3, the degradation efficiency was highest, removing 72.41% of CM. The breakdown of IC dye increases in rate as the pH ranges from 3 to 9, with the maximum efficiency at pH 7. Beyond this limit, photocatalytic activity decreases with increasing pH. Although pH 7 is close to neutral pH, which promotes the development of reactive species such as hydroxyl radicals, strongly acidic and highly alkaline conditions can inhibit their production. Lower pH values, for example, inhibit intermediate formation, limiting total breakdown efficiency.

In the photocatalytic process, the concentration of the dye is crucial in influencing the effectiveness of photodegradation. Numerous investigations have found that increasing dye concentration generally reduces the degradation efficiency and the reaction rate. In the current investigation, equivalent degradation rates were reported for both dyes at 10 ppm concentrations, under identical conditions: 0.1 mg/mL catalyst, pH 3 for CM and pH 6 for IC, and UV light irradiation (250 W, 365 nm) (Figs. 9b and 10b). These results align with previous studies, further confirming that higher dye concentrations hurt photocatalytic efficiency. At a dye concentration of 50 ppm, both the degradation efficiency and reaction rate declined significantly. This decrease can be attributable to numerous sources. First, larger dye concentrations cause the dye to absorb more incident light, particularly within the photocatalyst’s activation region, reducing the amount of light available to excite the catalyst. Second, too many dye molecules can saturate the catalyst surface, limit active sites, and inhibit the production of electron-hole pairs required for photocatalysis. Third, increased optical path length at greater concentrations restricts photon penetration into the solution, lowering overall photocatalytic activity. As a result, the effectiveness of Bulk GCN in degrading dyes declines dramatically at high dye concentrations, eventually plateauing at lower concentrations.

The efficiency of dye degradation is significantly impacted by the quantity of photocatalyst used, and the goal is to find out the ideal dose to optimise degradation while avoiding excessive catalyst usage. To assess this impact, the catalyst loading was changed from 0.1 to 1.0 mg/mL at pH 3 for CM and pH 6 for IC, as illustrated in Figs. 9c and 10c. Both the degradation efficiency and reaction rate increased with catalyst loading up to 1.0 mg/mL. The primary cause of this enhancement is a greater number of active sites on the surface of GCN nanomaterial, which promotes the production of extremely reactive ●OH radicals that are essential for efficient dye degradation.

The intensity of light is crucial in photocatalytic degradation as it directly affects the production of reactive hydroxyl radicals. In semiconductor photocatalysts, photon energy drives the formation of electron–hole pairs, an essential step for producing reactive species involved in dye degradation. As light intensity increases, more electrons are excited from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB), leading to enhanced formation of hydroxyl and oxygen radicals. To evaluate the effect of light source intensity, we employed different irradiation sources: UV light (250 W, 365 nm), visible light (300 W), and natural sunlight, while keeping all other parameters constant (pH 3 for CM, pH 6 for IC, dye concentration of 10 ppm, and catalyst dosage of 1.0 mg/mL), as illustrated in Figs. 9d and 10d. Among these, UV light irradiation resulted in the highest reaction rates for both dyes. Photocatalytic study of GCN 550 nanomaterial for the degradation of food dyes

The photocatalytic activity of GCN 550 nanomaterial was investigated by observing the breakdown of CM and IC food dyes under UV irradiation for 120 min. The rate of degradation was evaluated regularly. A UV-Vis spectrophotometer was used to measure the degradation kinetics at regular intervals, and the results showed a steady decrease in absorbance at 517 nm for CM and 617 nm for IC, indicating progressive dye decomposition. Among the various samples tested, GCN-550 exhibited the highest photocatalytic performance and was therefore selected for further studies and comparison with bulk GCN.

Effect of pH

The pH level of the reaction media is critical for the degradation of food dyes, as it affects the catalyst’s surface charge. Consequently, this controls how the catalyst and dye molecules interact. The current study systematically investigated the pH range of 3 to 9 using buffer solutions, maintaining a constant dye concentration of 10 ppm and a catalyst loading of 1 mg/mL. Figures 11a and 12a show the degradation of CM and IC under UV radiation. The results reveal that maximum degradation efficiencies of 93.2% for CM and 95.5% for IC were achieved at pH values less than the point of zero charge (PZC), which was determined to be 7.7. This indicates that the catalytic activity is significantly enhanced when the surface charge favors strong electrostatic interactions with the anionic dye molecules. As the optimum pH lies below the point of zero charge (PZC), the surface of GCN550 becomes positively charged, thereby enhancing the electrostatic attraction toward the negatively charged CM and IC dye molecules. At this acidic pH, enhanced adsorption occurs due to the protonation of dye molecules, potentially leading to the formation of zwitterionic species, which further promotes effective contact with the surface of the catalyst. Influence of concentration

Optimizing the concentration of analytes like CM and IC becomes essential for their practical use in photocatalytic applications. Photocatalytic studies of CM/IC were carried out at different initial dye concentrations: 10–50 ppm. The catalyst dosage was fixed at 1 mg/mL, and the pH was maintained at pH 3 for CM and pH 6 for IC throughout the experiments. As shown in Figs. 10b and 12b, the degradation efficiencies for CM were found to be 93.2%, 78.3%, 73.6%, 52%, 30.2%, for IC 95.6%, 85.3%, 78.3%, 59.6%, 39.5%, respectively. The associated first-order rate constants are included in Tables S3 and S4. Here, it’s clearly visible that dye concentration has a significant negative correlation with photocatalytic efficiency. This decline in performance at higher concentrations is likely due to excessive dye molecules occupying the catalyst surface, thereby hindering the interaction between the active sites and light photons, and subsequently reducing the overall photocatalytic activity.

Effect of catalyst dosage

The impact of the photocatalytic dosage on CM and IC dye degradation under light irradiation. The experiments were carried out by varying the catalyst concentrations to 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mg/mL, while keeping the dye concentration fixed at 10 ppm and maintaining the solution at pH 3 for CM and pH 6 for IC. The corresponding degradation efficiencies recorded for CM dye degradations were 55.2, 67.2, 93.2, 83.2, and 85%, and for IC dye, 59.3%, 63%,71.5%, 84.9%, and 95.5%, respectively. As depicted in Figs. 11c and 12c, the degradation performance initially improved with increasing catalyst amount, reaching an optimal value at 1.00 mg/mL. At larger catalyst concentrations, light scattering and less photon penetration led to a drop in performance.

Influence of light sources

Light intensity plays a critical role in the production of reactive •OH, considered necessary for the efficient photocatalytic decomposition of pollutants. In semiconductor materials, the strength of incoming photons determines the creation of electron–hole pairs.

The production of reactive species required for dye mineralization is started by these charge carriers. The production of hydroxyl (•OH) and superoxide (•O2−) radicals is increased as incident light intensity rises because more electrons are driven from the valence band (VB) to the conduction band (CB) [48]. In this study, the effect of light intensity was examined using 250, 150 W UV light (365 nm), 300 W visible light, and natural sunlight. Although the Tauc analysis indicates a band gap of ~ 2.7 eV, the visible-light activity remains limited due to rapid charge recombination. In contrast, UV irradiation, particularly at 250 W, significantly enhanced degradation efficiency by enabling effective charge separation. Among the catalysts, thermally exfoliated GCN 550 consistently showed higher photocatalytic activity than bulk GCN under all light sources, attributed to its increased surface area, reduced layer thickness, and improved charge transport. Photocatalytic mechanism

Scavenger studies were conducted to identify the reactive species responsible for photodegrading CM and IC dyes, as shown in Fig. 14. The GCN 550 catalyst was used in these studies to assess the contribution of various reactive species produced throughout the reaction. To specifically quench particular radicals, a variety of scavenging agents were introduced: silver nitrate (AgNO3) was used as an electron trap; ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid disodium salt (EDTA) was used to capture photogenerated holes (h+); isopropyl alcohol (IPA) was used as a scavenger for hydroxyl radicals (•OH); and p-benzoquinone (PBA) was utilized to inhibit superoxide radicals (•O2−). Following the addition of p-benzoquinone (PBQ), a notable decrease in dye degradation efficiency was observed, particularly for all tested dyes. A moderate decline in activity was also detected in the presence of EDTA with statistical significance (p = 0.0038), suggesting that photogenerated holes (h+) appear to play a minimal role in the photocatalytic process [49]. Whereas, adding IPA as a hydroxyl radical scavenger resulted in a negligible change in degradation efficiency, indicating a limited contribution from •OH radicals. This observation aligns with previous reports, which suggest that the valence band potential of GCN is not sufficiently positive to facilitate •OH radical formation [50–53]. To support these findings and further clarify the roles of reactive species, the band edge positions of the synthesized catalysts were evaluated. The introduction of scavengers resulted in a significant decrease in degradation efficiency from 95% (without scavengers) to 78%, 77%, 52%, and 31% upon the addition of IPA, AgNO3, EDTA, and PBQ, respectively. The inhibition order follows PBQ > EDTA > AgNO3 > IPA, indicating that superoxide radicals (•O2−) play a dominant role in the degradation process, while photogenerated holes (h+) also contribute significantly. GCN 550 functions as an effective photocatalyst when exposed to UV light by creating electron–hole pairs via photon absorption. The excited electrons in the conduction band (CB) of GCN can react with oxygen (O₂) molecules present on the surface, leading to the formation of superoxide radicals (•O2−). The photocatalytic degradation of CM and IC dyes mostly depends on these reactive oxygen species. The scavenger study also proves that •O2− is the primary active species, because when p-benzoquinone was added, the degradation process was highly reduced. Photogenerated holes (h+) also participate as a secondary species, directly oxidizing dye molecules. Reusability of the GCN 550 photocatalyst

The reusability and stability of the most efficient photocatalyst, GCN 550, were investigated over five successive degradation cycles using CM and IC. As illustrated in Fig. 16a, the catalyst maintained excellent photocatalytic activity throughout the cycles, with just a minor decline in degradation efficiency from roughly 92% to 86.2% after the fifth cycle. This modest decline could be ascribed to material loss during the recovery process, notably from centrifugation. XRD measurements were used to determine structural stability after reusability. Conclusion

GCN 550 was prepared by thermally exfoliating the GCN bulk at 550 °C. Among the tested samples, the material treated at GCN 550 °C exhibited superior photocatalytic efficacy compared to the bulk GCN, and samples were exfoliated at lower temperatures. Structural characterization using SEM and BET confirmed that the thermally exfoliated GCN possessed a rough, porous morphology and a higher surface area of 48.203 m²/g. The enhanced surface area and a band gap of approximately 2.7 eV contributed to its improved performance. Under 120 min of UV light irradiation, GCN 550 achieved degradation efficiencies of 93.2%for CM and 95.5% for IC, both of which followed first-order kinetics. Analytical techniques such as FT-IR, TEM, SEM, XPS, BET, PL, EIS, UV-Visible absorption spectroscopy, and analysis were employed. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) and photoluminescence (PL) analyses supported these findings, demonstrating enhanced charge mobility and reduced recombination rates across the materials. These improvements followed the order: bulk GCN < GCN 450 < GCN 500 < GCN 550. The radical scavenging experiments revealed that superoxide (•O2−) species were primarily responsible for the dye degradation process, while photogenerated holes contributed to a lesser extent. However, further research is still needed to fully understand the contribution of photogenerated holes to the overall photocatalytic process. Due to its low cost, straightforward synthesis method, high photocatalytic efficiency, and strong reusability, GCN 550 can be considered a versatile catalyst for degrading CM/IC dyes from industrial wastewater. This study also opens avenues for the tailored design of photocatalysts with enhanced activity, paving the way for broader applications in environmental cleanup and pollutant remediation.

Limitations of the study

Although the thermally exfoliated GCN sample demonstrated remarkable photocatalytic activity toward the degradation of industrial food dyes, a few constraints should be recognized. The prolonged operational stability and structural endurance of the photocatalyst under continuous irradiation were not thoroughly evaluated, emphasizing the need for additional long-term studies. While high degradation efficiency was achieved in controlled laboratory dye solutions, the performance may diminish in real wastewater environments because of the presence of complex constituents, such as inorganic ions, surfactants, organic matter, and suspended particles, which can impede the process by blocking active sites, scattering light, or deactivating reactive radicals. Furthermore, although the mechanistic behavior was explored through radical trapping and spectroscopic analyses, the quantitative identification of degradation intermediates and the stepwise transformation pathway remains insufficiently understood. The relationship between the modified surface structure, charge carrier separation, and photocatalytic performance also requires deeper exploration. Moreover, the synthesis route adopted in this work was demonstrated only at a small laboratory scale, and its scalability, energy efficiency, and economic viability for industrial implementation have not yet been validated. Therefore, future investigations should focus on assessing the real-world applicability of wastewater, evaluating the long-term reusability of catalysts, and designing doped or hybridized GCN systems to enhance visible-light-driven photocatalytic efficiency further.

Future studies

Future studies should concentrate on assessing the photocatalyst’s performance in actual industrial wastewater under continuous flow circumstances in order to progress the practical use of this photocatalytic system. For real-world deployment, it will be crucial to examine its long-lasting durability, regeneration capability, and efficiency under various environmental circumstances. It may be possible to enhance charge carrier mobility and extend light absorption by combining GCN with different semiconductors, metals, or carbonaceous substances to create heterostructures or doped composites. For industrial-level applications, it will also be essential to optimize synthesis methodologies toward reproducible, energy-efficient, and environmentally friendly ways. Integrating GCN-based photocatalysts into constant-flow or solar-driven reactors could improve practical implementation. Overall, future efforts should prioritize material engineering, mechanistic knowledge, and reactor design in order to transfer laboratory findings into long-term, large-scale wastewater treatment systems.
