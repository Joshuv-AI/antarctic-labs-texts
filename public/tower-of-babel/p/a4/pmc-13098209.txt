Disparities in HPV/PAP Testing Among U.S. Women: A Cross‐Sectional Analysis of Healthcare Access, Internet Use, and Sociodemographic Factors
Authors: Grace Kwakyewaa Kyei, Anita Frimpomaa Oppong, Rockson Ansong, Hannah Durowaah Odei Opoku, Evans Frimpong Kyei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsCervical cancer remain a major public health concern, especially among underserved women. Early detection through Pap or HPV testing reduces incidence and mortality, yet screening disparities persist due to socio‐demographic factors, healthcare access, and digital health literacy. Because NHIS does not distinguish between Pap and HPV tests, screening prevalence may be slightly overestimated. This study examines how healthcare access, internet use, and socio‐demographic characteristics shape screening behaviors using 2021–2023 NHIS data.MethodsA weighted sample of 106,218,939 U.S. adult women from NHIS was analyzed using logistic regression models. The outcome variable was ever having undergone an HPV or Pap test. Independent variables included healthcare access, internet use, self‐reported health status, age, race/ethnicity, education, and marital status. Interaction terms between race/education and race/age were examined to assess subgroup disparities.ResultsWomen with a usual place for care (OR = 1.67, p < 0.001) and those who used the internet for health information (OR = 1.54, p < 0.001) had significantly higher odds of undergoing screening. Younger age, higher education, and being single/never married were associated with increased screening participation. Racial/ethnic disparities were evident, with Non‐Hispanic Black (OR = 1.41, p < 0.01), Non‐Hispanic Asian (OR = 2.96, p < 0.001), and Hispanic women (OR = 1.98, p < 0.001) being more likely to be screened than Non‐Hispanic White women. Interaction analyzes revealed that education significantly increased screening rates among Hispanic women, while older Hispanic and NHB women had higher screening odds compared to NHW women in the same age groups.ConclusionHealthcare access, digital health literacy, and socio‐demographic factors significantly influence cervical cancer screening uptake. Targeted interventions should prioritize early screening adherence, enhance healthcare access, and improve digital health literacy to address disparities in cervical cancer prevention, particularly among younger women and underserved racial/ethnic.Keywords: healthcare access, HPV testing, NHIS, sociodemographic disparities

Introduction

Cervical cancer remains a significant public health concern, particularly in low‐ and middle‐income countries, but it also affects women in the United States [1]. Each year, over 13,000 U.S. women are diagnosed with cervical cancer, and approximately 4000 die from the disease [2]. Screening through Pap smears and HPV tests is critical for early detection, reducing both incidence and mortality. Pap tests detect abnormal cervical cells, while HPV tests identify high‐risk virus strains that cause cervical cancer. NHIS does not differentiate between Pap and HPV tests, which may slightly overestimate screening prevalence. Despite medical advances, disparities persist in screening rates across different U.S. populations [1]. Several socio‐demographic factors, including race, education, income, and healthcare access, contribute to these disparities. Minority groups such as non‐Hispanic Black (NHB), Hispanic, and non‐Hispanic Asian (NHA) women are often less likely to receive routine screenings than non‐Hispanic White (NHW) women [1, 3, 4]. Barriers include systemic inequities in healthcare, cultural beliefs, and communication gaps with providers [5, 6]. Access to a usual source of care, such as a primary care provider, has been identified as a key determinant of screening behavior [7, 8]. Women without a consistent healthcare provider are less likely to undergo preventive screenings.

The rise of digital technology, particularly the internet, has become an emerging factor in healthcare access [9, 10]. By 2016, 89 percent of U.S. households had a computer, reflecting high internet access [11]. Many adults use the internet to search for medical advice, learn about diseases, and explore treatment options [12]. In the context of cervical cancer screening, internet access may empower women by increasing awareness of screening recommendations and encouraging participation. However, disparities exist in internet use, with older, less educated, and minority women being less likely to use online health resources [13, 14]. This gap may limit access to crucial information that promotes screening adherence. Although studies have examined healthcare access and internet use separately, there is a need to understand how these factors intersect with socio‐demographics in shaping screening behaviors. Research consistently shows that women with a usual healthcare provider are significantly more likely to undergo routine screenings [7, 15, 16]. Similarly, internet use for health information has been linked to higher screening across different cancer types [17, 18]. Screening guidelines have also evolved and understanding how these shifts influence disparities is important. While earlier recommendations suggested started screening at 21, the 2020 American Cancer Society guideline shifted the starting age to 25, potentially affecting adherence among younger women.

The National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) is a comprehensive dataset that provides insights into health behaviors across the U.S. NHIS does not specify whether HPV testing refers to DNA or RNA assays, and responses are treated as a combined category. By analyzing NHIS data from 2021 to 2023, this study examines the association between healthcare access, internet use, and socio‐demographics with cervical cancer screening uptake. The outcome variable is whether women have ever undergone a Pap smear or HPV test. Independent variables include access to a usual healthcare provider, internet use for health‐related information, self‐reported health status, age, race, education, and marital status. This study excludes women under 21 to align with current screening guidelines, since routine cervical cancer screening is not recommended before this age. These factors were selected because they represent the strongest predictors of disparities identified in prior national studies (US Preventive Services Task Force, 2018). The analysis assesses screening disparities across age groups, considering how guideline shifts such as the ACS recommendation to start at 25 may influence participation. This study provides critical insights into cervical cancer screening disparities, particularly among underserved populations. Understanding how healthcare access and internet use intersect with socio‐demographics can inform strategies to improve screening rates and healthcare equity. The findings can guide policy decisions related to preventive care and digital health literacy, ultimately contributing to reducing cervical cancer incidence and mortality in the U.S.

Methods

Design and Data Source

This study is a secondary data analysis using data from the National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) 2021‐2023 cycle. NHIS is a nationally representative, cross‐sectional survey conducted annually by the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) to assess health behaviors, healthcare access, and health outcomes among U.S. residents. The primary purpose of NHIS is to monitor national health trends, inform public health policies, and evaluate healthcare access and utilization. While NHIS collects extensive data on preventive health services, including cancer screening behaviors, it is not designed explicitly for cervical cancer screening research. In this context, a key limitation of NHIS is that the survey does not differentiate between specific types of cervical cancer screening tests. The screening question asks whether respondents have ever received a cervical cancer screening test, which includes both Pap and HPV tests without distinguishing between them or identifying whether testing was done through co‐testing. As a result, this study cannot separately analyze trends in Pap‐only testing, HPV‐only testing, or co‐testing. Prior research indicates that combining Pap and HPV measures in NHIS may slightly overestimate true screening prevalence due to participant misclassification, although population‐level disparities remain consistent [19]. Despite this limitation, NHIS remains one of the most comprehensive and widely used datasets for studying population‐level disparities in cancer screening behaviors. Its large, representative sample and inclusion of key socio‐demographic and healthcare access variables provide valuable insights into factors influencing cervical cancer screening uptake in the U.S. population. This study follows established epidemiological methodologies for analyzing cross‐sectional survey data [20]. The study design adheres to guidelines for reporting observational studies (STROBE) to ensure transparency and replicability [21].

Study Variables

Outcome Variable

The primary outcome variable was whether the respondent had ever received a cervical cancer screening test. This was based on the question: “Have you EVER HAD a test or tests to check for cervical cancer?” This includes the Pap smear (Pap test) and the HPV (Human Papillomavirus) test. The variable was dichotomized as Yes (1) for those who had ever received a test and No (0) for those who had never been screened. NHIS does not distinguish whether HPV testing was performed using DNA or RNA assays; therefore, all self‐reported HPV tests are treated as a combined category.

Independent Variables

1. Health Access: This variable assesses whether the respondent has a usual place for healthcare. Respondents were asked, “Do you have a usual place to go when you are sick or need advice about your health?” The responses were categorized as Yes (1) if the individual reported having one or more places for care and No (0) if they reported having no place of care.

2. Internet Use for Health Information: Internet use was measured by the question, “During the past 12 months, have you used the internet to look for health or medical information?” Responses were coded as Yes (1) for those who used the internet for this purpose and No (0) for those who did not.

3. Self‐Reported Health Status: This variable was categorized into three groups based on the respondent's self‐reported overall health status. Statistical Analysis Plan

The statistical analysis examined the relationship between having a usual source of healthcare and cervical cancer screening (HPV or Pap testing), while controlling for internet use, health status, and sociodemographic factors. All analyzes followed the Statistical Analyzes and Methods in the Published Literature (SAMPL) guidelines to ensure rigorous reporting [22]. For descriptive analysis, we calculated weighted percentages to summarize participant characteristics and the distribution of cervical cancer screening across key variables. Chi‐square tests assessed differences in categorical variables between screened and unscreened women, with significance set at p < 0.05. For multivariable analysis, we developed three distinct logistic regression models: Model 1 A: Main Effects Model. This model assessed the direct associations of all independent variables with HPV/Pap testing, including healthcare access, internet use, health status, age, race/ethnicity, education, and marital status. Model 1B: Race‐Education Interaction Model Building on Model 1 A, this model incorporated interaction terms between race/ethnicity and education to examine whether the effect of education on screening varies across racial/ethnic groups. Model 1 C: Race‐Age Interaction Model This model included interaction terms between race/ethnicity and age to identify whether age‐related screening patterns differ across racial/ethnic groups. Race–education and race–age interactions were selected based on established evidence that these factors are the strongest social determinants driving screening disparities, while other interactions lack comparable theoretical support. All models included the same covariates and were adjusted for the complex survey design of NHIS, incorporating survey weights, clustering, and stratification to ensure nationally representative estimates. Interaction terms were tested for statistical significance, with a significant interaction identified between race/ethnicity and education (p < 0.05). Results are presented as Odds Ratios (ORs) with Standard Errors (SE) and p‐values. Sensitivity analyzes were performed to account for potential biases in self‐reported screening behaviors, examining variations in screening rates across different subgroups. All statistical tests were two‐sided. Analyzes were conducted using Stata version 17.0 [23], a statistical software designed to analyze complex survey data properly. This study adheres to the CONSORT guidelines to ensure transparency and replicability [24].

Healthcare access was significantly associated with screening status (p < 0.001), with 93.6% of screened women reporting a usual place for care compared to 87.8% of unscreened women. Similarly, internet usage showed a significant relationship with screening behavior (p < 0.001), as 72.1% of screened women reported using the internet for health information versus only 57.6% of unscreened women.

Age distribution varied significantly between screened and unscreened groups (p < 0.001). Notably, women aged 21‐24 comprised only 4.9% of the screened group but 20.8% of the unscreened group, highlighting substantially lower screening rates among younger women.

The highest screening rates were observed among women aged 35–49 (27.3%) and 50–64 (27.1%). Racial/ethnic disparities were evident in the screening patterns (p < 0.001). Non‐Hispanic White (NHW) women constituted 67.4% of the screened group compared to only 47.9% of the unscreened group. In contrast, Hispanic women represented 15.3% of the screened group but 27.2% of the unscreened group, suggesting lower screening rates in this population.

Education level was strongly associated with screening status (p < 0.001). Women with bachelor's degrees (24.2%) and graduate degrees (16.3%) were better represented in the screened group compared to the unscreened group (18.7% and 7.5%, respectively). Conversely, women with less than a high school education made up only 6.7% of the screened group but 12.2% of the unscreened group.

Marital status also demonstrated significant differences between groups (p < 0.001). Married women constituted 57.1% of the screened group versus 41.8% of the unscreened group, while single/never married women represented 34.0% of the screened group but 49.8% of the unscreened group.

Logistic Regression Analysis

Model 1A: Main Effects

Model 1A examined the direct associations between our key variables and cervical cancer screening (Table 2). Healthcare access emerged as a significant predictor, with women having a usual place for care being 67% more likely to undergo screening compared to those without regular healthcare access (OR = 1.67, p < 0.001). Similarly, women who used the internet for health information had 54% higher odds of being screened (OR = 1.54, p < 0.001). Self‐reported health status showed significant associations with screening behavior, as women reporting “Good” health status had 19% higher odds of screening (OR = 1.19, p < 0.01) and those reporting “Poor” health had 27% higher odds (OR = 1.27, p < 0.01) compared to women with “Excellent” health. Age demonstrated a consistent pattern, with all age groups showing lower odds of screening compared to women aged 21–24 (reference group). Women aged 25–34 (OR = 0.21, p < 0.001), 35–49 (OR = 0.21, p < 0.001), 50–64 (OR = 0.20, p < 0.001), and 65–84 (OR = 0.25, p < 0.001) all had significantly lower odds of screening. Racial/ethnic differences were pronounced, with Non‐Hispanic Black (OR = 1.41, p < 0.01), Non‐Hispanic Asian (OR = 2.96, p < 0.001), and Hispanic women (OR = 1.98, p < 0.001) all having significantly higher odds of screening compared to Non‐Hispanic White women. Higher education was associated with lower odds of never being screened. Compared to women with less than high school education, those with some college (OR = 0.71, p < 0.01), bachelor's degrees (OR = 0.60, p < 0.001), and graduate degrees (OR = 0.46, p < 0.001) had significantly lower odds of never receiving screening. Regarding marital status, single/never married women had 45% higher odds of screening compared to married women (OR = 1.45, p < 0.001).

Model 1B: Race‐Education Interaction

Model 1B incorporated interaction terms between race/ethnicity and education to examine whether educational attainment differentially affects screening across racial/ethnic groups (Table 2). The main effects of healthcare access (OR = 1.69, p < 0.001) and internet use (OR = 1.55, p < 0.001) remained consistent with Model 1 A. The most notable finding from this model was the significant interaction between Hispanic ethnicity and bachelor's degree education (OR = 2.39, p < 0.01). This indicates that Hispanic women with bachelor's degrees had more than twice the odds of screening compared to the reference group (Non‐Hispanic White women with less than high school education), even after accounting for other factors. This pattern suggests that educational attainment exerts a disproportionately stronger influence on screening behavior among Hispanic women, indicating that higher education may mitigate certain structural or informational barriers that contribute to lower screening rates in this population. While other interaction terms between race/ethnicity and education were not statistically significant, the overall pattern indicates that race modifies the effect of education primarily for Hispanic women rather than uniformly across all groups, reinforcing that education may serve as a key enabling factor for specific populations.

Model 1 C: Race‐Age Interaction

Model 1 C examined interactions between race/ethnicity and age to identify age‐specific racial/ethnic disparities in screening (Table 2). As in previous models, healthcare access (OR = 1.67, p < 0.001) and internet use (OR = 1.54, p < 0.001) remained significant predictors of screening. This model revealed significant interactions between Hispanic ethnicity and specific age groups. Hispanic women aged 35–49 had 2.59 times higher odds of screening compared to the reference group (Non‐Hispanic White women aged 21–24) (OR = 2.59, p < 0.001). Similarly, Hispanic women aged 50–64 had significantly higher odds of screening (OR = 2.26, p < 0.01). Among Non‐Hispanic Asian women, those aged 35–49 showed significantly higher odds of screening (OR = 2.67, p < 0.05). These findings indicate that racial and ethnic disparities in screening vary across the life course, with Hispanic and Non‐Hispanic Asian women experiencing more pronounced increases in screening during midlife than their Non‐Hispanic White counterparts. This suggests that screening disparities narrow or even reverse in mid‐adulthood for these groups, potentially reflecting greater healthcare engagement, targeted health communication, or improved access during these age periods. No other age interactions were significant, indicating that race modifies age‐related screening behavior only for selected groups rather than uniformly across all populations.

Discussion

This study comprehensively analyzes factors associated with HPV or Pap testing among U.S. adult women, utilizing data from the NHIS 2021–2023 cycles. The findings highlight key predictors of cervical cancer screening, including healthcare access, internet use, age, race/ethnicity, education, and marital status. Understanding these factors is critical for addressing disparities in screening and improving preventive care access, particularly for underserved populations.

Healthcare Access and Internet Use

Our analysis confirms the pivotal role of healthcare access in cervical cancer screening. Women who reported having a usual place for care were significantly more likely to undergo HPV or Pap testing (OR = 1.67, p < 0.001), consistent with prior research emphasizing the importance of regular healthcare interactions in promoting preventive services [8]. Regular contact with healthcare providers increases screening recommendations and awareness, leading to higher participation in preventive care. The significant association between internet use for health information and HPV or Pap testing (OR = 1.54, p < 0.001) highlights the growing importance of digital health literacy in facilitating preventive care [25]. The internet is a major health education tool, and individuals seeking online health information are more likely to engage in proactive health behaviors, including cancer screening (GÜL, KILIÇLI, and SENİM 2024). However, disparities exist in who uses the internet for health purposes, with older, less‐educated, and minority women often having lower digital health literacy, potentially limiting access to critical information about screening [14].

Age‐Related Screening Patterns

The study's findings on age and screening behavior align with evolving national screening guidelines. The American Cancer Society (ACS) 2020 guideline recommends initiating screening at age 25, while previous USPSTF 2018 and ACOG 2012 guidelines recommended starting at 21 [26, 27]; US Preventive Services Task Force, 2018). In our study, women aged 21‐24 had the lowest screening rates (4.9%), which likely reflects the transition in national screening recommendations toward later initiation. Importantly, screening rates peaked among women aged 25‐49 and declined significantly in older age groups. While lower screening rates in women aged 65+ (21.6%) might initially seem concerning, this decline is actually consistent with national guidelines, which recommend discontinuing screening after age 65 if there is no history of CIN2+ in the past 20 years.

These findings support the current ACS recommendation to initiate screening at age 25 rather than 21, as our data show that screening uptake naturally aligns with these updated guidelines. This reinforces the importance of aligning public health messages with current screening guidelines to avoid unnecessary over‐screening among both very young women and those over 65.

Racial/Ethnic Disparities and Educational Factors

Our first regression model (Model 1 A) revealed significant racial/ethnic differences in screening rates, with Non‐Hispanic Black (OR = 1.41, p < 0.01), Non‐Hispanic Asian (OR = 2.96, p < 0.001), and Hispanic women (OR = 1.98, p < 0.001) all showing higher odds of screening compared to Non‐Hispanic White women. However, when we introduced interaction terms in Models 1B and 1 C, these racial differences became more nuanced, suggesting that racial disparities in screening are not uniform but interact with other social determinants such as education and age. Model 1B demonstrated that education's impact on screening varies across racial/ethnic groups. Most notably, Hispanic women with a bachelor's degree had 2.39 times higher odds of being screened than the reference group (p < 0.01). This suggests that targeted educational interventions could be particularly effective for increasing screening rates among Hispanic women. Similarly, Model 1 C revealed that age‐related screening patterns differ by race/ethnicity. Hispanic women aged 35–49 (OR = 2.59, p < 0.001) and 50–64 (OR = 2.26, p < 0.01) had significantly higher odds of screening compared to the reference group, as did Non‐Hispanic Asian women aged 35‐49 (OR = 2.67, p < 0.05). These findings suggest that while screening rates start lower among minority women, they increase more substantially with age compared to Non‐Hispanic White women.

Marital Status

Marital status emerged as a significant determinant of screening behavior, with single or never‐married women having higher odds of HPV or Pap testing (OR = 1.45, p < 0.001) compared to married women. This finding is noteworthy, as prior research often links marriage to better health outcomes, including higher screening rates [28, 29]. Future studies should explore how relationship status affects preventive health behaviors beyond traditional marital status classifications.

Policy Implications

Given the strong association between healthcare access and cervical cancer screening, expanding policies that improve healthcare accessibility is crucial for increasing screening rates. Programs such as the Affordable Care Act (ACA) and Medicaid expansion have been linked to higher preventive care utilization by making healthcare more affordable and accessible [30, 31, 32]. Policy efforts should focus on ensuring that all women have access to a usual source of care, as this remains a key determinant of screening participation. Additionally, our findings highlight the growing role of digital health literacy in promoting cervical cancer screening. Public health campaigns should leverage digital platforms to disseminate information about cervical cancer screening, particularly targeting women aged 25–34 who are in the early years of the recommended screening age range according to current ACS guidelines. Policies that support digital health literacy initiatives, particularly among older and minority women, could help bridge gaps in preventive care access.

Implications for Clinical Practice

Clinicians play a critical role in increasing cervical cancer screening rates. The strong association between healthcare access and screening underscores the need for healthcare providers to recommend screening consistent with current guidelines actively. While screening rates were highest among women aged 25–49, clinicians should ensure that women begin screening at age 25, in alignment with current ACS recommendations, to establish lifelong adherence. Moreover, healthcare providers should be aware of the complex racial/ethnic patterns in screening uptake revealed by our interaction models. Clinicians should use culturally tailored approaches to improve screening engagement, particularly for low‐education and underinsured populations. Additionally, given the growing influence of digital health resources, providers should consider integrating online health tools into patient education.

Future Recommendations

Future research should continue exploring the complex interactions between socio‐demographic factors, healthcare access, and HPV or Pap testing. Longitudinal studies could examine how these factors evolve over time and influence long‐term screening behaviors. Additionally, qualitative studies are needed to better understand the barriers and facilitators to screening, particularly among women around the recommended initiation age of 25 and those from low‐education backgrounds. Future studies should also examine whether internet use for health information represents an effective strategy for reaching women without usual healthcare access. Understanding if digital health literacy particularly benefits those disconnected from traditional healthcare systems could inform targeted outreach efforts.

Limitations

This study provides valuable insights into the factors influencing cervical cancer screening among U.S. women; however, several limitations should be considered. A primary limitation is recall bias, particularly among older women, who may misremember past screening behaviors. Because NHIS asks about ever having been screened, some participants may overestimate or underestimate their screening history, affecting data reliability. Additionally, the NHIS does not differentiate between Pap tests, HPV tests, or co‐testing, limiting the ability to analyze specific screening trends. This prevents a deeper understanding of screening preferences and potential disparities between test types. Furthermore, the cross‐sectional design of NHIS does not allow for causal inferences, meaning associations between healthcare access, internet use, and screening behaviors cannot establish cause‐and‐effect relationships. Despite these limitations, NHIS remains a robust, nationally representative dataset, providing essential insights into cervical cancer screening disparities.

Conclusion

This study highlights the critical role of healthcare access (OR = 1.67, p < 0.001), digital health literacy (OR = 1.54, p < 0.001), and socio‐demographic factors in shaping cervical cancer screening behaviors. Women with a usual source of care were significantly more likely to undergo screening, reinforcing the need for expanding healthcare accessibility.

Our findings support current ACS guidelines recommending screening initiation at age 25, as we observed naturally lower screening rates among women aged 21–24, with rates increasing substantially in the 25–49 age range. By enhancing healthcare access, expanding culturally tailored education, and integrating digital health strategies, policymakers and clinicians can reduce disparities and improve cervical cancer outcomes for all women.
