Best of 2025—Thoracic and thoracoabdominal aneurysms and dissections
Authors: Stéphan Haulon
Published: 2026

I was both fortunate and honored to be asked to be the guest editor for the Journal of Vascular Surgery Cases and Innovative Techniques (JVSCIT) virtual special issue, “Best of 2025—Thoracic and Thoracoabdominal Aneurysms and Dissections.” The case reports, innovative techniques, and videos shared across all six issues of Volume 11 reflect the remarkable breadth and depth of our field. From this exceptional body of work, I have selected the manuscripts that best illustrate current trends in the treatment of patients with complex aortic pathologies.

As vascular surgeons, we benefit from a unique training that combines expertise in both open and endovascular techniques. This allows us to better select the appropriate treatment for our patients after careful physiological and anatomical assessment. The era of debating one approach over the other is over. We have reached a state of maturity in which staged approaches combining open and endovascular repairs are routinely discussed in high-volume aortic centers offering a high level of expertise in both techniques. Endovascular complications are treated with open surgery, just as complications of open surgery are treated with endovascular repair. The two approaches are now inseparable, and mastery of both is imperative to delivering the highest standard of vascular care.

In this context, one of the most challenging repairs is the explantation of infected endografts, especially fenestrated endografts. Hanif et al1 share a video of such a repair with a staged extra-anatomic axillobifemoral reconstruction, specific aortic stump reinforcement, cryopreserved allograft to the renovisceral vessels, aortic and endograft explantation, and pedicled omentoplasty. Another reported open approach following endovascular repair is the treatment of complex type II endoleaks. Kobayashi et al2 describe their technique through a right thoracotomy to ligate bronchial and intercostal arteries. Such endoleaks can persist despite multiple complex embolization procedures and can be associated with aneurysm growth, especially in chronic dissections. In similar cases involving patients with very large aneurysms at risk of esophageal or lung fistula during follow-up, my group has described combined branch ligation and aneurysmoraphy.3 I am convinced that, in the near future, JVSCIT will publish a similar procedure performed using robotic surgery.

On the same topic of complex open repairs, Soler et al4 describe their three-stage approach to a multifocal aneurysm in a young patient. This article illustrates nicely the multidisciplinary “aortic team” approach described previously, involving cardiothoracic and vascular surgeons. It also highlights that, unlike a decade ago, both open and endovascular repairs of multifocal aneurysms are now treated with a staged strategy, which has significantly reduced the morbidity and mortality associated with these graft/endograft total aortic replacements.

The Food and Drug Administration approval in 2024 of an off-the-shelf branched endograft (TAMBE; W. L. Gore & Associates) significantly changed the endovascular treatment options for thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysm (TAAA) and complex AAA in the United States. Previously, apart from 10 physicians with Physician-Sponsored Investigational Device Exemption who had access to custom-made device (CMD) fenestrated and branched endovascular aortic repair and the T-Branch off-the-shelf branched endograft (Cook Medical), off-label physician-modified endografts (PMEGs) and in situ fenestrations were widely used and reported. In experienced hands, the level of expertise with these two latter approaches is remarkable. These techniques have largely replaced the off-label CHIMPS (Chimney, Periscope, Snorkel) approach. Bastianon et al5 nicely described how PMEG and in situ fenestrations can be combined in acute cases to reduce the time to modify a standard endograft and to reduce visceral ischemia. Shu et al6 described an innovative repair of a chronic dissection, with PMEG fenestrations to perfuse the target vessels originating from the true lumen and two reversed iliac branch devices to perfuse the two target vessels originating from the false lumen.

The TAMBE (W. L. Gore & Associates) learning curve has generated a number of publications. Pitcher et al7 have highlighted the risk of preloaded wires wrap and described technical considerations for the recognition and management of this issue, which are nicely summarized in a table. To avoid access vessel injury and mitigate the risk of stroke, many authors have described a total transfemoral approach with this device, used outside the instructions for use. The technique by Gupta et al8 involves snaring individually the preloaded wires from a 16F steerable sheath advanced from the contralateral groin. Han et al9 refined this approach in a patient with ruptured TAAA requiring temporary aortic balloon occlusion. They described a double preloaded up-and-over technique using the preloaded wires by reverse loading each wire through the renal and visceral portals in combination with the use of an 8.5F steerable sheath. Innovative approaches have also been described with CMD BEVAR and T-Branch (Cook Medical). Park et al10 nicely described staggered BEVAR deployment with initial retrograde catheterization of the endograft lumen through the celiac trunk branch with a steerable sheath, which is then steered to access the superior mesenteric artery and both renal arteries.

Kalipatnapu et al11 emphasized how CMDs can be tailored to the patient's anatomy. They described, in a video, the repair of a type II TAAA chronic dissection with a five-fenestration endograft (to accommodate two left renal arteries), a bifurcated component with an inverted limb (because of a short distance from the lowest renal to the aortic bifurcation), and bilateral iliac branch devices to exclude distal entry tears. Another interesting CMD used to treat chronic dissections is the false lumen occluder (FLO; Cook Medical), which is nowadays routinely used in centers with access to this device to exclude false lumen persistent perfusion from re-entry tears located distal to the thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR).12 Saouti et al13 described a possible pitfall associated with this device. To implant the FLO, access to the false lumen is obviously required. A communication tear is thus identified and often catheterized swiftly, especially if fusion guidance is used. If the communication tear is located too close to the landing zone, the unstented self-occluding tail of the FLO can protrude into the true lumen. Specific maneuvers have been described to push it into the false lumen, but these do not always succeed. To avoid this issue that can result in thrombus formation in the true lumen and malperfusion, catheterization of more distal re-entry tears is recommended, especially those located in the iliac arteries. It is also important to ensure that the tail does not protrude in front of a target vessel origin located in the false lumen. Hamo et al14 described how FLO implantation was successful to treat a hemodynamically unstable patient with left hemothorax hours after TEVAR performed for a ruptured chronic dissection. The persistent false lumen perfusion following TEVAR was ultimately successfully occluded after FLO implantation. The FLO is also used to stage TAAA repairs in chronic dissections, in order to reduce the risk of spinal cord ischemia. Other methods to stage these extensive repairs include the use of sac perfusion branches, which deliberately create an endoleak to maintain perfusion to the sac and its collaterals. Perfusion branches can be additional branches designed within a CMD, a renovisceral branch that is not connected to its target vessel, or a contralateral iliac gate that is not connected to its iliac artery. The endovascular repair is generally completed a couple of weeks later percutaneously under local anesthesia. In the case described by Lubberdink et al,15 disseminated intravascular coagulation was diagnosed after the index BEVAR procedure, leaving the celiac trunk unstented. It only resolved once bridging stenting of the celiac trunk was completed. This article highlights the risk associated with perfusion branches if no (or limited) outflow to the sac is anticipated. This scenario results in turbulent blood flow within the aneurysm sac, activating the coagulation system and fibrinolysis. If one wants to use a perfusion branch, the safest way is thus probably to delay the iliac connection of the contralateral gate of the distal bifurcated component.

Other staging methods used to prevent spinal cord ischemia include pre-emptive coverage with first-stage TEVAR and intercostal/lumbar embolization procedures. The latter has become commonplace in a number of centers now that three-dimensional overlay imaging (fusion imaging) and steerable sheaths are used routinely. Banks and Beck16 include fiber optic real shape (Philips Medical) imaging software to perform these complex embolization procedures. Combining these advanced imaging applications has the major benefit of providing multiple working views and reduced radiation exposure, in addition to reduced operative time and contrast administration. Another interesting imaging tool that can be combined with fusion imaging is intravascular ultrasound guidance. Piazza et al17 described its benefit when performing septotomy in the setting of chronic dissections. Septum tear cannulation is facilitated, which again reduces procedural length and radiation exposure during complex endovascular repairs.

Finally, I would like to highlight some of the many manuscripts describing procedures in the ascending aorta, which increasingly require the aortic endografting expertise of vascular surgeons. Short 10-cm thoracic endografts can often accommodate the distance from the sinotubular junction to the innominate artery (outer curvature length), and length mismatch or TEVAR misplacement can be addressed by positioning a wire into the descending thoracic aorta from a right radial approach prior to TEVAR deployment. Through this access, balloon angioplasty and subsequent balloon expandable stenting can be performed to flare the distal endograft into the innominate artery ostium. This technique is nicely demonstrated in a video shared by Baghbani-Oskouei et al.18 When a 10-cm endograft is definitely too long, the proximal extender of the thoracic branch endoprosthesis (W. L. Gore & Associates) is now an alternative from a femoral approach.19 Implantation of endograft cuffs through supra-aortic access is no longer the only option. Interestingly, transcatheter aortic valve replacement complications, including valve migration and iatrogenic type A20 and B21 dissections, have also been addressed by ascending or descending thoracic endografting and reported in the JVSCIT 2025.

The opportunity to carefully review all six issues of JVSCIT 2025 (Volume 11) made me appreciate how fast our field is evolving. Thanks to the authors from all over the globe who shared their experience, we have been exposed to new devices, new surgical techniques, and new imaging technologies. Vascular surgeons, with their unique open and endovascular expertise, play a key role in the multidisciplinary aortic team, closely interacting with the other specialties. I deeply appreciated learning from all these manuscripts that have had a significant impact on my everyday practice.

As I look to the future, I am profoundly optimistic. The manuscripts in this volume reflect a field defined by relentless innovation, and the pace of that innovation shows no signs of slowing. Our specialty has always been defined by its willingness to evolve, and I have no doubt that the vascular surgeons reading these pages will continue to lead that evolution.
