Post-translational modification in the pathogenesis of vitiligo
Authors: Lu Lu, Huimin He, Jindi Feng, Zhonghui Hu, Shiyu Zhang, Lu Yang, Yuehua Liu, Tao Wang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitiligo is a chronic dermatological condition marked by the loss of skin pigmentation. Its complex etiology involves multiple factors and has not been completely elucidated. Protein post-translational modification pathways have been proven to play a significant role in inflammatory skin diseases, yet research in the context of vitiligo remains limited. This review focuses on the role of post-translational modifications in vitiligo pathogenesis, especially their impact on cellular signaling pathways related to immune response and melanocyte survival. Current therapeutic strategies targeting these pathways are discussed, emphasizing the potential for novel treatments in vitiligo management.Keywords: Vitiligo, Post-translational modification, Pathogenesis, Therapeutics

Introduction

Vitiligo is a chronic skin condition characterized by the development of depigmented patches that affects approximately 0.5–2% of the global population [1]. The etiology of vitiligo is multifactorial and includes genetic, environmental, oxidative stress, metabolic, and autoimmune factors [2]. The treatment of vitiligo remains one of the most challenging endeavors in dermatology, largely due to the incomplete understanding of the pathogenesis and the absence of well-defined therapeutic targets.

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) of proteins play a crucial role in regulating various cellular pathways and include phosphorylation, glycosylation, methylation, and ubiquitination. These PTMs covalently modify the functional domains of proteins and influence protein function and stability, thereby significantly impacting cellular signaling pathways, such as immune responses, cellular inflammation, and the proliferation of keratinocytes [3]. Previous studies have identified aberrant protein PTM pathways as contributing factors in the pathogenesis of several skin diseases, including psoriasis, atopic dermatitis, and systemic sclerosis [4].

Emerging evidence has suggested that aberrant PTMs may significantly contribute to the pathogenesis of vitiligo by influencing melanocyte survival, activating immune cells, and exacerbating cellular oxidative stress. These factors either initiate or act as intermediate pathways in the development of vitiligo. For example, the JAK-STAT pathway, in which protein phosphorylation plays a crucial regulatory role, is instrumental in recruiting CD8 + T cells and instigating the subsequent destruction of melanocytes [5]. Therapies targeting the JAK-STAT pathway have demonstrated efficacy in numerous clinical trials for vitiligo.

In this review, we discuss the current understanding of the potential roles of PTMs in vitiligo. Better understanding of the underlying mechanisms of PTMs in vitiligo may help identify novel therapeutic targets. Elucidating these pathways and their molecular foundations may lead to new strategies for effective vitiligo treatment.

Phosphorylation

Phosphorylation is one of the most extensively studied types of PTMs and plays a crucial role in regulating a multitude of cellular functions including cell proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis. The phosphorylation process is catalyzed by protein kinases, which facilitate the transfer of a phosphate group from ATP or GTP to a protein substrate. Serine, threonine, and tyrosine are the most common amino acids targeted by phosphorylation, although histidine and aspartate can also be phosphorylated [6]. Protein phosphatases are responsible for catalyzing dephosphorylation, the process of removing phosphate groups from proteins. Under normal conditions, there is a delicate balance between phosphorylation and dephosphorylation activities within the cell. Disruption of this equilibrium is often associated with the onset and progression of various diseases (Fig. 1).

Melanin synthesis

Melanin synthesis, a crucial process in the treatment of vitiligo repigmentation, is regulated by tyrosinase, and this regulation is significantly influenced by PTMs [7]. The microphthalmia-associated transcription factor (MITF) is a key regulator in melanocytes and melanomas. MITF translocates to the nucleus where it regulates the expression of target genes such as TYR, TYRP1, DCT, MLANA, SILV, and SLC24A5, all of which are integral to melanin synthesis [8]. Upon exposure of melanocytes to UV radiation, MITF is activated through several pathways [9]. The cAMP responsive element-binding protein (CREB), which recognizes the TGACGTCA sequence in the MITF promoter, plays a pivotal role in regulating MITF expression. The binding of factors such as α-melanocyte-stimulating hormone (α-MSH) to the melanocortin 1 receptor (MC1R) activates cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP). This activation triggers cAMP-dependent protein kinase (PKA), leading to the phosphorylation of CREB and subsequent promotion of MITF expression [10]. MITF is also regulated by phosphorylation through pathways involving MAPK, ribosomal S6 kinase (RSK), and glycogen synthase kinase-3β (GSK3β) [7]. The activation of c-Kit can rapidly initiate the RAS pathway, leading to a cascade involving RAF, MEK, and ERK, which result in MITF phosphorylation [11–13]. Furthermore, lipid signaling and ultraviolet (UV) irradiation activate the p38 signaling pathway, resulting in enhanced CREB phosphorylation and activation of downstream signaling pathways [14].

The PTM pathway centered around MITF represents a promising target for vitiligo repigmentation therapy. Several drugs have been reported to influence this pathway. Tacrolimus selectively inhibit calcineurin and may promote melanocyte proliferation and melanin synthesis by inducing the production of stem cell factor (SCF) by keratinocytes, thereby maintaining the activation of the MAPK pathway [15]. Topical application of tacrolimus has achieved good efficacy in vitiligo [16]. Mamat et al. reported that 1,5-diCQA, an analog of the antioxidant chlorogenic acid, increased MITF expression in B16 cells, a murine melanoma cell line. The increase in MITF expression was achieved by upregulating the phosphorylation levels in the p38 and ERK-MAPK pathways, subsequently enhancing the expression of melanogenic enzymes such as TYR, TRP1, and TRP2, and thereby boosting melanin production. Additionally, 1,5-diCQA upregulates PKA via cAMP, further enhancing the CREB-mediated promotion of MITF [17]. Similar effects have been observed with other compounds, such as the coumarin analog fraxinol [18], the flavonoid pinostrobin [19], Cirsium extracts [20, 21], and 5-demethylnobiletin [22]. Certain photosensitizers, like the benzophenone maclurin, psoralen derivative MPFC, and 5D3PC, were shown to increase the expression of p38 MAPK and CREB, thus inducing melanin synthesis via the MITF pathway [23–26]. However, drugs other than topical tacrolimus targeting the MITF-related pathway are still in the experimental stage. Currently, their use is largely confined to cellular models. Additional research is required before these treatments can be applied in human therapeutics.

Melanocyte destruction

Janus kinase (JAK) is a family of intracellular, non-receptor tyrosine kinases that non-covalently bind to cytokine receptors. When a cytokine, such as IFN-γ, binds to its corresponding cytokine receptor, the interaction triggers receptor dimerization. This dimerization leads to the phosphorylation of JAK. The tyrosine kinase structural domain within phosphorylated JAK is responsible for mediating receptor phosphorylation. JAK phosphorylation subsequently recruits and phosphorylates signal transducer and activator of transcription (STAT). Once activated, STAT translocates to the nucleus, where it binds to DNA, thereby regulating gene expression [5].

The JAK-STAT pathway has been identified as a crucial contributor to the pathogenesis and progression of vitiligo. The production of IFN-γ by autoimmune CD8 + T cells that results from oxidative stress is a key factor in the destruction of melanocytes [27]. This process further stimulates the expression of CXCR3 in keratinocytes via the JAK-STAT pathway. The interaction between CXCR3 and its ligand CXCL9 leads to the recruitment of more autoimmune CD8 + T cells, perpetuating a vicious cycle that results in continued melanocyte loss. Drugs that target the JAK-STAT pathway, such as baricitinib, ruxolitinib, and tofacitinib, have demonstrated effectiveness in the treatment of vitiligo. It should be noted that JAK inhibitors do not eliminate tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs), which are associated with the recurrence of vitiligo [28]. In addition to IFN-γ, TNF-α and IL-15 can activate JAK-STAT pathway [29]. These agents disrupt the aforementioned cycle, offering a novel approach to managing this complex skin condition.

Melanocyte protection

Nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a critical transcription factor that governs the cellular response to oxidative stress. Upon phosphorylation, activated Nrf2 mediates the expression of genes related to oxidative stress, including genes encoding antioxidant proteins, transport proteins, growth factors, and their receptors, by binding to the antioxidant response element (ARE) in the promoter regions [30].

Kelch-like ECH-Associating protein 1 (Keap1) is a classical regulator of Nrf2. Under normal conditions, Keap1 forms a functional E3 ubiquitin ligase complex with Cul3 and Rbx1, targeting Nrf2 for ubiquitination and subsequent degradation [31]. However, under oxidative stress conditions, Nrf2 dissociates from the Keap1-Nrf2 complex, undergoes phosphorylation, and translocates into the nucleus to promote anti-oxidative stress gene expression. The MAPK and PERK pathways also directly phosphorylate Nrf2. Additionally, ERK2, a subfamily member of MAPK, indirectly activates Nrf2 by phosphorylating Bach1. This phosphorylation leads to a reduction in Bach1’s inhibitory effects on Nrf2, further enhancing Nrf2-mediated transcriptional activity [32].

Dysfunction in the Keap1-Nrf2-ARE pathway is a significant factor in the pathogenesis of vitiligo. Jian et al. showed that in melanocytes affected by vitiligo, the nuclear translocation and transcriptional activity of Nrf2 are diminished. This reduction leads to a decreased antioxidant capacity in melanocytes, making them more vulnerable to oxidative stress. Furthermore, the authors observed a decrease in the oxidative stress marker HO-1 and an increase in IL-2 levels in the serum of vitiligo patients. These findings suggest that an impaired Keap1-Nrf2-ARE pathway might be a critical link between oxidative stress and immune activation in vitiligo [33].

Nrf2 activators, such as dimethyl fumarate (DMF), curcumin, and epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), have been shown to offer protective effects on melanocytes. Notably, DMF and EGCG function by promoting the phosphorylation of Nrf2 [34, 35]. This activation, coupled with the inhibition of Nrf2 ubiquitination and subsequent degradation, presents a promising avenue for vitiligo treatment strategies. Among these drugs mentioned above, EGCG and curcumin have been shown to be effective against vitiligo in clinical trials. EGCG is the main and most important polyphenol in green tea. In a mouse model of vitiligo, it protects melanin melanocytes and inhibits the activation of CD8 + T cells and inflammatory mediators [36]. Wenting Hu et al. found in a randomized controlled trial that topical EGCG treatment for vitiligo resulted in efficacy similar to that of pimecrolimus [37]. They also found that topical application of AcEGCG, an acetylated derivative of EGCG, promoted repigmentation of lesions and inhibited the progression of depigmentation in patients with vitiligo [38]. Curcumin is a common antioxidant, containing a high level of superoxide dismutase activity. Samin Jalalmanesh et al. found that topical curcumin cream was effective in promoting repigmentation of vitiligo lesions compared to placebo [39]. Pravit Asawanonda et al. found in a randomized controlled trial that tetrahydrocurcuminoid cream plus targeted narrowband UVB phototherapy had a slightly higher rate of repigmentation than phototherapy alone in long-term treatment, but there was no statistical difference [40]. DMF has been approved by the FDA for the treatment of multiple sclerosis and has been found to protect vitiligo melanocytes from oxidative stress damage through Nrf2 activation in mouse experiments [41]. However, there are no relevant clinical studies.

MAPK pathway also plays a role in melanocyte protection. When MAPKs are improperly activated, they perpetuate the activation of the inflammatory cascade. Glucocorticoids (GCs) are a class of steroid hormones mainly secreted by the adrenal cortex or synthetically produced. They have anti-inflammatory, immunosuppressive, and anti-shock effects and have been used to treat many autoimmune, inflammatory, and allergic diseases. Glucocorticoids primarily exert their effects through the MAPK pathway [42]. When glucocorticoids bind to receptors on the cytoplasm/cell membrane, they induce the expression of anti-inflammatory protein genes, thereby inhibiting the activation of p38 and ERK, probably protecting melanocytes from oxidative stress damage [43]. Previous studies have shown that both oral and topical glucocorticoid treatments for vitiligo can exhibit good efficacy [44].

Cell interaction

P53, a well-established tumor suppressor, plays a crucial role in protecting cells from various stressors. In response to stress conditions such as radiation exposure and oncogene activation, p53 becomes activated and translocates to the nucleus through a complex pathway. Once in the nucleus, p53 regulates the expressions of genes related to cell growth, repair, apoptosis, and senescence. Activation of p53 is primarily regulated by phosphorylation, which prevents its ubiquitin-mediated degradation and consequently extends its half-life [45].

In vitiligo, p53 exerts an influence on keratinocytes. Becatti et al. revealed that reactive oxygen species (ROS) induced aberrant activation of p38 and ERK pathways in keratinocytes in vitiligo lesions, potentially through the regulation of p53 phosphorylation. This aberrant activation resulted in increased apoptosis of keratinocytes. The authors also observed the activation of the NF-κB pathway, which further contributes to the development of vitiligo [46].

While current therapies targeting p53 have been predominantly focused in cancer treatments, their potential application in vitiligo is emerging. For example, Becatti et al. discovered that compounds such as curcumin and capsaicin can protect keratinocytes by inhibiting the activation of the p38 pathway, thereby influencing p53 pathway [46].

TRMs are tissue-resident T lymphocytes, which are generated from naïve T cells after stimulation and can persist long-term in tissues such as the skin, gastrointestinal tract, and respiratory tract [47]. Under normal circumstances, TRMs provide rapid defense functions at the site of stimulation, but when abnormally activated, they can lead to various autoimmune diseases [48]. CD49 + CD103 + TRMs exert cytotoxic effects in vitiligo skin tissues [49] and play an important role in the recurrence of vitiligo [50]. Targeting TRMs is a potential therapeutic approach for vitiligo and may help in preventing recurrence [50, 51].

Azathioprine has been shown to inhibit the Rac1/MEK signaling pathway in CD4 + T lymphocytes, leading to apoptosis [52, 53]. In the treatment of vitiligo, azathioprine can effectively halt disease progression, induce repigmentation, and has a higher safety profile compared to oral glucocorticoids [54, 55]. Although there is currently no direct evidence proving a relationship between azathioprine and TRMs, their connection is an interesting research direction that may aid in the treatment of vitiligo recurrence.

Acetylation

Acetylation is the second most common PTMs. It regulates protein function by the transfer of acetyl groups to specific amino acid residues. Acetylation plays a pivotal role in a wide array of cellular activities, including gene transcription, DNA damage repair, cell division, signal transduction, protein folding, autophagy, and metabolism. By altering the structure and interaction dynamics of proteins, acetylation significantly contributes to the modulation of vital cellular processes [56] (Fig. 2).

Cell interaction

Silent information regulator sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a widely studied deacetylase, is considered to be an important molecular switch that regulates inflammation [57]. In vitiligo, SIRT1 plays a significant role in regulating keratinocytes, which are key players in the pathogenesis of the disease. Becatti et al. revealed that in normal keratinocytes, SIRT1 mitigates apoptosis by mediating the deacetylation and activation of Akt. The activation of Akt further activates MAPK pathway and downregulates pro-apoptotic molecules. Conversely, in keratinocytes adjacent to vitiligo lesions, SIRT1 expression is reduced, leading to diminished keratinocyte resistance to oxidative stress. The destruction of keratinocyte has downstream effects on melanin production through interactions with melanocytes [58]. UV radiation and H2O2 downregulate SIRT1 expression in skin keratinocytes [59]. Brahmbhatt et al. demonstrated that promoting SIRT1 expression in keratinocytes through lncRNA MALAT1 results in protection against UVB-induced damage [60]. Given these findings, SIRT1 is a potential novel target for vitiligo treatment.

Melanocyte destruction/melanin synthesis

SIRT3 is a member of the sirtuin family of deacetylases and is associated with cellular aging and age-related diseases [61]. Yi et al. discovered that SIRT3 regulates mitochondrial dynamics in normal melanocytes by deacetylating optic atrophy 1 (OPA1), thereby protecting melanocytes from oxidative stress damage. However, in melanocytes affected by vitiligo, the expression and activity of SIRT3 are reduced, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and the release of cytochrome c, ultimately inducing melanocyte apoptosis. The authors found that honokiol activates SIRT3 and effectively reduces melanocyte apoptosis. Notably, the study also observed an increase in carbonylation of SIRT3 in vitiligo-affected melanocytes, further illustrating the network-like role of PTMs in the pathogenesis of vitiligo [62]. Nie et al. discovered that the GATA3-SIRT3 axis increases melanin synthesis by mediating the deacetylation of HMGB1 [63]. These research findings suggest that SIRT3 may be a new target for vitiligo treatment.

Ubiquitination

Ubiquitination is a PTM that involves the attachment of ubiquitin to specific amino acid residues on a protein via a series of enzyme-catalyzed reactions. Ubiquitin is an evolutionarily conserved protein and is activated by the ubiquitin-activating enzyme (E1) and transferred to the ubiquitin-conjugating enzyme (E2); the ubiquitin ligase (E3) catalyzes the attachment of ubiquitin to the target protein. Ubiquitination plays roles in protein localization, functional regulation, and degradation [64] (Fig. 2).

Several studies have examined the differentially expressed genes in the skin lesions and peripheral blood of vitiligo patients and found that these genes are enriched in the ubiquitin-related proteolytic pathway [65–67]. This suggested that ubiquitination may be related to the onset and progression of vitiligo. Research has further clarified the role of ubiquitination in vitiligo. Vitiligo was shown to be associated with abnormal autoimmunity, and CD8 + T cells were identified as the main culprits in the destruction of melanocytes [68]. Antigen presentation is a key step in the specific immune process, in which TCRs on CD8 + T cells recognize antigens presented by MHC I. These antigens come from membrane proteins marked by ubiquitination and degraded by LMP2 and LMP7, encoded by the PSMB9 and PSMB8 genes. Dani et al. found that after the stimulation of vitiligo PBMCs with IFN-γ, the expression of PSMB8 was reduced, leading to inflammation induced by the accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins, resulting in the production of reactive oxygen species and autoimmune reactions [69]. This indicates that the accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins may be upstream of the autoimmune inflammatory response, acting as an initiator in the pathogenesis of vitiligo.

Monobenzone is commonly used for the chemical induction of a vitiligo model in mouse. This model shares similarities with vitiligo in human patients in terms of CD8 + T cell infiltration and histopathological features [70]. Zhu et al. found that after exposure to monobenzone, the ubiquitination of tyrosinase in melanoma cells increased. Ubiquitinated tyrosinase is directed to the MHC-1 degradation pathway, reducing melanin production; monobenzone also promotes autophagy in melanocytes [71]. This indirectly confirms the impact of ubiquitination on melanin production and melanocyte death.

Together, these studies indicate that reducing the accumulation of ubiquitinated proteins and promoting their degradation may be a therapeutic strategy to block the onset and progression of vitiligo. However, therapeutic targeting of ubiquitination in vitiligo has not been well explored.

SUMOylation

SUMOylation, a modification similar to ubiquitination, is a PTM involves the covalent attachment of the small ubiquitin-like modifier (SUMO) protein to the ε-amino group of lysine residues on target proteins [72]. The SUMOylation process is mediated by various enzymes, including sentrin-specific proteases (SENPs), SUMO-activating enzyme E1 (SAE1/UBA2), SUMO-conjugating enzyme E2 (UBC9), and SUMO ligase E3. The de-SUMOylation process is mediated by SENPs. SUMOylation plays an important role in the regulation of the cell cycle, transcription, nuclear transport, DNA replication and repair, chromosomal dynamics, apoptosis, and ribosome biogenesis [73] (Fig. 2).

Research has confirmed the role of SUMOylation in vitiligo. Zhou et al. discovered the reduction in the expression of the UBC9 protein and an increase in SENP1 expression in keratinocytes affected by vitiligo, indicating an imbalance between SUMOylation and de-SUMOylation. Furthermore, HaCaT cells with knocked-out SUMO1 exhibit cell cycle progression dysregulation through a mechanism involving CDK2, CDK6, and Rb [74]. Regulation of SUMOylation in keratinocytes may be a new target for the treatment of vitiligo.

Glycosylation

Protein glycosylation is a PTM in which monosaccharides or oligosaccharides are covalently bonded to proteins [75]. Carlie et al. found that treating Mel-1 melanoma cells and normal human melanocytes in vitro with khellin plus ultraviolet A promoted melanin production. The mechanism involved increased glycosylation of tyrosinase and dopachrome tautomerase (DCT) [76]. Glycosylated proteins are mostly secreted extracellularly and have significant potential as biomarkers for disease diagnosis or severity assessment. Little is known about the role of glycosylation in vitiligo (Fig. 2).

Conclusion

PTMs play key regulatory roles in various cellular pathways through influencing protein function and stability and thereby influence disease onset and response to treatment. In this review, we summarized the roles of phosphorylation, acetylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation, and glycosylation in the pathogenesis of vitiligo. These PTMs are involved in regulating melanocyte protection, melanocyte destruction, melanin synthesis, and cell interaction pathways, influencing both melanocytes and keratinocytes (Table 1).

Summary of post-translational modifications in vitiligo

These several protein post-translational modifications may play a role in vitiligo initiation, disease progression, and disease recurrence. When lesional melanocytes and keratinocytes in vitiligo are damaged by oxidative stress, the phosphorylation pathway and the acetylation pathway are activated, leading to melanocyte destruction/reduced melanin synthesis through a series of cascading reactions. Of these, the ubiquitination pathway is the most specific and may exist as a bridge between intrinsic and adaptive immunity. The role of SUMOylation and glycosylation still needs further investigation. Inhibition of the MAPK pathway was observed in keratinocytes in the non-lesional skin, which may contribute to the progression of vitiligo lesions into the surrounding skin [46].

The pathogenesis of vitiligo is not yet fully understood and the network of PTM pathways in vitiligo has not been fully elucidated, and most pathways still lack targeted drugs. Nevertheless, PTM is still important in the pathogenesis and treatment of vitiligo, and new PTM mechanisms deserve attention. The treatment for PTM has been widely studied in the clinic, among which the JAK/STAT pathway is a popular and potential pathway for the treatment of vitiligo and it has been the focus of extensive research and attention. In addition, many of the mentioned pathways have been studied in other autoimmune diseases, while little is known in vitiligo, and the development of drugs targeting these pathways is also a new direction for vitiligo treatment. A deeper study of PTMs in the pathogenesis of vitiligo will help in the elucidation of the disease mechanism and may provide new therapeutic targets for the treatment of vitiligo.
