Parental experiences in pediatric advance care planning for children with life-limiting illnesses: a qualitative meta-synthesis
Authors: Sheng He, Kaibo Shen, Baolin Huang, Ju Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo systematically synthesize the experiences of parents of children with life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) in pediatric advance care planning (pACP), extract core perceptions and influencing factors, and provide practical guidance to optimize the clinical implementation of pACP.MethodsA systematic search was conducted in the Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM) for qualitative studies related to parental involvement in pACP for children with LLIs. The search period was from database inception to April 2025. The aggregative meta-synthesis method was employed for data analysis and synthesis.ResultsA total of nine qualitative studies were included, from which 33 themes were identified and structured into ten themes and corresponding subthemes. These themes and subthemes were further synthesized into three core findings: (1) Behavioral coping patterns of parents in pACP; (2) Parental perceptions and experiences regarding the value of pACP; and (3) Challenges and barriers encountered by parents in engaging with pACP.ConclusionTo facilitate the effective implementation of pACP, it is crucial to respect children’s preferences, address parental decision-making needs, and enhance palliative care services along with educational and support systems for pACP. These efforts will improve the acceptability and practical application of pACP in pediatric care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12904-026-02047-7.

Introduction

Life-limiting illnesses (LLIs), such as malignant tumors and Duchenne muscular dystrophy, are progressive and incurable conditions characterized by an increasing symptom burden and functional decline. Children with LLIs often require long-term, complex medical care and multidisciplinary support to manage their symptoms and improve their quality of life [1]. Advancements in medical technology have extended the life expectancy of children with LLIs, yet they have also introduced greater clinical complexity and uncertainty in disease outcomes [2, 3]. LLIs not only compromise children’s well-being but also place significant emotional and decision-making burdens on their families, especially as parents are often required to navigate difficult treatment choices while balancing the risks of overtreatment with the need for timely advance care planning, of which end-of-life planning is a crucial part.

In response to these challenges, international guidelines for pediatric palliative and hospice care advocate for early, comprehensive assessments of the needs of children with LLIs and their families. Such assessments aim to alleviate suffering, optimize clinical management, and bolster family coping mechanisms [4]. Pediatric advance care planning (pACP), which encompasses palliative care planning as a crucial component, is widely recognized in clinical practice guidelines as a gold-standard approach to improving medical decision-making [5]. The primary goal of pACP is to facilitate structured, collaborative discussions among healthcare providers, children, and families to enhance shared understanding of illness, clarify care preferences, and align treatment goals with family values [6]. Current research indicates that pACP can improve the quality of life for children with LLIs and their families, while also reducing decisional conflict and uncertainty [7]. Nonetheless, in comparison to adult patients [8], the implementation of pACP in pediatric care remains limited and uneven [9]. Here, ‘uneven’ refers to variability in uptake across clinical settings/services and inconsistency in key implementation elements, including initiation timing, documentation/transfer of plans, and multidisciplinary coordination.

Conceptual clarity regarding the boundaries between advance care planning (ACP) and end-of-life (EOL) planning is essential, given the frequent conflation of these terms in both clinical practice and the literature. Distinguishing them is particularly critical for interpreting the scope and implementation challenges of pACP. Advance care planning (ACP) is a dynamic, longitudinal process that supports patients and families in articulating values, setting goals, and specifying preferences for future medical care across the illness continuum [10]. End-of-life (EOL) planning, by contrast, focuses narrowly on care decisions made when death is imminent, including symptom management, preferred place of death, and palliative interventions [11]. In this meta-synthesis, pACP is conceptualized as encompassing a broad range of anticipatory discussions, including value clarification and shared decision-making across the trajectory of serious illness—regardless of whether explicit end-of-life decisions are present [7].

Since parents are generally the primary decision-makers in pediatric settings, their perspectives are pivotal for pACP conversation success. Parental emotional responses, cultural values, illness perceptions, and prior experiences with medical care significantly shape pACP depth, timing, as well as its outcome [12]. The emotional and ethical dimensions of these issues often give rise to internal conflicts, including tension between hope and acceptance, or between personal values and medical recommendations [7]. Whereas more and more qualitative research studies have investigated pACP from a parental perspective, the findings remain disjointed and context-specific, reducing the generalizability and applicability of individual studies.

This study therefore aims to conduct a qualitative meta-synthesis of international research on parental experiences with pACP for children with LLIs. Specifically, we address the following research question: How do parents experience and engage in pACP, what value do they attribute to pACP, and what barriers and contextual factors shape their engagement? By integrating evidence from diverse contexts, this review seeks to identify core emotional, relational, and decision-making themes. The findings will provide theoretical and practical insights to inform the development of family-centered and culturally responsive pACP interventions—particularly in settings where implementation remains nascent.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative meta-synthesis approach, guided by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) methodology, specifically Chap. 3: Systematic reviews of qualitative evidence from the JBI Manual for Evidence Synthesis [13]. The methodology followed JBI’s standardized procedures and adhered to the meta-aggregation approach, which emphasizes preserving the original meanings of participants’ experiences while synthesizing findings across studies [14]. Meta-synthesis was chosen for its strength in systematically synthesizing qualitative evidence and generating higher-order themes. The review protocol was prospectively registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD420251037035, ensuring methodological transparency and accountability (link: https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD420251037035).

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was implemented across Cochrane Library, PubMed, Embase, CINAHL, Web of Science, Scopus, China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and the Chinese Biomedical Literature Database (CBM). The search aimed to identify qualitative studies exploring the experiences and perceptions of parents of children with life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) participating in pediatric advance care planning (pACP). The search was initially conducted on November 2024 and updated on April 2025 using the same databases and search strategy. The search utilized a combination of subject headings and free words.

The Chinese search terms included “advance care planning/advance directives/advance medical decision-making/preemptive medical care/end-of-life decision-making,” “pediatrics/children/adolescents/youth,” “parent/family/“caregivers,” and “qualitative study/qualitative research/rooted theory/ethnography/phenomenology/narrative.”

The English search terms included “advance care planning,” “advance health care planning,” “advance directives,” “advance medical planning,” “end-of-life planning,” “pediatric*,” “child*,” “adolescent*,” “parent*,” “mother*,” “father*,” “experience*,” “perception*,” “expectation*,” “challenge*,” “qualitative study,” “interview,” “phenomenology,” “grounded theory,” “focus group,” “thematic analysis,” and “ethnography.”

As an example, the complete search strategy used in PubMed is provided, as shown in Fig. 1. In addition to database searches, relevant literature reviews were screened using reference tracking (snowballing) to identify additional eligible primary qualitative studies.

Eligibility criteria

The inclusion and exclusion criteria were developed using the PICoS framework (Participant, Interest of Phenomena, Context, and Study Design), as recommended by the Joanna Briggs Institute [13]. This model, adapted from the widely used PICO framework in quantitative research, is designed for qualitative study formulation.

Inclusion Criteria: (1) P (Participant): Parents of children (0–18 years old) diagnosed with life-limiting illnesses. (2) I (Interest of Phenomena): To explore the experiences and perceptions of parents participating in pediatric advance care planning (pACP). (3) Co (Context): Parents engaging in pACP discussions initiated or guided by healthcare professionals. (4) S (Study Design): Included were qualitative studies employing methods such as phenomenology, grounded theory, ethnography, case studies, or action research.

Exclusion Criteria: (1) Studies without full-text access or incomplete information. (2) Non-Chinese or non-English literature. (3) Literature reviews and conference papers.

Data extraction

Two researchers, both trained in evidence-based nursing and qualitative research methods, independently reviewed and screened the retrieved literature using EndNote reference management software. First, titles and abstracts were reviewed to identify potentially relevant studies based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Second, the full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed in detail. When full texts were not readily accessible, we attempted to obtain them through institutional/library resources and by contacting corresponding authors. Studies that remained unavailable after these attempts were excluded. Where studies included multiple participant groups (e.g., parents and healthcare professionals, or parents and young people), we extracted and synthesized only parent-derived findings and quotations relevant to pACP participation. Any disagreements at this stage were resolved through discussion, and if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer was consulted. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 2, in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. For the final set of included studies, the following data were extracted: author (country), publication year, research methodology, study participants, study objectives, and key findings.

Quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies was appraised using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research [14], which evaluates ten key aspects of research rigor. The appraisal encompassed ten criteria, each rated as “Yes”, “No”, “Unclear” or “Not applicable”. Based on the total number of “Yes” responses, studies were categorized as follows: A (High Quality/Low risk of bias): 9–10 Yes; B (Moderate Quality/Moderate risk): 7–8 Yes༛C (Low Quality/High risk): ≤6 Yes. Two researchers independently conducted the quality assessment, discussing any discrepancies, and if disagreements persisted, a third researcher was consulted for arbitration. Only studies rated A or B were included in the meta-synthesis, while those rated C were excluded. Data synthesis

A meta-aggregation approach was employed to synthesize findings from selected qualitative studies [14]. Researchers first systematically extracted and categorized the original findings (themes, interpretations, and supporting quotations) from each study. Through repeated in-depth reading, coding, and comparison, the data were then aggregated into categories based on similarities in meaning. These categories were further synthesized into higher-order integrated findings, providing a comprehensive understanding of parental experiences in the context of pediatric advance care planning (pACP).

Results

Title and abstract screening narrowed the selection to 27 studies for full-text review. Following a thorough assessment, 9 studies met the inclusion criteria and were incorporated into this meta-integration. Assessment of methodological quality of the included studies

All nine included studies underwent methodological appraisal using the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Qualitative Research. Each study satisfied at least 7 of the 10 quality criteria, with overall scores ranging from 7 to 9, indicating satisfactory methodological rigor. Detailed results of the quality assessment are presented in Table 1. All studies were deemed methodologically sound and were therefore included in the final synthesis.

Following an in-depth review of the nine included studies, a total of 33 findings were extracted. These were grouped into 10 categories, which were further synthesized into three synthesized findings. Figure 3 presents the synthesized findings and their corresponding categories. Synthesized finding 1: behavioral coping patterns of parents of children with LLIs in pACP

Category 1.1: active adjustment and proactive planning

In the included studies, some parents reported an active and positive approach toward pediatric advance care planning (pACP), reflected in proactive information-seeking and engagement in anticipatory decision-making. However, the emphasis varied across accounts: some parents foregrounded aligning care with goals for the child’s quality of life and preserving “normality” [17], whereas others emphasized seeking information and asking questions to remain informed participants [16]. Rather than resisting difficult conversations, these parents fully engaged in decision-making and prioritized their child’s quality of life. As one parent described, “Our team has been amazing cause they understood the goals that I had for her and how badly I want to make her life normal and fun and positive” [17].

Similarly, parents expressed a strong desire to be informed participants in the planning process, actively seeking knowledge and asking questions to better support their child. One parent shared, “For me, there was a sense of needing and wanting to know more and wanting to ask questions” [16].

In particular, parents described feeling reassured and better prepared when plans and next steps were clearly arranged. Reflecting on this experience, a parent explained, “And we knew everything had been arranged. And even if it gets bad one day, one knows what to do. We were not afraid there at all” [22].

Category 1.2: decision avoidance and discussion resistance

When approaching pACP discussions, some parents described marked emotional turmoil and avoidance, portraying these conversations as psychologically and morally difficult. However, the reasons for resistance varied across accounts: some parents emphasized a perceived parental duty to protect their child and not “prepare” for death [19], whereas others highlighted religious or spiritual beliefs that made discussions about death or end-of-life matters feel incompatible [16]. One parent reflected this struggle, stating, “Even the slightest preparation for our child’s death would have been, in my opinion, not only a useless waste of effort, but also a selfish abdication of responsibilities germane to our parent-child contract” [19].

In some accounts, parents described pACP discussions as incompatible with their spiritual convictions. As one parent explained, “The words that were being said to me, did not match up with what the Bible’s teaching about. they don’t plan or consider death or end of life” [16].

In addition, even when parents indicated awareness of the medical prognosis, accounts suggested that emotional denial and continued hope could persist, limiting readiness for anticipatory planning. One parent recalled, “We knew she would pass away, but truly we did not know, could not prepare, could not believe any of it was happening to us. I believed there would be a miracle” [19].

Category 1.3: decision hesitation and ethical conflict

Across accounts, parents who recognized the importance of pediatric advance care planning (pACP) still often struggled to reconcile ethical, emotional, and practical considerations, leading to profound hesitation. Emotional attachment and the parental instinct to protect conflicted with the role of decision-maker, making conversations about end-of-life care exceptionally difficult. Some parents described feeling constrained by limited or “predetermined” options presented in acute end-of-life contexts [19], whereas others highlighted moral conflict about whether their choices served their own needs or the child’s best interests [16], and difficulty verbalizing clinical realities even when they were cognitively understood [21]. As one parent described, “Only one option was presented in the end and that was that she would not survive and we should consider ending life support” [19].

Similarly, parents described anxiety about making decisions that might feel self-serving rather than aligned with the child’s best interests. One parent reflected, “If I was to get (son) resuscitated. I’d be doing it for myself, not for him” [16].

In addition, some accounts suggested that even when clinical realities were understood, parents found it emotionally difficult to acknowledge or verbalize them. One parent recalled, “If I’m honest, the moment they ventilated her, I think deep down we knew she wasn’t coming back that time … I didn’t want to say it out loud and I didn’t want to admit it, but I think that we all knew” [21].

Synthesized finding 2: parental perceptions and experiences of pACP values

Category 2.1: the positive impact of pACP on the child’s quality of life and dignity

Across the included studies, parents described pACP as supporting their child’s comfort and dignity by helping to avoid non-beneficial interventions and by prioritizing quality of life. Rather than solely prolonging life, pACP emphasizes enabling children to live their final days more comfortably and meaningfully, free from overtreatment. One parent reflected on their experience, stating: “She was tormented nonstop and we didn’t want to be doing anything to prolong that needlessly” [16]. Another parent echoed this concern by contrasting experiences they had witnessed abroad: “I had watched them in America having them on ventilators and tube feeding them and no quality of life” [16].

When pACP facilitated the choice of a non-hospital setting for end-of-life care, families described the experience as more peaceful and emotionally resonant. As one mother explained, “As much as I pictured [daughter] dying in the hospital because of my medical mind… it worked at [hospice], and it was beautiful” [18].

Importantly, pACP also supports the principle of child-centered care by ensuring that the child’s voice is acknowledged, even when they are not capable of making decisions independently. This was illustrated by parents who emphasized that “They felt that their child should be heard and taken seriously even if unable to make treatment decisions” [22].

Category 2.2: the supportive role of pACP in family decision-making and coping

Across the included studies, parents described pACP as supporting family decision-making and coping by reducing uncertainty, easing decisional stress, and helping families plan and communicate more openly about the future. As one parent expressed, “And even if it gets bad one day, one knows what to do” [18]. By participating in pACP parents gain a clearer vision for the future, reducing anxiety caused by uncertainty: “That conversation shaped the way we do things in our family. Trips that we think we’ll put off for five years, it’s more like…if my child is doing well, maybe we should look at doing that sooner than later” [17].

Meanwhile, the pACP experience fosters deeper self-reflection and family introspection, promoting long-term psychological adaptation and personal development. One participant shared, “I feel more confident now because we’ve been through the ACP process. Now we can make plans and we can do things, and we can make the most of the time we’ve got” [15].

Furthermore, some parents described pACP as an ongoing process that supported revisiting preferences over time, enabling families to consider future options with less emotional burden. One parent emphasized the importance of revisiting these conversations periodically: “To say actually in the future, whether its 6 months or 2 years it would be worth sitting down with someone to say what do you want to happen” [15].

Beyond immediate practical benefits, pACP also exerts a lasting emotional and developmental influence: “The hospital experiences, the relationships built, and the perspectives will affect me for a lifetime” [19].

Category 2.3: the positive role of pACP in physician-patient communication and trust building

Across several included studies, parents described pACP as strengthening communication and trust with healthcare professionals and supporting more transparent, collaborative decision-making. Some parents highlighted shared goals and mutual understanding as foundations for trust [20], whereas others emphasized practical benefits such as improved information continuity, reduced repetition, and having time and space to ask questions [16]. As one caregiver noted, “I believe that as long as the shared goal is being put forward, you are already halfway there. Then you’ll have an understanding of each other [parents and clinicians], respect each other and appreciate each other deeply” [20].

At the same time, parents also valued practical mechanisms that supported communication and reduced burden: “Instead of having to explain your history. you’d be able to just hand this over and they can read it and it can be adjusted” [16]. Also, the approach encourages healthcare providers to create a non-pressured, supportive space for families to process information and ask questions: “She just gave us lots of time to ask our questions and at no point was she kind of, I don’t know, she never ever gave us the impression there was a hurry” [16].

Additionally, the continuity of care model established through pACP supports sustained trust between families and medical teams, enabling more personalized and effective care. One parent shared,

“I think the hard thing was that, you know, they were kind of, like, ‘what do you think?’, which is great that they wanted to ask our opinion but, at the time, we were like so overwhelmed” [21]. The other emphasized, “Continuity of care and information should be improved through regular pediatric advance care planning meetings with the various care providers” [23].

Synthesized finding 3: dilemmas and barriers to parental participation in pACP

Category 3.1: insufficient healthcare competency undermines parental trust

The effective implementation of pACP relies on the professional competency of healthcare providers, standardized communication processes, and interdisciplinary collaboration. However, parents reported that some clinicians were reluctant to initiate pACP discussions in a timely manner, often in the context of prognostic uncertainty or avoidance of difficult conversations, which was perceived as undermining trust and making participation in pACP more difficult. As noted in one study, “Other barriers included the physicians’ reluctance to engage in pACP conversations because of prognostic uncertainty or because they do not face up to the facts” [22]. Parents also described inadequate information delivery and lack of clarity, which contributed to feelings of mistrust. One parent recounted, “Surely someone should have said to us this is the beginning of the end, but nobody said that, they just skirt around the issue” [15].

Additionally, inconsistency in care teams and the absence of standardized communication procedures contribute to parents’ frustration. As one parent described, “There were always different doctors and you always tell them the same things” [22]. Another parent emphasized the lack of unified care approaches: “One would be happy to do something or they’d put him on something. And then another one would come in and say, ‘now take him off that…’ so that was where we found it a bit hard—it’s different” [21].

Furthermore, the absence of an integrated, streamlined pACP implementation process adds to parental dissatisfaction. Parents reported redundant administrative procedures and poor coordination across healthcare settings. One participant reflected, “It’s lots of paperwork. It’s lots of sitting down and doing the same paperwork over and over again. If you could do it once then that would be fantastic. [.] You have to do it for every agency that comes in, and for everywhere you go” [23].

Category 3.2: inappropriate timing and communication strategies affecting pACP discussions

The effective implementation of pACP relies on appropriate timing and proper communication strategies. However, some parents described that healthcare team often lacks professional communication skills when discussing pACP, and some physicians adopt a directive approach, merely informing parents of decisions rather than engaging them in a collaborative discussion. One parent described this experience, noting: “They just told us ‘We don’t plan to do anything more’ instead of asking our thoughts” [19].

Poor timing further exacerbates parental resistance, making them more reluctant to engage in pACP: “To have to decide to end life support when you are in such a state of grief and shock is too difficult, we needed more time to make this decision and we felt rushed” [19].

In addition, parents described that pACP discussions were sometimes initiated in non-private environments, which left them feeling unprepared and undermined the quality of sensitive conversations. As one parent expressed, “Having a conversation at the end of the hallway. feels rushed” [17].

Category 3.3: imbalance of decision-making power weakens the autonomy of parents and children

Across the included studies, parents described imbalances in decision-making power during pACP discussions, reporting that their views (and, where applicable, their child’s views) were sometimes overlooked or marginalized, leaving families feeling powerless and undermining trust. Some healthcare teams make decisions without sufficient communication, leading to a decline in parental trust and triggering feelings of frustration and dissatisfaction: “I think things could have been done differently if they would have listened to me rather than just doing their job” [18].

In some cases, parents described being offered predetermined outcomes rather than an array of possibilities, which reinforced perceptions of limited involvement. One parent recounted, “Only one option was presented in the end and that was that she would not survive” [19].This erosion of trust can intensify when families are excluded from critical discussions altogether. In one instance, one mother discovered that care decisions had been made while she was absent and was quoted saying, “you have broken my trust again completely and don’t lie to me” [21].

In addition, some parents emphasized that child-centred care requires acknowledging the child’s voice, even when the child is not able to make treatment decisions independently. As one parent described, “They felt that their child should be heard and taken seriously even if unable to make treatment decisions” [22].

Category 3.4: post-decision stress and emotional conflict aggravate psychological burden

Parents also tend to experience significant psychological turmoil after having made pACP decisions as a result of unresolved doubts and feelings of guilt and emotional distress. They may constantly question whether their choices were the best for their child, with this emotional burden extending beyond immediate reflective judgment and potentially evolving into long-term guilt that affects their mental well-being. One parent, reflecting on their experience, admitted, “This whole thing still haunts me and horrifies me. My mind keeps going back to watching my daughter die” [19].

In addition, parents described that the emotional imprint of decision-making could contribute to fear or avoidance when facing similar decisions in the future. As one caregiver expressed, “That decision didn’t come about easy. It didn’t—people think oh well, you chose to switch his life support off. Yeah, we did but we also chose to say, we had to choose to say goodbye to him, you know what I mean?” [21].

Discussion

This meta-synthesis identified three synthesized findings that influence parental involvement in pediatric advance care planning (pACP), including behavioral coping patterns, value-based perceptions and experiences, and multifaceted participation barriers. These collectively reveal the emotional, ethical, and systemic complexities that are negotiated by parents of children with life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) throughout the pACP process. While some parents demonstrate proactive engagement and meaning-oriented reflection, others exhibit hesitancy, avoidance, or experience constraints due to cultural norms, poor provider communication ability, and deficits in institutions [24]. The perceived value of pACP—as in its potential to improve quality of life, promote family-centered decision-making processes, and foster physician-parent trust—is often moderated by contextual limitations and imbalanced power relations in clinical interactions [25].

Taken together, these synthesized findings indicate that parental engagement in pACP is dynamic rather than static, shifting between avoidance/resistance, hesitation/adjustment, and proactive participation across the illness trajectory. The model is inductively derived from our synthesis and summarizes the interplay between intrinsic enabling factors, external interfering factors, and a behavioral transition pathway across the pACP engagement continuum. Conceptually, the PEPA model is consistent with transactional stress-and-coping perspectives [26], which highlight how coping responses shift as parents appraise stressors and mobilize resources over time, and with shared decision-making frameworks [27], which emphasize bidirectional information exchange, deliberation, and mutual participation in clinical decisions. These theories are used as interpretive lenses to enhance conceptual clarity, without constraining the inductive, data-driven development of the PEPA model.

Building upon this framework, the following discussion is organized around the three synthesized findings identified in the Results: (1) parents’ behavioral coping patterns in pACP engagement; (2) parents’ perceived values and experiences of pACP; and (3) dilemmas and barriers constraining engagement. We then derive practice implications explicitly grounded in these findings to inform clinical implementation and future research.

The progressive implementation of pACP

The implementation of pACP is a dynamic and progressive process, influenced by both disease progression and the family’s cognitive adaptation. Given the uncertainty of LLI trajectories and the evolving perceptions of parents, they generally advocate for a phased approach to pACP, with repeated discussions [22] and dynamic decision adjustments based on disease progression [23].

In the early stages of the disease, parents primarily focus on medical and technical aspects such as treatment options, medication selection and survival predictions [15], often prioritizing curative interventions. At this stage, a family-centered approach in pACP introduction can help facilitate adaptation by parents in accepting the decision-making process and granting a sense of control over uncertainty, thereby alleviating psychological distress due to choice anxiety [28]. Nonetheless, the timing of pACP intervention directly impacts its effectiveness. Research suggests that pACP introduction can trigger parental resistance if initiated prematurely while imposition of choice during a crisis can significantly increase psychological load [12]. Therefore, precisely determining the optimal timing for pACP initiation is critical to improving its acceptance.

As the child enters the disease transition phase, professionals should guide parents in reconstructing what is meant by quality of life, progressively moving from “extending survival” towards “optimizing quality of life” [23]. Research has shown that granting parents decision-making autonomy and encouraging them to prioritize quality of life considerations can help reduce regret and distress following the loss of a child [29]. In this process, a shift in communication approach is pivotal—from a “disease-oriented” approach to a “value-oriented” one. The healthcare team can adopt a “progressive information disclosure” strategy [22], whereby information is conveyed through a series of small-scale conversations over time, given sufficient time for reflection and processing. With this approach, parents are able to make informed decisions that align with their family values and medical ethics at the most appropriate time [17, 18, 22].

Therefore, healthcare providers must not only accurately identify the optimal timing for pACP initiation but also make guidance on decision making responsive to parents’ readiness for information. A stepwise and phased approach in making decisions is essential to both enhancing the acceptability and improving the effectiveness of pACP implementation.

Practical challenges and limitations in the implementation of pACP

Despite robust evidence that pediatric advance care planning (pACP) can improve end-of-life care quality, facilitate family-centered communication, and reduces decisional burden [25, 29], its clinical implementation remains limited across diverse healthcare settings. Beyond its pragmatic role in guiding future medical decisions, pACP also fosters early, value-based conversations that align care trajectories with the goals and priorities of children with life-limiting conditions and their families [12]. When effectively integrated, it enhances shared understanding, minimizes conflict, and supports goal-concordant care. Nonetheless, routine uptake is still hindered by structural constraints, inadequate provider training, and persistent sociocultural resistance across healthcare systems.

Lack of preparedness and training among healthcare providers

A major barrier to the implementation of pediatric advance care planning (pACP) is healthcare providers’ limited preparedness and confidence in initiating sensitive, value-laden conversations. Many clinicians report inadequate training in pediatric palliative care communication, limited exposure to structured ACP protocols, and concerns about triggering emotional distress in patients and families [30, 31].These apprehensions frequently lead to the deferral of pACP discussions until the child’s condition deteriorates significantly, thereby missing the optimal window for earlier, proactive, and value-aligned decision-making. Even in high-income countries, formal education in pediatric palliative care remains underdeveloped, and interdisciplinary training opportunities are limited [32, 33]. As a result, clinicians frequently lack the skills to navigate the emotional, spiritual, and cultural complexities inherent in pACP. To ensure broader and more consistent implementation, comprehensive training in pACP should be systematically integrated into pediatric residency curricula, continuing medical education, and institutional practice guidelines [31].

Cultural and social barriers to acceptance

Cultural norms and social values play a critical role in shaping attitudes toward pediatric advance care planning (pACP) [34, 35]. In many cultures, discussing death, especially when it concerns children, is considered taboo and often evokes feelings of fear, hopelessness, or a perception of clinical failure [16]. Engaging in end-of-life conversations with children is perceived as being psychologically harmful or even morally wrong. Consequently, families may demonstrate intense resistance to pACP and prefer aggressive life-sustaining interventions, while undervaluing considerations of comfort and quality of life [31]. These values not only influence family preferences but also shape the practice of clinicians, who may hesitate to initiate pACP discussions due to societal expectations or institutional norms [34–37]. To address these barriers, there is a need for culturally sensitive communication strategies, public education, and community engagement. These efforts aim to reposition pACP as a process that promotes dignity, empowerment, and alignment with the family’s beliefs and goals, rather than as a sign of therapeutic failure.

Insufficient policy and legal support

At a systemic level, pACP integration is often hindered by the absence of clear legal frameworks and supportive policy infrastructure. In many countries, inconsistent regulations and the lack of formal recognition for advance directives in minors contribute to clinical uncertainty, discouraging routine documentation and uptake of pACP [6, 30]. Moreover, ongoing global gaps in pediatric palliative care (PPC) professionals in the workforce further widen gaps in implementation. For instance, as of 2019, the United States had only 364 board-certified pediatric palliative care physicians, even though more children are living with limiting conditions [30]. In health systems where PPC remains underdeveloped, a shortage of interdisciplinary teams, standardized protocols, and institutional support further undermines the scalability of pACP [38]. Breaking these structural barriers requires coordinated policy action, including legal recognition of pediatric ACP documents, sustainable funding for PPC programs, and investments in developing the workforce and facilitating care integration.

Recommendations for enhancing parental engagement in pACP

Providing disease and decision-making information to guide parents toward active participation

Empowering parents with clear, tailored information about their child’s illness and care options is fundamental for engaging them in pACP. This synthesis found that parents of children with life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) may experience role conflicts and cognitive dissonance during end-of-life decision-making, and that information asymmetry can intensify anxiety. The medical teams are advised by the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) to have open communication and involve families while making decisions about end-of-life care. Through open communication, medical teams are able to ensure that decisions align with the best interests of the child, particularly when considering the initiation or withdrawal of life-sustaining treatments [39, 40].

In this process, the family serves as the central decision-making entity but remains highly dependent on continuous support and guidance from medical professionals [31]. Thus, healthcare teams should adopt a phased and personalized communication strategy, providing clear and accurate disease-specific information and treatment options to support parents’ understanding of the child’s condition and informed decision-making [7]. Using brief written summaries or visual aids may further reinforce understanding. Additionally, clinicians should actively promote active question-asking among parents, thereby enhancing parents’ decision-making capability and empowerment [41]. This, in turn, can advance their psychological adaptability and optimize the medical decision-making process.

Optimizing timing and communication strategies while respecting parental decision-making authority

The timing and approach to initiating pACP discussions are critical factors that affect parental acceptance and decision-making quality [30]. Studies suggest that conducting pACP discussions during relatively stable disease states is more appreciated by parents than introducing them during critical illness episodes [7, 24, 42]. Given the complexity and rapid progression of life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) in children, initiating pACP conversations during stable disease states provide parents with ample time for consideration, alleviate psychological distress, and enhance their sense of involvement in decision-making [25, 43]. Healthcare providers should therefore proactively introduce pACP early in the illness trajectory and revisit discussions as the child’s condition evolves, while remaining attentive to parents’ readiness to engage in “what if” scenarios.

In terms of communication strategies, a gradual and empathetic approach is paramount. Providers must avoid overly blunt or highly technical language and use empathetic dialogue and open-ended questions to elicit values and goals [31]. For example, asking “What do you understand about child’s condition?”or “What are your biggest hopes and worries right now?” invites parents to share perspectives and facilitates a more reflective, collaborative discussion. Such open-ended probing can lead parents toward active, thoughtful participation in decisions, rather than feeling pushed or rushed. Also, of utmost importance is respecting the autonomy of the parents in making decisions. While providing professional guidance, the medical team must fully consider the family’s cultural beliefs and values to ensure that pACP decisions align with both medical ethics and parental preferences. Overall, aligning timing and communication with parents’ readiness and values may enhance the acceptability of pACP and support shared decision-making in the child’s care.

Incorporating pediatric decision-making capacity assessments to empower children’s voices

According to international standards for pediatric palliative care, every care decision should be based on shared decision-making among children, families, and healthcare providers [4]. This principle upholds the child’s right to have a say in all matters affecting them. However, in actual pACP processes, medical decisions are predominantly made by parents and healthcare professionals, often overlooking the child’s autonomy. To bridge this gap, healthcare teams should formally assess and acknowledge the child’s decision-making capacity and level of understanding, and incorporate it into planning.

Children’s cognitive abilities and illness insight can vary widely: some school-age children can articulate simple preferences or feelings about treatment, and many adolescents demonstrate maturity comparable to adults in understanding consequences. Integrating pediatric capacity assessment tools tailored to the child’s age, developmental stage, and cognitive level can help determine how a child can be involved [44]. For example, clinicians might use structured frameworks to evaluate if a child can understand their condition and express consistent choices.

Even when children are too young to make decisions outright, seeking their assent and input in age-appropriate ways is recommended. This may involve using play, drawings, or simple language to gauge a younger child’s feelings, or employing guided decision aids for adolescents. Such tools give children and teens a concrete way to express what matters to them, thereby informing the adults’ decisions with the child’s perspective [45].

Incorporating the child’s voice in pACP not only enhances the ethical justification of decisions but can also benefit the family psychologically. Healthcare providers and parents should collaborate to balance respect for the child’s expressed wishes with the practicality of medical decisions. In doing so, parents are encouraged to pay attention to the child’s verbal and non-verbal cues (questions, emotions, body language), as these often communicate the child’s preferences. Research suggests that when parents feel their child has been heard or has “participated” in the decision, parents experience less decisional regret and burden. Thus, systematically empowering the child’s voice in a capacity-appropriate manner can improve the shared decision-making process.

Enhancing palliative care education and pACP training to strengthen healthcare professionals’ competency

This synthesis indicates that healthcare providers are central members of the pACP team, playing a crucial role in decision-making. Regardless of the specific diagnosis, clinical healthcare providers should possess fundamental palliative care competencies [31]. Parents generally expect pACP discussions to be led by professionals familiar with the child’s condition, family background, and treatment history, ensuring informed decision-making support [30]. However, many physicians and nurses lack systematic training in end-of-life communication, emotional support and ethical considerations. As a result, pACP discussions often become perfunctory, with healthcare providers exhibiting hesitation or even avoidance when initiating such conversations [46].

Therefore, structured palliative care education and pACP training are essential. Training for primary care clinicians should encompass communication skills, ethical sensitivity, and interdisciplinary collaboration to help them better support family decision-making and foster trust in patient-provider relationships. Advanced training can be offered through hospitals, universities, and professional conferences to ensure that healthcare providers continuously enhance their competencies in clinical practice [31]. Furthermore, strengthening collaboration between primary and specialized palliative care teams can optimize pACP implementation, improve care quality and enhance family satisfaction [31, 47], for example through consultation or co-management for complex cases. Notably, train-the-trainer programmes such as EPEC-Pediatrics have been disseminated internationally to build palliative care capacity, with evaluations reporting improvements in clinicians’ knowledge and practice [48].

Within the healthcare team, nurses play a particularly vital role in pACP. As a bridge between families and medical teams, nurses are responsible for educating parents about pACP and actively guiding them through discussions [49]. Therefore, it is crucial to reinforce nurses’ training in pACP conversations, enabling them to provide decision-making support that aligns with the values and needs of both patients and families. This may include training bedside nurses to initiate gentle conversations during routine interactions and enabling nurse case managers to coordinate dedicated family meetings for advance care planning.

In summary, improving healthcare professionals’ pACP competencies through sustained education, skills practice, and team-based learning is essential for embedding pACP into paediatric care and strengthening decision support for families.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it only includes qualitative and mixed-method studies published in indexed journals in English or Chinese, excluding gray literature, conference proceedings, and dissertations. This may introduce selection bias and limit the breadth of perspectives captured. Secondly, most of the included studies were conducted in Western countries, raising concerns about cultural applicability. This pattern reflects the absence of eligible qualitative studies from the Chinese database searches, which may limit the cultural transferability of the synthesis and the PEPA model. Variations in healthcare policies, cultural norms, and parental decision-making processes across regions may influence how families perceive and engage in pediatric advance care planning (pACP). Thirdly, differences in study methodologies sample characteristics, and data collection methods may contribute to heterogeneity in the findings. Finally, this study exclusively focuses on parental experiences, without incorporating the perspectives of healthcare providers or children. This may result in an incomplete understanding of the complex interactions that shape pACP discussions.

Conclusion

This study systematically explores, through meta-aggregation, the decision-making process, emotional experiences and influencing factors of parents of children with life-limiting illnesses (LLIs) in pediatric advance care planning (pACP). The findings highlight the behavioral coping strategies of parents, their perceptions of pACP value, and the barriers they face during the planning process. These insights contribute to a more nuanced understanding of the complex dynamics involved in pACP. However, significant challenges remain in the implementation of pACP, including information asymmetry, cultural influences, insufficient policy support, and limitations in multidisciplinary collaboration. To further optimize pACP practices, it is essential to establish transparent communication mechanisms, respect family decision-making autonomy, incorporate pediatric decision-making capacity assessments, and reinforce healthcare providers’ pACP training. Future research should explore the applicability of pACP across diverse cultural contexts and develop practice models tailored to local healthcare systems and sociocultural norms. In addition, further studies are needed to confirm, refine, and expand the proposed PEPA framework developed from this meta-synthesis, in order to strengthen its theoretical and practical value in clinical settings.
