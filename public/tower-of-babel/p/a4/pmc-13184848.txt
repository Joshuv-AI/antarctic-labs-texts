“Migrant” Ductal Tissue in the Aortic Arch
Authors: Amit Kumar Pandey, Mahesh Kappanayil, Navaneetha Sasikumar, Brijesh Kottyail, Indu Nair, Raman Krishna Kumar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeonatal aortic arch obstruction is typically related to ductal insertion and usually improves with prostaglandin E1 (PGE1). However, functional constriction caused by ectopic ductal tissue at atypical arch locations is rare and may mimic fixed coarctation.Case SummaryA one-day-old neonate with suspected coarctation was found to have a right aortic arch with transverse arch hypoplasia, an aberrant left subclavian artery arising from a Kommerell diverticulum, and a large patent ductus arteriosus. Severe neonatal coarctation of the aorta is among the most common causes of duct-dependent systemic circulation, in which maintenance of ductal patency with prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) is the standard initial management prior to definitive surgical intervention.1,2 Classical juxtaductal coarctation is believed to result from extension of contractile ductal tissue into the adjacent aortic isthmus, leading to progressive luminal narrowing following postnatal ductal constriction. Consequently, prostaglandin responsiveness is typically confined to the aortic isthmus or the immediate ductal insertion site.2,3 Right aortic arch with an aberrant left subclavian artery (LSCA) arising from a Kommerell diverticulum represents an uncommon arch branching variant that may complicate anatomical interpretation and surgical planning in neonatal arch obstruction.4 In such configurations, the ductus arteriosus is often anatomically remote from the transverse arch, and prostaglandin responsiveness of proximal arch segments would not be anticipated based on conventional understanding.3,5

While juxtaductal coarctation may exhibit PGE1 responsiveness due to ductal tissue extension into the adjacent aortic isthmus, prostaglandin-sensitive narrowing in more proximal aortic segments without anatomical ductal continuity has not been previously described.

Initial Echocardiography on Prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) Infusion Demonstrates a Widely Patent Aortic Arch

Following PGE1 withdrawal, repeat imaging at 12 hours shows new discrete narrowing of the distal arch proximal to the right subclavian artery, with a significant Doppler gradient and diastolic tailing consistent with coarctation physiology. Serial Doppler images during reintroduction of PGE1 demonstrate progressive reduction in the gradient over time with resolution of the coarctation segment, supporting a prostaglandin-responsive functional obstruction due to ectopic ductal tissue.

Case Report

A one-day-old mechanically ventilated newborn on PGE1 infusion was received in the Emergency-room, with a suspected diagnosis of severe coarctation and left ventricular dysfunction. Bedside echocardiography showed the right aortic arch with transverse-arch hypoplasia (proximal = 2.5 mm; Z score: −3.71 and distal = 4 mm; Z score: −2.16) (Z-score measurements were calculated using the Boston Children's Hospital z-score calculator6) (peak Doppler gradient 16 mm Hg, minimal diastolic tailing), aberrant LSCA. A large patent ductus arteriosus (PDA) connecting to the proximal descending thoracic aorta as a Kommeral's diverticulum (KD), giving rise to LSCA. No right ductus with good biventricular function. Four-limb pulses, blood pressures, and saturations were normal (Figure 1).

Baseline Echocardiographic Findings During Prostaglandin E1 Infusion

(A) Pulse-wave Doppler of the abdominal aorta demonstrates a pulsatile flow pattern. (B) Modified parasternal short-axis view shows a widely patent ductus arteriosus with left-to-right shunt. (C) Suprasternal view reveals a widely patent aortic arch with unobstructed antegrade flow.

PGE1 infusion was withheld initially. Repeat echocardiography 12 hours later revealed discrete constriction at the distal arch, proximal to the right subclavian artery (peak systolic gradient of 52 mm Hg, diastolic tailing), and mild left ventricular systolic dysfunction (Figure 2). Restarting PGE1 infusion resulted in a significant reduction of coarctation severity over 4 hours. This rapid, reversible change in gradient with withdrawal and reintroduction of PGE1 suggested a functional, prostaglandin-responsive mechanism.

Echocardiographic Findings After Discontinuation of Prostaglandin E1

(A) Continuous-wave Doppler across the aortic arch demonstrates a peak gradient of 52 mm Hg with marked diastolic tailing. (B and C) Suprasternal views show discrete narrowing of the transverse arch with flow turbulence evident on color Doppler imaging.

Computed tomography–angiogram, 3D-reconstruction (Figure 3) to address arch and ductal anatomical uncertainties revealed a right aortic arch, midline descending aorta; large PDA from the roof of the main pulmonary artery continuing into KD well away from aortic isthmus, LSCA from KD. The transverse arch showed mild hypoplasia (proximal = 4.5 mm; Z score: −2.62 and distal = 4 mm; Z score: −2.16) without discrete narrowing.

Computed Tomography Angiogram Findings

Computed tomography angiography demonstrated a right aortic arch with mild hypoplasia and no evidence of coarctation. Three-dimensional reconstruction revealed a patent ductus arteriosus arising from the main pulmonary artery via a Kommerell’s diverticulum, associated with an aberrant left subclavian artery origin.

Since anatomy suggested nonductal dependency, a controlled second PGE1 withdrawal trial was undertaken to confirm reproducibility of the functional obstruction. (Figure 4, Video 1). Serial echocardiograms showed the onset and progression of isolated, discrete distal-arch constriction, beginning at approximately 4 to 6 hours and evolving over 10 hours into classical 2D/Doppler features of discrete coarctation. The aortic end of PDA remained wide open, with constriction at the pulmonary artery end (left-right shunt). Restarting the PGE1 infusion resolved the distal-arch constriction.

(C) Following reinitiation of PGE1 infusion, the suprasternal view shows resolution of the transverse arch narrowing with reduction in gradient and absence of diastolic tailing on CWD. Through a median sternotomy, the arch vessels and PDA were meticulously dissected, and the left common carotid artery was cannulated for arterial inflow. Following systemic heparinization, cardiopulmonary bypass was initiated (cardiopulmonary bypass time, 169 minutes; aortic cross-clamp time, 82 minutes). The LSCA was divided from the Kommerell diverticulum, and the ductus arteriosus was doubly ligated to relieve the vascular ring. After cooling to 20 °C, the aorta was cross-clamped, and cardioplegic arrest was achieved. All ductal tissue, including the hypoplastic isthmus, was excised. The descending aorta was mobilized and anastomosed to the reconstructed arch in a tension-free fashion using a continuous 8-0 polypropylene suture. The right subclavian artery was subsequently reimplanted with anterior pericardial patch augmentation. “Ductal tissue” was visibly identified in resected arch tissue but was notably absent from the duct-insertion site. Histopathologic examination with special stains (Orcein and Masson's trichrome) (Figure 5) confirmed subendothelial proliferation of elastic fibers consistent with ductal tissue. The infant had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on day 7. At the 3-month follow-up, the infant remained asymptomatic with no residual gradient across the aortic arch.

(A) Orcein stain demonstrates subendothelial proliferation of elastic fibers (pink arrowhead) resembling ductal tissue. (B) Masson's trichrome stain confirms this finding, highlighting pink/magenta elastic fiber (arrow) proliferation within the subendothelium.

Discussion

The ductus arteriosus derives from the distal portion of the left sixth aortic arch and functions as a critical fetal vascular shunt, diverting blood from the pulmonary artery to the descending aorta.1 Its closure after birth is governed by oxygen tension and local prostaglandin levels.1,2 The normal ductus contains a circumferential layer of smooth muscle that differs from the elastic walls of the pulmonary artery and aorta.2,3

After birth, declining prostaglandin levels and rising oxygen tension lead to ductal closure. This closure can be reversed pharmacologically in neonates with duct-dependent lesions using PGE1.5

Neonatal management of suspected coarctation relies heavily on distinguishing fixed anatomical obstruction from functional ductal-related narrowing. In equivocal cases, prostaglandin infusion serves both as therapy and as a diagnostic tool; improvement in gradients suggests ductal tissue contribution. However, this paradigm assumes anatomical continuity between the ductus and the site of obstruction.2, 3, 4, 5 Juxtaductal coarctation is thought to arise from encroachment of ductal tissue into the adjacent isthmic aorta.2 However, prostaglandin-responsive narrowing has been described only at this junction or in rare accessory arch configurations, such as a persistent fifth aortic arch.2,7 No prior reports have demonstrated reversible PGE1-responsive narrowing localized to the transverse aortic arch, particularly when anatomically distinct from the PDA.

This case reveals a unique pathology, ectopic ductal tissue embedded in the transverse arch wall, functionally mimicking coarctation but anatomically distant from the ductus arteriosus. The reproducible pattern of gradient development and resolution in response to PGE1 administration, together with histopathological confirmation of ductal-type smooth muscle within the resected transverse arch, and its absence of continuity with the PDA site, strengthens the hypothesis of ectopic ductal tissue—possibly related to aberrant migration or incomplete regression of sixth-arch derivatives.1,3,7

An additional unusual feature of this case was the coexistence of coarctation with a right aortic arch, which added complexity to surgical planning and operative strategy.4 The findings in this case broaden the current understanding of prostaglandin-sensitive tissue distribution in congenital heart disease. It reinforces the importance of functional testing with PGE1 in neonates with equivocal aortic arch obstruction. It suggests that, in rare instances, arch narrowing may be temporized medically with PGE1. Surgeons should anticipate the possibility of ectopic ductal tissue and ensure complete excision to prevent recurrence.

Conclusions

This is the first reported case of isolated prostaglandin-responsive functional narrowing of the transverse aortic arch in a newborn, anatomically distinct and not contiguous with the PDA. The case highlights the need to consider functional coarctation due to ectopic ductal tissue in the differential diagnosis of neonatal arch obstruction and supports a conservative approach using PGE1 in selected ambiguous cases.
