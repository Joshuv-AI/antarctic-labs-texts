Complete healing of a Dooshivishajanya non-healing wound with Jalaukavacharana - A case report
Authors: Praveen Balakrishnan, Emy S Surendran, GN Sree Deepthi, A Abhayadev, Lisha S Raj
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA 35-year-old male patient, who is non-diabetic, non-dyslipidemic, normotensive, and euthyroid, and works as a hotel manager, visited our outpatient department with complaints of two ulcers on the inner side of his right lower leg, which developed rapidly after intense itching for about a week due to scratching. The ulcers were clean, had granulation tissue, caused mild pain, showed no discharge, and were surrounded by dark discolouration. The patient's history indicated that he had experienced severe itching in that area for four to five years, along with recurrent fevers, which began after being bitten by an unidentified type of black ant. The Bates Jensen wound assessment tool scored 33 for the larger wound and 27 for the smaller wound. Initially, the patient received some Ayurvedic treatments aimed at promoting healing for approximately 40 days, but these did not improve. A venous Doppler ultrasound of his right leg indicated chronic venous insufficiency. Given the persistent nature of the ulcers despite medical treatment, the condition was diagnosed as Dooshi Vishajanya Vrana over chronic venous insufficiency. Therefore, a single session of Jalaukavacharana was performed using three Jalaukas on the ulcer site, which healed within one week of the treatment. After the treatment, the Bates Jensen wound assessment scores improved to 18 for the larger wound and 17 for the smaller wound. Follow-up evaluations conducted after four months and one year revealed no recurrence of the ulcers. The dark discolouration of the leg began to diminish from the fourth month of the follow-up period.Keywords: Ayurveda, Ayush 64, Case report, Dooshi Visha, Jalaukavacharana, Venous leg ulcer

Introduction

An insect or bug bite refers to the bites and stings from members of the phylum Arthropoda. Insects, which constitute nearly half of this phylum, have the most significant influence on humans through the spread of vector-borne illnesses and various health issues. Typically, bug bites present as red and swollen areas, sometimes accompanied by hives or raised bumps. In some cases, the toxicity of the venom can lead to systemic effects such as autonomic instability, neurotoxicity, organ failure, and anaphylactic reactions, which can ultimately result in circulatory collapse. A sudden onset of pain or itching, along with the identification of the insect responsible, provides strong evidence of an insect bite. Itching can be alleviated with topical treatments like calamine lotion or corticosteroids; in some cases, oral antihistamines and systemic corticosteroids may be necessary. Symptomatic treatments are also provided based on the individual's condition. Antibiotics may be prescribed if a secondary infection develops at the site of the bite. When assessing a patient who an insect has bitten, it is crucial to note that the clinical signs of an insect bite may not appear for weeks, months, or even years [1].

Venous leg ulcers (VLUs) are advanced manifestations of chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) and venous hypertension. Proper blood flow from the legs to the heart is facilitated by functioning valves in the veins and regular contraction of the calf muscles. When there is an obstruction or backward blood flow, venous stagnation occurs, leading to venous hypertension and various dermatological and venous issues, including VLUs [2]. Typically, VLUs are found on the inner side of the lower legs and present as shallow, well-defined, and irregular ulcers with a fibrinous base. An affordable, non-invasive method for assessing superficial veins, which allows for the identification of thrombi and valve incompetence, is colour-flow duplex ultrasound. The standard treatment for VLUs includes compression therapy, direct wound management, and measures to decrease leg oedema. Healing occurs, but the risk of recurrence is notable, with over 22 % within three months, 57 % within twelve months, and 78 % within three years [3]. Ayurveda encompasses a wide range of toxicology under the term Visha (poison), which includes Krimi-visha (poisoning from various organisms), Keeta-visha (poisoning from insects), Gara-visha (artificial poison), and Dooshi-visha (latent toxicity) [4]. A specific chapter in Ayurveda discusses the Lakshana (symptomatology) and Chikitsa (treatment) of Keeta-visha in the Susruta Samhita, emphasising its significance [5]. (Kalpa sthana; Keeta Kalpa: Chapter 08) The pathophysiology and treatment of a particular type of poisoning known as Dooshi-visha are well-defined in Ayurveda. Ulcers, whether VLUs or arising from other causes, are treated based on the principles of Vrana-chikitsa (ulcer treatment principles). The effectiveness of Jalaukavacharana (also known as Medicinal Leech Therapy or Hirudo therapy today) has been established in previous studies [[6], [7], [8]]. The application of Jalaukavacharana for treating Vrana caused by Dooshi-visha in a patient with CVI, resulting in immediate complete healing and prevention of recurrence, has yet to be documented. Therefore, we present a case of complete healing and no recurrence of a Dooshi-vishajanya non-healing ulcer following a single session of Jalaukavacharana, which developed as a result of dooshi-visha from an insect bite in a patient with chronic venous insufficiency (CVI). This case report has been prepared following the CARE guidelines [9].

Patient information

A 35-year-old male hotel manager, who is normotensive, non-diabetic, non-dyslipidemic, and euthyroid, visited our outpatient department on October 19, 2023, with complaints of two ulcers on the inner side of his right lower leg that had developed over the past week. The ulcers arose from scratches caused by severe itching in that area. The ulcers were mildly painful but showed no signs of discharge. The patient reported being bitten by an unidentified type of black ant on the medial aspect of his right leg about four to five years ago. Initially, he experienced only redness and swelling at the bite site and treated it with local herbal remedies (applying Moringa oleifera leaf paste), but his condition worsened. He subsequently developed fever and significant body aches, prompting him to seek help from a local health center, where he was prescribed paracetamol and other unspecified medications. All his symptoms resolved within a week. Six months later, he experienced a sudden episode that included fever, headache, watery eyes, severe body aches, and chills. Erythema and redness appeared over the medial part of his right leg, centered around the bite site. He self-administered paracetamol and other medications obtained from a local primary health centre, which alleviated his symptoms. Similar episodes have recurred approximately every six months since then. He began to notice a gradual development of pedal oedema and blackish discoloration with persistent itching over the medial aspect of his affected leg and ankle, which worsened with each fever episode. Due to the lack of complete resolution of his condition, he sought Ayurvedic treatments at our hospital. Beginning August 3, 2023, the patient was given Ayush 64 (two tablets daily after meals), Amruthotharam Kwatha and Punarnavadi Kwatha (60 ml two times daily before meals), Avipatti Churna (2 g), and Godanti Bhasma (500 mg), taken 2.5 g twice daily after meals, which resulted in relief from recurrent fever (with no further episodes since then) and pedal oedema. By October 5, 2023, the severity of his itching had decreased significantly. However, he experienced a sudden episode of severe itching on October 12, 2023, and scratching led to the formation of the ulcers.

Clinical findings

The patient had two ulcers since October 12, 2023. One ulcer was located on the medial aspect of the right leg at its midpoint. The other one was just above the superior pole of the medial malleolus of the right ankle. The larger ulcer was vertically oval, measuring approximately 2 × 1 cm (length x width) and had a depth of about 2–3 mm. Its edges were regular and thick, surrounded by dense pigmentation. There were no indications of slough or necrotic tissue; the edge had a sloping contour and felt hard to the touch. The floor was clear and showed signs of granulation tissue. The smaller ulcer was 5 mm × 3 mm x 1 mm in size, located near the second ulcer. The Bates Jensen wound assessment tool indicated scores of 33 for the larger wound and 27 for the smaller one, respectively (Refer Table 01). Both the ulcers were free from discharge. We did some Ayurveda management for about 40 days, but did not yield any results. (Refer Table 02).

Diagnostic assessments

Venous doppler ultrasound of his right leg indicated the presence of incompetent saphenofemoral junction (SFJ), along with a dilated greater saphenous vein (GSV) and short saphenous vein (SSV). Multiple incompetent perforators were observed on the medial and anterior-medial sides of the leg in the upper, mid, and lower thirds. Additionally, a few inflamed inguinal-femoral lymph nodes (lymphadenitis) were noted. The examination revealed diffuse subcutaneous oedema, more pronounced in the mid and lower thirds. [Refer Annexure - I]. Venous leg ulcers (VLU) resulting from chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) often manifest with pruritus with or without a rash and evening pedal oedema [1]. In this case, pruritus, pedal oedema of the lower limb, and a rash were present. However, there was no evening increase in pedal oedema or associated night cramps. Since he did not have diabetes, both diabetic ulcer and neuropathic ulcer were ruled out. The location of the ulcer on the inside of the leg, situated between the knee and the ankle, indicates a venous ulcer and rules out an arterial ulcer. Since the ulcer did not show any signs of fever or discharge from the wound and responded well to non-antibiotic treatment, the likelihood of an infection was also excluded. Since, there was associated lymphadenitis, a diagnosis of CVI along with inflammation was made.

From an Ayurvedic perspective, we noted the presence of Jihvatalabha Lakshana (appearance of the tongue), Nirasrava (absence of discharge), and a regular shape (su-vyavasthitah). Typically, these characteristics promote rapid healing of an ulcer. Nevertheless, in this instance, the ulcer did not heal. The ulcer exhibited Kshipra Janma (rapid onset) or, in other terms, Bhrisham Paaki (rapid suppuration) and was associated with Mandavedana (mild pain). There were no signs of spider nevi, nodules, or neurological symptoms such as hyperesthesia, burning sensation, or fluctuating or severe pain. Given the (a) previous history of Keeta-visha (insect bite poison) and recurrent fever; (b) alongside sudden Kandu Bahulya (intense itching), Krishna (blackish discoloration) of the affected area, Shotha (pedal oedema/lymphadenitis); (c) and the ulcer's appearance following the insect bite incident, we reached a diagnosis of Vrana caused by Dooshi-visha (as a consequence of Keeta-visha), in a patient with CVI. In other words, the Dooshi-visha/Keeta-visha may have triggered the development of ulcers in a patient already diagnosed with CVI, meaning that Dooshi-visha/Keeta-visha serves as the Sannikrishta Nidana (immediate cause) while CVI acts as the Vipikrishta Nidana (distant cause) of the ulcer.

Therapeutic Intervention

We performed Jalaukavacharana on December 7, 2023. Three leeches were attached to the ulcer sites: two on the larger ulcer and one near the smaller ulcer. It was carried out as an outpatient procedure using the standard operating procedure detailed in Annexure - 2 in accordance with the classical guidelines [5].(Sutra sthana; Jalaukavacharaniyam adhyaya: Chapter 13 verse 19).

Follow-up and outcomes

The ulcer, which had not healed even after 40 days of oral treatments and topical applications, resolved within a week following a single session of Jalukavacharana. There was no pain or itching reported. One week post-Jalukavacharana, the Bates Jensen wound assessment indicated scores of 18 for the larger wound and 17 for the smaller one. A follow-up after two months confirmed no recurrence. The blackish discolouration on the leg remained the same until the second month of follow-up but diminished by the fourth month of assessment (Refer to Fig. 1 (d)). The ulcer did not reappear even one year after administering the leech therapy. With the exception of the follow-up in the fourth month, during which the patient visited the institute in person, information for all subsequent months was gathered via telephone.

Discussion

Discussion on differential diagnosis of the leg ulcer

The differential diagnosis of the ulcers has already been described in section 4. Leg ulcers that do not respond to conventional wound treatment and display unusual visible characteristics or clinical signs∗, should undergo a wound biopsy and histopathological assessment. In our case, even though the wound did not show improvement with the first line of ayurvedic treatment, it ultimately healed completely with Jalaukavacharana. Therefore, a wound biopsy was not necessary in this case.

Discussion on differential diagnosis of the leg ulcer

Keeta (insects) are broadly categorised into three types based on the predominance of Mahabhutas, determining the symptomatology following an insect bite. These types are Vayu Prakriti Keeta, Agni Prakriti Keeta, and Ambu Prakriti Keeta. Bites from these insects can exacerbate Vata, Pitta, and Kapha disorders, respectively. Symptoms such as Jvara (fever), Angamarda (muscle cramps), and Shopha (swelling) are commonly observed manifestations of Keeta-visha, which can even arise during the aggravation of Dooshi-visha (latent or inactive toxin) [5].(Kalpa sthana; Keeta Kalpa: Chapter 08 Verse 19–23) Bites from ants produce sensations similar to burning (agni sparshavat daha) and cause swelling at the bite site and surrounding areas (damshe shvayathu … shophau) [5],(Kalpa sthana; Keeta Kalpa: Chapter 08 Verse 34) indicating that they can be associated with Agni Prakriti Keeta, leading to a vitiation of Pitta.

Discussion on dooshi-visha, consequences of improper administration of herbal drugs, and patho-physiology of formation of the ulcer

Dooshi-visha is a form of Visha that arises in the body due to incomplete detoxification and elimination processes regarding any poisoning. This incomplete detoxification, induced either through medication or the body's immune response, results in the Visha being encapsulated in Kapha, remaining within the body in a Manda (weak) state. Various factors like seasonal changes, environmental conditions, dietary habits, and sleep patterns could result in intensified effects of the Visha that can provoke additional immune reactions like Jvara and the symptoms as mentioned above. Such a state over time can lead to Dooshana (vitiation) of Dhatus (tissues), which is why it is termed Dooshi-visha. Itching is a symptom that appears when Manda-visha is present [5]. (Kalpa sthana; Keeta Kalpa: Chapter 08 Verse 19–23) When Dooshi-visha is Prabala (strong), it may lead to discoloration of the affected body parts and irregular fever (Vishama-jvara) [5]. (Kalpa sthana; Sthavaravisha vijnaniya kalpa: Chapter 02 Verse 50) All the signs and symptoms associated with Dooshi-visha were displayed in the patient.

There was initially swelling at the bite site, for which a paste of Shigru-patra (leaves of Moringa oleifera) was applied topically. Shigru-patra possesses Katu-rasa (spicy taste), Ushna (hot property), Pachana (digestive properties), and Vishaghna (toxic neutralising) and is beneficial in pacifying Vata and Kapha [10].(Mulakadi varga: Chapter 07 Verse 131) [11](Karaviradi varga: Chapter 04 Verse 40) In simpler terms, it promotes Pitta. Therefore, it is helpful in cases of Visha where Vata and Kapha are predominant and should be avoided in conditions that primarily involve Pitta. However, in this situation, the condition was mainly Pitta. The manifestation of fever was a result of Pitta vitiation [12].(Chikitsa sthana; Jvara chikitsa: Chapter 01 Verse 16–17) Hence, the incorrect application of Shigru-patra might have led to an aggravation of Pitta in an already vitiated area, causing the onset of fever. Following the application of Shigru-patra, fever was observed in the patient, for which paracetamol and other medicines were administered. These medications likely alleviated the symptomatic immune responses. The literature lacks evidence regarding the anti-poisonous effects of such medications. Since Visha is Apaki, and no Vishagna Chikitsa (antidotal treatments) were conducted, the poison from the bite may have been weakened by the body's immune reactions, surrounded by Kapha (Kapha Avarana), potentially forming a Dooshi-visha. With the arrival of a favourable season, its effects could have intensified leading to recurrent fever in the patient. Initially, this Dooshi-visha could have been in a weak state, which resulted in itching. Over time, this Dooshi-visha likely grew to become Prabala (strong), which could have brought discoloration, progressively darkening year after year as its potency increased.

The Ayurvedic treatments initially prescribed may have diminished the potency of Dooshi-visha, which could have halted the fever's recurrence and alleviated the itching. Since no Shodhana (purification therapy) was administered, the Visha may have persisted in a Manda form confined to that region. Any ahara or vihara that might have triggered the effects of Dooshi-visha could have worsened his itching (the exact trigger for the acute exacerbation could not be identified). This could have initiated Vrana formation in four ways:

(a)Since the ant bites result in Visha, leading to an increase in Pitta, the aggravated Dooshi-visha may have triggered localized Pitta Dushti. This Pitta Dushti could have resulted in localised Rakta Dushti, potentially inducing Paka, which leads to Vrana.

The Vrana observed was clean, featuring visible granulation tissue, sloping edges, and lacking additional infection or discharge, but remained unhealed even after 40 days of oral and topical treatments. This persistence could be attributed to the localised presence of Visha and Rakta Dushti, resulting in Pitta-Rakta predominance that caused the non-healing condition. In such scenarios, removal of the Dushta Rakta through Siravyadhana/Raktamoksha (bloodletting therapy) is recommended.[05](Kalpa sthana; Annapanaraksha kalpa: Chapter 01 verse 75-8) Jalukavacharana is preferred for Raktamokshana in Rakta/Pitta predominant ailments, which may manifest as Vaivarnya (discolouration), Kandu, and Tvak Paka or Vrana. It is also the recommended treatment for Vrana caused by Visha. Additionally, it is a straightforward and practical method for Raktamokshana compared to other techniques.[05](Sutra sthana; Jalaukavacharaniyam adhyaya: Chapter 13 verse 3–7) Jalukavacharana can extract vitiated blood from Avagadha Sthana (deep-seated areas).[05](Sutra sthana; Jalaukavacharaniyam adhyaya: Chapter 13 verse 6) (Shareera sthana; Siravyadhavidhi shareeram: Chapter 08 verse 26). Therefore, through Jalukavacharana, the localised Dushta-rakta and Visha could have been eliminated from the wound's vicinity, facilitating healing within a week. The absence of ulcer recurrence even one year after Jalaukavacharana suggests that the Dooshi-visha in that region may have been entirely eradicated. The complete extraction of the localised Visha may have contributed to the reduction of the blackish discoloration observed during the second follow-up. All these observations indicate that the wound was not a VLU, as such changes would not have occurred, and the ulcer would have reappeared, especially since the patient was continuing his profession (which involves constant standing).

Discussion on choice of oral medications and topical medicines

Every medication operates through either Rasa, Veerya, Vipaka, or Prabhava [12].(Sutra sthana; Dravyadi vijnaniyam adhyaya: Chapter 09 verse 22–23) Among the six principal tastes (Shadrasas), Tikta-rasa possesses the distinct characteristic of Vishahara, indicating that any Vishahara dravya must contain Tikta-rasa.[05](Kalpa sthana; Sthavara visha vijnaniyam kalpam: Chapter 02 verse 25–33). Other tastes can be selected based on the Dosha imbalance present.

Ayush 64

Developed by the Central Council for Research in Ayurvedic Sciences (CCRAS) primarily for malaria, Ayush 64 was later adapted for COVID-19 treatment. The components of this drug, including Kiratatikta (Picrorhiza kurroa Royle ex. Benth), Katurohini (Swertia chirata Pexbex. Karst), Saptaparna (Alstonia scholaris R. Br.), and Kuberaksha (Caesalpinia crista L.), are effective in eliminating Shesha Dosha found in the Dhatus (dhatu leena gata avastha) to avert Punaravartaka jvara [13]. Almost all the components primarily exhibit Tikta/Katu-rasa, where Tikta-rasa works to balance Pitta and Kapha, remove toxins (Visha-hara), and reduce fever (Jvara-hara) while also aiding in the expulsion of excess fluid (Kleda). The Katu component addresses Kapha-related issues, helping to alleviate swelling (Shopha) and Kleda, and supports wound healing (Vrana-avasadana) through Shodhana (cleansing the wound) [7].(Sutra sthana; Rasabhediyam adhyaya: Chapter 10 verse 14–19) Ayush 64 emerged as the appropriate option in this scenario, as evidenced by the lack of fever recurrence following its administration. The prescription of Ayush 64 was maintained, considering the relevant Ayurvedic pharmacodynamics. Ayush 64, thus, can be a powerful treatment option for Visha/Dooshi-visha, which can be confirmed with additional research-based evidence.

Kaishora guggulu

Kaishora guggulu is also recommended for Mandagni (decreased metabolic fire), Vrana (ulcers), Kushta (skin disorders), Shvayathu (oedema), and Prameha pidaka (diabetic carbuncle) [14]. It alleviates Kapha and Pitta. Given the presence of an ulcer, leg discolouration and severe itching, Kaishora guggulu was a suitable choice. It is effective in healing all types of wounds, reducing swelling, detoxifying Ama-visha, purifying the blood, and balancing all three doshas while promoting wound healing and managing toxins. It exhibits strong efficacy against Pitta-related conditions and Pitta-Kapha combinations [15].

Avipatti Churna and Godanti Bhasma

Avipatti Churna is a Pitta-Kapha balancer promoting downward Pitta movement. It acts as a toxin remover [16]. Godanti Bhasma is beneficial for reducing Pitta Jvara, abdominal pain (Shula), chronic fever (Jeerna jvara), and low digestive fire (Agnimandya). It is indicated for Pitta disorders, Visha, and anemia (Pandu) [17]. This medicine is particularly effective for conditions characterised by excessive Pitta/Pitta-Kapha or diseases caused by blood impurity (Rakta-dushti). The formulation primarily consists of Tikta, Madhura, and Katu Rasa, with drying (Rooksha) properties and slightly heating (Shitoshna) [18].

Godanti Bhasma is advantageous for Pitta-related fever, chronic fever, variable fever, digestive issues, and wounds. It is fundamentally tridosha-balancing, promotes waste movement (Mala-anulomana), and combats fever. It can mitigate Pitta's sharpness (Tikshanatva) and fluidity (Dravatva) [19].

Murivenna and Jatyadi Taila

Murivenna is recommended for conditions such as bone fractures, burns, acute wounds, and joint injuries [20]. It effectively balances all three doshas, promotes fast wound healing, reduces inflammation, and has drying and detoxifying properties. It is also utilised for wound binding and is beneficial in cases of swelling or wounds related to Pitta and blood disorders [21]. Jatyadi Taila is particularly suited for dental and nail injuries, itching, and chronic or non-healing ulcers [22]. Murivenna, applied topically, aids in the recovery of wounds and fractures, while Jatyadi Taila cleanses wounds. It is indicated for dental and nail injuries, ulcers, chronic wounds, and wounds that do not heal. It generally alleviates Pitta and Kapha, improves blood circulation, promotes wound healing, detoxifies, and reduces wound discharge [23]. Therefore, it is clear that all the treatments prescribed for the patient were the most suitable for the current condition.

Discussion on primary cutaneous nocardiosis

Primary cutaneous nocardiosis, a rare skin condition, can occur in individuals with an intact immune system. In adults, it typically affects the limbs following a minor injury such as an insect bite or sting. Specific antibiotics, such as Trimethoprim sulfamethoxazole, are generally required for a treatment duration of three to five months for effective recovery [[24], [25], [26]]. In our case, the erythema and redness surrounding the bite area would resolve following each session of his oral medications. If Nocardiosis was the cause, specific antibiotics would have been necessary for alleviation, but was never given/required in this case. Again, there was an absence of discharge and pustule formation. After a session of Jalaukavacharana, the wound completely healed, and there was no recurrence of either the wound or cellulitis for one year. All these rule out the possibility of a nocardial infection.

Discussion on Jalaukavacharana

Leech therapy, also known as Medicinal Leech Therapy or Hirudo therapy, is commonly utilised in Asian and Arab nations for healing wounds, while in Western countries, it is employed to alleviate venous congestion after reconstructive and plastic surgery. Medicinal Leech Therapy has demonstrated anti-inflammatory, thrombolytic, and lymphatic enhancement properties. This therapy is beneficial in healing ulcers and decreasing leg swelling [27,28]. Previous research indicates the effects of medicinal leeches can be observed within 3.5–6 months [29,30]. Using Jalukavacharana in this instance might have helped remove the impure blood along with the accumulated toxins, thus facilitating faster wound healing. The anti-inflammatory effects of leech therapy may have contributed to a decrease in lymphadenitis. A repeat venous doppler ultrasound would be necessary for a definitive conclusion, but it could not be carried out since the patient was unwilling to undergo the procedure. One additional benefit of using leeches is their ability to extract only the impure blood from the affected area [7]. (Sutra sthana; Shastravidhi adhyaya: Chapter 26 verse 42) Previous studies confirm that leeches can effectively draw out venous blood. Consequently, it can be concluded that the toxins mixed with localised impurities might have been drawn out by the leeches, leading to quick wound healing, reducing the likelihood of recurrence, and starting to diminish discoloration. There is over 22 % of recurrence within three months and 57 % chance of recurrence within 12 months of healing, and 78 % recurs within three years [3]. In this case, the ulcer has not recurred even after one year. Therefore, it suggests that the Dooshi-visha in the Rakta may have been eliminated through Jalaukavacharana.

Discussion on the limitations of this case report

We could not perform a follow-up venous doppler study (status after the Jalaukavacharana) of the right leg to evaluate the condition of lymphadenitis and chronic venous insufficiency (CVI) because the patient declined the procedure. We were unable to conduct a skin biopsy on the patient to check for nocardiosis because the patient refused the biopsy. We maintained phone contact with the patient for a year (until December 2024) since he preferred not to come to the hospital, expressing that he felt entirely well and had experienced no new ulcer episodes. The specific cause (related to diet or lifestyle) of the severe itching on October 12, 2023, could not be determined.

Conclusion

Our findings emphasise the importance of considering Rakta-dushti and the presence of Visha in non-healing ulcers. It is strongly advised to begin Jalukavacharana when the practitioner identifies Rakta-dushti and/or Visha-dushti. This method is also very cost-effective in achieving focused results. Jalukavacharana should be applied only in instances of Pitta/Rakta involvement; otherwise, alternative bloodletting methods may be necessary. Ayush 64 might be considered a practical option for treating Visha-vikara, which can be validated only through comprehensive research.
