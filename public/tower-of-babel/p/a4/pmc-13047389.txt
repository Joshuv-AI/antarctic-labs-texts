Specifying the Entry Point for a Transiliac-Transsacral Screw in Posterior Pelvic Ring Fixation
Authors: Guangping Liu, Junjun Tang, Jie He, Nazhi Zhan, Haoyu Liu, Zhengguo Zhu, Hua Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo stabilize posterior pelvic ring injuries with a transiliac-transsacral (TITS) screw percutaneously, it is crucial to determine the adequate entry point as an initial procedure. Here, we propose a universal method for specifying the entry point for the TITS screw under fluoroscopic guidance.MethodsThrough analyzing 239 pelvic computed tomography (CT) images retrospectively, we determined the entry point for the TITS screw at the midpoint between the anterosuperior and posteroinferior corners of the respective vertebra in the strict mid-sagittal CT plane. Virtual surgical procedures were performed to verify the feasibility of using the midpoint as the entry point and to evaluate the accuracy of screw placement. Clinical surgical procedures with our proposed method of determining the entry point on the lateral fluoroscopic view were performed to validate the safety and accuracy of screw placement. Then, the efficacy of this method was evaluated compared to the previously applied method.ResultsIn the virtual surgical procedures, a total of 148 S1 TITS screws were securely placed in all pelvises with normal sacra, while 239 S2 TITS screws were securely placed in all pelvises regardless of dysmorphism. In clinical surgical procedures, 27 S1 screws and 15 S2 screws were securely placed in 41 pelvises, with 39 screws (92.9%) classified as grade 1 and 3 screws (7.1%) classified as grade 2. The rate of excellent or good functional outcomes was 100%. Compared to the previous method, our proposed method revealed superior efficacy regarding surgical duration (p < 0.001), fluoroscopic shots (p < 0.001), and guidewire attempts (p < 0.001).ConclusionsOn the lateral fluoroscopic view, the midpoint between the anterosuperior and posteroinferior corners of the respective vertebra can consistently serve as the entry point for the TITS screw, applicable to S1 and S2 screws in a normal sacrum, as well as to the S2 screw in a dysmorphic sacrum.

Percutaneous sacroiliac (SI) screw placement has increasingly gained popularity for stabilizing an unstable posterior pelvic ring.12) The transiliac-transsacral (TITS) screw, a type of SI screw, provides superior biomechanical advantages over the regular SI screw, as it can engage more cortices and better resist vertical forces.1) It is practically beneficial for bilateral injuries, comminuted sacral fractures, fragility fractures, and revision procedures.3456) However, it is a technical challenge for surgeons to securely place the TITS screw under fluoroscopic guidance. The rate of screw breach has been reported to exceed 30%,7) and neurologic complication rates have reached as high as 18% due to screw mispositioning.8) To ensure the safety of screw placement, the priority of the operative procedure is to determine an adequate entry point for the guidewire after satisfactory reduction.9)

Previous studies tended to identify a trapeziform area on the lateral pelvic view for the safe entry zone.1011) That depended on fluoroscopic images of anatomic landmarks, such as the iliac cortical density, the sacral ala, and the sacral nerve root tunnel.1012) However, these landmarks are hardly detected in scenarios of bone overlap, obesity, poor bone quality, and bowel gas.13) The sacral cortices, visible even in poor quality fluoroscopy, were adopted as the reference for locating the patient-specific entry point.14) However, it necessitated a complicated planning in computed tomography (CT) reconstructions preoperatively. So far, a unified and standardized algorithm for determining the entry point has yet to be developed.

The development of algorithms might benefit from comprehensive analysis and measurements in the CT sagittal plane, due to its versatility. This plane has been validated for assessing the size of osseous corridors and the number of screws at a single level.15) Particularly, the significance of the midline sagittal plane cannot be over-emphasized, demonstrating practicality in calculating the anticipated intraoperative inlet and outlet angles16) and transferring the entry point to the lateral fluoroscopic view.17) In this study, we reconstructed a strict mid-sagittal pelvic CT plane to mimic the lateral pelvic view and hypothesized that the midpoint between the anterosuperior and posteroinferior corners of the sacral vertebra consistently locates within the safe trapeziform area for guidewire insertion. On the basis of this hypothesis, we performed a virtual surgical procedure to of whether the feasibility whether the midpoint serves as the entry point for the TITS screw and conducted clinical practice to evaluate its safety, accuracy, and efficacy.

METHODS

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chinese PLA General Hospital (IRB No. 2023KY147-KS001), and written informed consent was obtained from each patient treated with our proposed method preoperatively.

Virtual Surgical Procedure

Patients

We retrospectively reviewed the records of patients who underwent pelvic CT scans at our institution from April 2023 to July 2023. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the patient was ≥ 18 years old, (2) an intact posterior pelvic ring, (3) a pelvic posterior ring with a nondisplaced or minimally displaced fracture or dislocation, and (4) the CT scan slice thickness ≤ 1 mm. Exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) the patient with a history of pelvic fracture or surgery, (2) the patient with congenital pelvic diseases, (3) the patient with pelvic destruction due to tumors or other pathological diseases. Based on the criteria, 239 CT datasets were enrolled in our study, comprising 126 males and 113 females, with an average age of 41.7 years (range, 21–69 years; standard deviation, 11.4 years). The clinical diseases for CT scans are presented in Table 1.

Formulation and verification of the entry point

The CT data were imported into the Mimics software (Mimics v21.0; Materialise) and reconstructed to obtain the strict mid-sagittal plane using the “View-Fluoroscopy-Create” command (Fig. 1A and B). Point O1 was marked as the center of the circle, with the diameter defined as the distance between the anterosuperior and posteroinferior corners of the S1 vertebra. Similarly, point O2 was established for the S2 vertebra. Points O1 and O2 were the midpoints between the anterosuperior and posteroinferior corners of the respective vertebra.

Whether the point could serve as the entry point for the TITS screw should be evaluated on the semi-transparent lateral 3-dimensional (3D) view of the pelvis (Fig. 1C). It was considered feasible if the point was located within the safe trapeziform area of the respective vertebra. The margins of the trapeziform area in the S1 segment were the sacral alar slope superiorly, the sacral cortex anteriorly, the sacral canal posteriorly, and the transition from S1 to S2 and the S1 root tunnel inferiorly.10) Accordingly, the margins of the trapeziform area in the S2 segment were the transition from S1 to S2 and the S1 root tunnel superiorly, the sacral cortex anteriorly, the sacral canal posteriorly, the transition from S2 to S3 and the S2 root tunnel inferiorly.

Screw placement and evaluation

For pelvises with a normal sacrum, S1 and S2 TITS screws were virtually placed (Fig. 1). Two guidewires, simulated by 2 cylinders 2.5 mm in diameter, were inserted and anchored on the outer cortex of the ilium, with their tips aligned at points O1 and O2 (Fig. 1C). The guidewires were manipulated in the orientation, then advanced within the bone while ensuring no violation of the sacral foramen on the outlet view and maintaining parallel to the anterior alar cortex on the inlet view (Fig. 1D). Subsequently, two 6.5-mm cannulated screws in Standard Tessellation Language format were imported into Mimics software and placed over the wires (Fig. 1E). The pelvis with a dysmorphic sacrum was identified based on some specific characteristics, such as an S1 segment not recessed within the pelvis, mamillary bodies, misshapen noncircular sacral foramen, a residual disc between the upper 2 sacral segments, an acute alar slope on radiographs and undulating tongue-in-groove SI articulations on CT scan.1218) There was insufficient space to accommodate the TITS screw in the S1 segment.81920) Thus, the TITS screw was virtually placed only in the S2 segment (Fig. 2).

To evaluate screw accuracy, we calculated the screw mask in Mimics software and performed the reconstruction to obtain the coronal and axial planes of the sacrum (Fig. 1F and 2F) then graded the positioning of the TITS screw as illustrated in Fig. 3, referring to the grading criteria for oblique SI screws.9)

Clinical Surgical Procedure

Patients

Between June 2022 and April 2024, a total of 90 patients with posterior pelvic ring injuries, including both high-energy injuries and fragility fractures, were stabilized with TITS screws and enrolled in this study. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients aged 18 years or older, (2) patients with a nondisplaced or minimally displaced fracture/dislocation, (3) patients with a displaced fracture or dislocation that can be reduced with a closed maneuver, (4) the injured posterior pelvic ring was stabilized with the TITS screw. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with an open or pathological pelvic injury, (2) patients with a history of pelvic injury or surgery, (3) patients associated with serious neurovascular injuries.

Among all 90 patients, 49 patients were treated between June 2022 and July 2023 and were classified as group A. Their entry points were determined with alternating inlet and outlet views.22) The remaining 41 patients underwent stabilization with our proposed method after July 2023 and were classified as group B. The demographic data of 2 groups is shown inTable 2. All surgeries were performed by the same senior surgeon (HC).

Screw placement and evaluation

Following the preparation and draping, the acceptable reduction was initially completed in a closed manner. We adjusted the position and projection angle of the C-arm until the accurate lateral view of the pelvis was caught. The midpoint between the anterosuperior and posteroinferior corners was marked as point O using a ruler placed on the screen (Fig. 4B). While maintaining the position and angle of the C-arm relative to the pelvis, we repeatedly moved the tip of a forceps on the skin surface to locate the incision. A small incision was made when the fluoroscopic image of the tip overlapped with point O. Subsequently, a guidewire was inserted, anchoring its tip at point O on the screen. Finally, the screw was placed following our aforementioned procedure. For the pelvis with a dysmorphic sacrum, the placement procedure of the S2 TITS screw is shown in Fig. 5.

Intraoperatively, the surgical duration, the number of guidewire attempts, and the number of fluoroscopic shots associated with each TITS screw were documented. Postoperatively, physical examinations were conducted to detect any potential neurovascular injuries, and imaging examinations were performed to evaluate screw placements safety and grade screw positioning. The follow-up was scheduled at 6 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and 1 year after surgery to evaluate fracture healing and functional outcomes. Functional outcomes were categorized using the Majeed score23) as excellent (≥ 85 points), good (70–84 points), fair (55–69 points), or poor (< 55 points).

Statistical Analysis

Quantitative variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, and qualitative variables were presented as frequency. Statistical analysis was conducted with IBM SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp.). The 2-tailed Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test was used for continuous variables, while the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test was used for categorical variables. A p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Virtual Surgical Procedure

In all patients, point O was consistently located within the trapeziform area of the respective vertebra on the semi-transparent pelvic lateral 3D view with our proposed method, regardless of S1 or S2. A total of 148 S1 TITS screws were virtually placed, while the remaining 91 pelvises were identified as dysmorphic, lacking a sufficient osseous corridor for the S1 TITS screw. Remarkably, all S2 TITS screws were virtually placed. Following the virtual screw placement, the coronal and axial CT planes of the sacrum were reconstructed. The screw accuracy evaluation revealed that S1 screws (grade 1: 134, 90.5%; grade 2: 14, 9.5%) and S2 screws (grade 1: 127, 85.8%; grade 2: 21, 14.2%) were classified as grade 1 or 2 in the normal pelvises. Notably, all S2 screws in the dysmorphic pelvises were classified as grade 1. Consequently, this method has been proven to be feasible for determining the entry point for the TITS screw.

Clinical Surgical Procedure

According to the criteria for sacral dysmorphism, 14 pelvises were classified as having sacral dysmorphism and stabilized with S2 screws. A total of 42 TITS screws were placed in 41 patients, including 27 S1 screws and 15 S2 screws. One patient with a normal pelvis was stabilized with both S1 and S2 screws. The average surgical duration and number of fluoroscopic shots for each screw were 21.0 ± 3.5 minutes and 16.2 ± 7.7 shots, respectively. The majority of patients required only 1 guidewire attempt, with an average of 1.1 ± 0.3 attempts per patient. No neurovascular injuries were found after surgery in any patients. Screw positioning was evaluated in all planes of postoperative CT scans, confirming that all screws were within the osseous corridor. Thirty-nine screws (92.9%) were classified as grade 1, while 3 screws (7.1%) were classified as grade 2 (Fig. 4). The average follow-up time was 12.4 ± 1.5 months. Fracture healing was confirmed 14.8 ± 1.6 weeks after surgery. At the final follow-up, the average Majeed score was 86.1 ± 4.3, with excellent results in 36 patients and good results in 5 patients. The rate of excellent or good functional outcomes was 100%. To evaluate the efficacy of our proposed method, the comparison of perioperative data between the 2 groups was conducted and shown in Table 3. Our method demonstrated superior advantages in surgical duration, fluoroscopic shots, guidewire attempts, fracture healing time and Majeed score. However, no significant difference was detected in screw positioning, screw accuracy and follow-up time.

DISCUSSION

The TITS screw fixation has been widely adopted for addressing unstable posterior pelvic ring injuries. Compared to the regular SI screw, the TITS screw can engage more cortices (up to 6 cortices across the bilateral ilium and sacrum). Thus, it can enhance fixation stability, enabling better resistance to vertical deforming forces and decreasing the risk of reduction loss or implant loosening.4) However, percutaneous screw placement is a highly demanding and challenging technique under fluoroscopic guidance. Compared to robot-assisted screw placement, our proposed method demonstrated equivalent superiority in the guidewire attempts and accuracy of screw position.24) Furthermore, the average duration for each screw decreased, as the robotic supporting facilities necessitated extra preparation time.25) Nevertheless, our method did not show an advantage in fluoroscopic shots.26) Although robotics or navigation systems have been introduced for the placement of screws in the recent decade,2427) the majority of surgeons still perform screw fixation with fluoroscopic guidance. Compared to the method we adopted previously, this method proposed in the study revealed superior efficacy in surgical duration, fluoroscopic shots and guidewire attempts. Upon evaluating the screw positioning accuracy, 3 screws placed in the S1 segment were classified as grade 2. Even associated with adequate entry points, the deviation in guidewire orientation resulted in cortical engagement in 3 screws (Fig. 4). No screws were classified as grade 3 or 4. It was reported that a deviation of only 4° may violate the anterior cortex of the sacrum or S1 foramina.28) As a result, the margin for error is extremely narrow at each step of screw placement. Therefore, determining an adequate entry point as the initial step is crucial to the whole procedure.

To determine the adequate entry point for the SI screw on the fluoroscopic view, Durusoy et al.29) utilized the pelvic lateral view with the mapping technique. Furthermore, the lateral view was employed to identify the entry point for the SI screw in a dysmorphic sacrum.9) The entry point for the TITS screw, a type of SI screw, was also determined on the pelvic lateral view by Routt et al.12) Giannoudis et al.30) similarly recommended locating the entry point for the TITS screw on the pelvic lateral view primarily, as it not only diminished radiation exposure duration but also improved screw positioning. We also prefer the lateral view for determining the entry point, as it demonstrates the alar slope and the interval between the S1 and S2 vertebrae more accurately.

Most studies tended to to constrain a trapeziform area for the safe entry zone on the lateral view,1031) rather than a specific point. It predominantly depends on individual experience to determine a point within the safe zone, whereas directly formulating a specific point offers greater practicality and simplicity. Additionally, the identification of the trapeziform requires the simultaneous visualization of several distinct anatomic landmarks in a single fluoroscopic shot. It does not frequently meet the requirements due to bone overlap, obesity, poor bone quality, and bowel gas during surgery.13) Previous studies have indicated that the sacral cortical outline was a valuable reference for determining the entry point,32) since it was easily visible even on poor quality fluoroscopy. The corner of the vertebra, as the junction of the sacral cortices, can also be easily identified on the lateral view. Therefore, determining a specific point instead of a safe zone, referred to the reliable fluoroscopic landmarks, facilitates the development of the standardized algorithm.

To determine the S1 entry point, Carlson et al.33) selected a point between the sacral ala and foramen, aligning with the midpoint of the S1 body in the anteroposterior direction. They further concluded that the TITS screw was the least safe SI screw for each sacrum type. Javidmehr et al.32) located the entry point at the posteroinferior quadrant of the anterosuperior quadrant of the S1 body in a cadaveric study. To place an S2 TITS screw, it is more difficult because of a smaller less cross-sectional area than S1 and more obscure fluoroscopy.34) Carlson et al.33) identified the entry point as just inferior and posterior to the mid S2 vertebra on the lateral ilium. Osterhoff et al.35) chosen the entry point of S2 in the center plane between cranial and caudal endplate. Moed and Geer36) targeted the center of the S2 body on the lateral view as screw insertion site. Previous studies have shown that no single method for determining the entry point is universally applicable for S1 and S2 screws. Our proposed method is a universal method. It has been proven effective for S1 and S2 screws in a normal sacrum, as well as for the S2 screw in a dysmorphic sacrum. Preoperatively, surgeons are only required to identify the sacral morphology (a normal or a dysmorphic sacrum) associated with the injured pelvis.

The dysmorphic sacrum often lacks a sufficient osseous corridor for the S1 TITS screw, while S2 more consistently offered a sufficient zone.31) As we found in the virtual placement procedure, not all pelvises can accommodate an S1 TITS screw since the presence of dysmorphism. Furthermore, pelvises with the grade 2 screws in the S1 segment were associated with sufficient space (intermediate sacrum), not a large space (capacious sacrum) in the intraosseous corridor.33) In contrast, all pelvises can accommodate the S2 screw regardless of dysmorphism, particularly the dysmorphic pelvises provide a large space for the S2 screw. In clinical practice, sacral dysmorphism is a spectrum of variations,37) and the representative characteristics are obscure in some dysmorphic sacra.20) Therefore, S2 screw placement could be recommended in case of uncertainty about the sufficient passage for S1 screw during surgery.

Our study has several limitations. The first limitation is the small number of participants enrolled in the clinical practice and absence of a randomized assignment in the controlled trial. Well-designed multicenter and prospective studies should be conducted in the future. The second limitation is that only 1 screw was placed for each sacral segment. The study did not consider the influence of an additional screw placed in the same segment.

In conclusion, our proposed method of specifying the entry point for the TITS screw as the midpoint between the anterosuperior and posteroinferior corners of the respective vertebra has been validated. It is universally applicable to S1 and S2 screws in a normal pelvis, as well as to the S2 screw in a dysmorphic pelvis.
