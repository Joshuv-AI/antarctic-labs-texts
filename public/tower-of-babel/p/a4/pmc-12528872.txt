Assessment of the correlations among 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds, advanced glycation end products, and raw material chemical components of fried shiitake mushroom (Lentinula edodes) crisps
Authors: Yong Li, Jingyu Liu, Junlong Meng, Cuiping Feng, Jie Chen, Maomao Zeng, Hua Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigated the formation of harmful Maillard reaction products (MRPs), particularly advanced glycation end products (AGEs) and 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds, in fried shiitake mushroom crisps prepared from ten types of Lentinula edodes, in relation to their inherent chemical components. Analyzed harmful MRPs included 1,2-dicarbonyls (3-deoxyglucosone/3-DG, glyoxal/GO, methylglyoxal/MGO, diacetyl/DA), hydroxymethylfurfural/HMF, and AGEs (carboxymethyllysine/CML, carboxyethyllysine/CEL, methylglyoxal-derived hydroimidazolone1/MG-H1). Results revealed the positive effect of glucose, 3-DG, Asp, Arg, and Lys on the HMF levels. Conversely, ergothioneine negatively correlated with 3-DG, DA, MGO, CML, and CEL; vanillic acid and caffeic acid were inversely related to GO and MG-H1, respectively. Additionally, Ser positively correlated with CML, while CEL had significant negative correlations with Asp, Gly, and total free amino acid. Overall, there was a complex relationship between the chemical components and harmful MRPs in fried shiitake crisps, with ergothioneine, caffeic acid, and vanillic acid showing potential inhibitors of AGEs and 1,2-dicarbonyls generation.Keywords: Maillard reaction products; Deep-fried shiitake mushroom; Advanced glycation end products (AGEs); 1,2-Dicarbonyl compounds; Correlation analysis

Introduction

Edible mushrooms are essential to the human diet because of their unique flavor, rich nutritional content, and medicinal benefits (Contato & Conte-Junior, 2025). Shiitake mushrooms (Lentinula edodes), the world's second most commercially grown mushroom species, account for roughly 25 % of global production (Marçal et al., 2021). In China, L. edodes accounts for the largest proportion of total edible mushroom production, representing approximately 30 % (China Edible Fungi Association, 2023). L. edodes is high in protein, polysaccharides, essential vitamins, and polyphenolic bioactive compounds, showing significant health effects like antioxidant and antimicrobial actions (Nam et al., 2021). Importantly, heat treatment—such as frying, stir-frying, or roasting—is needed before consumption, whether during industrial processing or household cooking (Morales et al., 2024). Mushroom crisps are popular for their unique flavor, crunchy texture, and nutritional benefits. These products are usually processed using high-temperature methods like frying (Morales et al., 2024). This process causes non-enzymatic browning through the Maillard reaction, giving the product attractive color, flavor, and aroma (Zhou et al., 2022); however, it can also produce harmful compounds related to the Maillard reaction (Geng et al., 2024).

During food thermal processing, the Maillard reaction initiates when a reducing sugar attaches to amino groups, forming the early-stage Amadori compound, using furosine as the marker (Vistoli et al., 2013). As the reaction progresses, this compound degrades into 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds such as 3-deoxyglucosone (3-DG), methylglyoxal (MGO), and glyoxal (GO) (Kou et al., 2024). 3-DG also undergo enolization, and water elimination to produce hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), which also arise from caramelization. These 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds are key reactive intermediates that modify lysine and arginine residues, leading to the formation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) such as Nε-carboxymethyllysine (CML), Nε-carboxyethyllysine (CEL), and methylglyoxal-derived hydroimidazolone (MG-H1) (Gu et al., 2024; Vistoli et al., 2013). These reactions also generate low-molecular-weight amino acid degradation products along with nitrogen-rich macromolecules, termed as melanoidins (Poulsen et al., 2013). Dietary AGEs are associated with age-related diseases, such as diabetes, cataracts, atherosclerosis, inflammation, and allergy (Gu et al., 2024).

While the formation mechanisms of 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds and AGEs are well-understood in model systems, and their formation patterns and mitigation strategies in various foods are extensively documented (Gu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2020), the reaction pathways in complex food systems are affected by numerous factors(Poulsen et al., 2013; Turan-Demirci et al., 2025). Elements such as amino acid profiles, reducing sugar levels, and the presence of antioxidants can profoundly influence Maillard reaction pathways, thereby impacting the formation of harmful Maillard reaction products (MRPs) (Li et al., 2024; Turan-Demirci et al., 2025). Existing research on harmful MRPs has primarily focused on animal-based, dairy, cereal, and other plant-based products (Kou et al., 2024; Lin et al., 2023; Turan-Demirci et al., 2025; Zhang et al., 2020). However, there has been limited investigation into harmful MRPs of edible mushroom (macrofungi) products, which differ structurally and compositionally from plant and animal tissues. Edible mushrooms are rich in various antioxidants, such as phenolic compounds and notably ergothioneine—a sulfur-containing compound also known as 2-thiol-1-histidine betaine (Martinez-Medina et al., 2021; Pratti et al., 2024). Synthesized primarily by certain bacteria and fungi but not by animals (Song et al., 2017), ergothioneine acts as a potent biological antioxidant that can chelate metal ions and scavenge free radicals (Martinez-Medina et al., 2021). Consequently, the accumulation of harmful MRPs—particularly 1,2-dicarbonyls and AGEs—in processed mushroom products and the effects of inherent chemical components (e.g., phenolic compounds and ergothioneine) on the formation of these MRPs in specific processed items are interesting topics for investigation.

Thus, this study explored the relationship between raw L. edodes components and the formation of 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds and AGEs in deep-fried shiitake mushroom crisps. In addition, the furosine and HMF were also monitored. Ten types of L. edodes were selected, and their carbohydrate, amino acids, and bioactive compounds (such as phenolics and ergothioneine) were analyzed. Multivariate statistical analysis was used to identify the inherent chemicals in the raw L. edodes that are the main factors influencing the formation of these harmful MRPs.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and shiitake (L. edodes)

Methanol and acetonitrile of LC-MS-grade, phenylisothiocyanate (PITC), triethylamine, and amino acid standards were obtained from ANPLE (Shanghai, China). Ergothioneine, acetic acid, D-(+)-glucose, norleucine, sodium acetate, trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), phenyl-3-methyl-5-pyrazolone (PMP), o-phenylenediamine, hydroxymethylfurfural (HMF), ammonium acetate, and 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine (DNS), methylglyoxal, and glyoxal came from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). CML, CEL, MG-H1, Nε-(carboxy[2H4]methyl)lysine (CML-d4), and Nε-(carboxy[2H4]ethyl)lysine (CEL-d4) were obtained from Toronto Research Chemicals, Inc. (Toronto, Canada). Alta-Scientific (Tianjin, China) provided 3-deoxyglucosone and furosine. Standard compounds from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA) included vanillic acid, gallic acid, quercetin, naringenin, vanillin, catechin, 3,4-dihydroxybenzoic acid, hydrocinnamic acid, trans-cinnamic acid, epicatechin, p-hydroxycinnamic acid, rutin, quercetin-3-β-D-glucoside, caffeic acid, luteoloside, luteolin, apigenin, 4-hydroxybenzoic acid, and kaempferol-3-O-glucoside. China Oil and Foodstuffs Corporation (Beijng, China) provided the soybean oil.

This study examined 10 types of fresh L. edodes sourced from different regions in China, labeled as L1 (Tieling, Liaoning), L2 (Beijing), L3 (Xingtai, Hebei), L4 (Jinzhong, Shanxi), L5 (Xixia, Henan), L6 (Yichang, Hubei), L7 (Suqian, Jiangsu), L8 (Taizhou, Zhejiang), L9 (Sanming, Fujian), and L10 (Kunming, Yunnan). The fresh fruiting bodies were transported under cold chain conditions to the Food Science and Engineering Laboratory at Shanxi Agricultural University for immediate pre-treatment. Some of the L. edodes were freeze-dried to analyze their chemical components, while the rest of the fresh samples were then used for deep-frying experiments.

Proximate analyses

The moisture, protein, and fat contents of fresh L. edodes were determined using official AOAC methods (AOAC International, 2015).

Determination of total reducing sugars and glucose

The reducing sugars were analyzed using the DNP methods (Li et al., 2024). Samples (0.1 g) were dissolved in 3 mL of 80 % methanol, vigorously shaken, and sonicated for 10 min. Afterward, they were centrifuged at 4500×g for 10 min. Then, 400 μL of the extract was combined with 300 μL of DNP solution. The mixture was boiled in water for 5 min, quickly cooled in water, and the reducing sugars were measured at 540 nm.

The glucose content in L. edodes was measured following the method outlined by Bai et al. (2015). In brief, 100 μL of 0.6 M NaOH solution was added to 200 μL of the extract, followed by 200 μL of PMP solution (0.5 M). After reacting at 70 °C for 30 min, 200 μL of 0.3 M HCl and 300 μL of water were added. Subsequently, 700 μL of chloroform was added to extract 3 times. After membrane filtration (0.22 μm), 1 μL of the solution was analyzed by liquid chromatography. The chromatographic column used was Waters Acquity BEH C18 (100 mm × 2.1 mm, 1.7 μm). Mobile phase A was ammonium acetate (0.1 M, pH 5.5), and mobile phase B was acetonitrile. Isocratic elution was performed at a volume ratio of 80:20 (A: B), with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min at 40 °C. Detection was at 245 nm.

Analyses of amino acids

Samples were defatted using n-hexane, extracted with 0.1 M HCl, and then collected for free amino acids (FAA) analysis. Total lysine and arginine, including free and protein-bound lysine and arginine were determined following the method of Lin et al. (2023). For sample acid hydrolysis, 8 mL of 6 M HCl was used, heated at 110 °C for 24 h in glass tubes under nitrogen. Following sodium hydroxide neutralization, the hydrolysate was adjusted to 25 mL. The PITC derivatization was performed according to the protocol of Y. Li et al. (2021), by adding 100 μL of PITC and incubated at room temperature for 1 h. Analysis was performed using a Waters UPLC (Milford, MA, USA) coupled with a UV detector. Separation was achieved on an XBridge C18 column (2.1 × 150 mm, 3.5 μm) at a temperature of 35 °C. The elution employed a gradient of solvents: A (methanol/acetonitrile/water (2:6:2, v/v)) and B (50 mM sodium acetate, pH 6.5) at a flow rate of 0.4 mL/min. The gradient program was: 5 % A (0 min), 28 % A (6.5 min), 47 % A (11.5 min), 100 % A (11.6 min), maintained until 12.5 min, then reverting to 5 % A from 12.6 to 16 min. A 1 μL sample was injected and analyzed at 254 nm.

Determination of bioactive compounds

Analyses of ergothioneine

The extraction and determination of ergothioneine followed the method described by Pratti et al. (2024), with necessary modifications. Specifically, 0.1 g of the sample was mixed with 3 mL of 90 % methanol and vortexed for 1 min. Ultrasonic extraction was carried out for 10 min. After centrifuging at 12,000 ×g for 5 min, the supernatant was collected for analysis. Chromatographic conditions included a UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm), mobile phase of 0.1 % formic acid acetonitrile solution and 0.1 % aqueous formic acid solution (80:20, v/v), flow rate of 0.4 mL/min, column temperature at 40 °C, detection at 262 nm, and injection volume of 2 μL.

Analyses of phenolic compounds

This procedure was based on the method of Kim et al. (2023) and has been slightly modified. To 50 mg of the samples, 1 mL of 80 % methanol was added and extracted using ultrasonication for 30 min. After centrifugation and filtration, two microliters of each sample were analyzed using a UPLC-Q Exactive mass spectrometer (Thermo, USA). Separations of phenolic compounds were performed on a Waters HSS T3 column (50 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) using a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, with the column oven at 40 °C. The mobile phases were A (0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile solution) and B (0.1 % formic acid). The gradient elution program was set as follows: from 0 to 2 min, 90 % B; at 6 min, 40 % B; from 6 to 9 min, maintained at 40 % B; and from 9.1 to 12 min, returned to 90 % B. Mass spectrometric data were acquired in negative ion mode using an EI source with the following parameters: spray voltage was at −2.8 kV, ion transfer tube temperature was at 320 °C, ion source temperature was at 350 °C, in full scan-ddMS2 mode, with the first MS scan was m/z 100–900.

Total phenolics content (TPC) and total flavonoids content (TFC)

The Folin-Ciocalteu method was employed to measure TPC (Elhusseiny et al., 2021). To prepare the sample, 0.1 g of powder was mixed with 3 mL of 70 % ethanol and sonicated for 10 min. Next, 100 μL of the extract was combined with an equal amount of 10 % Folin-Ciocalteu reagent. Following that, 800 μL of 5 % Na₂CO₃ was added, and the mixture was reacted in the dark at 40 °C for 20 min. The TPC was then assessed at 765 nm, based on a gallic acid standard curve, with results expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents (GAE)/g.

The TFC was measured using a modified method (Kim et al., 2023). A mixture of 120 μL of 5 % NaNO2 and 200 μL of the sample extract was vortexed. After 5 min of incubation, 120 μL of 10 % AlCl₃ was added, followed by 400 μL of 1 M NaOH. The mixture was then kept in the dark for 15 min. Values were obtained at 510 nm. The TPC data are reported as mg rutin equivalents (RE)/g.

Deep-frying of shiitake mushroom crisps

The L. edodes were uniformly sliced into 5 mm-thick pieces. The cooking times and temperatures were determined based on preliminary tests and literature (Morales et al., 2024). 150 g of mushroom slices were immersed in soybean oil preheated to 180 °C (oil-to-sample ratio 1:10, w/w), fried for 5 min, drained for 1 min, and cooled at room temperature after removing the residual oil. Each experimental group was prepared in triplicate. The deep-fried mushroom crisps were freeze-dried to analyze their harmful MRPs.

Analyses of furosine in mushroom crisps

Furosine was determined as described by Spanneberg et al. (2012). Briefly, 60 mg of the sample was defatted twice with n-hexane. After drying, it was mixed with 6 mL hydrochloric acid (6 M) and heated at 110 °C for 24 h. The hydrolysate was then diluted with ultrapure water to 25 mL. 1 mL of this solution was evaporated, and 300 μL of 0.05 M HCl was added. The samples were centrifuged (12,000×g, 10 min, 4 °C) before UPLC-UV analysis. A Welch Xtimate C18 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) was used to separate the injected sample (1 μL) at 30 °C. The mobile phase was (A) acetonitrile and (B) 0.1 % trifluoroacetic acid (TFA), with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. Detection occurred at 280 nm. The gradient elution: 0–2 min, 99 %B; 3 min, 70 %B; 5 min, 90 %B; 6.5–8.5 min, 99 % B.

Measurement of HMF in mushroom crisps

One gram of the sample was suspended in 1 mL of ultrapure water and 7 mL of 0.15 M oxalic acid, shaken, and incubated in a boiling water bath for 25 min. Next, 2 mL of a 9.2 % potassium ferrocyanide solution and an equal volume of 18.3 % zinc acetate were added. The solution was then diluted with methanol to 25 mL. Before analysis, the extract was centrifuged and filtered. HMF was measured referencing the method of Hamzalıoğlu et al. (Hamzalıoğlu & Gökmen, 2017) with appropriate modifications. For analysis, a Waters UPLC-UV system equipped with a Waters HSS T3 column (2.1 mm × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) was used. The oven was set at 40 °C. Solvent A was acetonitrile, and solvent B was 0.1 % formic acid. A gradient elution program was applied (0–0.5 min, 15 % A; 0.5–4 min, 35 % A; 4.1–5 min, 90 % A; 5.1–7 min, 15 % A) at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. An injection volume of 2 μL was used, with detection at 285 nm.

Analyses of 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds in mushroom crisps

This was conducted according to Degen et al. (2012). A Welch Xtimate UHPLC C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) was operated at 35 °C. Phase A was acetonitrile, and Phase B was 0.1 % formic acid. A gradient elution program was utilized with a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min. The program included the following steps: 0–0.1 min, 90 % B; 2.5 min, 65 % B; 4.0 min, 35 % B; 6.0 min, 0 % B; 6.1–8.5 min, 90 % B. The injection volume was 5 μL. Before analysis, 500 μL of the methanol extract was incubated with 100 μL of 0.1 % OPD at 4 °C in the dark for 12 h. After centrifugation (12,000 ×g, 10 min), the supernatant was analyzed with UPLC-UV, recording spectra at 314 nm. The quinoxalines of 3-DG, GO, MGO, and DA were identified by external calibration. Fig. S1 depicts the UPLC-UV chromatograms for both mixed standards and a crisp sample.

Analyses of AGEs in shiitake mushroom crisps

To quantify the total AGEs (protein-bound and free forms) including CML, CEL, and MG-H1, the samples were subjected to acid hydrolysis. AGEs extraction and detection were performed using an optimized protocol adapted from previous methods (Li et al., 2021). The process involves sample defatting, reduction, acid hydrolysis, and purification prior to UPLC-MS/MS detection. Fifty milligrams of freeze-dried shiitake crisp powder was defatted with 5 mL n-hexane three times, followed by centrifugation at 4000 ×g for 5 min. Then, the dried residues were reduced by mixing with 1 mL of borate buffer (pH 9.2, 0.2 M) and an equal amount of sodium borohydride (2 M), kept at 4 °C for 12 h. Acid hydrolysis was performed with 2 mL of HCl at 110 °C for 24 h. Thereafter, the hydrolysate was diluted to 10 mL with ultrapure water. 0.6 mL of the solution was evaporated to dryness under nitrogen at 50 °C. Prior to solid-phase extraction, the dried residue was reconstituted in 2 mL of HCl (0.1 M), and 50 μL of internal standards (CML-d4 and CEL-d4) were added. MCX cartridges were preconditioned with 3 mL each of methanol and 0.1 M HCl at a flow of 0.5 mL/min. Samples were loaded and washed with 3 mL of 0.1 M HCl, followed by 3 mL of methanol. Target AGEs were eluted with 3 mL of a solution containing 5 % ammonium hydroxide in methanol. The eluate was dried under nitrogen at 50 °C, redissolved in 300 μL of acetonitrile, and centrifuged at 12,000×g for 10 min. The supernatant was analyzed using UPLC-MS/MS.

The analysis used Waters' UPLC (Milford, MA, USA) equipped with a triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer. Detection was performed in ESI+ mode and multiple reaction monitoring, with a capillary voltage of 1 kV. The source temperature was maintained at 150 °C, with a desolvation temperature of 550 °C and a cone voltage of 30 V. The MS/MS parameters for AGEs measurement (precursor ions, product ions, dwell times, cone voltage, and collision energy) are shown in Table S1. Eluents consisted of A (0.1 % formic acid) and B (0.1 % formic acid in acetonitrile), flowed at 0.3 mL/min. The gradient elution program was: 0–1 min 99 % A, 3.5–4 min 95 % A, and 5.1–6 min 99 % A. The target AGEs were separated on an BEH C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) after injecting 3 μL, with the column maintained at 40 °C. The chromatograms for both mixed standards and a crisp sample are shown in Fig. S2.

Statistical analysis

The data were presented as the dry weight of the samples. Differences among samples were evaluated by a one-way analysis of variance followed by LSD post-hoc tests at the P < 0.05 level. Pearson's correlation was evaluated by Origin Pro. The heatmap was created using TBtools, and multivariate analysis was conducted with SIMCA.

Results and discussion

Proximate composition and sugar analysis

Table 1 presents the contents of moisture, protein, fat, glucose, and total reducing sugars in the raw materials. The moisture content in fresh L. edodes ranged from 83.95 % to 89.12 %, aligning with the values reported by Wen et al. (2022). The crude protein and fat contents ranged from 26.83 to 35.61 g/100 g and 1.79 to 2.20 g/100 g, respectively, consistent with the protein levels previously documented for L. edodes (Zhou et al., 2022). Among the samples, L3 showed the highest protein content, followed by L6 and L9, while L1 had the lowest value.

As precursors to the Maillard reaction, glucose and total reducing sugars were measured. In fresh L. edodes, glucose was the main reducing sugar (Wen et al., 2022), with its level differing significantly across samples, ranging from 1.37 to 8.11 mg/g. The highest glucose content was found in L8, with L8's value being 5.9 times higher than the lowest observed in L5. The total reducing sugar content varied from 7.57 to 14.27 mg/g, with the highest values in L8 and L9 (Table 1). Conversely, L5, L10, and L4 had notably lower amounts, about 53–59 % of the content in L8.

Amino acid contents in raw materials

The total FAA content in L. edodes ranged from 1221.33 to 1759.21 mg/100 g (Table 2). Zhou et al. (2022) reported similar values, with total FAA levels in L. edodes between 1400 and 2110 mg/100 g, aligning with these findings. Gly was the most abundant FAA, with a content ranging from 245.26 to 635.52 mg/100 g, followed by Arg. Free Arg levels ranged from 199.73 to 390.67 mg/100 g, representing 16.16–23.41 % of total FAA. Free Lys was present at lower levels (68.79–111.49 mg/100 g) than Arg. Among the samples, L8 had the highest Lys content, followed by L10, L6, L7, and L9. L2 had the lowest Lys level, only 60 % of L8. Other major FAAs included Ala, Glu, Thr, and Ser, with contents from 98.51 to 186.95 mg/100 g, 97.07 to 227.36 mg/100 g, 82.17 to 126.8 mg/100 g, and 76.57 to 134.17 mg/100 g, respectively. The contents of Asp, Tyr, Val, and Ile in L. edodes were all below 10 mg/100 g. Total Lys and total Arg (combining free and bound forms) are key precursors of AGEs and were also analyzed. Samples L1 and L2 showed a significantly lower total Lys concentration compared to other samples (P < 0.05). The total Arg content follows this order: L3 > L9 > L5. Various factors, including mushroom ripeness, quality grade, preservation methods, and storage duration after harvest, influence the amino acid content (Nam et al., 2021).

Ergothioneine and phenolic compounds

L. edodes are rich in bioactive compounds, particularly ergothioneine and phenolic substances, which may affect harmful MRPs production (Kim et al., 2008; Martinez-Medina et al., 2021). Table 3 presents the main bioactive compounds in fresh L. edodes, along with the TPC and TFC values. L. edodes contains high levels of ergothioneine with potent antioxidant properties that can effectively scavenge radicals (Kim et al., 2023). The ergothioneine content was found to be highest in samples L6 and L9 (1.70 mg/g), while L1 and L8 contained significantly lower amounts (0.99 and 0.81 mg/g, respectively), which are less than 47 % of that found in L6. This aligns with the findings of Pratti et al. (2024), who reported an ergothioneine level of 1.02 mg/g in fresh L. edodes. Furthermore, these levels fall within the 0.4 to 2.0 mg/g range reported for mushrooms (Rathore et al., 2017). It is worth noting that besides mushroom type, postharvest storage conditions, such as temperature and duration, also influence ergothioneine content (Kim et al., 2023).

Phenolic compounds play a key role in the antioxidant properties of mushrooms. In fresh L. edodes, nineteen phenolic compounds were measured based on a previous report (Kim et al., 2008; Nam et al., 2021). None of the ten L. edodes samples contained gallic acid, catechin, epicatechin, quercetin, or naringenin. The other 14 detailed phenolic compounds include five simple phenolics, four polyphenols, and five flavonoids (Table 3). Among these compounds, vanillic acid showed the highest concentration (766.06–1554.81 ng/g), followed by 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (383.7–1180.69 ng/g). 3,4-Dihydroxybenzoic acid was only found in samples L1, L2, L7, and L10, at levels between 472.75 and 802.46 ng/g. Concentrations below 100 ng/g were observed for vanillin, hydrocinnamic acid, caffeic acid, p-hydroxycinnamic acid, and quercetin-3-β-D-glucoside. Notably, luteoloside, luteolin, and apigenin were more abundant (P < 0.05) in L3 and L10 than in other samples. Phenolic acids are secondary plant metabolites that are widely present in plant foods, and their levels tend to be higher than those of flavonoids and polyphenols (Nam et al., 2021). The phenolic compound content in L. edodes varies greatly, influenced by cultivation conditions, the environment, and the mushroom's metabolic status (Kim et al., 2008; Nam et al., 2021). Elhusseiny et al. (Elhusseiny et al., 2021) detected quercetin and catechin, but did not discover gallic acid, chlorogenic, or caffeic acid in L. edodes. In contrast, Kim et al. (2008) reported gallic acid, protocatechuic acid, and catechin in L. edodes. The TPC was lowest in sample L1 (1.26 mg GAE/g) and highest in L2 (1.99 mg GAE/g), similar to prior studies by Roncero-Ramos et al. (2017), but lower than the 6.37–8.68 mg GAE/g range reported by Zhou et al. (2022). The TFC was significantly higher in L2 and L6 compared to other samples (P < 0.05).

Principal component analysis (PCA) showed that L1 and L3 were positioned on opposite sides of the x-axis (Fig. 1B). L1 exhibited higher concentrations of caffeic acid, vanillin, quercetin-3-β-D-glucoside, and kaempferol-3-O-glucoside, whereas L3 had increased amounts of trans-cinnamic acid, p-hydroxycinnamic acid, luteolin, apigenin, and luteoloside (Fig. 1B, C). In contrast, compounds more abundant in L1 were found at lower levels in L3. This distribution pattern matches the clustering seen in the heatmap analysis (Fig. 1D). The heatmap showed that L2, L6, and L7 grouped, marked by higher TPC and TFC. Additionally, L6 and L7 formed their subcluster with higher levels of hydrocinnamic acid, ergothioneine, rutin, vanillin, and caffeic acid.

1,2-Dicarbonyl compounds, HMF, furosine, and AGEs in shiitake mushroom crisps

1,2-Dicarbonyl compounds are key intermediates in the Maillard reaction, common in thermally processed foods (Degen et al., 2012; Kou et al., 2024). The typical 1,2-dicarbonyls, including 3-DG, GO, MGO, and DA, were analyzed in the present study. Among these, 3-DG was the most abundant, with levels ranging from 1.38 to 6.36 μg/g in deep-fried mushroom crisps. Short-chain 1,2-dicarbonyls, such as MGO, GO, and DA, were present in all samples. Specifically, GO ranged from 0.14 to 0.60 μg/g, MGO from 0.51 to 1.54 μg/g, and DA from 0.32 to 0.65 μg/g. In fact, 3-DG is the predominant 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds in most foods studied (Kou et al., 2024), with mushroom crisps exhibiting a similar pattern. Studies have found that sugar oxidation contributes more to 3-DG formation than the breakdown of Amadori products does (Hellwig et al., 2010). In addition, 3-DG also undergoes further oxidative cleavage, generating more reactive short-chain 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds, such as GO, MGO, and DA (Kou et al., 2024). Due to their high reactivity, these compounds quickly react with Lys and Arg side chains. Previous research has demonstrated that GO and MGO exhibit the highest reactivity, displaying arginine-binding activity approximately 200 times greater than that of 3-DG (Thornalley et al., 1999). Thus, the observed lower concentrations of MGO and GO compared to 3-DG can be attributed to both their rapid reactivity with amino acids and delayed generation kinetics during saccharide degradation (Lin et al., 2023). DF8 had the highest levels of 3-DG, MGO, GO, and DA, likely due to its higher reducing sugar and glucose content in raw material (Table 1), since reducing sugar degradation is a main precursor to the formation of 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds (Yan et al., 2023). Although research on 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds in edible mushroom products remains limited, substantial studies have been conducted in various other foods. Comparable levels observed including bread (GO: 0–1 μg/g; MGO: 0–3.5 μg/g) (Jost et al., 2021) and tea (3-DG: 1.18–35.22 μg/g; GO: 0.03–5.69 μg/g; MGO: 0.09–5.69 μg/g) (Zhu et al., 2024).

HMF is another important Maillard reaction intermediate. The highest HMF contents were also observed in sample DF8 (15.03 μg/g), followed by DF9 and DF6. Conversely, DF1 exhibited the lowest HMF level at 6.00 μg/g. During thermal processing, HMF is mainly generated via the enolization of Amadori products and through the dehydration of sugars (Hamzalıoğlu & Gökmen, 2017). The content of HMF was affected by the type and level of sugars, as well as the amount and structural characteristics of amino acids (Zhang et al., 2019). The notably high reducing sugar content in shiitake mushroom raw material may account for the elevated levels of HMF observed in the crisp products (Table 1). Furosine is formed through the acid hydrolysis of Amadori compounds (Spanneberg et al., 2012). As shown in Table 4, furosine levels in the deep-fried shiitake mushroom crisps ranged from 81.22 ± 6.25 μg/g to 133.82 ± 6.22 μg/g. DF3 had the highest furosine content, likely due to its higher total Lys content (Table 2). The amounts of furosine gradually decreased in DF8, DF5, and DF9. Meanwhile, furosine contents in DF4, DF7, and DF10 showed no significant differences and were the lowest recorded.

1,2-Dicarbonyl compounds participate in Maillard reaction pathways by reacting with amino acids to produce aroma compounds through Strecker degradation (Kou et al., 2024). Simultaneously, they promote the formation of AGEs (Gu et al., 2024). The amounts of glycation compounds ranged from 35.80 ± 4.04 μg/g to 75.49 ± 6.32 μg/g for CML, from 2.40 ± 0.13 to 7.79 ± 1.17 μg/g for CEL, and from 1.46 ± 0.08 to 1.97 ± 0.09 μg/g for MG-H1. Among the AGEs analyzed, CML was the most abundant in fried shiitake mushroom crisps, with the highest levels found in DF5. One possible explanation is that CML formation can occur through oxidation of Amadori products or GO modification of Lys residues, whereas CEL is exclusively formed by the reaction of MGO with Lys (Vistoli et al., 2013). For CEL, the maximum was detected in DF4, with decreasing levels in DF1, DF3, and DF5. The fried samples with the highest and lowest MG-H1 contents were DF3 and DF1, respectively. In the Maillard reaction, MG-H1 is an arginine-derived AGE generated when Arg residues react with MGO (Lin et al., 2023). Thus, factors associated with the chemical profile that influence MGO formation in the Maillard reaction pathway could contribute to differences in MG-H1 content in crisp products. This relationship will be detailed in the subsequent correlation analysis.

In contrast, DF4, DF7, DF9, and DF10 cluster on the left side of the plot, opposite to the position of 3-DG, MGO, GO, DA, HMF, CML, and MG-H1 suggesting these fried mushroom crisps have lower amounts of these MRPs. The heatmap showed that DF3 and DF8 have high MRPs overall (Fig. 2B). Additionally, DF1 and DF4 formed a distinct subcluster characterized by higher CEL and lower MG-H1 levels.

Exploring the association between L. edodes composition and harmful MRPs

Correlation analysis and partial least squares (PLS) analysis were used to examine the relationship between material components and MRPs. Fig. 3 shows that the contents of 3-DG, GO, and MGO exhibited a weak positive correlation with reducing sugars and glucose in L. edodes. In fact, the pathways for 1,2-dicarbonyl formation include glucose autoxidation (Yan et al., 2023). We also found that HMF, an intermediate formed during the thermal degradation of hexoses, correlated positively with glucose (P < 0.05) and reducing sugar (slight: P > 0.05). This indicated that sugars, as a potential precursor, likely promote HMF formation in deep-fried mushroom crisps. Besides direct acid-catalyzed hexose dehydration, HMF can also be generated from 3-DG dehydration, explaining the significant positive correlation between HMF and 3-DG (P < 0.05; Fig. 3). Similarly, 3-DG was positively correlated with the analyzed short-chain 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds. Additionally, 3-DG, GO, MGO, and DA showed inverse correlations with ergothioneine. Vanillic acid was significantly negatively correlated with GO (P < 0.05) and had a slight negative correlation with other 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds (P > 0.05). These close negative correlations suggest that ergothioneine and vanillic acid could act as mitigating factors that reduce the formation of 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds in fried mushroom crisps. Both vanillic acid and ergothionine can react with electrophilic 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds to capture these compounds (Aldini et al., 2007; Wang et al., 2019). In a phenolic acid-GO system, Wang et al. (2019) found that vanillic acid trapped GO with an efficiency of up to 47.74 %. Additionally, free radicals generated in the Maillard reaction accelerate the degradation of sugars and amino acids, thereby promoting the formation of reactive 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds (Poulsen et al., 2013). Phenolic compounds and ergothioneine in foods function as free radical scavengers(Gulcin, 2020; Martinez-Medina et al., 2021). They can efficiently neutralize reactive oxygen species such as superoxide anions and hydroxyl radicals generated in the Maillard reaction (Gulcin, 2020).

Pearson's correlations between inherent chemicals of raw Lentinula edodes and three stages of Maillard reaction products in deep-fried mushroom crisps. *P < 0.05.

For AGEs analyzed, significant negative correlations were found between CML, CEL, and ergothioneine (P < 0.05; Fig. 3). Similarly, another study showed that ergothioneine significantly reduced levels of AGEs and reactive oxygen species (Song et al., 2017). GO and MGO are primary precursors in the formation of CML and CEL (Vistoli et al., 2013). As previously indicated, ergothioneine can act as a 1,2-dicarbonyl trapping agent, thereby potentially limiting the formation of AGEs. The oxidative degradation of Amadori products into CML is metal ion-catalyzed (Poulsen et al., 2013). Through its metal-chelating ability (Cheah & Halliwell, 2012; Martinez-Medina et al., 2021), ergothioneine may reduce the catalytic efficacy of these ions, thus indirectly suppressing CML formation. Consequently, the low level of ergothioneine might explain the elevated CML and CEL amounts observed in DF8 (Fig. 1D and Fig. 3B). However, further research is needed to understand its inhibitory mechanism on key MRPs in thermally processed foods. A negative correlation was found between MG-H1 and caffeic acid (P < 0.05; Fig. 3). The formation of MG-H1 occurs when MGO reacts with Arg residues in the Maillard reaction (Vistoli et al., 2013). Factors affecting radicals and MGO could influence MG-H1 formation and inhibition (Poulsen et al., 2013). The ability of caffeic acid to inhibit AGEs formation might share similar mechanisms. As an antioxidant, caffeic acid can effectively scavenge free radicals and exhibit metal chelating activity (Gülçin, 2006). Consequently, it inhibits the oxidation of fatty acids, amino acids, and carbohydrates (Geng et al., 2024). Furthermore, as a derivative of cinnamic acid, caffeic acid can capture MGO to form adducts via nucleophilic addition (Lund & Ray, 2017), thereby inhibiting the formation of MRPs. Notably, some phenolic compounds may exhibit pro-oxidative activity under specific conditions (Turan-Demirci et al., 2025). In the present study, positive correlations were observed between trans-cinnamic acid and certain AGEs (specifically, CML and MG-H1, P < 0.05) in deep-fried samples.

Amino acids, which are key precursors of the Maillard reaction, greatly influence the formation of harmful MRPs (Poulsen et al., 2013). HMF correlated positively with Asp, Arg, and Lys (P < 0.05). In amino-sugar model systems, Kavousi et al. (2015) reported that Asp accelerated HMF formation, whereas Lys and Arg suppressed it. These results partly aligned yet partly conflicted with observations in fried shiitake mushroom crisps, which might be due to the complexity of the real-food matrix (Poulsen et al., 2013). Although Lys and Arg are AGE precursors, they showed no significant correlation with AGEs in the mushroom crisps (Fig. 3). The reactivity of amino acids plays a critical role in the Maillard reaction (Gu et al., 2024). Lys and Arg can react with 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds to form AGEs, while certain other amino acids may compete with these precursor amino acids, thereby influencing AGEs generation (Geng et al., 2024). Additionally, precursor amino acids can not only form AGEs but also participate in competitive pathways such as flavor compound formation and browning reactions (Poulsen et al., 2013). Compounds in mushrooms that participated in the Maillard reactions might also greatly influence the reaction network. These factors may collectively alter the correlation between precursors and resulting products. CML levels had a significant positive correlation with Ser, aligning with the findings of F. Li et al. (2024), who associated HMF and CML formation with amino acids in soy sauce. Regarding CEL, positive correlations were observed with Tyr and Val, while significant negative correlations were found with Asp, Gly, and total FAA (P < 0.05). These results suggest that these amino acids might serve as mitigating factors for CEL formation in fried mushroom crisps. Amino acids that inhibit AGEs formation may possess higher reactivity, enabling them to react with 1,2-dicarbonyls more rapidly than Lys or Arg (Geng et al., 2024). Glu, Gly, and Pro potentially inhibit MRPs formation, possibly by competitively reacting with intermediates (Ghassem Zadeh & Yaylayan, 2021; Hu et al., 2022). Furosine, commonly used as a marker for early-stage MRPs, exhibited positive correlations with Arg, but negative with Phe (P < 0.05). CML can be formed when GO reacts with Lys residue; in addition, the oxidation of Amadori products can also produce CML (Vistoli et al., 2013). Therefore, the weak correlation between CML and furosine, along with the significantly positive correlation between CML and GO in fried mushroom crisps, might indicate that CML formation mainly involves GO and Lys residues (Fig. 3) (Lin et al., 2023). However, further research is necessary to clarify the mechanisms of AGEs formation and how they relate to other MRPs in fried shiitake mushroom products.

PLS analysis was further employed to elucidate relationships between raw material composition and these harmful MRPs. The permutation plot strongly validated the model (Fig. 4C). The score plot (Fig. 4A) showed that samples L3 and L8 were distinctly separated from other samples along the x-axis. At the same time, L1, L4, and L10 clustered on the negative x-axis, with the remaining samples near the origin. In the loading plot (Fig. 4B), vanillic acid, caffeic acid, ergothioneine, Phe, and Ala—located distally in the third quadrant—had negative correlations with MRPs on the positive-x-axis. Conversely, Lys, Arg, and reducing sugars near MRPs in quadrant II showed positive correlations. Variable importance for projection (VIP) analysis identified key components with greater impact on the MRPs variation (VIP > 1), including ergothioneine, vanillic acid, trans-cinnamic acid, luteolin, glucose, reducing sugars, protein, and specific amino acids (Lys, Ile, Asp, Arg, His, Ser, Glu, Gly, total Arg, and total FAA) (Fig. 4D). PLS results suggest that shiitake samples (such as L3, L8, and L9) with a high amount of glucose, total reducing sugars, Arg, Lys, Ser, total Lys, total Arg, luteolin, or trans-cinnamic acid generated higher levels of furosine, HMF, GO, and MG-H1. In contrast, samples L1, L4, and L10 exhibited lower levels of these MRPs, indicating that ergothioneine, specific phenolics, and several amino acids may inhibit their formation during thermal processing.

Conclusion

In summary, this study provided a comprehensive view of the relationship among the chemical profile of raw materials, 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds, and AGEs in deep-fried shiitake mushroom crisps. The results show that the formation of these harmful MRPs is affected by multiple compositional factors of the raw mushrooms, particularly reducing sugars, amino acids, and antioxidants. Ergothioneine was negatively correlated with AGEs (CML, CEL) and 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds (3-DG, DA, MGO). Similarly, caffeic acid and vanillic acid were inversely associated with MG-H1 and GO, respectively, suggesting their potential roles as natural inhibitors in mitigating AGEs and 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds formation during thermal processing. The formation of HMF was predominantly driven by high reducing sugar content, specific amino acids (Asp, Arg, Lys), and the key intermediate 3-DG. These findings suggest that selecting raw materials with lower reducing sugar content or enhancing them with specific antioxidants could be effective strategies to control AGEs and 1,2-dicarbonyl compounds in fried mushroom products. Nevertheless, further research is needed to investigate harmful MRPs over a broader range of materials and different household cooking or industrial processing methods.
