Evolving Trends and Practice in Plastic Surgery in a Resource-constrained Setting: A 27-year Review From Nepal
Authors: Anup Thapa, Prajjwol Luitel, Sujan Paudel, Sadmarg Thakur, Aron Shrestha, Samit Sharma, Sangam Rayamajhi, Jayan Man Shrestha, Ishwar Lohani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Plastic surgery in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) has expanded during the past decades, but data reflecting trends and practices in plastic surgery in LMICs are scarce. This study reviewed trends and practice patterns in an LMIC to identify evolving trends and practices in plastic surgery from the perspective of an LMIC.Methods:A retrospective study was conducted reviewing data from patients admitted to the Department of Plastic Surgery and Burns at a major tertiary care referral center in Nepal from 1998 to 2024. Data on sociodemographics, clinical variables, and operative procedures were collected. For comparison, cases were divided into an early cohort (1998–2005) and a recent cohort (2021–2024). Average annual procedures were compared using unpaired Student t tests, with significance set at a P value of less than 0.05.Results:A total of 6482 procedures were performed, with an annual average of 240 (range, 128–438). The majority were men (61.2%) and had a mean age of 29.5 ± 18.8 years. Traumatic injuries (29.6%) and burns (17.4%) were the common diagnosis. Reconstructive procedures accounted for half (50.1%) of all procedures. Compared with the early cohort, reconstructive procedures increased by 306.2% (P < 0.0001), and breast procedures by 7600% (P = 0.03) in the recent cohort. The number of aesthetic, hand, and congenital procedures increased, although not to the point of statistical significance, whereas burn procedures declined.Conclusions:In the past three decades, plastic surgery in Nepal has evolved with a rise in reconstructive and breast procedures, and a fall in burn procedures, reflecting shifting patient priorities, evolving specialty trends, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

Takeaways

Question: How have procedural trends in plastic surgery evolved in Nepal from 1998 to 2024?

Findings: In the past 3 decades plastic surgery practice in Nepal expanded from primarily burn care to a more diverse specialty. Significant increases were observed in reconstructive surgery and breast surgery, along with an increase in aesthetic, hand, and congenital surgery.

Meaning: Plastic surgery in Nepal now addresses a broad spectrum of functional, aesthetic, and psychosocial demands of the patients. These evolving trends emphasize the importance of investment in specialist training, and surgical infrastructure for plastic surgery in Nepal.

INTRODUCTION

Approximately two billion individuals globally lack access to essential surgical care, with the greatest impact observed in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).1 Surgical conditions account for 11% of the global burden of disease.2 Among the surgical specialties, plastic surgery is a rapidly evolving specialty, including a diverse range of procedures. In LMICs such as Nepal, expertise in specialized surgical services has historically been limited. Yet, the demand for plastic surgery continues to rise with increasing awareness, evolving patient needs, and expanding infrastructure.3 Nepal, in addition, is an earthquake-prone region, and the role of plastic surgery in such disaster-prone areas has been recognized in previous studies.4

Despite the increasing traction over plastic surgery, data on evolving trends and practices are limited, particularly in LMICs.5 Existing literature is derived from high-income settings, which may not reflect the unique challenges and priorities in LMICs.6 The absence of local data in LMICs represents a significant gap in global plastic surgery. Gaining a deep understanding of these trends is crucial, as it enables practitioners to (1) recognize shifts in patient and healthcare demand, (2) refine training programs, and (3) create policies to expand their role to better equip future plastic surgeons.7

Plastic surgery services in Nepal began at Tribhuvan University Teaching Hospital (TUTH) and Bir Hospital in the late 1990s. By 2025, approximately 40 reconstructive plastic surgeons are practicing across the country, with the capital, Kathmandu, serving as the central hub. This study aimed to identify the trends and practices of plastic surgery at TUTH, a major tertiary care referral center in Nepal.

METHODS

This retrospective study was conducted following the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki at the Department of Plastic Surgery and Burns, TUTH, Kathmandu, Nepal. Plastic surgery services began at TUTH in 1998 with a single consultant. By 2025, the department had grown to 6 full-time consultants supported by a rotating pool of residents. As one of Nepal’s earliest formal institutions to offer plastic surgery, TUTH has evolved into a leading tertiary care referral center, serving patients from across the country. This study was approved by the institutional review board of the Institute of Medicine (approval no.: (6-11)E2 572/081/082) and has been reported in line with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology criteria.8

Data were collected from medical records and annual departmental audits from January 1998 to December 2024. Records of all patients who underwent plastic surgery procedures after admission to the study site were included. Patients admitted for conditions other than plastic surgery procedures, undergoing only conservative management, and with readmission for the same diagnosis were excluded. Data with incomplete records of diagnosis and procedures were excluded.

Patient anonymity was maintained, and the following variables were recorded: year of admission; sociodemographic variables including age, sex, and address; and clinical variables including diagnosis and procedure performed. The year was divided into 5 groups: 1998–2005, 2006–2010, 2011–2015, 2016–2020, and 2021–2024. These intervals were chosen to reflect key phases in institutional development and national milestones, including the establishment of formal plastic surgery training, infrastructure growth, and the 2015 earthquake, which had a notable impact on surgical volume and documentation.

Age was categorized into the following groups: younger than 1 year, 1 year to younger than 5 years, 5 years to younger than 18 years, 18 years to younger than 25 years, 25 years to younger than 40 years, 40 years to younger than 60 years, and 60 years or older. The addresses were categorized into 7 provinces in Nepal and abroad. The diagnostic groups were classified based on etiology, case volume, and clinical presentation into the following categories: aesthetic conditions, breast disorders, burn injuries, congenital anomalies, infectious conditions, neoplastic disorders (including benign and malignant tumors of skin, soft tissue, and peripheral nerves), pressure injuries, vascular malformations, and miscellaneous conditions (tattoo removal, split ear repair, and removal of wart/corns).

Surgical procedures were divided into modules (9 modules) and submodules based on anatomical focus, etiology, frequency of procedures, and classification in previous studies.6 (See table, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays the modules and submodules, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E452.)

The primary objective was to assess the change in plastic surgery procedures by comparing the mean annual number of procedures between the early cohort (1998–2005) and the current cohort (2021–2024). The secondary objective was to review demographics, diagnostic categories, and procedural distributions over time. To compare procedural trends over time, we defined 2 cohorts: the early cohort (1998–2005) and the recent cohort (2021–2024) reflecting distinct phases in institutional growth. To account for unequal durations, we analyzed annual averages rather than cumulative totals. Additionally, year-wise trends were plotted to provide more granular insight.

The data were entered into a predesigned proforma and compiled into a Microsoft Excel sheet (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond). Statistical analysis was done using IBM SPSS (v.26.0). Results were expressed as mean ± SD with a confidence interval of 95%. For the comparative analysis, annual average procedures were compared in the early cohort and current cohort. Linear regression analysis was used to analyze the trend in the number of procedures over time. The percentage change in the average number was calculated, and unpaired Student t tests were performed to identify significant changes (P < 0.05) in frequency.

RESULTS

Trend on Total Plastic Surgery Procedures

Between 1998 and 2024, 6482 plastic surgery procedures were performed, with an annual average of 240 procedures (Fig. 1). The highest numbers of procedures were performed in 2021 and 2024. The number of procedures steadily increased at an average rate of 11.3 cases per year (R² = 0.551, P < 0.001) (Fig. 1).

Age and Gender Trends

The mean age was 29.5 ± 18.8 years, and the male-to-female ratio was 1.58 to 1. The majority of patients (54.2%) were from Bagmati Province. The mean age showed a weak but statistically significant upward trend over the years, increasing by 0.41 years annually (R² = 0.022, P < 0.001).

Diagnostic Groups and Procedures

Among the diagnostic groups, traumatic injuries (31.2%) were the most common, followed by burn injuries (24.9%) and congenital anomalies (10.7%) (Fig. 2). The various surgical procedure modules were further divided into submodules and are detailed in Supplemental Digital Content 2. (See Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the distribution of cases by modules and submodules, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E453.) Reconstructive procedures were the most common (50.1%), followed by burn procedures (24.9%).

Procedural Trends

A significant increase in the annual average number of reconstructive procedures (+306.2%, P < 0.0001) and breast procedures (+7600.0%, P = 0.03) occurred between 1998–2005 and 2021–2024 (Fig. 3). There were increases in aesthetic facial procedures (+659.0%, P = 0.146), hand procedures (+403.5%, P = 0.211), vascular and lymphatic malformation procedures (+126.3%, P = 0.067), and pediatric and congenital anomaly procedures (+35.0%, P = 0.539). Annual average of Body contouring procedures and brachial plexus and peripheral nerve reconstruction procedures did not change (P = 0.18 and P = 0.07, respectively). Burn surgery decreased by 24.6% (P = 0.439). A comparison of the average annual procedures based on the type of procedure module in each cohort is shown in Figure 4.

DISCUSSION

This study represents the first comprehensive review of plastic surgery trends in Nepal over the past 3 decades. The findings reveal a shifting pattern with an increase in reconstructive and breast procedures.6 This rise in reconstructive procedures is likely due to the traumatic injuries being common and the increased use of flap-based and microsurgical techniques in our setting.9 The high prevalence of traumatic injuries aligns with the global trend of increasing road traffic accidents and workplace-related injuries.10 In Nepal, the increase can also be attributed to large-scale natural disasters, such as the 2015 Gorkha earthquake, which led to an increase in trauma cases requiring reconstructive surgery, causing a spike in reconstructive procedures in the periods after it, ie, 2016–2020 and 2020–2024. A prior study from our center also outlined the importance of reconstructive surgery in an earthquake-prone country such as Nepal.11 Likewise, prior studies have highlighted a need for coordination between humanitarian surgeons and local surgical teams after disasters.12 In addition, visiting foreign surgeons on humanitarian missions have also contributed to the growth of reconstructive surgery. Taken together, these factors have strengthened our reconstructive capacity and increased the frequency with which such cases are performed.

The steady increase in aesthetic and reconstructive breast procedures can be attributed to an evolving perception of the aesthetic importance of the breast, increasing breast cancer cases, and cosmetic breast interventions in men (eg, gynecomastia).13 Plastic surgery’s role in oncological reconstruction, particularly after excision of head and neck, breast, and skin tumors, is being increasingly recognized.14 However, our data did not show a sizable frequency of postmastectomy breast reconstruction cases, a crucial component of oncological care.15 This gap may suggest potential underuse of the same in breast cancer patients, underscoring the need for greater awareness, multidisciplinary collaboration, and inclusion of reconstructive counseling in oncology.

Aesthetic facial procedures increased in the recent cohort, aligning with global trends that reflect changing patient needs, cultural shifts, increased social acceptance, and advancements in surgical cosmesis.16 This also suggests that Nepalese patients are increasingly seeking quality-of-life improvements alongside medically necessary interventions. Procedures such as rhinoplasty and blepharoplasty were frequent, likely due to their high visibility and shorter recovery periods.16 The nonsignificant rise may be because our center was a government hospital, and patients desiring aesthetic surgery tend to visit private centers more often. Public hospital availability improved accessibility, but cosmetic procedures remain excluded from Nepal’s National Health Insurance Program, which offers limited coverage for medically necessary reconstructions.

A rise in hand surgery was observed in the recent cohort (2021–2024), which can be attributed to occupational injuries, for example: construction, carpentry, factory work, and workplace accidents; moreover, road traffic accidents and falls continue to be significant contributors to hand injuries. Improving workplace safety regulations and expanding surgical expertise in microsurgery and reconstructive procedures are crucial for optimizing patient outcomes in this regard.

Vascular malformation procedures increased, which may be attributed to improved diagnostic imaging and plastic surgery’s role in collaboration with dermatology, interventional radiology, and vascular surgeons.17–19 Plastic surgeons’ expertise in microsurgery and reconstructive techniques allows them to manage complex lesions and minimize functional and aesthetic impairments.20 Congenital anomaly surgery, particularly cleft lip and palate procedures, were steady, confirming their status as essential reconstructive procedures in pediatric plastic surgery over the decades.21

Conversely, burn surgery decreased over time, with the most pronounced decline occurring during 2011–2015. This reduction may be attributed to a decrease in burn injuries during the 2015 earthquake, when fewer people were confined indoors, along with the mobilization of resources in managing traumatic injuries due to earthquake. The broader downward trend may reflect the establishment of specialized burn centers in different regions of Nepal; however, burn care has not yet been truly decentralized. The majority of burn referral centers remain concentrated near the capital, Kathmandu (such as the Nepal Cleft and Burn Center at Kirtipur Hospital, Sushma Koirala Memorial Hospital), requiring most patients from rural areas to travel long distances to access definitive burn care. Although the frequency of burn surgery at our center has decreased, it was the second most common surgery after reconstructive surgery. Burn surgery remains a fundamental component of plastic surgery training and involves a diverse range of cases in our center and in LMICs as a whole. Our previous study underscored persistent systemic challenges in burn care in Nepal, including limited awareness of first aid and substantial delays in reaching specialized centers.22

We observed a steady increase in the number of procedures, with a 3-fold increase in average annual procedures in the initial and final cohorts, except during 2011–2015. This increase may be due to the expansion of the country’s infrastructure, surgical infrastructure, workforce, patients’ healthcare–seeking practices, and interdepartmental collaboration.23 The decline in 2011–2015 was due to the major earthquake in Nepal, leading to disruptions in hospital operations, affecting record-keeping, procedural documentation, and mobilization of departmental resources in disaster care. Improving disaster preparedness through digitized medical records and standardized interdisciplinary documentation could minimize future data loss.11 The COVID-19 pandemic led to a temporary decline in procedural volume during 2020–2021, followed by a rapid rise in aesthetic surgery demand, especially for gynecomastia and facial procedures. This aligns with global postpandemic trends and reflects changing perceptions of body image due to the impact of social media.

There was a demographic shift toward the older population. Adult men accounted for the majority of cases, which may be due to traumatic injuries being the most common diagnosis, indicating that plastic surgery services are needed in the active workforce of the country. Hence, expansion of plastic surgery services has the potential to uplift the economic productivity of the nation. A previous study analyzing the surgical trips performed by ReSurge International reported that the cost-effectiveness of the surgical trips ranged from $52 to $11,410 per disability-adjusted life-year averted, and a total benefit of $9,795,384 for an investment of $551,188.24,25 Our study highlighted the distribution of patients, with Bagmati Province accounting for the highest proportion of cases. This concentration may be attributed to the location of the study site in the same province, but also partly reflects disparity in plastic surgery services, with less access in other provinces. Similar disparities in healthcare accessibility have been documented in studies examining surgical service distribution.12

In the past 3 decades, procedural volume in plastic surgery at our institution increased. Advanced reconstructive, breast , aesthetic, hand, and vascular malformation procedures increased, reflecting the specialty’s development, interdepartmental collaboration, and increasing institutional capacity. On the other hand, burn surgery decreased. Despite growing demand, Nepal faces a scarcity of plastic surgeons, which is further compounded by the disproportionate distribution of plastic surgeons across the country. Limited government posts, lack of career pathways for progression outside the urban areas, and minimal regional investment are a few reasons behind it. Addressing these challenges requires political buy-in, advocacy, and policy to strengthen plastic surgery capacity and equitable plastic surgery services across the country.

LIMITATIONS

This was a single-center retrospective review; therefore, the findings may not reflect nationwide trends. Cases with incomplete records were excluded, which may have introduced selection bias. Additionally, the study was conducted at a government hospital and may not capture the entire spectrum of procedures, especially aesthetic ones. Nonetheless, a key strength of this study lies in the use of department-maintained audit records from one of Nepal’s largest tertiary care referral centers for plastic and reconstructive surgery. The duration of data collection adds to its robustness. Notably, the data were systematically recorded by the supervisor (last author) throughout his tenure in the department, who is also the first board-certified plastic surgeon in Nepal, with extensive clinical experience in the field, adding to the credibility of the dataset.

CONCLUSIONS

This 27-year review reflects the evolving landscape of plastic surgery in Nepal, with a rise in reconstructive, breast, aesthetic, hand, and vascular malformation procedures, highlighting specialty growth, shifting patient priorities, and interdisciplinary collaboration. These trends underscore the growing demand for plastic surgery in Nepal, emphasizing the need for investment in the workforce, specialized training, and surgical infrastructure.
