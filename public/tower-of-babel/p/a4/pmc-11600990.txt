Ventilator variables predicting extubation readiness in extremely premature infants with prolonged mechanical ventilation: A retrospective observational study
Authors: Truong An Nguyen, Josef Matoušek, Aleš Kuběna, Kilián Resl, Petr Kudrna, Klára Dunajová, Richard Plavka
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe current generation of neonatal ventilators enables periodic storage of set, measured, and calculated ventilatory parameters.DesignRetrospective observational study.ObjectivesTo evaluate and identify the ventilatory, demographic, and clinical pre‐extubation variables that are significant for estimating extubation readiness.MethodsEligible subjects included premature infants <33 weeks of gestation weaned from mechanical ventilation (MV) lasting >24 h. A total of 16 relevant ventilator variables, each calculated from 288 data points over 24 h, together with eight demographic and three clinical pre‐extubation variables, were used to create the generalized linear model (GLM) for a binary outcome and the Cox proportional hazards model for time‐to‐event analysis. The achievement of a 120‐h period without reintubation was defined as a successful extubation attempt (EA) within the binary outcome.ResultsWe evaluated 149 EAs in 81 infants with a median (interquartile range) gestational age of 25+2 (24+3–26+1) weeks. Of this, 90 EAs (60%) were successful while 59 (40%) failed. GLM identified dynamic compliance per kilogram, percentage of spontaneous minute ventilation, and postmenstrual age as significant independent positive variables. Conversely, dynamic compliance variability emerged as a significant independent negative variable for extubation success. This model enabled the creation of a probability estimator for extubation success with a good proportion of sensitivity and specificity (80% and 73% for a cut‐off of 60%, respectively).ConclusionsVentilator variables reflecting lung mechanical properties and the ability to spontaneously breathe during MV contribute to better prediction of extubation readiness in extremely premature infants with chronic lung disease.

INTRODUCTION

Determining the appropriate time for extubation from invasive mechanical ventilation (MV) is more an art than an evidence‐based clinical skill. The decision to extubate rests with the treating physician, who evaluates readiness based on the patient's current clinical condition and ventilatory support level. 1 Repeated reintubations require undesirable lung re‐recruitment and may contribute to worse outcomes. 2 , 3 Additionally, infants who fail an extubation attempt (EA) may not achieve their pre‐extubation respiratory conditions until several days after reintubation. 4

Unfortunately, existing predictors and tests designed to identify extubation readiness of premature infants exhibit low accuracy and limited benefit. The population of infants selected for these tests, who are clinically deemed ready for extubation, introduces bias and can contribute to a disproportion between high sensitivity and low specificity. 5 , 6 Unsurprisingly, previous studies evaluating individual ventilator variables such as tidal volume, spontaneous minute ventilation, static compliance of the respiratory system, and inspiratory and expiratory times individually have shown suboptimal results. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 Although more complex composite assessments appear promising with better specificity while maintaining high sensitivity, they are deemed impractical for routine clinical use. 5 , 10 , 12 A recently developed estimator, incorporating several significant markers, attempts to enhance extubation readiness prediction. 13 However, its suitability may be more aligned with premature infants resolving respiratory distress syndrome (RDS) than with extremely premature infants requiring prolonged MV due to evolving or established chronic lung disease (CLD).

We hypothesized that certain ventilator variables reflecting lung mechanical properties and the capacity for spontaneous breathing could enhance the accuracy of extubation readiness assessments, especially when using well‐synchronized modes of MV such as Pressure Support Ventilation with Volume Guarantee (PSV‐VG). This mode is usually provided by modern neonatal ventilators that store the measured and calculated data from prior MV periods. Analyzing these specific MV variables can provide insights into the lung properties and spontaneous breathing potential of premature infants. Continuous monitoring of these variables over a defined period provides clinicians with independent and unbiased information about the patient's respiratory status, significantly improving the accuracy of extubation readiness estimation when combined with clinical conditions. 14 Our investigation focused on evaluating 16 ventilator variables alongside demographic and clinical data to pinpoint the most indicative factors of extubation readiness in infants with heterogeneous lung diseases undergoing prolonged MV.

METHODS

This retrospective, single‐center observational study, was conducted at the tertiary‐level NICU of the General University Hospital in Prague between January 2019 and February 2022. We included all EA in premature infants born at less than 33 weeks of gestation, who were invasively ventilated for at least 24 h before attempting extubation from PSV‐VG mode and had all their predefined data available for analysis. We excluded infants with non‐respiratory reasons for extubation failure, developed or diagnosed during the postextubation period, such as late‐onset sepsis including necrotizing enterocolitis, birth defects affecting respiratory functions, subglottic stenosis or tracheomalacia, and acute requirement of surgery. All infants were ventilated using the Babylog VN500 ventilator (Dräger). A protective low‐volume strategy with an appropriate positive end‐expiratory pressure (PEEP) level was employed to maintain optimal recruitment throughout the respiratory cycle, allowing the use of physiological tidal volumes. Despite internal practice guidelines recommending specific mean airway pressure (MAwP) and fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) levels to be achieved and maintained for at least 12 h before extubation, the final decision was based on a combination of current clinical and respiratory conditions, at the discretion of the attending physician who had control over ventilator settings and the extubation process. As a standard practice, 10 mg/kg of caffeine was administered 30–60 min before extubation as an additional dose above daily maintenance of 5 mg/kg. All patients underwent extubation to nasal continuous positive airway pressure (nCPAP), initially set at a pressure of 8 cmH2O. We utilized one of two variable flow devices, Viasys Infant Flow (Vyaire Medical Inc.) or Fabian HFOi (Accutronic Medical Systems AG), where pressure is variably proportional to flow. If FiO2 increased, as per our standard care protocol, flow was further increased up to 12 L/min, which could generate a pressure of approximately 10 cmH2O. Nasal prongs and masks (Intersurgical Ltd.) were periodically switched to minimize nasal injuries. The need to reintubate is defined by a significant increase of FiO2, or the development of severe respiratory instability accompanied by repeated desaturations, but the final decision to reintubate ultimately depended on the discretion of the attending physician.

Data collection and statistical analysis

Ventilator data were exported at 5‐min intervals from the Babylog VN500, yielding 288 daily collected observations of 82 ventilator parameters. For this study, 16 variables reflecting proper mechanical conditions and the central nervous system's breathing drive were selected. For each chosen variable, we computed medians and interquartile ranges using numeric data gathered from the 24‐h period preceding each EA. In this context, the interquartile range represented 24‐h variability. Descriptive variables (gestational age, birthweight, antenatal steroid use, histological chorioamnionitis, and total duration of MV), pre‐extubation clinical variables (postmenstrual age [PMA], current weight, and systemic steroid use rather than [PMA]), and selected ventilator variables were compared between infants with successful and failed EAs. The Chi‐square test was used for binary variables, and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test was used for continuous variables. Further statistical analyzes primarily focused on the binary extubation outcomes. Successful EA was defined as remaining ventilator‐free for >120 h. Analysis was conducted using Wolfram Mathematica software version 13.2 and Rstudio version 2023.03.1 + 446. Statistical significance was set at p < .05.

Adjusted analysis

We evaluated several approaches to select the most effective artificial learning method for predicting success. These methods were compared based on the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve using a training‐test approach. To ensure a sufficient sample size for training and testing, we adopted a 1:1 training‐to‐test ratio. The logistic regression model proved to be the most precise while also facilitating the identification of key demographic, clinical pre‐extubation, and ventilatory variables for predicting successful EA. We employed a generalized linear model (GLM) with a Bernoulli distribution and a logit link function for the binary outcomes and adjusted odds ratios calculation. We also tested the duration until EA failure via time‐to‐event analysis using Cox regression to recognize the potential bias of the binary time‐fixed definition.

RESULTS

In total, 112 infants were mechanically ventilated during the study period. Among them, four patients had a birth defect, eight were extubated from high‐frequency oscillatory ventilation, 11 died before their first EA, seven had incomplete data, and one was transferred to another ward before extubation. Finally, we included 81 infants with a median (interquartile range [IQR]) gestational age of 25+2 weeks (24+3–26+1) and a median (IQR) birthweight of 710 g (605–825), who underwent 149 EAs. The MV duration and the number of EAs correlated with gestational age. A total of 89% of all attempts were performed in infants born at <27 weeks of gestation. Their median (IQR) duration of invasive MV was 31.5 days (18–45.25) (Figure 1).

In the GLM, we identified one clinical variable and three ventilatory variables significantly influencing the likelihood of successful extubation. The critical clinical variable was the PMA. Each additional week of age, all else being equal, increases the probability of success by more than twice (adjusted odds ratio [aOR] = 2.49, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.7–2.42, p < .001). Regarding ventilator variables, success probability increased with higher Cdyn/kg (aOR = 6.026 per IQR, 95% CI = 2.1–17.9, p < .001) and %MVspon (aOR = 2.53 per IQR, 95% CI = 1.03–6.01, p = .042). Conversely, a higher 24‐h variability in Cdyn/kg decreases the probability of extubation success (aOR = 0.43 per IQR, 95% CI = 0.25–0.76, p = .004). MAwP did not influence extubation success as a binary outcome, but its higher levels negatively affected the duration of staying ventilator‐free as indicated by the Cox regression analysis (aOR = 0.5 per IQR, 95% CI = 0.29–0.88) (Table 3).

ROC curves with corresponding areas under the curve (AUCs) were generated for two predictive models: the first with PMA only and the second with PMA together with four significant ventilator variables including MAwP. The multivariate model increased the AUC from 0.795 to 0.889 (Figure 3).

The receiver operator characteristic (ROC) curve created for two predictive models, the first one with the only significant clinical variable—postmenstrual age only (ROC1) and the second one with postmenstrual age in combination with four significant ventilator variables (ROC1 + 4). The multivariate model extended the area under the curve (AUC) from 0.795 to 0.889.

The regression formula was used to construct an estimator of extubation success probability (ESP), available here: https://extubation.lf1.cuni.cz. This calculator achieved a sensitivity and specificity of 80%/73%, 74%/78%, 65%/86%, and 52/93% for probability cut‐offs of 60%, 70%, 80%, and 90%, respectively.

DISCUSSION

In this study concerned with identifying ventilator parameters associated with a successful extubation, we observed a 60% success rate of EAs in 81 premature infants weaned from MV. Sixty‐six (81%) of them were born at less than 27 weeks of gestation, were invasively ventilated for more than 3 weeks, and 43 of them underwent repeated EAs (Figure 1). These extremely premature infants constitute the dominant subset of our cohort and are characterized by heterogenous lung disease requiring prolonged MV. These characteristics distinguish our study from the other ones, which focused on predictive factors of the first successful EA during recovery from RDS. 5 , 6 , 13 We identified higher PMA as a significant clinical pre‐extubation variable, with each additional week increasing the probability of extubation success by more than twice. Furthermore, compared to relying solely on routine demographic and clinical pre‐extubation information, incorporating ventilator variables improved the accuracy of predicting extubation success.

This set of significant variables may better predict extubation readiness in premature infants with CLD. PMA may reflect the progress of immature lung development and improvement of central regulation of breathing. The 24‐h median of dynamic compliance per kg (Cdyn/kg) can provide insights into the mechanical properties of the lungs, whereas its variability may help assess its stability.

However, Cdyn/kg is probably higher during spontaneous than mandatory breaths as this measurement likely also reflects the inspiratory effort of the infant as much as the mechanical properties of the lungs. So, this clinically available variable may reflect more than lung mechanics. Similarly, the variability of Cdyn/kg may reflect more the irregularity of spontaneous breathing than instability of lung compliance. The percentage of spontaneous minute ventilation (%MVspon) provided feedback on the level of central respiratory drive. Finally, the required MAwP roughly represents the extent of secondary pathological changes in the lungs.

Recently, Gupta et al. 13 published a predictive model for extubation readiness, identifying higher gestational and chronological ages, pre‐extubation pH, lower FiO2, and lower peak respiratory severity score (RSS) in the first 6 h of life as being associated with successful extubation. Although the studies shared similar goals, designs, and methods, the cohorts and pre‐extubation conditions were fundamentally different. Our infants were born 1–2 weeks earlier, and while Gupta's 13 infants were mainly extubated from invasive ventilation during the recovery phase of RDS, ours had a longer course of invasive ventilation and were primarily weaned from MV during the evolving or established CLD. This could explain why our infants were extubated at higher MAwP and PMA, which were identified as significant variables in both studies. However, FiO2 level, a marker often tested for predicting extubation readiness, 5 was not identified as a significant variable in our study. This may be explained either by the well‐managed PEEP level in our infants during PSV‐VG 15 or that FiO2 levels <0.30 may not be a particularly sensitive predictor in infants with heterogeneous lung disease requiring prolong MV. The other significant variables found in Gupta's 13 study, such as pH before extubation and the peak RSS, were not tested in our study because we did not consider them relevant to infants with an extended duration of MV. Furthermore, while most of our infants (75%) failed during the first 24 h, failures were more uniformly distributed over the 120‐h period in their study. The relatively quick alveolar‐saccular collapse in our patients may be explained by the difficulty in maintaining sufficient pressure noninvasively in the heterogeneous character of lung disease, as well as their use of Non‐Invasive Positive Pressure Ventilation (NIPPV) in infants mostly recovering from RDS. 16

In our study, we exclusively used the PSV‐VG mode to wean patients from MV. This pressure‐limited, flow‐cycled mode synchronizes with the beginning of inspiration, with expiration starting when the flow decreases to 15% of its peak level. Therefore, the mechanical properties of the lungs are dominant determinants of inspiration time. Spontaneous inspiration time and time constant, corresponding to better lung mechanics, were longer in infants with successful attempts. However, none of these differences remained significant after adjustment. 5

Similarly to Gupta et al., 13 we developed a calculator for ESP for premature infants with evolving CLD. A key feature of effective predictive tests is their ability to balance sensitivity and specificity. While some composite tests have achieved this balance, others, especially those requiring a period of spontaneous breathing on endotracheal CPAP or assessing respiratory muscle strength, have shown less sensitivity than standalone factors such as gestational age or birthweight, and thus have not been recommended for clinical practice. 5 Our calculator exhibited an inverse relationship between sensitivity and specificity across various ESP levels. As ESP increased, sensitivity decreased, while specificity increased. This suggests the potential utility of monitoring ESP trends during the weaning phase to optimize extubation timing. However, before advocating its clinical application, the estimator's validity must be confirmed through a currently ongoing prospective study.

This study has several limitations. This was a single‐center study in which all infants were ventilated exclusively using the PSV‐VG mode before extubation and the decision to reintubate might be subject to clinician bias at a single center. 1 We cannot confirm the applicability of our results to other conventional modes in which synchronization and VG modes are not utilized and the inspiratory time is fixed. Additionally, ventilator variables such as Cdyn/kg, resistance, and time constants, which are measured during both mandatory and triggered breaths, pose interpretative challenges due to their variability and instability in real time. To address this, we collected 24‐h data before extubation to better represent the current pulmonary mechanics and respiratory drive. However, the median values derived from these measurements can be significantly skewed by the ratio of triggered to mandatory breathing. The prevalence of spontaneous breaths, yielding more favorable measurements, may be a critical determinant in evaluating patient readiness. Readers should be aware that the %MVspon, as a significant variable of extubation readiness, is influenced by a relationship between the back‐up frequency set on ventilator and spontaneous breaths. This means that a lower back‐up frequency results in a higher %MVspon and vice versa. Our infants, having short time constants and spontaneous inspiratory times, usually breathe with frequencies between 40 and 60 per minute. Our standard back‐up frequency is set at 40/min, occasionally lower, but never higher during weaning from MV. Thus, only an insignificant overestimation of %MVspon may be present. Finally, our cohort of infants represents a heterogeneous group of premature infants where gestational age is negatively correlated with the number of invasively ventilated infants and with their duration of MV. This results in an unequal distribution of statistical influence across gestational ages, with the most significant influence likely occurring at the lowest gestational ages and to the longest durations of MV. Consequently, our predictive model of extubation readiness may not be applicable to early extubation of infants with resolving RDS.

Despite these limitations, our study has notable strengths. The predictive model was created from a unique cohort of premature infants with the highest rate and duration of MV due to evolving CLD, and for whom prolonged unnecessary MV and repeated reintubations are particularly detrimental. 3 The ESP estimator, integrating a complex set of variables indirectly reflecting postnatal maturation and lung development, may contribute to reducing MV duration primarily by lowering reintubation rates rather than accelerating successful extubation. This is especially relevant for premature infants with heterogeneous lung disease who are susceptible to alveolar and small airway collapse.

In conclusion, 24‐h dynamic compliance per kilogram, its variability, and the percentage of spontaneous ventilation, provide significant information on the mechanical properties of the lung and respiratory drive of infants. These variables, combined with PMA and mean airway pressure, form an integral complex of data that enhances the prediction of extubation readiness. The calculator of ESP requires prospective validation before its recommendation for clinical practice.
