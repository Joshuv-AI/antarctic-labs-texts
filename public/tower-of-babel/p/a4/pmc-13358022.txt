Propofol-induced opisthotonos in the post-anaesthesia care unit
Authors: Evan Shorr, Laura Scholtz, Gabriel Soto Arzon, Kenneth Sutin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe report a case of opisthotonos following propofol for induction of general anaesthesia in a man in his 30s who underwent elective dental extraction. Within minutes of arrival in the post-anaesthesia care unit, he developed intermittent involuntary motor episodes characterised by retrocollis, axial hyperextension and decorticate-like posturing. He remained alert and oriented throughout, and neuroimaging and laboratory studies were unremarkable. The patient later reported a similar experience after a previous anaesthetic involving propofol. This case highlights the importance of recognising propofol-induced opisthotonos as a rare but distinct clinical entity that may be mistaken for seizure or stroke.Keywords: Neurology, Epilepsy and seizures, Neuroanaesthesia, Movement disorders (other than Parkinsons), Anaesthesia

Background

Since its approval by the US Food and Drug Administration in 1989, propofol has been widely adopted as an intravenous anaesthetic owing to its rapid onset and short duration of action. It acts by allosterically modulating the GABAA (gamma Aminobutyric acid-A) receptor complex, which is located throughout the central nervous system, and enhances GABAergic inhibitory responses.1 While generally well-tolerated, rare neuroexcitatory reactions such as opisthotonos have been reported. Opisthotonos is a stereotyped abnormal posture characterised by sudden-onset severe extensor hypertonia of the neck, trunk and lower extremities, producing marked backward arching of the body, which may be sustained or episodic.2 Propofol-induced opisthotonos (PIO) describes a rare propofol-related neuroexcitatory or dystonic (‘seizure-like’) reaction characterised by episodic or sustained extensor hypertonia, typically occurring during anaesthetic emergence with preserved consciousness3 first recognised in reports from the late 1980s.4 These events may present with dramatic motor findings and can be mistaken for seizures, cerebrovascular incidents or psychiatric disturbances, potentially leading to inappropriate interventions. Due to its rarity and variable presentation, PIO remains under-recognised among clinicians. A deeper understanding of its distinguishing features and clinical course can facilitate timely diagnosis and appropriate management.

Case presentation

A man in his 30s with a history of recurrent sinusitis secondary to an oroantral fistula presented to an ambulatory surgery centre in the New York City area for elective dental extraction under general anaesthesia. Additional medical history included selective IgM deficiency, asthma, a remote childhood seizure disorder characterised by brief absence-like episodes, and a remote minor head injury without documented intracranial haemorrhage or neurosurgical intervention. He had multiple prior endoscopies for colon polyps as well as surgical repair of a paraoesophageal hernia. Outpatient medications included temazepam, prednisone and fluticasone propionate. He described his childhood seizures as brief staring spells without generalised convulsions and reported being seizure-free for approximately two decades. He had previously been treated with valproate, which was discontinued more than 3 years prior, and he was not taking any antiepileptic medications at the time of surgery.

On the morning of surgery, the anaesthetic pre-evaluation was unremarkable. General anaesthesia was induced with intravenous midazolam 2 mg, fentanyl 100 mcg, lidocaine 100 mg, propofol 150 mg (1.88 mg/kg) and rocuronium 50 mg. The airway was secured with a 7.5mm Shiley™ nasal RAE endotracheal tube (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA), placed atraumatically using video laryngoscopy. Anaesthesia was maintained with air, oxygen and sevoflurane, with an end-tidal concentration of 1.7%–2.5% during the maintenance phase. The procedure involved extraction of right maxillary molars (teeth 2 and 3) with a right-sided buccal advancement flap. The procedure was uncomplicated and lasted approximately 1 hour. Intraoperative medications included ampicillin–sulbactam 3 g, cefazolin 2 g and dexamethasone 10 mg. At the request of the surgeon to maintain systolic blood pressures below 100 mmHg, labetalol 5 mg was administered. A brief period of hypotension followed that resolved with four boluses of phenylephrine, total 800 mcg. Toward the end of the case, he received ondansetron 4 mg and sugammadex 200 mg. The patient emerged uneventfully from anaesthesia, was extubated and transferred to the recovery area in stable condition.

Approximately 15 min after arrival in the post-anaesthesia care unit, the patient was first observed by nursing staff to exhibit disconjugate gaze and abnormal motor activity. On clinical evaluation, he demonstrated recurrent paroxysms of involuntary motor movements, characterised by hyperextension of the proximal muscles of the upper and lower extremities, involvement of the axial musculature, retrocollis and decorticate-like posturing (see online supplemental video in media). These episodes occurred approximately 2–3 times per minute, each lasting several seconds.

Between episodes, the patient was sleepy but arousable and oriented to person, place, time and situation. He was able to speak in short sentences and clearly describe his symptoms as “I feel herky-jerky movements.” He remained fully aware during each episode but was unable to voluntarily suppress the movements.

Vital signs remained stable throughout, with no significant variation during or between episodes. No apnoea, oxygen desaturation, laryngospasm, tongue biting, urinary incontinence or post-event confusion were observed. Neurological examination revealed intact cranial nerves II through XII, with preserved strength and sensation in all extremities. Nystagmus was not observed; the gaze abnormality was limited to transient disconjugate gaze. A neurology consultation was obtained for further evaluation of the abnormal movements.

Investigations

Laboratory studies, including complete blood count, comprehensive metabolic panel (including glucose, calcium and magnesium) and hepatic panel were unremarkable. A non-contrast CT of the head revealed no evidence of acute or chronic intracranial pathology. Of note, he had a normal brain MRI with Gadavist contrast 4 years prior.

Differential diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for acute abnormal motor activity in the post-anaesthetic period includes cerebrovascular events, epileptic seizures (generalised or focal) and non-epileptic motor activity, including dystonia. A vascular event was considered unlikely given the episodic nature of the movements, their complete resolution between episodes and the absence of focal neurological deficits.

Seizure activity was considered unlikely. Despite a remote history of seizures, the patient demonstrated preserved awareness throughout each episode and returned immediately to his neurological baseline without a postictal phase. Heart rate, blood pressure and oxygen saturation remained stable during the events, arguing against epileptic activity, which is typically accompanied by autonomic activation. In addition, there was no loss of consciousness, tongue biting, urinary or faecal incontinence, postictal confusion or other features characteristic of epileptic seizures.

Although suspicion for an intracranial process was low, a non-contrast CT scan of the brain was obtained and was unremarkable. The observed pattern of repetitive, involuntary extensor contractions involving the back, neck and proximal limb musculature was consistent with opisthotonos. Treatment

Shortly before transport for the CT imaging, intravenous lorazepam 1 mg was administered to suppress involuntary movements and facilitate completion of the study.

Outcome and follow-up

The patient continued to recover in the post-anaesthesia care unit, during which the frequency and amplitude of the abnormal motor movements gradually diminished and resolved completely within approximately 1 hour. He remained haemodynamically stable throughout and returned to his neurological baseline without residual symptoms.

On subsequent structured interviews, the patient reported a prior episode of abnormal, uncontrollable motor activity following recovery from a propofol-based anaesthetic for colonoscopy approximately 20 years earlier. He further described having undergone multiple subsequent endoscopic and surgical procedures with propofol-based sedation or anaesthesia, during which abnormal movements, shaking or twitching were intermittently observed during emergence. Review of available medical records, together with patient report, indicated that similar post-anaesthetic motor phenomena had been noted by clinical staff during recovery from propofol exposure as recently as 2022, although these episodes were less severe and self-limited.

Following the present event, the patient was counselled regarding the low likelihood of long-term neurological sequelae and advised to inform future anaesthetists of this reaction and to avoid further exposure to propofol. Propofol was added to his electronic medical record as an allergy, with documentation specifying abnormal involuntary motor movements during recovery. Consistent with this guidance, the patient subsequently underwent a recent upper and lower endoscopy using midazolam-based sedation without propofol and he reported an uneventful emergence, with no abnormal movements or post-procedural complications. The potential use of dexmedetomidine as an adjunct for future sedation was also discussed as a possible strategy to minimise neuroexcitatory responses.

Discussion

The incidence of PIO is unknown. While it is thought to be rare, case reports have documented this association since the 1980s.4 Seizure-like phenomena have been associated with propofol, with generalised tonic-clonic seizures, likely epileptic in origin, reported predominantly in patients with epilepsy, whereas opisthotonos is likely non-epileptic in origin.3

Many of these reports detail opisthotonos or seizures occurring in the post-anaesthesia setting; however, there have been reports of PIO on induction of anaesthesia.3 5 The association between propofol-induced epileptic or opisthotonos events in patients with underlying epilepsy was salient enough that, initially, several authors recommended avoiding propofol in patients with epilepsy.3 6 Other reports detail propofol-induced neuro-excitation or ‘frenzy’ described by other uncontrollable body movements while recovering from propofol-based anaesthetics.7 Most patients who experienced PIO in the recovery room had resolution within one hour; however, several cases persisted for up to four hours, and one case lasted approximately three weeks.8

Pathophysiology

The pathophysiology of PIO remains incompletely defined and is likely multifactorial. Importantly, general anaesthesia typically involves the administration of multiple agents—including opioids, benzodiazepines, volatile anaesthetics, neuromuscular blockers, antiemetics and vasoactive drugs—making it inherently difficult to isolate propofol as the sole causal factor in postoperative neuroexcitatory phenomena. Nevertheless, propofol has been most consistently implicated in published reports.1 3 9 Propofol is a potent modulator of γ-aminobutyric acid type A (GABAA) receptors and, to a lesser extent, glycinergic inhibitory pathways. Rapid changes in cerebral propofol concentration, particularly during emergence, may result in transient disinhibition of brainstem and spinal motor circuits.1 3 9 This mechanism has been proposed to involve acute tolerance or rebound refractoriness of inhibitory neurotransmission, leading to reduced descending inhibition and pathological activation of axial extensor motor pathways characteristic of opisthotonos.2 8 10 In addition, systematic reviews demonstrate that many opisthotonic and seizure-like events associated with propofol are non-epileptic in origin, frequently occurring with preserved awareness and normal electroencephalography, supporting a predominantly brainstem–spinal rather than cortical mechanism.3 4 9

Risk factors

At present, there are no high-quality data defining patient-specific risk factors for the development of opisthotonos or other propofol-associated neuroexcitatory phenomena. The largest systematic review to date identified 81 reported cases, of whom 11 had a pre-existing diagnosis of epilepsy.3 The authors appropriately cautioned against drawing epidemiological inferences from these data, noting that case reports are inherently subject to selection and observer bias and lack suitable comparator groups.3 Accordingly, any attribution of individual risk must be made with low certainty.

The contribution of a remote seizure history or minor traumatic brain injury to susceptibility for PIO remains uncertain. In this case, the seizure disorder had resolved decades earlier without recurrence off of therapy, and adult neuroimaging and electroencephalography were normal. These historical factors may indicate individual vulnerability; however, available evidence suggests a multifactorial mechanism rather than direct causation. Prior reports indicate vulnerability may be further influenced by concomitant medications and underlying neurological factors. Antiepileptic drugs such as carbamazepine, phenytoin and valproate have been implicated in altering inhibitory–excitatory balance, and paradoxically, prolonged or recurrent opisthotonic episodes have been described in patients receiving these agents.8 10 Chronic exposure to GABAergic medications, including benzodiazepines and non-benzodiazepine hypnotics, may similarly contribute to receptor-level adaptations that increase vulnerability to paradoxical neuroexcitation during propofol exposure.7 8 10 11

Management considerations

There is no established standardised treatment protocol for PIO; management recommendations are derived primarily from case reports and small series. Available evidence suggests that antiepileptic drugs are generally ineffective in this setting and may prolong recovery when the phenomenon is non-epileptic in origin.3 8 9 In contrast, benzodiazepines and other centrally acting sedatives appear to provide symptomatic benefit by enhancing inhibitory neurotransmission and reducing motor excitability.3 4 9

Initial management should prioritise patient safety, including maintaining a patent airway and haemodynamic stability, and measures to prevent self-injury or falls during recurrent extensor spasms. Environmental stimulation should be minimised, and close observation maintained. For symptomatic control, a benzodiazepine may be administered (eg, intravenous lorazepam 0.5–2 mg or intravenous diazepam 2–5 mg, titrated to effect).3 8 9 In refractory or severe cases, short-acting barbiturates (eg, intravenous thiopental 25–50 mg boluses) or sedation with dexmedetomidine infusion (0.2–0.7 µg/kg/hour) have been reported to attenuate the symptoms.7 9 Anticholinergic agents (eg, intravenous benztropine 1–2 mg or intravenous diphenhydramine 25–50 mg) may be considered when dystonic features predominate, although responses are variable.8 11

Re-administration of propofol should be avoided. Also, routine escalation to antiepileptic therapy should be avoided unless electroencephalography confirms epileptic activity. Most reported cases resolve spontaneously within hours with supportive care, judicious sedation and injury-prevention measures.3 8 9

Summary

In this case, we describe a patient who developed opisthotonos following propofol induction of general anaesthesia. Subsequent interviews revealed similar abnormal involuntary motor phenomena occurring during recovery from multiple prior propofol exposures over many years. Although the contribution of historical neurological factors remains uncertain and the absolute risk of recurrence is unknown,3 8 9 recurrence of temporally associated events supports individual susceptibility, strengthens the clinical diagnosis of PIO and reinforces the rationale for avoiding re-exposure. His presentation was recognisable as opisthotonos, enabling timely diagnosis and management. The patient recovered fully without long-term neurological sequelae and was counselled to avoid future exposure to propofol.

Patient’s perspective

I am a man in my 30s, and I underwent elective dental surgery to address a chronic sinus problem caused by an oroantral fistula. Before the operation, I felt calm and reassured. After years of recurrent infections involving my mouth, teeth and sinus cavity, I was hopeful that this procedure would finally resolve a long-standing issue. I was aware that my medical history was somewhat complicated, but the surgical and anaesthesia teams explained the plan clearly, and I felt comfortable proceeding.

I was told that the surgery itself went as planned. I remember being taken into the operating room, receiving anaesthesia and then waking up afterwards feeling slightly groggy but otherwise well. At that point, I did not feel that anything was wrong.

Not long after arriving in the recovery area, however, I began to experience unusual, uncontrollable movements. It felt as though my body was jerking or tightening on its own. I recall describing these as ‘herky-jerky movements’. Although I remained awake and aware, I could not suppress the movements. This was unsettling, particularly because I was conscious throughout and able to describe what was happening. I did not feel confused or in pain, but I was aware that something out of the ordinary was occurring.

The medical team responded promptly and monitored me closely. Their calm approach helped me remain as relaxed as possible during the episode. After some time, the movements resolved, and I gradually returned to normal. I was later reassured to learn that my vital signs had remained stable and that imaging and laboratory tests were normal.

Reflecting on my experience, it was both surprising and, at moments, daunting. At the same time, I felt well supported throughout the process. The experience highlighted for me the unpredictable nature of medical procedures, reminding me that even those that appear straightforward can hold surprises. By sharing my experience, I hope to contribute to a better understanding of patient experiences and to support efforts to improve care for others.
