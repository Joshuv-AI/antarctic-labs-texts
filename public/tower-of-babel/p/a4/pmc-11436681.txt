B3GALT4 modulates tumor progression and autophagy by AKT/mTOR signaling pathway in breast cancer
Authors: Yongliang Sha, Huijie Zhuang, Jin Shi, Song Ge, Shiqing He, Yiqiu Wang, Li Ma, Hao Guo, Hui Cheng
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Backgroundβ-1,3-Galactosyltransferase-4 (B3GALT4), a member of the β-1,3-galactosyltransferase gene family, is essential to the development of many malignancies. However, its biological function in breast cancer is still unknown.MethodPublically accessible datasets, as well as quantitative real-time PCR, western blot, and immunohistochemistry on our patient cohort were used to investigate the expression levels of B3GALT4 in breast cancer. The correlation of B3GALT4 expression with clinical histopathological data and mortality in breast cancer patients was investigated. The effects of B3GALT4 in breast cancer in vitro and in vivo were investigated. RNA-seq, western blot, autophagolysosomes, and the fluorescence intensity of LC3 were used to explore the effects of B3GALT4 on autophagy. Western blot and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were used to identify the AKT/mTOR pathway.ResultsB3GALT4 was significantly overexpressed in breast cancer tissues and was positively correlated with some aspects of clinicopathological status and poor prognosis. B3GALT4 overexpression significantly promoted cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, both in vitro and in vivo. B3GALT4 inhibition suppressed breast cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion in vitro. Suppression of B3GALT4 triggered autophagy and hindered the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.ConclusionAccording to the present research, B3GALT4 blocked autophagy via the AKT/mTOR pathway and accelerated the growth of breast cancer. B3GALT4 may be an effective target for patients with breast cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-024-01371-9.

Introduction

Breast cancer is a common and aggressive tumor that affects women, with its occurrence rate consistently rising and presenting a substantial danger to women’s overall health and emotional state. Although surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy have improved the survival rate of patients to a certain extent, the prognosis of advanced patients is still poor [1]. Therefore, it is essential to discover molecules that impact both the development and prognosis of breast cancer.

The β-1,3-galactosyltransferase-4 (B3GALT4) gene, a member of the β-1,3-galactosyltransferase gene family, is notably overexpressed in various malignant tumor tissues. Research indicates that high expression of B3GALT4 in colorectal cancer tissue was an independent risk factor for poor prognosis of patients [2]. Zhang et al. [3] conducted bioinformatics analysis and identified a correlation between B3GALT4 and the prognosis of osteosarcoma patients, indicating that B3GALT4 may serve as a potential risk factor for osteosarcoma. Furthermore, in breast cancer cells, inhibition of the Smad3/4 complex binding to the B3GALT4 promoter SBE can lead to the down-regulation of the B3GALT4 gene expression, consequently hindering the epithelial-mesenchymal transition process in tumor cells [4]. However, the role of B3GALT4 in regulating the malignant phenotype of breast cancer is not clear.

Autophagy, an evolutionarily process of degrading proteins through lysosomes, plays a dual role in cancer development [5]. In the early stage, autophagy can facilitate cells survive by clearing harmful substances in cells, but excessive autophagy can lead to cell death. Several studies have shown that increasing autophagy may suppress the growth, invasion, and migratory capabilities of breast cancer cells [6–8]. It has been reported that glycosyltransferase is closely related to autophagy [9, 10], but the correlation between B3GALT4 and autophagy in breast cancer cells is still ambiguous. Based these findings, this study aimed to explore the role and underlying mechanisms of B3GALT4 on the progression of breast cancer through bioinformatics, cell, and animal experiments, which provides a potential therapeutic target for breast cancer.

In present study, we investigated the capacity of B3GALT4 to induce autophagy in breast cancer and explored the effects of B3GALT4 on breast cancer cell proliferation, invasion, and migration both in vitro and in vivo. We further elucidated the mechanism underlying the B3GALT4-mediated induction of autophagy by AKT/mTOR signaling pathway.

Materials and methods

Clinical samples

Breast cancer patients (n = 40) who underwent surgical treatment at Department of Thyroid and Breast Surgery in Xuzhou Central Hospital from October 2019 to December 2023 were included. Inclusion criteria: (1) postoperative pathology confirmed the diagnosis of primary breast cancer; (2) no preoperative chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and immune-targeted therapy; (3) complete clinical and pathological data; (4) female. Exclusion criteria: (1) other serious infectious diseases, autoimmune diseases, etc.; (2) complicated with other types of malignant tumors. Clinical pathology information for all patients is shown in Table S1. Both breast cancer tissues and corresponding normal breast tissues (2 cm away from the tumor) were collected and stored at − 80 °C. This study was approved by the hospital ethics committee (XZZXYY2018034), and the patients signed an informed consent form. All the experimental protocols were approved by the Xuzhou Central Hospital ethics committee.

Bioinformatics analysis

B3GALT4 expression levels in different tumor tissues and adjacent normal tissues were evaluated in the Human Protein Atlas (THPA) database. Subsequently, GEPIA and bcGenExMiner databases were used to verify the expression levels of B3GALT4 between normal breast tissues and breast cancer tissues. The relationship between B3GALT4 expression levels and prognosis of breast cancer patients was investigated by the THPA database and Kaplan–Meier plotter website. The relationship between B3GALT4 expression levels and clinicopathological characteristics of breast cancer was analyzed using the UALCAN database.

Cell culture and cell lines

Human breast cancer cell lines MDA-MB-468 and MCF-7, generously provided by Dr. Xuchen Cao from Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital, were used in this study. The cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, BI) and 0.1% Penicillin–Streptomycin Solution (Gibco) at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All experiments were conducted using cells in the logarithmic growth phase. The cells were incubated for the indicated time points and harvested for biochemical or molecular assays. To ensure the reliability of the results, all experiments were performed independently at least three times.

Establishment of stable cell lines for B3GALT4 knockdown and overexpression

Lentiviruses that encoded B3GALT4-knockdown (sh-B3GALT4), B3GALT4-overexpression (OE-B3GALT4), and empty vectors (Scramble and Vector) were purchased from Ribobio (Guangzhou). MDA-MB-468/MCF-7 cells in the logarithmic growth phase were chosen and seeded to 6-well culture plates. The cells were transfected when they reached 50–60% growth by adding a mixture of lentiviruses and lipo2000 (TAKARA, Japan) to the 6-well culture plates. Stably transfected MDA-MB-468 and MCF-7 cells were selected with puromycin (4 μg/ml and 3 μg/ml) for 48 h, and then the transfection efficiency was assessed using real-time reverse transcriptase polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) and western blot.

CCK-8

Logarithmic growth phase MDA-MB-468/MCF-7 cells were selected and seeded into 96-well culture plates. Following a 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h incubation, the cells were exposed to CCK-8 reagent (Solarbio, China) for 3 h. The optical density (OD) values were measured using a microplate reader (Biotek Instruments Inc., USA), and the proliferation curves were generated using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software.

Colony formation assay

One thousand re-suspended MDA-MB-468 and MCF-7 cells were seeded into 6-well plates containing culture medium for 2 weeks. Then, the cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet, and the colonies formed were counted using the ImageJ software.

Invasion and migration assay

A total of 2 × 104 MDA-MB-468/MCF-7 cells in serum-free medium suspension were grown in the upper compartment of a 24-well permeable plate with 8 μm pore size (Corning, USA). The upper compartments were coated with Matrigel (Corning, USA) for the invasion assay, while no coating was applied for the migration assay. The bottom chamber was filled with 600 μl of complete medium. After 48 h, the non-migrated cells in the upper chamber were carefully removed. The cells that migrated to the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained using a 0.1% crystal violet solution. Three fields of view were captured for each sample, the migrated cells in each field were quantified using ImageJ software, and the average value was calculated.

RNA sequencing analysis and gene set enrichment analysis

MDA-MB-468 cells transfected with sh-B3GALT4 or Scramble were sent to Novogene Bioinformatics Technology (Beijing, China) for RNA sequencing. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was conducted on the obtained data. Enriched gene sets with a false discovery rate (FDR) below 0.25 and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All bioinformatics and statistical analyses were performed using The R software (version 3.3.2).

Examination of GFP-LC3B punctuation

Cells transfected with sh-B3GALT4 or Scramble were cultured until they reached approximately 70% confluence and then transfected with Ad-GFP-LC3B (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer’s instructions. The Ad-GFP-LC3B-transduced MDA-MB-468/MCF-7 cells were subsequently treated with chloroquine (CQ) (10 μM) for a duration of 24 h. After the treatment, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. The formation of LC3B puncta was observed using a confocal microscope (Zeiss LSM880, Germany). Confocal analysis of the images was performed using ImageJ software.

Electron microscope

MDA-MB-468/MCF-7 cells transfected with sh-B3GALT4 or Scramble were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with CQ (10 μM) for 24 h. A rubber spatula was used to collect the cells, which were then fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde and 1% osmotic acid for 2 h. Following washing, the cell pellets were dehydrated and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrathin sections (45 nm) were obtained and stained with lead acetate. Finally, the sections were examined using a JEM-2000EX electron microscope.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), transcribed to cDNA using a commercial reverse transcription supermix (Bimake, USA), and quantified by qRT-PCR using SYBR The 2xSG Fast qPCR Master Mix (Sangon Biotech, China) on the Bio-Rad CFX96 machine following manufacturer’s protocols. For normalization of mRNA levels, GAPDH was used as an internal control. The primer sequences for B3GALT4 were as follows: Forward: 5′-CACCCTCCCCCATATCAGGA-3′, Reverse: 5′-GTTTCGGGTGCTGGAGTACA-3′, and the primer sequences for GAPDH were as follows: Forward: 5′-GGTATGACAACGAATTTGGC-3′, Reverse: 5′-GAGCACAGGGTACTTTATTG-3′.

Western blot

Cells, tumor samples, and matched normal breast specimens were lysed for 30 min on ice using RIPA buffer. After centrifugation at 12,000×g at 4 °C for 20 min, the protein concentration in the supernatant was determined using the BCA assay. Then, the proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes, blocked with 5% skim milk for 1 h at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Table S2). The following day, the membranes were washed and incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Visualization of protein bands was performed using the GelView 6000Plus system, and the band images were quantified using ImageJ software. β-Actin was used as an internal control for normalization. The result of gels images was cropped and original blots with membrane edges visible were included in the Supplementary Materials. In reference to the lack of sufficient length original blots, we offered the following reasons: (1) The membranes were sliced before the antibodies were hybridized since some of the proteins have different molecular weights. (2) We employed the Bio-Rad imaging system to do blot scanning; however, the scanning parameters resulted in blot margins that were narrower than the original bands, which prevented the edge areas of the incomplete bands from being fully visible. We assure that the samples derive from the same experiment.

Establishment of a xenograft model

All animal experiments were conducted following the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tianjin Medical University Cancer Institute and Hospital (AE-2021112). BALB/c nude mice (aged 4 weeks) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China) and were housed in pathogen-free environments. To evaluate the anti-tumor effects of B3GALT4 in the breast cancer xenograft model, MDA-MB-468 cells transfected with sh-B3GALT4 or Scramble (1 × 106/100 μl PBS per mouse) were injected into the back of each mouse. Tumor volumes were measured every 3 days for 4 weeks using the formula (length × width2)/2. Cervical dislocation (following anesthesia with intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital sodium at a dose of 40 mg/kg) was used as the method of euthanasia. The tumors were collected, weighed, and further examined. A portion of the newly formed tumors was used for flow cytometry analysis, while another section was fixed in formalin for immunohistochemical staining. Each treatment group consisted of five mice to assess changes in the tumor phenotype. The maximal tumor size/burden permitted by our ethics committee review board was 2000 mm3. We confirm that none of the mice included in this study exceeded this limit.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Histological sections of 4 μm thickness were deparaffinized and dehydrated after overnight baking at 60 °C. Antigen retrieval and endogenous peroxidase inhibition steps were performed. Subsequently, the sections were incubated with anti-Ki-67 (1:200, Abcam, ab16667), anti-B3GALT4 (1:100, Abcam, ab169759) primary antibodies for 24 h at 4 °C in a moist container. Afterward, the sections were incubated with corresponding secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Diaminobenzidine (DAB, ZSGB-BIO) was used as a chromogen for 5 min. Following thorough washing, the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, and coverslipped. Five randomly selected fields of view were examined at low magnification using a light microscope. The IHC results were evaluated by two independent pathologists blinded to the experimental methods. The frequency of positive cells was classified into five categories: 0 (no positive cells), 1 (1–25%), 2 (26–50%), 3 (51–75%), and 4 (76–100%). The staining intensity was assessed and categorized as follows: 0 (none), 1 (weak), 2 (moderate), and 3 (strong). The IHC scores for B3GALT4 was calculated by multiplying the preceding two scores. The number of Ki-67 positive cells were quantified in 400× magnification images of tumor tissues.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed in triplicate, while the in vivo experiments were repeated five times. Data analysis and graphing were conducted using Prism 8.0 software (Graph Pad Software Inc.). Quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. Data shown are the mean ± SEM from multiple experiments, as indicated in figure legends. Median OS and DMFS were evaluated using the Kaplan–Meier curve estimator. The logrank test was used to compare median OS and DMFS. Statistical differences between two groups were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t-test. For comparisons involving multiple groups, one-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni test was employed. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. The p-values in the figures are represented by the following symbols: *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001. At least three times were done in each experiment.

According to study of the BCGenExMiner database, B3GALT4 mRNA expression level in breast cancer tissues was notably higher than that of paracancerous tissues and normal breast tissues (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2B). The findings demonstrated a significant upregulation of B3GALT4 in the matched breast cancer samples (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2D, E). Silencing of B3GALT4 induces autophagy in breast cancer cells

We conducted high-throughput sequencing analysis and employed bioinformatics methods to analyze the results. Differential genes were screened and imported into the STRING database to plot the corresponding PPI (Fig. 8A). Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed a significant enrichment of genes involved in lysosome, lysosomal lumen, and endomembrance system (Fig. 8B), suggesting that B3GALT4 may regulate autophagy in breast cancer cells. To further investigate the effect of B3GALT4 on autophagy in breast cancer cells, we examined the expression of autophagy-related proteins using the western blot assay. The results demonstrated that knockdown of B3GALT4 significantly increased the protein expression levels of Beclin1 and LC3BII, while decreasing the protein expression of p62 (Fig. 8C). Moreover, we assessed the presence of autophagosomes in B3GALT4-knockdown breast cancer cells. The findings showed that knockdown of B3GALT4 led to elevated GFP-LC3B fluorescence and the production of autophagosomes, as observed through transmission electron microscopy (Fig. 8D, E). To clarify the effect of B3GALT4 on actual autophagic flux, CQ (10 μM), was administered. CQ is known to inhibit autophagy at the late stage by preventing autophagosome-lysosome fusion and increasing lysosomal pH, thereby impairing protein degradation. Therefore, we investigated whether the inhibition of autophagy would rescue the knockdown of B3GALT4-induced autophagy. Western blot results showed that CQ significantly increased the expression of p62 and LC3BII, while reducing the expression of Beclin1 in B3GALT4-knockdown cells, suggesting that CQ blocked knockdown of B3GALT4-induced autophagic flux (Fig. 8C). Similarly, CQ elevated the expression of LC3BII (Fig. 8D) and increased autophagosome production (Fig. 8E) in B3GALT4-knockdown cells. Taken together, these findings indicate that knockdown of B3GALT4 could induce autophagy in breast cancer cells.

B3GALT4 activates the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway

To understand the regulatory mechanism of autophagy induced by B3GALT4, we conducted RNA-seq GSEA analysis, which revealed enrichment of genes involved in the PI3K/AKT/mTOR and mTORC3 pathways in B3GALT4-knockdown group (Fig. 9A). It is well-established that the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is closely associated with autophagy. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that knockdown of B3GALT4 could stimulate autophagy in breast cancer cells through the AKT/mTOR pathway. Discussion

Breast cancer is the malignant tumour with the high morbidity and mortality rate in women, and although multiple therapeutic approaches have improved the 5-year survival rate of patients to a certain extent, the late-stage breast cancer is prone to recurrence and metastasis. Therefore, it is urgent to explore the biological mechanisms of breast cancer occurrence and development and to identify the underlying molecular targets. In the current study, we observed that there was high expression of B3GALT4 in breast cancer tissues. In addition, we confirmed that down-regulation of B3GALT4 inhibited breast cancer progression and that B3GALT4 regulated autophagy in breast cancer via regulating the AKT/mTOR pathway.

B3GALT4 belongs to the β1,3galactosyltransferase gene family, which transfers a galactose to the N-acetylgalactosamine residue of GM2/GD2 with a β1-3 linkage [11]. In our previous study, we found that B3GALT4 was downregulated in neuroblastoma tissues and loss of B3GALT4 promoted neuroblastoma progression in vitro and in vivo [12, 13]. However, it is less clear the expression of B3GALT4 in adult tumour tissues. In this study, we performed a pan-cancer analysis of B3GALT4 expression in the TCGA database. Our findings revealed that B3GALT4 was differentially expressed in most normal and tumour tissues, suggesting that B3GALT4 might play an important role in tumour progression. Ma et al. [4] reported that overexpression of B3GALT4 induced ganglioside GM1 expression in breast cancer cells, which promoted the epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) process. In this study, the data revealed a significant increase in the protein and mRNA expression levels of B3GALT4 in breast cancer cells compared to normal breast tissues by bioinformatics and clinical samples analysis. Furthermore, we investigated the relationships between B3GALT4 expression levels and pathological characteristics of breast cancer. The results showed that B3GALT4 mRNA expression was obviously higher in ER-positive, PR-positive, not-TNBC, wild-type BRCA1, not-basal-like, high-stage breast cancer patients. Subsequently, survival study revealed that elevated B3GALT4 levels were related with a better prognosis for breast cancer patients. Increasing researches have indicated that B3GALT4 could serve as biomarkers to predict patients’ prognosis [2, 3]. These results showed that B3GALT4 behaves as an oncogene, and overexpression of B3GALT4 might be a viable predictive biomarker in breast cancer.

Several studies have shown that dysregulated B3GALT4 is linked to malignant transformation in several malignancies. Ma et al. [4] reported that the upregulation of B3GALT4 can trigger the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) pathway in MCF-10A cells. Conversely, in neuroblastoma, B3GALT4 overexpression was shown to inhibit tumorigenesis [12]. In order to get a deeper understanding of the functional significance of B3GALT4 in breast cancer, we conducted many functional tests. Both in vitro and in vivo experiments shown that the suppression of B3GALT4 greatly impeded the proliferation, migration, and invasion of breast cancer cells in vitro, as well as the growth of tumors in vivo. Overexpression of B3GALT4 had a contrasting impact. These findings indicate that B3GALT4 might be a promising molecular target for breast cancer therapy.

In order to elucidate the regulation mechanism of B3GALT4, we conducted bioinformatics prediction analysis and RNA-seq. As a result, we detected the involvement of autophagy. Autophagy has emerged as a significant area of research in oncology, with its implications in tumor biology, immunology, and therapy [14–16]. It has been observed that autophagy can be activated in cancer cells, enabling cancer cells to survive [17]. A key feature of autophagy is the conversion of LC3-I to the lipidated LC3-II form, which is crucial for the generation of autophagosomes [18]. Additionally, p62 is a marker that indicates autophagic flow, and its levels are inversely correlated with autophagic activity [19]. In this study, we found that down-regulation of B3GALT4 induced autophagy in breast cancer cells both in vitro, as evidenced by the formation of vesicular organelles, upregulation of LC3-II levels, and inhibition of p62 levels. Notably, CQ effectively blocked down-regulation of B3GALT4-induced autophagy. The aforementioned discovery indicates that B3GALT4 may have significant impacts on tumor cells by triggering autophagy signals and controlling the pathophysiology of breast cancer. Therefore, targeting autophagy represents a promising strategy to improve the prognosis of high-risk breast cancer patients.

The PI3K/AKT signaling pathway is an important cancer cell pathway. Constitutive activation of the PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling pathway has been found in multiple human cancers, including breast cancer [20], ovarian cancer [21], and colorectal cancer [22]. This signaling pathway is essential in regulating tumor cell growth, metastasis, and apoptosis [23, 24]. We previously reported that B3GALT4 suppressed the progression of neuroblastoma by inhibiting the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway [12]. In addition, the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway is closely related to autophagy regulation [25]. Numerous reports have shown that autophagy was regulated by the AKT/mTOR signalling pathway, which regulated tumor progression [26–28]. In this study, we confirmed that B3GALT4 regulates the AKT/mTOR pathway in breast cancer cells. However, the effect of AKT/mTOR signal pathway on B3GALT4-induced autophagy in breast cancer cells needs to be further explored.

Despite the interesting findings reported, this present study still had several limitations. First, the molecular mechanism was investigated in mice models, which could not fully represent human cancer biology. Second, while we investigated the link between AKT/mTOR and autophagy, there may be additional underlying connections that require further exploration. Finally, our study focused on certain aspects of B3GALT4-induced autophagy, and there may be other factors and pathways involved that were not investigated in this study, which could provide potential directions for future research.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that B3GALT4 was clinically overexpressed in breast cancer tissues. Furthermore, our in vitro and in vivo experiments clearly confirmed knowdown of B3GALT4 inhibited breast cancer progression. Collectively, B3GALT4 played an important role in breast cancer and may be a potential therapeutic target for breast cancer.
