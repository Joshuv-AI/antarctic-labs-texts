Orthogonal relay system for efficient CO-to-ethanol electrosynthesis
Authors: Senlin Chu, Yanpu Niu, Libin Zeng, Xinyue Wang, Yifei Xu, Weixiao Lin, Zilin Zhao, Cheng-Jie Yang, Xiahan Sang, Cheng Lian, Bin Yang, Zhongjian Li, Chung-Li Dong, Lecheng Lei, Bingjun Xu, Yang Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe electrosynthesis of ethanol from carbon monoxide (CO) can potentially provide low-carbon-intensity fuels and platform chemicals. However, the process performance is compromised by low selectivity, due to the predominant generation of competing products, especially ethylene. Here, we show an orthogonal relay catalysis mechanism that lowers the hydrogenation energy barrier while suppressing carbon-oxygen (C−O) cleavage. Atomic doping with lead electronically tunes the copper (Cu) catalyst to lower the hydrogenation barrier of the key intermediate *CH2COH, making ethanol formation feasible. An interfacial ionomer-catalyst heterojunction (IICH) creates partially desolvated cesium (Cs⁺) cations that selectively stabilize the C−O bond in *CH2COH, making ethanol formation selective. This mechanistic enhancement enables a 71.5% CO-to-ethanol Faradaic efficiency in flow cell. We demonstrate a membrane electrode assembly (MEA) electrolyzer achieving competitive ethanol production: 28.1% energy efficiency and 300 hours of stable operation. Scaled operation at 100 cm2 delivers a total current of 15 A with 39.8 mmol h−1 ethanol productivity, suggesting its potential for practical applications.Subject terms: Electrocatalysis, Chemical engineering, Electrocatalysis

Introduction

Ethanol, a vital industrial chemical with a global market exceeding $100 billion, is predominantly produced via biomass routes that generate over 1.7 kg CO2-equivalent emissions per kg product1–4. While CO2 electroreduction offers a sustainable alternative, its development in alkaline media is hindered by CO2 loss to carbonates and subsequent costly regeneration5–14. In contrast, CO electroreduction reaction (CORR) enables carbonate-free ethanol synthesis, emerging as a promising pathway for decarbonizing chemical manufacturing14–18. However, achieving high selectivity toward ethanol in CORR remains a formidable challenge, primarily due to an intrinsic kinetic and selectivity bottlenecks at the electrode-electrolyte interface2.

The core of this challenge lies in the inability of conventional catalytic systems to independently control two critical, yet competing, interfacial processes essential for ethanol formation: (i) efficiently reducing the hydrogenation barrier of key intermediates *CHxCOH (especially *CH2COH) to advance the ethanol pathway, and (ii) simultaneously stabilizing the carbon-oxygen (C−O) bond within *CH2COH to prevent its cleavage toward ethylene2,19,20. This dilemma results in fierce selectivity competition between ethylene and ethanol (Fig. 1a)21–25. Although alkali metal cations (AM⁺) are reported to participate in regulating key intermediate transformations, their strong solvation in aqueous electrolytes severely weakens interactions with adsorbed intermediates26–30. Crucially, relying solely on AM⁺ modulation fails to simultaneously achieve the dual functions of hydrogenation enhancement and C–O bond stabilization31.

To overcome this impasse, we propose and implement a coordinated “orthogonal relay catalysis” strategy (Fig. 1b). This approach decouples the complex reaction network into sequential, addressable bottlenecks, each tackled by a functionally specialized intervention operating in a logical relay. The implementation of this orthogonal relay strategy follows a deliberate “activate-then-steer” logic. In the first relay, atomic-level Pb doping modifies the electronic structure of the Cu catalyst to addresses the initial activity bottleneck by intrinsically lowering the hydrogenation barrier of the key intermediate *CH2COH on Cu, thereby activating the ethanol pathway. Further observations in density functional theory (DFT) calculations reveal that Pb doping concurrently reduces the energy barrier for the competing C–O cleavage in *CH2COH, which favors ethylene formation. Consequently, while the hydrogenation step is enabled, the product distribution remains poorly controlled, resulting in a limited ethanol Faradaic efficiency (FE) of only 33.9% for PbCu alloys alone Thus, the first relay transforms the challenge from kinetic stagnation into a selectivity problem at the *CH2COH bifurcation point.

The second relay component is designed to tackle this newly exposed selectivity bottleneck. Through the construction of an interfacial ionomer–catalyst heterojunction (IICH) and the use of weakly hydrated Cs⁺, we introduce an orthogonal mechanism within the interfacial microenvironment: partially desolvated Cs⁺ stabilizes the C–O bond in *CH2COH via specific ion–dipole interactions. This action selectively suppresses C–O cleavage without directly affecting hydrogenation steps, thereby steering the reaction toward ethanol. The sequence is causal: stabilizing the C–O bond on an inactive Cu surface would be ineffective, as the reaction would stall; the catalyst must first be activated before selectivity can be imposed.

Through the functional decoupling of activity modulation (catalyst surface) and selectivity control (electrode-electrolyte interface) via this orthogonal-relay design, the resulting PbCu-ionomer catalyst delivers competitive performance, achieving a CO-to-ethanol FE of 71.5% at −0.8 V vs. RHE in a flow-cell electrolyzer. Implementing the design in a membrane-electrode-assembly (MEA) electrolyzer enables efficient ethanol production with 65.1% FE, a record ethanol energy efficiency (EE) of 28.1%, and stable operation for over 300 h at 400 mA cm−2. Furthermore, scaling the MEA to a 100 cm2 area achieves a total current of 15 A while maintaining an ethanol FE of 56.9% and a production rate of 39.8 mmol h−1.

Results

Theoretical-guided design of CO-to-ethanol electrocatalysts

Building on the mechanistic framework for the orthogonal relay strategy, we began by optimizing the catalyst surface. To identify a metallic base capable of specifically lowering the hydrogenation barrier for the key *CH2COH intermediate, we screened a representative library of metal dopants (M = Zn, Al, In, Sn, Pb, Ag, and Pd)32 to experimentally identify those that favor the ethanol pathway (Supplementary Figs. 1–13 and Supplementary Table 1). As shown in Supplementary Fig. 14 and Supplementary Table 2, although all dopants modulate catalytic activity, their product selectivity differs markedly: Ag and Pd promote acetate, whereas Al and Zn enhance ethylene. The PbCu alloy emerged as the optimal substrate to fulfill this mechanistic requirement, delivering the highest ethanol FE of 33.9% together with a low H2 FE of 5.6% at −0.8 V vs. RHE (Fig. 1c and Supplementary Fig. 15).

We further performed in situ Raman spectroscopy on bare Cu and PbCu alloys electrodes across a range of applied potentials (Supplementary Figs. 16–22). Notably, a distinct Raman peak at ~380 cm−1, which is characteristic of the Cu–*CH2 stretching vibration33. The signal intensity of this *CH2-related peak on the PbCu alloys is higher than that on the bare Cu electrode, providing direct spectroscopic visualization that Pb doping intrinsically modifies the catalyst surface to effectively stabilize the *CH2 species (Supplementary Fig. 18). Furthermore, the results suggest that the PbCu alloys exhibits a stronger adsorption affinity for the *OCCOH intermediate than bare Cu does, consistent with its enhanced capacity to promote C–C coupling. DFT calculations suggest that Pb doping markedly alters the electronic structure of Cu, primarily by facilitating *CO activation and subsequent C–C coupling (Supplementary Figs. 23–28). Concurrently, this electronic modification attenuates hydrogen adsorption, as reflected in the higher free-energy barrier for water dissociation on the PbCu surface (1.27 eV) relative to bare Cu of 1.16 eV (Supplementary Fig. 29). The increased barrier provides a mechanistic explanation for the experimentally observed the suppression of the competing hydrogen evolution reaction (HER).

Subsequent DFT calculations map the free energy profiles for C2+ products formation on both bare Cu and PbCu alloys surfaces. While the fate of *CH2COH, whether undergoing hydrogenation to form *CH3COH or C−O bond cleavage, dictates the ethanol/ethylene selectivity bifurcation. The free energies of both *CH2COH and *CH3COH are lowered on the PbCu alloys relative to bare Cu (Fig. 1b). Moreover, the energy barrier for *CH2COH hydrogenation to *CH3COH is reduced on PbCu (ΔG = −0.23 eV), which is 0.13 eV lower than that for C–O bond scission to form *O and ethylene (C2H4), thereby thermodynamically favoring ethanol production (Fig. 1e). However, we note that PbCu also lowers the barrier for the competing C–O cleavage in *CH2COH, which promotes ethylene formation (Fig. 1e). Consequently, achieving higher ethanol selectivity requires the concurrent suppression of C–O bond cleavage.

We reasoned that further enhancement of ethanol selectivity requires precise control of the branching point between ethanol formation and competing pathways—especially ethylene generation. This necessitates stabilizing the C–O bond in the key intermediate *CH2COH to suppress its cleavage toward ethylene. To this end, we introduce second orthogonal relay within the interfacial microenvironment, distinct from the catalyst’s electronic structure through atomic-level Pb doping: partially desolvated alkali metal cations (AM+) selectively stabilize the C–O bond in *CH2COH via specific ion-dipole interactions. This approach aims to suppress the C–O cleavage pathway without directly affecting the hydrogenation steps, thereby steering selectivity at the critical bifurcation.

To identify the most effective cation, we evaluated CORR performance over a potential range of −0.7 to −1.2 V in 1.0 M electrolytes containing LiCl, NaCl, KCl, or CsCl. As shown in Fig. 2a and Supplementary Fig. 30, the product selectivity exhibits a dependence on cation size. In Li⁺-based electrolyte, ethylene formation dominated, reaching a maximum ethylene FE of 62.3% at −1.1 V, with the ethylene selectivity trend following Li⁺> Na⁺ > K⁺ > Cs⁺. In contrast, larger cations progressively enhanced ethanol production across the same potential window. The ethanol FE increased in the order Li⁺ < Na⁺ < K⁺ < Cs⁺, attaining a maximum of 33.9% in CsCl electrolyte at −0.8 V. Notably, the ethanol FE in CsCl was 2.4 times higher than that in LiCl at −0.8 V, suggesting the promoting effect of Cs⁺ in steering the reaction toward ethanol (Supplementary Fig. 31).

Fundamentally, larger AM⁺ (e.g., Cs⁺) exhibit weaker hydration energies, thereby facilitating partial desolvation. These cation-size-governed hydration shell fluctuations critically determine CORR selectivity pathways31,34,35. Specifically, we propose that the partially desolvated Cs⁺ strengthen ion-dipole interactions with the *CH2COH, which stabilizes its C−O bond and consequently promotes ethanol formation (Fig. 2b). To validate this mechanism, we performed projected crystal orbital Hamiltonian population (pCOHP) analyses under varying hydration states. The results discover that the IpCOHP (C−O) value was −2.77 eV in the presence of partially dehydrated Cs+(H2O), which is more negative compared to −2.54 eV for the more hydrated Li+(H2O). The fully desolvated Cs+ exhibited the most negative IpCOHP (C−O) value of −3.16 eV (Fig. 2c). Additionally, the C−O bond lengths in the Li+(H2O), Cs+(H2O), and Cs+ systems were 1.435 Å, 1.426 Å, and 1.410 Å (Supplementary Fig. 32), respectively. These calculations definitively demonstrate that complete Cs⁺ desolvation maximizes C−O bond stabilization in *CH2COH intermediates.

Interfacial designs for disrupting hydration structures of AM+

Inspired by the favorable effect of partially desolvated Cs+, we engineered an ionomer-modified catalyst surface to modulate the electric double layer (EDL) field strength and disrupt the solvation structures of AM⁺. This design aims to further stabilize the C–O bond in the *CH2COH intermediate through strengthened ion–dipole interactions. The ionomer was uniformly deposited across the catalyst layer on the gas-diffusion electrode, as confirmed by microscopic characterization (Supplementary Figs. 33 and 34). PbCu-ionomer catalysts modified with sulfonic acid ionomers (nafion/aquivion) demonstrated significantly enhanced ethanol selectivity in 1.0 M CsCl electrolyte during CORR testing (Supplementary Figs. 35 and 36). The ethanol FEs reached 60.7% and 51.6%, respectively, representing 1.8-fold and 1.5-fold improvements compared to the unmodified PbCu alloys (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Fig. 37). In contrast, the quaternary ammonium-based XA-9 ionomer modification reduced ethanol selectivity to 24.6% FE (Fig. 3a). We propose that sulfonic acid groups in nafion disrupt Cs+ solvation structures through EDL field modulation, as illustrated in Fig. 3b. Building on this mechanism, we systematically investigated nafion coating amount effects on PbCu for ethanol production during CORR. A volcano-shaped relationship was clearly observed between the nafion coating amount and ethanol FE (Supplementary Fig. 38). When the coating amount reached 40 μL cm−2, product ethanol achieved the highest FE of 60.7%, outperforming the unmodified PbCu alloys across all tested potentials. At nafion coating amounts of 80 μL and 100 μL cm−2, the ethanol FE decreased to 44.6% and 32.9%, respectively, while the FE for H2 increased to 5.4% and 13.1%, respectively. An overly thick coating layer (>40 μL cm−2) appears to hinder CORR performance by blocking catalytic active sites, thereby promoting competitive hydrogen evolution36. In Fig. 3c, PbCu-ionomer achieved a peak ethanol FE of 60.7%, much higher than those of bare Cu (FE of 16.2%) and PbCu alloys (FE of 33.9%). Also, we found that the FE(ethanol)/FE(C2H4) ratio decreased with increasing hydration strength of the AM+ ions in the electrolyte (Supplementary Fig. 39). The Cs⁺-containing electrolyte with weakest hydration strength showed the highest FE(ethanol)/FE(C2H4) ratio of 15, significantly exceeding the 0.2 observed in Li⁺-containing electrolyte (strongest hydration). The parameters of the CORR system could be further optimized to achieve a higher FE of ethanol (Supplementary Figs. 40–42). Cs+ concentration optimization revealed that 0.5 M CsCl achieves peak ethanol FE (71.5%, Fig. 3d). Conversely, elevated Cs⁺ concentrations increased local catalyst surface pH17,37–39, thereby promoting acetate formation at the expense of ethanol selectivity.

To validate that nafion disrupts the hydration structure of AM+ within the EDL, we performed ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) simulations. Specifically, these simulations quantified the coordination numbers between AM⁺ and oxygen atoms in their first-shell water molecules within the EDL of PbCu alloys and PbCu-ionomer systems (Fig. 4a, b, Supplementary Figs. 43–49). The results demonstrated that nafion modification reduced the coordination number of Li⁺ with oxygen atoms in first hydration shell compared to the pristine PbCu alloys system. This disruption trend was consistently observed for both K⁺ and Cs⁺ (Fig. 4c, d, Supplementary Fig. 47), further confirming that nafion disrupts the hydration structure of AM+ within the EDL. Moreover, in the PbCu-ionomer system, the shorter C−O bond length of the *CH2COH compared to the PbCu alloys system (Supplementary Fig. 48) provides additional evidence supporting the influence of the altered cation environment on intermediates. We investigated the correlation between applied potential and the extent of AM+ desolvation, as the PbCu-ionomer catalyst exhibited potential-dependent ethanol selectivity trends. To elucidate this relationship, we performed AIMD simulations to study the potential dependence of Cs+ solvation structure at the electrochemical interface. The simulation results offer molecular-level insights into the potential dependence of Cs⁺ desolvation that more negative potentials progressively promote cation desolvation. More importantly, these results indicate that desolvation approaches saturation at moderately cathodic potentials (approximately −0.2 V in the present model) (Supplementary Fig. 49). This finding carries an important implication for our experimental observations: across the main CORR potential window studied (−0.7 V to −1.2 V vs. RHE), the degree of Cs⁺ desolvation likely remains maximized and largely invariant. Consequently, the potential-dependent trend in ethanol FE within this range cannot be ascribed to further variations in Cs⁺ desolvation. Instead, it is governed primarily by the intrinsic activity of the PbCu alloy catalyst and the potential-sensitive kinetics of the surface electrochemical steps.

To elucidate the mechanistic link between disrupted Cs⁺ hydration structures and enhanced ethanol production during CORR, we performed in situ ATR-SEIRAS spectra on PbCu alloys and PbCu-ionomer systems (Supplementary Figs. 50 and 51). The spectra displayed absorption peaks at 1040, 1145, and 2090 cm−1, unambiguously assigned to key intermediates *COH, *OC2H5, and *CO, respectively (Fig. 4e, f)36. Notably, the absorption intensity of the *COH intermediate at 1040 cm−1 is substantially enhanced on the PbCu-ionomer compared to the pristine PbCu alloys. This direct spectroscopic visualization confirms that the tailored interfacial microenvironment effectively stabilizes the *COH. Critically, the peak intensity at 1145 cm−1, corresponding to the downstream ethanol-critical intermediate *OC2H53,36,40 was also significantly stronger on the PbCu-ionomer system than on pristine PbCu alloys. This pronounced enhancement of the *OC2H5 signal indicates that Cs⁺ with disrupted hydration structures stabilized the *CH2COH intermediate via ion-dipole interactions, facilitating the stability of its C−O bond. Consequently, the hydrogenation of *CH2COH to *OC2H5 is enhanced, thereby promoting the overall ethanol generation pathway during CORR.

The record performance of our integrated PbCu-nafion system stems from a deliberately engineered synergy, rather than the additive effect of three independent boosting strategies. This is critically underscored by control experiments designed to test the substitutability of its core components. For instance, replacing the Pb dopant with iodine—a known modifier of Cu’s hydrogenation properties—failed to “activate” the ethanol pathway on the Cu-I catalyst alone, which exhibited a product profile and a low ethanol FE of 14.5% similar to bare Cu (Supplementary Fig. 52). This confirms that the function of our first relay, Pb doping, is not a generic enhancement but the specific resolution of the initial kinetic bottleneck for ethanol formation (i.e., the hydrogenation of *CH2COH). Introducing the IICH to Cu-I (Cu-I-nafion) provided a measurable selectivity boost (34.6% FE), suggesting that the second relay, Cs+ & IICH, can steer available *CH2COH toward ethanol. However, this performance remained fundamentally limited by the inadequately “activated” catalyst base, highlighting a causal interdependence: the second relay, which operates orthogonally by stabilizing the C–O bond in *CH2COH, achieves its full potential only when paired with a catalyst that first delivers this intermediate to the critical branch point with high efficiency.

Therefore, the “orthogonal relay catalysis” presented here is not merely a conjunctive terminology but represents a validated, non-substitutable component synergy. Its power lies in the precise functional match and sequential logic between components, where Pb doping solves the activity bottleneck to make ethanol formation feasible, and the Cs⁺ & IICH interface subsequently solves the exposed selectivity bottleneck to make it dominant. This successful deconvolution and sequential optimization of orthogonal challenges offer a systems-engineering paradigm for complex electrocatalytic reaction design.

Ethanol electrosynthesis performance in MEA electrolyzer

We constructed a scalable two-compartment electrolyzer incorporating the PbCu-ionomer catalyst to assess the feasibility of industrial CO-to-ethanol electrosynthesis (Supplementary Fig. 53). At a full-cell voltage of 2.44 V, the system achieved a current density of 400 mA cm−2, with FEs of 65.1% for ethanol and 79.3% for C2+ alcohols (Fig. 5a, b). Notably, the EE, a critical metric for industrial and economic viability of CO-to-ethanol conversion, reached 28.1% at 400 mA cm−2 (without iR correction, ~25 °C) (Fig. 5c), exceeding the typical EE of less than 20% reported for ethanol-selective catalysts in MEA electrolyzer (Supplementary Table 3). Furthermore, the PbCu-ionomer-based MEA electrolyzer demonstrated stable operation for over 300 h at 400 mA cm−2, maintaining ethanol FE above 65% (Fig. 5d). The voltage remained very stable, increasing by only ~0.02 V over 300 h of continuous operation, which further underscores the exceptional stability of our system. Our electrolyzer was comprehensively benchmarked against the state-of-the-art MEA systems for ethanol synthesis, with key metrics—current density, FE, EE, and operational stability—quantitatively compared to literature values. Critically, this configuration achieved record-high FE and EE values at practically relevant ethanol production rates (Supplementary Table 3). Techno-economic analysis (TEA) based on MEA electrolyzer performance demonstrated the commercial viability of PbCu-ionomer technology, with estimated factory gate-levelized ethanol production costs below current market prices (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Figs. 54 and 55). Crucially, scaling the reaction area to 100 cm2 achieved 15 A total current while maintaining 56.9% FE for ethanol electrosynthesis, corresponding to a production rate of 39.8 mmol h−1. This represents a 33-fold output enhancement over the 1 cm2 benchmark system (1.2 mmol h−1) (Fig. 5e, f).

Discussion

In summary, we develop an orthogonal relay catalysis strategy to lower the hydrogenation barrier and suppress C−O cleavage, enabling efficient ethanol electrosynthesis. The hydrogenation barrier of *CH2COH is effectively reduced by electronically tuning the Cu catalyst through Pb doping, enabling feasible ethanol formation. The formation of IICH enables the selective production of ethanol through partially desolvated Cs⁺, which preferentially stabilize the C−O bond in the *CH2COH intermediate. We achieve a CO-to-ethanol FE of 71.5% and CO-to-C2+ alcohol FE of 81.6% in flow cell. The MEA electrolyzer demonstrates efficient ethanol production with a FE of 65.1%, an EE of 28.1%, and stable ethanol electrosynthesis over 300 h at 400 mA cm−2. Scaling the MEA electrolyzer area to 100 cm2 enables a total current of 15 A, achieving an ethanol FE of 56.9% and a production rate of 39.8 mmol h−1. TEA projects the PbCu-ionomer strategy achieves factory-gate levelized ethanol costs below market price. This finding will pave the way for modulating product selectivity in CORR via interface design principles.

Methods

Synthesis of PbCu-ionomer

The precursor of PbCuOx was prepared by using a simple hydrothermal method. In a typical synthesis, a specified amount of Pb(NO3)2 (Macklin, 99.9%) dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water and 1.20 g of Cu(NO3)2·3H2O (Macklin, 99.0%) were added to a round-bottomed flask. Then 50 mL of deionized water, treated with an ice water bath (≤4 °C), was added under magnetic stirring to form a uniform light blue solution. Subsequently, 0.67 g of KOH (Macklin, 85%) solution was added dropwise over 1 min, and the mixture was stirred for an additional 15 min. The mixture was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless-steel autoclave, which was sealed and maintained at 140 °C for 4 h. After naturally cooling to room temperature (25 °C), the resulting precipitate was collected by filtering and repeatedly washed using deionized water and ethanol at least four times and vacuum-dried at 60 °C for 10 hours to obtain a black solid. For comparison, CuO was synthesized under identical conditions but without the addition of Pb(NO3)2.

This PbCuOx precursor was then transferred onto gas diffusion layers to form a PbCuOx precursor electrode. The PbCu-ionomer electrodes were prepared by drop-casting a specified amount of nafion ink (Dupont, 5 wt.%) onto the PbCuOx precursor electrode. The coated electrode was subsequently dried under an infrared lamp. Finally, the PbCu-ionomer electrode was obtained by electroreducing the dried electrode in 1.0 M KCl solution at a current density of 100 mA cm⁻2 for 200 s.

Material characterization

The morphologies of the samples were characterized using field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM) (SU8010). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM) images of the samples were collected using a JEOL-2100F and a Tecnai G2 F20S-TWIN, respectively. The fine morphology of samples was obtained by high-angle annular dark field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM, Titan Cubed Themis G2 300). X-ray powder diffraction (XRD) measurements of the samples were performed using a MiniFlex600 with Cu-Kα radiation. A Varian VISTA-MPX inductively coupled plasma-atomic emission spectrometry (ICP-AES) instrument was utilized to determine the metal loadings. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements of the samples were recorded on a Perkin-Elmer PHI 5000CESCA system. The samples were evacuated in a load lock chamber and then transferred to the analysis chamber (10-9 mbar). The Cu K-edge and Pb K-edge XANES and EXAFS spectra were measured at the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (Hard branch of BL20U1) and the BL12B2 station in Japan. The energy resolution (DE/E) for incident X-ray photons was approximately 1.4 × 10−4 eV, achieved using a Si(111) double crystal monochromator.

Electrochemical measurements

To prepare the PbCuOx precursor electrode, 20 mg of a catalyst was suspended in a solution of isopropanol (1 mL), deionized water (1 mL), and nafion perfluorinated resin solution (5 wt.%, 20 μL), and then the mixture was subjected to bath ultrasonication for 2 h to form a catalyst slurry. 200 μL of catalyst ink was loaded onto gas-diffusion electrode (YLS-30T carbon paper) with an area of 1.0 cm2 and dried naturally to achieve PbCuOx precursor with loading amount of 2 mg cm−2.

CORR measurements were conducted in flow cell and MEA configurations via the electrochemical workstation (CHI 760E) and high-current amplifier (CHI 680D). In the flow cell setup, the catalyst-deposited gas-diffusion electrode (YLS-30T) served as the working electrode, while nickel foam was employed as the counter electrode. A cation-exchange membrane (Dupont, Nafion 117, 183 μm, 3 × 3 cm2) was utilized to separate the anodic and cathodic compartments. The membrane was treated in 5 wt.% H2O2 solution at 80 °C for 1 h to remove organic contaminants and metal ions. It is then immersed in deionized water for 30 min to rinse off residual H2O2, followed by treatment in 5 wt.% dilute H2SO4 solution at 80 °C for 1 h to exchange ions in the membrane with protons and activate proton conductivity. Finally, the membrane was immersed again in deionized water for 30 min. The flow cell assembly consisted of the gas-diffusion electrode, cation-exchange membrane, and nickel anode, with a 1.0 M CsCl solution (pH = 7.0 ± 0.2) acting as the electrolyte. The 1.0 M CsCl solution were prepared in deionized water at room temperature (25 °C) and used immediately. CO was introduced behind the gas-diffusion electrode using a mass flow controller, and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode (saturated KCl solution) was placed within the cathodic compartment for half-cell potential measurements. In situ SHINERS and ATR-FTIR characterizations

The Au@SiO2 nanoparticles (SHINs) were prepared as previously reported41. In situ shell-isolated nanoparticle-enhanced Raman spectroscopy (SHINERS) measurements were carried out using an Xplora plus Raman spectrometer (HORIBA Jobin Yvon). A 50 × magnification with a long distance of 8 mm objective was used. The excitation wavelength was 638 nm from a He-Ne laser. The Raman spectrometer was calibrated using a Si wafer. A custom-made spectroelectrochemical cell with a Pt wire counter electrode and an Ag/AgCl reference electrode was used.

The BRUKER INVENIO R spectrometer, equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled mercury cadmium telluride (MCT) detector, was utilized for in situ ATR-FTIR spectroscopy measurements. An Ag/AgCl electrode served as the reference electrode, while a Pt foil was employed as the counter electrode. During the collection of spectra, the optical path was continuously purged with nitrogen gas to minimize interference from water vapor and CO2 in the air. The background spectrum of the catalyst electrode was recorded at an open-circuit potential prior to each measurement. Then, the absorbance spectra (-log(R/R0)) at different potentials were collected at a spectral resolution of 8 cm−1.

Computational Method

DFT calculation

All the computations were carried out by the spin-polarized DFT method including van der Waals (vdW) corrections, as implemented in the Vienna ab initio Simulation Package (VASP)42–44. The generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew-Burke-Emzerhof (PBE) functional45 was employed to describe the exchange correlation energy. Projector augmented wave (PAW)46 methods were used for pseudopotentials. An energy cutoff of 450 eV was adopted for the plane-wave basis. In structural optimizations, Cu and Pb/Cu models were constructed in a 4 × 4 × 1 supercell, with a 40 Å vacuum layer along the z direction. The energy and force convergence criterion of geometry relaxation was set to 10−5 eV and 0.02 eV Å−1. The DFT-D3 method was used to describe the vdW interaction47.

AIMD calculation

All calculations were performed using the Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) with the projector-augmented wave (PAW) method. The Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange-correlation functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was employed, along with Grimme’s DFT-D3 dispersion correction to account for van der Waals interactions47,48. A plane-wave basis set with a cutoff energy of 400 eV was used, and electronic self-consistent iterations were converged to 1 × 10−5 eV. Ionic relaxations were performed until forces on free atoms dropped below 0.02 eV/Å. AIMD simulations were conducted in the canonical (NVT) ensemble using the Nose-Hoover thermostat at 300 K with a 1 fs time step49,50. To equilibrate the system, a 10 ps relaxation trajectory was generated.

It is important to note the inherent limitations of the present theoretical models. The DFT and AIMD simulations were performed under idealized conditions (e.g., specific model structures), which may not fully capture the complexity of the actual catalytic interface under operating conditions, such as dynamic structural reconstruction, the explicit solvent effects, and the influence of applied potential. Therefore, the computational results primarily provide mechanistic insights and hypotheses, which require support from and are interpreted in conjunction with experimental observations.
