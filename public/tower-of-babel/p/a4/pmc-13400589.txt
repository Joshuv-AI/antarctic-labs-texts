Behavioral and Structural Interventions for PrEP Adherence Among Young Female Sex Workers from Western Kenya
Authors: Kawango Agot, Domonique M Reed, Matthew R Lamb, Dan Omollo, Julie Franks, Jane Moraa, Joanne E Mantell, Allison Zerbe, Timothy Okello, Maria Lahuerta, Lubbe Wiesner, Doris Naitore, Marylyn Ochillo, Wafaa M El-Sadr
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHIV incidence is high among young female sex workers (YFSW) in Kenya. Pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) for HIV prevention is recommended for at-risk populations, but its effectiveness requires consistent access and adherence. The IPrEP study compared the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of two adherence support interventions on PrEP adherence among YFSW in Kisumu, Kenya. Study follow-up coincided with national restrictions on travel and gatherings due to the COVID-19 pandemic. We conducted an unblinded, randomized-controlled trial enrolling 18–24 year-old HIV-negative YFSW with no current or recent PrEP use (NCT03988387, registered: June 17, 2019). Participants were initiated on oral PrEP and randomized to either peer-delivered adherence support (PS), or SMS reminders and resource transfer (RRT) to support adherence for 12 months. PrEP was provided without these interventions for an additional 12 months to assess the durability of their effect. Intention-to-treat analysis of effectiveness was conducted at 12, 18, and 24 months using plasma tenofovir levels and self-report. Of the 289 YFSW screened, 200 were enrolled (100 per arm). Median age was 22 years, 46% reported condom use at last sex, and 26 male clients averaged in the past month. At 12, 18, and 24 months, detectable levels of tenofovir in plasma were 3%, 1%, and 0% in the PS arm and 9%, 9%, and 1% in the RRT arm (p-value = 0.4). Perfect 7-day adherence was self-reported by 85%, 81% and 83%, and 86%, 87% and 76% in the PS and RRT arms at 12, 18 and 24 months, respectively. Two seroconversions occurred during follow-up: one at 12 months and one at 18 months of follow-up. In this population of YFSW, no difference in tenofovir levels by arm was noted. The very low levels of tenofovir in the plasma, in contrast to high self-reported adherence, may be due in part to perceived lower HIV risk resulting from decreased frequency of sex work during the COVID-19 period. Findings highlight the urgent need for long-acting PrEP for this population.Keywords: Female sex workers, Kenya, Pre-exposure prophylaxis, Adherence

Background

Globally, sex workers face a ninefold increased risk of acquiring HIV compared to people in the general population [1, 2]. In eastern and southern Africa, where the majority of people living with HIV reside, female sex workers (FSW) represented 13% of all new infections in 2022 [1], although they make up only 1% of the female population aged 15 to 49 years [3]. This disproportionate burden is particularly acute in Kenya, where the incidence among FSW is estimated at 2.2% compared to just 0.2% among adult women. [4, 5]. HIV prevalence and incidence in Kenya are highest in western counties, such as Kisumu, where this study was conducted. [6] While HIV prevalence in Kisumu County reaches 18.7%, the prevalence is disproportionately concentrated among key populations, like FSW. [7] As such, effective strategies to improve uptake of and adherence to effective HIV prevention interventions, such as oral pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP), among FSW are urgently needed.

Although reliable HIV prevalence estimates among FSW disaggregated by age are lacking, research on adolescent girls and young women suggests that younger FSW (YFSW), under 25 years old, are particularly vulnerable due to a confluence of biological, behavioral, and structural risk factors for HIV, such as unequal power dynamics between YFSW and men who buy sex, engagement with multiple sexual partnerships with unknown status, elevated risk for gender-based violence, and inadequate legal protection [6, 7, 8, 9]. As such, YFSW require HIV programming that responds to their complex lived experiences. Despite PrEP’s established effectiveness and the substantially elevated risk in this population, several studies have reported suboptimal adherence to oral PrEP among young women overall and among FSW [10, 11], including in Kenya [12, 13]. This may be due to the fact that current interventions designed for YFSW do not fully address the adherence challenges reported by this group, such as busy schedules, running out of pills, and reduced risk perception stemming from sporadic or short-term involvement in sex work [14]. A growing body of evidence indicates that peer support interventions can significantly improve PrEP adherence among FSW [15, 16–19]. Peer support leverages social learning, role modeling, and self-efficacy support, and promotes adaptive coping strategies to overcome potential challenges to adherence. Regular SMS reminders have been studied to support adherence to HIV treatment and, more recently, PrEP, as have conditional economic incentives to reinforce and build motivation for PrEP adherence [20]; Both approaches may therefore be effective support measures for FSW.

Optimizing PrEP as an HIV prevention strategy for YFSW, requires investigation of key research questions, including how to reduce PrEP-related challenges to adherence and strengthen social support for PrEP use [21, 22]. To support PrEP adherence among YFSW, we compared the feasibility, acceptability, and effectiveness of two adherence support interventions: peer support (PS) and SMS reminders plus resource transfer (RRT) in Kisumu, a Lake Victoria urban area with one of the highest HIV burdens in Kenya [5].

Methods

Study Design

We conducted the IPrEP study, an unblinded randomized-controlled trial among HIV-negative YFSW, randomized 1:1 to receive either oral PrEP with emtricitabine/tenofovir (FTC/TDF) plus intensive PS, or FTC/TDF plus RRT. The study enrolled and randomized participants at baseline (BL), and then compared PrEP adherence between PS and RRT arms at 12 months, and adherence durability at 18 and 24 months after these adherence support interventions were withdrawn. During the follow-up period after the interventions were withdrawn, participants received standard of care support for PrEP adherence, per the national guidelines as described below [23]. Procedures

Eligibility, Recruitment, and Enrollment

Eligibility for recruitment included being cisgender female; aged 18–24 years, HIV negative, living or working in Kisumu County with no intention of moving out for the next 24 months of follow-up, exchanging vaginal or anal sex with men for goods, money, favors or other services in the previous 3 months, having no current or recent (i.e., within the past 3 months) PrEP use [24], willingness to be randomized and adhere to study procedures, and willingness and competence to provide written informed consent for study participation. We have excluded females below the age of 18, as in Kenya, people below the age of 18 are defined as minors and therefore, they are considered sexually exploited children.

We used respondent-driven sampling to recruit participants. Six YFSW aged 18–24 years were recruited by study staff to serve as “seeds” (primary recruiters) and invited to use coupons to refer YFSW within their social networks for potential study participation. Seeds were purposively selected to represent salient characteristics of the study population, including community of residence and sex venue in the study catchment area. Each seed received three coded referral coupons that included study contact information and the words: “Please bring this coupon to see if you are eligible to participate in IPrEP.

If eligible, you may receive: HIV testing, follow-up services, and a cash incentive for participation.” Seeds were counseled to offer a card to three known peers whom they thought could be eligible for the study, i.e., YFSW friends or acquaintances who were not known to the seed as HIV-positive or currently taking PrEP. Seeds were then reimbursed the equivalent of $2 US dollars for each person successfully referred and screened. Subsequent study participants were offered the same number of study referral coupons and the same incentive to recruit members of their FSW networks. Enrolment was completed when the required sample size of 200 YFSW was enrolled and randomly allocated to the two study adherence intervention arms.

All study procedures took place in the offices of Impact Research and Development Organization (IRDO), a well-established community-based center that delivers HIV prevention and reproductive health services as well as implements high quality public health research (with dedicated clincal research space, short stay beds, and on-site laboratory services). Research assistants fluent in English, Kiswahili, and Dholuo screened prospective participants for eligibility and obtained written consent for enrolment in their language of choice. Participants were then counselled and tested for HIV per Kenya Ministry of Health guidelines [25]. Those with HIV-negative test results were screened for PrEP eligibility following national guidelines [23], and if PrEP-eligible, were administered a baseline interview, then randomized 1:1 to either PS or RRT intervention arms and prescribed once-daily PrEP. All randomized participants received a one-month supply of daily oral PrEP for the first month after enrollment and a three-month supply every three months thereafter.

PrEP Service Provision

Clinical services were provided in the IRDO clinical offices adjacent to data collection rooms. At enrollment and every follow-up visit, participants received standardized clinical assessments and HIV prevention services consistent with national guidelines.

Study Assessments

Over the 24-month study period, participants underwent post-enrollment study visits at months 1, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18, 21, and 24. At each visit, participants received HIV testing per national guidelines for PrEP use, as well as HIV prevention counseling and provision of condoms and screening for adverse events or symptoms of acute HIV infection. Blood and urine specimens were collected for testing for sexually transmitted infections, pregnancy and HIV infection, and a focused clinical examination. Women who became pregnant during the study were referred for local antenatal care, offered the opportunity to continue PrEP and followed as per usual visit schedule. Participants who tested HIV-positive were immediately linked to HIV care for follow-up; PrEP was discontinued for such participants. After the interventions were withdrawn at month 12, during the remainder of the follow-up visits, participants received standard of care PrEP adherence counselling per the national guidelines, which involves HIV testing, adherence counselling, and clinical monitoring [23].

A face-to-face interview was conducted at every follow up visit, eliciting depressive symptoms measured through the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) [26], which assesses the degree of depression severity; a score of at least 4 indicates mild depression, and the higher the score the more severe the depression, mobility; contraceptive use; alcohol and drug use; socio-economic stressors; perceived risk for HIV; and sex work history and characteristics with paying clientele, regular non-paying partners, and causal non-paying partners. A 10mL blood sample was taken at all follow-up visits to measure plasma tenofovir drug levels, with samples batched and assayed at the end of the study. Serum creatinine was assessed at 12 and 24 months, following national guidelines. In addition, hepatitis B surface antigen testing was conducted at baseline. As per Kenya’s standard of care guidelines, contraceptives were offered at all visits, and participants who became pregnant were referred for antenatal services at a health facility of their choice. Participants received compensation in Kenyan shillings equivalent to $5 USD for their time and effort.

Adherence Support Interventions

PrEP Dispensing

All participants received a one-month supply of daily oral PrEP (emtricitabine 200 mg/tenofovir 300 mg; FTC/TDF) for the first month of enrollment and were instructed to take one tablet orally daily with or without food. At the month 1 visit and then every follow-up visit thereafter, a 3-month supply of TDF/FTC was provided for the 24-month duration of the study, after assessment for acute HIV infection or drug toxicity. Continuous access to PrEP was maintained through a study-specific supply of medication, stored and dispensed directly by the research team at IRDO, independent of routine health services. As a result, PrEP provision was not affected by health system disruptions such as stock-outs or COVID-19–related service closures at standard-of-care clinics.

Peer Support

YFSW randomized to the PS arm were assigned a FSW peer supporter recruited by the study staff from FSW advocacy and service organizations. The peer supporters were trained on their roles in the study, including how to assess facilitators and barriers to PrEP adherence, conduct adherence counselling, and provide ongoing social and practical support (e.g., referrals to needed services and tailored appointment reminders). They were required to communicate with their assigned participants at least once a week, either in person or by phone, and to track those who missed study visits. Participants were encouraged to call in if they had any questions or concerns. Peer supporters received regular supervision and coaching from senior study staff to ensure fidelity with study procedures.

Reminders and Resource Transfer

YFSW randomized to the RRT arm received weekly SMS texts (or voice messages for low-literacy participants who consented to receive a call) to encourage healthy behaviors, study engagement, and PrEP adherence. Messages were not specific to HIV or PrEP, for example, “Remember to look after yourself today” and “See you tomorrow,” and were shared with RRT participants following randomization. Participants were asked to respond to the message with a brief answer. They were compensated an equivalent of $0.85 USD per response, sent directly to their phone number through mobile cash transfer. To ensure the message and money transfer went to the participant’s own or trusted telephone, participants were asked to provide primary and alternate contact telephone numbers for receiving and sending texts.

Duration of Adherence Support

Both adherence interventions were delivered in conjunction with PrEP over the first 12 months of study participation. Thereafter, the interventions were withdrawn, and PrEP was continued for an additional 12 months with standard support as per national guidelines (described above; Fig. 1), including counseling on HIV risk-reduction, routine PrEP adherence counseling, and provision of condoms and lubricants [23]. The additional 12-month period was intended to assess the durability of the adherence interventions’ effect after delivery.

Adherence

Tenofovir Measurement

Tenofovir was measured at 12-, 18-, and 24-month visits. Plasma tenofovir concentrations were determined with a validated liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry assay developed at the Division of Clinical Pharmacology, University of Cape Town. The method utilized plasma protein precipitation, followed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with tandem mass spectrometry (MS/MS) detection. HPLC separation was achieved on a Waters Atlantis T3 column (2.1 mm x 100 mm, 3 μm) with a total runtime of 6 min. A Sciex 5500 Qtrap mass spectrometer at unit resolution in the multiple reaction monitoring mode was used to monitor the transition of the protonated precursor ions 288.1 and 294.1 to the product ions 176.1 and 182.1 for tenofovir and tenofovir-d6 (internal standard), respectively. Electrospray ionisation was used for ion production. The calibration curves fitted quadratic (weighted by 1/concentration) regressions based on peak area ratios over the range of 0.500 to 300 ng/mL. The combined accuracy (%Nom) of the limit of quantification, low, medium, and high-quality controls (3 validation batches, N=18) of tenofovir were between 93.8% and 105.1%, with precision (%CV) less than 13%. However, as plasma tenofovir reflects recent drug ingestion within approximately 24–48 h, these measurements provide only a short-term snapshot of adherence and may not capture longer-term dosing patterns [27]. While the reliance on plasma tenofovir concentrations limits our ability to determine cumulative adherence, prior work shows that plasma tenofovir levels are nonetheless strongly correlated with adherence patterns over preceding weeks and thus afford a meaningful proxy of recent PrEP use [27, 28].

Self-reported Adherence

We defined adherence based on self-report using an adherence score of 90 or higher on Wilson’s three-item adherence measure [29] as many people find it difficult to take their pills every day for last seven days. We asked: How has it been taking PrEP? Would you say, ‘very poor’, ‘poor’, ‘fair’,’ good’, ‘very good’, or ‘excellent’?; Since your last visit, how many days did you miss taking your PrEP medication?; and Since your last visit, how often did you take PrEP in the way you were instructed to? [30].

Statistical Analysis

For primary outcomes, we conducted an intention-to-treat analysis to assess differences in measures of PrEP adherence at months 12, 18, and 24 follow-up visits. Adherence to PrEP was assessed in two ways, measuring tenofovir levels in plasma and self-reported adherence in past seven days. For our primary outcome analysis, adherence to PrEP was based on a detectable concentration of plasma tenofovir, which indicates ingestion of PrEP medication within two or three days prior to sample collection. In secondary analyses, self-report adherence was assessed at months 12, 18, and 24. We also examined the change in adherence over time using the Cochran-Armitage test for trend. Additionally, we conducted two sensitivity analyses to assess the impact of loss to follow-up on our measures of the relationship between study arm and adherence.

For the first sensitivity analyses, we used inverse probability weighting (IPW) with stabilized weights to correct for potential selection bias due to loss to follow-up (LTFU). We chose IPW because it allows us to re-weight the observed data captured during follow-up to reflect the baseline study population, thereby reducing bias that may arise due to participants who were lost to follow-up and different systematically from those who remained in the study. We assumed that individuals’ LTFU had similar outcomes within strata of variables predicting LTFU as the outcome value in the retained population (i.e., missing at random assumption). To build our weights, we first constructed a model to predict the likelihood of being retained in the study as a function of measured variables, and then weighted by the inverse of this probability to obtain an estimate of our outcomes of interest in a population had there been no loss to follow-up. For our second sensitivity analysis, we conducted a complete cases analysis of all participants who completed baseline, 12-month, 18-month, and 24-month visits. All analyses were conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS, Cary, NC). We used descriptive statistics to characterize baseline demographic and sexual behaviour characteristics of study participants.

Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Maseno University Scientific and Ethics Review Committee in Kenya and the Columbia University Irving Medical Center Institutional Review Board in the US, and registered with clinicaltrials.gov (NCT03988387). Study staff were trained in good clinical practice and standard operating procedures. Prior to being enrolled, all participants provided written informed consent.

Results

Of 289 individuals screened for eligibility, 89 were excluded from enrollment. Of the 89, 77 (87%) were excluded as they did not fulfill study eligibility criteria. Of the 212 eligible YFSW, 12 declined to participate and 200 (94%) were enrolled in the study (see CONSORT Fig 2).

Discussion

This study of adherence with daily oral PrEP for HIV prevention among YFSW in Kenya compared two adherence support interventions, PS and RRT, and found no difference in adherence. While self-reported adherence to the daily PrEP dosing in both arms averaged 83% (76–87%) over a 24-month follow-up period, tenofovir detection in plasma over the same period showed a considerably lower adherence, averaging only 3.8% (0–9%). The low adherence observed at 12 months persisted during 12 months of post-intervention follow-up at 18 and 24 months with the use of standard of care adherence support as per the national PrEP guidelines [23].

In another study conducted in Kenya more than a decade earlier to assess the efficacy of daily oral PrEP, low adherence as measured by blood tenofovir levels was also noted despite high adherence per self-report and pill count (26%, 95% and 88%, respectively) [30]. This low adherence was posited to be due the lack of evidence for PrEP efficacy among women at that time. Since then, numerous studies have demonstrated efficacy of PrEP for HIV prevention in multiple populations globally [14, 31–35], including in sub-Saharan Africa [36] but similar to our findings many of these studies reported low PrEP adherence as per objective measures, particularly among women [37]. In IPrEP, low adherence is especially concerning in light of two HIV seroconversions that occurred during follow-up, an indication of ongoing risk for HIV acquisition in the study population.

While discordance between self-reported and objective measures of adherence have been reported across many health conditions and in multiple PrEP studies [38], the large difference between tenofovir drug-level detection and self-reported adherence in IPrEP was notable. The study overcame limited awareness of and access to PrEP, structural barriers that can inhibit PrEP adherence [39–42], by providing education, counseling, and PrEP-related services. Low PrEP adherence can result from social-level barriers including stigma and negative narratives about PrEP at the community-level [43–46], limited decision-making capacity and lack of support from male partners [47–50], individual-level barriers such as forgetting to take PrEP daily [51, 52], fear of pill burden [53–55], perceived or experienced side effects [51, 53, 56, 57], low perceived HIV risk [55, 58], and doubt over the efficacy of PrEP [54, 59]. Despite innovative interventions, such as SMS reminders [60], peer group support [61], decision support tools [62], PrEP social marketing campaigns [63, 64] and drug-level feedback [63, 64], barriers still remain that inhibit people from persisting on and adhering with PrEP, particularly for the long-term. The availability of new products for PrEP, including the long-acting options like injectable cabotegravir, lenacapavir and the dapivirine ring, may enhance PrEP coverage, as individuals struggling to use one form of PrEP can switch to another [65–67]. Future qualitative research, including in-depth interviews with YFSW, could help elucidate the underlying reasons for the observed discordance between self-reported and biologically measured adherence. Such work could explore how perceptions of HIV risk, social desirability, stigma, partner dynamics, and lived experiences with PrEP influence reporting and use over time, thereby informing the design of more responsive adherence support interventions.

It should be noted that our study implementation coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic and its associated restrictions in movement and operation of entertainment venues in Kenya. It is possible that the very low levels of adherence we found could be partly explained by YFSW perceiving themselves to be at lower HIV risk due to the decrease in frequency of sex work that we noted during the study follow-up period (November 2019 – March 2022) [68]. Other studies have found low perceived risk for HIV to be associated with lower adherence to daily oral PrEP [69, 70]. COVID-19 restrictions instituted during study implementation closed entertainment venues where sex workers typically met clients; restricted domestic travel, including for long-haul truckers and salesmen; and enforced strict evening curfews, all factors that hindered sex work. Qualitative interviews could further clarify how COVID-19-related disruptions shaped participants’ risk perceptions, motivation for PrEP use, and engagement with prevention services during this period.

A key limitation of the IPrEP study, which was unfortunately outside the control of the study, is that the follow-up period coincided with the COVID-19 pandemic and the associated restrictions on sex work in Kenya. The study ensured that all study procedures were delivered during COVID-19 including participants’ access to PrEP medications and HIV self-testing. The study had several strengths, including the engagement of YFSW peers as adherence supporters.and a biologic assessment of PrEP adherence, rather than only relying on self-report which is prone to social desirability bias [71, 72]. Lastly, a critical limitation of this study is that since this study assessed adherence using plasma tenofovir levels, which reflect ingestion only in the preceding ~24–48 h rather than cumulative use, our ability to interpret longer-term adherence patterns is limited, which may have underestimated our adherence estimates [27]. Despite these limitations, there are several strengths to this study. First, it addresses a critical gap in HIV prevention research by focusing on YFSWs, a highly vulnerable and understudied population in Kenya. Second, the randomized controlled trial design strengthens the validity of its findings by enabling direct comparison between two adherence support strategies, without the influence of confounding beyond chance. Third, the study integrates both objective (plasma tenofovir levels) and subjective (self-reported) adherence measures, offering a nuanced understanding of adherence behaviors. Finally, its implementation during the COVID-19 pandemic provides valuable insights into how structural disruptions affect PrEP adherence, adding broader contextual relevance to the results.

Conclusions

Among YFSW at high risk for HIV acquisition, we found no difference in PrEP adherence between intervention arms, with objectively measured adherence remaining very low despite high self-reported adherence. This discordance likely reflects a combination of factors, including changes in sex work patterns and perceived HIV risk during the COVID-19 pandemic, as well as broader structural barriers to consistent access, including pandemic-related disruptions to sex work venues, mobility restrictions that limited access to outreach and clinic-based services, and economic instability that constrained engagement with HIV prevention services. These factors may also contribute to ongoing vulnerability to HIV acquisition, as evidenced by observed seroconversions during follow-up, underscoring a critical gap between daily PrEP requirements and real-world adherence challenges in this population. These findings have important implications for HIV prevention policy and practice. Long-acting PrEP agents, such as cabotegravir and lenacapavir, represent promising alternatives that overcome daily adherence barriers. Future interventions should prioritize offering YFSW a choice among multiple PrEP modalities and support flexible switching between options to optimize HIV prevention in this vulnerable population.
