Natural selection on apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) of an emerging zoonotic malaria parasite Plasmodium inui
Authors: Chaturong Putaporntip, Napaporn Kuamsab, Somchai Jongwutiwes
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) of malaria parasites plays an important role in host cell invasion. Antibodies to AMA1 can inhibit malaria merozoite invasion of erythrocytes while vaccine-induced specific cytotoxic T cell responses to this protein are associated with clinical protection. Polymorphisms in AMA1 of Plasmodium falciparum (PfAMA1) and P. vivax (PvAMA1) are of concern for vaccine development. To date, little is known about sequence diversity in ama1 of P. inui (Piama1), an emerging zoonotic malaria parasite. In this study, 80 complete Piama1 coding sequences were obtained from 57 macaques in Thailand that defined 60 haplotypes clustering in two phylogenetic lineages. In total, 74 nucleotide substitutions were identified and distributed unevenly across the gene. Blockwise analysis of the rates of synonymous (dS) and nonsynonymous (dN) nucleotide substitutions did not show a significant deviation from neutrality among Thai isolates. However, significantly negative Tajima’s D values were detected in domain I and the loop region of domain II, implying purifying selection. Codon-based analysis of dN/dS has identified 12 and 14 codons under positive and negative selections, respectively. Meanwhile, 85 amino acid substitutions were identified among 80 Thai and 11 non-Thai PiAMA1 sequences. Of these, 48 substituted residues had a significant alteration in physicochemical properties, suggesting positive selection. More than half of these positively selected amino acids (32 of 48) corresponded to the predicted B-cell or T-cell epitopes, suggesting that selective pressure could be mediated by host immunity. Importantly, 14 amino acid substitutions were singletons and predicted to be deleterious that could be subject to ongoing purifying selection or elimination. Besides genetic drift and natural selection, intragenic recombination identified in domain II could generate sequence variation in Piama1. It is likely that malarial ama1 exhibits interspecies differences in evolutionary histories. Knowledge of the sequence diversity of the Piama1 locus further provides an evolutionary perspective of this important malaria vaccine candidate.

Introduction

Several proteins on the surface of merozoites play crucial roles in a complex multi-step erythrocyte invasion process1. Of these, apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) is essential for the formation of the moving junction between the merozoite surface and the erythrocyte membrane by interaction with rhoptry neck 2 (RON2) proteins2. Furthermore, Plasmodium falciparum AMA1 (PfAMA1) plays a role during the invasion of sporozoites into hepatocytes3. Anti-AMA1 antibodies can inhibit merozoite invasion of erythrocytes4,5. Experimental immunizations of mice6,7 and monkeys8,9 with AMA1-derived immunogens have conferred protection against challenge infections. Naturally acquired anti-PfAMA1 antibodies elicit interference in merozoite invasion of erythrocytes in vitro10. Meta-analysis has supported that people in endemic areas who could mount antibodies to PfAMA1 have a tendency toward protection against subsequent infections11. Therefore, AMA1 is a promising target for malaria vaccine development.

AMA1 is synthesized in micronemes as an 83-kDa precursor protein and undergoes proteolytic processing to a 66-kDa fragment prior to relocalization onto the surfaces of mature merozoites and sporozoites3,12,13. AMA1 is an integral membrane protein with 8 disulfide bonds forming three ectodomains, designated domains I-III10. The gene encoding PfAMA1 comprises 622 codons in a continuous open reading frame. PfAMA1 exhibits extensive sequence variation among isolates, characterized by amino acid substitutions clustering mainly in the ectodomains, particularly in domain I14–16. Domains I and II are targets for invasion-inhibitory antibodies; therefore, polymorphism in these regions could be influenced by host immune pressure17,18. The ama1 gene of P. vivax (Pvama1) contains 562 codons that display extensive sequence diversity among isolates akin to Pfama119,20. Although the genes encoding Pfama1and Pvama1 share 55.4% sequence identity, both proteins exhibit general conservation in structural organization, particularly the cysteine residues that form intramolecular disulfide bridges18. Importantly, the highly conserved loop region in domain II of PfAMA1 and PvAMA1 forms a hydrophobic cleft that could serve as a receptor pocket while it is also a target for invasion-inhibitory antibodies5,21.

During the recent decades, human zoonotic malaria has emerged amid a decline in an overall malaria incidence in Thailand22,23. An increasing number of zoonotic malaria cases may complicate malaria elimination because macaque reservoir hosts are not targeted by measures to control human malaria while simian malaria vectors seem to differ from those transmitting human malaria species24. In Thailand, P. inui and P. fieldi have recently emerged as zoonotic malaria species, in addition to P. knowlesi and P. cynomolgi25–27. All these simian malaria parasites are phylogenetically related to P. vivax28,29. Although the ama1 gene of P. knowlesi (Pkama1) has been analyzed30–32, little is known about sequence diversity at this locus of other P. vivax-related malaria species.

In this study, we analyzed the complete coding sequences of P. inui ama1 (Piama1), the most prevalent malaria parasite circulating among long-tailed and pig-tailed macaques in Thailand25,26,33. Results revealed microheterogeneity in the Piama1sequences, particularly in the ectodomain-encoding regions. In silico analysis has suggested that natural selection and intragenic recombination influenced sequence variation at this locus. Most of the positively selected amino acids in PiAMA1 corresponded to residues predicted to be B-cell and/or T-cell epitopes, suggesting that natural selection could be mediated by host immune responses.

Results

Sequence variation in PiAMA1

The 1.9-kb amplicons corresponding to the expected length of PiAMA1 were obtained from 57 P. inui-infected macaque blood samples. No size variation was observed on agarose gel electrophoresis. Direct sequencing of the purified amplicons showed clear and non-superimposed signals of the electropherograms in 41 isolates. The remaining 16 isolates with superimposed electropherograms were subject to plasmid cloning and sequencing. Of these, 10, 5 and 1 isolates possessed 2, 3 and 4 distinct Piama1 clones, respectively. In total, 80 Piama1 sequences were obtained in this study consisting of 74 nucleotide substitutions that defined 60 distinct haplotypes (Table 1). These mutations rendered 37 amino acid substitutions, resulting in 43 amino acid haplotypes among Thai clones/isolates (Fig. 1). Nucleotide diversity and its standard error for Thai isolates was 0.0044 ± 0.0008 while haplotype diversity and its standard deviation was 0.978 ± 0.010. Meanwhile, the 11 previously reported Piama1 clones/isolates from elsewhere (non-Thai isolates) exhibited distinct haplotypes with 128 nucleotide substitutions. Of 91 sequences including Thai and non-Thai clones/isolates, 85 amino acid substitutions were found, resulting in 71 amino acid haplotypes (Fig. 1). Blockwise analysis of natural selection

The Piama1 domains were assigned based on the sequence homology corresponding to those of Pvama118,21. Domain II was further partitioned into the loop region and the remainder of the domain akin to Pvama120. The number of nucleotide substitutions per site (d) was variable across Piama1 which were more pronounced in the transmembrane domain and domain II excluding the loop region (Table 1). For domain II as a whole, dS was less than dN albeit not significantly different (p = 0.62). In the loop region, dS exceeded dN but the difference was not statistically meaningful (p = 0.51). Likewise, dS outnumbered dN in domains I, III and cytoplasmic tail. However, the significant difference between dS and dN was observed only in the cytoplasmic domain of non-Thai clones/isolates (p = 0.03)(Table 1). It is noteworthy that the magnitudes of d, dS, and dN for each domain of non-Thai clones/isolates were greater than or significantly outnumbered the corresponding values of Thai clones/isolates (p < 0.05). Meanwhile, Tajima’s test for 80 Thai taxa yielded a significantly negative D value when the entire coding sequences were analyzed (p = 0.01). Blockwise analysis has shown that all D values were negatives. However, significant levels of Tajima’s statistics were observed in domain I and in the loop region of domain II (p = 0.001 and < 0.0001, respectively) (Table 1).

Codon-based analysis of neutrality

To determine whether deviation from neutrality could affect individual codons rather than particular domains in the protein, a Fast, Unconstrained Bayesian AppRoximation (FUBAR) method was deployed for analysis34. For Thai and non-Thai clones/isolates excluding possible recombination events among these sequences, 12 positively selected codons were identified, comprising 1, 2, 6 and 3 codons in the regions encoding signal peptide, domains I and II, and transmembrane domain, respectively (Table 2). Based on 31 categories of amino acid properties implemented in the Tree-based Sensitive and Accurate Phylogenetic Profiler (TreeSAAP) program35, 48 codons were identified encoding alternative amino acids with significant alteration in their physicochemical properties consistent with positive selection. Using more stringent categories (7 and 8) and p values (< 0.001), positive selection occurred predominantly in domains I and II, comprising 21 and 17 codons, respectively. Intragenic recombination

Analysis of 80 Piama1 sequences from macaques in Thailand using a Genetic Algorithm for Recombination Detection (GARD) method has identified a recombination breakpoint between nucleotides 1047 and 1048 in domain II. This finding was supported by the results from the Recombination Detection Program 4 (RDP4) package36 in which the three potential recombination signals encompassed domain II: (i) between nucleotides 68 and 956 (Maxchi, p = 3.56 × 10−3; SiScan, p = 2.71 × 10−4), (ii) between nucleotides 819 and 1166 (Maxchi, p = 4.48 × 10−3; 3Seq, p = 0.039) and (iii) between nucleotides 984 and 1676 (Maxchi, p = 0.025)37–39.

Phylogenetic analysis

Maximum likelihood tree was constructed from the complete coding region of Piama1 with P. hylobati ama1 as a closely related outgroup sequence. The Piama1 sequences from Thai clones/isolates were placed in two clusters. Most isolates (77 out of 80 taxa) were phylogenetically related to the sequence from the Leucosphyrus strain (GenBank accession no. KJ569882) isolated from the western Malay Peninsula in 196440. The remaining isolates HB78, HB282 and HB693 from Narathiwat Province were clustered and more related to isolates from Southern China (San Antonio I and Taiwan I strains) (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Maximum likelihood tree of Piama1. Predicted B-cell epitopes

Although human B-cell epitopes are not perfectly matched with the epitopes recognised by macaques’ B cells, it has been shown that a number of B-cell epitopes were shared between humans and macaques41–43. BepiPred 2 prediction identified several linear B-cell epitopes that seemed to be unevenly distributed across PiAMA1. The transmembrane domain had scores below the threshold for predicting B-cell epitopes. Compared to PvAMA1, a similar profile of predicted B-cell epitope scores was observed across PiAMA1 except a few short regions of the sequences in domains I, II and III (Fig. 3). Searching for conformational B-cell epitopes in PiAMA1 was performed by using the Discotope 2 prediction method. Predicted HLA class I-binding peptides in rhesus macaques

HLA class I-binding peptides in PiAMA1 were predicted by using the algorithms and matrices provided on the MHCPathway-Macaque website44. Although the main natural hosts for P. inui are long-tailed and pig-tailed macaques, infections in rhesus macaques have been well established in experimental studies40. Meanwhile, a high number of HLA-A alleles were shared between long-tailed and rhesus macaques45. Based on the IC50 values of less than 50 nM in the prediction algorithm, 4 peptides in PiAMA1 have been predicted to bind to Mamu-A1 while 10 and 7 peptides could, respectively, bind to Mamu-A2 and Mamu-A11. Of these, 3 peptides comprising LTRSANNVL, MSNPWKAFM and RTHAASFVM were predicted to bind to both Mamu A1 and Mamu A2. For Mamu A2, the variant forms of the peptides LTRSANNVL, MSNPWKAFM, ITNSTCNFY and CVVGVASMF did not appear to drastically alter the predicted IC50 values. Discussion

Although P. inui is similar to P. malariae in terms of a 72-h asexual erythrocytic cycle the duration, the morphological similarity of blood stage parasites and the possible association with chronic nephropathy47,48, phylogenetic inferences from the small subunit ribosomal DNA and mitochondrial genes have consistently revealed a close phylogenetic affinity of P. inui to malaria parasites belonging to ‘vivax-like’ lineages28,29,49,50. Furthermore, analysis of the ama1 locus has shown that it is more closely related to P. hylobati and Plasmodium species infecting eastern Bornean orangutans (Pongo pigmaeus morio)51. Likewise, similar genetic relatedness has been observed in phylogenetic trees inferred from the merozoite surface protein 1 locus and the complete mitochondrial genome sequences of ‘vivax-like’ lineages, suggesting that these simian malaria species could share a common ancestor51. Analysis of P. inui isolates from macaque populations in Thailand29 and Malaysian Borneo52 have revealed evidence of intraspecific lineage divergence of parasites classified in the P. inui clade. In this study, analysis of 80 Piama1 sequences from macaques in Thailand has shown a markedly lower rate of nucleotide substitutions per site (d) than that of 11 clones/isolates outside Thailand (Table 1), which implied that the genetic diversity in Piama1 within an endemic country appears to be less than that observed across endemic regions. Phylogenetic analysis has shown that most Thai isolates (77 taxa) were clustered together with the isolate from a Leucosphyrus mosquito in Peninsular Malaysia (KJ569882) while the remaining isolates (HB78, HB282 and HB693) were more related to the South China and Taiwan strains. Furthermore, intraspecific lineage divergence of Piama1 from Sabah was observed in the phylogenetic tree (Fig. 2). Although Piama1 seemed to display two phylogenetic lineages, the relatively low bootstrap support implies that the divergence is incomplete which could be due to the presence of recombination and natural selection observed in this locus. Taken together, P. inui comprises heterogeneous lineages that are widely distributed across endemic countries.

Analysis of 57 P. inui-infected macaque blood samples from Thailand and 11 clones/isolates elsewhere has revealed a number of nucleotide substitutions distributed across all domains in PiAMA1. Although dS exceeded dN for the entire coding region, no evidence of deviation from neutrality was detected (p > 0.05) (Table 1). Variation in the magnitude of dS relative to that of dN for each PiAMA1 domain has suggested that different regions of this locus may have experienced different evolutionary histories. Blockwise analysis did not yield a significant difference between dS and dN in each domain among 80 PiAMA1 sequences from Thailand. However, a significantly greater dS than dN was observed in the cytoplasmic tail of non-Thai clones/isolates, suggesting purifying selection in this region. Meanwhile, codon-based analysis of deviation from neutrality has identified 14 codons under purifying selection, 10 of which occurred in the ectodomains and two each in the signal peptide and the cytoplasmic domain. Despite a preponderance of amino acid substitutions in the ectodomains (67 residues in a total of 85 amino acid substitutions), negative selection in these domains could suggest structural and/or functional importance of these amino acids.

The cytoplasmic tail of AMA1 appears to be involved in intermolecular interaction during the invasion process53. Unlike the ectodomains, interspecies complementation of the cytoplasmic tail could not be experimentally achieved54–56, despite the meager sequence heterogeneity in this region across apicomplexan AMA1 orthologues53. Importantly, in vitro mutagenesis of Pfama1 involving codons for amino acids D593, E594, D597, P598, E599, F602 and W603 has resulted in inactivation of the protein function, suggesting that these residues could be of functional importance56. Although no similar study has been performed in other malarial ama1s, the corresponding codons 533, 534, 537–539, 542 and 543 in Pvama1 were perfectly conserved among 231 P. vivax isolates20. Despite 4 amino acid substitutions in the cytoplasmic tail of PiAMA1 observed in this study, none were deleterious as predicted by the SIFT algorithms, while all substitutions occurred outside the corresponding functionally indispensable residues in PfAMA1. Likewise, no amino acid substitution occurred in the corresponding codons in Pkama130–32. The phosphorylation of the cytoplasmic tail of AMA1 has been shown to be crucial for host cell invasion and the initiation of the parasite replicative cycle, while most amino acid residues in this domain are indispensable for the protein function56–58. The presence of two negatively selected codons (R548 and P560) in the cytoplasmic tail of Piama1 that were predicted to constitute phosphorylation sites (DKRASHTTP and EKPYY, underlined residues)59,60 may suggest that amino acid alterations at these positions could have a detrimental effect.

By applying Tajima’s statistics, significantly negative D values were obtained for domain I and the loop region of domain II of Piama1 (Table 1). Consistently, the loop regions in domain II of both Pfama1 and Pvama1 are highly conserved among parasite populations8,19,20,61 that could have evolved under strong purifying selection20. Although a significant deviation from zero in the negative direction of Tajima’s D indicates an excess of rare variants in the population as a result of a recent population expansion after a population bottleneck, it may also suggest purifying selection. In contrast, blockwise analysis of Pfama1 has shown significantly positive Tajima’s D values in domains I and III, implying positive selection in these ectodomains. However, the D value for domain II of Pfama1 did not support deviation from neutrality61. The discrepancy in the Tajima’s D statistics between Pfama1 and Piama1 was consistent with the differential evolutionary histories of these genes.

Although blockwise analysis has shown an excess of dN compared to dS in domain II excluding the loop region and the transmembrane domain of Piama1, the difference was not sufficient to support a deviation from neutrality. However, 12 positively selected codons were identified by FUBAR; 6 of these were found in domain II outside the loop region. The remaining positively selected codons were found in the signal peptide, domain I and transmembrane domain. The inconsistent results between blockwise and codon-based analyses could be due in part to the combination of sites under natural selection with those that are highly conserved or neutrally evolved in blockwise analysis62. Therefore, molecular analysis did not appear to support strong evidence for natural selection in particular domains of Piama1 but rather suggested that natural selection could have influenced a limited number of individual codons in this gene.

Although deviation from neutrality can predict natural selection by analysis of DNA sequences, the preponderance of more synonymous than nonsynonymous substitutions in conservative genes has been suggested to compromise detection of sites influenced by positive selection63. On the other hand, selection can influence protein traits, including their biochemical activity and biophysical properties such as folding and stability 64. Therefore, analysis of deviation from the expected random distribution of possible amino acid changes inferred from phylogenetic trees taking into account the magnitude of changes in the physicochemical properties of substituted amino acids has provided an alternative approach to detect natural selection at the amino acid level65,66. Analysis of the physicochemical properties of amino acid replacements using the TreeSAAP program has revealed that 48 of 85 (56.47%) amino acid substitutions had significant changes that are in line with positive selection. Six out of 12 positively selected codons detected by FUBAR corresponded to positively selected amino acid residues predicted by TreeSAAP, implying that the incongruence between these results arises from differences in assumptions and prediction algorithms of these methods. While comparison between dN and dS has been suggested to be less sensitive to detect subtle deviation from neutrality than the analysis of the physicochemical properties of their encoded amino acids62, protein evolution is considered to be influenced by structure and function including phenotypic plasticity or capability of change in response to stimuli or inputs from environment67. Phenotypic plasticity of malarial AMA1 may be supported by the capability of the ectodomains of PvAMA1 to complement the function of PfAMA1 in host cell invasion55. It could be that both species shared similar crystal structures of the ectodomains due to interspecific conservation of cysteine residues forming disulfide bonds; thereby, the functional conformation of the host-cell invasion ligand could be retained18,21,53. Meanwhile, it has been proposed that new adaptive phenotypes can arise in the absence of positive selection67,68. Slightly deleterious mutations can accumulate in a species and the fates of these variants may be fixed or eliminated depending on the population process69. Among the 18 deleterious amino acid changes predicted by the SIFT algorithms, 12 were located in the positively selected amino acid residues defined by TreeSAAP and 14 occurred as singletons. Singleton substitutions may be newly generated and may be taken to fixation mainly by genetic drift of selectively neutral or nearly neutral mutations. Although positive selection of advantageous mutations can generate an adaptive phenotype, deleterious mutations are more common that are subject to purifying selection69,70. Importantly, most of the deleterious amino acid substitutions predicted in PiAMA1 occurred at relatively low frequencies, particularly as singletons. It may suggest that these mutations could be subject to ongoing elimination, probably from immune selective pressure because they were located at predicted B-cell or T-cell epitopes of the protein (Fig. 5).

Although both linear and conformational B-cell epitopes have been mapped to conserved or polymorphic ectodomains of PfAMA1 and PvAMA118,71,72, none has been investigated in PiAMA1. In this study, predicted linear and conformational B-cell epitopes seemed to be distributed mainly in all ectodomains and the cytoplasmic tail of PiAMA1 (Fig. 5). It is noteworthy that 32 of 48 (66.7%) positively selected amino acid residues predicted by TreeSAAP corresponded to amino acid residues predicted to be B-cell epitopes, implying that selection pressure could have been mediated by antibody responses to PiAMA1. Although sliding window plots of scores for linear B-cell epitopes gave similar profiles for PvAMA1 and PiAMA1, the most discernible difference spanned the loop region of domain II in which the scores for the former protein were below the threshold limit and vice versa (Fig. 3). In fact, the loop region of domain II is a target for the invasion-inhibiting antibody (4G2) against PfAMA15 while the corresponding region in PvAMA1 contains highly immunogenic epitopes71,73. As noted previously, the low scores for the linear B-cell epitope in the domain II loop of PvAMA1 contrasting its immunogenicity would rather suggest the conformational B-cell epitope of the region72. In PiAMA1, the loop region of domain II was predicted to contain both linear and conformational B-cell epitopes because it received scores above the threshold limits for BepiPred 2 and Discotope 2 predictions, although experimental confirmation is required.

In addition to anti-parasitic activity of anti-AMA1 antibodies during blood stage infections4,74, it has been shown that specific CD8 + T cell responses to this protein also play an important role in clinical protection during vaccination trials in volunteers75,76. Importantly, two MHC class I-restricted immunodominant CD8 + T cell epitopes (Ap8: KSHGKGYNW and Ap10: STCRFFVCK) associated with protection have been mapped to domains II and III of PfAMA1, respectively77. Although pre-erythrocytic immunity conferred by cytotoxic T-cell responses during P. inui infection requires experimental confirmation, amino acid substitutions in 4 of the predicted HLA class I-binding peptides in PiAMA1 (residues 51–59, 148–156, 151–159 and 439–447) could remarkably alter the predicted peptide-binding scores (Table 4). Variation in the cytotoxic epitopes of PiAMA1 could narrow the repertoire of cellular immune response through T-cell priming resulting in immune evasion as observed in P. falciparum circumsporozoite protein72,78. Interestingly, all amino acid substitutions in these peptides have also been predicted to be under positive selection by the TreeSAAP method (Fig. 5). Taken together, cellular immunity could probably contribute to natural selection on PiAMA1.

Although host immunity appears to exert a complex array of selective pressure on most PiAMA1 regions, no immunogenic epitope was identified in the transmembrane domain, in which it is unlikely to be accessible to the antibody. AMA1 is proteolytically cleaved after microneme secretion to the surface of the merozoite prior to the host cell invasion process. Intramembrane cleavage of the Toxoplasma AMA1 (TgAMA1) transmembrane domain results in the shedding of the ectodomains, while the cytoplasmic tail could interact with factors that trigger the parasite switch from an invasive to replicative mode58. Importantly, certain amino acid substitutions at the TgAMA1 transmembrane domain cleavage site may enhance, attenuate, or block the proteolytic efficiency that, in turn, affect host cell invasion79. For PfAMA1, the intramembrane cleavage site is located between amino acid residues A550 and S55180 corresponding to residues G491 and I492 in the transmembrane domain of PiAMA1. Likewise, a mutation of the intramembrane cleavage site can abolish proteolytic processing and shedding of PfAMA1, although the growth of P. falciparum bearing non-cleavable PfAMA1 is unaffected80. It has been suggested that successful shedding of the ectodomains containing epitopes recognised by invasion inhibitory antibodies could sequester and prevent the accumulation of immune complexes on the invading merozoites; therefore, parasites bearing non-cleavable PfAMA1 are more vulnerable to invasion-inhibitory antibodies81. Intriguingly, the positively selected codons T493S/G and G495C/F/S, could be located around the intramembrane cleavage site of PiAMA1, although experimental elucidation is required. Meanwhile, PiAMA1 is a monotopic membrane protein in which hydrophobicity and preferential occurrence of positively charged residues (the ‘positive-inside rule’) drive the lateral integration of the helical segment into the membrane. The process requires thermodynamic equilibration between the translocon and the lipid bilayer82. Sequence differences within the helical domain, as well as in the upstream and downstream regions, have been shown to influence the efficiency of the membrane insertion82–85. Therefore, the positive selection observed in the transmembrane domain of PiAMA1 could be mediated by factors other than host immune pressure.

Apart from genetic drift and natural selection, meiotic recombination can influence the efficacy of selection and the fate of new variants86,87. When selection gives rise to the favored alleles at several loci in a finite population size, recombination can generate new advantageous genotypic combinations88. Meanwhile, genes under positive selection are likely to be subject to a high rate of recombination because recombination can lead to the fixation of advantageous variants69. In this study, intragenic recombination was detected in domain II of Piama1 among the parasite population in Thailand, while the rate of nonsynonymous substitution in this domain was higher than most other domains except the transmembrane-encoding region (Table 1). Intriguingly, this in turn could suggest that some amino acid substitutions in domain II of PiAMA1 could be selectively advantageous, probably due to immune evasion.

In conclusion, Piama1 exhibited sequence variation among the P. inui population in Thailand. Most amino acid substitutions occurred in ectodomains akin to those observed in other malarial AMA1. Purifying selection in domain I and the loop region in domain II has implied their structural or functional constraints. Positive selection in terms of significant changes in the physicochemical properties of substituted amino acids was observed across the protein, particularly in the ectodomains and cytoplasmic tail. Several positively selected amino acids corresponded to the predicted B-cell and T-cell epitopes, suggesting that immune pressure could have influenced sequence variation at this locus. The results reported herein could be useful for the development of vaccines derived from AMA1 regarding possible immunogenic epitopes in simian malaria parasites, including experimental trials in animal models. Furthermore, this study provides comparative knowledge on the evolution of malarial AMA1, a promising vaccine candidate against malaria.

Materials and methods

P. inui isolates

Fifty-seven P. inui isolates were obtained from 51 pig-tailed macaques (Macaca nemestrina) and six long-tailed macaques (M. fascicularis) diagnosed by species-specific nested polymerase chain reaction (PCR) targeting Plasmodium mitochondrial cytochrome genes as previously described26,29. Blood samples (< 1 ml) were obtained from superficial leg veins of domesticated macaques without prior sedation. The collection period was between 2008 and 2018 from Narathiwat Province in southern Thailand (n = 55) except for two isolates, each from Yala and Chanthaburi Provinces in southern and eastern Thailand, respectively26,29. The study was conducted in accordance with ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org).

DNA preparation

Genomic DNA was extracted from 200 μl of blood samples using a QIAGEN DNA mini kit (Hilden, Germany). The DNA preparation procedures basically followed the manufacturer’s instruction. The DNA was eluted with 30 μL of TE buffer. The purified genomic DNA was stored at – 40° C until use.

PCR amplification

The complete coding region of PiAMA1 was amplified by PCR using primers derived from genomic scaffold supercontig 1.8 of the whole genome shotgun sequence of the San Antonio I strain of P. inui (NW008481842). The forward primer PiAMA1-F0: 5’- CTGCTCACCTGTGTGACG-3’ was from the 5’untranslated region (nucleotides -167 to -150) and the reverse primer PiAMA1-R0: 5’CCGTCCCCACTTGTTACG-3 from the 3’untranslated region (nucleotides 1728 to 1745 from the start codon). PCR amplification was performed in a total volume of 30 μL using 2 μL of genomic DNA and ExTaq DNA polymerase in the recommended reaction mixture (Takara, Seta, Japan). Thermocycler profile contained a preamplification denaturation at 94° C, 1 min; 35 cycles of denaturation at 94° C, 30 s; annealing at 60° C, 30 s; extension at 72° C, 2 min, and post amplification extension at 72° C, 5 min20. Analysis of the PCR products was done by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis.

DNA templates and sequencing

The PCR products were purified using QIAquick PCR purification kit (Qiagen, Germany). DNA sequences were determined directly and bidirectionally from the PCR-purified templates. For isolates showing unreadable or possessing superimposed signals on electropherograms, the purified PCR products of these samples were ligated into pGEM-T-Easy Vector (Promega, Madison WI, USA) and transformed into Escherichia coli strain JM109. DNA sequences were determined from purified recombinant plasmids. At least 10 recombinant plasmid clones of each isolate were used as DNA templates for sequencing.

Data analysis

The Piama1 sequences were aligned using the CLUSTAL_X program89. Eleven previously reported sequences were included for comparative analysis consisting of 5 sequences from Sabah, Malaysian Borneo (GenBank accession nos. KJ569885-KJ569888) and the remaining sequences, one each from Southern mainland China (XM008817929), Taiwan (KJ569880), Kuala Selangor, Malaysia (KJ569879), Negeri Sembilan, Malaysia (KJ569882), Southern India (KJ569884) and Indonesia (KJ569883). The number of haplotypes, haplotype diversity and Tajima’s D statistics were determined by using the DnaSP 6.12.3 program90. Statistical significance of the observed Tajima’s D values was obtained by coalescence simulation with intermediate recombination after 10,000 replicates implemented in the program90. Nucleotide diversity (π) was calculated from the average number of pairwise sequence differences in the sample. The number of nucleotide substitutions per site (d) was estimated based on the Tamura 3-parameter model91. The number of synonymous substitutions per synonymous site (dS) and nonsynonymous substitutions per nonsynonymous site (dN) were computed using the method of Nei and Gojobori92 with Juke and Cantor correction93. The standard errors of π, d, dS and dN were computed by the bootstrap method with 1,000 pseudoreplicates implemented in the MEGA 6 program94. Statistical significances of d, dS and dN were computed by using the Z test which corresponds to the t test with an infinite number of degrees of freedom95. Codon-based analysis of departure from neutrality was performed using the FUBAR method34 from screened sequence data by excluding recombination sites. Positive selection inferred from significant alteration in substituted amino acid properties was analysed by using the TreeSAAP program35. The magnitude of radicality in selection is classified into eight categories taking into account the mean percent differences in physicochemical amino acid properties, the magnitudes of goodness-of-fit scores and the levels of significant differences between substituted residues. Category 1 is the most conservative and category 8 the most radical. For a robust result, categories 7 and 8 were considered in this analysis35. Evidence for intragenic recombination was determined by a set of algorithms implemented in the RDP4 package program36 and the GARD method implemented in the Datamonkey web server96. Phylogenetic tree was constructed using the maximum likelihood method using the best substitution model for the sequence data that showed the lowest Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) score as implemented in the MEGA 6 program94. The final tree topology was selected on the basis of the superior log likelihood value. Prediction of linear and conformational B-cell epitopes was performed using BepiPred Linear Epitope Prediction 2.0 and Discotope 2.0 implemented in the IEDB Analysis Resource, respectively97. Prediction of peptide binding to MHC class I molecules of the rhesus macaques was performed per the algorithm on the website www.mamu.liai.org44. Amino acid substitutions were classified into functionally neutral and deleterious changes using the SIFT program46.
