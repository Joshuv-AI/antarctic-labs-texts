From screening to subtyping in a single glance
Authors: Sarthak Pati
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFragmented AI tools struggle with the complex diagnosis of fine-grained diseases from radiological images. A new study in Patterns introduces the “screening-to-subtyping” (S2S) framework, a holistic deep learning system integrating the entire diagnostic workflow from detection to subtyping. Validated on complex thoracic cancers, the S2S-Med system demonstrated superior accuracy, outperforming existing benchmarks. A human-AI experiment revealed that the AI’s performance surpassed AI-assisted physicians and that physician trust correlated with greater improvement. The S2S framework is a significant step toward enhancing precision medicine and establishing a new paradigm for human-AI partnership.

Main text

A new deep learning framework, screening-to-subtyping (S2S), integrates the entire radiological workflow, from lesion detection to fine-grained disease subtyping, into a single, cohesive system. Tang et al.1 demonstrate its power on a complex set of thoracic cancers, showing that the artificial intelligence (AI) not only assists but can even outperform human experts, suggesting a new paradigm for human-AI collaboration in medicine.

The promise of AI in medicine is to see the invisible and connect the dots in ways that augment human expertise. In radiology, this means easing the immense burden on clinicians who must meticulously analyze complex scans to not only find disease but also characterize it with enough precision to guide treatment. Diagnosing fine-grained diseases, such as the many subtypes of cancer, is particularly challenging. The subtle differences between subtypes, often barely perceptible on a computed tomography (CT) scan, can mean the difference between effective, targeted therapy and a less optimal, generalized approach.

While many AI tools have been developed for medical imaging, they often operate in silos. One model might excel at detecting a potential tumor, another at segmenting its boundaries, and a third at classifying it into a broad category. This fragmented approach fails to capture the holistic reasoning that defines a radiologist’s workflow, where insights from one stage of analysis inform the next. A new study in Patterns by Tang et al.1 introduces a powerful framework that bridges this gap. Their S2S AI is designed to mirror and enhance the complete diagnostic journey, integrating multiple tasks into a unified, highly accurate system.

The core innovation of the S2S framework is its comprehensive design, which simultaneously addresses what the authors term the “width” and “depth” of radiological diagnosis. “Width” refers to covering all the essential tasks in the clinical workflow: detecting an abnormality, precisely segmenting its boundaries, and classifying it. “Depth” refers to drilling down from a coarse-grained disease type (e.g., thymoma) to a highly specific, fine-grained subtype (e.g., thymoma type B1), which is critical for personalized medicine.2

To achieve this, S2S integrates several specialized modules that work in concert.

To validate their framework, Tang et al. developed S2S-Med, a system specifically designed to diagnose mediastinal neoplasms, a diverse group of thoracic cancers with 43 challenging subtypes. Using a large-scale, multicenter dataset of thousands of CT scans, S2S-Med demonstrated remarkable performance across the board.

In the critical first step of detection, S2S-Med achieved a sensitivity of 99.6%, successfully identifying nearly every lesion and minimizing the risk of missed diagnoses. When delineating tumor boundaries, which is a key step for assessing tumor size and planning treatment, it achieved a Dice score of 0.804, significantly outperforming a previous state-of-the-art model, CAIMEN-CL.3

The most demanding challenge, however, was classification. Here, S2S-Med showed its true strength. It accurately classified not only the 12 coarse-grained disease types but also the 43 fine-grained subtypes with high accuracy, again surpassing existing benchmarks. The authors conducted ablation studies, systematically removing key modules from the framework, which confirmed that every component of the integrated S2S design contributed to its superior performance. The cross-stage module, for instance, proved essential; providing the classification model with precise lesion location information from the earlier stages dramatically improved its accuracy.

Perhaps the most compelling findings from the study come from a human-AI collaboration experiment. The researchers recruited 9 physicians with varying levels of experience and asked them to diagnose 86 cases, first on their own and then with the assistance of S2S-Med’s predictions.

As expected, AI assistance significantly boosted the diagnostic accuracy of all physicians, regardless of their seniority. The improvement was most pronounced for the difficult task of fine-grained subtyping, highlighting the system’s clinical value. However, the results held a surprise: even after being aided by the AI, no physician’s performance surpassed that of S2S-Med operating alone.

This finding challenges the conventional view of AI as a simple support tool. For highly complex, fine-grained diagnostic tasks, AI may be capable of functioning as an independent, or at least a senior, partner. The study also uncovered a fascinating link between physician behavior and performance: the more a physician trusted the AI’s suggestions (by changing their initial diagnosis to match the AI’s), the greater their accuracy improvement. This correlation was strongest for the most challenging fine-grained tasks, suggesting that in areas of high uncertainty, trusting a highly accurate AI can lead to better outcomes. These insights into the dynamics of trust and performance are crucial for designing effective clinical workflows that optimally combine human and machine intelligence.4

The work by Tang et al. represents a significant step toward the next generation of medical AI. The S2S framework provides a flexible and powerful blueprint for developing comprehensive diagnostic systems for other complex diseases, from neurological disorders to various cancers. By creating an AI that thinks more like a clinician, integrating information across tasks, views, and scales, they have built a tool that is not only more accurate but also more aligned with the real-world needs of medical practice.

Of course, challenges remain for clinical deployment, including the need for robust computational infrastructure and the development of training programs to help clinicians navigate the nuances of collaborating with such powerful AI systems. Nonetheless, by demonstrating a system that can master the entire diagnostic workflow from screening to subtyping, this research brings the promise of AI-driven precision medicine much closer to reality.
