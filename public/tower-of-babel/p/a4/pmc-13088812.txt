Machine learning-based model for triage-stage prediction of emergency department disposition
Authors: Kentaro Yoshimura, Takeshi Moriguchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly disposition decisions in the emergency department (ED) are sometimes made before laboratory results, imaging, or diagnostic labels become available. We developed disposition prediction models using only triage-stage information and quantified the incremental gain from adding downstream clinical data within the same cohort.MethodsWe conducted a single-center retrospective cohort study at a secondary-level emergency medical facility in Japan, including adult ED encounters. The outcome was ED disposition, dichotomized as discharged home versus admission or worse (hospital admission, intensive care unit admission, death in the ED, or inter-hospital transfer). We trained L2-regularized logistic regression models using prespecified predictor sets. The Pre-test (triage-stage) model included demographics, trauma status, comorbidities, symptoms, and vital signs. The Clinical model was defined as the Pre-test model plus four binary clinical-process indicators (yes/no): blood gas analysis performed, computed tomography performed, magnetic resonance imaging performed, and consultation requested. The Comprehensive model was defined as the Clinical model plus laboratory tests and diagnosis-related variables. Discrimination was assessed using stratified 5-fold cross-validation with fold-wise preprocessing to prevent leakage.ResultsAmong 2,080 encounters, 1,252 (60.2%) were discharged home and 828 (39.8%) had admission or worse. The area under the receiver operating characteristic curve was highest for the Comprehensive model 0.921 (95% confidence interval (CI), 0.909–0.933), followed by the Clinical model 0.887 (95% CI, 0.873–0.902) and the Pre-test model 0.796 (95% CI, 0.776–0.816). Background

Emergency departments (EDs) worldwide continue to face crowding, ambulance diversion, and shortages of inpatient beds [1–3]. Prolonged ED length of stay and bottlenecks in patient flow have been associated with worse clinical outcomes and lower patient satisfaction, as well as disruption of hospital-wide care delivery [4]. In this context, making timely and accurate decisions regarding ED disposition (home discharge, general ward admission, intensive care unit (ICU) admission, or inter-hospital transfer) is a key challenge for ensuring both the efficiency and safety of emergency care systems.

To support early risk stratification at presentation, several triage scales such as the Emergency Severity Index and the Japan Triage and Acuity Scale have been developed and implemented in many EDs [5, 6]. In parallel, early warning scores including the National Early Warning Score and the quick Sequential Organ Failure Assessment have been proposed to predict clinical deterioration, ICU admission, or mortality using simple bedside parameters [7–9]. While these tools have demonstrated usefulness in various settings, they also have limitations, including interobserver variability, implementation complexity, and heterogeneous adoption across institutions and regions. As a result, they may not always function as fully standardized, universally applicable, and reproducible tools for disposition decision support when clinicians must rely solely on the very limited information available at or immediately after triage.

Recent advances in machine learning and deep learning have enabled the development of risk prediction models in several domains, including image-based diagnosis and critical care medicine [10–12]. Machine learning models that combine electronic health record data, vital signs, and laboratory results have been developed to predict adverse outcomes, such as ICU-level care and short-term mortality [11, 13, 14]. Although these models often achieve high discriminative performance, many of them rely on “rich” information such as laboratory test results, imaging findings, and final diagnostic labels, which typically become available only after initial evaluation and diagnostic workup. Consequently, the extent to which ED disposition can be predicted using only the minimal clinical information available at triage (demographics, trauma status, comorbidities, symptoms, and vital signs, without laboratory or diagnostic information) remains insufficiently explored. Moreover, few studies have systematically compared, within the same patient cohort, the discriminative performance of models based solely on triage-level information with that of models that progressively incorporate laboratory test results, imaging, and diagnostic labels. In particular, the incremental improvement in discrimination obtained by adding such downstream diagnostic information has not been systematically quantified. Such triage-level models may be particularly valuable when timely decision-making is required but access to laboratory and imaging results is not immediately available, including resource-constrained emergency departments.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to develop ED disposition prediction models using only clinical information available at the time of triage, namely demographics, trauma status, comorbidities, symptoms, and vital signs, without laboratory test results or diagnostic labels, in a cohort of adult patients presenting to a secondary-level emergency medical facility in Japan that provides 24-hour emergency care for non-life-threatening but potentially serious conditions. We then compared their discriminative performance with that of models that sequentially add information from laboratory tests, imaging studies, and diagnostic labels within the same cohort. By doing so, we sought to clarify both the potential and the limitations of disposition decision support based solely on triage-level information, without relying on subsequent laboratory or diagnostic data.

Methods

Study design, population, and data collection

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study conducted at a secondary-level emergency medical facility in Japan. During the study period from December 2019 to August 2023, we screened ED encounters, including walk-in presentations. Routinely collected clinical data at the time of ED presentation were extracted from the hospital’s electronic health records and laboratory information systems, including demographics, mode of arrival, trauma status, comorbidities, presenting symptoms, vital signs, and initial laboratory test results where available. Information on clinical-process indicators (whether blood gas analysis (BGA) was performed, computed tomography (CT) was performed, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was performed, and consultation was requested) was also obtained where available, along with final ED diagnoses. These variables were treated as binary process indicators reflecting clinician-driven actions during the early ED workup and did not include analyte-level blood gas results (e.g., pH, pO2, or pCO2) as predictor values.

For the present analysis, we restricted the cohort to ED encounters from patients aged ≥ 20 years whose race was recorded as Asian (predominantly Japanese) in the medical record. This restriction was pragmatic because non-Asian encounters were rare in this setting (n = 24), and language discordance in a small minority of patients may reduce the reliability of interview-derived triage variables (e.g., symptoms and medical history); we therefore limited the cohort to reduce potential misclassification without implying biological differences. We additionally excluded encounters with missing ED disposition. The cohort selection process and the final analytic cohort are summarized in Fig. 1, and baseline characteristics of the analytic cohort are shown in Table 1.

Operational definition of triage-stage predictors

In our ED, the Pre-test predictors were captured at presentation according to the local triage protocol using standardized intake documentation. Hereafter, we use the term ‘Pre-test’ to refer to triage-stage information available at ED triage prior to diagnostic testing (laboratory testing, neuroimaging, and specialist consultation), and not to Bayesian Pre-test probability. Detailed symptom flags, comorbidity items, medication status, and neurologic assessments (including Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) subscores) were recorded as part of the initial nurse-led intake workflow at arrival. For ambulance arrivals, key information was obtained from pre-arrival communication and the handover at arrival and entered into the same intake fields; for walk-in patients, the same items were collected during the initial intake at presentation.

Discrimination modeling and performance evaluation

The analysis unit was an ED encounter, and encounters were analyzed as independent observations; repeat visits by the same patient were not collapsed. The primary outcome was ED disposition, recoded as a binary variable (0 = discharged home; 1 = admission or worse) (Additional file 1: Table S1). Predictor sets were prespecified for three models (Additional file 2: Table S2). In total, the Pre-test, Clinical, and Comprehensive models comprised 34, 38, and 58 prespecified predictor variables, respectively. The Pre-test model included demographics, trauma status, comorbidities, symptoms, and vital signs. The Clinical model was defined as the Pre-test model plus four binary clinical-process indicators (BGA performed, computed tomography performed, magnetic resonance imaging performed, and consultation requested); these variables represent process/execution indicators rather than test result values. The Comprehensive model was defined as the Clinical model plus laboratory tests and diagnosis-related variables.

We prespecified an L2-regularized logistic regression model as an interpretable baseline and held the model class constant across predictor sets so that performance differences primarily reflect predictor availability. We used prespecified hyperparameters (penalty = l2, solver = lbfgs, C = 1.0, max_iter = 10000, class_weight = None). Continuous predictors were standardized using training-fold means and standard deviations (z-scoring), binary indicators were coded as 0/1, and missing values were imputed within each training fold (median for continuous variables and the most frequent value for binary indicators). To prevent information leakage, all preprocessing parameters (imputation and standardization) were estimated using the training portion of each cross-validation split only and then applied to the corresponding held-out fold. Model discrimination was evaluated using stratified 5-fold cross-validation with shuffling enabled (StratifiedKFold; n_splits = 5, shuffle = True, random_state = 42). Within each split, the model was trained on four-fifths of the data and used to generate predicted probabilities for the held-out fold. These out-of-fold (OOF) predicted probabilities were concatenated across folds to obtain a single OOF probability for every encounter. A single receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for each model was constructed by varying the classification threshold over the pooled OOF probabilities, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated from the pooled OOF predictions. This OOF evaluation ensures that every probability contributing to the ROC curve was generated by a model trained without the corresponding encounter.

To quantify uncertainty, the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the AUC was estimated by nonparametric bootstrap resampling of encounter-level OOF predictions (2,000 resamples; percentile method; random_state = 42) for each model. In addition to the threshold-independent AUC, we summarized classification performance at a single operating point by determining the Youden index-derived threshold (maximizing sensitivity + specificity – 1) on the pooled OOF ROC curve for each model and then calculating sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) at that threshold. All analyses were performed using Python 3.11.2 with scikit-learn 1.4.2, NumPy 1.24.0, pandas 2.2.3, and Matplotlib 3.7.5.

Calibration and clinical usefulness were evaluated using the pooled OOF predicted probabilities for each model. Calibration plots were generated by grouping encounters into deciles of predicted risk and plotting the mean predicted probability against the observed admission or worse fraction within each decile. Overall calibration error was summarized by the Brier score. Calibration intercept and slope were estimated by fitting a logistic regression model with the outcome as the dependent variable and the logit-transformed OOF predicted probability as the independent variable (ideal intercept = 0, slope = 1). Clinical usefulness was evaluated using decision curve analysis based on the pooled OOF predicted probabilities; net benefit was calculated across a range of threshold probabilities and compared with treat-all and treat-none strategies.

Principal component analysis

To explore the structure of the predictor space, principal component analysis (PCA) was performed separately for the predictor sets used in the Comprehensive, Clinical, and Pre-test models. Predictors were median-imputed; continuous predictors were standardized (z-scored), and binary indicators were retained as 0/1 prior to PCA. Score plots of principal component 1 (PC1) versus principal component 2 (PC2) were generated. The percentages shown on the PC1 and PC2 axes indicate the proportion of total variance explained by each component, and explained variance ratios and cumulative explained variance were summarized using scree plots.

Permutation importance and ablation analyses

For the Pre-test model, permutation-based feature importance was estimated as the decrease in AUC after permuting a single predictor in the held-out fold while keeping all other predictors unchanged. Using the same stratified 5-fold cross-validation splits (random_state = 42), each predictor was permuted in the held-out fold with 30 repeats (n_repeats = 30; random_state = 42), and the mean decrease in AUC relative to the unpermuted baseline (held-out fold AUC) was computed; importance scores were then averaged across folds. In addition, we performed a sequential ablation analysis by removing the top-ranked predictors (k = 1–5) in order of permutation importance, refitting the model, and re-estimating discrimination using the identical cross-validation splits. For the ablation curve, the point estimate was the pooled OOF AUC, and error bars represent the standard deviation of fold-wise AUCs across the five held-out folds.

Results

Cohort selection and baseline characteristics

Figure 1 summarizes the cohort selection process during the study period. Prespecified eligibility criteria were applied to 2,216 ED encounters. Encounters were excluded if the patient was aged < 20 years, if race was recorded as non-Asian, or if ED disposition was missing, yielding a final analytic cohort of 2,080 encounters. Of these, 1,252 (60.2%) were discharged home and 828 (39.8%) were classified as admission or worse.

Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Compared with the discharged home group, encounters in the admission or worse group were older (72.8 ± 18.3 vs. 58.1 ± 22.1 years) and had lower GCS total scores (13.4 ± 3.2 vs. 14.8 ± 1.2). Laboratory measures also tended to be more abnormal in the admission or worse group, including lower albumin (3.6 ± 0.8 vs. 4.2 ± 0.5) and higher white blood cell count (10,160 ± 6,632 vs. 8,265 ± 4,365), C-reactive protein (5.3 ± 15.7 vs. 1.1 ± 2.3), and lactate (3.0 ± 3.8 vs. 1.9 ± 1.4). These differences suggest that, compared with the discharged home group, the admission or worse group tended to present with greater acuity, including lower level of consciousness, along with laboratory abnormalities consistent with heightened inflammatory responses (e.g., elevated white blood cell count and C-reactive protein) and metabolic stress potentially reflecting circulatory compromise and/or tissue hypoperfusion (e.g., elevated lactate). Some laboratory measures in this descriptive comparison may reflect selective test ordering in routine care (selection bias), which can yield counterintuitive group averages (e.g., a higher mean procalcitonin in the discharged home group).

Among the 828 encounters classified as admission or worse, 702 (84.8%) resulted in hospital admission, 84 (10.1%) in ICU admission, 18 (2.2%) in inter-hospital transfer, and 24 (2.9%) resulted in death in the ED. To facilitate interpretation of the admission or worse case mix, we descriptively summarized a prespecified set of structured diagnosis labels; the most frequent labels were pneumonia (19.3%), urinary tract infection (7.1%), and intracerebral hemorrhage (6.3%) (Table 2).

Predictive performance for ED disposition using staged predictor sets

To reflect the clinical timeline in the emergency department, we prespecified three staged predictor sets that become available sequentially as care proceeds from arrival to early workup and then to subsequent test results (Additional file 2: Table S2). The Pre-test predictor set comprises information available at arrival/triage: demographics, trauma status, comorbidities, symptoms, and vital signs. The Clinical predictor set adds early ED management signals that are typically generated after initial assessment: four binary clinical-process indicators (BGA performed, CT performed, MRI performed, and consultation requested). These are clinician-driven process indicators (actions taken) and do not encode blood gas component measurements. Finally, the Comprehensive predictor set augments the Clinical set with information that generally becomes available later in the ED course, incorporating laboratory tests and diagnosis-related variables.

As shown in Fig. 2A-C, discrimination increased stepwise as additional information was incorporated. The Pre-test model achieved an AUC of 0.796 (95% CI, 0.776–0.816). Adding clinical-process indicators improved discrimination to an AUC of 0.887 (95% CI, 0.873–0.902), an absolute increase of 0.091 compared with the Pre-test model. Further adding laboratory tests and diagnosis-related variables yielded an AUC of 0.921 (95% CI, 0.909–0.933), representing an additional increase of 0.034 over the Clinical model (0.125 over the Pre-test model). In a sensitivity analysis excluding ICU admission and death in the ED (n = 1,972), discrimination remained similar across staged predictor sets (AUC 0.919 for the Comprehensive model, 0.892 for the Clinical model, and 0.805 for the Pre-test model; Additional file 3: Figure S1).

At the Youden index-derived threshold (Fig. 2D-E), the Comprehensive and Clinical models showed a balanced trade-off between sensitivity and specificity, whereas the Pre-test model had lower sensitivity, consistent with its lower AUC. Notably, NPV remained relatively high across models, suggesting that encounters predicted as “discharged home” had a low probability of admission even when predictions were based only on pre-test information. Overall, removing laboratory and diagnosis-related variables primarily reduced sensitivity for “admission or worse,” while triage-stage data alone still supported risk stratification.

Beyond discrimination, calibration and clinical usefulness were additionally assessed using pooled OOF predicted probabilities. Calibration plots showed generally good agreement between predicted and observed admission or worse risk across deciles, with Brier scores of 0.174 (Pre-test), 0.127 (Clinical), and 0.107 (Comprehensive). Logit recalibration further suggested limited systematic miscalibration, with calibration intercept/slope of − 0.028/0.940 (Pre-test), − 0.014/0.947 (Clinical), and − 0.010/0.965 (Comprehensive). Decision-curve analysis suggested net benefit over treat-all and treat-none strategies across a range of threshold probabilities, particularly for the Clinical and Comprehensive models (Additional file 4: Figure S2).

Principal component analysis of model variables

To provide an unsupervised overview of the predictor space (as a transparency/quality control check) and to contextualize the supervised results, we performed PCA separately using the variables included in the Comprehensive, Clinical, and Pre-test models (Fig. 3A-C). In each panel, the score distributions for the discharged home and admission or worse groups overlapped substantially, indicating that ED disposition is not well separated by a small number of linear components. Notably, the Clinical and Pre-test score plots appeared highly similar, consistent with the limited expansion of the predictor space from Pre-test to Clinical (i.e., the addition of four binary clinical-process indicators). Across panels, the limited variance explained by PC1 and PC2 (as indicated on the axes) suggests that disposition-related information is distributed across multiple dimensions rather than concentrated in the first two components, which is consistent with the need for multivariate supervised modeling for ED disposition prediction. Because PCA was conducted separately for each variable set, the principal component axes are not directly comparable across panels.

Scree plots (Additional file 5: Figure S3) showed a gradual decay of the explained variance ratio without a clear inflection point, indicating that variance is distributed across many components rather than concentrated in the first few. Consistent with this, cumulative explained variance increased only gradually as additional components were added. These findings support the interpretation that the dominant variance structure is not low-dimensional, complementing the overlap observed in the PC1-PC2 score plots.

Feature importance of the Pre-test model

To improve the interpretability of the Pre-test model, we quantified permutation-based feature importance using the held-out test sets in 5-fold cross-validation (Fig. 4A). Permuting age produced the largest mean decrease in AUC, indicating that baseline patient factors contributed substantially to early disposition prediction. Among presenting features, markers of acute neurologic compromise, including impaired consciousness and paresis, showed the next largest AUC drops, consistent with the clinical intuition that altered mental status and focal deficits strongly influence downstream care needs at triage. Several vital signs and symptom variables (e.g., body temperature, dyspnea, and systolic blood pressure) also contributed, suggesting that physiologic derangement and symptom burden provide complementary information beyond age and neurologic status. Importantly, the permutation-induced AUC drops were modest for individual predictors, indicating that no single variable dominated performance; rather, discrimination emerged from the combined contribution of multiple triage-available features. Because permutation importance can distribute importance across correlated predictors (e.g., overlapping measures of consciousness such as GCS components), the absolute ranking should be interpreted as reflecting shared predictive signal across related variables rather than a unique effect of any single measure.

We further performed a sequential ablation analysis by removing the top-ranked predictors one by one (Fig. 4B). Model discrimination decreased progressively but not precipitously as predictors were removed (AUC approximately 0.80 with no removal vs. approximately 0.74 after removing the top five predictors), supporting the notion that the Pre-test model integrates multiple complementary signals instead of relying on a single determinant. Collectively, these analyses suggest that early disposition prediction in this cohort reflects the combination of age, acute severity (particularly neurologic status) and broader physiologic and symptom-level information available at ED presentation.

Discussion

In this single-center retrospective cohort of 2,080 adult ED encounters at a secondary-level emergency medical facility in Japan, we found that ED disposition (discharged home vs. admission or worse) showed moderate discrimination using triage-stage information alone, and that discrimination improved as prespecified predictor sets were added in a staged manner. Specifically, the AUCs were 0.796 for the Pre-test model, 0.887 for the Clinical model, and 0.921 for the Comprehensive model. Our objective was not merely to show that adding information improves performance, but to quantify, within the same cohort, the incremental contribution of clearly defined information bundles to discrimination. In this respect, the magnitude of incremental gains is informative: the improvement from Pre-test to Clinical was substantial (+ 0.091 AUC), whereas the additional gain from Clinical to Comprehensive was relatively modest (+ 0.034 AUC). This pattern suggests that clinical-process indicators incorporated in the Clinical model are closely tied to early diagnostic and management decisions in routine practice and may serve as proxies for clinical judgment relevant to disposition. Importantly, the BGA indicator in this model indicates only whether blood gas analysis was performed (yes/no), not blood gas analyte values; thus, its contribution should be interpreted as a workflow/decision signal rather than a blood gas result effect. Accordingly, the Clinical and Comprehensive models should be interpreted as staged benchmarks that reflect progressively richer information (including clinician-driven processes), rather than as triage-only decision-support tools. In contrast, the Pre-test model represents the triage-stage setting and is the primary focus for earlier risk stratification.

The discriminatory performance of the Pre-test model indicates that triage-stage data can support early risk stratification, while also highlighting intrinsic limitations of prediction based on minimal initial information. Variables available at triage, such as age, chief complaint, comorbidities and medications, symptom descriptors, vital signs, and basic neurologic findings, can reflect physiologic severity and the likelihood of requiring inpatient care. However, ED presentations are often nonspecific, and clinically important abnormalities may only become apparent after laboratory testing or imaging, particularly in patients with nonspecific complaints [15]. Accordingly, the Pre-test model should not be considered a replacement for clinician disposition decisions. Rather, its most appropriate role is as an adjunct tool early after arrival to support situational awareness and operational planning (e.g., anticipating bed demand, prioritizing diagnostic pathways, and preparing staffing and resource allocation). In addition, predictive values at a single operating point should be interpreted carefully. Using the Youden index-derived threshold, the Pre-test model yielded a sensitivity/specificity of 0.676/0.784 and a PPV/NPV of 0.674/0.785. Because PPV and NPV depend on admission prevalence in the target population and on the operating threshold, any implementation would require threshold selection tailored to the intended use case (e.g., minimizing under-triage) and further validation, particularly with respect to calibration. In this cohort, OOF calibration and decision-curve analyses suggested generally good agreement and net benefit across a range of threshold probabilities (Additional file 4: Figure S2), but these assessments remain internal and warrant external validation and recalibration prior to implementation.

Several limitations merit consideration. First, this was a single-center retrospective study; thus, external validity has not been established. Second, the availability and quality of triage-stage information, especially history, symptom characterization, and background factors, can vary across institutions depending on workflows, documentation practices, and prehospital information capture; consequently, model performance may differ even when nominally similar predictor sets are used. Therefore, the Pre-test model’s performance should be interpreted in the context of institutions with similar intake documentation practices, and discrimination may be reduced under more minimal triage data availability. In addition, documentation of interview-derived items can be incomplete in time-critical situations; therefore, future work should evaluate reduced feature sets and perform external validation with recalibration across sites. Third, because the Pre-test model relies on interview-derived information, performance may degrade in encounters where reliable history taking is difficult (e.g., impaired consciousness, severe pain, or cognitive impairment). Fourth, the analytic cohort was restricted to adult encounters recorded as Asian in the medical record, which limits generalizability to other populations. This restriction largely reflects the local case mix of a single-center ED in Japan, where encounters recorded as non-Asian were rare (n = 24). Because the predictors are derived from clinical presentation and triage documentation rather than biological biomarkers, differences in transportability across settings are more plausibly driven by case mix and the fidelity of information capture. Given that the Pre-test model relies on interview-derived triage items, we additionally applied this restriction to reduce potential misclassification due to language discordance in a small minority of encounters, without implying biological differences; external validation with recalibration and subgroup performance assessment in more diverse cohorts is needed. Fifth, we dichotomized outcomes as “discharged home” versus “admission or worse,” and therefore could not distinguish heterogeneity in care intensity such as general ward admission, ICU admission, inter-hospital transfer, or death. Misclassification was not uniform across disposition severity strata. In a supplemental severity-stratified error analysis using out-of-fold predictions at the prespecified operating point (Additional file 6: Table S3), high-acuity outcomes (ICU admission or death in the ED) were less frequently misclassified as discharged home than hospital admissions, and false-negative rates decreased as richer predictor sets became available. However, these estimates are threshold-dependent and high-acuity event counts were limited; therefore, the findings should be interpreted as descriptive. For clinical translation, these limitations motivate several next steps: standardizing input data elements across sites, conducting external validation with recalibration to account for inter-institutional differences, and developing purpose-specific models with multiclass outcomes that better reflect gradients of care intensity.

Our findings are consistent with prior work aimed at supporting earlier decision-making using information available before diagnostic results are fully obtained. For example, admission prediction models have been developed using structured triage-stage data such as age, vital signs, triage category, and comorbidities [16]. In addition, natural language processing of free-text triage notes has been shown to enable prediction of ED disposition using text features alone [17]. Even before ED arrival, telephone triage systems [18] and digital/online symptom checker tools used by the general public for self-triage have been deployed to guide decisions about ED attendance or ambulance activation, although systematic evaluations have highlighted variability in triage accuracy and important safety concerns [19]. Collectively, these efforts share the goal of stratifying risk at progressively earlier stages and informing decisions about resource mobilization and care pathways. In this context, the present study contributes a complementary perspective by quantifying, within a single cohort, how much discrimination is gained as prespecified information bundles are added. Future work should integrate robust external validation and recalibration with implementation-oriented design choices (threshold selection, accountability, and safety safeguards) and evaluate such systems prospectively to establish generalizable, clinically deployable decision support.

Conclusions

In this single-center retrospective cohort of adult ED encounters at a secondary-level emergency medical facility in Japan, ED disposition (discharged home vs. admission or worse) showed meaningful discrimination using triage-stage information alone. Discrimination increased with the addition of clinical-process indicators (BGA performed, CT performed, MRI performed, and consultation requested) and improved further when laboratory tests and diagnosis-related variables were incorporated. Together, these findings support a staged risk-stratification approach for early ED workflow planning, while underscoring the need for external validation, recalibration, and prospective evaluation before clinical implementation.
