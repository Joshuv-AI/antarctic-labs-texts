Clinical significance of CD161+CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment of esophageal squamous cell carcinoma and their association with the 4-1BB signaling pathway
Authors: Shao-jun Xu, Chao Chen, Ming-Qiang Ye, Jin Huang, Jia-hua Tu, Yan-Ming Shen, Yun-fan Luo, Zhao-Min Sun, Jie Chen, Ji-Hong Lin, Jun Guan, Han-Xing Huang, Shu Chen Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCD161+CD8+ T cells, a cytotoxic T cell subset, remain poorly defined in terms of their clinical significance and functional role in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), which has limited the advancement of precision immunotherapy strategies.MethodsWe integrated a multicenter cohort of ESCC patients who underwent either surgery or neoadjuvant therapy (chemotherapy or chemoimmunotherapy). Multiplex immunofluorescence staining, survival analyses, and assessment of major pathological response (MPR) and pathological complete response (pCR) were performed to elucidate the clinical relevance of CD161+CD8+ T cell infiltration. The functional state and cellular interactions of this subset were characterized using single-cell RNA sequencing. A CT-based radiomics model was also developed for non-invasive prediction.ResultsHigh intratumoral infiltration of CD161+CD8+ T cells was identified as an independent prognostic factor for prolonged overall survival and disease-free survival (both p<0.01) and appeared to correlate with a higher MPR and pCR rate following neoadjuvant therapy. This subset was enriched in treatment responders, exhibited transcriptional features associated with cytotoxicity and activation, and showed gene expression profiles suggestive of potential interactions with tumor-associated macrophages, possibly involving the TNFRSF9/TNFSF9 (4-1BB/4-1BBL) signaling axis. A radiomics model built on the XGBoost algorithm accurately predicted the infiltration level of this subset (area under the curve=0.889), and predicted high infiltration was correlated with favorable pathological response.ConclusionsWe identify CD161+CD8+ T cells as a pivotal prognostic and predictive biomarker in ESCC. This subset is associated with a putative 4-1BB/TNFSF9-mediated interaction network involving macrophages, correlating with a coordinated anti-tumor immune microenvironment. The CT-based radiomics model could provide a noninvasive means for assessing immune phenotypes, with potential applicability in patient stratification and treatment selection.Keywords: Esophageal Squamous Cell Carcinoma, Tumor Microenvironment, co-stimulatory molecules, Immunosuppression

Introduction

Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC) remains a malignancy of dire prognosis, presenting a persistent clinical challenge. Despite the introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) offering therapeutic promise for advanced-stage disease, objective response rates remain unsatisfactory (~20%),1 highlighting an urgent need for reliable predictive biomarkers. Currently employed biomarkers, such as programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB), exhibit limited predictive power.2 3 This limitation stems largely from the pronounced heterogeneity and immunosuppressive nature of the ESCC tumor immune microenvironment (TIME), which frequently presents a “cold” phenotype.4 Characterized by sparse effector T cell infiltration and an abundance of immunosuppressive cell populations—including myeloid-derived suppressor cells and regulatory T cells—this milieu fosters immune evasion and confers therapeutic resistance.5,7 A deeper understanding of the dynamic regulatory networks governing key immune cell subsets within the TIME is therefore critical for advancing ESCC immunotherapy.

A CD161-expressing subset of CD8+ T cells, exhibiting properties of both innate and adaptive immunity, has recently garnered significant interest. Characterized by the expression of the natural killer cell receptor CD161 (encoded by KLRB1), this population displays potent cytotoxicity, tissue-homing capability (particularly to mucosal sites), and memory potential.8,10 In several solid tumors, their intratumoral abundance correlates with favorable patient outcomes. For instance, in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, high infiltration of CD161+CD8+ T cells is associated with prolonged overall survival and an effector phenotype marked by elevated expression of cytokines (eg, TNF-α, IFN-γ) and co-inhibitory receptors (eg, TIM-3, TIGIT).11 12 Similarly, in HPV-associated head and neck squamous cell carcinoma, this subset enhances anti-tumor immunity via cytokine secretion.13 However, the function of CD161+CD8+ T cells appears context-dependent. In hepatocellular carcinoma, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) analyses have revealed a “pre-exhausted” state, co-expressing effector and regulatory signatures, suggesting a potentially dual or antagonistic role contingent on the local immune context.14

Despite this emerging pan-cancer relevance, the phenotypic characteristics, functional states, and definitive prognostic value of CD161+CD8+ T cells in ESCC—a cancer with a distinct etiological and anatomical profile—remain largely uncharted. Given the notably immunosuppressive and heterogeneous TIME of ESCC,4 15 a systematic investigation into the distribution, function, and regulatory circuitry of this T cell subset is of paramount importance. Such an exploration could unveil novel mechanisms of immune evasion specific to ESCC and provide a crucial foundation for developing new therapeutic targets, identifying patient subsets most likely to benefit from immunotherapy, and rationalizing combination treatment strategies.

To explore the role of CD161+CD8+ T cells in the ESCC immune microenvironment, this study integrated multicenter clinical data, single-cell sequencing, and radiomic analysis. Clinical analyses indicated that this T-cell subset shows some association with patient prognosis and treatment response. Single-cell sequencing results suggested its transcriptional state is related to immune activation. Based on these findings, we developed a CT-based imaging model to estimate its infiltration levels and predict treatment response, providing a reference for patient stratification and individualized therapy.

Methods

Patient selection and tissue samples

This study retrospectively enrolled 901 ESCC patients who underwent radical resection at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (FMUUH) and Putian First Hospital. Based on the treatment strategy, patients were allocated into four independent cohorts: a Surgery-only Cohort (n=562, comprising a training set of 186 patients, an internal validation set of 211 patients, and an external validation set of 165 patients), a Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy Cohort (NACT Cohort, n=219), a Neoadjuvant Immunotherapy combined with Chemotherapy Cohort (NICT Cohort, n=116), and a scRNA-seq Cohort (n=4). The sample selection flow diagram is detailed in online supplemental figure 1 and the flow chart of this study in figure 1.

Study flow chart. Overall design, cohort allocation (Surgery-only Cohort, NACT Cohort, NICT Cohort), and study objectives. AI, artificial intelligence; ESCC, esophageal squamous cell carcinoma; MPR, major pathological response; NACT, Neoadjuvant Chemotherapy Cohort; NICT, Neoadjuvant Immunotherapy combined with Chemotherapy Cohort; pCR, pathological complete response; ROC, receiver operating characteristic; scRNA-seq, single-cell RNA sequencing; TME, tumor microenvironment; UMAP, uniform manifold approximation and projection.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) histopathologically confirmed ESCC with an R0 resection margin and (2) availability of complete clinicopathological and survival follow-up data. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) a history of other primary malignancies and (2) unavailable or inadequately processed formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tumor tissue from the initial diagnostic biopsy.

Multiplex immunofluorescence and quantitative image analysis

To spatially map the infiltration and co-localization of CD161+CD8+ T cells and CD68+ macrophages, we performed multiplex immunofluorescence (mIHC) on FFPE tissue sections using tyramide signal amplification (TSA). Briefly, 4 µm sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval in EDTA buffer (pH 9.0). Sequential staining was performed in the order: (1) CD68 (rabbit monoclonal, Abcam ab213363; 1:500) with Opal 620, (2) CD8 (mouse monoclonal, ZSGB-BIO ZA-0508; 1:100) with Opal 570, and (3) CD161 (rabbit monoclonal, Abcam ab259916; 1:200) with Opal 520, with TSA amplification between each step.

Sequential TSA detection: CD161, CD8, and CD68 were sequentially labeled with HRP-TSA complexes. Steps included HRP inactivation and blocking, with thorough washing throughout. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI; single-stain/isotype controls validated specificity. Whole-slide images were acquired via high-resolution scanning and analyzed quantitatively using an AI platform (Aipathwell). Tumor regions were automatically segmented, and CD8+CD161+ double-positive cells were identified by a pretrained CNN (fluorescence intensity >3× background in isotype controls). Cell density was calculated as the mean of three high-power fields (200×, 0.305 mm²/field) in the tumor core. Automated counts were independently verified by two blinded board-certified pathologists, with discrepancies resolved by consensus.

The method was validated, showing good consistency between single-plex and multiplex staining, and the AI analysis pipeline demonstrated stability (online supplemental figure 2). Further spatial analysis: cells were segmented using the StarDist model, features were extracted, and subsets were identified via UMAP clustering. Nearest-neighbor distances from CD68+ cells to CD161+CD8+ cells were calculated, distance distributions were plotted, and inter-group differences were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test.

Plasma cytokine profiling

Circulating levels of 12 cytokines (IL-1β, IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-6, IL-8, IL-10, IL-12, IL-17, TNF-α, IFN-γ, IFN-α) were quantified using a bead-based multiplex immunoassay (Luminex xMAP technology) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Hemolyzed or lipemic plasma samples were excluded, and freeze-thaw cycles were minimized to preserve analyte stability. Briefly, 50 µL of plasma or standard was incubated with antibody-conjugated magnetic beads for 2 hours at room temperature with shaking. After washing, a biotinylated detection antibody cocktail was added for 1 hour, followed by streptavidin-R-phycoerythrin for 30 min. Following final washes, beads were resuspended and analyzed on a Luminex 200 system. Cytokine concentrations were interpolated from a 5-parameter logistic standard curve generated for each analyte.

scRNA-seq and data processing

Fresh ESCC tissues were dissociated immediately after resection to generate single-cell suspensions. Only suspensions with viability >85% and total cell count >2×10⁴ were processed. Single-cell capture, barcoding, and cDNA library construction were performed using the Singleron GEXSCOPE platform. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (PE150). Raw sequencing data were processed with the Celescope pipeline to generate a gene-expression matrix, including steps for cell barcode/UMI assignment, read alignment, and gene-level quantification.

Analysis of scRNA-seq and paired scTCR-seq data

Data analysis was conducted in R using the Seurat package. After filtering low-quality cells (high mitochondrial gene percentage or low feature count) and normalizing for library size, the top 2000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA). The first 20 principal components were used for graph-based clustering (resolution=0.8). Cell clusters were annotated based on canonical marker expression: epithelial cells (EPCAM, KRT5), T/NK cells (CD3D, CD8A, NKG7), B cells (CD19, MS4A1), myeloid cells (APOE, C1QA), endothelial cells (PECAM1), fibroblasts (DCN, ACTA2), and mast cells (TPSAB1). The T-cell compartment was re-clustered to identify subsets including CD8+ effector (GZMB+), exhausted (PDCD1+), follicular helper (BCL6+), naïve (CCR7+), regulatory (FOXP3+), and CD161+CD8+ T cells (KLRB1+CD8A+).

Paired scTCR-seq data were integrated to assess clonality. T-cell clonotypes were defined by identical CDR3 amino acid sequences. Clonotype expansion, trajectory inference (Monocle 3), and gene-set enrichment analysis (GSEA) were performed to characterize the differentiation and functional state of CD161+CD8+ T cells.

For identifying cell type-specific differentially expressed genes, we employed a method based on a generalized linear mixed model (specifically implemented using the MASTrapackage in R). In this model, we included “patient individual” as a random effect to control for inherent variation between individuals, while “cell cluster” or “experimental condition of interest” was included as a fixed effect. This ensures that the expression differences we identify are more likely to reflect genuine biological variation rather than random inter-individual fluctuations.

Immune cell states were systematically characterized through integrated analyses: M1/M2 signature scores quantified macrophage polarization; Monocle 3 pseudotemporal analysis delineated state transitions in TNFSF9/TNFRSF9-defined subsets; SCENIC revealed transcription factor regulatory networks; differential expression profiling identified secretory signatures; and T-cell functional states were defined by co-expression patterns of effector, exhaustion, and survival molecules. All analyses were corrected for multiple testing (p<0.05).

Cell–cell communication analysis

Cell–cell interactions were inferred using two complementary approaches. First, the CellChat package was applied to identify significant ligand–receptor pairs among annotated cell clusters. Second, the NicheNet framework was used to predict upstream ligands regulating gene-expression programs in target cells (eg, differential genes in major pathological response (MPR) responders versus non-responders, or in CD161+CD8+ T cells vs other T-cell subsets). Ligand activity scores were calculated, and regulatory networks were visualized.

Single-cell trajectory inference

The differentiation dynamics of CD161+CD8+ T cells were reconstructed using an integrated trajectory analysis. RNA velocity was computed with Velocyto/scVelo in dynamical mode. The Dynamo algorithm was applied to model gene-expression kinetics. Finally, Slingshot was used to fit principal curves onto a diffusion-map embedding, delineating potential state transitions from naïve to effector/exhausted phenotypes.

Bioinformatic analysis

Integrated analysis of scRNA-seq data (GSE197677) and bulk transcriptomic data (GSE53622) was conducted to characterize CD8+ T cell heterogeneity in ESCC. After quality control, normalization, and Harmony-based batch correction, 100 104 high-quality cells were analyzed through PCA dimensionality reduction and UMAP clustering. CD8+ T cell subpopulations were annotated using established marker genes and further stratified into CD161+ and CD161⁻ subsets based on KLRB1 expression. Differential expression analysis identified 485 significantly upregulated genes in CD161+CD8+ T cells. Univariate Cox regression analysis of these genes in the GSE53622 cohort revealed an 11-gene prognostic signature. Using ssGSEA algorithm, enrichment scores were calculated and patients were stratified into high-risk and low-risk groups based on median scores, showing significant survival differences in Kaplan-Meier analysis.

Study cohort and data processing workflow for CT-based radiomics

This retrospective study established clear inclusion and exclusion criteria to improve cohort consistency. Eligible patients were treatment-naïve individuals with locally advanced ESCC scheduled to receive NICT. All patients underwent baseline contrast-enhanced CT scans before treatment and had complete clinicopathological data and follow-up records. Exclusion criteria mainly included cases with significant imaging artifacts or missing key data.

To ensure data quality, a dual-independent review and expert adjudication process was implemented for patient screening. Imaging data were required to meet the following basic standards: complete scan coverage of relevant anatomical regions, image quality sufficient for quantitative analysis, and standardized data formats compatible with automated processing.

Although imaging equipment originated from different vendors, key acquisition parameters (eg, tube voltage 120 kV, slice thickness 5 mm, standard reconstruction kernel) were harmonized to control technical variability. All imaging data underwent systematic preprocessing before analysis, including grayscale normalization, noise reduction filtering, and isotropic resampling. These steps were performed using standardized tools such as PyRadiomics to enhance methodological reproducibility.

Arterial-phase contrast-enhanced CT images acquired before treatment were used. Two experienced thoracic radiologists, blinded to clinical data, independently performed slice-by-slice manual segmentation of the primary tumor to define the gross tumor volume (GTV). A third senior radiologist adjudicated discrepancies to generate a consensus GTV. A peritumoral volume of interest (VOI) was created by isotropically expanding the GTV by 5 mm. The combined intratumoral and peritumoral VOI was used for feature extraction.

Using PyRadiomics (V.3.0) compliant with the Imaging Biomarker Standardization Initiative, 1223 radiomic features were extracted, encompassing shape, first-order statistics, and texture classes. Images were resampled to 1.0 × 1.0 × 1.0 mm³ isotropic voxels, and intensity was normalized. The ComBat algorithm corrected for inter-scanner batch effects.

A multistep selection process was employed: (1) Mann-Whitney U test to remove non-discriminative features; (2) maximum Relevance Minimum Redundancy to reduce redundancy; and (3) LASSO regression to identify the most predictive feature subset. Using this subset, 11 machine-learning classifiers (eg, XGBoost, Random Forest, SVM) were trained and evaluated via fivefold cross-validation. Class imbalance was addressed using SMOTE. Model performance was compared using the DeLong test on area under the curve (AUC) values. The optimal model was interpreted via SHAP analysis.

Treatment protocols and efficacy assessment

The NACT regimen consisted of a taxane combined with platinum, with or without fluorouracil, administered every 3 weeks for 2–4 cycles. The neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NICT) regimen added a PD-1 inhibitor to the same chemotherapy backbone. Radical esophagectomy was performed 4–8 weeks after treatment, primarily via a minimally invasive approach, with gastric conduit reconstruction.

Pathological response was assessed using the TRG grading system: TRG1–2 (≤10% residual viable tumor) was defined as MPR, and TRG1 as pathological complete response (pCR). Overall survival (OS) was calculated from the date of surgery to death from any cause, and disease-free survival (DFS) to recurrence or death. Patients alive and recurrence-free at the last follow-up were censored.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages), and continuous variables as medians (IQRs). Between-group comparisons for categorical variables were performed using the χ² or Fisher’s exact test; for continuous variables, the Mann-Whitney U test (two-group) or Kruskal-Wallis test (multigroup) was applied. Survival analyses were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method (log-rank test) and Cox regression (univariable/multivariable). The interaction between CD161+CD8+ T-cell density and treatment regimen was evaluated by including an interaction term (likelihood-ratio test), and the proportional-hazards assumption was verified using Schoenfeld residuals. Model discrimination was assessed via t-AUC in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. DeLong’s test for comparing ROC curves.

Predictive performance of the radiomics model was evaluated using ROC curves (cut-off determined by the Youden index), and subgroup results are presented as forest plots. For all statistical tests involving multiple comparisons (eg, differential expression analysis across cell clusters in scRNA-seq), the Benjamini-Hochberg method was applied to control the false discovery rate, with an adjusted q-value<0.05 considered statistically significant. All tests were two-sided, with p<0.05 regarded as statistically significant. Analyses were performed using R V.4.3.0 and SPSS V.26.0.

Results

Clinical significance of CD161+CD8+ T cells in ESCC: prognostic value, predictive efficacy, and association with treatment response

This study systematically investigated the clinical significance of CD161+CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment (TME) of ESCC using multicenter cohorts (online supplemental table 1). Patients were stratified into high- and low-infiltration groups (figure 2A) based on the median infiltration density of this subset (27.3 cells/HPF) (online supplemental figure 3) and corresponding thresholds for CD8+ (online supplemental figure 4A) and CD3+ T cells (online supplemental figure 4B). Analysis revealed that high CD161+CD8+ T-cell infiltration was not associated with conventional pathological parameters such as TNM stage, but showed a significant correlation with overall T-cell infiltration levels, suggesting that it reflects biological features independent of anatomical staging (online supplemental table 2).

(B) Kaplan-Meier survival curves comparing overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) between CD161+CD8+ T-cell high-infiltration and low-infiltration groups in the training cohort, internal validation, and external validation cohorts. Survival analysis demonstrated that the high-infiltration group had significantly better 5-year OS and DFS in the training, internal validation, and external validation cohorts (figure 2B). Multivariable Cox regression confirmed that low CD161+CD8+ T-cell infiltration was an independent risk factor for poor prognosis, whereas total CD3+/CD8+ T cells lacked independent prognostic value (online supplemental tables 3–5). A combined model integrating T/N stage and this immune marker significantly outperformed the conventional TNM staging system in predicting survival outcomes (figure 2C).

Further stratified analysis by treatment regimen showed that the interaction terms in the multivariate models were not statistically significant, suggesting that the prognostic impact of this marker was generally consistent across treatment groups. In the stratified analysis, patients with low infiltration in the neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy subgroup had the highest prognostic risk (OS HR=4.74, DFS HR=3.78), while the surgery-alone subgroup showed a relatively lower increase in risk (OS HR=2.70, DFS HR=2.76) (online supplemental figure 5A,B).

In the neoadjuvant treatment setting, high CD161+CD8+ T-cell infiltration was associated with a higher MPR rate and better prognosis (online supplemental table 6; online supplemental figure 6A,B), with its predictive value being particularly pronounced in the immunotherapy-combined cohort: it not only correlated with a higher MPR rate but also significantly predicted pCR, demonstrating a predictive efficacy comparable to that of PD-L1 expression (MPR: p=0.597; pCR: p=0.781) (online supplemental table 7; figure 3A–C). Dynamic monitoring showed that this T-cell subset expanded significantly in treatment responders (figure 3D). Correspondingly, patients with high infiltration exhibited substantial long-term survival benefits after neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (figure 3E).

Single-cell sequencing analysis of the potential role of CD161+CD8+ T cells in NICT response

To decipher the immune mechanisms underlying differential responses to NICT, we performed scRNA-seq on paired tumor biopsies from four ESCC patients collected before and after treatment, stratified by pathological response (MPR vs non-MPR) (n=4). After quality control, 45 819 high-quality cells were analyzed. Unsupervised clustering resolved 10 major lineages, recapitulating the cellular complexity of the ESCC microenvironment (online supplemental figure 7A). Comparative analysis revealed a profound reshaping of the TME in responders: the non-MPR group was dominated by malignant epithelial cells, whereas the MPR group exhibited a significant expansion of the lymphoid compartment, particularly cytotoxic T cells (online supplemental figure 7B).

A Transcriptionally Active CD161+CD8+ T-Cell Subset is Enriched in Responders. Subclustering of the T-cell compartment identified a selective enrichment of effector-like subsets in the MPR group, including CD8+ effector T cells, CD8+ precursor-exhausted T cells, and the population of interest—CD161+CD8+ T cells (figure 4A). GSEA of this subset demonstrated significant activation of pathways governing T-cell activation, lymphocyte proliferation, immune effector processes, and cytotoxicity (q<0.001) (figure 4B).

Clonal and developmental relationship with effector T cells: To trace the origin of this subset, we integrated paired TCR sequencing. Clonotype analysis revealed substantial overlap between CD161+CD8+ T cells and canonical CD8+ effector T cells (q<0.001) (figure 4C), indicating a shared clonal ancestry. Pseudotime trajectory inference positioned CD161+CD8+ T cells on a continuum with effector T cells, occupying an adjacent, non-exhausted state along the differentiation path (q<0.01) (figure 4D).

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of CD161+CD8+ T cells within the TME, we performed systematic cell–cell communication analysis (figure 4E). The results revealed a prominent interaction network between CD161+CD8+ T cells and macrophages in the MPR TME, which was specifically enhanced in treatment responders (figure 4F–H).

Validation by mIHC and spatial analysis in an independent cohort confirmed spatial co-localization and a positive correlation between the densities of CD161+CD8+ T cells and macrophages exclusively in MPR samples (online supplemental figure 8A). Nearest-neighbor distance analysis showed that the median distance between the two cell types was shorter in MPR patients (30.9 µm), with distances mostly concentrated within 30 µm, whereas non-MPR patients exhibited a longer median distance (55.2 µm), suggesting that immune cells are more prone to form localized clusters conducive to direct interactions in the responsive TME (online supplemental figure 8B–D). At the molecular level, the TNFSF9/TNFRSF9 (4-1BBL/4-1BB) pathway is involved in this interaction. In MPR patients, macrophages showed elevated TNFSF9 expression while T cells simultaneously exhibited increased TNFRSF9 expression (q<0.001) (figure 4I) (online supplemental figure 9).

Observations on the association between TNFSF9 and macrophage/T-cell function

Characteristic gene scoring analysis revealed that TNFSF9-high macrophages exhibited elevated M1 signature scores (q<0.001) (online supplemental figure 10A). Pseudotemporal analysis suggested that this subset branched early in the differentiation trajectory, potentially representing a cell population with an inherent activation tendency (online supplemental figure 10B). Regulatory network analysis indicated that this subset specifically activated transcription factors such as AP-1 complex members and ETS2, differing from the STAT1-dominant pathway in classical M1 macrophages (online supplemental figure 10C).

This CD161+CD8+ T cell population co-expressed effector molecules (eg, GZMB, IFNG), exhaustion-associated genes (eg, PDCD1), and survival-related receptors (eg, IL7R), displaying a state consistent with coexistence of activation and partial exhaustion features (online supplemental figure 11A). In TNFRSF9-high cells, sustained expression of T-cell function-related genes (eg, CXCL13) was observed, along with altered expression levels of various immune-regulatory factors (eg, CCL2, CCL4) (online supplemental figure 11B–D).

Further analysis showed that CLEC2D (encoding LLT1) was highly expressed in terminally exhausted T-cell subsets and was associated with treatment non-response (online supplemental figure 12A). This gene was also expressed in myeloid cells, suggesting its potential involvement in cross-cellular signaling regulation (online supplemental figure 12B). Concurrent elevation of CLEC2D levels in TNFSF9-high cells indicated that 4-1BB signaling activation may coexist with inhibitory signaling pathways (online supplemental figure 12C).

Integrated single-cell and transcriptomic analysis reveals CD161+CD8+ T-cell-associated gene signature in ESCC

Analysis of single-cell data from CD45+ immune cells in ESCC and normal tissues (GSE197677) identified CD161+ and CD161⁻ subsets within CD8+ T cells (online supplemental figure 13A). Differential expression analysis identified 485 genes upregulated in the CD161+ subset, with functional enrichment suggesting an association with adaptive immunity and cytotoxic activity (online supplemental figure 13B).

Using an ESCC transcriptomic cohort with survival information (GSE53622), an 11-gene prognostic signature was constructed. Signature scores were calculated via the ssGSEA algorithm, and patients were stratified into high- and low-score groups based on the median value. Survival analysis indicated that patients in the low-score group had shorter OS, suggesting a potential association between this gene signature and patient prognosis (online supplemental figure 13C,D).

Putative association of the CD161+CD8+ T-cell–macrophage axis with systemic immune modulation

Prior literature indicates that 4-1BB engagement can activate downstream signaling cascades (eg, NF-κB, MAPK) implicated in T-cell proliferation and effector differentiation.16 We therefore investigated the systemic immunological correlates of this intratumoral axis.

Profiling 12 plasma cytokines in 60 NICT-treated patients revealed that pre-treatment tumor infiltration by CD161+CD8+ T cells correlated positively with a pro-inflammatory, Th1-skewed cytokine milieu (eg, IL-2, IL-12, TNF-α, IFN-γ) and inversely with IL-1β (online supplemental figure 14A,B). Following therapy, this association evolved dynamically: in responders (MPR/pCR), we observed a significant post-treatment increase in Th1-type cytokines (IL-2, IL-12, TNF-α) and a concomitant decrease in suppressive or pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-1β, IL-6, IL-8, and IL-10 (figure 5A–C).

A non-invasive radiomics signature for pretreatment identification of the responsive immune phenotype

To explore a non-invasive method for assessing CD161+CD8+ T-cell infiltration levels, this study developed a radiomics model based on pretreatment CT images (figure 6A). Imaging features were extracted from the peritumoral regions of 109 NICT patients, and seven key features were selected via LASSO regression (online supplemental figure 15). The XGBoost model demonstrated relatively good predictive performance in the training set (figure 6B). SHAP analysis identified the most contributory features to the model’s decision (figure 6C,D).

The derived radiomics score showed a negative correlation with the actual density of CD161+CD8+ T cells (figure 6E,F; online supplemental figure 16A). Patients with a low score exhibited a trend toward higher pathological response rates, and the score demonstrated some predictive value for pathological response (figure 6G–I; online supplemental figure 16B). However, the score was not significantly associated with the densities of total CD8+/CD3+ T cells, suggesting a degree of specificity for the particular immune microenvironment (online supplemental figure 17A–D). Correlations were observed between three key radiomic features and immune infiltration/necrosis extent. wavelet.LHL_firstorder_Median showed negative correlations with CD161+CD8+ T-cell density (r=−0.45, p<0.001) and necrosis proportion (r=−0.33, p=0.016). wavelet.HHL_firstorder_Maximum was negatively correlated with immune cell density (r=−0.39, p=0.004) but positively correlated with necrosis (r=0.34, p=0.016). The Small Area High Gray Level Emphasis feature exhibited similar trends (immune infiltration r=−0.37, p=0.006; necrosis r=0.32, p=0.018) (online supplemental figure 18A–F; online supplemental figure 19A–F). These associations suggest that imaging features may reflect spatial relationships between immune and necrotic structures within the TME.

Discussion

Immune checkpoint therapy has transformed the landscape of cancer treatment, yet its application in ESCC remains limited by a lack of biomarkers and insufficient understanding of immune responses.2 4 This study integrated multi-center clinical data, single-cell sequencing, and radiomics to preliminarily explore the potential role of tumor-infiltrating CD161+CD8+ T cells in ESCC. The findings suggest that this T-cell subset is associated with a putative 4-1BB/TNFSF9-mediated interaction network involving macrophages. Based on this, we developed a non-invasive CT-based assessment method, offering a potential tool for pretherapeutic patient stratification (figure 7).

Core findings of the study: CD161+CD8+ T cells interact with tumor-associated macrophages via binding of the TNFRSF9 (4-1BB) receptor, expressed on their surface, to the TNFSF9 (4-1BBL) ligand highly expressed on macrophages. Through secretion of cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, along with sustained co-stimulatory signaling, this interaction establishes a self-reinforcing positive-feedback immune-synergistic loop. This loop drives a potent local anti-tumor immune response and induces systemic Th1-type cytokine polarization, ultimately leading to enhanced immune efficacy and long-term survival benefit. TME, tumor microenvironment.

Our findings establish CD161+CD8+ T cells as a robust, TNM-stage-independent prognostic biomarker. The consistent association between high infiltration and superior survival across independent surgical cohorts, along with its additive value beyond traditional TNM staging, challenges the current anatomy-centric prognostic paradigm.17 18 These findings highlight that the functional composition of the TIME conveys biological information beyond tumor burden and support its integration into future risk-stratification models.19,22 While the interaction between treatment modality and CD161+CD8+ T-cell density was not statistically significant, stratified analysis revealed a clear quantitative risk gradient. The adverse prognostic effect of low CD161+CD8+ T-cell infiltration was most pronounced in patients receiving neoadjuvant chemo-immunotherapy (NICT), moderate in those treated with other therapies, and smallest—though still significant—in those undergoing surgery alone. This pattern suggests the prognostic significance of this T-cell subset is context-dependent, potentially amplified in therapeutic settings where adaptive immune responses are a critical role. The predictive power of this subset is particularly pronounced in the context of NICT. Patients with high pre-treatment infiltration were significantly more likely to achieve a pCR—a robust surrogate for long-term survival—and enjoyed markedly superior outcomes. This positions CD161+CD8+ T cells as a biomarker that appears to outperform conventional markers like PD-L1 and TMB in predicting deep response to immunotherapy combinations. Our study identifies CD161+CD8+ T cells as a key predictive biomarker for neoadjuvant immunotherapy in ESCC. Compared with PD-L1, it shows superior predictive value and dynamic expansion in responders, transitioning from a static marker to a real-time immune activity indicator. Simultaneous assessment of “immune effector force” (CD161+ T cells) and “immune brake signal” (PD-L1) enhances prediction accuracy and provides a more comprehensive view of the tumor-immune interface. Observations suggest that high CD161+ infiltration correlates with immunotherapy benefit irrespective of PD-L1 status, while low CD161+ infiltration may limit efficacy even in PD-L1-positive cases. These findings support the development of microenvironment-based precision immunotherapy strategies.

Mechanistically, the observed predictive association appears consistent with specific biological characteristics of this T-cell subset.23,25 Integrated single-cell and TCR analyses indicate that CD161+CD8+ T cells exhibit clonal relatedness to effector T cells and display a precursor-like state during differentiation, suggesting a potential “immune reservoir”–like function within the TIME.26 27 Existing models propose that immunotherapy may act not by de novo immune activation but via modulation of the inhibited state of this reservoir population, promoting its differentiation into effector cells.28,30

Further investigation revealed that the functional activity of CD161+CD8+ T cells correlates with their interaction with tumor-associated macrophages, in which the 4-1BB/4-1BBL co-stimulatory axis may play a significant role. Activation of this pathway was observed in treatment responders, consistent with a potential bidirectional regulatory relationship between macrophages and T cells.16 31 32 Macrophages likely provide essential co-stimulation to “prime” the T cells, which in turn, through cytokine secretion, may further polarize macrophages toward an anti-tumor phenotype.33 34 These findings provide a reference for exploring combination targeting strategies.35

Spatial quantitative analysis reveals spatial proximity between CD161+CD8+ T cells and macrophages in the ESCC microenvironment (mean distance: 30.9 µm), reaching a distance suggestive of potential interaction. This provides morphological reference for studying the formation of functional connections between the two via the 4-1BB pathway. Mechanistic studies suggest that 4-1BB signaling may exert differential effects in different immune cells: in myeloid cells, it is associated with the maintenance of specific pro-inflammatory subtype phenotypes; in T cells, it is linked to the establishment of an effector-exhaustion transitional state. It is noteworthy that this signaling pathway may have dual effects on anti-tumor immunity and T-cell exhaustion. Our study further indicates that CD161 serves a dual role as both a lineage marker (indicating a specific differentiation state) and an active regulatory receptor (mediating inhibitory signals through LLT1). Its co-existence with 4-1BB signaling suggests that T-cell function is finely regulated by a synergistic stimulatory/inhibitory network. Future research should further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of their interaction and explore strategies to target this balance for optimizing immunotherapy in ESCC. The functional impact of this axis extends beyond the tumor niche. We observed that high CD161+CD8+ T-cell infiltration correlates with a systemic Th1-skewed cytokine profile (elevated IL-2, IL-12, IFN-γ, TNF-α). This is consistent with the notion that an effective local immune response may influence a pro-inflammatory systemic state, which could contribute to controlling micrometastases and explains the association with prolonged DFS.29 36 37 It also raises the possibility of using peripheral immune signatures as accessible surrogates of the intratumoral immune context.

We explored the feasibility of predicting CD161+CD8+ T-cell infiltration levels using CT-based imaging features. In the neoadjuvant treatment cohort, the imaging model demonstrated a correlation with the degree of pathological response. This non-invasive assessment method, building on existing clinical imaging data, provides a potential tool for quantifying the TIME.38 39 The “virtual biopsy” concept may offer reference information to inform clinical treatment decisions.40,43 A key feature of our model is its specificity for identifying CD161+CD8+ T cells, rather than the entire CD8+ population. Radiomic analysis revealed that specific imaging features serve as potential non-invasive biomarkers for an immune-excluded or necrotic microenvironment; their negative correlation with CD161+CD8+ T-cell density links the imaging phenotype to the underlying immune landscape rather than presenting it as an independent biological narrative. This approach suggests that CT-based radiomics may contribute to the non-invasive assessment of functional states within the immune microenvironment. To further explore clinical applicability, the following directions could be considered: promoting standardization of CT scanning protocols (eg, 120 kVp, soft-tissue reconstruction, ≤5 mm slice thickness); conducting small-sample local validation at new centers (n=10–20); and developing automated analysis workflows to reduce technical variability.

Our study has several limitations. First, while multi-center, the sample size of the NICT cohort warrants validation in larger, prospective trials. Second, the precise role of the CD161 receptor itself in ESCC requires functional dissection; its engagement with ligands like LLT1 may impart contextual signals that modulate T-cell function.8 Third, the generalizability of the radiomics model must be rigorously tested across diverse imaging platforms and patient populations. Future work should focus on: (1) prospective validation of the biomarker and radiomics model; (2) mechanistic studies to dissect the CD161 signaling pathway; and (3) exploring therapeutic strategies, such as 4-1BB agonists, designed to amplify the identified macrophage-T-cell axis. 44 45Additionally, while CD161+CD8+ T cells correlate with overall T-cell infiltration, it remains unclear whether this subset represents a distinct biological entity associated with anti-tumor immunity or merely serves as a proxy for a globally “hot” TME. We acknowledge the exploratory nature of the mechanistic insights derived from our limited scRNA-seq cohort. While the prognostic value of the identified CD161+CD8+ T-cell signature was validated in an independent bulk RNA-seq cohort, the detailed functional and trajectory analyses remain hypothesis-generating. Future validation in larger-scale scRNA-seq studies is warranted.

This study explores the construction of a multidimensional framework for analyzing the immune microenvironment in ESCC. Observations indicate that CD161+CD8+ T cells are associated with the anti-tumor response, correlating with macrophage-mediated co-stimulatory processes. Additionally, a non-invasive assessment method has been developed for further investigation. These findings provide reference points for the future development of more immune-targeted diagnostic and therapeutic approaches for ESCC.
