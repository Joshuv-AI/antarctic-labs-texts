Sevoflurane induces developmental cardiotoxicity via AMPKα2 dysregulation in zebrafish
Authors: Huiyu Hao, Yuanhao Li, Xiaoyu Li, Qiaofang Zhang, Ruoting Bai, Kexin Shi, Xiaoli Yang, Fangqi Duan, Jiangang Xu, Zhiqiang Yu, Dongyan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSevoflurane, a widely used volatile anesthetic, has raised concerns regarding its potential developmental toxicity, particularly due to its extensive application in non-obstetric surgeries and fetal intervention procedures during pregnancy. However, its effects on heart development and function remain unclear. Using zebrafish larvae as a model, we investigated the effects of prolonged sevoflurane exposure (0.04–0.08%) from 10 to 72 h post-fertilization (hpf). Under these conditions, treated larvae exhibited dose-dependent developmental abnormalities, including reduced body length, pericardial edema, and impaired heart tube looping. Cardiac function analysis revealed significant decreases in ejection fraction, stroke volume, heart rate, and cardiac output, indicating impaired cardiac contractility and pumping efficiency. These functional impairments were accompanied by structural changes including ventricular wall thinning and chamber dilation, along with upregulation of cardiac stress markers (nppa, nppb) - characteristic features of dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM). Molecular analysis demonstrated downregulation of sarcomeric (tnnt2a, mybpc3) and calcium-handling (atp2a2a, slc8a1a) genes, suggesting disruption of sarcomere integrity and calcium homeostasis. Additionally, sevoflurane exposure elevated inflammatory cytokines (il-6, tnf-α, il-1β) and promoted leukocyte infiltration into cardiac tissue. RNA sequencing analysis implicated dysregulation of Apelin signaling pathway, with reduced prkaa2 (AMPKα2) expression and phosphorylation observed in both zebrafish and H9C2 cardiomyocytes. Critically, pharmacological activation of AMPK using A-769662 effectively mitigated sevoflurane-induced cardiotoxicity, identifying AMPKα2 as a potential therapeutic target. Collectively, these findings delineate the molecular mechanisms underlying sevoflurane-induced developmental cardiotoxicity following prolonged exposure in zebrafish and suggest that targeting AMPKα2 signaling merits investigation as a potential strategy to mitigate anesthetic-related cardiac developmental risks.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-026-01553-8.Keywords: Sevoflurane, AMPK, Zebrafish, Cardiotoxicity, DCM

Introduction

Anesthetics toxicity has emerged as a significant health issue due to its extensive application in various surgical operations and invasive medical procedures [1–3]. Numerous studies have focused on the neurotoxicity of anesthetics on the developing brain [2, 3], often neglecting their potential side effects on cardiac development.

Recent studies have reported that the rate of anesthesia-related cardiac arrest in children younger than one year is significantly higher than that in older children [4]. Adverse responses to anesthetics are the leading cause of anesthesia-related cardiac arrest in neonates and infants [4], indicating the potential negative effect of anesthetics on cardiac function in this populations. Annually, more than 75,000 pregnant women in the United States undergo non-obstetric surgeries and fetal intervention procedures with anesthesia during pregnancy [5]. There is a risk of anesthesia-induced cardiac toxicity to the fetus during these procedures due to the early onset of heart development in the first trimester, the ability of most anesthetics to traverse the placenta, and the known susceptibility of developing hearts to dysfunction caused by anesthetics [3, 6]. Despite these risks, the specific effects of anesthetics on heart development and heart function remain poorly understood.

Sevoflurane, a widely used volatile anesthetic, has been associated with neurotoxicity and cardiac dysfunction. Previous studies have demonstrated that sevoflurane can activate nuclear factor kappa B, trigger inflammation in the hippocampus, increase intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS), disrupt calcium homeostasis, and promote apoptosis in neurons [7–9]. Additionally, sevoflurane has been reported to induce negative inotropic and chronotropic effects on the heart, potentially leading to a reduction in cardiac output and blood pressure [10, 11]. However, the impact of sevoflurane on cardiac development and heart function remains largely unexplored.

This study aimed to elucidate how sevoflurane affects the heart development and function in zebrafish and to develop target intervention strategies. Zebrafish have proven to be an excellent model organism due to their high fertility, external development, and physiological similarities to mammals. Notably, the zebrafish heart is structurally and genetically comparable to that of humans [12]. Here, we evaluated the effects of sevoflurane and provide evidence that sevoflurane disrupts heart tube looping and causes dilated cardiomyopathy (DCM)-like symptoms including reduced cardiac contractility, enlarged ventricle cavity, and upregulation of nppa and nppb. To investigate the underlying cardiotoxicity, we analyzed key pathways and found that sevoflurane disrupts sarcomeric integrity, calcium handling, and promotes cardiac inflammation with enhanced leukocyte infiltration. Mechanistically, RNA sequencing revealed significant enrichment of the Apelin signaling pathway and identified prkaa2 (encoding AMPKα2) as a key downregulated gene. Importantly, we demonstrated that decreased AMPKα2 abundance and phosphorylation are central to sevoflurane’s cardiotoxic effects in both zebrafish and H9C2 cardiomyocytes. Critically, pharmacological activation of AMPK with A-769662 effectively attenuated sevoflurane-induced cardiac dysfunction in vivo and in vitro, providing preclinical evidence for a potential therapeutic strategy targeting AMPKα2 to alleviate sevoflurane-associated cardiac dysfunction. These findings collectively illuminate the molecular underpinnings of sevoflurane-induced cardiotoxicity and offer a promising intervention approach for clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Zebrafish husbandry

The zebrafish AB strain, along with the Tg (myl7: GFP), Tg (lyz: DsRed), and Tg (mpeg1.1: GFP) strains, were utilized and raised at 28 °C in an automated housing system with a photoperiod of 14 h light:10 h dark. Adult zebrafish were fed with brine shrimp daily. Larvae were maintained in E3 medium with methylene blue.

Wild-type AB strain zebrafish and transgenic lines [Tg(myl7:GFP), Tg(lyz:DsRed), and Tg(mpeg1.1:GFP)] were maintained at 28 °C in a recirculating aquaculture system under a 14:10 h light: dark cycle. Adults were fed live brine shrimp twice daily, while larvae were reared in E3 embryo medium (5 mM NaCl, 0.17 mM KCl, 0.33 mM CaCl₂, 0.33 mM MgSO₄) supplemented with 0.003% methylene blue. All procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of Nankai University (Protocol No. 2008) and conducted in compliance with NIH guidelines for laboratory animal care (Publication No. 8023, revised 1996).

Cell culture

The H9C2 rat cardiomyocyte cell line (Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, China) was cultured in high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 10% heat-inactivated fetal bovine serum, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO₂ atmosphere, with media changes every 48 h and subculturing at 80–90% confluence using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA.

Drug exposure

Sevoflurane (Hengrui Pharmaceutical) was dissolved in dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 44% (v/v) stock solution, stored protected from light at 25 °C. Working concentrations (0.01–0.15% v/v) were prepared in E3 medium immediately before use. Embryos were exposed to freshly prepared sevoflurane (0.04%, 0.06%, or 0.08% v/v) in E3 medium from 10 to 72 hpf and from 10 to 30 hpf using a static aqueous system in sealed 6-cm dishes (10 mL medium). The vehicle control contained 0.1% DMSO. Solutions were refreshed every 12 h. Mortality was recorded at 6, 10, 24, 48, and 72 hpf. For AMPK activation experiments, larvae were co-treated with 0.06% sevoflurane and A-769662 (25, 50, or 100 µM; MedChemExpress) from 10 to 72 hpf.

Cells at 70% confluence were exposed to 0.04–0.08% sevoflurane for 24 h. For AMPK activation, cells were co-treated with 0.06% sevoflurane and A-769662 (25–100 µM) for 24 h.

Zebrafish morphological evaluation

Embryos were exposed to 0.04%, 0.06%, or 0.08% sevoflurane and imaged at 10, 24, 48, and 72 hpf using a stereomicroscope (Leica M205 FA). Body length, areas of head, eye and pericardial in AB strain larvae were quantified in AB strain larvae at 72 hpf using ImageJ (NIH). Cardiac morphology was assessed by measuring the BA-SV distance (linear span between the bulbus arteriosus and sinus venosus) from lateral-view images using Tg (myl7: GFP) larvae at 72 hpf.

H9C2 cell morphometry

Cells were seeded on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips (40% confluence) prior to drug treatment. Post-exposure, cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and stained with 100nM phalloidin for 1 h and 1 mg/mL DAPI for 15 min. After PBS washing, H9C2 cells were visualized with a fluorescence microscope (Olympus, Japan) and analyzed with ImageJ software. Cell size was determined by measuring the cross-sectional area of phalloidin-stained cytoskeletons.

Anesthesia evaluation

Wild-type zebrafish larvae (120 hpf) were immersed in sevoflurane solutions ranging from 0.01% to 0.15%. Anesthesia induction time was defined as the interval from immersion until loss of the tail-withdrawal reflex, assessed by forceps pinch applied every 10 s [13, 14]. The response loss rate was calculated for each time point in all experimental groups, with each group consisting of 15 larvae.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (HE) staining

The larvae at 72 hpf were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) overnight at 4 °C. After the larvae were embedded in paraffin, and 8-µm-thick sections were cut and mounted onto glass slides. The tissue samples were stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and photographed under a microscope [15].

O-Dianisidine staining

Larvae exposed to sevoflurane were dechorionated and immersed in a staining solution containing 0.6 mg/ml O-dianisidine, 0.01 M sodium acetate (pH 4.5), 0.65% H2O2 and 40% ethanol in the dark for 15–30 min. After washing with PBST for 3 times, the larvae were captured under a stereomicroscope.

Cardiac function evaluation in zebrafish larvae

Tg (myl7: GFP) larvae exposed to 0.04%, 0.06% and 0.08% sevoflurane solutions were used to evaluate the cardiac function at 72 hpf. Heart rate (HR) was recorded over a one-minute interval. Video recordings of the hearts of Tg (myl7: GFP) larvae were captured using a fluorescence microscope equipped with a camera, which facilitated the analysis of ejection fractions (EF), stroke volume (SV), and cardiac output (CO). Images were extracted from the video frame by frame using ImageJ. Images representing maximum ventricular systole (max VS) and maximum ventricular diastole (max VD) were utilized to measure the diameters of the ventricular long-axis (lax) and short-axis (sax) at end-systole and end-diastole, respectively. The ventricular volume was calculated using the formula V = 0.5× sax2× lax. Stroke volume was determined by subtracting the maximum systolic volume (Vmax Sys) from the maximum diastolic volume (Vmax Dia). Cardiac output was calculated by multiplying the heart rate (in beats per minute) by the stroke volume. Ejection fractions (EF) were calculated using the formula (Vmax Dis – Vmax Sys)/Vmax Dis × 100%.

Quantitative RT‑PCR analyses

Total RNA was extracted from zebrafish larvae (20 larvae) and H9C2 cells using TRIzol reagent. cDNA) was synthesized from the extracted RNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Promega, USA). qRT-PCR was performed with a SYBR Mixture (CWBIO, China) on an Applied Biosystems system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, US). Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2 − ΔΔCt method, normalized to the expression level of beta-actin. The primer sequences used are listed in Table S1.

Whole‑mount in situ hybridization

20 larvae were collected at 33 hpf (for myl7, myh6 and myh7). Whole-mount in situ hybridization was performed as previously described [16]. Briefly, the embryos fixed in 4% PFA were used for hybridization to antisense probes overnight after washing with PBST and proteinase K incubation. Subsequently, the embryos were incubated with AP-conjugated anti-DIG (Roche, Germany) after PBST washing and blocking. Finally, NBT/BCIP was used for color development.

RNA-sequencing analysis

Total RNA was extracted from 40 larvae per group (0.1% DMSO control and 0.06% sevoflurane treatment groups, n = 3 biological replicates per group) using standard protocols. RNA quantity and integrity were verified prior to sequencing. High-throughput RNA sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 4000 platform (Shanghai Aksomics Biotech, China). Briefly, RNA libraries were constructed using the KAPA Stranded RNA-Seq Library Prep Kit (Roche) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Library quality was assessed using an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer, and quantification was performed using an ABI StepOnePlus RT-PCR system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA). Raw sequencing reads were quality-trimmed and aligned to the zebrafish reference genome (GRCz11) using HISAT2 (v2.1.0). Transcript abundance was estimated using StringTie (v1.3.3), and FPKM values were calculated for genes and transcripts using the R package Ballgown (v2.10.0). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on the following criteria: fold change > 1.5 and adjusted P-value < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analyses were performed using the SRplot platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn) for biological interpretation and visualization of results.

Western blot assay

Protein lysates were prepared from juvenile zebrafish and H9C2 cells using RIPA lysis buffer (CWBIO, China) containing 1 mM PMSF protease inhibitor (Sigma-Aldrich). Protein concentrations were determined by BCA assay (Cinotohi, China) with bovine serum albumin as standard. Equal protein amounts (20–50 µg/lane) were separated by 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore). Membranes were blocked using 3% BSA in TBST (for phosphoproteins) or 5% non-fat milk in TBST (for non-phosphorylated proteins). After 1 h blocking at room temperature, membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: mouse anti-GAPDH (1:6000; Proteintech, #60008-1-Ig), rabbit anti-AMPKα (1:3000; Proteintech, #10929-2-AP), rabbit anti-phospho-AMPKα (Thr172) (1:3000; Cell Signaling, #2535), rabbit anti-phospho-ACACA(Ser79) (1:1000; Sangon, #D155180), rabbit anti-ACACA (1:1000; Sangon, #D155330). Following TBST washes, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies: goat anti-mouse IgG (1:2000; Proteintech, #SA00001-1) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:2000; Proteintech, #SA00001-2). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence substrate (Thermo Scientific) and quantified by densitometry using ImageJ software (NIH). Phosphoprotein signals were normalized to total protein levels (phospho-AMPKα/AMPKα ratio) and loading controls (GAPDH).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM from at least three independent experiments. Normality was assessed using Shapiro-Wilk test. The data are shown as the mean ± SEM. Between-group comparisons were performed using: Unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test (two groups), One-way ANOVA with Bonferroni post-hoc test (multiple comparisons), and Two-way ANOVA for factorial designs. Statistical significance was defined as: *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001. All analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (GraphPad Software).

Results

Sevoflurane induces general anesthesia in zebrafish larvae and results in abnormal morphology during embryonic development

To evaluate the anesthetic effect of sevoflurane in zebrafish larvae, we recorded the anesthesia induction time by testing the larvae’s escape responses to tail fin pinches immediately following the exposure in various concentrations of sevoflurane. The induction time for anesthesia exhibited a dose-dependent effect. 91% of larvae exposed in 0.08% sevoflurane, 76% in 0.15% sevoflurane, 56% larvae in 0.06% sevoflurane, and 31% larvae in 0.04% sevoflurane showed no response to the tail fin pinches within 0.5 min. Almost all larvae lost their response when exposed to sevoflurane concentrations of 0.04% or higher in 1.5 min (Fig. 1A). In contrast, only 22% of larvae exposed to 0.02% sevoflurane lost their response in 5 min, and none responded to tail fin pinches in a 0.01% sevoflurane solution. Consequently, we chose 0.04%, 0.06%, 0.08%, and 0.15% for the further analysis of mortality rates. As illustrated in Fig. 1B, the mortality rates of larvae exposed to sevoflurane of 0.08% or lower were below 16% at 72 hpf, while the mortality rate for larvae in 0.15% sevoflurane increased to 69% at 12 hpf (Fig. 1B). Due to the high lethality associated with 0.15% sevoflurane, we proceeded with concentrations of 0.04%, 0.06%, and 0.08% for the subsequent experiments.

Furthermore, we assessed the morphological characteristics of embryos exposed to 0.04%, 0.06%, and 0.08% sevoflurane starting at 10 hpf (Fig. 1C). At 24 hpf, embryos in all groups exhibited normal morphology (Fig. 1C). However, as development progressed, larvae exposed to sevoflurane displayed dose-dependent reductions in body length, eye size, and head area compared to wild-type controls at both 48 hpf and 72 hpf (Fig. 1C-F). Notably, pericardial edema became prominent in larvae exposed to 0.06% and 0.08% sevoflurane by 72 hpf (Fig. 1C, G). These findings establish the anesthetic and developmental effects of sevoflurane in zebrafish larvae while identifying appropriate concentration ranges for further study.

Sevoflurane impairs the looping process of heart development

To further investigate the impact of sevoflurane on cardiac development, we analyzed the heart morphology using Tg (myl7: GFP) at 72 hpf. Compared to control larvae exhibiting the characteristic twisted heart morphology, sevoflurane-exposed larvae (0.04%, 0.06%, and 0.08%) displayed dose-dependent cardiac elongation and straightening (Fig. 2A). Quantitative measurements showed a progressive increase in the bulbus arteriosus to sinus venosus (BA-SV) distance across exposure groups (Fig. 2A), correlating with morphological abnormalities. The incidence of abnormal cardiac phenotypes increased from 65% (0.04% sevoflurane) to 96% (0.08% sevoflurane) (Fig. 2A). Histopathological analysis via hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining further demonstrated structural defects. Control hearts exhibited thick-walled ventricles and thin-walled atria, whereas sevoflurane-exposed hearts showed ventricular wall thinning, and pericardial enlargement (Fig. 2B). Additionally, erythrocyte density in heart cavity was visibly reduced, a finding confirmed by o-dianisidine staining: about 50% of 0.04%-exposed larvae showed depressed cardiac haemoglobin, with severity and prevalence increasing at 0.06% and 0.08% (Fig. S1).

Subsequently, the heart morphology of heart at 30 hpf was detected by in situ hybridization. Sevoflurane exposure disrupted expression pattern of key cardiac markers, including myl7 (myosin, light chain 7, regulatory), myh6 (myosin, heavy chain 6, cardiac muscle, alpha) and myh7 (myosin heavy chain 7) (Fig. 2C). All concentrations inhibited cardiac looping in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 2C), mirroring later structural defects. Accordingly, transcript levels of the key cardiac transcription factors gata4, nkx2.5, and tbx5a—genes essential for cardiac development and implicated in disease pathogenesis [17] —were significantly downregulated across all sevoflurane exposure groups (Fig. 2D–F). Collectively, the data indicate that sevoflurane disrupts zebrafish heart development from the earliest stages—blocking heart-tube looping and suppressing essential regulatory genes—culminating in the dilated, hypo-contractile phenotype observed at 72 hpf. Therefore, sevoflurane exposure disrupts zebrafish heart development, particularly by inhibiting heart tube looping and reducing the expression of critical regulators of early cardiac development.

Sevoflurane triggers cardiac dysfunction and dilated cardiomyopathy phenotypes in zebrafish larvae

To systematically evaluate sevoflurane-induced cardiac dysfunction, we performed comprehensive functional analyses in zebrafish larvae at 72 h post-fertilization (hpf). Sevoflurane exposure caused significant, dose-dependent reductions in ventricular contractility, as evidenced by the decrease in the difference between end-systolic and end-diastolic ventricular volumes (Fig. 3A, B). Quantitative analysis revealed that EF decreased from 39% in controls to 33.6%, 29%, and 25% at 0.04%, 0.06%, and 0.08% sevoflurane, respectively (Fig. 3C). Furthermore, cardiac pumping function was severely compromised across all measured parameters (Fig. 3D-F). Stroke volume declined from 251 pL (control group) to 222 pL (0.04% group), 151 pL (0.06% group), and 108 pL (0.08% group) (Fig. 3D). Heart rate decreased from 157 bpm (control group) to 134 bpm (0.04% group), 123 bpm (0.06% group), and 77 bpm (0.08% group) (Fig. 3E). Consequently, cardiac output was reduced from 39 nL/min (control group) to 28 nL/min (0.04% group), 23 nL/min (0.06% group), and 10 nL/min (0.08% group) (Fig. 3F). These data confirm that sevoflurane simultaneously impairs the ventricle contraction and heart’s pumping function.

To determine whether early exposure alone is sufficient to induce sustained dysfunction, we exposed embryos to sevoflurane from 10 to 30 hpf and then transferred to drug-free E3 medium until 72 hpf. Under these conditions, ventricular volume, SV–BA distance, stroke volume, ejection fraction, heart rate, and cardiac output showed no significant differences compared with controls at 72hpf (Fig. S2). These findings indicate that transient early exposure is insufficient to produce persistent cardiac impairment, and that prolonged exposure is required for the development of the late-stage functional phenotype observed at 72 hpf.

To further investigate the effect of sevoflurane on the myocardial stress and remodeling, we used qPCR analysis to examine the transcript levels of nppa (encoding atrial natriuretic peptide, ANP) and nppb (encoding brain natriuretic peptide, BNP) [17]. Expression of both natriuretic peptides was markedly elevated at all sevoflurane concentrations relative to controls (Fig. 3G, H). Collectively, these findings, along with the histological features, establish a robust pattern of cardiac structural and functional derangements consistent with a DCM-like phenotype, characterized by ventricular dilation, wall thinning, systolic dysfunction, and pericardial enlargement—hallmarks that align with the canonical morphologic and hemodynamic features of DCM.

Sevoflurane disrupts calcium homeostasis and sarcomeric gene expression

Myocardial contraction is governed by a Ca²⁺-dependent cycle of sarcomeric activation and relaxation [18, 19]. To test whether sevoflurane interferes with this cycle, we quantified mRNA abundance of four genes that anchor the contractile apparatus and Ca²⁺ homeostasis: mybpc3 (cardiac myosin-binding protein C), tnnt2a (cardiac troponin T), slc8a1a (solute carrier family 8 member 1a; Na⁺/Ca²⁺ exchanger 1, NCX1), and atp2a2a (ATPase sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum Ca2+ transporting 2a; SERCA2a). Mybpc3 and tnnt2a maintain thick-filament stability and tune myofilament Ca²⁺ sensitivity, respectively. Both genes were significantly downregulated in in higher sevoflurane groups (0.06% and0.08%) compared to controls (p < 0.01 for mybpc3; p < 0.05 for tnnt2a; Fig. 4A, B), consistent with transcriptional signatures of maladaptive cardiac remodeling [20, 21]. Concomitantly, expression of the Ca²⁺ transporters slc8a1a and atp2a2a—which extrude Ca²⁺ across the sarcolemma and re-sequester it into the sarcoplasmic reticulum [19, 22]—was either down-regulated in sevoflurane groups (Fig. 4C, D). These transcriptional changes suggest slowed cytosolic Ca²⁺ clearance, reduced sarcoplasmic reticulum Ca²⁺ loading, and blunted myofilament responsiveness to Ca²⁺, providing a molecular rationale for the sevoflurane-induced decline in fractional shortening and the emergence of a dilated-cardiomyopathy-like phenotype.

Sevoflurane trigers myocardial inflammation by activating immune cell infiltration and cytokine release in zebrafish

Inflammation represents a critical pathogenic mechanism in DCM progression, driving deleterious immune responses and promoting adverse myocardial remodeling [23]. Given previous reports that sevoflurane anesthesia increases hippocampal inflammation in elderly patients and aging mice [24], we investigated its cardiac inflammatory effects through comprehensive cytokine profiling and immune cell tracking. Quantitative PCR analysis revealed substantial sevoflurane-induced upregulation of pro-inflammatory cytokines in a dose-dependent manner. Transcript abundance of il-1β rises about 10-fold at 0.06% sevoflurane significantly, and about 6-fold at 0.04% and 0.08% sevoflurane groups (Fig. 4E). Tnf-α expression level in 0.06% group exhibited an approximately five-fold increase compared to the control group, while the 0.08% group demonstrated a six-fold increase (Fig. 4F). Additionally, the expression of il-6 in zebrafish larvae also increased following sevoflurane treatment, with the 0.06% group showing a roughly six-fold increase and the 0.08% group exhibiting a four-fold increase (Fig. 4G).

To characterize the cellular immune response, we employed transgenic zebrafish lines Tg (lyz:Dsred) and Tg (mpeg1: GFP) to visualize neutrophil and macrophage recruitment. Sevoflurane exposure induced significant cardiac leukocyte infiltration (Fig. 4H). Neutrophils accumulation in heart is marked increased in 0.06% sevoflurane, reaching maximal infiltration after 0.08% sevoflurane treatment (Fig. 4H). Macrophage recruitment in heart is significantly elevated in 0.06% and 0.08% sevoflurane groups vs. control (Fig. 4H). These findings position inflammation as a key pathological mechanism underlying sevoflurane-induced cardiac toxicity, with neutrophils initiating acute inflammatory responses and macrophages potentially contributing to subsequent tissue remodeling processes [25]. The dose-dependent nature of these effects suggests a direct relationship between sevoflurane exposure and inflammatory cardiac injury.

Sevoflurane causes DCM via inhibit AMPK activation in zebrafish larvae

To investigate the mechanisms by which sevoflurane causes the DCM, RNA-sequencing was performed on larval zebrafish of 0.06% sevoflurane and control group. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analyses revealed that, compared with the control group, down-regulated differentially expressed genes in 0.06% sevoflurane larvae were predominantly enriched in pathways related to phototransduction, apelin signaling pathway and retinol metabolism (Fig. 5A). Since the apelin signaling potently enhances cardiac contractility and its downregulation directly contributes to DCM progression [26], the related differentially expressed genes (DEG) were analyzed and visualized in a heatmap (Fig. 5B). Among the DEGs, prkaa2, encoding AMPKα2 catalytical subunit, was chose to be the target of the sevoflurane caused DCM, for AMPK deficiency results in dilated cardiomyopathy in rat and its role in cardiac remodeling [27, 28]. The activation of AMPK was inhibited by sevoflurane as evidenced by attenuated phosphorylation of AMPKα2 at T172 (43% reduction) in larvae of 0.06% sevoflurane group (Fig. 5C).

To further establish the causal relationship between AMPK inactivation and sevoflurane-induced dilated cardiomyopathy, sevoflurane-exposed larvae were treated with the AMPK activator A-769662. Morphological analysis revealed that A-769662 at 50 µM and 100 µM (but not 25 µM) rescued the sevoflurane-induced cardiac elongation and straightening (Fig. 6A), as quantified by BA-SV length (Fig. 6B). In the sevoflurane-exposed group and the 25 µM A-769662 co-treatment group, the BA-SV length remained elevated (~ 0.2 mm), whereas 50 µM and 100 µM A-769662 reduced it to 0.16 mm and 0.15 mm, respectively-approaching the control value (0.13 mm) (Fig. 6B). This demonstrates that AMPK activation mitigates sevoflurane-induced cardiac morphological defects. To corroborate these findings, cardiac function was assessed via ejection fraction (EF), heart rate (HR), stroke volume (SV), and cardiac output (CO). AMPK activation by A-769662 (50 and 100 µM) significantly attenuated sevoflurane-induced impairments in EF, HR, SV, and CO (Fig. 6C-F). Furthermore, A-769662 treatment downregulated the mRNA levels of nppa and nppb compared to the sevoflurane group (Fig. 6G, H), indicating reduced myocardial stress.

To biochemically validate AMPK pathway activation under these conditions, we examined canonical signaling markers. In addition to increased phosphorylation of AMPK, A-769662 treatment produced dose-dependent increases in phosphorylation of acetyl-CoA carboxylase (p-ACC), a well-established downstream target of AMPK signaling. These findings confirm effective activation of the canonical AMPK cascade in sevoflurane-exposed larvae (Fig. 6I, J). Importantly, A-769662 treatment alone significantly increased p-AMPK and p-ACC levels but did not alter cardiac morphology or functional parameters compared with controls (Fig. S3), indicating that the observed rescue is not attributable to nonspecific developmental stimulation by the drug itself.

In addition to cardiac-specific effects, we examined whether AMPK activation ameliorates broader developmental abnormalities induced by prolonged sevoflurane exposure. We found that 25 µM and 50 µM A-769662 significantly improved body length and eye area compared with the sevoflurane group. These results suggest that AMPK activation mitigates systemic developmental impairment associated with sevoflurane exposure, rather than exerting isolated effects on cardiac tissue (Fig.S2).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that sevoflurane-induced cardiac dysfunction is mechanistically linked to AMPK inactivation, and pharmacological AMPK activation effectively ameliorates both functional and molecular hallmarks of DCM.

AMPK activation attenuates sevoflurane-induced H9C2 hypertrophy

To determine whether the cardiotoxicity observed in sevoflurane-exposed zebrafish originated from AMPK-dependent mechanisms is conserved in mammalian cardiomyocytes, we examined sevoflurane’s effects in H9C2 cells. Treatment with sevoflurane at all tested concentrations triggered cellular hypertrophy (Fig. 7A), with a dose-dependent increase in cell size confirmed by phalloidin staining (Fig. 7B). Additionally, sevoflurane significantly upregulated the mRNA levels of nppa and nppb (Fig. 7C), indicative of pathological cardiac hypertrophy—an initial compensatory response to hemodynamic overload. Critically, sevoflurane (0.06%) suppressed AMPK phosphorylation (Fig. 7D), consistent with the AMPK inactivation observed in sevoflurane-exposed zebrafish larvae. Notably, AMPK activation via A-769,662 reversed sevoflurane-induced hypertrophy in H9C2 cells, normalizing cell size and suppressing nppa and nppb expression (Fig. 7E-G), which parallels the rescue of cardiac morphology and function in zebrafish. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that sevoflurane-induced DCM-like phenotypes in zebrafish and hypertrophy in H9C2 cells converge on AMPK inactivation, triggering remodeling in cardiomyocytes. The conserved rescue by A-769662 underscores AMPK’s central role in mitigating sevoflurane associated cardiotoxicity across models.

Discussions

Growing concerns regarding anesthetic safety in pediatric and fetal medicine have emerged due to the increasing use of anesthesia during pregnancy for non-obstetric surgeries and fetal interventions [5, 29]. Preclinical studies demonstrate that maternal anesthesia exposure induces fetal neurotoxicity, including impaired myelinogenesis, axonal growth inhibition, and cognitive deficits [3, 30]. Clinical retrospective analyses further support an association between prenatal general anesthesia exposure and subsequent neurobehavioral abnormalities in offspring [30]. Beyond neurological effects, anesthetics adversely impact cardiovascular parameters, including myocardial contractility, blood pressure regulation, and cardiac output [6, 10], with anesthetic-related adverse reactions representing a leading cause of cardiac arrest in neonates [4].

Here, we provide compelling evidence that sevoflurane exposure induces cardiac toxicity through AMPK suppression, with conserved pathological effects observed in both zebrafish embryos and mammalian cardiomyocytes. The multi-level characterization of these effects - spanning morphological, functional, cellular, and molecular analyses - reveals that prolonged sevoflurane exposure dose-dependently impairs cardiac function, as evidenced by reduced ejection fraction (from 39% to 25%), stroke volume (from 251 to108 pL), and cardiac output (from 39 to 10 nL/min). These changes, accompanied by ventricular dilation and wall thinning, upregulation of nppa and nppb, phenocopy the hallmarks of dilated cardiomyopathy(Fig. 8) [31].

We identify two interconnected pathways underlying sevoflurane-induced dysfunction: [1] sarcomeric destabilization via mybpc3 downregulation, compromising thick-filament stability, and [2] calcium dysregulation due to reduced tnnt2a expression (impairing calcium sensitivity) [21, 31] and diminished slc8a1a/NCX1 and atp2a2a/SERCA2a expression [22]. This dual impairment disrupts calcium extrusion and reuptake, leading to diastolic dysfunction and pathological calcium overload (Fig. 8). Furthermore, sevoflurane disrupts cardiac morphogenesis, as zebrafish embryos exposed to sevoflurane exhibit impaired heart looping at 72 hpf, likely due to downregulation of key regulators (gata4, nkx2.5, tbx5a) [32–34]. Given that proper looping is essential for chamber alignment [34], this defect may contribute to the functional deficits observed in larval hearts.

The cardiovascular effects of sevoflurane remain complex. While recognized for cardioprotective effects in ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) models via mitochondrial pathwaysit [35, 36], our findings demonstrate that prolonged exposure induces toxicity via AMPK suppression. To our knowledge, this is the first study linking sevoflurane-induced cardiac dysfunction to AMPK inhibition, which aligns with prior reports that AMPK deficiency promotes DCM [27]. Notably, AMPK sustains SERCA2a expression and mitigates neutrophil infiltration [28, 37], both of which are disrupted by sevoflurane. Consequently, pharmacological AMPK activation emerges as a promising strategy to counteract sevoflurane-associated cardiotoxicity, supported by the functional rescue achieved with A-769662.

Importantly, AMPK is not a heart-restricted regulator but a systemic metabolic coordinator governing growth, inflammation, and energy homeostasis. In our study, AMPK activation not only rescued cardiac abnormalities but also partially restored global developmental parameters, including body length and eye size, suggesting that the cardioprotective effects may arise from broader metabolic stabilization rather than an exclusively myocardium-specific mechanism. Accordingly, we interpret AMPKα2 as a dominant mediator within this experimental context, while recognizing that additional intersecting pathways may cooperate to shape the full spectrum of sevoflurane-induced developmental cardiotoxicity.

Several limitations warrant consideration. First, our observed cardiac toxicity required prolonged sevoflurane exposure; shorter durations yielded no significant effects, suggesting a threshold or duration-dependent mechanism. In addition, because sevoflurane is highly volatile, the actual dissolved concentration in the static aqueous exposure system may have gradually declined over time depending on headspace volume and sealing conditions. We did not directly quantify the time-dependent concentration of sevoflurane during each 12-hour exposure interval, which represents a methodological limitation. Although functional monitoring suggested sustained anesthetic effects within each renewal cycle, precise chemical validation of exposure stability would further strengthen the model. Second, the exposure durations used here, particularly the in vitro paradigms, significantly exceed typical clinical anesthesia timescales. The prolonged anesthesia may cause reduced activity, bradycardia, or secondary effects related to circulation. Therefore, while mechanistically revealing, the direct translation of these specific exposure conditions to human developmental cardiotoxicity requires careful evaluation and further investigation. Third, while zebrafish provide a powerful model for developmental cardiac studies, inherent species differences necessitate caution in directly extrapolating findings to humans. Nevertheless, the conserved AMPK pathway and the profound toxicity observed serve as a critical warning, highlighting the need for focused research on the potential cardiac developmental risks of sevoflurane in vulnerable pediatric and fetal populations.

In summary, we establish that sevoflurane induces cardiotoxicity via AMPK suppression, manifesting as developmental defects (impaired looping), functional impairment (reduced contractility), pathological hypertrophy in H9C2 cells, molecular remodeling (elevated nppa/nppb). Crucially, these effects were reversible upon AMPK reactivation. Given sevoflurane’s ubiquity in clinical practice, these findings underscore the imperative to investigate AMPK-targeted protective strategies and rigorously evaluate the potential long-term cardiac consequences of anesthetic exposure during development.
