SARS-CoV-2 infection, not vaccination, the true association between COVID-19 and myocardial infarction mortality: A nationwide study in Ecuador
Authors: Esteban Ortiz-Prado, Vladimir Ullauri Solorzano, Diana Moreira-Vera, Jorge Vasconez-Gonzalez, Juan S. Izquierdo-Condoy
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMisinformation linking COVID-19 vaccines to myocardial infarction (MI) has fueled vaccine hesitancy, particularly in high-mortality settings like Ecuador. This study examines the association between COVID-19 infections, vaccinations, and MI mortality to address these concerns.ObjectiveTo investigate whether COVID-19 vaccinations or infections are associated with increased MI mortality in Ecuador.MethodsThis descriptive observational ecological time-series study analyzed 710 days of national health registry data from January 2021 to December 2022. The dataset included daily counts of myocardial infarction (MI) deaths, COVID-19 deaths, and COVID-19 vaccinations. Time-series regression models were used to assess the association between MI mortality (outcome) and both COVID-19 deaths and vaccination rates (predictors), with analyses stratified by sex.ResultsWe recorded a total of 26,637 myocardial infarction (MI) deaths and 24,066 COVID-19 deaths during the study period. Daily COVID-19 deaths were significantly associated with an increase in MI mortality (β = 0.08667, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.2048), with a stronger effect observed in women (β = 0.1103, p < 0.0001) compared to men (β = 0.07343, p < 0.0001). Additionally, new COVID-19 cases were positively correlated with daily MI deaths (β = 0.002725, p < 0.0001, R2 = 0.2543). In contrast, COVID-19 vaccination rates were associated with a small but statistically significant protective effect against MI mortality (β = −1.188e–5, p = 0.0218, R2 = 0.0074).ConclusionCOVID-19 infection, rather than vaccination, is strongly associated with increased myocardial infarction (MI) mortality in Ecuador. In contrast, the small but statistically significant protective effect observed for vaccination reinforces its cardiovascular safety and public health value. These findings directly counter prevailing misinformation linking vaccines to MI and provide evidence to support vaccination campaigns, particularly in high-risk and high-mortality settings.

Introduction

Myocardial infarction (MI) remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, and emerging evidence suggests that COVID-19 infection may exacerbate cardiovascular risks through mechanisms such as systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and prothrombotic states [1,2]. Although global data have shown an increased incidence of acute MI (AMI) following SARS-CoV-2 infection and a potential protective effect of vaccination [3], region-specific data particularly from high-burden countries like Ecuador remain limited.

Ecuador experienced one of the highest excess mortality rates globally during the pandemic, with up to 408 % above expected daily deaths in 2020 [4,5]. Despite successful vaccination campaigns, misinformation about vaccine safety including false claims linking vaccines to MI has undermined public trust [6,7]. This issue is particularly relevant in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where vaccine hesitancy intersects with systemic health disparities.

While several high-income countries have published analyses on post-infection and post-vaccination cardiovascular events (e.g., Denmark, Korea, USA), Latin American data are scarce. To address this gap, we analyzed 710 days of national mortality and vaccination data in Ecuador to examine the associations between COVID-19 infections, vaccinations, and MI mortality. By disentangling these relationships, our study aims to provide evidence to counter vaccine-related misinformation and to guide public health interventions in similarly affected regions.

Methodology

Study design

This descriptive observational ecological study evaluated the association between COVID-19 vaccinations, COVID-19 deaths, and myocardial infarction (MI) deaths in Ecuador from January 2021 to December 2022. An ecological design was chosen to assess population-level trends using comprehensive national health data, enabling the examination of temporal and sex-specific patterns in MI mortality relative to COVID-19 infections and vaccination rates.

Setting

The study utilized daily data from Ecuador's national health registries, including the National Institute of Statistics and Census (INEC) and Ministry of Public Health databases. These registries provided standardized reports on COVID-19 deaths, MI deaths, and vaccinations across all geographic regions of Ecuador, encompassing diverse demographic and healthcare settings. The study period spanned 710 days, offering a robust temporal framework to analyze health outcomes during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Participants

The study included all individuals vaccinated against COVID-19 and patients diagnosed with MI admitted to hospitals in Ecuador during the study period, as well as all reported COVID-19 deaths. No exclusion criteria based on age, sex, or comorbidities were applied, ensuring a comprehensive population-level analysis. Data were stratified by sex to explore potential differences in the associations between COVID-19 deaths, vaccinations, and MI deaths in men and women.

Variables

The primary outcome was the daily number of MI deaths, stratified by sex. Predictor variables included daily COVID-19 deaths and daily vaccination rates, also stratified by sex. Due to the ecological design, individual-level data (e.g., cardiovascular comorbidities, healthcare access) were unavailable. However, the population-based approach provided valuable insights into national trends, capturing broad associations relevant to public health.

Data sources and measurement

Data on daily COVID-19 deaths, MI deaths, and vaccinations were sourced from INEC and Ministry of Public Health databases, which employ standardized reporting protocols to ensure consistency and reliability. Diagnoses of MI were based on International Classification of Diseases (ICD-10) codes (I21–I22), and COVID-19 deaths were confirmed via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing or clinical criteria. Vaccination data included doses administered (first, second, or booster) without differentiation by vaccine type. Sex-stratified data enabled the assessment of differential impacts on men and women. Data quality was maintained through cross-validation with hospital records, ensuring comparability across the study period.

Bias

Bias was minimized by using nationally representative health databases with uniform reporting procedures. However, the ecological design introduces potential residual confounding due to the lack of individual-level data on factors such as cardiovascular comorbidities or healthcare access. Regional variations in healthcare infrastructure may also influence outcomes, though the nationwide scope mitigates this to some extent.

Study size

The dataset covered 710 days, including all reported MI deaths (26,637), COVID-19 deaths (24,066), vaccination doses (over 39 million), and COVID-19 cases (804,937). The comprehensive dataset ensured no missing data, providing a robust foundation for national-level inferences.

Quantitative variables

Quantitative variables included daily MI deaths (stratified by sex), daily COVID-19 deaths, and daily vaccination rates, all treated as continuous variables. These were analyzed to assess their associations with MI mortality.

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were applied to summarize the distributions of daily myocardial infarction (MI) deaths, COVID-19 deaths, and confirmed COVID-19 cases throughout the 710-day study period. To assess the association between daily MI mortality (dependent variable) and two primary predictors COVID-19 deaths and COVID-19 vaccination rates a time-series linear regression model was employed. This model was chosen over logistic regression due to the continuous and count-based nature of the outcome variable, and because it allows for the incorporation of temporal autocorrelation inherent in longitudinal ecological data. Both predictors were entered simultaneously into the model to reduce confounding bias. Sex-stratified analyses were performed to explore potential differences in associations by sex; however, interaction terms were not formally tested given the descriptive and hypothesis-generating scope of this study. Sex-stratified analyses were conducted as a pre-specified exploratory approach [8]. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2), and model assumptions were assessed through residual diagnostics. Sensitivity analyses were conducted by excluding extreme outlier days—specifically, those corresponding to national mortality peaks—to evaluate the stability of the associations. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1), with a complete dataset and no missing data. The modeling strategy was selected to align with the ecological and exploratory nature of the study, which focuses on detecting temporal population-level patterns rather than individual-level causal inferences. Furthermore, the transparency and interpretability of this approach facilitate the communication of results to inform evidence-based public health messaging, particularly in contexts challenged by misinformation and vaccine skepticism.

Results

General results

In Ecuador, from January 1, 2021, to December 31, 2022, the results showed a total of 26,637 deaths due to myocardial infarction (MI) and a total of 24,066 deaths from COVID-19. Of these, women accounted for 11,892 MI deaths, representing 44.65 % of all MI deaths, while men accounted for 14,745 MI deaths, or 55.35 %. During the same period, COVID-19 caused 9515 deaths in women, making up 39.53 % of total COVID-19 deaths, while men accounted for 14,551 COVID-19 deaths, representing 60.47 %.

Analysis of date trends

Throughout the period from January 2021 to December 2022, there were notable peaks in both COVID-19 deaths and myocardial infarction (MI) deaths in Ecuador. The data reveals that the highest number of daily COVID-19 deaths occurred on April 19, 2021, with 209 deaths recorded. Similarly, the peak in MI deaths took place on March 30, 2021, when 60 deaths were reported in a single day.

The month with the most COVID-19 deaths was April 2021, which saw consistently high death counts, peaking on several days. The MI deaths also saw a significant increase during March and April 2021.

Association between COVID-19 mortality and myocardial infarction deaths

In the analysis of daily myocardial infarction (MI) deaths and daily COVID-19 deaths, a statistically significant association was observed between these two variables (Fig. 1.). The regression model, using least squares, demonstrated that for every additional COVID-19 death, the number of daily MI deaths increased by 0.08667 (P < 0.0001), with an intercept of 31.71. This suggests that as COVID-19 deaths rise, there is a corresponding, albeit small, increase in MI deaths. In the analysis of the association between new COVID-19 cases and daily myocardial infarction (MI) deaths, a significant relationship was observed (Fig. 2.). The regression model, using least squares, demonstrated that for every additional new COVID-19 case, there was an increase of 0.002725 MI deaths per day (P < 0.0001). The model's intercept was estimated at 31.40, indicating the baseline number of MI deaths when no new COVID-19 cases are reported. The analysis explained 25.43 % of the variability in daily MI deaths, as reflected by the R2 value of 0.2543. Discussion

By 2022, approximately 65.8 % of the global population had received at least one dose of a COVID-19 vaccine, with Pfizer (613 million doses), Moderna (147 million), AstraZeneca (67 million), Janssen (19 million), Sputnik V (1.85 million), and Sinopharm (2.31 million) being the most administered [9,10]. By 2023, 13.64 billion doses had been administered worldwide, with 67 % of the population completing a primary vaccination series and 32 % receiving at least one booster dose [9,10]. In Ecuador, 39.59 million doses were administered by 2023, with 15,333,873 individuals (86.7 % of the population) receiving at least one dose and 14,240,587 (80.4 %) completing the vaccination schedule [11,12]. An analysis of self-reported adverse events following immunization for the first COVID-19 vaccine dose in Ecuador showed a predominance of mild symptoms (87 %), supporting the overall safety profile of the national vaccination campaign [13]. This high vaccination coverage, achieved despite Ecuador's early pandemic challenges, including one of the world's highest excess mortality rates, provides a critical backdrop for evaluating vaccine safety and cardiovascular outcomes in a high-burden setting.

Our study identified a statistically significant, albeit small, negative association between COVID-19 vaccine administration and daily myocardial infarction (MI) deaths (β = −1.188e-005, p = 0.0218), suggesting a slight protective effect. This aligns with Gupta et al.’s findings in a cohort of 1578 participants (1086 vaccinated, 492 unvaccinated), which showed no temporal clustering of acute MI (AMI) post-vaccination, indicating no significant association with vaccination [14]. Similarly, Jabagi et al. reported relative risks of 0.97 and 1.04 for MI after the first and second doses of the BNT162b2 vaccine, respectively, demonstrating no increased risk [15]. Studies in the United States and Iran by Klein et al. and Barda et al. further confirmed that mRNA vaccines do not elevate MI risk [16,17]. Proposed mechanisms for rare post-vaccination cardiovascular events include Kounis syndrome, characterized by acute coronary syndromes with allergic reactions (types I–IV, involving coronary spasm, thrombosis, stent-related events, or anaphylaxis in grafted patients) [18]. Other hypotheses suggest a prothrombotic autoimmune response against platelets or stress-induced demand ischemia following vaccination [19]. However, our findings and the broader literature indicate that such events are rare and do not outweigh the protective benefits of vaccination.

In contrast, our study found a significant positive association between COVID-19 deaths and MI mortality (β = 0.08667, p < 0.0001), with a stronger effect in women (β = 0.1103, p < 0.0001) than men (β = 0.07343, p < 0.0001). This is consistent with local evidence from Ecuador, where 38.6 % of men and 61.4 % of women reported tachycardia post-COVID-19, and palpitations were noted in 32.4 % of men, 67.6 % of women, and 2.4 % of pregnant women [20,21]. The stronger effect observed in women may be partly explained by a higher prevalence of cardiovascular risk factors in this group. In Ecuador, rates of overweight and obesity are notably higher among women (67.4 % and 30.9 %, respectively) compared to men (59.7 % and 20.3 %). Similarly, the 2018 STEPS Survey reported a higher prevalence of hypercholesterolemia in women (40.7 %) than in men (28.3 %)[22].These metabolic risk factors may increase women's susceptibility to myocardial infarction during or following COVID-19 infection. Moreover, Nanavaty et al. found that women accounted for approximately 36 % of patients with concurrent COVID-19 and acute myocardial infarction (AMI), while El-Menyar et al. reported that women with a prior history of AMI were more likely to experience a recurrent event[23,24]. These findings suggest that sex-based physiological and clinical differences may contribute to the greater impact of COVID-19 on MI mortality observed in women. The increased MI risk during and after COVID-19 infection is well-documented, with mechanisms including downregulation of ACE2 receptors, endothelial dysfunction, a procoagulant state, and elevated pro-inflammatory cytokines [1]. Xie et al. reported a heightened risk of cardiovascular and thrombotic events up to 12 months post-COVID-19 recovery, with survivors facing higher mortality from cardiovascular diseases [25,26]. Patients with COVID-19 are at greater risk of AMI within 30 days of infection, with incidence rising with disease severity, and atrial fibrillation is twice as prevalent in post-COVID-19 patients [1]. Our finding of a significant correlation between new COVID-19 cases and MI deaths (β = 0.002725, p < 0.0001) further underscores the virus's cardiovascular impact.

These results counter persistent myths linking COVID-19 vaccines to MI, reinforcing their safety and slight protective effect against cardiovascular mortality. In Ecuador, where vaccine hesitancy has been fueled by misinformation [27], our findings support targeted public health campaigns to increase uptake, particularly in high-risk populations. This challenge is consistent with previous evidence showing that poor regulation, misinformation, and public desperation during the pandemic led to widespread self-medication and inappropriate prescribing, including a 2057 % increase in ivermectin sales and a 171 % increase in hydroxychloroquine sales, despite the lack of proven benefit [28,29]. Such patterns underscore the urgent need for accurate risk communication and strong regulatory oversight to prevent the spread of unfounded medical practices during public health crises. The strong association between COVID-19 infection and MI mortality highlights the need for continued prevention strategies, including vaccination and early treatment to mitigate cardiovascular complications. Future research should explore individual-level risk factors, such as comorbidities and vaccine types, to further elucidate these associations and inform tailored interventions in high-burden settings like Ecuador.

Limitations

First, the lack of data on specific vaccine types administered in Ecuador prevented analysis of potential differences in myocardial infarction (MI) risk across vaccines (e.g., Pfizer, AstraZeneca). This limits our ability to assess whether certain vaccines might be associated with rare cardiovascular events. Second, the absence of individual-level medical histories precluded evaluation of comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, diabetes) or prior MI events that could predispose individuals to heart attacks. Similarly, we could not distinguish between individuals who received a single vaccine dose, completed the primary vaccination schedule, or received a booster dose, limiting our ability to assess dose-specific effects on MI incidence. Additionally, data on medications, healthcare access, and the quality of medical care were unavailable, which may have influenced MI outcomes, particularly in regions with variable healthcare infrastructure.

The ecological design introduces the potential for residual confounding, as individual-level risk factors could not be controlled for, limiting causal inferences. Furthermore, MI deaths occurring at home may have been underreported if relatives declined autopsies, potentially underestimating the true MI mortality burden. Finally, the absence of age-specific data restricted our ability to identify age groups at higher risk of MI in the Ecuadorian population, a critical factor given the age-dependent nature of cardiovascular disease.

Despite these limitations, the use of comprehensive national health registries ensured robust, population-level data, minimizing selection bias. The large sample size and absence of missing data further strengthen the study's findings. Future research should incorporate individual-level data, including vaccine types, comorbidities, and age, to refine these associations and inform targeted interventions in high-burden settings like Ecuador.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that COVID-19 vaccines are associated with a slight protective effect against myocardial infarction (MI) mortality in Ecuador, supporting their cardiovascular safety. In contrast, COVID-19 infection significantly increases both the incidence and mortality of MI, highlighting the virus's role as a key driver of cardiovascular complications. These findings counter misinformation linking vaccines to MI risk and underscore the importance of vaccination campaigns to mitigate cardiovascular burdens in high-mortality settings like Ecuador. Further research is needed to elucidate the mechanisms underlying the protective effects of COVID-19 vaccines against cardiovascular events in both infected and uninfected populations. Specifically, studies should investigate the impact of vaccine types, dosing schedules, and long-term effects to better understand their role in preventing cardiovascular outcomes and to clarify the biological pathways involved. Such evidence will inform targeted public health strategies to enhance vaccine uptake and reduce COVID-19-related cardiovascular risks.
