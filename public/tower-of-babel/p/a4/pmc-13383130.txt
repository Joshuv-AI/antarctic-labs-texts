Digital health risks and well-being: internet addiction, fear of missing out, and life satisfaction in Lebanese adults
Authors: Shams Al Khateeb, Khaled Azzam, Nour Bannout, Chaimaa Abou Yassin, Abir Ghosson, Fatima Soufan, Miriam Al Battal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExcessive internet use might be linked to Internet Addiction Disorder (IAD), which is negatively linked to all aspects of life, including physical, mental, and social well-being. Internet use can bring about the sense of fear of missing out (FOMO), which is a social anxiety where the individual feels they are being left out; thus, it is a motivation to use the internet even more. Both IAD and FOMO are highly related to lower life satisfaction; however, this relationship is largely understudied in Lebanon. This study examined the relationship between internet addiction, fear of missing out, and life satisfaction in Lebanese adults, given the country’s shortage of mental health care and high internet usage.MethodologyA cross-sectional online survey enrolled 549 Lebanese residents aged 18 or older. Participants provided answers to demographic and social media use questions, the Internet Addiction Test, the Satisfaction with Life Scale, and the Fear of Missing Out Scale.ResultsMultiple regression analysis adjusting for age and education showed that Internet Addiction was significantly associated with higher FOMO (B = 0.014, p < 0.001), while Life Satisfaction was not significantly associated (B = -0.006, p = 0.106). The model explained 27.0% of the variance in FOMO (R² = 0.270). A separate multiple regression analysis for life satisfaction, adjusting for financial stability, smoking, and health problems, showed that Internet addiction remained significantly associated with lower life satisfaction (B = -0.038, p = 0.027), along with financial stability (B = 1.5, p < 0.001), smoking (B = -1.561, p = 0.013), and health problems (B = -3.9, p = 0.002). The model explained 9.7% of the variance in life satisfaction (R² = 0.097).ConclusionInternet addiction was significantly associated with higher levels of fear of missing out, while life satisfaction was not associated with FOMO among Lebanese adults. Additionally, higher levels of internet addiction were significantly associated with lower life satisfaction. These findings highlight the meaningful associations between digital behaviors and well-being and emphasize the importance of promoting digital literacy and mental health interventions among populations at risk.

Introduction

The internet is considered one of the most well-known innovations throughout history. Its value is justified due to its ease of access, the ability to communicate across the globe with no hindrances, the advancement of social relationships, and the supportive role it plays in the sharing of thoughts and knowledge [1] It is an important necessity for education, leisure, communication, and sharing of information [2]. Nonetheless, the very features of the internet that render it useful, like social networking, raise concerns regarding the possibilities of internet addiction [2]. Of note, in reviewing 68 studies, internet addiction was found to range between 0.8% and 26.7% [3]. There was an especially concerning prevalence among students in Iran and the Gulf Cooperation Council [4, 5].

Internet Addiction Disorder (IAD) is described as a condition whereby a person is unable to control the amount of time spent online, which results in personal, school, or work-related difficulties [6]. IAD is associated with the person suffering chronic health issues, obesity, musculoskeletal pain, strain injuries to the eyes and ears, and a sedentary lifestyle [7]. Additionally, IAD is associated with some mental health issues, which include depression and loneliness [8], and it shows one of the strongest associations with the psychological phenomenon known as the Fear of Missing Out, suggesting that excessive internet use may have broader social and psychological consequences.

The social media trend FOMO is a psychological phenomenon within social networking sites [9]. In addition, social media use is both shapes and is shaped by FOMO [10].Furthermore, FOMO is said to enhance the “exclusion awareness syndrome,” which highlights situations of social media omissions [11]. J. Walter observes that more than 75% of young adults experience social anxiety at the mere contemplation of being out of the loop with their peers’ social gatherings [10]. FOMO involves two components: a two-part process involving the sense of absence and the compulsive desire to sustain social connections [12]. The phenomenon entails a constant fear that others are partaking in enjoyable activities without their company and requires constant engagement with the lives of others through the internet [9]. FOMO could be a fleeting emotional reaction, a repetitive cognitive response, or a chronic attitude that could be linked to social dissatisfaction and loneliness [13]This pattern of compulsive engagement with social media may be explained by the Compensatory Internet Use Model which posits that individuals with unmet psychological needs may engage in excessive internet use as a coping mechanism for negative emotions or social dissatisfaction [14].Consistent with this model, research shows that higher levels of internet addiction are associated with greater FOMO, largely due to heightened social comparison and the need to belong [15–18].

Another intriguing aspect of FOMO is its connection to psychological health and overall well-being. A Polish study focused on the relationship between FOMO and life satisfaction, noting that FOMO had an inverse relationship with life satisfaction, as individuals with higher FOMO scores tended to have lower life satisfaction [18, 19]. Additionally, more pronounced internet addiction appears to be correlated with lower national life satisfaction. In the Middle East, internet addiction is particularly rampant [20].

To further contextualize the relationship between FOMO and well-being, it is important to consider the construct of life satisfaction. Proctor et al. (2009)describe life satisfaction as an indicator of well-being that is subjective in nature and encompasses one’s cognitive appraisal and emotional reaction to one’s situation, including achievements, interpersonal bonds, and feelings of contentment [21]. Several factors contribute to life satisfaction, such as age, sex, mental and emotional health, lifestyle, engagement in social and leisure activities, and satisfaction with one’s leisure activities [22, 23]. Further, individuals with mental health disorders tend to have lower SWLS scores (Satisfaction With Life Score)than mentally healthy individuals, indicating lower subjective well-being [24]. Understanding these aspects of life satisfaction provides a foundation for examining how it is associated with internet addiction and FOMO in the present study.

With such daily integration of social media platforms, concerns about their impact on mental health have already arisen and have been proven. Digital problems, such as FOMO, are considered emerging public health concerns and need to be addressed through eHealth interventions and policy frameworks [25, 26]. Recent studies have emphasized the importance of monitoring online behaviors and implementing early detection strategies within digital health systems, particularly in populations with high internet penetration and limited mental health resources [27]. Understanding these patterns is essential for designing prevention and intervention programs that can be integrated into national eHealth strategies, especially in regions like Lebanon, where such evidence is scarce.

Although life satisfaction and internet addiction have been associated with FOMO, these relationships, as well as their contextual and cultural determinants, are likely to differ in the case of Lebanon. As an example, one study comparing German and Spanish samples found significantly greater trait-based FOMO in Germans [28]. Conducting such research in Lebanon could shed light on the FOMO phenomenon and its interrelations with internet addiction and life satisfaction, thereby addressing an important literature gap. There is a lack of such studies in the Middle East and the Arab world, which highlights the possible contribution of our study in advancing the understanding of these psychological phenomena within our region.

Using the Compensatory Internet Use Model as a basis, an integrated conceptual model can be proposed (see Fig. 1) to investigate the interrelations between the constructs. In the model, it is proposed that higher internet addiction is related to higher FOMO, which is also associated with lower life satisfaction. This model enables the statistical testing of the proposed interrelations in Lebanese adults.

Therefore, the present study examines associations between internet addiction, FOMO, and life satisfaction among Lebanese adults. We hypothesize the following: H1) Internet addiction is positively associated with FOMO; H2) Life satisfaction is negatively associated with FOMO; H3) Internet addiction is negatively associated with life satisfaction.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

As a part of our cross-sectional study, an online questionnaire was developed via Google Forms and distributed to Lebanese citizens, aged 18 years or above, residing in all governorates.

Participants and recruitment

Using the convenience sampling method, an online invitation link was sent on multiple social media platforms, mainly WhatsApp. A total of 618 participants were recruited, among whom only 549 met the eligibility criteria and completed the survey. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were 18 years or older, Lebanese, currently residing in Lebanon, and voluntarily consented to participate in the study. Moreover, several gentle reminders were sent.

Questionnaire

A questionnaire comprising 5 sections was utilized. The first section collected demographic data, including age, gender, governorate, highest education level, employment status, marital status, socioeconomic status, Body Mass Index (BMI) using self-reported height and weight, lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and drinking habits), and health conditions (physical and mental). The second section measured the fear of missing out (FOMO) using the Fear of Missing Out Scale. The third section assessed internet usage habits and measured internet addiction using the Internet Addiction Test (IAT). The fourth section examined social media use. The final section evaluated life satisfaction using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS). Initially designed in English for accessibility, the questionnaire included both English and Arabic versions of the scales and custom questions. A pilot study involving 30 participants helped validate the survey’s quality by identifying and addressing any discrepancies or issues, ensuring its reliability and effectiveness for our research purposes.

Measures

Fear of missing out scale (FOMO)

The Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) was assessed using the Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) Scale. It consists of 10 items, rated on a five-point Likert-type scale, ranging from 1 = “Not at all true” to 5 = “Extremely true.” The scale shows good internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha of (α = 0.87), where a higher score indicates a greater level of fear of missing out [9]. This scale is available in the Arabic language [29].

Internet Addiction Test (IAT)

Internet addiction (IA) was assessed using the Internet Addiction Test (IAT). It contains 20 items, rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1 = “Never” to 6 = “Always.” It shows good internal consistency with Cronbach’s alpha of (α = 0.91), where a higher score indicates a greater level of Internet addiction [30]. This scale is available in the Arabic language [31].

Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS)

Life satisfaction (LS) was assessed using the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS). It consists of five items, rated on a seven-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 = “Strongly Disagree” to 7 = “Strongly Agree.” It shows good internal consistency with a Cronbach’s alpha of (α = 0.85), where a higher score indicates a greater level of life satisfaction [32]. This scale is available in the Arabic language [33].

Sample size

The sample size of 385 participants was determined using an online sample size calculator called Statulator [34]. However, considering potential dropout rates, a recalculation was performed, resulting in an adjusted sample size of 481 participants. The calculation had a power of 80%, a confidence level of 95%, a margin of error of 5%, and a dropout rate of 20%.

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Each participant was given an informed consent form, enabling them to voluntarily take part in the survey. To maintain each participant’s anonymity, no personal information was collected. All collected information will be kept strictly confidential and used solely for research purposes. Furthermore, this research was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Beirut Arab University (IRB number: 2024-H-0142-M-R-0584, received on 21/03/2024), attesting to compliance with ethical benchmarks.

Statistical analysis

For our study, we utilized SPSS version 28 for statistical analysis. Descriptive statistics were used to analyze categorical and numerical variables, including sociodemographic characteristics, social and medical history, internet usage, and social media usage. We presented mean, standard deviation, frequencies, and percentages. Bivariate analysis included independent t-tests for comparing means between two groups and analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparing means across more than two groups. Multiple linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the associations between study variables while controlling for potential confounders. For the FOMO model, variables included in the model were Internet Addiction Score and Life Satisfaction Score, with age and education level included as covariates based on their significant bivariate associations (p < 0.2). For the life satisfaction model, variables included were the Internet Addiction Score, with covariates selected from demographic and health factors showing significant bivariate associations (p < 0.2), including financial stability, smoking status, and health problems. Non-significant associations (p > 0.05) were excluded using a stepwise approach, while primary variables of theoretical interest were retained regardless of statistical significance. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Descriptive analysis

Socio-demographics

In total, our study recruited 549 adults with a mean age of 34.69 ± 14.58 years and a mean BMI of 26.49 ± 10.17 kg/m². The participants were distributed across different governorates, with approximately half residing in both Beirut and Mount Lebanon (24% in Beirut and 19.3% in Mount Lebanon), 31.5% in Bekaa, and the rest residing in other regions. Among our participants, 61.2% were females, 45.2% were married, 44.3% were single, and 45.5% held a university degree (bachelor’s degree). Regarding employment status, participants were divided equally between employed and unemployed; 38% of them reported that their financial stability was somewhat stable with occasional difficulties, while 31.4% were struggling to cover daily expenses (Table 1).

Socio-demographic, social, and medical characteristics of participants (N = 549)

Social and medical history

Medical and social history, detailed in Table 1, revealed that almost three-fourths of the participants were non-smokers (74%), with a significant majority refraining from alcohol consumption (87.2%). Additionally, most participants reported that they neither suffer from general health conditions (75.6%) nor mental health conditions (94.7%). Nevertheless, of those suffering from mental health conditions, 72.4% reported having anxiety, and 51% reported having depression, while participants having general health conditions (N = 134) were distributed almost equally between diseases affecting different body systems (Table 1).

Internet usage

In terms of internet usage, almost half of the participants spent an average of 1–5 h on devices with internet access (50.3%). Concerning the main purpose behind utilizing the internet, 22.8% of the participants revealed making use of the internet for work- or school-related tasks, 27.0% for entertainment, and 28.2% for social media and staying in touch with friends and family. Moreover, in terms of managing internet use during important life events or exams, 63.4% reported that they do not log out or delete apps. Regarding the impact of internet use on relationships, 68.3% reported that it did not affect their satisfaction with relationships. Despite that, around half of our participants reported occasionally texting to check on others (52.1%) (Table 2).

Social media usage

Social media usage, summarized in Table 2, highlighted that 91.1% of participants use social media platforms other than WhatsApp, with Instagram and Facebook being the preferred choices for 41.6% and 29.1%, respectively. Additionally, 39.6% of participants engage in occasional posting on social media platforms, while 40.4% do so rarely. Among different content types, 16.2% followed news and current events, while 27.4% preferred entertainment. Regarding influencers, 48.0% indicated that they don’t care when a social media influencer they follow stops posting, and 35.0% reported not following influencers at all. Emotionally, 70.6% report no discomfort when comparing themselves online, and 82.2% do not feel pressured to maintain a specific online image for approval or validation (Table 2).

Reliability analysis of scales

For the Satisfaction with Life Scale (SWLS), the total mean score ± SD is 3.87 ± 1.34, evaluated over 7, with good internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.839). Regarding the Fear of Missing Out (FOMO) scale, the total mean score ± SD yielded 1.89 ± 0.66, evaluated out of 5, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.811. Concerning the Internet Addiction Test (IAT), the total mean score ± SD is 1.28 ± 0.92, computed out of 5, with a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.941. Bivariate analysis

Association between fear of missing out scale mean and other factors

Bivariate analysis demonstrated significant correlations between FOMO and both age (p < 0.001) and marital status (p < 0.001). The youngest participants (18–24 years) had the highest FOMO scores (X̄ = 2.15), followed by the 25–34 group (X̄ = 1.93). The 55–64 group (X̄ = 1.59) and the group over 64 (X̄ = 1.57) had the lowest scores. A notable exception was seen in the 45–54 group (X̄ = 1.70), which slightly exceeded the 35–44 group (X̄ = 1.69).

Regarding marital status, individuals in relationships had the highest FOMO (X̄ = 2.21), followed by single (X̄ = 2.15), widowed (X̄ = 1.83), married (X̄ = 1.71), and divorced participants (X̄ = 1.70).

Health status was also significantly related to FOMO. Those without physical health conditions (X̄ = 1.93) and those with mental health conditions (X̄ = 2.21) showed higher FOMO compared to participants with physical illnesses (X̄ = 1.78) or without mental health issues (X̄ = 1.88) (p = 0.020 and p = 0.007, respectively).

Education level also influenced FOMO (p < 0.001). The highest scores were reported by those with only elementary education (X̄ = 3.10), followed by high school (X̄ = 2.18), bachelor’s degree (X̄ = 1.88), and postgraduate education (X̄ = 1.66). Similarly, employment status was significant (p < 0.001); unemployed individuals had the highest FOMO (X̄ = 2.11), while self-employed individuals had the lowest (X̄ = 1.59).

Screen time was also linked. Participants who spent more than 5 h/day online had higher FOMO (X̄ = 2.08) than those with 1–5 h (X̄ = 1.77) or less than 1 h (X̄ = 1.64). Moreover, users of social media platforms other than WhatsApp had higher FOMO (X̄ = 1.93) compared to non-users (X̄ = 1.54) (p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study analyzed the association of Internet Addiction (IA), Fear of Missing Out (FOMO), and life satisfaction in Lebanese adults. The results showed a significant association between greater internet addiction and higher levels of FOMO. Whereas, life satisfaction was not associated with FOMO. Furthermore, higher internet addiction was significantly associated with lower life satisfaction. These results add to the understanding that intense online participation (internet addiction) was associated with greater anxiety about social exclusion, isolation, or reduced well-being perceptions. This is one of the first studies exploring these associations in the Middle Eastern region, thereby furthering cross-cultural studies of technology and mental health.

We found that on the internet, IA has a positive association with FOMO. This aligns with the Alabri (2022) and Wang et al. (2020)studies that highlight social comparison and the need to belong as powerful motivators of FOMO in internet-dependent individuals [15, 16]. Alongside the findings of Manap et al. (2024),who demonstrated strong relationships between IA and FOMO in a Malaysian sample, particularly younger demographics who felt the need to stay perpetually connected [17]. Elhai et al. (2021) also reinforced the fact that individuals with heightened FOMO are more likely to use the internet compulsively as a way to cope with psychological distress [18].

Contrary to our second hypothesis, FOMO was not significantly associated with life satisfaction after adjusting for age and education (β = −0.060, p = 0.106), suggesting that variations in life satisfaction were not linked to differences in the fear of missing out. This finding contrasts with previous research.

However, a meta-analysis by Akbari et al. (2021)indicated that FOMO’s relationship with internet use was not associated with life satisfaction, suggesting that FOMO can operate independently of life satisfaction in certain contexts [35]. Collectively, these observations imply that the connection between FOMO and life satisfaction may be more nuanced and context-dependent than previously assumed [35].

Interestingly, Abd Ellatif Elsayed (2025) observed a positive association between FOMO and life satisfaction among university students in Saudi Arabia [36]. She proposed that in collectivist cultures, FOMO may enhance social awareness and engagement, thereby strengthening individuals’ sense of inclusion and satisfaction [36]. The divergence between our findings in Lebanon, her results in Saudi Arabia, and the generally negative associations reported globally highlights the potential role of cultural context in shaping how FOMO relates to life satisfaction.

Our third hypothesis was supported: Internet addiction remained significantly associated with lower life satisfaction after adjusting for financial stability, smoking, and health problems (β = −0.103, p = 0.027). As previously mentioned, Shi and Wang (2023), studying Chinese students, found that higher rates of internet addiction were associated with lower life satisfaction, asserting that increased online engagement is linked to lower life satisfaction [37]. This, in turn, aligns with the Kardefelt-Winther (2014) compensatory model that claims people with certain psychological deficits tend to excessively use the internet as a form of escapism, a behavior associated with lower well-being [14].

Notably, financial stability emerged as the strongest association of life satisfaction in our multivariable model (β = 0.223, p < 0.001). Lebanon has faced severe economic challenges in recent years, and this finding reflects the broader socioeconomic context in which this study was conducted. Additionally, smoking (β = −0.102, p = 0.013) and health problems (β = −0.13, p= 0.002) were negatively associated with life satisfaction, consistent with established literature on correlates of well-being [22, 23]. The prominence of financial factors aligns with Rajani et al. (2019),who found that financial satisfaction is a key correlate of overall life satisfaction across populations [22]. This suggests that interventions aimed at improving well-being in Lebanon must address both digital behaviors and the economic realities facing the population.

In the present study, FOMO was negatively associated with educational level. This association may reflect differences in digital literacy across educational levels, as higher education is commonly linked to greater digital literacy [38]. Digital literacy has been associated with better mental health outcomes and lower perceived stress by enabling individuals to navigate digital environments with greater critical awareness, manage cyber-stress, and access reliable support systems [39]. This may help explain why individuals with higher educational levels experience lower FOMO.

In this study, younger age was associated with higher FOMO. This is consistent with previous research showing that younger adults are more likely to engage in digitally mediated social comparisons [40, 41]. Such tendencies may help explain why FOMO is higher in younger individuals, as social comparison has been linked with greater FOMO [42].

Additionally, being single and unemployed was associated with higher levels of internet addiction and FOMO, consistent with Luhmann and Hawkley’s (2016) study on the social isolation and loneliness hypothesis related to overusing the internet [43] Meanwhile, the association that we found between social media activity and FOMO also replicates the work of Song and Kim (2022), who found that social media, by its very nature, constructs and curates a reality that accentuates exclusion and disconnection [44].

Implications

Our results show that eHealth interventions could help with the associations we found between internet addiction, FOMO, and life satisfaction. Tools like app-based behavioral monitoring, digital well-being dashboards, and online cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) programs could help the Lebanese population. These technologies could potentially help people manage their online habits, reduce behaviors related to fear of missing out (FOMO), and show improvement in their mental health in general [45].

Adding eHealth solutions to national mental health policies could make them more effective. For instance, public health officials, telecom companies, and tech developers could work together to make it easier to send targeted interventions to people who use the internet a lot. Public awareness campaigns, digital literacy initiatives, and culturally tailored mobile health programs could further support healthier online engagement and enhance life satisfaction [46].

Together, these results indicate that internet addiction is linked to higher FOMO and, after accounting for socioeconomic factors, remains associated with lower life satisfaction. This is a crucial concern for mental health practitioners, educators, and public health policymakers. Targeted interventions that promote digital well-being, teach mindfulness of online behavior, and foster real-life social connectedness could potentially address this pattern of associations.

In school and community settings, digital literacy interventions supporting equitable use of the internet and critical examination of social media may be especially useful for youths. For mental health clinicians, measurement of FOMO and IA in clients presenting with anxiety, depression, or dissatisfaction may help improve treatment individualization and effectiveness. Furthermore, culturally tailored campaigns in Lebanon may also encourage healthier digital habits, particularly as internet use and mobile phone connectivity continue to expand in the region.

Limitations

This research has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design prevents the establishment of causal relationships between internet addiction, FOMO, and life satisfaction. Second, self-reported data are susceptible to recall bias and social desirability bias, which may affect the accuracy of the responses.

Third, sampling-related methodological factors affect the level of generalization of the results obtained. The fact that we used a convenience sampling method through online platforms may have biased the sample in terms of representing a sample of the Lebanese population that is more technology-savvy, younger in age, and has a higher proportion of females (61.2%), compared with the total Lebanese adult population. In addition, the sample was not evenly distributed in terms of Lebanese governorates; thus, Beirut, Mount Lebanon, and Bekaa were overrepresented in the sample. No stratified sampling approach was used, and no post-stratification weighting procedures were applied to adjust for demographic imbalances, as recruitment relied on convenience-based online distribution. Therefore, the findings are most representative of digitally active Lebanese adults and may not accurately reflect older individuals, those with limited internet access, or underrepresented geographic regions. Consequently, generalization to the broader Lebanese adult population should be made with caution. The fact that we used an online survey may have also biased the sample by excluding individuals who do not have access to reliable internet services.

Fourth, while internet addiction remained significantly associated with lower life satisfaction after adjustment (β = −0.103, p = 0.027), the overall model explained only 9.7% of the variance in life satisfaction (R² = 0.097). Notably, financial stability emerged as the strongest association in the model, underscoring the importance of socioeconomic context in understanding well-being during Lebanon’s ongoing economic crisis. The relatively modest explanatory power implies that other unmeasured psychosocial, economic, and mental health factors beyond those included (financial stability, smoking, and health problems) may be more salient in influencing life satisfaction in Lebanese adults.

Suggestions for future research

Future research should utilize probability sampling to enhance generalizability, include additional psychosocial and economic factors (particularly given the strong association between financial stability and life satisfaction observed here), and employ longitudinal or mixed-method designs to clarify the temporal direction of these complex relationships.

Conclusion

This research offers important contributions to understanding the relationship between internet addiction, fear of missing out (FOMO), and life satisfaction in Lebanese adults. Our results indicated that greater internet addiction is significantly associated with elevated levels of FOMO, even after accounting for age and education. While life satisfaction was not associated with FOMO in the multivariable model, internet addiction remained significantly associated with lower life satisfaction after adjusting for financial stability, smoking, and health problems. Together, these findings highlight internet addiction as a consistent correlate of both FOMO and life satisfaction in Lebanese adults. This, in turn, reveals the psychological strain that an overabundance of internet use places on individuals, specifically in the context of an emotionally detached, digitally connected world.

These findings highlight the need to formulate more culturally appropriate and evidence-based strategies that address both the emotions and behavior related to internet use. Programs and policies that aim to advance digital literacy, balanced and mindful internet use, and preemptive interventions in schools and communities may be helpful in addressing the psychological burden of FOMO and dysfunctional internet use. In addition, targeted public health mental services designed to provide flexible psychological care for FOMO and compulsive internet use are needed.

Research shows that families, schools, communities, organizations, and public health authorities all share the responsibility, which in turn relates to and correlates with the use of the internet and the health of individuals as a whole. Finally, longitudinal and intervention-based research is required to further explain causality, assess the efficacy of suggested approaches, and investigate how changing digital behaviors affect mental health both inside and outside of Lebanon.
