Can Artificial Intelligence Make the Cut? Dissecting Large Language Model’s Surgical Exam Performance
Authors: Shankargouda Patil, Frank W Licari
Published: 2024

To the Editor:

We read the article by Ostrovsky et al1 with great interest. This article provides valuable insights into the capabilities and limitations of large language models (LLMs) in the context of medical education. The authors have meticulously evaluated the accuracy and reliability of 5 different LLMs in answering surgery clerkship examination questions, presenting a nuanced view of the potential roles these tools might play in enhancing surgical education.

The observation in the study that the various artificial intelligence (AI) models may yield differing responses to identical queries highlights an important reliability concern. Reliability implies consistent and repeatable results, a standard that appears unmet when responses vary across multiple trials. The retest reliability varied widely for the 5 models examined. The inability of LLMs to provide consistent answers across multiple trials challenges their utility as reliable resources.2 For instance, medical students or professionals relying on such technology for study or diagnostic assistance might receive varying advice for the same query, which could have potentially deleterious outcomes. When reliability is questionable, trust is inherently undermined. In critical fields such as health care, any variation in system performance can significantly erode trust.

Furthermore, the inherently opaque nature of AI systems compounds these reliability issues. Often referred to as “black box” operations, many AI models process and generate outputs based on complex algorithms and vast data sets that are not readily understandable even to their developers.3 This raises questions about the appropriateness of their widespread implementation without thorough understanding and regulation. Lawsuits regarding the lack of transparency and data used for training AI are currently making their way through the legal system.4,5 This lack of transparency is problematic in health care, where understanding the reasoning behind decisions and recommendations is crucial for trust and accountability. Without clear insight into how conclusions are drawn, health care professionals and regulators cannot fully assess the safety, efficacy, or biases of AI applications. This situation brings us back to the fundamental issue of trust, underscoring its critical role in the adoption and integration of AI technologies in health care.

The authors have made commendable contributions to understanding the implications of AI in health care through this article. Their findings of variability in LLM outputs serve as a crucial reminder of the need for ongoing evaluations, transparency in AI development, and stringent regulatory standards to govern the use of AI within sensitive fields like medicine. The deployment of such AI technologies without a comprehensive understanding and robust regulatory oversight could lead to unintended and unpredictable consequences.
