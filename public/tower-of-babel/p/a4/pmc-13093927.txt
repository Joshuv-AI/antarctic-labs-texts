Stress hyperglycemia ratio and short-term mortality in critically ill septic patients: stratified analysis by diabetes status
Authors: Heping Xu, Xue Lv, Yan Xia, Huan Niu, Jiangyan Li, Ping He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSepsis is a dysregulated immune response to infection, often leading to metabolic abnormalities. The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR), a reliable predictive indicator, has been widely used in the prediction of adverse outcomes in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases. However, studies on the relationship between the SHR and adverse outcomes in critically ill septic patients are limited. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the relationship between the SHR and short-term mortality in critically ill septic patients.MethodsA retrospective analysis of septic patients from the MIMIC-IV database was conducted. The Boruta algorithm was used to identify key features that predict short-term mortality in septic patients. Time-varying Cox proportional hazards model, subgroup analysis, restricted cubic splines (RCSs), and Kaplan‒Meier (K‒M) survival analysis were used to assess the relationships between the SHR and 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality, along with a subgroup analysis based on diabetic status.ResultsThis study included 15,876 patients with sepsis, 60.6% of whom were male. Boruta feature selection identified the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) as an important clinical variable. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models with time‑varying coefficients showed a positive association between SHR and both 28‑day and 90‑day mortality (HR = 1.683, 95% CI 1.489–1.903, P < 0.0001; HR = 1.533, 95% CI 1.397–1.681, P < 0.0001). Restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses further supported a linear relationship between SHR and 28‑day and 90‑day mortality. Subgroup analyses suggested that the association between SHR and short‑term mortality may be stronger in non‑diabetic than in diabetic patients. Sensitivity analyses were consistent, indicating robustness of the findings.ConclusionThe SHR is closely associated with short-term mortality in septic patients, suggesting that it could serve as a potential predictive factor for early risk assessment and clinical intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-026-13008-z.Keywords: Sepsis, Stress hyperglycemia ratio, Diabetes, Mortality, MIMIC-IV

Introduction

Sepsis is a life-threatening syndrome of acute organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection [1]. Despite advances in pathogen identification, supportive care, and antimicrobial strategies, sepsis remains a major global health burden, with high incidence, substantial ICU resource utilization, and persistently elevated mortality across regions and age groups [2–6]. Early and accurate risk stratification is essential to guide timely interventions and optimize outcomes in this heterogeneous population.

Stress hyperglycemia is common in critical illness and reflects the combined effects of neuroendocrine activation, inflammation, and insulin resistance [7]. Acute elevations in glucose have been linked to adverse outcomes in critical care and cardiovascular settings [8–12]. However, interpretation of a single in-hospital glucose measurement is confounded by chronic glycemic status: the same absolute glucose level may indicate severe hyperglycemia in non-diabetic patients but only a minor deviation from baseline in those with longstanding diabetes. To address this, the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR)—defined as admission glucose divided by the estimated average glucose derived from HbA1c—has been proposed as an individualized metric that adjusts for chronic glycemia [13]. Prior studies in acute coronary syndromes and other ICU cohorts have shown that higher SHR is associated with worse outcomes [8–12].

Patients with sepsis are particularly prone to stress hyperglycemia due to cytokine surges and metabolic dysregulation [14], yet the relationship between SHR and short-term mortality in sepsis remains insufficiently characterized. Existing evidence is limited by heterogeneous definitions, small sample sizes, and inadequate control of confounding, and few studies have delineated the exposure–response shape or assessed consistency by diabetes status.

Accordingly, we aimed to determine whether the SHR calculated at ICU admission is independently associated with 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality among adults with sepsis. We further used restricted cubic splines to characterize the dose–response relationship and formally test for nonlinearity, and we evaluated robustness across diabetes subgroups and key sensitivity analyses.

This retrospective study leveraged the contemporary, large-scale MIMIC‑IV v3.1 ICU database and employed prespecified diagnostic criteria and systematic confounding adjustment to enhance validity and transparency. By focusing on SHR, our work addresses a critical evidence gap in sepsis risk stratification.

Methods

Data source

This study leverages the publicly available MIMIC-IV database (Version 3.1) [15], a critical care dataset developed by the Complex Systems Monitoring Group at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts. It includes detailed data from over 50,000 patients admitted between 2008 and 2022. As the database is anonymous and deidentified, the BIDMC Institutional Review Board approved a waiver for informed consent and authorized the use of the data for research. Data extraction was conducted by the corresponding author, Heping Xu, who was granted access following completion of the CITI Program online training (Record ID: 59568270).

Definitions

The stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was calculated via the following formula: SHR = (admission blood glucose (mg/dL))/ (28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7) [13]. Sepsis is diagnosed on the basis of the Sepsis-3 criteria and requires a Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score of 2 or greater. Septic shock is identified as sepsis with a lactate level exceeding 2.0 mmol/L, along with the need for vasopressor support [1]. Acute kidney injury (AKI) is defined by the KDIGO guidelines, which include either an increase in serum creatinine (SCr) by ≥ 0.3 mg/dL within 48 h of baseline or urine output < 0.5 mL/kg/h for at least 6 h [16]. Baseline serum creatinine is the lowest creatinine level within the 7 days prior to admission and is used as a reference for KDIGO staging [17].

Outcome

The primary endpoint was 28-day all-cause mortality, whereas the secondary endpoint was all-cause mortality at 90 days.

Data extraction

Data for this study were extracted from the MIMIC-IV database via PostgreSQL software. The dataset includes detailed demographic and clinical information such as age, sex, ethnicity, weight, and comorbid conditions such as myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, chronic lung disease, diabetes, cerebrovascular disease, malignant cancer, and severe liver disease. It also contains initial SOFA scores and the Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II). The laboratory data included white blood cell count; hemoglobin, anion gap, bicarbonate, chloride, sodium, potassium, and creatinine levels; blood urea nitrogen; calcium; serum glucose; and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) levels. The primary outcomes were 28‑day and 90‑day all‑cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Patients were grouped into three categories on the basis of the SHR tertiles. Continuous variables are expressed as the means (standard deviations) or medians (interquartile ranges), whereas categorical variables are presented as percentages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to assess normally distributed continuous data, whereas the Kruskal‒Wallis H test was used for nonnormally distributed data.

To enhance the reliability and validity of our findings, we employed multiple statistical methods. For missing data, the missing rates for all key covariates were low (< 3%, Table S1) and were assumed to be missing at random. Accordingly, we used multiple imputation rather than complete-case analysis to handle missing values. To assess potential selection bias related to sample inclusion, we compared the baseline characteristics of the final analytic cohort (n = 15,876) with those of patients excluded due to lacking stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) data—specifically, those who did not provide admission glucose or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) values (n = 15,849); details are provided in Table S2. We used means or medians and standardized mean differences (SMD) to evaluate between-group differences.

To identify key predictors of short-term mortality in patients with sepsis, we applied the Boruta algorithm to evaluate the importance of candidate variables, with particular emphasis on the predictive value of the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR). In Boruta, random forest–derived Z scores of each original feature’s importance are compared with the maximum Z score among corresponding “shadow” features generated by permuting the data. Features whose Z scores are repeatedly and significantly greater than those of the shadow features across iterations are confirmed as important; otherwise, they are deemed unimportant and removed (Table S3, Table S4). We used the default Boruta parameters (significance level p = 0.01 and up to 300 iterations) [18]. To address multicollinearity, we calculated variance inflation factors (VIF) (Table 5S) and excluded variables with VIF > 5 [19]. This screening process yielded 22 key predictors.

We then investigated the association between SHR and short-term mortality using a multivariable Cox proportional hazards model with time-varying coefficients. Four nested models were constructed through stepwise adjustment: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, race, body weight, Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score, and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II); Model 3 further adjusted for comorbidities, including congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, renal disease, malignant cancer, and severe liver disease; and Model 4 additionally adjusted for laboratory and clinical parameters, including white blood cell count, hemoglobin, platelet count, calcium, blood urea nitrogen, creatinine, anion gap, bicarbonate, sodium, and potassium. SHR was included as the primary exposure in all models.

Subgroup analyses were performed on the basis of age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years), sex, race, and history of myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic lung disease, and diabetes. The restricted cubic spline method was used to explore the nonlinear relationship between the SHR and short-term mortality in sepsis patients. Kaplan‒Meier survival analysis was applied to compare survival rates by SHR stratification in ICU patients with sepsis. Separate analyses were performed for sepsis patients with and without diabetes via time-varying coefficients Cox regression, subgroup analysis, and restricted cubic splines. To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses. All data analyses were carried out via R 4.2.1 and Stata 18.0 software. Statistical analysis was conducted via two-sided tests, with a P value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Ethics approval

MIMIC-IV is an anonymized public database that has been approved by the institutional review boards of the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC). All patient information within the database has been fully anonymized and de-identified, and as such, the requirement for informed consent was waived in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Additionally, all research procedures were carried out in strict compliance with applicable ethical guidelines and regulations. The study protocols were reviewed and approved by the institutional review boards of both MIT and BIDMC.

Results

Baseline characteristics

This study included 15,876 patients with sepsis (Fig. 1). Patients were categorized into tertiles of the SHR: T1 (< 0.940), T2 (0.940–1.208), and T3 (> 1.208). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The overall cohort had a mean age of 67.5 ± 14.3 years, and 60.6% were male. There were no significant differences across tertiles in chronic pulmonary disease, congestive heart failure, platelet count, or serum calcium (P > 0.05). Compared with T1/T2, patients in T3 had lower body weight, a lower proportion of males, and lower chloride and HbA1c levels, but higher prevalence of malignant cancer and severe liver disease, higher SAPS II scores, white blood cell counts, anion gap, serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and glucose. The T3 group also had higher 28‑day and 90‑day mortality rates, at 20.2% and 27.1%, respectively.

Association between the SHR and short-term mortality

Patients were divided into tertiles of the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR). To evaluate the independent association of SHR with mortality among ICU patients with sepsis, with and without diabetes, we fitted four progressively adjusted Cox proportional hazards models with time‑varying coefficients (Table 2). After multivariable adjustment, higher SHR was significantly associated with increased 28‑day all‑cause mortality (Model 4: HR = 1.683, 95% CI 1.489–1.903, P < 0.0001). Subgroup analyses showed that this association was consistent in both the diabetes group (Model 4: HR = 1.616, 95% CI 1.324–1.973, P < 0.0001) and the non‑diabetes group (Model 4: HR = 1.706, 95% CI 1.465–1.988, P < 0.0001). When SHR was analyzed as a categorical variable, using T1 (low SHR) as the reference, the T3 (high SHR) group had a higher risk of 28‑day mortality (Model 4: HR = 1.689, 95% CI 1.479–1.928, P < 0.0001). A similar pattern was observed for 90‑day all‑cause mortality (Table 3). Overall, higher SHR was significantly associated with increased short‑term mortality in sepsis, irrespective of diabetes status; moreover, the association appeared slightly stronger among patients without diabetes.

Restricted cubic spline

We applied restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression to characterize the relationship between the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality, aiming to identify potential thresholds. After adjustment for confounders, the test for nonlinearity indicated an approximately linear association (P for nonlinearity > 0.05). Increasing SHR was progressively associated with higher risks of 28-day and 90-day mortality among patients with sepsis. Subgroup analyses demonstrated similar SHR–mortality patterns in patients with and without diabetes. All results are presented in Fig. 3.

Nonlinear relationships between the SHR and short-term mortality rates in sepsis patients with and without diabetes mellitus

Kaplan‒Meier analysis

Patients were stratified into tertiles of the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR): T1, T2, and T3. Kaplan–Meier analyses were used to evaluate 28‑day and 90‑day mortality among sepsis patients, separately for those with and without diabetes. As shown in Fig. 4, the survival curve for T3 was significantly lower than those for T1 and T2 (log‑rank test, P < 0.0001), indicating that higher admission SHR is associated with increased short‑term mortality.

Kaplan‒meier survival curves for short-term mortality in sepsis patients stratified by the SHR

Subgroup analysis

To investigate potential clinical heterogeneity, we performed interaction and subgroup analyses (Fig. 5). During the analysis, we stratified by age (< 65 years and ≥ 65 years), sex, race, history of myocardial infarction, congestive heart failure, cerebrovascular disease, chronic lung disease, and diabetes. The results revealed a significant interaction effect on the relationship between the SHR and short-term mortality in septic patients with diabetes (P < 0.001). To explore this further, we assessed the relationship between the SHR and 28-day and 90-day mortality in septic patients with and without diabetes. The analysis revealed no significant interaction effect between SHR and mortality at 28 days or 90 days (P > 0.05). These findings suggest that higher SHR is associated with increased short-term mortality risk in patients with and without diabetes mellitus.

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of our findings (Table 4), we performed three sensitivity analyses: (1) excluding participants with missing baseline or outcome data; (2) excluding those with an ICU length of stay < 2 days; and (3) excluding patients with myocardial infarction. Across these analyses, the association between the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and 28-day and 90-day mortality among patients with sepsis remained consistent in magnitude, direction, and statistical significance, with no material changes. These results further support the robustness of our primary findings.

Discussion

In this study, we first examined the association between the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) and 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality in patients with sepsis. We found that higher SHR was significantly associated with an increased risk of both 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality, and this association remained robust after adjustment for multiple confounders. We also observed a linear relationship between SHR and short-term mortality. Subgroup analyses stratified by diabetes status indicated that higher SHR values were significantly associated with increased short-term mortality in sepsis patients regardless of diabetes. The consistency of these findings across subgroups suggests that SHR can serve as an independent predictor of short-term mortality in sepsis. These results provide new insights for risk assessment and preventive strategies in sepsis.

As a relative blood glucose indicator, SHRs have been studied primarily in the context of cardiovascular diseases. The SHR was first proposed by Roberts et al. to investigate the impact of acute hyperglycemia on inpatient mortality [13]. Over time, the SHR has been shown to be a reliable predictor of mortality and morbidity in many clinical diseases, including heart failure [20], acute myocardial infarction [12], acute ischemic stroke [21], and acute kidney injury [22]. Recently, Li Le et al. [23] and Zhang Shijie et al. [24] explored the relationships between the SHR and hospital mortality as well as one-year all-cause mortality in septic patients. Their studies indicated that high SHR values were associated with increased hospital and one-year mortality in critically ill septic patients. However, they did not analyze the relationship between the SHR and short-term mortality in septic patients. Additionally, these studies did not fully account for the influence of diabetes status on SHRs, especially in the context of diabetic and nondiabetic subgroups. In contrast, our study results suggest that higher SHR values are significantly associated with increased short-term mortality in septic patients, regardless of diabetes status. This result is consistent with the findings of Li Le [23]. Moreover, in the subgroup analysis, we found that nondiabetic septic patients had a greater short-term mortality risk than diabetic (DM) patients did, even with similar SHR values. This difference may be related to a potential “preadaptation effect” of acute stress hyperglycemia in diabetic patients [25]. This response may enhance antioxidant defenses and protect tissues from oxidative stress [26]. In contrast, nondiabetic patients lack this adaptive mechanism, increasing their susceptibility to the acute metabolic and inflammatory consequences of stress hyperglycemia. This increased susceptibility may exacerbate immune dysfunction, endothelial injury, and oxidative stress, further worsening the course of sepsis [27]. On the basis of these findings, we propose that nondiabetic septic patients may require closer monitoring and intervention, even those with similar SHR values, to mitigate the risk of adverse outcomes.

Sepsis may induce hyperglycemia through multiple mechanisms. First, the stress response in critically ill patients activates the hypothalamic‒pituitary‒adrenal axis, increasing the secretion of glucocorticoids, catecholamines, glucagon, and growth hormones, which in turn induces stress hyperglycemia [28, 29]. Second, the release of cytokines (such as tumor necrosis factor-α) synergizes with catecholamines to promote hepatic glucose production. Additionally, tumor necrosis factor-α not only inhibits insulin secretion but also promotes hyperglycemia [30, 31]. These mechanisms indicate that sepsis exacerbates stress hyperglycemia through various pathways. Furthermore, research suggests that hyperglycemia weakens immune function, activates cytokines, and intensifies hepatic oxidative stress [32, 33]. Hyperglycemia can also stimulate monocytes, increasing the synthesis and release of IL-6 [34], further worsening insulin resistance and promoting hepatic glucose release, thus exacerbating hyperglycemia [35, 36]. Notably, hyperglycemia itself has a proinflammatory effect, promoting the accumulation of monocytes and macrophages and stimulating the production of cytokines, IL-6, and IL-8, thereby intensifying inflammation and thrombosis and promoting the occurrence of disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC) [37]. These mechanisms suggest that sepsis promotes stress hyperglycemia through multiple pathways and that stress hyperglycemia may interact with immune responses and cytokines to aggravate the course of sepsis, significantly increasing mortality risk [38].

Although our study indicates that the stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) is an important predictor of short‑term mortality in patients with sepsis, several limitations should be noted. First, our inclusion/exclusion criteria were constrained by data availability, which may have introduced selection bias. Second, the retrospective design limits causal inference; despite multivariable adjustment and subgroup analyses, residual confounding may persist. Third, sample selection may have been influenced by known or unknown factors, potentially reducing representativeness and thereby affecting external validity. In addition, glucose and HbA1c were collected only within the first 24 h of admission, and SHR was derived from single time‑point measurements; missing data for some cases and the lack of longitudinal measurements may have affected the accuracy and robustness of our estimates. Finally, this was a single‑center study; multicenter prospective studies are warranted to further validate the utility of SHR as a prognostic biomarker.

Conclusion

In conclusion, among critically ill patients with sepsis, a higher stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) was significantly associated with increased 28-day and 90-day all-cause mortality, independent of diabetes status. SHR may serve as an effective predictor of adverse outcomes in this population, but multicenter prospective studies are warranted to validate these findings.
