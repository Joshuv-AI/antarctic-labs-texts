Mismatch Negativity in Subjects at Clinical High Risk for Psychosis: No Evidence of Progressive Decline
Authors: Jiseon Jang, Eugenie Choe, Minji Ha, Minah Kim, Jun Soo Kwon
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTDuration‐deviant mismatch negativity has been investigated as a potential biomarker for predicting early psychosis clinical outcomes. Although progressive mismatch negativity changes have been linked to neurodegeneration and cognitive decline after psychosis onset, it is unclear whether similar changes occur in patients at clinical high risk for psychosis. Therefore, we examined longitudinal mismatch negativity changes and their associations with cognition and functioning in 24 clinical high risk for psychosis individuals and 22 healthy controls. Participants underwent duration‐deviant mismatch negativity recordings at baseline and the 1–2‐year follow‐up. Neurocognitive functioning was assessed via Trail Making Test parts A and B, and general functioning was measured with the Global Assessment of Functioning scale. Group differences in mismatch negativity amplitude change over time were evaluated using repeated‐measures analysis of covariance, and its associations with cognitive and functional changes were assessed via Spearman correlations. Compared with healthy controls, individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis had significantly reduced mismatch negativity amplitudes at the FCz electrode site at baseline. However, there was no significant group‐by‐time interaction in mismatch negativity amplitude, nor were its changes significantly correlated with neurocognitive or general functioning changes, suggesting that progressive changes in duration‐deviant mismatch negativity may not occur prior to psychosis onset in individuals at clinical high risk for psychosis. Given the heterogeneous outcomes in the clinical high risk for psychosis population, larger samples and longer follow‐up durations are needed to elucidate the clinical significance of duration‐deviant mismatch negativity in this group.Keywords: clinical high risk for psychosis, cognitive function, event‐related potential, general functional status, longitudinal change, mismatch negativity

Introduction

Mismatch negativity (MMN) is one of the most widely utilized and well‐validated event‐related potentials (ERPs) across various clinical fields. Its noninvasive nature, the fact that it reflects preattentive auditory processing, as well as its high temporal resolution (Gevins et al. 1999), reliability, and reproducibility (Roach et al. 2020), makes MMN a valuable tool for clinical and research applications. It is elicited when a deviant stimulus violates the regularity of recently established auditory patterns, reflecting a preattentive and automatic process of detecting unexpected auditory changes (Näätänen et al. 2001). Since its conceptual introduction by Näätänen et al. (1978), MMN has been extensively studied not only in neurological disorders but also in the field of mental health (Gené‐Cos et al. 1999).

Especially in psychotic disorders, MMN is considered a promising biomarker. It has been consistently reported that reduced MMN amplitude is observed in patients with schizophrenia, suggesting its association with N‐methyl‐D‐aspartate (NMDA) receptor hypofunction, one of the proposed pathophysiological mechanisms of psychotic disorders (Javitt et al. 1996; Olney et al. 1999). These reductions in MMN amplitude, especially in duration‐deviant MMN, have been detected in subjects at clinical high risk for psychosis (CHR‐P) (Erickson et al. 2016; Haigh et al. 2017). In addition to its simple amplitude reduction, MMN has also been found to be associated with prognostic outcomes. Numerous studies have reported that MMN can be used to predict prognosis in early psychosis patients, including individuals at CHR‐P (Bodatsch et al. 2011; Higuchi et al. 2013; Kim et al. 2018, 2023; Shaikh et al. 2012), with more attenuated MMN amplitude at baseline associated with later transitions to overt psychotic disorders or other poor prognostic outcomes.

Cross‐sectional studies comparing MMN amplitudes across the different stages of psychotic disorders have shown stagewise MMN impairment in the following order: CHR‐P, first‐episode psychosis (FEP), and chronic schizophrenia (Erickson et al. 2016; Rissling and Light 2010; Umbricht et al. 2006). Longitudinal studies have reported progressive MMN impairment over time after the onset of psychotic disorders (Devrim‐Üçok et al. 2008; Higgins et al. 2021; Kaur et al. 2013; Lho et al. 2020; Salisbury et al. 2007), and MMN appears to reflect pathophysiologic changes associated with cognitive deterioration or structural brain changes following psychosis onset (Lho et al. 2020; Salisbury et al. 2007). For example, Lho et al. (2020) reported that progressive MMN impairment was observed in FEP patients regardless of improvements in patients' symptoms or overall functioning. Rather, the degree of MMN impairment was associated with cognitive decline over time, suggesting that MMN impairment in FEP patients reflects progressive pathophysiological changes.

Unlike FEP studies, longitudinal studies on MMN changes in subjects at CHR‐P are limited in number and have yielded inconsistent results. Four studies have examined longitudinal MMN changes in individuals at CHR‐P (Atkinson et al. 2017; Koshiyama et al. 2017; Lavoie et al. 2018; Tateno et al. 2021). Three of these studies tried to examine whether MMN amplitude impairment was associated with the transition to psychotic disorders by comparing individuals who transitioned to psychosis with those who did not (Atkinson et al. 2017; Lavoie et al. 2018; Tateno et al. 2021). Two studies reported progressive MMN impairment in the transition group (Lavoie et al. 2018; Tateno et al. 2021), but one study did not find such a change (Atkinson et al. 2017). Given the heterogeneous prognosis of CHR‐P and the growing interest in outcomes beyond the transition to psychotic disorders (Michel et al. 2018), Koshiyama et al. (2017) examined longitudinal changes in MMN among individuals at CHR‐P regardless of transition status and reported no significant change over time. These inconsistencies in previous studies may stem from small sample sizes, relatively short follow‐up durations, and the lack of accompanying data reflecting underlying pathophysiological changes, such as cognitive performance or general functional status.

Accordingly, the present study aimed to investigate whether individuals at CHR‐P exhibit longitudinal changes in duration‐deviant MMN that are associated with progressive declines in cognitive function or general functional status over a 1–2‐year follow‐up period. The Trail Making Test (TMT) was used to assess cognitive domains related to MMN comprehensively, including cognitive flexibility, working memory, visuospatial processing, and processing speed (Sánchez‐Cubillo et al. 2009). We hypothesized that, compared with healthy controls (HCs), patients at CHR‐P would show a progressive decline in duration‐deviant MMN amplitude. We also hypothesized that changes in MMN amplitude would be associated with changes in neurocognitive function, as measured by the TMT, and general functional status.

Materials and Methods

Participants and Clinical Data

A total of 24 individuals at CHR‐P and 22 HCs completed MMN assessments at both baseline and follow‐up, with an interval of 1–2 years. Individuals at CHR‐P were recruited from the inpatient and outpatient clinics of the Department of Neuropsychiatry at Seoul National University Hospital as well as the Seoul Youth Clinic (www.youthclinic.org) (Kwon et al. 2012). Clinical interviews were conducted with participants at CHR‐P via the Structured Clinical Interview for the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition, Axis I Disorders (SCID‐I). The CHR‐P state was diagnosed using the Structured Interview for Prodromal Symptoms (SIPS) (Miller et al. 2003), and symptom severity was evaluated using the validated Korean version of the Scale of Prodromal Symptoms (Jung et al. 2010). Antipsychotic treatment was recorded and standardized as daily olanzapine equivalent doses (Gardner et al. 2010). HCs were recruited via internet advertisements and screened using the SCID‐I Nonpatient Edition (SCID‐NP), with individuals with a family history of psychotic disorders in first‐ to third‐degree relatives excluded. In both the CHR‐P and HC groups, functioning was assessed using the modified Global Assessment of Functioning (mGAF), the intelligence quotient (IQ) was evaluated using the abbreviated version of the Korean‐Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (K‐WAIS), and neurocognitive function was assessed using the TMT and part A and B reaction times.

The exclusion criteria for both groups included substance abuse or dependence (except nicotine), neurological disorders, a history of significant head trauma or neurosurgery, other underlying medical conditions deemed to be associated with psychiatric symptoms, and intellectual disability (IQ < 70).

All participants received a detailed explanation of the study and provided written informed consent (IRB No. H‐1110‐009‐380). For those under 18 years of age, consent was additionally obtained from their legal guardians. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Hospital (IRB No. H‐2411‐094‐1587) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2013).

MMN Acquisition

The MMN acquisition protocol is identical to that previously used in our laboratory (Kim et al. 2017). Electroencephalography (EEG) data were continuously recorded using a Neuroscan SynAmps system (Compumedics, VC, Australia) and collected via a Quick‐Cap equipped with 64 electrodes positioned according to the modified international 10–20 system. The EEG signals were digitized at a sampling rate of 1000 Hz and bandpass filtered online between 0.05 and 100 Hz. Both mastoids served as reference electrodes. Horizontal and vertical electrooculograms (EOGs) were recorded using electrodes placed below and at the outer canthus of the left eye, respectively. Electrode impedance was kept below 5 kΩ throughout the recording. The duration‐deviant MMN data were recorded using a passive oddball paradigm. During the recording, the subjects were instructed to focus on a “Where's Waldo?” pseudo task. A total of 818 frequent standard auditory stimuli (1000 Hz, 80 dB, 50 ms) and 182 deviant stimuli (1000 Hz, 80 dB, 100 ms) were pseudorandomly presented using the STIM 2 sound generator (Compumedics, VC, Australia). The interstimulus interval was set at 600 ms.

Data Preprocessing

EEG data were preprocessed using Curry 8 software (Compumedics, VC, Australia). The data were rereferenced to a common average reference and bandpass filtered between 0.1 and 30 Hz. Bad channels (up to 7%) were interpolated using adjacent channels, and an artifact reduction algorithm was applied to remove eyeball movement artifacts (Semlitsch et al. 1986). Data were epoched from 100 ms prestimulus onset to 400 ms poststimulus onset, with baseline correction performed using the mean amplitude of the 100 ms prestimulus period. Epochs with voltages exceeding ±75 μV were considered artefacts and automatically removed. MMN was obtained from the ERPs recorded at the FCz electrodes, where duration‐deviant MMN showed the maximal amplitude (Garrido et al. 2009), by subtracting the ERPs elicited by the standard stimuli from those elicited by the deviant stimuli. The MMN peak amplitude was determined by identifying the most negative deflection occurring between 130 and 250 ms poststimulus.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 29.0 (IBM, New York, United states). To compare baseline demographic variables, neuropsychological test scores, and MMN amplitudes at the FCz electrode site between the CHR‐P and HC groups, independent t tests (for variables with equal variances) or Welch's t tests (for variables with unequal variances) were performed for continuous data, whereas chi‐square analyses or Fisher's exact tests were used for categorical data. Baseline and follow‐up MMN amplitudes for the CHR‐P subjects and HCs were compared using a repeated‐measures analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), with group (CHR‐P vs. HC) as the between‐subject factor, time (baseline vs. follow‐up) as the within‐subject factor, and interval (days) as a covariate. Additionally, Spearman's correlation analyses were performed to explore the associations between changes in MMN amplitude and TMT performance as well as mGAF scores over time in individuals at CHR‐P. Exploratory correlation analysis was conducted to examine the association between MMN amplitude changes and follow‐up duration.

Results

Participant Characteristics

Table 1 presents the baseline and follow‐up characteristics of the CHR‐P and HC participants. There were no significant group differences in age, sex, handedness, or education years. Compared with HCs, subjects at CHR‐P had a shorter follow‐up duration (t = 2.66, p = 0.013) and lower IQ (t = 4.89, p < 0.001) and mGAF scores (t = 12.26, p < 0.001). There was no significant group difference in the TMT‐A and TMT‐B reaction times. When baseline and follow‐up characteristics in the CHR‐P group were compared, significant improvements in the mGAF (t = 2.383, p = 0.026), total SOPS (t = 3.431, p = 0.002), positive (t = 4.326, p < 0.001), and disorganization (t = 2.520, p = 0.019) scores were observed. No significant differences were detected for the TMT‐A (t = 0.047, p = 0.963) or TMT‐B (t = −0.745, p = 0.465) scores from baseline to follow‐up in the CHR‐P group. At baseline, eight CHR‐P participants were taking antipsychotics, eight were taking antidepressants, seven were taking anxiolytics, and three were taking mood stabilizers. At follow‐up, the numbers of participants taking those medications were 13, 11, 8, and 5, respectively. Some individuals were prescribed more than one type of medication. The antipsychotic dose ranged from 0 to 18.32 mg (olanzapine equivalents) at baseline, and from 0 to 10.05 mg at follow‐up.

MMN Characteristics

Table 2 and Figures 1, 2, 3 display MMN characteristics at baseline and follow‐up. At baseline, the MMN amplitude was significantly lower in the CHR‐P group than in the HC group (t = 2.03, p = 0.047). Two‐way repeated‐measures ANCOVA, with group (CHR‐P vs. HC) as the between‐subject factor, time (baseline vs. follow‐up) as the within‐subject factor, and interval (days) as a covariate, revealed no significant group‐by‐time interaction for MMN amplitude at the FCz electrode (F 1,44 = 0.30, p = 0.587). Spearman's correlation analysis did not reveal any significant relationship between changes in MMN amplitude at the FCz electrode site and changes in neurocognitive function, as measured by the TMT‐A (r = 0.051, p = 0.794) and TMT‐B (r = 0.129, p = 0.319) reaction times or general functional status, as measured by mGAF scores (r = −0.038, p = 0.804). No significant associations were found in exploratory analyses between follow‐up duration and MMN amplitude changes in either group (HC: r = 0.262, p = 0.237; CHR‐P: r = −0.390, p = 0.059). Despite a borderline p‐value in the CHR‐P group (r = −0.390, p = 0.059), visual inspection of the data revealed no meaningful association.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to investigate whether individuals at CHR‐P exhibit a significant decline in duration‐deviant MMN and to explore whether there is an association between MMN changes and cognitive and functional outcomes. Consistent with previous studies, we confirmed that the MMN amplitude at the FCz electrode site in subjects at CHR‐P was impaired compared with that in HCs at baseline (Erickson et al. 2016). Unlike our initial hypothesis, there was no longitudinal deterioration in MMN amplitude in individuals at CHR‐P. There was no significant correlation between changes in MMN amplitude and changes in neurocognitive function or general functional status. These results suggest that duration‐deviant MMN amplitude may not be progressively impaired before the onset of psychotic disorders, such as in the CHR‐P stage.

In the present study, we did not observe a longitudinal MMN change in the CHR‐P group that differed from that in the HC group. Although we conducted our research with a larger sample size and more consistent intervals between the HC and CHR‐P groups than did the previous study (Koshiyama et al. 2017), we reached the same conclusion. In the other two previous studies that reported longitudinal changes in the high‐risk state (Lavoie et al. 2018; Tateno et al. 2021), significant longitudinal changes were observed in the transitioned subgroup compared with the nontransitioned subgroup. When examining the CHR‐P group as a whole, the longitudinal change did not appear to be prominent.

The CHR‐P characteristics of the current study may provide an explanation for these results. First, the heterogeneity within the CHR‐P group might mask a progressive MMN change, given that only a small subset of individuals actually transitions to overt psychotic disorders. It has been reported that only approximately 35% of the CHR‐P population develops overt psychotic disorders (de Pablo et al. 2021), whereas approximately 50% develop nonpsychotic psychiatric disorders (Michel et al. 2018). While other psychiatric disorders also exhibit MMN changes, schizophrenia is known to show the most pronounced alterations (Erickson et al. 2016; Kaur et al. 2011), and each disease may have a different trajectory. This could make it difficult to detect longitudinal MMN changes in the entire CHR‐P group. Second, the characteristics of the individuals at CHR‐P who participated in the follow‐up assessment may have influenced MMN changes over time. The inconsistent findings in CHR‐P research appear to depend on the characteristics of the recruited CHR‐P sample, and the group that underwent follow‐up sometimes presented different features than did those that did not (Atkinson et al. 2017; Kaur et al. 2013; Salisbury et al. 2007). In this study, the CHR‐P group exhibited marked improvements in SOPS and mGAF scores during the follow‐up period, and the differences in MMN observed at baseline between the CHR‐P and HC groups were no longer evident at follow‐up. Notably, none of the individuals in the CHR‐P group transitioned to psychosis during the follow‐up period. These findings suggest that the CHR‐P group in our study may more accurately represent a particular subgroup of individuals, potentially those at a lower risk of transition to psychotic disorders.

Additionally, it may be associated with the nonlinear developmental trajectory of MMN related to age. MMN is known to increase during late adolescence until the twenties, after which it tends to decrease with age (Bishop et al. 2011; Cheng et al. 2013). Since the CHR‐P group predominantly comprises individuals in adolescence to young adulthood, nonlinear developmental changes in MMN may distort disease‐related changes. During adolescence, increased connectivity between frontal and temporal regions is observed, which is associated with changes in MMN (Cooray et al. 2016). In the CHR‐P group, the normal developmental processes of frontotemporal functional connectivity and white matter were distorted (Merritt et al. 2021; Mills et al. 2016). In addition, reports indicate that children and adolescents who experience psychological difficulties or who have risk factors for schizophrenia have different developmental trajectories of MMN than do those without such risks (Laurens et al. 2020; Usui et al. 2023). These findings raise the possibility that changes in MMN among patients at CHR‐P may emerge in more complex patterns through interactions with age and developmental processes.

MMN has been linked to NMDA receptor function (Javitt et al. 1996), and NMDA receptor hypofunction has been proposed as a mechanism underlying schizophrenia (Olney et al. 1999). MMN impairment from the prodromal stage has been speculated to reflect NMDA receptor abnormalities preceding psychosis onset (Snyder and Gao 2013). One possible reason for the absence of clear longitudinal MMN changes in this study is the heterogeneous prognosis among CHR‐P individuals, which may lead to variability in MMN trajectories. Previous studies have reported greater MMN impairment in CHR‐P converters than nonconverters and have associated MMN deficits with poorer clinical outcomes (Bodatsch et al. 2011; Higuchi et al. 2013; Kim et al. 2018, 2023; Shaikh et al. 2012). In longitudinal CHR‐P studies stratified by transition status, two studies (Lavoie et al. 2018; Tateno et al. 2021) reported significant MMN decline in the transition group. In the present study, however, no participants converted to psychosis during follow‐up, and mean clinical symptom and functional scores were improved at follow‐up compared to baseline. This variability in outcomes may help explain why progressive MMN changes were not clearly observed in our sample. A second possible explanation is that the NMDA–MMN relationship may be complex and not strictly linear. Although NMDA antagonists generally suppress MMN, low‐dose or low‐affinity antagonists have paradoxically been shown to enhance it (Harms et al. 2021). Moreover, while glutamate—the primary NMDA receptor agonist—has been associated with MMN in HCs and FEP, no such relationship has been observed in CHR (Nagai et al. 2017), suggesting that this relationship may be more variable than typically assumed and complicating the detection of longitudinal changes.

This study has several limitations. First, the limited sample size may have reduced the statistical power to detect subtle changes in MMN or weak associations with cognitive and functional measures. Second, medication use in the CHR‐P group was not controlled, making it difficult to exclude effects of psychotropic drugs. However, previous studies suggest that antipsychotics, hypnotics, and anxiolytics do not significantly affect MMN (Kasai et al. 2002; Nagai et al. 2013). Medication may still influence cognition and functioning, though effects on tasks like the TMT appear inconsistent (McGurk et al. 2004; Meltzer and McGurk 1999; Veselinović et al. 2019). Therefore, future longitudinal studies focusing on medication‐naïve CHR‐P individuals are needed to clarify MMN and cognitive changes without medication confounds. Third, there is a possibility of selection bias. The samples included in our study may have different characteristics from those who were lost to follow‐up or who transitioned to psychosis within a relatively short period of 1–2 years of follow‐up.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we did not find significant longitudinal changes in MMN among individuals at CHR‐P, and the degree of MMN change was not associated with changes in cognitive or general functional status. These findings suggest that, unlike in patients with overt psychotic disorders such as FEP, MMN alterations may not be prominent during the CHR‐P stage and may be limited in their sensitivity to detect early neurophysiological changes or subtle cognitive decline. However, this interpretation should be considered with caution given the small sample size, limited follow‐up duration, and the possibility that the included participants may not fully represent the broader population of individuals at CHR‐P.
