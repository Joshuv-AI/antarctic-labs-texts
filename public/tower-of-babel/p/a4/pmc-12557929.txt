Renal complications in pediatric Crohn’s disease: a rare case of bilateral hydronephrosis and review of the literature
Authors: Marta Yagüe Barrado, D. Cabezali Barbancho, C. Tordable Ojeda, A. Gómez Fraile
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Crohn’s disease (CD) can affect the genitourinary tract in various ways, with acalculous hydronephrosis being a rare complication first described by Hyams in 1943 (Hyams et al. Am J Med. 95:257-9, 1943), and few pediatric cases have been published in the literature. We present the case of a 14-year-old patient with CD who developed bilateral acalculous hydronephrosis. The diagnosis was made through computed tomography (CT), and it was successfully managed with the placement of double-J catheters. This article highlights the importance of monitoring and timely treatment of genitourinary complications in CD.

Introduction

The genitourinary tract can be affected in CD in various ways, such as the formation of kidney stones, intestinal fistulas, perivesical infections, or perinephric abscesses. One of the described complications is acalculous hydronephrosis [1].

The incidence varies between 0.3% and 25%, primarily occurring in the third decade of life, commonly in the right urinary tract and in patients with a perforating pattern of the disease [2–6].

Ureteral obstruction typically occurs at the terminal line of the pelvis and is due to the penetration of the inflamed distal ileum into the retroperitoneum, as well as to intestinal microperforations or retroperitoneal fistulas that generate abscesses. Other possible causes include ureteral akinesia secondary to inflammation and perirenal fibrosis [2–6].

Case report

We present the case of a 14-year-old male with a known diagnosis of Crohn’s disease (inflammatory phenotype), under treatment with adalimumab due to previous therapeutic failure. The patient was admitted with fever, catarrhal symptoms, and abdominal and lumbar pain, in the absence of diarrhea. Renal function was preserved.

Abdominal ultrasound revealed bilateral ureterohydronephrosis without a clearly identifiable obstructive cause. These findings were confirmed by non-contrast computed tomography (CTNC), which is the preferred modality when evaluating for nephrolithiasis. However, no lithiasis was identified. Given the suspicion of an inflammatory origin, a contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) was subsequently performed to evaluate for associated complications such as abscesses or fistulas; none were observed.

Congenital abnormalities, including ureteropelvic junction obstruction, duplicated ureters, or compression by aberrant lower polar vessels were considered but not identified on imaging studies. Although no fixed anatomical obstruction was found, a transient inflammatory ureteropelvic junction obstruction could not be excluded, particularly in the context of an underlying systemic inflammatory condition such as Crohn’s disease. Dietl’s crisis was ruled out as it is less commonly bilateral.

Given the persistence of symptoms and imaging findings, bilateral double-J stents were placed, leading to rapid clinical improvement and radiological resolution, suggesting a reversible and likely functional cause. The DJ stents were maintained for six months, after which they were removed without complications. Follow-up ultrasounds showed sustained resolution of the hydronephrosis, and the patient remained asymptomatic. The patient is currently under ongoing long-term follow-up, with continuous monitoring of both renal function and Crohn’s disease activity.

This case highlights the diagnostic challenges in pediatric patients with complex comorbidities such as Crohn’s disease, where systemic inflammation may contribute to transient but clinically significant obstructive uropathy.

Discussion

Acalculous hydronephrosis is an uncommon but increasingly recognized extraintestinal manifestation of Crohn’s disease (CD), particularly in patients with ileocolic involvement. This condition typically affects the right side due to anatomical proximity, and presents with nonspecific symptoms such as flank, pelvic, or thigh pain, a palpable abdominal mass, and occasionally hypertension mediated by the renin-angiotensin system [2, 6].

Diagnostic evaluation often begins with imaging, particularly CT, which can identify both ureteral obstruction and intra-abdominal inflammatory masses. While intravenous urography has historically been used—as in the classic series by Siminovitch et al., who reported ureterohydronephrosis in 45 out of 958 CD patients [2]—modern imaging techniques like CT and magnetic resonance urography are now preferred due to better resolution and lower radiation exposure [5]. It should be noted that while CT and MRI can define anatomical alterations, nuclear medicine imaging (e.g., diuretic renography) may be superior in evaluating drainage patterns and detecting functional obstruction, such as a segmental adynamic ureter.

Urinary tract infections, although not commonly associated with this complication, have occasionally been documented. In one series of 27 patients, Escherichia coli was the most frequently isolated pathogen, with less frequent occurrences of Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Enterococcus spp., and Klebsiella spp. This underscores the need for microbiological assessment in patients with obstructive uropathy and systemic symptoms [4].

Initial management typically involves immunosuppressive therapy and urinary diversion via double-J catheter placement. Ben-Ami et al. reported favorable outcomes in 4 CD patients, all of whom responded to corticosteroids or urinary diversion without recurrence [4]. In more severe or refractory cases, surgical interventions such as intestinal resection or ureterolysis may be necessary, as demonstrated by Fukunaga et al., where 7 patients—especially those with penetrating disease—required operative treatment [5].

Pediatric cases, while less frequently reported, are clinically significant. Kent et al. described a 14-year-old girl who developed right ureterohydronephrosis following corticosteroid therapy, ultimately requiring ileal resection and abscess drainage [3]. Smith et al. reported a case of bilateral hydronephrosis with renal failure and posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome in an 8-year-old girl with CD [7].

Gupta et al. found that 14.7% of pediatric CD patients showed renal symptoms [8]. Johnson et al. and Lee et al. have also reported pediatric renal manifestations in recent years [9, 10].

This growing body of literature supports the notion that acalculous hydronephrosis in CD is not limited to adult patients or historical contexts, but remains a relevant and potentially underdiagnosed complication in modern pediatric practice. Conclusion

Acalculous hydronephrosis in patients with Crohn’s disease is a rare but significant complication. Initial management with double-J catheter placement is usually effective, although some patients may require additional surgical intervention. Close follow-up is essential to prevent long-term complications.
