Evaluation of perceived urgency from single-trial EEG data elicited by upper-body vibration feedback using deep learning
Authors: Haneen Alsuradi, Jiacheng Shen, Wanjoo Park, Mohamad Eid
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractNotification systems that convey urgency without adding cognitive burden are crucial in human–computer interaction. Haptic feedback systems, particularly those utilizing vibration feedback, have emerged as a compelling solution, capable of providing desirable levels of urgency depending on the application. High-risk applications require an evaluation of the urgency level elicited during critical notifications. Traditional evaluations of perceived urgency rely on subjective self-reporting and performance metrics, which, while useful, are not real-time and can be distracting from the task at hand. In contrast, EEG technology offers a direct, non-intrusive method of assessing the user’s cognitive state. Leveraging deep learning, this study introduces a novel approach to evaluate perceived urgency from single-trial EEG data, induced by vibration stimuli on the upper body, utilizing our newly collected urgency-via-vibration dataset. The proposed model combines a 2D convolutional neural network with a temporal convolutional network to capture spatial and temporal EEG features, outperforming several established EEG models. The proposed model achieves an average classification accuracy of 83% through leave-one-subject-out cross-validation across three urgency classes (not urgent, urgent, and very urgent) from a single trial of EEG data. Furthermore, explainability analysis showed that the prefrontal brain region, followed by the central brain region, are the most influential in predicting the urgency level. A follow-up neural statistical analysis revealed an increase in event-related synchronization (ERS) in the theta frequency band (4–7 Hz) with the increased level of urgency, which is associated with high arousal and attention in the neuroscience literature. A limitation of this study is that the proposed model’s performance was tested only the urgency-via-vibration dataset, which may affect the generalizability of the findings.Subject terms: Electrical and electronic engineering, Biomedical engineering, Sensory processing, Prefrontal cortex

Introduction

Notification systems that elicit a desirable level of urgency are critical in human–computer interaction. Haptic feedback systems have the potential to modulate perceived urgency in a wide spectrum of applications such as in-vehicle1 or mobile phone2 alerting systems, interaction with impaired individuals3, and battlefield awareness for soldiers4. Haptic feedback does not increase cognitive load5, is robust to audible noise6, engages people at different emotional levels7, and is confidential8. In particular, vibration feedback systems are becoming increasingly popular in notification systems due to their low cost, wearability, and expressiveness9. Research has demonstrated the ability of vibration feedback to elicit a desirable sense of urgency with minimal distraction or annoyance10,11. For instance, a low urgency vibration is shown to inform the user about the time during oral presentations without distracting them from the presentation. A high urgency vibration alerts a driver that they need to pay immediate attention to avoid a collision.

Existing approaches to evaluate the perceived urgency are based on self-reporting (questionnaires) and/or performance measures (such as response time and hit rate)12. While these approaches serve as indirect indicators of cognitive state, they do not provide accurate evaluation of the mental experience of the user13. Brain scanning technologies (such as fMRI, EEG, and fNIRS) enable direct and non-intrusive evaluation of cognitive state14. Among these, EEG is a promising brain imaging technique, as it is non-invasive, inexpensive, safe, and fully wearable, and characterized by high temporal resolution15.

Although EEG data contains abundant information on cognitive processes16, analyzing single-trial EEG data is challenging due to the low signal-to-noise ratio, the non-stationarity of brain signals17, and contamination of EEG data with physiological (e.g., muscular, ocular) and non-physiological (e.g. interference, cable movement) artifacts. To mitigate these challenges, deep learning models are utilized to extract EEG features that correlate with cognitive functions such as emotional responses18, attention19, mental workload20, and motor imagery21. Notably, models such as DeepConvNet and ShallowConvNet22 are deep learning models that optimize various network and design parameters to account for the characteristics of the EEG data. Furthermore, EEGNet, a compact 2D CNN model, utilizes depth-wise and separable convolutions to better extract temporal and spectral features of EEG23. Building on this foundation, EEGNeX24 is a recent 2D CNN model that is developed further on the EEGNet model to improve the classification accuracy on several EEG datasets. Additionally, TCNet-Fusion incorporates a temporal convolutional network (TCN) for time-series classification25.

In this study, we utilize deep learning to evaluate the perceived urgency conveyed by vibration stimulation on the upper body using single-trial EEG data. The proposed model combines a 2D convolutional neural network-based model with a temporal convolutional network to capture both spatial and temporal information. The proposed model outperforms several benchmarked EEG models. Shapley values are utilized as a model explainability method in order to verify the reliability of the proposed model and gain deeper insights into the features that are most prominent for the evaluation of perceived urgency. Furthermore, those features identified by the explainability analysis are verified through a statistical neural analysis, thus complementing the model’s interpretability and ensuring robustness in feature selection.

Methods

Participants

In this study, 31 participants (21 males and 10 females, aged between 18 and 39 years old) were enrolled. All participants met the inclusion criteria: above 18 years old, normal vision or corrected-to-normal vision, no neurological disorder or brain injury, and pain-free upper body. All participants were informed about the experiment protocol and signed the informed consent form. Furthermore, informed consent was obtained from all subjects for the publication of identifying information and images in an online open-access publication. The protocol followed in this study was approved by the New York University Abu Dhabi Institutional Review Board (HRPP-2022-96) and followed the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental setup and protocol

In this experiment, vibrotactile stimulation to the upper body was delivered using a haptic vest known as the TactSuitX40 (https://www.bhaptics.com/tactsuit) (bHaptics Inc., Daejeon, South Korea). The participant faced a screen where the experimental sequence was presented and the experiment was designed using Psychopy (Version 2023.1.2). As for EEG recording, EEG data were recorded using a BrainAmps amplifier (https://brainvision.com/products/brainamp-standard/) (Brain Products GmbH, Germany). The EEG signals were collected using a 64-channel EEG cap (EasyCap, Herrsching, Germany), which was arranged based on the 10-20 international system. The FPz location hosted the ground electrode, and the FCz location was used for the online reference electrode. The experimental setup is shown in Figure 1a.

Experimental setup and stimulation design. (a) Experimental setup showing a participant wearing an EEG cap and the haptic vest. A screen is used for visual instructions and a keypad is used for self reporting. (b) Details of the vibration patterns used to elicit urgency for the UVP and VUVP patterns.

The experiment included 13 runs, with the initial run designated for training purposes. In each run, there were nine trials distributed equally between three conditions: no vibration pattern (NVP), urgent vibration pattern (UVP), and very urgent vibration pattern (VUVP). The design parameters for the UVP and VUVP conditions are illustrated in Figure 1b. The UVP had a duration of 550 ms with a vibration intensity of 0.39g, while the VUVP had a duration of 300 ms and a vibration intensity of 1.96g. Furthermore, each vibration pattern employed a different number of vibration motors located on the chest and abdomen: 4 actuators were used for the UVP, while 20 actuators were used for the VUVP. Figure 2 shows the sequence of events in a single trial of the experimental protocol. The trial commences with a period of rest (1500 or 3500 ms), followed by the presentation of visual and auditory cues to ensure the participant’s attention (500 ms). This is subsequently followed by a 2000 ms stimulation phase. During this phase, participants were subjected to either the NVP, UVP, or the VUVP. Consequently, each condition was repeated a total of 36 times by each subject. The stimulation period was followed by three questions assessing the level of perceived urgency, annoyance, and acceptance. These questions were assessed using a 5-point Likert scale. Five trials of the Stroop task26 for wash-off purposes were presented after every trial of stimulation to avoid saturation due to repetitive vibration.

Experimental protocol depicting the timeline of a single trial. A trial starts with a rest period, followed by an auditory cue for grabbing the participant’s attention. The vibration stimulation is then delivered through the haptic vest, followed by a self-reporting period on the urgency, annoyance and acceptance levels experienced by the participant.

EEG data preprocessing

The EEG data used to train the deep learning models underwent minimal preprocessing, validating the ability of the models to extract meaningful features from within the noise, which is needed in brain–computer-interface (BCI) and online-evaluation applications. The EEG data acquired from 60 channels (excluded four channels around the ears from recording) was filtered between 0.1 and 80 Hz, as this frequency range is where human EEG signals primarily exhibit power. This was followed by a notch filter to suppress power line noise (50 Hz) from the data. Baseline correction was then performed, with the baseline set between −1000 and −500 ms before the stimulation onset. Next, EEG data were epoched to strictly include the 2000 ms stimulation period. Finally, the data was downsampled from 1 kHz to 200 Hz.

However, for the neural cross-validation (“Neural cross-validation” section), where we validate the neural correlates identified by the explainability analysis, the EEG data processing includes additional steps. After applying bandpass and notch filters, we use the artifact subspace reconstruction method for artifact removal (muscular, ocular, etc.) and data cleaning27. This is followed by re-referencing the EEG data using the common average referencing method. Subsequently, EEG data were epoched such that 3000 ms before and 4000 ms after the vibration onset were extracted. Epoched EEG data in time-domain were baseline corrected using a baseline between −1000 and −500 ms before the onset through a simple subtraction. Time domain EEG data is then converted to the time-frequency domain through Morlet Wavelet transformation with 3-cycle wavelet considering a frequency range between 1 and 80 Hz. After obtaining the power of the transformed epochs, trials of the same condition were averaged for each participant. This is followed by decibel conversion and baseline normalization by subtraction using a baseline window from −1000 to −500 ms with respect to the onset.

Dataset and notation

The urgency-via-vibration dataset consists of three classes referring to the three stimulation conditions: NVP, UVP, and VUVP, denoted as yi∈{0,1,2}. The data is collected from N = 31 subjects, where each subject has 36 trials per condition. The input Xi∈RC×T contains C = 60 channels and time-points T = 400, corresponding to a 2-second stimulation period with a sampling frequency of 200 Hz. Therefore, the dataset can be represented as S={Xi,yi}i=1m, where m = 3348 (total number of trials). All models considered in this manuscript take as an input a single EEG trial Xi and aim to map it to its corresponding label yi.

Proposed model

EEGNeX is an entirely 2D convolutional neural network-based model that can directly extract features from raw EEG data. This architecture showed efficacy as it outperformed 16 prevailing models and their variants across four BCI paradigms23. However, EEG data often exhibit intricate temporal dependencies. Thus, it is essential to capture long-term dependencies in EEG data. TCN is particularly effective at capturing long-range dependencies within sequences using dilated convolutions30, which enables it to create adaptable receptive fields and consider remote contexts while processing data. As such, in our proposed model, we combined EEGNeX head with TCN to better capture both spatial and temporal information.

As shown in Figure 3a, the proposed model is comprised of three blocks: (1) a convolutional Block, (2) a TCN block, and (3) a classification block. Each layer in the convolutional block is followed by a LayerNorm regularization and an Exponential linear unit (ELU) activation function. The first layer applies 8 filters with a 1×32 kernel to capture local patterns within each channel. Subsequently, another layer follows with 32 filters and the same 1×32 kernel to extract more complex features. Lastly, depth multipliers and average pooling are applied to reduce spatial dimensions while preserving relevant EEG patterns. It is important to note that there are two main differences between the proposed convolutional block and EEGNeX model. The first change is the use of the LayerNorm layer instead of the BatchNorm layer, as originally proposed in the EEGNeX architecture, after each convolutional layer. Layer normalization layers are better suited for sequence modeling (i.e. EEG data is a time-series data), training with relatively small batch sizes (i.e. we use a batch size of 64), and in situations where covariate shifts are present (i.e. the distribution of the training and testing data could vary—this is the case for leave-one-subject-out testing, where we test on completely novel subject)31. The second change is the inclusion of an activation function after the first convolutional layer, which is not present in the original EEGNeX model. We found that adding/removing this layer did not impact the performance of the model, and thus, for consistency with the rest of the convolutional layers, we opted to add it. To further address the temporal nature of the EEG data, we employ the TCN block (Figure 3b). Specifically, we incorporate the Dilated Causal Convolution (DCN) layer to account for temporal context and causal relationships. Following the DCN layer, we add batch normalization to enhance training stability. We add a residual part to the output of two DCNs with batch normalization before feeding it into the final ELU activation function. Finally, the Classification Block consists of a single dense layer as shown in Figure 3a; the layer takes in the features extracted by the temporal convolution block and maps those features to one of the three classes. A constraint was set on the layer weights such that the maximum norm value of the weights does not exceed 0.25. The dense layer was followed by a softmax to normalize the output of the model to a probability distribution over predicted output classes: NVP, UVP, and VUVP. We determine the class of the single trial by selecting the highest probability class from the output of the softmax function.

Training and validation

Perception of urgency can vary among individuals based on their previous experiences, leading to differences in EEG signals associated with different levels of urgency. At the same time, due to variations in brain structure and activity, EEG data can also exhibit subject-specific patterns. The strength of leave-one-subject-out cross-evaluation lies in its ability to provide a robust assessment of a model’s generalization performance across different subjects32. In leave-one-subject-out, the model is trained on data from all subjects except one and then tested on the held-out subject, allowing us to evaluate how well the model generalizes to new subjects. Therefore, to ensure that the model will perform well across a diverse population, we apply the leave-one-subject-out strategy for all the considered models in this work. All the models were trained for 400 epochs with a batch size of 64 and an initial learning rate of 9×10-4. During the training, we employed a learning rate scheduler to reduce the learning rate as the epoch increases such that the learning rate is reduced by half between 50 and 100 epochs and reduced to 9×10-5 beyond 100 epochs. In this model, the Adam optimizer was employed to minimize the cross-entropy loss function, which is commonly used to optimize classification models by measuring the difference between predicted probabilities and actual class labels. All experiments were conducted using Keras 2.10 with an NVIDIA A100 GPU.

Shapley values

Shapley values analysis is a popular explainability method that has garnered considerable attention in recent years and has applications in areas such as medical imaging33, genomics34, social networks35, and EEG36. This method, rooted in game theory, assigns a credit to each input feature, indicating its contribution to the model’s prediction37. To determine the Shapley value of a specific feature, a series of feature sets are generated, consisting of all possible coalitions of the N existing features, excluding the feature of interest i. The worth function, v, is used to evaluate the worth of feature i by considering coalitions with and without feature i. In the context of deep-learning, the worth function is simply the function of the model that is used for predicting an input instance to a particular class. The worth function (model’s mapping function) is evaluated with and without feature i, and the difference in the prediction for the input instance is calculated. The difference in the prediction is the marginal contribution of the feature i in prediction.

To determine the Shapley values for all features and subjects across all conditions, we utilized the SHAP package37. Following this, we calculated the average of the absolute Shapley values across subjects and conditions, and then normalized them. We report on the absolute scaled Shapley values for the three-class urgency classification model. This analysis is helpful in identifying the most influential brain regions and the duration within the input epoch in predicting the urgency level.

Time-frequency analysis

Time-frequency representation of the EEG data is calculated for the region of interest identified by the Shapley values analysis. For that, Morlet wavelet transformation was used with 3-cycle wavelet. After calculating the power of the transformed epochs, trials within the same condition were averaged for each participant. This process was followed by conversion to decibels and baseline normalization, which involved subtracting the baseline, defined between −1000 and −500 ms relative to the onset. Lastly, the power of delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–13 Hz), beta (13–30 Hz), and gamma (31–80 Hz) bands were extracted.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted to ascertain whether statistically significant differences exist in neural activation in the region of interest identified through the Shapley values analysis, thereby validating the features attended to by the model. Statistical analysis was performed on the power of the theta band over time, where the difference between the three urgency conditions is assessed at each time bin within the identified brain region. We have considered the 2000 ms of stimulation, where the resolution of each time-bin after the Morlet wavelet transformation is 19 ms. The normality of the activation for each time-bin is assessed using the Jarque–Bera test; if normality is confirmed, one-way ANOVA test is employed, otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis test is applied. The Holm–Bonferroni correction method was used to correct the p values across the three groups, and the Benjamini and Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) test was used to correct the p values across the time bins.

Furthermore, statistical analysis was conducted to verify the superior performance of the proposed model compared to the baseline models. Jarque–Bera test was used to evaluate the normality of the accuracy values obtained for each model; it was found that the distribution of the accuracy values in all cases was normal. Consequently, the paired t-test was employed. To adjust the p values across the three comparisons involving the three baseline models, the Holm-Bonferroni correction method was applied.

Results

Urgency classification

The performance of the baseline models was evaluated on the urgency-via-vibration dataset and a summary of the single-trial leave-one-subject-out classification results are listed in Table 1. All the models showed ability in discriminating the three classes apart, with accuracy values significantly above the chance level (i.e. 33.3 %). Notably, it can be observed that among the baseline models, EEGNeX achieves the highest average classification accuracy of around 81%. Furthermore, the proposed model, which incorporates an EEGNeX head followed by a TCN block, exhibited further enhancement with an average classification accuracy of 83%. It can be observed that the performance of the proposed model is significantly higher than the other three models (N=31 samples; paired t-test; Holm-Bonferroni correction).

The achieved classification accuracy ranged from approximately 95– 58%. Explainability analysis

Explainability analysis using Shapley values was employed to identify the most influential features, namely EEG channels and time-points, towards a correct classification of the trial. Figure 5 depicts the aggregated Shapley values, represented by the average absolute scaled values, for the correctly classified trials across all subjects. Shapley values are presented in a topography plot format, facilitating the comprehension of the important underlying spatio-temporal features that are used towards a correct urgency classification. Each topography plot displays the average Shapley values over a period of 500 ms interval. Higher Shapley values signify more influential features that exert a greater influence on the classification outcome. Temporally, it can be observed that the first second of the stimulation period plays a crucial role in the classification outcome. Additionally, both the central region and the prefrontal region appear to be prominently utilized by the model to achieve accurate predictions. These regions maintain their significant influence, although to a lesser extent, for the entire 2-s duration of the stimulation process.

The model relies on channels in the prefrontal and central regions and mainly uses the first second of the stimulation period for its predictions. The involvement of the central region pertains to sensory processing, whereas the involvement of the prefrontal region pertains to cognitive processing.

In a real-time urgency evaluation application where it is required to extract the urgency level from an EEG epoch, it is of interest to provide a prediction with fewer EEG channels and in a faster fashion. Thus, we conducted optimization analysis, where we show the impact of reducing the number of channels used in the prediction, retaining the most influential channels as highlighted by their average Shapley values during the 2-s stimulation period. Particularly, we examined the model’s performance while considering 60 (all), 50, 40, 30, 20, 15, 10, 8, and 5 channels, selected as rated by the Shapley values analysis (see Figure 6a). Furthermore, the impact of reducing the epoch size (the duration of the single trial) has been investigated, considering 2 (full stimulation period), 1.75, 1.5, 1.25, 1, 0.75, 0.5, and 0.25 seconds, starting the onset, and the results are reported in Figure 6b. It can be observed from Figure 6 that utilizing the 10 most prominent channels and at least the first second of the stimulation period can guarantee an acceptable performance that is above 80%.

Neural cross-validation

The model primarily focuses on the prefrontal region of the brain to predict urgency levels, with the central region also playing a significant role, as demonstrated by the Shapley value analysis. To determine the validity of these two regions, the neural activations in these areas were examined.

In Figure 7a, the time-frequency representation of the neural activation in the prefrontal region for the three urgency levels can be observed. In the NVP condition, event-related-desynchronization (ERD) is seen in the lower frequency bands, such as the theta band, but in the UVP and VUVP conditions, ERD is replaced with event-related-synchronization (ERS) that intensifies in the VUVP condition compared to the UVP condition. Additionally, the time-course of the theta power shown in Figure 7b shows a significant difference in theta power between the three conditions in the frontal region, specifically in the (’Fp1’, ’Fp2’, ’AF3’, ’AF4’, ’AFz’, ’AF7’, ’AF8’) channels, from 250-710 ms (one-way ANOVA or Kruskal-Wallis test, Holm-Bonferroni correction for groups and Benjamini and Hochberg false discovery rate correction for time bins, p < 0.01).

Discussion

The objective of this study is to introduce a generalizable and explainable deep-learning model for evaluating the perceived urgency induced by vibration stimulation on the upper body using single-trial EEG data. While neural signatures of experiences such as urgency may differ among individuals, common patterns do exist across different populations. Consequently, we have opted for the leave-one-subject-out training and cross-validation approach to identify and extract these commonalities. This method enables the model to learn generalized features that are representative of the urgency level experienced by the entire population. Moreover, the model’s validity is confirmed through a sanity check, employing Shapley values for model explainability, followed by neural analysis cross-validation.

The proposed model, equipped with a modified EEGNeX head and a TCNet block, significantly outperformed the three baseline models. This suggests that the TCNet block’s ability to extract higher-order temporal features played a crucial role in enhancing the model’s performance. Furthermore, the proposed model has lower variability in terms of performance across the subjects, as can be observed from the standard deviation values in Table 1. Achieving an average classification accuracy of 83% in a three-class problem using single-trial EEG data and a leave-one-subject-out approach confirms the model’s generalizability across population in capturing the urgency experience elicited through vibration feedback on the upper body. An interesting observation is the model’s ability to distinguish between UVP and VUVP more effectively than between NVP and UVP (see Figure 4b). This is inline with the outcome of the explainability analysis which shows that the model achieves higher performance by giving more emphasis to the prefrontal EEG channels capturing cognitive rather than sensory features. Although distinguishing between NVP and UVP may be easier from a sensory perspective, the model’s focus on cognitive features likely makes UVP and VUVP more distinguishable.

Using Shapley values, we found that the first second after the onset, which covers the period during and shortly after stimulation, is sufficient for effective evaluation. Additionally, the analysis revealed that the frontal region is the most influential in the evaluation process, followed by the somatosensory region. This is supported by the involvement of the frontal region in cognitive processes39,40 and the somatosensory region’s role in detecting somatosensory inputs from the body41,42. For a real-time urgency evaluation application, it is shown that using around 10 electrodes (mainly from the frontal region) and utilizing the first second of stimulation is sufficient to achieve an accuracy exceeding 80%.

Despite the clearer spatial prevalence of the central neural signatures, the model predominantly utilizes neural features from the frontal region. Examining the neural correlates in the frontal region revealed a statistically significant difference in the theta power, with an ERS of a proportional power to the level of urgency for the VUP and VUVP conditions. It has been shown that theta ERS in the frontal region is a strong arousal indicator43 which is generally associated with strong emotions44. Additionally, increased theta ERS over the frontal region has been reported to reflect attention-related processes45,46. As for the NVP condition, interestingly, despite the lack of stimulation, it exhibited a theta ERD correlate in the prefrontal region, while no activation was observed in the central region. This further confirms that the activity in the prefrontal region is cognitive. While prefrontal theta synchronization is generally well-studied and is known to be associated with short-term/working memory and emotion regulation47, prefrontal theta desynchronization is far less explored. Nonetheless, a few studies highlighted that the theta ERD in the prefrontal region has been linked to uncertainty48 and resting49. These psychological states are particularly relevant to the NVP condition, where subjects experience uncertainty about upcoming stimulation and are in a resting state due to the absence of stimulation.

Conclusion

In this work, we presented a deep learning model designed to classify urgency through vibration feedback on the upper body, using single-trial EEG data. Our model was shown to outperform baseline models with a mean accuracy of 83% in a three-class problem using leave-one-subject-out cross-validation. Our analysis also revealed that the model relies primarily on features extracted from channels in the prefrontal region, as supported by neural statistical analysis which showed a significant difference in theta band power among the three conditions in the prefrontal region. This is consistent with literature which associates theta ERS in the prefrontal regions with higher-order cognitive functions such as attention and emotional arousal, which are linked to the sense of urgency.

A limitation of this study is that the proposed model’s performance was tested on only one dataset (urgency-via-vibration), which may affect the generalizability of some of the findings that relates to performance, when tested on other datasets. Future work should aim to classify urgency levels while subjects are immersed in context-rich situations, including more than just vibration feedback, to confirm the model’s accuracy in predicting urgency levels in realistic scenarios. Additionally, it would be valuable to explore the model’s generalizability to cases where haptic feedback is applied to different body parts such as the arms, hands, legs, or head, depending on the application. Exposing the model to a diverse set of conditions is expected to enhance its performance and robustness against variations in the employed haptic feedback. Lastly, incorporating other sensing modalities, such as data acquired from PPG and/or GSR sensors, would probably enhance the performance of the model, given the expected physiological changes that accompany the sense of urgency.
