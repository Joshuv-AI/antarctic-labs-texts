Breaking boundaries: exploring recent advances in anticoagulation and thrombosis management: a comprehensive review
Authors: Zaheer Qureshi, Faryal Altaf, Abdur Jamil, Rimsha Siddique, Shivendra Shah
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Thromboembolic disorders globally contribute to morbidity and mortality, emphasizing adequate anticoagulation and thrombosis management. Therapeutic advances are essential in preventing complications like pulmonary embolism, stroke, and myocardial infarction. This review summarizes recent anticoagulation advances, current challenges, future directions, and novel anticoagulants and drug delivery systems on clinical outcomes.Methods:This paper assesses the effectiveness and safety of new anticoagulants through a systematic review of recent clinical trials, meta-analyses, and guideline publications. Key studies, including PACIFIC-AF, RIVER, ENAVLE, ENVISAGE-TAVI AF, and ARCADIA, were analyzed to provide a perspective on therapeutic advancements.Results:The review highlights key findings from vital clinical trials. Asundexian, in the PACIFIC-AF trial, demonstrated a 34% reduction in bleeding events compared to Apixaban. In the RIVER trial, Rivaroxaban reduced significant bleeding events by 20% compared to warfarin in patients with bioprosthetic mitral valves. In the ENAVLE trial, Edoxaban achieved a 3.7% decrease in thromboembolic events compared to warfarin without increasing significant bleeding rates. In the ENVISAGE-TAVI AF trial, edoxaban was noninferior to VKAs in preventing thromboembolic events but showed a slight increase in major bleeding events by 1.5%. Lastly, the ARCADIA trial highlighted that apixaban did not significantly reduce recurrent stroke risk compared to aspirin, with both treatments having an annualized stroke rate of 4.4%.Conclusion:Advances in anticoagulant therapies and drug delivery systems aim to enhance patients’ clinical outcomes for thromboembolic disorders. While recent trials show promising data, ongoing patient-specific responses and monitoring challenges require further research. Continuous innovation and investigation are essential to refine anticoagulation practices and tailor treatments.Keywords: anticoagulation, clinical implications, hemostasis, management, novel therapies, thrombosis

Thromboembolism is a condition in which a blood clot (thrombus) breaks off from its original site and travels through the bloodstream (as an embolus) to obstruct a blood vessel, causing tissue ischemia and organ damage1. Thromboembolic disorders are the leading cause of death and disability in high-income nations and are increasingly common in middle-income countries and low-income countries1. Arterial thromboses, known as white clots, usually develop in areas of arterial damage with high shear stress2. These clots are rich in platelets and low in fibrin. They can cause myocardial infarction and stroke2. On the other hand, venous thromboses, which contain a high amount of fibrin and red blood cells but a low amount of platelets (often referred to as red clots), are the cause of venous thromboembolism (VTE), which encompasses disorders including deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE)2,3.

An anticoagulant, commonly known as a blood thinner, is a chemical substance that prevents or reduces blood coagulation, prolonging the clotting time2,3. Anticoagulation therapy is crucial in treating certain thrombotic illnesses and avoiding the start or advancement of blood clot formation1. The global burden of thromboembolic illness is rising due to reasons such as aging populations and the increasing prevalence of risk factors like obesity, sedentary lifestyles, and type 2 diabetes mellitus1. As a result, efficient anticoagulation treatments are becoming more critical. This therapy employs medicines that disrupt the process of blood clot formation, enhancing blood circulation and decreasing the likelihood of severe consequences linked to blood clots2,3. These drugs are essential not just in emergencies but also for the ongoing care of individuals who are at risk of experiencing repeated blood clotting events. The efficacy of these medications is extensively demonstrated in decreasing the occurrence of stroke, myocardial infarction, and other severe consequences linked to thromboembolism2,3.

This review article explores the comprehensive scope of anticoagulation therapy used to prevent and treat thromboembolic diseases, emphasizing new developments and contemporary approaches that refine patient care. It will cover the pharmacological properties of anticoagulants and the latest clinical trials that evaluate their efficacy and safety profiles. In synthesizing current knowledge and new research findings, this review aims to provide a detailed, evidence-based resource for clinicians navigating the complexities of anticoagulation therapy.

Methodology

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted to identify relevant articles reporting on recent advances in anticoagulation therapy. Multiple electronic databases were systematically searched, including PubMed, Embase, Scopus, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy employed a combination of keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to anticoagulation, novel anticoagulants, and recent advancements. Search filters were applied to limit results to articles published in English. Additionally, reference lists of relevant review articles and primary studies were hand-searched to identify additional sources.

Study selection criteria

Articles were included if they provided insights into recent developments in anticoagulation therapy, including but not limited to the discovery of new anticoagulant agents, updates on clinical trial outcomes, and innovative approaches to anticoagulation management. Both experimental and observational studies, as well as review articles and expert opinions, were considered eligible for inclusion. Articles were only included if they focused on recent advancements in anticoagulation or were available in full text.

Data collection and synthesis

One reviewer performed data extraction and verified it by a second reviewer using a standardized data extraction form. The extracted data included study characteristics (e.g. author(s), publication year, and study design), intervention details (e.g. type of anticoagulant and dosage regimen), and key findings related to recent advances in anticoagulation therapy. The extracted data were synthesized narratively to provide a comprehensive overview of recent developments in the field.

Quality assessment

No formal quality assessment of included studies was conducted, as this review aimed to provide a narrative synthesis of recent advancements in anticoagulation therapy rather than evaluate the methodological quality of individual studies. However, the credibility and relevance of the included sources were carefully considered during data synthesis and interpretation.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was not required for this review, as it involved analyzing existing literature and did not involve direct interaction with human participants.

Results

Historical perspective

The initial anticoagulants, unfractionated heparin, and warfarin, were haphazardly discovered in the early 20th century. They represented a notable breakthrough in preventing and treating venous thromboembolism (VTE)4. Unfractionated heparin, obtained from liver tissue, was utilized for its anticoagulant solid characteristics. At the same time, warfarin, generated from rotten sweet clover, was subsequently chosen for its capacity to hinder vitamin K-dependent clotting elements. These findings were fundamental, influencing the initial approaches to managing VTE5.

Throughout the years, these medications have undergone refinement and optimization. Unfractionated heparin has been transformed into low molecular weight heparins (LMWH), which offer more reliable pharmacokinetics and improved safety characteristics5. Improved monitoring and dose management methods have boosted the effectiveness of Warfarin therapy by reducing its narrow therapeutic range and minimizing its interactions with food and other drugs4. Heparin’s mechanism of action is different from warfarin; it binds to the enzyme inhibitor antithrombin III (AT), causing a conformational change that results in its activation through an increase in the flexibility of its reactive site loop. The activated AT then inactivates thrombin, factor Xa, and other proteases5,6.

Recently, innovative anticoagulant medications have entirely transformed the profession. These novel medicines, which directly target critical enzymes in blood clotting, coagulation factor IIa (thrombin), and coagulation factor Xa (FXa), have various benefits compared to conventional treatments6. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), such as dabigatran, which hinders thrombin, and rivaroxaban, apixaban, and edoxaban, which hinder factor Xa, diminish the necessity for regular monitoring and have fewer dietary limitations, offering a more straightforward treatment plan for patients5,6. Advances in pharmacology and DOAC

The limitations associated with heparins and warfarin, such as the requirement for frequent dose modifications and laboratory monitoring, led to the invention of novel anticoagulant medications7. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have become widely used for patients who need anticoagulation therapy. This is because they are easy to use and have favorable pharmacokinetic properties, such as short half-lives, minimal drug-drug interactions, and no need for regular laboratory monitoring7.

These agents block certain coagulation stages, improving effectiveness and safety7,8. Additionally, they can be taken orally in predetermined doses, substantially minimizing the need for regular laboratory tests9. Currently, there are two categories of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) that have received approval for the treatment of thromboembolic disorders: factor Xa inhibitors, such as apixaban, edoxaban, and rivaroxaban, and the thrombin inhibitor, dabigatran9,10. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are a notable progression in antithrombotic therapy, offering a highly efficient and patient-centric substitute to conventional anticoagulants10.

Dabigatran etexilate was the first-factor IIa (thrombin) inhibitor to be developed, establishing the basis for a novel category of anticoagulant medications7,11. Dabigatran binds to the active site of thrombin, preventing the enzyme’s free and clot-bound forms from functioning11. Dabigatran is a prodrug, which means that it does not have an immediate therapeutic impact when it is given. Instead, it needs to undergo metabolic conversion to become active. The conversion process is facilitated by microsomal carboxylesterases, mainly located in the liver7. The bioavailability of dabigatran etexilate is relatively low, at 6%, which means that higher dosages must be given to provide a practical anticoagulant effect. The limited bioavailability of this substance must be considered when determining the dosage to ensure that sufficient quantities of the substance are present in the blood plasma7,11. This is necessary to prevent thrombin from participating in coagulation and achieve the desired therapeutic effect. A therapeutic dose of dabigatran leads to prolonged laboratory measurements such as partial thromboplastin time (PTT) or thrombin time7,11.

Apixaban is an oral anticoagulant that directly inhibits factor Xa, an enzyme that plays a crucial role in blood clotting by generating thrombin and forming clots12,13. Unlike many other anticoagulants, Apixaban’s antithrombotic impact does not rely on antithrombin. It efficiently blocks free factor Xa and factor Xa within the clot while interfering with prothrombinase activity12,13. Apixaban inhibits factor Xa, leading to a decrease in thrombin generation. Due to its high potency in activating platelets, a reduction in thrombin production indirectly reduces platelet aggregation initiated by thrombin. Nevertheless, apixaban does not directly affect platelet aggregation14.

Apixaban is characterized by its relatively high bioavailability, exceeding 50%, enabling efficient absorption and a robust anticoagulant action. After being taken by mouth, apixaban reaches its highest levels in the blood within 3–4 h. When taken many times, apixaban stays in the body for a period ranging from 10 to 14 h12,14,15. Apixaban is metabolized through the involvement of CYP3A4, an essential enzyme in the metabolism of numerous drugs. Although there is some interaction, apixaban does not have a substantial inhibitory or inducing effect on CYP enzymes, which reduces the likelihood of drug-drug interactions. This attribute is especially advantageous in patients requiring many medications, decreasing the probability of unfavorable pharmacological interactions12,14,15.

The kidneys eliminate ~25% of apixaban, while the remaining portion is broken down by CYP3A4 and additional liver routes that are not dependent on CYP14,15. Considering this method of removal, the administration of apixaban may necessitate careful administration or dosage modifications in individuals with kidney failure to avoid buildup and the subsequent heightened susceptibility to bleeding. The pharmacokinetic characteristics of apixaban, including its good bioavailability and well-balanced elimination pathways, play a significant role in its efficacy and safety as an anticoagulant treatment14.

Rivaroxaban represents a significant advancement in oral anticoagulants as it is the first inhibitor of direct factor Xa16,17. This oxazolidinone derivative exerts its anticoagulant effect by directly and reversibly attaching to the active site of factor Xa. It primarily interacts with the S1 and S4 pockets. The drug’s inhibitory impact on factor Xa is particular, with a specificity over 10 000 times higher for factor Xa than other serine proteases in the coagulation cascade16.

Rivaroxaban functions independently of cofactors, such as antithrombin, differentiating it from indirect factor Xa inhibitors18. It effectively inhibits both circulating free factor Xa and factor Xa that are already bound within the thrombus and decreases the action of the prothrombinase complex. These actions effectively delay the blood clotting process16,18.

Rivaroxaban demonstrates remarkable oral bioavailability, with the 10 mg dosage being absorbed to a high extent (80–100%), and this absorption is not influenced by food. The absorption of higher dosages, such as the 15 and 20 mg doses, seems directly proportional to the dose. Additionally, meals enhance the bioavailability of the 15 and 20 mg doses. The plasma half-life of the drug changes depending on age, ranging from 5 to 9 h in younger adults to 11–13 h in older adults. This should be considered when determining the dosage, particularly for elderly patients18,19.

Regarding metabolism and excretion, almost two-thirds of the administered dose of rivaroxaban undergoes metabolic breakdown. The hepatobiliary pathway removes 50% of this digested component, while the remaining 50% is excreted through the kidneys16,18,19. One-third of the medicine is excreted in the urine as an unmodified active drug, indicating that it is eliminated from the body without undergoing metabolism. The pharmacokinetic profile of rivaroxaban provides essential information for determining the appropriate dosage, particularly in patients with liver or kidney problems. These patients may need to have their dosage adjusted to prevent the buildup of the drug and the associated risk of bleeding16–18.

Edoxaban is a specific and highly targeted direct inhibitor of factor Xa, similar to apixaban and rivaroxaban20. This particular characteristic benefits anticoagulant treatment accuracy and reduces unintended side effects. When taken by mouth, edoxaban is quickly absorbed into the bloodstream, and food does not substantially affect how the drug is processed and its effects on the body20,21.

After being absorbed, a substantial amount of the given dose of edoxaban remains unaltered in the bloodstream. The medication undergoes metabolic processing, primarily hydrolysis, creating several metabolites21,22. In addition, a small proportion of edoxaban’s metabolites are generated by the cytochrome P450 3A (CYP3A) enzyme system. However, this does not significantly impact the drug’s effectiveness or safety profile22,23.

Although there may be differences in the highest levels of edoxaban in the blood and some effects on how the drug works, the tablet and solution forms of edoxaban are equally effectively absorbed, as shown by their consistent bioavailability. The plasma often reaches peak concentrations of edoxaban within 1–2 h after administration20.

The distribution of edoxaban into tissues outside blood vessels is marked by moderate binding to proteins, with 40–59% of the medication attaching to proteins in the plasma20,22,23. The binding rate remains constant for 12 h after delivery, ensuring a continuous level of pharmacological action during this time22. The elimination half-life of edoxaban ranges from 5.8 to 10.7 h. This applies to tablet and solution formulations and is observed for single-dose and multiple-dose delivery regimens23.

Edoxaban is eliminated from the body through both renal and fecal routes. Edoxaban is actively secreted by the kidneys, as evidenced by its renal clearance rate exceeding the glomerular filtration rate. Approximately 60% of the medication is excreted through feces, while the remaining 40% is eliminated through urine21,23. Due to the presence of two elimination pathways, it is essential to carefully examine the dosage in patients with kidney problems to avoid the buildup of drugs and the resulting higher chance of negative consequences22. Edoxaban possesses pharmacokinetic properties, making it a practical and effective choice for anticoagulation. Its predictable profile enables its utilization in diverse patient populations21. Reversal of anticoagulant medications

Vitamin K1, phylloquinone, is essential for producing vitamin K-dependent clotting components. Medication administration, whether taken by mouth or injected into a vein, is necessary to reverse the effects of blood thinners like warfarin. Administering vitamin K intravenously decreases the International Normalized Ratio (INR) after 1–2 h, reaching its peak impact around 4–6 h24.

Fresh frozen plasma (FFP) is a counteractive substance obtained from contributors’ plasma. The clotting factors are rapidly frozen to maintain integrity and should be stored at −18°C or below. FFP encompasses all coagulation factors, such as fibrinogen, protein C, and von Willebrand factor (vWF), and successfully rectifies coagulopathy when the INR exceeds 1.7. FFP is administered at a 200–250 ml volume per unit and can be given 13–48 h after being thawed. Before transfusion, it is obligatory to conduct compatibility testing for the ABO blood group, while Rh compatibility testing is not required. FFP transfusion carries the risk of virus transmission and the possibility of transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) and allergic responses24,25.

Prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs) are formulations that contain nonactivated clotting factors II, VII, IX, and X. Some preparations also include proteins C and S. Both three-factor and four-factor prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs) are available, with the three-factor form containing lower levels of factor VII. Powdered PCCs can be quickly reconstituted into a tiny volume of less than 100 ml, allowing quick administration. The reversal process with Prothrombin Complex Concentrate (PCC) is rapid, usually occurring within 10–30 min. Additionally, the International Normalized Ratio (INR) quickly decreases below 1.5. The duration of PCC’s impact ranges from 12 to 24 h, and the concurrent administration of vitamin K aids in reducing the occurrence of rebound anticoagulation24–26.

Prothrombin complex concentrates (PCCs) have the benefit of rapid initiation and a reduced amount of medication required for administration when compared to fresh frozen plasma (FFP), hence minimizing the chances of transfusion-related acute lung injury (TRALI) and excessive fluid accumulation. Nevertheless, no definitive data supports the notion that PCCs are superior to FFP in terms of clinical outcomes. Additionally, PCCs may be linked to higher expenses and a slight risk of thromboembolic events24–26.

Protamine is employed as a particular reversal agent to counteract the effects of heparin. It also has a partial reversal effect on low molecular weight heparins (LMWHs) such as enoxaparin. Nevertheless, it merely counteracts ~60% of the impact caused by LMWH25.

Idarucizumab (Praxbind) is a specific antidote for the direct thrombin inhibitor dabigatran. This monoclonal antibody fragment is highly attracted to dabigatran, effectively canceling its blood-thinning effects24,25.

Andexanet alfa, commercially known as Andexxa, is a specific antidote for factor Xa inhibitors. The FDA approved it in 2018. It functions as a decoy molecule that attaches to the inhibitors without coagulation since it lacks a membrane-binding GLA domain24,25.

The creation of these reversal medicines signifies notable progress in the safety and administration of anticoagulation therapy, offering choices to alleviate the potential for bleeding linked to anticoagulants27.

Understanding the pathogenesis of thrombosis

Most arterial thrombi originate on disrupted atherosclerotic plaques, where exposed collagen and von Willebrand factor are crucial sites for platelet adhesion and activation28. While platelets are a prominent component of arterial thrombi, they also play a significant role in the pathogenesis of venous thrombosis. Their contribution is evident in experimental models where thrombosis is markedly reduced in thrombocytopenic mice, lacks von Willebrand factor, or is treated with antiplatelet agents29,30.

In conditions of venous flow restriction, such as those caused by immobility or compression, endothelial cells lining the veins become activated30. This activation triggers the recruitment of platelets and neutrophils, leading to the formation of heterotypic aggregates. These aggregates are stabilized by releasing neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), composed of DNA and histones, creating a scaffold that captures additional platelets31. Once activated, platelets promote coagulation through two primary mechanisms: first, by providing a surface for the assembly of clotting factors that generate thrombin, and second, by releasing inorganic polyphosphates that activate the contact system, further amplifying thrombin production2,28,29.

The initiation of coagulation by tissue factor (TF) through the extrinsic pathway is central to the mechanism of thrombosis. TF binds to and activates factor VII (FVII), forming a complex that activates factor X and factor IX. Activated factor IXa, in conjunction with factor VIIIa on the surface of activated platelets, forms the intrinsic tenase complex, a more efficient activator of factor X than the extrinsic tenase complex formed by TF and FVIIa. This amplification step is critical as it enhances the generation of thrombin, a key enzyme that drives the progression of thrombosis2,3,32.

TF is exposed during the rupture of atherosclerotic plaques in arterial thrombosis, initiating the coagulation cascade. In venous thrombosis, the endothelial cells react to reduced flow or inflammatory stimuli by expressing P-selectin and other adhesion molecules, which help recruit TF-bearing leukocytes and microparticles. This process leads to the initiation of coagulation in both arterial and venous contexts30,32,33.

Thrombin, as the final effector of coagulation, plays multiple roles: it converts soluble fibrinogen into fibrin, leading to clot formation; it activates additional platelets and amplifies its generation through the activation of factors V and VIII; and it can also activate factor XI, which further activates factor IX, enhancing the intrinsic pathway of coagulation and perpetuating thrombin generation. This broad role of thrombin underscores its centrality in both arterial and venous thrombosis2,3,30.

Elevated thrombin levels can persist for months in clinical settings, particularly after acute coronary syndrome (ACS). This persistent prothrombotic state supports the use of vitamin K antagonists, like warfarin, for secondary prevention in patients who have experienced an acute myocardial infarction. Using low-dose rivaroxaban with dual-antiplatelet therapy has also effectively prevented recurrent ischemia and coronary stent thrombosis in ACS patients29.

The contact system is essential in stabilizing and propagating thrombi, functioning through intricate biochemical reactions that enhance thrombin generation, which is crucial for effective coagulation. Comprising primarily factor XII (FXII), prekallikrein, and high-molecular-weight kininogen (HK), the contact system is activated when FXII binds to negatively charged surfaces, including those presented by polyanionic compounds like heparin and inorganic polyphosphates released during cellular injury or inflammation34.

Upon activation, FXII is converted into FXIIa, which then catalyzes the conversion of prekallikrein to kallikrein. This reaction, facilitated by HK, not only sustains the activation of FXII in a positive feedback loop but also leads to further activation of FXI to FXIa33,35. HK plays a dual role, acting both as a scaffold that brings these proteins into proximity and as a cofactor enhancing their interactions. Once activated, FXIa is a potent catalyst in the intrinsic pathway, promoting FIX conversion to FIXa, which then joins with FVIIIa on the surfaces of activated platelets to form the intrinsic tenase complex. This complex is highly efficient at activating FX to FXa, culminating in robust thrombin generation36.

Thrombin, the central molecule in the clotting process, not only converts fibrinogen into fibrin to form the structural basis of a clot but also feeds back to activate FXI further, perpetuating the cycle of thrombin production. Thus, the contact system initiates coagulation and amplifies it, making it a critical component in developing both arterial and venous thrombi33,34.

In pathological conditions such as arterial or venous thrombosis, where rapid and stable clot formation is detrimental, targeting the contact system presents a promising therapeutic avenue32,33,36. Inhibiting components of this system, particularly FXII or FXI, could reduce thrombus formation without significantly impacting hemostasis, offering a safer profile than traditional anticoagulants that inhibit thrombin directly36. This selective inhibition might reduce the risk of bleeding while effectively preventing the excessive clot formation characteristic of thrombotic diseases. As such, the contact system provides a deeper understanding of the clotting cascade and opens new pathways for developing anticoagulant therapies that could better manage thrombotic risks with fewer side effects37.

Inhibition of factor XIa (FXIa)

Patients with atrial fibrillation (AF) face a heightened stroke risk, necessitating anticoagulation for those with a stroke risk of ≥2.2% per year or a CHA2DS2-VASc score of ≥2 for males and ≥3 for females. Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) are the preferred treatment due to their lower associated risks of stroke, mortality, and intracranial hemorrhage compared to warfarin. However, the bleeding risk of 2.7–3.5% per year with DOACs contributes to their underutilization38.

The bleeding risks associated with anticoagulants are tied to their specific mechanisms within the coagulation cascade. For example, targeting factor Xa (as with apixaban or rivaroxaban) inhibits the formation of pathological thrombi through the contact pathway but also diminishes clot formation via the tissue factor pathway. To mitigate this, targeting a factor predominantly affecting the contact pathway, such as factor XIa (FXIa), has emerged as a strategy to prevent pathological thrombi while maintaining hemostatic clotting capabilities37,39.

Research shows that FXIa inhibition could be a viable strategy. Patients with genetic FXIa deficiencies exhibit lower rates of venous thromboembolism (VTE) and stroke, and animal models with reduced FXIa activity display minimal thrombosis without increased bleeding risks. Clinical trials in orthopedic surgery patients have found FXIa inhibitors effective and well-tolerated, with minimal bleeding risks39.

The PACIFIC-AF trial recently evaluated the safety of asundexian, an oral FXIa inhibitor, in AF patients at risk for stroke, comparing two doses (20 mg and 50 mg) to apixaban. Asundexian was selected for its minimal renal elimination and low drug interaction profile. The trial revealed significantly lower rates of severe bleeding with asundexian compared to apixaban. Notably, bleeding rates were comparable between the two doses of asundexian, suggesting that the risk was not dose-dependent, contrasting with DOACs40.

Furthermore, a proprietary assay showed over 80% FXIa inhibition at the lowest dose of asundexian, with nearly complete inhibition at the higher dose39,40. Despite the limited duration and small number of strokes in this trial, asundexian was well-tolerated, supporting the potential of FXIa inhibition in AF patients. The OCEAN trial aimed to extend these findings by assessing the safety, efficacy, and overall clinical benefit of asundexian compared to apixaban in a more extensive, longer-term study, potentially reshaping clinical practices for managing AF. However, as the asunder lacked efficacy, the trial stopped41.

Advancements for bioprosthetic heart valves

Valve replacement is the most effective treatment for severe valvular heart disease. Whether a mechanical or bioprosthetic valve is used depends mainly on the patient’s age and clinical factors. Mechanical valves, more durable and hence recommended for younger patients, require long-term use of vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) to reduce the risk of blood clot formation and subsequent blood vessel blockage. On the other hand, bioprosthetic valves are preferred for older populations due to their lower incidence of thromboembolic problems and the fact that they do not necessitate ongoing anticoagulation42.

Nevertheless, progress in imaging technologies, such as 4D echocardiography and computed tomography, has shown that subclinical valve thrombosis is present in 10–20% of individuals who receive bioprosthetic valves within the initial year following surgery. This discovery has heightened debates on the most effective antithrombotic approaches for these individuals, especially during the crucial initial three months following implantation when the likelihood of thromboembolism reaches its highest point42,43.

Although there is less data from randomized studies, current guidelines provide cautious suggestions for addressing these patients. It is recommended to use VKAs (Vitamin K Antagonists) for the initial 3–6 months after surgical bioprosthetic valve implantation. However, for transcatheter valve replacements, antiplatelet medication is preferred over VKAs. Patients with mechanical valves are often advised against using direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) due to adverse results in trials such as REALIGN, which have shown that DOACs are less effective than VKAs. Nevertheless, the relevance of these discoveries to bioprosthetic valves remains uncertain, necessitating additional research.

The efficacy and safety of DOACs in this context have been investigated in recent research, such as the GALILEO and RIVER trials. GALILEO suggested that Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) could reduce the occurrence of valve leaflet-motion anomalies in comparison to conventional antiplatelet medication44. This indicates a possible function for DOACs in preventing subclinical thrombosis. Meanwhile, the RIVER study discovered that Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs) were just as effective as Vitamin K Antagonists (VKAs) in avoiding death, major cardiovascular events, or bleeding in patients with bioprosthetic mitral valves with atrial fibrillation45. This highlights the possibility of DOACs being a suitable substitute for traditional anticoagulants46,47.

Additionally, investigating factor XI inhibitors presents an optimistic opportunity for improving the safety of antithrombotic treatments. These medications selectively focus on the contact pathway of blood coagulation to decrease the chances of thromboembolic events while minimizing bleeding problems. Preliminary clinical trials have demonstrated that factor XI inhibitors might effectively avert thrombosis with a reduced risk of bleeding, indicating a noteworthy progression in anticoagulant therapy for individuals with heart valves48,49.

These advancements signify a shift in the approach to anticoagulation therapy, emphasizing the importance of targeting specific coagulation cascade components for more personalized and effective treatment strategies. The development and clinical evaluation of new anticoagulants, especially those targeting FXIa, are promising for improving patient outcomes in various thrombotic conditions, including those with atrial fibrillation and after bioprosthetic valve replacement.

Critical analysis of the PACIFIC-AF trial

The PACIFIC-AF trial40 evaluating asundexian against apixaban for atrial fibrillation underscores a potentially transformative approach in anticoagulation therapy, particularly regarding bleeding risks associated with stroke prevention. Despite its innovative design and scope, the trial navigates several complexities that temper its immediate clinical implications. While adequate for initial investigations, the sample size remains relatively modest for the scope of cardiovascular research, which typically demands larger cohorts to discern subtle but clinically significant differences between treatments. The small number of bleeding events, crucial for evaluating the safety of anticoagulants, further complicates robust statistical analysis. This limitation is significant because it could underpower the study, potentially overlooking rare but serious adverse effects or failing to capture an accurate measure of efficacy and safety.

Furthermore, the study’s duration and the follow-up period need to be specified, which is a critical gap given the chronic nature of atrial fibrillation and the lifelong risk management it necessitates. Long-term data would be instrumental in understanding whether the reduced bleeding risk observed with asundexian is sustained over time and how it impacts patients’ overall quality of life and survival.

Another point of consideration is the generalizability of the trial results. The participants, primarily elderly with a significant prevalence of chronic kidney disease, represent a demographic that typically presents multiple comorbidities, potentially affecting the metabolism and pharmacodynamics of the studied drugs. Therefore, results may not directly apply to younger, healthier populations or those with different comorbidity profiles.

While adverse events were similarly distributed across treatment groups, indicating good tolerability of asundexian, a detailed analysis of these events is essential. Understanding the nature, severity, and specific types of adverse events reported could provide deeper insights into the safety profile of asundexian, informing clinicians and patients about potential risks associated with its use.

The study sets a promising foundation for using FXIa inhibitors like asundexian in clinical settings; it inherently calls for more extensive phase 3 trials. These future studies should not only aim to replicate these findings in more diverse and more extensive populations but also extend the duration of observation to capture long-term outcomes. Such comprehensive data will validate asundexian’s efficacy and safety, particularly its role in thromboembolic prevention and its potential to redefine anticoagulant therapy in atrial fibrillation management.

Critical analysis of river trial

The RIVER trial’s50 results offer compelling insights into using rivaroxaban versus warfarin in patients with bioprosthetic mitral valves and atrial fibrillation, highlighting its potential as a safer alternative anticoagulant. The trial’s primary outcome demonstrated that rivaroxaban could reduce the incidence of major or clinically relevant nonmajor bleeding events compared to warfarin, suggesting a favorable bleeding risk profile. The incidence ratios for the primary endpoint indicated fewer bleeding events in the rivaroxaban group, which underscores its safety benefits in this high-risk population. Further, asundexian doses of 20 mg and 50 mg showed significant and dose-dependent inhibition of Factor XIa, supporting the therapeutic targeting of this coagulation factor to balance thrombosis prevention with bleeding risks. The safety profiles were comparable across all treatment groups, as the rates of adverse events did not differ significantly, demonstrating that rivaroxaban is well-tolerated. These findings may influence future clinical decisions, particularly favoring rivaroxaban over warfarin to reduce bleeding complications in patients with bioprosthetic mitral valves. Ultimately, this trial contributes valuable data to the anticoagulation management discussion, suggesting that rivaroxaban could be a viable and safer option for this patient group, with further studies needed to confirm these results and expand the findings to broader clinical applications.

Critical analysis of the ENAVLE trial

The ENAVLE trial45 aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of edoxaban compared to warfarin in patients immediately following surgical bioprosthetic valve implantation or valve repair. This prospective, randomized, open-label clinical trial was conducted from December 2017 to September 2019. Participants were assigned randomly to receive either edoxaban (60 mg or 30 mg once daily) or warfarin for the initial 3 months postsurgery. The primary efficacy outcome was a composite measure of death, clinical thromboembolic events, or asymptomatic intracardiac thrombosis, while the primary safety outcome focused on major bleeding incidents.

Out of 220 participants, 218 were analyzed under a modified intention-to-treat approach, with 109 individuals per treatment group. The primary efficacy outcome manifested in four patients (3.7%) in the warfarin group, while no events were reported in the edoxaban group, demonstrating a significant risk difference favoring edoxaban (−0.0367; 95% CI: –0.0720 to –0.0014; P<.001 for noninferiority). Regarding safety, major bleeding occurred in one patient (0.9%) taking warfarin compared to three patients (2.8%) on edoxaban, with a calculated risk difference of 0.0183 (95% CI: –0.0172 to 0.0539; P=.013 for noninferiority).

These results suggest that edoxaban is noninferior to warfarin in preventing thromboembolic events and shows a comparable risk profile for significant bleeding in the early postoperative period following surgical bioprosthetic valve procedures. This finding indicates that edoxaban could be a viable alternative to warfarin for early anticoagulation following bioprosthetic valve surgery, offering similar safety and efficacy.

Critical analysis of ENVISAGE-TAVI AF trial

The ENVISAGE-TAVI AF trial51 examined the efficacy and safety of edoxaban (EDX) compared to vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in patients requiring oral anticoagulation after undergoing transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) for atrial fibrillation (AF). While edoxaban demonstrated noninferiority in preventing most adverse clinical events relative to VKAs, it was associated with an increased risk of major bleeding (MB). A particular focus of this analysis was to assess clinical outcomes in patients who met dose reduction criteria (DRC) — including low body weight (≤60 kg), reduced creatinine clearance (CrCL 15–50 ml/min), or concurrent use of specific P-glycoprotein inhibitors — which typically indicate a more vulnerable patient phenotype.

The ENVISAGE-TAVI AF trial, a multicenter, open-label, randomized controlled trial, contrasted the effects of EDX against VKA in AF patients following successful TAVI. This subanalysis specifically targeted outcomes such as net adverse clinical events (NACE), intracranial hemorrhage, ischemic stroke, all-cause death, cardiovascular and noncardiovascular death, major bleeding, fatal major bleeding, and significant gastrointestinal bleeding (MGIB). These outcomes were compared among patients with and without DRC, further stratified by age (<80 years or ≥80 years) and type of oral anticoagulation used.

Patients with DRC were typically older, more frequently female, and exhibited lower baseline CrCL compared to those without DRC. These patients also experienced higher rates of NACE and all-cause mortality. Notably, there were no significant differences in rates of MB or MGIB between the two groups. However, in the non-DRC cohort, edoxaban led to higher incidences of MB and MGIB compared to VKA. Among patients under 80 years with DRC, there were significant increases in all-cause and cardiovascular death rates compared to those without DRC. In patients over 80, while overall mortality and bleeding rates did not differ significantly by DRC status, MGIB rates were higher with edoxaban in the absence of DRC. Interestingly, all-cause mortality was lower in patients over 80 with DRC when treated with edoxaban compared to VKA.

Critical analysis of arcadia trial

In this robust, multicenter, double-blind, phase 3 randomized ARCADIA clinical trial52, researchers aimed to assess the efficacy of anticoagulation versus antiplatelet therapy for secondary stroke prevention in patients with cryptogenic stroke and evidence of atrial cardiopathy but without diagnosed atrial fibrillation. The study involved 1015 participants who met specific criteria indicating atrial cardiopathy, such as an elevated P-wave terminal force on an ECG, increased serum N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide levels, or a significant left atrial diameter index on echocardiography.

Participants were randomized to receive either apixaban (at doses of 5 mg or 2.5 mg twice daily) or aspirin (81 mg once daily), with the primary efficacy outcome being the rate of recurrent stroke measured in a time-to-event analysis. Despite thorough follow-up and substantial enrollment, the trial was halted for futility after a planned interim analysis showed no significant difference in the rate of recurrent stroke between the two groups; both groups exhibited an annualized stroke rate of 4.4%.

Furthermore, while no symptomatic intracranial hemorrhages were reported among apixaban users, seven such events were reported in the aspirin group, indicating a potential safety benefit with apixaban. However, rates of other significant hemorrhages were similarly low and statistically indifferent between the groups.

This trial’s results suggest that in patients with cryptogenic stroke who exhibit markers of atrial cardiopathy but do not have atrial fibrillation, using apixaban does not reduce the risk of recurrent stroke more effectively than aspirin. This finding is crucial for clinical practice as it challenges the assumption that anticoagulants, widely used and effective in atrial fibrillation, have a similar benefit in patients with atrial cardiopathy without atrial fibrillation. These results underscore the need for further research to explore tailored stroke prevention strategies in this unique patient population. Diagnostic and monitoring tools

Vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) have a delayed onset of action, typically requiring 24–72 h to commence and around 5–7 days to achieve peak therapeutic effects. The variability in half-life between patients (20–60 h) complicates standardization, but the International Normalized Ratio (INR) helps manage this by standardizing prothrombin time (PT) measurements across different laboratories53. The INR calculation is crucial, particularly for conditions like antiphospholipid syndrome that inherently prolong PT and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT). Monitoring liver function is also recommended to check VKA metabolism and identify baseline hemostatic issues53,54.

VKAs require regular INR monitoring to maintain a therapeutic range (usually 2.0–3.0), adjusted for specific conditions such as artificial heart valves. This involves frequent checks, which can be done at home using point-of-care (POC) devices, improving patient convenience and potentially reducing healthcare costs. However, POC devices may lack accuracy for INRs over 3.054.

Unfractionated heparin (UFH) is traditionally monitored by serial aPTT measurements, which are adjusted based on a therapeutic range of 1.5–2.5 times the patient’s baseline. However, this can be unreliable due to the variability of aPTT sensitivity to factors such as liver dysfunction or lupus anticoagulant. Alternatively, antifactor Xa activity measurement offers a more stable option, though it is less commonly available in smaller settings due to higher costs and complexity55.

Low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) and fondaparinux generally require less monitoring due to more predictable pharmacokinetics. However, monitoring might still be necessary in cases of renal insufficiency using anti-Xa levels55.

Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) do not typically require routine monitoring due to their more predictable effects. However, monitoring might be necessary in cases of severe bleeding, before surgery, or when assessing drug interactions or compliance. Specific anti-Xa assays can quantify the effects of DOACs more reliably than standard PT or aPTT tests, which are not sensitive enough for accurate measurement55.

While current monitoring methods like aPTT and PT are widely used, their variability and sensitivity issues highlight the need for more standardized and reliable methods, such as anti-Xa assays, especially in clinical settings where precise anticoagulation control is crucial. Future directions

Liposomes, particularly low molecular weight heparin (LMWH)-loaded flexosomes and ethosomes, are effective for transdermal drug delivery, with flexosomes showing superior skin permeability due to their smaller size and higher deformability56. Hydrogels, including nanogels and thermoresponsive hydrogels, are used for topical and subcutaneous drug release, offering prolonged drug activity while minimizing systemic effects. Polymeric nanoparticles (PNPs), especially those using chitosan and lipid hybrids, enhance the stability and oral bioavailability of drugs like enoxaparin, overcoming challenges associated with traditional oral delivery methods57.

Solid lipid nanoparticles (SLNs) and self-nano-emulsifying drug delivery systems (SNEDDSs) are other innovative approaches that facilitate controlled and enhanced drug release56,58. SLNs improve drug oral bioavailability by ensuring slow release from a solid lipid matrix, while SNEDDSs form oil-in-water nanoemulsions that enhance mucus diffusion and drug absorption58.

Monitoring the effectiveness of these delivery systems involves various assays like chromogenic anti-Xa and activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT), though the latter can be unreliable for specific formulations. The development of these drug delivery technologies shows promising improvements in the efficacy and safety of anticoagulant therapies, demonstrating sustained release and improved pharmacokinetic profiles suitable for clinical application56. Conclusion

In recent years, the field of anticoagulation and thrombosis management has seen remarkable advancements, particularly with the development of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), which offer benefits over traditional vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in terms of fewer monitoring requirements and reduced bleeding risks. Novel drug delivery systems such as liposomes, hydrogels, and polymeric nanoparticles have further revolutionized the delivery of anticoagulants, improving efficacy, safety, and patient compliance while addressing challenges like drug stability and bioavailability. These technologies facilitate controlled and targeted drug release, significantly enhancing therapeutic outcomes.

However, challenges remain, particularly in ensuring consistent drug delivery across diverse patient populations, managing the variable patient responses to anticoagulants, and overcoming the limitations of current monitoring techniques. Potential solutions include developing more sophisticated, personalized monitoring tools and refining drug delivery platforms to enhance adaptability and effectiveness across different physiological conditions.

As thrombotic disorders remain a significant cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, advancing our understanding of anticoagulation therapy and improving technological applications is crucial. Future research should explore new therapeutic targets and agents and integrate cutting-edge technology, such as artificial intelligence, to predict treatment outcomes and enhance personalized medicine approaches. Such efforts are essential for achieving the full potential of recent advancements and significantly improving patient care in anticoagulation and thrombosis management.
