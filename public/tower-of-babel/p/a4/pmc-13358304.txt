Indicated interventions for undergraduate university students experiencing anxiety and depression in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs): a scoping review protocol
Authors: Joanie Mitchell, Claire van der Westhuizen, Katherine Sorsdahl
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDepression and anxiety are significantly elevated among undergraduate university students worldwide. Despite this, treatment rates among this population remain low. To address this gap, there is a need for not only the provision of treatment options but also for relevant scalable prevention interventions. Such programmes have become more common in high-income countries but remain limited in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs). This scoping review will identify and map key components, delivery and assessment methods and reported outcomes of indicated prevention interventions conducted with undergraduate students with symptoms of depression and/or anxiety in LMICs.Methods and analysisA comprehensive search strategy will be developed and used to search the following databases: MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, Africa-Wide, CINAHL, PsycINFO, ERIC and SocINDEX (via EBSCOhost). Studies will be included if empirical research is presented on an indicated intervention that was delivered to undergraduate students in LMICs. Additionally, studies must report on depression and/or anxiety as outcomes, be published in English, peer-reviewed and available as full-text. There will be no limits placed on publication date and all study types will be included, such as randomised controlled trials, quasi-experimental and mixed methods. Study selection will be conducted by two independent reviewers using a two-step screening process with a third reviewer consulted to resolve conflicts. Data capturing important methodological and intervention-specific aspects of the included studies will be extracted and charted onto a pre-drafted spreadsheet and subsequently summarised numerically and thematically.Ethics and disseminationAs this review does not involve primary data collection, formal ethical approval was not required; however, permission was obtained from the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Faculty of Health Sciences at the University of Cape Town. The findings will be disseminated through publication, presentations and stakeholder engagement activities. This protocol has been registered on the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/juh2z).

Background

Globally, university students are increasingly recognised as a population experiencing significantly elevated levels of mental health conditions.1 Depression and anxiety have been reported as two of the most commonly occurring conditions. This was demonstrated in a recent global umbrella review, where mild depression and anxiety were experienced by an estimated 35% and 40% of students, while 13% and 17% were severely affected by these conditions, respectively.2 Undergraduate students, in particular, experience a multitude of transitions, accompanied by risk factors on an individual, relational and university level, which heighten their vulnerability to these conditions.35 Depression and anxiety have significant implications for students, including social withdrawal and academic failure.6 Furthermore, these symptoms can develop into diagnosable conditions, with more severe clinical risk, such as increased odds of suicidal and self-harming behaviour.7 8

Despite the high prevalence of these conditions and the known adverse implications, treatment rates among this population remain low.9 Several salient barriers to seeking help have been noted, including students preferring to manage their difficulties alone, stigma, poor mental health literacy and a lack of knowledge regarding available services.10 In addition to the noted barriers, the increasing demand for mental healthcare services often exceeding the resources available to students is an important factor contributing to the observed treatment gap. To address this imbalance, there has been a shift towards not only providing treatment options to university students but also implementing scalable interventions, including a focus on the prevention of conditions such as depression and anxiety.11 12

Given the high prevalence reported among this population, a more focused approach, such as an indicated prevention intervention, is likely the most appropriate to supplement an existing treatment model. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown such interventions to be effective in reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety among students internationally.1315 Many of these interventions draw on content from various psychological approaches, such as cognitive behavioural therapy, acceptance and commitment therapy and mindfulness-based approaches.16 Additionally, interventions have been delivered successfully in different settings (eg, digital vs in person), in various formats (eg, individual or group-based) and by various types of facilitators (eg, specialist vs non-specialist mental healthcare workers).15 17 18

While there is a body of literature on indicated prevention interventions for common mental health conditions among university students, it is principally based on studies set in high-income countries. This is despite low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) accounting for 90% of the world’s adolescents and the number of students enrolling in higher education in many of these regions increasing.19 20 Supporting the mental health of students in these settings is vital, given that their future role in the workforce and subsequent economic growth in their regions may be compromised by poor mental health.21 22

The objective of this scoping review is to provide an overview of indicated prevention interventions that have been conducted with undergraduates experiencing symptoms of depression and/or anxiety in LMICs. This overview will include identifying and mapping various key components, delivery methods and reported outcomes of the relevant interventions. While there have been recent reviews focused on university students,2325 these have either solely focused on efficacy or on other mental health issues, such as burnout and stress, or been limited to interventions with a specific format and facilitation model, such as group-based and peer-facilitated.

Methodology

A scoping instead of a systematic review was deemed more appropriate for this research, as the objective is broad and is to explore and report on different elements of interventions descriptively, rather than to assess analytical differences. This scoping review protocol was developed in accordance with best practice guidelines from the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI)26 and is registered with the Open Science Framework (https://osf.io/juh2z). To conduct the proposed scoping review, the methodological framework of Arksey and O'Malley27 and expanded on by Levac et al28 will be used. This framework consists of six stages: (1) identifying the research question, (2) identifying relevant studies, (3) study selection, (4) charting the data, (5) collating, summarising and reporting the results, and (6) consultation. During certain stages of the review, guidelines from the JBI will also be applied to ensure methodological rigour.29 The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) checklist30 has been used for this protocol,30 with relevant fields completed (online supplemental appendix 1). A separate PRISMA-ScR will be included and completed for reporting in the actual scoping review.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy to identify relevant studies is being developed through an iterative, collaborative process between the authors and a research librarian from the University of Cape Town. The search strategy is broadly being guided by a three-step process outlined by the JBI.29

The first step has been undertaken and involved compiling a list of keywords and conducting trial searches of the MEDLINE database (via PubMed). Initial key terms were identified by breaking down the primary research question into searchable concepts. Alternative search terms for these concepts were then identified, aided by consulting the index terms listed under the Medical Subject Headings (MeSH). These terms were then combined, with Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) and used to conduct an initial search of the MEDLINE database (via PubMed). Following this initial search, the titles, abstracts and index terms from the retrieved studies were examined for additional relevant terms. Multiple searches were conducted using different combinations of search terms, to be able to identify the most succinct and accurate search strategy as possible. For example, initially, multiple terms were included to reflect depression and anxiety; however, these did not significantly change the number of accurate search results, likely due to the MeSH terms for ‘Depression’ and ‘Anxiety’ including a range of related index terms. The list of compiled search terms is given in table 2, and the PubMed search strategy is included under online supplemental appendix 2.

The second step in this process will be to use the identified search terms and conduct a comprehensive search across all included databases. Together with the librarian, the following relevant databases have been identified for potential inclusion, MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, and Africa-Wide Information, CINAHL, ERIC, PsycInfo and SocINDEX (via the EBSCOhost). Search strategies will be adapted for specific databases to account for differences in controlled vocabularies and truncation functions. This search is planned to take place between January and April 2026.

The third step of this searching stage will be to examine the reference lists of included studies, to ensure that all relevant literature is identified.

Stage 3: study selection

The references of the studies identified using the search strategy outlined above will first be imported into EndNote V.21 (Clarivate, Philadelphia, PA, USA)31 and thereafter into Rayyan, a web-based tool designed to assist with the review process (Rayyan Systems Inc., Cambridge, MA, USA).32 Following the removal of duplicates, the studies will be screened for inclusion using Rayyan, which records all decisions and allows for the blinding of reviewers.

This process will be conducted by two reviewers, via a two-step process, as outlined by Levac et al.28 During the first screening step, the reviewers will independently screen the titles and abstracts of the identified studies for inclusion in the review, using the outlined eligibility criteria. The two reviewers will meet at the midpoint of the process, to ensure the eligibility criteria have been understood and applied uniformly and is resulting in an acceptable level of agreement.

All studies deemed relevant in step one will be pulled through to step two, the full-text screening. This will also be conducted independently, with a mid-process meeting. If at the end of step one or two, there is disagreement between the reviewers, in terms of the inclusion or exclusion of a study, a third researcher will be consulted. Rayyan will track the rates of agreement, disagreement and number of studies that went into arbitration. This study selection process is expected to be completed within a 6-week period. The PRISMA-ScR flow diagram will be populated and included, to depict the studies identified, screened and selected.

As a scoping review aims to map available evidence, methodological quality will not be used as a basis for exclusion; however, relevant critical appraisal tools from the JBI (https://jbi.global/critical-appraisal-tools) will be used qualitatively to gain an overview of the methodological rigour of the included studies. Additionally, the Template for Intervention Description and Replication (TIDieR) checklist33 will be used to evaluate how thoroughly the intervention and its procedures were described in the relevant studies.

Stage 4: charting the data

Following the screening process, data from the included studies will be extracted and charted onto an Excel spreadsheet. Proposed categories for this spreadsheet include: (1) year of publication, (2) author(s), (3) location, (4) sample description, age range and size, (5) intervention approach/es, (6) delivery setting (in person/online), (7) delivery format (individual/group/self-help), (8) facilitators (trained professionals/paraprofessionals/peers), (9) any adaptation procedures, (10) study design, (11) intervention duration and number of contact points, (12) outcome measures, (13) efficacy results (such as effect sizes and significance values), (14) effectiveness measures, (15) feasibility outcomes and (16) acceptability outcomes. The template for this data extraction is included under online supplemental appendix 3. Following guidelines,26 28 this spreadsheet will be populated independently by the two reviewers, with consultation at certain points. Any discrepancies between the entries of the reviewers may be discussed and resolved with a third researcher. This process will be iterative and flexible, possibly including categories being added following team consultations. The completed data extraction spreadsheet will be available in the final review.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

A three-step approach outlined by Levac et al28 will be followed, involving collating and summarising the data, reporting the results and discussing the implications of the findings. The data will be collated and summarised using descriptive numerical and thematic summaries, reflecting the objective and research questions of the review. The results will be presented using tables and/or appropriate graphics and will be accompanied by narrative descriptions of the data. The findings of the review, as well as any gaps, will form part of future research with key stakeholders and decision-making processes in terms of intervention design for this population in LMICs.

Stage 6: optional consultation exercise

This stage was described as optional but recommended by Arksey and O'Malley.27 Literature detailing scoping review guidelines and reflecting on current practices reported a lack of consensus and direction on the form this consultation should take.28 34 These publications noted that the descriptions of consultation practices given in studies are generally brief and vague. Additionally, the authors emphasised that consultation should only take place when it serves to enhance the usability and impact of research. Therefore, consultation will take place in future research focused on stakeholder engagement on the development of an intervention for this population, which will draw on the findings from this review.

Ethics and dissemination

As this review did not involve primary data collection, an ethical waiver was obtained (HREC REF: 767/2025), from the Human Research Ethics Committee (HREC), Faculty of Health Sciences, University of Cape Town (email: hrec-enquiries@uct.ac.za). To maintain transparency and methodological rigour, well established scoping review guidelines will be followed.27 28 35 As stipulated by the guidelines, to minimise the risk of bias, two independent reviewers will conduct the screening and data extraction processes, and there will be team consultations throughout. Findings will be disseminated through submission to a peer-reviewed journal for publication and through research seminars and conferences. Additionally, these findings will be used as the basis of discussions with key stakeholders in terms of the development of a future intervention aimed at common mental health conditions among university students in LMICs.
