Acceptability of HPV self-sampling in three urban academic emergency departments
Authors: Sara W Heinert, Ruth Salas, Pamela Ohman-Strickland, Ronald Ikechi, Cammeo Mauntel-Medici, Anjana Maheswaran, Shaveta Khosla, Janet Lin, Racquel E Kohler
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Background

Cervical cancer is one of the most common cancers among women globally [1]. Although screening for and treating pre-cancerous lesions has reduced incidence and mortality in the US [2], disparities persist for this preventable cancer. While several factors contribute to disparities, over half of cervical cancer cases are diagnosed among women who rarely or never screen [3, 4], with lower screening rates for racial/ethnic minority [5–8], low-income [9–11], and under-insured [9–12] women. Human papillomavirus (HPV) self-sampling, where women collect their own cervicovaginal sample, performs similarly to clinician-collected screening [13], effectively increases screening among under-screened women [14], and recently received US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) approval [15]. Compared to usual care, under-screened women report that self-sampling is more convenient, less embarrassing, and less stressful [16, 17]. Unfortunately, prior studies that have mailed HPV self-sampling kits to women with insurance coverage in large, integrated health systems have shown varied results, with 15% − 62% screened [18–20]. Alternative strategies for kit distribution are needed, especially for women who do not participate in routine primary care.

Emergency departments (EDs) often function in a safety net capacity for underserved patients [21] and thus can be a novel site to increase access to cancer screening. Prevention-based interventions in the ED have the potential to reach patients with disproportionate need [22] who may be open to education and enhanced connections with the health care system, particularly as they are a captive audience during the times in-between assessment, diagnostics, and treatment [23]. Prior ED-based prevention studies, such as universal diabetes screening [24, 25] and opt-out HIV testing [26], have proven feasible and successful to reach underserved populations who would not otherwise be screened. An optimal HPV self-sampling kit distribution strategy remains unknown [18, 19, 27], but ED-based, in-person distribution of self-sampling kits has the potential to alleviate issues identified in prior trials that mailed kits to women’s homes. Before developing and testing such a program, we surveyed women eligible for cervical cancer screening in three urban EDs to determine patient acceptability and preferences for HPV self-sampling in the ED to inform an ED-based prevention intervention.

Methods

This was a cross-sectional survey study of patients seen in three EDs in 2024 and 2025.

Sites included Robert Wood Johnson University Hospital (RWJUH) in New Brunswick, New Jersey (NJ), a Level I trauma center that sees 82,000 adult patients annually; University Hospital (UH) ED in Newark, NJ, a Level I trauma center and the only public medical center in NJ that treats 85,000 adults annually; and the University of Illinois Hospital and Health Sciences System (UI Health) ED in Chicago, Illinois (IL)- a level II trauma center with 34,000 adult patients annually.

Survey design

Survey questions included demographics, primary care access, cervical cancer screening history, willingness, confidence, and comfort of HPV self-sampling in the ED and preferences for HPV self-sampling modality (adapted from Montealegre et al. [28]). Prior to answering HPV self-sampling questions, the research assistant showed the participant a self-sampling swab (Copan FLOQSwab) and its instructional brochure, and offered a brief video on how to conduct self-sampling. Participants were told that all questions and preferences were hypothetical and that they would not be conducting actual self-sampling. The survey was available in English and Spanish (see English version in supplementary file).

Survey administration

The US Preventive Services Task Force [29], American Cancer Society [30], and professional associations like the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) and Society of Gynecologic Oncology (SGO) recommend primary HPV testing for women aged 30–65 years, partly due to the strong negative predictive value and fewer colposcopies [31]. As such, we surveyed 30–65-year-old women presenting to the ED with non-critical conditions, who spoke English or Spanish, and who had not had a hysterectomy. Research assistants (RAs) recruited in the ED using convenience sampling; they approached eligible patients during their recruitment shift, informed them about the study, and assessed eligibility. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all participants in the study. The RAs then conducted the survey with interested patients. The survey was housed in REDCap (Research Electronic Data Capture), a secure, web-based software platform designed to support data capture for research studies.

Most recruitment shifts were conducted during regular business hours. Several RAs were fluent in Spanish, aiding in communication with Spanish-speaking patients. Survey participants received a $15 physical gift card, which was distributed after survey completion. This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Rutgers University (Protocol #: Pro2024000440) and University of Illinois Chicago (Protocol #: STUDY2025-0299).

Statistical analysis

We characterized the overall sample and outcome variables of willingness, confidence, comfort, and preference for using HPV self-sampling in the ED with counts and frequencies.

Differences in demographic and medical characteristics for participants across sites were examined with chi-square tests. The outcome variables had three response choices- very, somewhat, and not. For willingness, participants were asked: If you were offered this test [HPV self-sampling] right now, how willing would you be to use it? For confidence, participants were asked: If the test were available right now, how confident would you be about doing the test correctly? For comfort, participants were asked: How comfortable would you feel using the self-collection kit in one of the restrooms of the ED? We conducted multivariable analysis using proportional odds models for outcomes of willingness, confidence, and comfort with HPV self-sampling in the ED while controlling for demographics, site, and overdue status. For the preference outcome, we conducted multivariable analysis using a binary logistic model (ED-based HPV self-sampling vs. traditional screening). For multivariable models, we used type 3 p-values for overall effect of covariates. Statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Version 9.4.

Results

Demographics

Across all sites, 359 women surveyed had at least one outcome question completed and were used in analysis (Table 1). Participants were 45.1 years on average (SD = 9.7); 40% identified as Black, 15% as Hispanic, and 57% had high school diploma or less education. 40% (40%) had private insurance, 34% had Medicaid, and 19% were uninsured. 30% (30%) did not have a primary care provider (PCP) and 21% were overdue for cervical cancer screening (i.e., last Pap > 3 years or Pap/HPV co-test > 5 years).

Participant characteristics by site (N = 359)

Education level, insurance type, language, race/ethnicity, and having a primary care physician differed by study site. The NJ sites had more participants who spoke Spanish than Chicago (p < 0.0001) and higher proportions of Hispanic participants whereas Chicago had more Black participants (p < 0.0001). More Newark participants had no primary care provider (p = 0.0001) and were overdue for cervical cancer screening (p = 0.034) than the other sites.

Acceptability of HPV self-sampling in the ED

Overall, most participants (84%) were somewhat or very willing to use a HPV self-sampling kit if it was offered while in the ED, and 91% were somewhat or very confident in their ability to collect their sample correctly (Fig. 1). Most participants (76%) said they were somewhat or very comfortable using the self-sampling kit in an ED bathroom. Of the rest who were not comfortable, open-ended responses found that 38% cited lack of bathroom cleanliness, 12% worried about doing the doing the test correctly, and 9% preferred testing at home (more clean, comfortable, and private). When given the choice, 58% of women would prefer to complete HPV self-sampling and 42% preferred traditional screening by a physician or nurse.

HPV self-sampling perceptions of survey participants

When given a list of five common concerns of using the test kit, 43% of participants had no concerns, 31% had one concern, and 26% had two or more concerns. Among those who endorsed a concern, the most prevalent concerns were that the test was not done right (47%) and not knowing how to perform the test (26%). Open-ended responses found the most common additional concerns to be physical or vision limitations (n = 6) and concern about the test accuracy or validity (n = 6).

Multivariable analysis of HPV self-sampling acceptability by patient characteristics (Table 2) used proportional odds ratio models for comfort, confidence, and willingness outcomes and logistic model for preference. Willingness significantly differed by insurance status.

Compared to those with private insurance, those with Medicaid had higher odds of being more willing to use the kit (OR = 1.69; 95% CI = 0.95,3.03). Age trended towards significance with the oldest age group (60-65-year-olds) having higher odds of being more willing to use the kit (OR = 3.11; 95% CI = 1.28,7.57). There were no significant predictors of confidence to correctly use the test kit. Hospital site was highly significant for comfort, with those from Chicago having increased odds of being more comfortable doing an HPV self-sampling test (OR = 3.17; 95%.

CI = 1.69,5.94). Site was also significant for preference with those from Chicago having lower odds of preferring HPV self-sampling versus traditional screening compared to participants at New Brunswick (OR = 0.51; 95% CI = 0.28,0.94). Language was marginally significant for preference with Spanish-speaking participants having lower odds of preferring HPV self-sampling compared to English-speaking (OR = 0.45; 95% CI = 0.19,1.11). Across all outcomes, there were no significant differences for education level, race/ethnicity, having a primary care provider, or screening status. Because the proportional odds assumption was violated in some cases, we conducted sensitivity analyses using binary logistic models for comfort, confidence, and willingness. Specifically, we collapsed somewhat and not into one category and compared it to very. Findings mirrored those found in the proportional odds model (results not shown).

Discussion

Our survey showed strong patient acceptability of HPV self-sampling in the ED. Most participants were willing to use a HPV self-sampling kit if it was offered while in the ED, were confident in their ability to collect their sample correctly and were comfortable using the kit in an ED bathroom. Over half of women would prefer to complete HPV self-sampling rather than traditional screening by a physician or nurse.

We found 84% of our sample from three EDs was willing to use the kit. This finding is mirrored by other studies that have investigated patient acceptability of HPV self-sampling in the ED. For example, one study prior to FDA approval of HPV self-sampling found high willingness (87%) among ED patients, but lower comfort using self-sampling in the ED (58%) [28]. This pattern of slightly lower comfort was similar to our findings, where 76% of our participants were comfortable using the self-sampling kit in an ED bathroom. A smaller (N = 38), more recent study in one ED also found high willingness to self-sample in an ED bathroom (76%), with Hispanic participants more willing than non-Hispanic using bivariate analysis [32]. We did not observe differences by race/ethnicity, though Spanish-speaking participants were marginally less likely to prefer HPV self-sampling than English-speaking. This may be due to perceptions of changes in care quality or provider encounters [33]. Additional cultural tailoring for intervention materials may be warranted. We also found differences in participant comfort and preference for doing an HPV self-sampling test by hospital site, which suggests the collection setting is important [34]. In the ED specifically, facility-level differences in bathroom location, cleanliness, or privacy may influence uptake and take-home options may encourage participation by allowing women a more comfortable and private setting.

Importantly, we did find differences in willingness by insurance type and age. Results indicated participants with Medicaid and older participants were more willing to use the test. In contrast to self-sampling studies [16, 35] among insured populations or part of integrated health systems, our sample included women without health care connections: approximately 19% uninsured women and 30% without a PCP. More comprehensive interventions may be needed for uninsured women as they were less willing to self-sample in the ED. Others have found low traditional screening trends among younger [36] and older [37] women, which may influence willingness to self-sample. Still, these findings suggest targeted messaging to increase awareness and make self-sampling more appealing to those in their thirties are warranted.

We also found 90% of the sample was confident in using the kit correctly, and no significant demographic differences in multivariable analysis. This may be due to our participants viewing a video and written instructions before completing the survey. Although over 40% reported no concerns about using the kit, some worried about the test being done right, which is consistent with US and global trends [38]. This underscores the need for clear instructions and opportunities to provide tailored support to women before or during kit use [39, 40].

Our study had several limitations. First, our survey asked about hypothetical HPV self-sampling use, so self-sampling behaviors can only be speculated. Second, in January 2026, the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA) announced guidelines that most insurance plans must cover costs for primary HPV self-sampling starting in January 2027 [41].

Surveys were collected prior to this announcement, and providing cost information may affect differences in patient willingness going forward. Third, there was varying staffing capacity across sites for recruiting Spanish-speaking patients, which resulted in differences across sites.

Moreover, the use of convenience sampling may affect the representativeness of the sample.

Nevertheless, the distribution of the sample was generally consistent with the demographic distribution of the ED sites.

While our findings of high patient acceptability and willingness are encouraging, it is important to note that follow-up management is vital to successful screening programs for underserved populations, especially those in the ED for which patients may not have a primary care physician. Patient navigation may be particularly helpful as it reduces time from abnormal screening to diagnosis and improves patient satisfaction with care [42–44], though contextually appropriate strategies are needed [45, 46]. As such, HPV self-sampling in the ED requires workflows with strong linkage to follow-up care as a core component. Additional research is needed to ensure that women who screen HPV-positive after self-sampling outside of traditional settings are able to obtain timely follow-up care.

Our study is the first to examine willingness among the ED population using multivariable analysis. Acceptability can be complex, so by controlling for potential confounders, our results provide robust findings on which factors may affect patient acceptability. EDs across the country vary in a variety of factors, including patient population, resources, and size, highlighting the importance of future multi-site studies for HPV self-sampling to improve generalizability of findings.

Conclusions

These findings of high patient willingness, confidence, and comfort are promising, but suggest some sub-groups may benefit from additional screening messaging and instructions for self-sampling are important components to increase uptake. Facility-level differences by hospital are also key contextual factors to consider for successful implementation. Our next step is to conduct a multi-site pilot trial implementing HPV self-sampling kit distribution in EDs to improve cervical cancer screening access.
