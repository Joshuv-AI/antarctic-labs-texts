Anlotinib treatment for refractory peritumoral brain edema in brain metastases
Authors: Qian Kong, Juping Zhang, Jibo Zhang, Yawei Sun, Jun Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeritumoral brain edema (PTBE) in refractory brain metastases significantly reduces the quality of life and even threatens the lives of patients with brain metastases. Although the molecular mechanism of PTBE remains unclear, the important role of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression and angiogenesis in PTBE has been confirmed. Therefore, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) that block VEGF/VEGFR signaling and inhibit angiogenesis may have therapeutic effects.Case presentationIn this study, we report four cases of refractory peritumoral brain edema in brain metastases treated with anlotinib, a novel small-molecule multi-target TKI. All patients were diagnosed through core needle biopsy, with pathological results indicating non-small cell lung cancer, nasal melanoma, skin cancer, and breast cancer, respectively. They received standard multiline treatment during which multiple brain metastases occurred. Following chemotherapy resistance, anlotinib was used to treat the tumors and control refractory brain edema. After two cycles of treatment, brain edema and related symptoms improved, and anlotinib monotherapy was continued until disease progression. The main adverse reactions observed during treatment were hypertension and fatigue, which were tolerable.ConclusionIn this small case series, anlotinib was associated with marked radiological reduction of peritumoral brain edema and symptom relief in patients with refractory brain metastases, suggesting it may be a potential therapeutic option that warrants further investigation in larger studies.Keywords: Brain metastases, Peritumoral brain edema, Anlotinib, Targeted antiangiogenetic therapy, Case report

Introduction

Malignant tumors have become a prominent threat to human health. As a common neurological complication of malignancy, brain metastasis (BM) occurs in approximately 10–40% of malignant tumors [1], and about 90% of BM patients suffer from peritumoral brain edema (PTBE) [2]. The formation of PTBE may cause or aggravate intracranial hypertension, cerebral hypoxia, decreased cerebral blood flow, neurological dysfunction, herniation, and even death [3]. PTBE not only affects the quality of life but also is life-threatening. Corticosteroids and dehydration are the conventional treatments for PTBE. However, corticosteroids often provide only temporary clinical benefits and are usually associated with numerous adverse effects [4], while dehydration may be accompanied by water–electrolyte disturbance and the “rebound phenomenon [5]. In addition, some refractory PTBE patients are insensitive to these treatments. In clinical practice, peritumoral brain edema is generally considered “refractory” when symptoms and radiological edema do not improve or even worsen despite adequate courses of corticosteroids and, where used, dehydration therapy (e.g., mannitol or hypertonic saline), and when MRI indicates persistent or progressive vasogenic edema. It should also be noted that dehydration therapy is not uniformly adopted worldwide as a standard strategy for PTBE; its use in our cases reflects local practice patterns and resource availability. Relevant studies suggest that PTBE and radiation-induced cerebral edema share similar pathological mechanisms, falling under the vasogenic edema classification [6, 7]. Increased expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) is a major factor contributing to PTBE [8]. Accumulated evidence has indicated the efficacy of VEGF/VEGFR pathway inhibitors, including bevacizumab, in treating refractory PTBE [9, 10]. In view of the high cost of bevacizumab, there is an urgent need to develop novel VEGF inhibitors for the treatment of PTBE.

Anlotinib is a novel small-molecule multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor independently developed in China. It potently inhibits VEGFR (VEGFR-1, VEGFR-2, and VEGFR-3), PDGFR (PDGFR-α and PDGFR-β), FGFR (FGFR-1, FGFR-2, and FGFR-3), and c-kit, exerting its effects on anti-tumor angiogenesis and tumor growth inhibition [5, 11]. Multiple clinical trials have confirmed anlotinib as an effective treatment option for malignancies such as non-small cell lung cancer, soft tissue sarcoma, and small cell lung cancer after the failure of chemotherapy [12–14]. Recently, reports have highlighted the high efficacy of anlotinib in the management of intracranial lesions in non-small cell lung cancer with brain metastases [15]. However, the effect of anlotinib on refractory PTBE has not been reported. In this paper, we report four cases of refractory PTBE in brain metastases treated with anlotinib. Details of four cases and those found in the literature are described.

Case selection

We retrospectively identified four patients with brain metastases and refractory peritumoral brain edema (PTBE) who were treated with anlotinib at Xingtai People’s Hospital between 2015 and 2019. Refractory PTBE was defined as persistent or progressive edema and/or neurological symptoms despite adequate courses of corticosteroid therapy and, where used, dehydration treatment with mannitol or hypertonic saline, together with MRI evidence of persistent or progressive vasogenic edema. These four patients had complete clinical and radiological data suitable for detailed description and are presented in this case series.

Case presentations

Case 1

A 54-year-old female non-smoker presented to Xingtai People’s Hospital in March 2016 with a “2-month history of a left facial tumor.” Computed tomography (CT) scans of the head, chest, and abdomen revealed a space-occupying lesion in the left masseter muscle, a space-occupying lesion in the lower lobe of the left lung, and multiple nodules in both lungs. Biopsy confirmed lung adenocarcinoma. The diagnosis was stage IV left lung adenocarcinoma with metastasis to the left masseter muscle (T4N0M1). From April 2016, the patient received first-line anti-tumor therapy with docetaxel, carboplatin, and bevacizumab for a total of 6 cycles. Starting from August 2016, bevacizumab maintenance therapy was administered for 8 cycles, with a best response evaluation of partial remission (PR). In January 2017, a chest CT showed an increase in the size of the lesion in the lower lobe of the left lung, indicating disease progression (PD). From February 2017, second-line treatment with docetaxel and bevacizumab was initiated for 2 cycles. In April 2017, a chest CT revealed cavities within the multiple nodular lesions in the lower lobe of the left lung and both lungs, with a response evaluation of PR. Due to intolerance caused by severe myelosuppression, the patient was switched to bevacizumab monotherapy for 3 cycles without chemotherapy from April 2017. In June 2017, cranial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) showed multiple abnormal signals in the brain, suggestive of metastatic tumors, with a response evaluation of PD. Genetic testing indicated a positive mutation in the second exon of EGFR, while mutations in anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) and KRAS were negative. In July 2017, the patient was switched to systemic therapy with gefitinib and bevacizumab for 6 cycles, achieving a stable disease (SD) status. In November 2017, lung CT and brain MRI scans indicated an increase in the size of both the left lung mass and brain metastases, with a response evaluation of PD. From November 2017, fourth-line treatment with pemetrexed (PEM), carboplatin, and bevacizumab was initiated for 2 cycles. In January 2018, the response evaluation was SD. Maintenance therapy with pemetrexed and recombinant human endostatin was then administered for 2 cycles starting from January 2018. In March 2018, chest CT and cranial MRI scans showed an increase in the size of both the lower lobe of the left lung and intracranial metastases, indicating PD. In April 2018, fifth-line treatment with albumin-bound paclitaxel and recombinant human endostatin was given for 1 cycle, followed by maintenance therapy with recombinant human endostatin for 1 cycle. In June 2018, the patient experienced worsened dizziness and headache. She received dehydration therapy with mannitol combined with systemic dexamethasone according to institutional practice for intracranial pressure control, but her symptoms persisted and there was no radiological improvement, fulfilling the definition of refractory PTBE. Cranial MRI showed progression of intracranial lesions, with a response evaluation of PD (Fig. 1A–C). From June 2018, the patient was administered oral anlotinib once daily on days 1–14 of a 21-day cycle, and symptoms of intracranial hypertension were relieved after 2 days. Two weeks later, cranial MRI showed a reduction in the size of multiple brain metastases and significant improvement in edema (Fig. 1D–F). Chest CT indicated stable lung lesions. The patient developed grade II fatigue and grade I gastrointestinal reactions, which were managed with symptomatic treatment and close monitoring without serious complications. In late September 2018, a cranial MRI review showed an increase in the size of intracranial metastases, with no progression of intracranial lesions lasting for more than 3 months. In November 2018, whole-serum genetic testing revealed mutations in EGFR exon 21 and TP53 intron 8; all other mutations were negative. From November 2018, anti-tumor therapy with anlotinib and afatinib was initiated. In May 2019, the patient died due to multiple organ failure.

Case 2

A 63-year-old female nonsmoker underwent nasal mass resection at Hebei Eye Hospital in March 2015 due to “nasal bleeding.” Postoperative pathological examination revealed malignant melanoma of the right nasal cavity. In May 2015, she was subjected to four cycles of temozolomide combined with cisplatin and recombinant human endostatin treatment. In June 2015, the patient received concurrent local radiotherapy for the nasal cavity. In August 2016, enlarged lymph nodes were detected in her right neck, and the treatment response was evaluated as PD. She then received one cycle of outpatient therapy with paclitaxel plus bevacizumab. However, the treatment was suspended due to intolerance to adverse reactions. In August 2018, the patient presented with symptoms of dizziness, headache, nausea, and vomiting. Brain MRI revealed multiple space-occupying lesions (Fig. 2A–C). CT scans of the chest and abdomen showed multiple space-occupying lesions in the spleen and bilateral adrenals. A bone scan indicated multiple bone metastases, leading to a diagnosis of stage IV malignant melanoma. Dehydration therapy with mannitol and systemic dexamethasone, administered according to institutional protocol, failed to effectively reduce intracranial pressure or improve her symptoms, indicating refractory PTBE. In August 2018, the patient was administered oral anlotinib once daily on days 1–14 of a 21-day cycle for two cycles. After three days, the symptoms of intracranial hypertension were alleviated, and the tolerance was good. In September 2018, a follow-up brain MRI and abdominal CT were performed, and the treatment response was evaluated as SD (Fig. 2D–F). No dose reductions were required, and adverse events were managed conservatively without serious complications. The patient died in January 2019.

Case 3

A 37-year-old female with no history of smoking underwent a radical mastectomy for left breast cancer in December 2013. Postoperative pathological examination revealed invasive ductal carcinoma of the breast, leading to a diagnosis of stage IIA left breast cancer (pT2N0M0). Immunohistochemistry was performed at the time of surgery; however, complete estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) staining results were not available in the archived medical records, and the receptor status of the tumor could not be reliably retrieved.

In March 2017, the patient was admitted to our hospital due to “dizziness and headache for one month.” Chest CT indicated lung metastasis, while brain MRI revealed a metastatic lesion in the right parietal lobe. The patient was diagnosed with stage IV breast cancer with lung and brain metastases. Starting from March 2018, she received first-line systemic treatment with capecitabine and bevacizumab for a total of four cycles. During this period, her symptoms of intracranial hypertension were alleviated.

However, in June 2018, the patient again presented with dizziness and headache, and a brain MRI suggested enlargement of the intracranial lesion accompanied by marked edema (Fig. 3A–C).

A–C July 2018: brain MRI (including T1-weighted post-contrast and T2-weighted FLAIR sequences) demonstrates a metastatic lesion in the right parietal lobe with surrounding vasogenic edema. D–F August 2018, after one cycle of oral anlotinib: follow-up MRI shows a reduction in the size of the parietal metastasis and improvement in peritumoral edema

A further course of dehydration therapy with mannitol and systemic dexamethasone was administered according to institutional practice, but there was no sustained clinical or radiological improvement, consistent with refractory PTBE. Removal of hydration therapy for reducing intracranial pressure was ineffective. Oral administration of anlotinib was initiated in July 2018 once daily on days 1–14 of a 21-day cycle. A follow-up brain MRI in August 2018 showed a reduction in the size of the space-occupying lesion in the right parietal lobe compared to the previous scan (Fig. 3D–F), and the symptoms of dizziness and headache disappeared. The patient tolerated the treatment well. The patient developed grade I thrombocytopenia, which was monitored with regular blood counts and managed conservatively without treatment interruption or dose reduction; platelet counts gradually returned to baseline. Brain MRI and chest and abdominal CT scans in February 2019 indicated progression of intracranial lesions and the emergence of new mediastinal lymph node enlargement. The efficacy evaluation was PD. The patient passed away in July 2019, with a progression-free survival period exceeding seven months.

Case 4

A 65-year-old male nonsmoker presented with multiple enlarged lymph nodes near the iliac vessels in the right groin area, detected by ultrasonography of the right lower extremity in December 2014. In March 2015, a right groin lymph node dissection was performed. Postoperative pathological examination indicated a malignant lymph node tumor, accompanied by skin or appendage metastasis of low to moderate malignancy. In April 2015, a skin mass resection was carried out on the lateral side of the right thigh, and pathological examination revealed squamous cell carcinoma. Radiotherapy was administered to the lymph node drainage area in the same month. However, regular follow-up or subsequent treatment was not conducted. In December 2017, a CT scan at our hospital showed multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the retroperitoneal space and near the iliac vessels. Other examinations did not suggest recurrence or metastasis. The patient was diagnosed with stage IV skin cancer, multiple lymph node metastases, and squamous cell carcinoma. First-line chemotherapy with docetaxel and cisplatin was administered for a total of six cycles, during which the efficacy was evaluated as SD. In November 2018, the patient presented with dizziness, headache, accompanied by decreased vision and diplopia. Dehydration treatment with mannitol and systemic dexamethasone to reduce intracranial pressure had a poor effect on both symptoms and MRI findings, indicating refractory PTBE. Chest and abdominal CT scans revealed enlarged lymph nodes. Brain MRI suggested multiple ring-enhancing lesions in the brain, indicating metastatic tumors (Fig. 4A–C). The efficacy was evaluated as PD. The diagnosis was changed to stage IV skin cancer, multiple lymph node metastases, brain metastases, and squamous cell carcinoma. The patient refused chemotherapy and was administered oral anlotinib once daily on days 1–14 of a 21-day cycle since November 2018. Symptoms were relieved after three days. A follow-up brain MRI after two cycles showed that multiple metastatic tumors in the brain were smaller than before (Fig. 4D–F). Chest and abdominal CT scans indicated stable lesions, and the efficacy was evaluated as SD. The patient developed grade I hypertension and fatigue, which were managed with oral antihypertensive therapy and rest; symptoms resolved within approximately one week, and anlotinib was continued at the same dose. Currently, the patient continues to take anlotinib orally, maintains a stable condition, and undergoes regular follow-up.

Discussion

Based on etiology, cerebral edema can be classified into four categories: cytotoxic cerebral edema, vasogenic cerebral edema, capillary permeability cerebral edema, and hydrocephalic cerebral edema [16]. PTBE associated with intracranial malignant tumors is mostly vasogenic cerebral edema, commonly seen in metastatic tumors, gliomas, and meningiomas [6]. Its main manifestation is the retention of water and electrolytes in the brain parenchyma. Peritumoral brain edema can cause dizziness, headache, lethargy, and coma, reducing patients’ quality of life, affecting the implementation of radiotherapy plans, promoting tumor cell growth, and increasing the possibility of further invasion of host cells [17]. Therefore, controlling peritumoral brain edema and its complications has profound clinical significance.

Currently, the control and treatment methods for peritumoral brain edema mainly include medication, surgery, radiotherapy, and hyperbaric oxygen therapy [4]. Surgery is the most direct method for treating peritumoral brain edema, and removing the primary lesion is beneficial for reducing tumor load, rebuilding the blood-brain barrier (BBB), and promoting the absorption of edema [6]. However, surgical trauma can be excessive, and some patients with multiple brain metastases have no surgical indication. Radiotherapy is one of the most important treatment methods for brain metastases, which can reduce edema [5]. Nevertheless, radiation-induced cerebral edema occurs early in radiotherapy, which can aggravate edema and limit its application to patients with severe peritumoral brain edema [5]. Theoretically, hyperbaric oxygen can promote vasoconstriction, effectively reduce cerebral blood flow, facilitate the remodeling of glial cells and vascular endothelial cells, and alleviate intracranial vasogenic edema [18, 19], but the actual effect is not significant.

Drug therapies, such as glucocorticoids and mannitol, are conventional treatments for cerebral hernia crisis and PTBE. Glucocorticoid treatment of PTBE can improve the stability of the cell membrane, repair the BBB, reduce the permeability of the BBB, and thus reduce exudation [20]. Mannitol has been used in the treatment of various types of cerebral edema for many years, and it has a significant effect in cerebral hernia crisis and emergency dehydration rescue [21]. However, its long-term use results in hyponatremia, decreased blood pressure, and renal impairment. Recent studies have found that hypertonic saline can reduce cerebral edema [22]. Hypertonic saline can improve microcirculation by increasing intravascular osmotic pressure to absorb water between brain tissues, increasing mean arterial pressure to increase cerebral perfusion [23]. Moreover, hypertonic saline has no obvious side effects, but the effect is still inferior to mannitol and glucocorticoids.

Recent work has also highlighted important uncertainties and risks associated with corticosteroid use in neuro-oncology. Wasilewski et al. reported substantial variation in perioperative dexamethasone dosing for patients undergoing brain metastasis resection and demonstrated that cumulative exposure was associated with overall survival, progression-free survival, and surgical site infection risk [24]. Martinez and Sabatier further emphasized the potential adverse metabolic and immunologic consequences of prolonged corticosteroid therapy in oncology and called for more rational, steroid-sparing strategies when feasible [25]. Together, these data underscore both the clinical importance and the limitations of steroid-based management of brain edema and support exploration of alternative or adjunctive approaches.

Currently, although the molecular mechanism of peritumoral brain edema has not been fully elucidated, it has been confirmed that vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and aquaporin-4 (AQP4) are involved in its formation [26]. Aquaporins are highly selective membrane channel proteins responsible for the transport of water molecules. As a bidirectional water channel protein located on the cell membrane, AQP4 is closely related to the opening and closing of water channels. AQP4 can promote the extravasation of water and electrolytes by increasing the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, leading to the occurrence of vasogenic brain edema [27]. However, although many compounds have been found to inhibit the expression of AQP4, they have not yet been confirmed in clinical studies [28]. VEGF is a secreted glycoprotein that can bind to VEGF receptors in solid tumors, stimulating tumor cell proliferation. Studies have found a positive correlation between VEGF and AQP4, suggesting that they may work together in the formation of peritumoral brain edema [29].

Furthermore, VEGF specifically targets vascular endothelial cells, promoting neovascularization, disrupting the normal function of the blood-brain barrier, and increasing the extravasation of water, electrolytes, and other substances, thereby facilitating the formation of edema. Therefore, VEGF/VEGFR-targeted anti-angiogenic drugs can reduce vascular permeability and alleviate tumor edema by inhibiting the VEGF pathway and normalizing tumor vasculature [8]. This inhibitory effect is accompanied by the reconstruction of the blood-brain barrier. Previous studies have demonstrated the significant efficacy of the anti-VEGF drug bevacizumab in treating severe cerebral edema associated with radiation-induced brain necrosis [30–32]. PTBE in brain metastases shares similar pathological mechanisms with radiation-induced cerebral edema, also classified as vasogenic edema. Hence, bevacizumab has the potential to be an effective treatment for peritumoral edema. Currently, there is limited research on the use of bevacizumab for the treatment of peritumoral edema, and further studies are needed to determine the appropriate dosage and treatment regimens. In this context, our observations with anlotinib add to the emerging evidence that targeting VEGF-driven angiogenesis may contribute to edema control in selected patients with brain metastases.

As a novel small-molecule, multi-target tyrosine kinase inhibitor, anlotinib exhibits potent inhibitory effects on VEGFR, PDGFR, FGFR, and c-kit, playing a role in anti-tumor angiogenesis and tumor growth suppression. Thus, from a mechanistic perspective, anlotinib may have advantages in reducing peritumoral edema of brain metastases via its VEGF/VEGFR and broader multi-target anti-angiogenic effects. Furthermore, due to its low 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50), favorable safety profile, and simple administration method, patient tolerance and compliance with anlotinib are considered to be superior to other similar drugs [33]. In this study, we clinically observed four patients with different types of brain metastases accompanied by peritumoral edema. Despite multiple lines of anti-tumor therapy and symptomatic treatment for dehydration and intracranial pressure reduction, there was no improvement in intracranial edema and hypertension before anlotinib initiation. After the introduction of anlotinib, all four patients experienced radiological reduction of PTBE and relief of symptoms of intracranial hypertension. In this small series, progression-free survival for individual patients reached up to approximately 10 months, suggesting a potentially durable benefit in some cases; however, these findings must be interpreted with caution, given the limited sample size and lack of a control group. The adverse reactions observed, including hypertension, fatigue, gastrointestinal symptoms, and thrombocytopenia, were mild to moderate in severity and manageable with standard supportive measures, and the patients’ quality of life appeared to improve during periods of edema control.

This report has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective case series with a very small sample size and no control group, which precludes any firm conclusions regarding causality or comparative efficacy. Second, we did not perform standardized quantitative volumetric measurements of peritumoral edema; radiological assessment was based on conventional MRI sequences and qualitative expert interpretation. Third, our cohort originated from a single center with local practice patterns (including the use of dehydration therapy), which may limit the generalizability of these observations. Finally, potential selection bias cannot be excluded, as only patients with sufficient clinical and imaging data were included. These limitations underscore the need for prospective, controlled studies to better define the role of anlotinib in the management of refractory PTBE.

Conclusion

In these four cases, anlotinib was associated with substantial clinical and radiological improvement of peritumoral brain edema in patients with brain metastases who had failed conventional therapies. While these observations suggest that anlotinib may be a promising option for refractory PTBE, larger prospective studies are required to confirm its efficacy and better characterize its safety profile.
