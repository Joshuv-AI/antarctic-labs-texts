A morphological indicator for aortic dissection: fitting circle of the thoracic aorta
Authors: Hongji Pu, Tao Peng, Zhijue Xu, Qi Sun, Zixin Wang, Hui Ma, Shu Fang, Yang Yang, Jie Wu, Ruihua Wang, Peng Qiu, Jinhua Zhou, Xinwu Lu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aims to identify a morphological indicator of aortic dissection (AD) based on the geometrical characteristics of the thoracic aorta.MethodsWe evaluated computed tomographic angiograms of 63 samples with AD (22 with type A AD, 41 with type B AD) and 71 healthy samples. Via centerline extraction and spatial transformation, the spatial entanglement of the aorta was minimized, and the expanded 2D aortic morphology was obtained. The 2D morphology of the thoracic aorta was fit to a circle. The applicability of the fitting circle method for identifying aortic dissection was verified by multivariable logistic regression analysis.ResultsVia the 3D coordinate transformation algorithm, the optimal aortic view was obtained. On this view, the geometrical characteristics of the thoracic aortas of the healthy controls were similar to a portion of a circle (sum of residuals: 3502.45 ± 2566.71, variance: 86.23 ± 56.60), while that of AD samples had poorer similarity to the circle (sum of residuals: 5404.78 ± 3891.69, variance: 129.90 ± 90.09). This difference was significant (p < 0.001). A logistic regression model showed that increased deformation of the thoracic aorta was a significant indicator of aortic dissection (odds ratio: 1.35, p = 0.034).ConclusionsThe morphology of the healthy thoracic aorta could be fit to a circle, while that of the dissected aorta had poorer similarity to the circle. The statistics of the circle are an effective indicator of aortic deformation in AD.Trial registrationThis study is registered in the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2000029219).Keywords: Aortic dissection, Computed tomographic angiography, Geometrical analysis, Diagnostic imaging, Algorithm

Background

Aortic dissection (AD) is one of the most common fatal aortic diseases, responsible for the deaths of approximately 30% of patients with type A AD and 10% of patients with type B AD before they can even arrive to the hospital [1, 2]. It is important to find a way to visually identify dissected aortas rapidly and quantifiably [3–5].

The pathological changes in AD can occur not only near the proximal tear but also throughout the entire aorta. The thoracic aorta is responsible for transporting blood and absorbing the ejection kinetic energy of the heart [6, 7]. The main reason for aortic dissection is an unbalanced transformation from the kinetic energy of blood flow to the elastic potential energy of the aortic wall. This imbalance leads to tear out of the aortic intima [8, 9]. According to previous studies, abnormal thoracic aorta shapes correlated with the abnormality of the aortic wall structure and hemodynamic stress [8–10]. Whether the biological cause of AD is an intima tear or media necrosis, macroscopic deformation of the aorta can be observed by imaging [11, 12].

In addition to the diameter of the aorta, other comprehensive imaging-based indexes are required to describe the risk of AD. Although aortic diameter greater than 5.5 cm is a recognized risk factor for AD [1], more than 60% of type A ADs and more than 80% of type B ADs occur in patients with normal-diameter aortas according to the IRAD global data [13, 14]. Recently, the length of the thoracic aorta was considered another morphological risk factor for thoracic AD [15, 16]. However, this indicator alone is also unable to describe the overall shape of the thoracic aorta [17]. According to previous studies, aortic tortuosity has been confirmed to be related to aortic dissection onset, which may be a manifestation of abnormal aortic histology. Additionally, it can induce abnormal hemodynamics and affect the aorta [18]. Sharper angulation and higher tortuosity of the aortic arch are considered geometric factors associated with aortic dissection [19, 20]. Our previous study confirmed that type B AD is related to the deformation of the arch and the proximal descending aorta. The deformation related to the dissection tends to be wider and inclined, and the dissected aorta appears slumped overall, and angulated and stiff locally [21]. Therefore, the tortuosity and slumpedness of the thoracic aorta may be indexes for AD, and it is desirable to find a method to quantify differences between dissected and healthy thoracic aortas.

To quantitatively describe the characteristics of the aorta, computed tomography angiography (CTA) images need to be transformed using methods including three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction and centerline extraction of the aorta. In our previous study, a 3D rotation projection modeling method was established to determine the optimal projection plane [3]. On this optimal projection plane, the morphological features of the thoracic aorta were extracted. This algorithm reduced the complexity of 3D morphological analysis and allowed the use of reproducible and quantificational parameters to describe the centerline extracted from CTA instead of subjective judgment.

Based on the previous works, an additional algorithm is needed to describe healthy and dissected aortas by quantitatively describing and identifying the characteristics of the aorta in AD. The morphology of the thoracic aorta was described by the difference between the aortic centerline and a fitted circle. The correlation between the fitted circle and AD was then verified using multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Methods

Figure 1 demonstrates the principal steps of the procedure in the study. Three-dimensional reconstruction of the aorta was performed with CTA data. The lumen centerline of the reconstructed aorta and the corresponding coordinates were extracted. The optimal aortic view was obtained via the 3D coordinate transformation algorithm, and the two-dimensional (2D) morphology was subjected to circle fitting. The applicability of the fitting circle method for the identification of AD was verified by multivariable logistic regression analysis.

Flow chart of the study

Population

This study is a retrospective multicenter cross-sectional study. Imaging data were retrospectively retrieved from the medical records of patients with continuous Stanford type A or B aortic dissection who underwent chest CTA examination in the two centers from January 2017 to December 2018 and were included in the dissection group. The exclusion criteria for the dissection group were as follows: (1) connective tissue disease (Marfan’s disease, Loeys‒Dietz syndrome or Ehlers‒Danlos syndrome, etc.); (2) iatrogenic dissection; (3) other aortic diseases, such as aneurysms; (4) previous aortic surgery; (5) a previous cardiothoracic disease or cardiothoracic surgery; (6) diseases that might cause distortion of the thoracic aortic morphology (pulmonary nodules with a diameter > 3 cm, mediastinal masses or lymph nodes with a diameter > 1 cm, pneumothorax, pulmonary bullae with a diameter > 3 cm, history of thoracic and mediastinal surgery, etc.); and (7) diseases that might cause distortion of the shape of the thoracic wall (scoliosis, barrel chest, pectus carinatum, history of spinal surgery, etc.) [21].

Imaging data were retrospectively retrieved from the continuous medical records of patients with healthy aortas who underwent chest CTA examination in four centers from April 2018 to December 2018 and included in the healthy control group. The exclusion criteria for the healthy control group were as follows: (1) existing or previous aortic diseases (aortic dissection, aneurysms, coarctation of the aorta, etc.); (2) a previous cardiothoracic disease or cardiothoracic surgery; (3) diseases that might cause distortion of the thoracic aortic morphology (pulmonary nodules with a diameter > 3 cm, mediastinal masses or lymph nodes with a diameter > 1 cm, pneumothorax, pulmonary bullae with a diameter > 3 cm, history of thoracic and mediastinal surgery, etc.); and (4) diseases that might cause distortion of the shape of the thoracic wall (scoliosis, barrel chest, pectus carinatum, history of spinal surgery, etc.)

Three-dimensional reconstruction of the aorta

Imaging

A Toshiba 64-slice CT scanner (Toshiba Medical Systems (China), Beijing, China) was used with a scanning range from the entrance of the thorax to the lower boundary of the lung. Nonenhanced scanning was performed first, followed by contrast-enhanced scanning. Files were saved in DICOM format and anonymized. The raw DICOM format CTA data were imported into reconstruction software.

Three-dimensional reconstruction of aorta

Two-dimensional cross-sectional images were adjusted to allow segmentation of the thoracic aorta in the segment module. The aorta was subjected to initial threshold segmentation according to dynamic area growth. Then, the calculate 3D function was used to reconstruct the processed mask in 3D. Afterward, the 3D model was smoothed to reduce spurious noise and maintain a biological shape. For the dissected aorta, dynamic area growth was used for the true and false lumen regions separately. After segmentation, the masks of the two parts were combined into one mask using the Boolean function under Boolean Operation. To facilitate the subsequent centerline extraction, the mask of the rupture site was repaired using Editing Masks and Multiple Slice to create a complete lumen.

Aortic centerline extraction and centerline coordinate construction

The centerline was extracted semiautomatically according to the reconstructed 3D thoracic aorta model using Mimics software. The distance between the control points was 3 mm, and the coordinates of the thoracic aortic centerline were kept between 100 and 150. The entire thoracic aorta was divided into 6 segments according to Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) segmentation standards (Zone 0: ascending aorta, including the innominate, Zone 1: from the distal edge of the innominate to the distal edge of the left common carotid, Zone 2: from the distal edge of the left common carotid to the distal edge of the left subclavian, Zone 3: first 2 cm distal to the left subclavian, Zone 4: from Zone 3 to the mid-descending thoracic aorta, Zone 5: mid-descending aorta to the proximal edge of the celiac artery) [22]. The coordinates from Zone 0 to Zone 4 were incorporated into the following calculations. The coordinates of the dissected segments of the aorta were labeled 1, and the coordinates of the undissected segments were labeled 0.

Determination of the optimal projection plane and circle fitting

As described in our previous study [3], the optimal projection plane can be determined according to the 3D coordinate transformation algorithm using MATLAB (2022, Mathworks, Inc.). The sum of absolute residuals (SAR) was used as the evaluation parameter, the XOY plane was used as the projection plane, and the two corresponding angles of rotation can be obtained. The best morphology of the corresponding projection plane can be obtained by rotating the original aortic centerline by the angle of rotation. The resulting 2D aortic morphology was segmented according to the six SVS classification criteria [22], and circle fitting was performed.

The improved algorithm of least-squares fitting using “area difference” was used to minimize the sum of the squares of errors and find the optimal function match. The coordinates of the fitted circle center are (A, B), the radius is R, the sample size is N, the sample set is (Xi,Yi),i=1,2,3,⋯,N, and the distance between the sample set and the center of the circle is di.

The degree of agreement between the fitted circle and the sample data was analyzed. The sum of the squared error (SSE) was used to describe the degree of discreteness.

The length of the aorta is associated with the degree of discreteness but is also influenced by body size. Therefore, the radius of the fitting circle was proposed to eliminate the influence of body size on the degree of discreteness. The ratio of SSE to radius could be expressed as 1R∑(Xi-X¯)2, and the ratio of variance to the radius was calculated by 1NR∑(Xi-X¯)2.

Clinical data analysis and correlation verification

Clinical data were processed with SPSS software (IBM SPSS 23.0, SPSS Inc.). All quantitative data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and the unpaired t test was used for comparisons between quantitative data. The correlation between the fitted circle and AD was tested using logistic regression models. The logistic regression model was adjusted by known risk factor of AD (age, sex, and hypertension) according to guideline and body size parameter (body surface area, BSA) which may affect aortic morphologic parameters [5]. BSA = 0.0061 × height (cm) + 0.0128 × weight (kg) − 0.1529. A receiver operative characteristic (ROC) curve was generated for the model. The predictive ability of the indicators was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, negative and positive predictive values. The cutoff value was calculated by ROC curve based on the best Youden’s index. The baseline prevalence of AD was from previous reported rate of AD patients in patients presenting with symptoms concerning for AD in whom physicians ordered a CT specifically for the purpose of ruling in/out AD [23]. Statistical significance was considered at P < 0.05.

Results

Characteristics of involved population

CTA records of 98 AD patients were complete and available, among them 63 records were involved in the analysis (22 samples with type A AD, and 41 samples with type B AD), and 71 samples from healthy people were involved. The average age of the AD group and control group was 56.90 ± 13.56 and 61.07 ± 17.16, respectively (p = 0.095). Between two groups, there were significant differences in the ratio of male (84.1% vs. 52.1%, p < 0.001), height (169.08 ± 8.07 vs. 163.29 ± 8.45, p < 0.001), weight (67.73 ± 13.18 vs. 61.40 ± 12.14, p = 0.014), BSA (1.75 ± 0.19 vs. 1.64 ± 0.20, p = 0.0026), and the ratio of hypertension patients (76.2% vs. 57.7%, p = 0.029) (Table 1). Considering that male sex and hypertension are risk factors for AD, this difference at baseline was not unexpected. These features may affect fitting-circle statistics and were supposed to be considered in our prediction model.

Aorta fitting result and circle-fitting distinctions of the thoracic aorta

The expanded aortic centerline of healthy aorta (Fig. 3A) and dissected aorta (Fig. 3B) was used to circle fitting. The coordinates of the center and the radius of the fitting circle were obtained via solving the parameters a, b, and c of Q(a,b,c). The representative diagram of the fitting circle of the healthy and dissected aorta was shown. The fitting circle parameters are listed in Table 2. The sum of residuals and variance was used to reflect the extent of similarity between the centerline of the thoracic aorta and the fitting circle. The sum of residuals was significantly higher in the AD group (5404.78 ± 3891.69 vs. 3502.45 ± 2566.71, p = 0.001). The variance of the fitting circle was also higher in the AD group (129.90 ± 90.09 vs. 86.23 ± 56.60, p = 0.001). Considering the non-negligible differences in sex and body size between the samples, the radius of the fitting circle was calculated (45.63 ± 7.43 vs. 43.30 ± 6.78, p = 0.061), and the variance divided by radius (3.04 ± 2.65 vs. 2.04 ± 1.36, p = 0.006) were used for AD prediction model.

Fitting circle prediction model

According to the above comparisons of fitting circle statistics and sample baselines, variance/radius, age, sex, hypertension, and BSA are involved in logistic regression analysis (Table 3). The receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve was used to assess the performance of this model. The area under the ROC curve (AUC) was 0.812 (Fig. 4). The optimum cutoff value was 0.545, and the sensitivity and specificity of the model derived from circle fitting were 73.3% and 81.2%, respectively. The negative predictive value of this model is 91.6% and the positive predictive value is 52.2%. The result of logistic regression analysis indicated that the variance/radius of the fitting circle was positively correlated with the probability of AD (OR = 1.350, β = 0.300, p = 0.034, the OR of variance/radius refers to the risk per 1-unit increase). The results matched the hypothesis: The shape of a healthy thoracic aorta could be reflected by a circle, while the shape of the aorta of AD patients did not fit the circle well.

Discussion

This study demonstrated a significant difference between dissected and healthy aortas. As shown in Fig. 3, the shape of the healthy ascending aorta, aortic arch, and part of the descending aorta resembles a circle, while that of the dissected aorta differs from a circle. This degree of mismatch between the centerline and the fitted circle reflects the tortuosity and slumpedness of the aorta, which is consistent with observations in clinical practice. Considering the clinical needs, the baseline prevalence of AD in patients presenting with symptoms concerning for AD and received CT specifically for AD diagnose (21.9%) [23] was used to calculate predictive values. The negative predictive value (91.6%) is high, while due to reasons including low prevalence of AD, the positive predictive value (52.2%) is relatively low.

Based on this result, we proposed a new question: Can aortic dissection be identified high-dimensional morphological indicators describing the degree of local aortic deformation? By answering this question, patients with a high risk of dissection can potentially be identified. In this study, the optimal plane was confirmed mathematically; viewed from this optimal plane, the degree of mismatch between the centerline and the fitted circle was used to quantitatively describe the morphological characteristics of the aorta. The result suggested that this indicator achieved good performance in identifying AD.

According to previous studies, morphological risk factors for AD can be evaluated by the aortic centerline of AD patients. Rylski et al. compared the geometry features before and after aortic dissection onset. This study involved 63 non-Marfan patients with nonbicuspid aortic valves who had undergone computed tomography angiography < 2 years before and within 12 h after aortic dissection onset. The result indicated that while the largest increase in diameter and volume induced by the dissection were observed, the centerline length showed no significant difference before and after onset in ascending aorta (99.6 vs.105.0, p = 0.090), aortic arch (39.8 vs.41.0, p = 0.677) and proximal descending thoracic aorta (123.2 vs. 125.8, p = 0.912) [24]. Although the sample size of this study needs to be further expanded, it provides strong evidence for the consistency of the aortic centerline before and after onset. Similarly, Trimarchi et al., reported that the diameter of the aorta increases sharply after aortic dissection while the index extracted from the centerline is relatively stable to aortic dissection [14]. Based on this, multiple cross-sectional studies have used morphological features of the aortic centerline to identify the risk factors or predictors for aortic dissection. Using the centerline method, Sun et al. reported that the incremental aortic arch angulation, the tortuosity, and the type III arch configuration are valuable geometric risk factors for type B dissection [25]. Corte et al. reported that along with aortic diameter and length, the asc-arch angle is a promising measurement to predict aortic dissection [20]. Another mainstream computer-aided diagnosis and treatment is achieved by segmenting the true and false lumen of aorta in AD patients, and there has been a wealth of positive results [25]. In 2006, Kovács et al. introduced model-based approaches to identify the true and false lumen of AD by the successive steps: segmentation of the aorta, detection of the intimal flap and identification of the two lumina [26]. Since each step is highly dependent on the preceding steps, the approach prone to failure on irregularly-shaped aortas. Besides, Lee et al. introduced an approach combines multi-scale wavelet analysis and generative-discriminative model matching to segment the lumens [27]. In recent years, deep learning has been an emerging approach to segment dissected aorta. Li et al. reported the first application of deep learning in AD, using two 2D cascaded convolutional networks to extract the contours of aorta and true lumen. Only the lumen contours are manually annotated with a fixed number of points [28]. Cao et al. designed a 3D U-Net that can segment true and false lumen simultaneously in a unified network, based on a large and manually annotated CTA datasets [29]. Based on previous studies, we presented the concept of the fitting circle using the centerline method. However, this evaluation of aortic geometry needs to be tested on a dataset that, for each subject, contains pre- and post-dissection imaging before any real predictive or risk-stratifying capability is claimed.

Furthermore, previous studies have indicated that the pathological changes in AD are not limited to the entry tear; rather, the whole aorta is affected and deformed [2, 21, 24]. These results raise new research questions for current aortic treatment: Does aortic stent implantation, which simply seals intimal tears, play the same role as “radical surgery” in aortic dissection? The onset of aortic dissection may be the result of pathological changes in the long section of the aorta. These pathological processes can lead to macroscopic deformation in properties such as the slumpedness, angulation, and stiffness of the aorta. In this case, we should pay attention to the general health of the aorta rather than only the intima tear.

Several limitations need to be acknowledged. The major shortcoming is that the sample size is relatively small. Larger sample size is required to draw more robust and accurate conclusions. Another shortcoming is that no external validation was performed. An independent cohort is needed to verify the conclusions. This series of studies is continuing, which may solve the above issues. Furthermore, we hope that this discrepancy will allow for rapid identification of thoracic AD at the onset, or even as a risk factor to predict AD before the onset. However, subgroup analyses based on interval from AD onset to CTA were not conducted. Therefore, although the parameter of fitted circle can describe the abnormalities associated with AD, it is unknown whether these parameters can identify early AD. Subgroup analysis is necessary after sample size increases. In addition, considering that the aim is to identify thoracic AD early, it would be more valuable if the control sample was people who had chest pain and in whom CTA was performed to rule out dissection, instead of healthy people. This could better reflect the situation in emergency and outpatient. Naturally, it is too early to consider such remote limitations in clinical practice based only on theoretical results. However, if new morphological indexes are proven to be superior to the current traditional diameter index in clinical practice, they may have the potential to form part of a new aortic dissection prevention and diagnose program.

We are committed to applying this work to the clinic. We established this reproducible approach by identifying SAR as the evaluation standard and obtaining the optimal 2D morphology with unified algorithms. Based on the current algorithm, we are developing software that can automatically calculate fitted circle parameters from CTA data. This tool would facilitate the expansion of sample size and the clinical application of the fitted circle model. In addition, other high-dimensional morphological indicators, such as collapse degree, are also considered in this tool.

Conclusion

The morphological characteristics of the thoracic aorta can be described by a fitted circle, while that of the dissected aorta had poorer similarity to a circle. The fitting circle model is an effective indicator for dissected thoracic aortas.
