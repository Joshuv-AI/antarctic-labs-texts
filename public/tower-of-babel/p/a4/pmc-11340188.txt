The benefits of undergraduate medical students delivering free preparticipation physical evaluations in collaboration with a K-12 school district
Authors: Kaitlin D Cappetto, Benjamin Ehrlich, Elise A Anderson, Amelia T Huynh, Joanne Tran, Katheryn Hudon
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStudent-run free clinics are present at many medical schools throughout the United States. While many work with adult homeless or uninsured populations, few work with pediatric populations. We investigated the impact of volunteering at a free preparticipation sports physical clinic on medical students’ educational motivation, school engagement, confidence in their clinical skills, connectedness to community, and self-efficacy.MethodsFirst and second year undergraduate medical students who volunteered at a preparticipation sports physical clinic were eligible for the study. 30 students participated in the study, some volunteering multiple times, for a total of 55 post-volunteering surveys. Pre- and post-volunteering surveys were administered via REDCap. The surveys were designed using Likert scales and the General Self-Efficacy scale. Paired two tailed t-tests were used to analyze pre- and post-volunteering data.ResultsStudents had statistically significant increases in their confidence in working with pediatric populations, performing a standard physical exam, working in an interdisciplinary setting, and communicating with patients. They also demonstrated statistically significant increases in their feelings of connectedness to their reason for practicing medicine and to the greater local community.ConclusionsThis research demonstrated measurable increases in motivation and confidence in clinical skills for medical students that participated. This is a valuable experience for medical students and is a model that other medical schools can adopt to help diversify and strengthen their curriculum. In the future, we would like to collect more data to further evaluate the impact of this volunteering on medical students, patients, families, and other community stakeholders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-024-05806-3.Keywords: Medical students, Medical education, Preclinical medical education, Student-run free clinic, Pediatrics, Mental health, Depression, Burnout

Background

Many medical schools in the United States are associated with student-run free clinics (SRFC) operating under faculty supervision [1–11]. SRFCs provide opportunities for students to practice their clinical skills in the early years of their medical training, while also immersing themselves in and serving their local community. The existing research surrounding student volunteering in free clinics demonstrates these opportunities assist students in developing both their physical exam and humanistic clinical skills [1, 7, 11].

A 2014 survey of 86 member institutions of the United States Association of American Medical Colleges found that over 140,000 patients were seen annually at 208 SRFCs, and that over 75% of accredited allopathic medical schools are associated with at least one SRFC [1, 3]. A 2005 survey identified 111 SRFCs. [2] These results suggest that the number of SRFCs almost doubled in less than 10 years [1–3]. Notably, neither of these surveys included the 41 osteopathic medical schools in the United States, many of whom also have SRFCs [12]. Most SRFCs focus on serving disadvantaged populations, including patients who are homeless, uninsured, or underinsured [3].

100% of SRFCs in Smith et al.’s 2014 survey reported offering outpatient adult medicine services, while only 40% offered pediatric services [3]. Thus, the literature describing pediatric care in SRFCs is limited. The University of Arizona College of Medicine-Tucson TotShots program provided preparticipation physical evaluations and vaccinations to uninsured children in Tucson [13]. However, their published research focused on patient satisfaction and cost savings, rather than the impact on medical student volunteers. Another study at the University of Arizona described the implementation of a medical student-run child and adolescent psychiatry clinic, but only 32% of their patients were pediatric [4]. Other research exists on exposure to pediatric populations without engaging in clinical care directly. For instance, a small 1994 study implemented a Big Brother Big Sister model to support and educate children with chronic illnesses [14].

A systematic review by Rotenstein et al. published in JAMA in 2016 estimated the prevalence of depression and suicidal ideation in medical students to be 27.2% and 11.1% respectively [15]. The authors found the prevalence of depressive symptoms were significantly higher in medical students than the general population. While significantly more research must be done to better understand the etiology of the prevalence of these symptoms amongst medical students, the trends are troubling and suggest a need for creative solutions to support medical student wellness [16]. Perfectionism and imposter syndrome are also linked to mental health concerns in medical students [17, 18]. In a survey of 138 medical students, Villwock et al. found 49% of female participants and 24% of male participants experienced imposter syndrome [17]. Additionally, imposter syndrome was significantly associated with burnout.

In addition to mental health concerns, there is a well-documented fatigue in medical students’ educational motivation, their original attitudes towards medicine and ultimately, their reasons for wanting to be a physician [19]. At the University of Minnesota, Sick et al. investigated the impact of volunteering in an interprofessional SRFC on medical students by comparing their attitudes towards the medically underserved to the attitudes of their peers that did not volunteer [6]. In both populations, their attitudes toward the practice of medicine dipped after beginning medical school. However, the group that volunteered in the SRFC for two years experienced less of a decline.

We examined how undergraduate medical students’ educational motivation, school engagement, confidence in their clinical skills, connectedness to community, and self-efficacy evolved as they volunteered at free preparticipation physical evaluation (PPE) clinics organized in collaboration with a local K-12 school district. Our goal was to learn more about the impact participating in SRFCs has on medical students.

Methods

First and second year medical students from Pacific Northwest University of Health Sciences College of Osteopathic Medicine (PNWU-COM) who volunteered at a preparticipation physical exam clinic were eligible to participate in this study. Members of the institution’s pediatric interest group received volunteering information via email. Study participants were recruited via email after signing up for a volunteer shift. Those who consented to participate were asked to complete a screening form and pre-volunteering survey prior to their first shift. During the shift, participants performed standardized preparticipation physical exams under the supervision of family medicine and pediatrics PNWU-COM faculty. Students received feedback from the supervising physician after each patient they saw. Additionally, there was a group debrief led by the preceptor at the end of each clinic. After their shift ended, they completed the post-volunteering survey. At subsequent volunteer shifts, only a post-volunteering survey was administered.

Surveys were administered and data were collected electronically via Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap) tools hosted at Pacific Northwest University of Health Sciences [20, 21]. These surveys included the General Self-Efficacy Scale and a study-designed questionnaire using Likert scales with 15 questions regarding educational motivation, school engagement, confidence in clinical skills, and connectedness to community [22]. Additionally, there was a section soliciting feedback about the volunteering opportunity.

Data were analyzed with descriptive statistics and two-tailed paired t-tests (α = 0.05) comparing the responses from the pre-volunteering and first post-volunteering surveys. Furthermore, T-tests were conducted comparing the first and second post-volunteering surveys in participants who completed more than one shift.

Results

30 first and second year undergraduate osteopathic medical students enrolled in this study. 9 participants identified as male, while 21 participants identified as female. At the time of enrollment, 17 participants were first year students and 13 were second year students. 29/30 participants had previous experience in a clinical environment as scribes, patient care technicians, medical assistants, volunteers, or other roles. 20 participants volunteered once, and 10 participants volunteered for two or more shifts. 55 post-volunteering surveys were completed.

Two-tailed paired t-tests demonstrated statistically significant increases in how confident students felt working with pediatric populations, communicating with patients, working in an interdisciplinary setting, and in performing a standard physical examination after completing one volunteer shift (Table 1). Additionally, there were statistically significant increases in how connected students felt to the greater Yakima Valley community and their original reason for practicing medicine after volunteering (Table 1). The differences seen between pre-volunteering and post-volunteering responses to the remaining questions were statistically insignificant. None showed statistically significant decreases.

In order to assess the quality of the volunteering experience, the post-volunteering survey asked students to rate three statements on a scale of 1–5 where 1 signified “strongly disagree” and 5 signified “strongly agree.” On average, students rated “this volunteering opportunity was a valuable learning experience” as (4.7 ± 0.6), “I made a contribution to the Yakima Valley community” as (4.4 ± 0.7), and “I felt properly educated to succeed in my volunteer role” as (4.4 ± 0.9).

The post-volunteering survey also requested general feedback from students about their experience. Overall, the feedback was positive and included statements such as: “I have had such a great experience working at these clinics. I think they are really beneficial for the students and for the community,” and “Very valuable for practicing and improving clinical skills, such as physical exam and oral presentation skills.” Most of the requests for improvement were related to wanting more volunteering opportunities or more interactions with patients. Students stated: “I wish there were more frequent opportunities because it reminds me of why I chose med school,” and “There were only two patients so I did not get to practice very much, but I think this experience is great for learning and practice.”

We compared the post-volunteering survey responses from participants’ first and second volunteering shifts for participants who volunteered more than once but did not find any statistically significant differences.

Discussion

Benefit to medical students

The results of our survey support our assertion that by collaborating with local school districts to provide PPEs, medical schools can create valuable opportunities that support student wellness and growth while providing meaningful services to their community. These sessions were designed to help students gain firsthand experience to observe and participate in clinical care. The goal of this opportunity was to enhance their comfort and build competence in performing various components of a physical exam. By practicing these skills in a supportive setting, students could freely ask questions and receive immediate assistance without the pressure of formal evaluation. Survey results indicated participants experienced increased confidence in several clinical skills they will employ for the rest of their medical careers. Specifically, students reported significantly increased confidence working with pediatric populations, communicating with patients, working in an interdisciplinary setting, and in performing a standard physical examination. Additionally, participants reported feeling more connected to the local community and their original reasons for practicing medicine.

It is well-established that mental health conditions like depression, anxiety, and burnout are more prevalent in medical students and physicians than the general population [15, 17]. Dyrbye et al. found that about 50% of fourth year medical students met criteria for burnout on the Maslach Burnout Inventory [23]. In the preclinical years, dissatisfaction with the learning environment has been identified as a cause of burnout, while increased patient interaction is negatively associated with burnout [19, 24]. Therefore, clinical volunteering activities like we have described have the potential to mitigate burnout in the preclinical years. Students described the sports physical clinics very positively and critiques were rooted in the desire for increased accessibility to the opportunities, so we feel similar curricular additions could be very helpful in preventing burnout symptoms. Additionally, it is well-established that stronger social connections are protective against mental and physical morbidity and mortality, so increased sense of connectedness to the local community may also be beneficial to students’ mental health [25, 26].

Benefit to the community

In addition to the positive impacts on medical students, free clinics like these can fill a need within the local community. There is a well-documented shortage of primary care physicians in the United States that is particularly acute in rural areas and for pediatric populations. A 2023 analysis by Ramesh and Yu found that while the number of general pediatricians in the United States increased by 6% from 2010 to 2020, the number of general pediatricians practicing in rural counties decreased [27]. Similarly, the number of family medicine physicians increased by 14.1% from 2010 to 2020, but the number practicing in rural counties decreased. This known shortage can prevent families from accessing care in a timely manner, particularly for non-emergent visit reasons like PPEs. However, it is important for families to be able to obtain PPEs to allow their children to participate in school athletic activities, which may support their physical and mental health. Research has shown organized sport participation can be protective against symptoms of anxiety and depression [28]. Additionally, participation in organized sports can have lasting positive effects on physical health into early adulthood [29].

Limitations

There were several limitations to this pilot study. Our scale was limited by several factors, including availability of preceptors and school district staff. There were a limited number of preparticipation evaluation clinics held and consequently, a limited number of volunteer shifts. This limited availability also dictated our sample size and restricted our ability to follow participants through several volunteering sessions. The clinics were met with such enthusiasm from medical student participants that volunteer shifts were frequently filled within minutes of being made available via email. In an attempt to increase the number of students able to access the opportunities, we began requesting participants limit their volunteering sessions to one session per month. While we believe student enthusiasm reflects the value of this program, we also recognize the importance of future research allowing for more significant follow-up opportunities. Furthermore, while most of the clinics were well attended, with each medical student performing several physical exams, some had a low patient turnout. Participant enrollment was also limited in these instances of low turnout, as not every medical student was able to actively participate in the PPEs. Additionally, while any first or second-year medical student was welcome to enroll, we specifically recruited from our institution’s pediatrics interest group. As these clinics involved the pediatric population and most of our participants were members of the pediatrics group, one may wonder if these results are fully generalizable to all medical students. However, our participants reported a wide variety of specialty interests including family medicine, OBGYN, internal medicine, anesthesia, sports medicine, and emergency medicine, in addition to pediatrics. Based on this range of specialties, we would expect the results could be applicable to medical students with interests outside of pediatrics. Finally, nearly all (97%) of these participants had previous experience working or volunteering in a clinical environment. It would be interesting to examine the impact of an intervention like this on students who have more limited experience in clinical settings.

Future directions

In the future, we would like to continue to strengthen the collaboration with the school district to expand volunteering opportunities and maximize the benefit to the community. More volunteering opportunities will allow for more data collection and robust analysis. We are interested in collecting a larger sample size and organizing longitudinal follow-up. We are also interested in delving deeper into student development from first to second year and investigating which specialties participants ultimately pursue. Specialty choice is particularly interesting as some past research has shown that volunteering in free clinics may encourage students to pursue primary care, while other studies have not found any significant relationship [5, 7, 8, 10].

Additionally, we would like to collect feedback from community members regarding their experiences receiving care at the sports physical clinics to ensure the clinics are beneficial to all involved parties. We believe that this model of volunteering could be a valuable opportunity for other schools to bring care to their communities, while supplementing and enhancing medical student education. We aspire to nurture a symbiotic relationship between medical education and community service that will ultimately shape a new generation of healthcare professionals dedicated to addressing the broader needs of the communities they serve.

Finally, we will consider introducing an exploration of the educational value of SRFCs to our project in the future. We would like to introduce a formal evaluation of clinical skills to be performed before and after the first volunteer shift to assess the objective impact on student competency, in addition to the positive impact on student confidence demonstrated in our pilot data. The addition of this evaluation would be carefully discussed with preceptors prior to implementation to ensure its feasibility.

Conclusions

This study provides evidence of the value of medical students participating in clinical volunteering during the preclinical years. After participating in just one PPE clinic, participants demonstrated increased confidence in clinical skills they will utilize throughout their medical careers. Participants also demonstrated increased connectedness to both the local community and their original motivation for practicing medicine, which may positively support their mental health [30]. The promotion of mental health in medical professionals is an ongoing concern for medical schools and healthcare institutions [16–19, 23, 24, 31, 32]. PNWU-COM partnered with the Yakima School District to host the PPE clinics study participants volunteered at. We believe partnerships between medical schools and local school districts are excellent opportunities for medical schools to fill needs within their local community and provide educational opportunities for their students.
