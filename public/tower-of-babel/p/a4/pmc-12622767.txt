Mesenchymal Stromal Cells Mitigate Prolonged Cardiopulmonary Bypass-Induced Neuroinflammation
Authors: Kei Kobayashi, Takuya Maeda, Nemanja Saric, Kamil Sarkislali, Akihisa Furuta, Chase D McCann, Patrick J Hanley, David Zurakowski, Nobuyuki Ishibashi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesBone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cell (BM-MSC) delivery through cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) can modulate CPB-induced systemic inflammation and inhibit microglial activation. The present study assessed whether BM-MSC treatment remains effective for extensive inflammatory reactions.MethodsTwo-week-old piglets were randomized into control, CPB with deep hypothermic circulatory arrest (DHCA), CPB with BM-MSCs, CPB with DHCA plus 180 min extended perfusion, and extended CPB with BM-MSCs. Human BM-MSCs (1 × 107 cells/kg) were delivered via the CPB circuit during rewarming. We measured 9 porcine plasma cytokines. Brains were harvested for microglial immunohistochemistry and premotor cortex RNA-sequencing.ResultsA total of 20 piglets were included (n = 4 per group). An extended CPB period increased the number of white matter microglia and plasma interferon-g (IFN-g) and TNF-a levels compared to standard CPB (P < .05). Notably, BM-MSCs normalized the extensive microglia expansion resulting from prolonged CPB (P < .001). In addition to reduced IFN-g and increased IL-10, BM-MSCs increased IL-1b and IL-8 levels, suggesting possible immunosuppressive reactions observed under inflammatory microenvironment. Gene ontology analyses in the premotor cortex post-CPB found involvement of NF-kB signalling. While there was a positive correlation between IFN-g and microglia expansion/activation, IL-10 levels were inversely correlated with the number of microglia. Consistent with immunosuppressive roles in the inflammatory environment, we found inverse correlations between IL-1b and IL-8 levels and CPB-induced microglial activation (P < .01).ConclusionsBM-MSC administration via CPB shows promise for reducing extensive inflammatory stress. Further investigation using this model will elucidate CPB-induced unique pathological events in the developing brain and mechanisms underlying the therapeutic actions of BM-MSC.

INTRODUCTION

Neurodevelopmental impairment is an important challenge for survivors after paediatric cardiac surgery.1,2 Our previous piglet study showed that reducing systemic inflammation minimized the risk of cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB)-induced white matter (WM) injury during immature stages of brain development.3 Microglia are the resident immune cells in the central nervous system, contributing not only to repair processes following injury but also to cortical grey and WM maturation during brain development.4,5 We have previously shown that CPB causes prolonged microglia activation up to 4 weeks after surgery.6

Bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stromal cells (BM-MSCs) are a unique cell population that can secrete a wide range of growth factors, cytokines, and other bioactive molecules capable of regulating cellular and tissue damage through their endocrine functions.7,8 BM-MSCs can promote endogenous repair processes after various injuries, including the brain, by modulating microglia proliferation and shifting microglial cells from a pro-inflammatory state to a repair-permissive state.9 Results from our previous studies indicate that intra-arterial delivery of BM-MSCs through CPB during the rewarming period can alter responses of neural stem/progenitor cells to CPB-induced brain insults and reduce microglial activation after paediatric cardiac surgery.10–12 As inflammatory responses vary with CPB duration, management strategies, and patient-related factors, we assessed whether BM-MSC treatment remains effective for extensive inflammatory reactions arising from a prolonged period of CPB. Based on the unique changes in anti/pro-inflammatory cytokines after BM-MSC delivery, our study further evaluated molecular profiles of tissue environments and analysed the relationship between systemic cytokine levels and microglial activation after paediatric cardiac surgery.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee of the Children’s National Hospital (IACUC protocol number 00030593).

Experimental model

This study involved a total of 20 male Yorkshire piglets (4.1 ± 0.3 kg) at 2 weeks of age (Archer Farms, Inc., Darlington, MD). Animals were randomly assigned to 5 groups: (1) Control group (No surgery, n = 4); (2) CPB group (CPB with 18°C deep hypothermic circulatory arrest [DHCA] for 60 min, n = 4); (3) CPB + MSC group (CPB with DHCA followed by BM-MSC administration, n = 4); (4) CPB-L group (CPB with DHCA followed by 3 hrs extra-perfusion, n = 4); and (5) CPB-L + MSC group (CPB-L with BM-MSC administration, n = 4) (Figure 1A). CPB-induced insults were amplified in the CPB-L group to define the impact of MSCs on extensive systemic and brain-specific inflammatory responses (Figure 1B).

BM-MSCs were manufactured using the same methods used for our phase 1 clinical trial at Children’s National Hospital.13 For cytokine/chemokine analysis, plasma samples were collected at the end of regular CPB duration (150 min from CPB initiation; termed T1), and 3 hrs after regular CPB period or at the end of the extra-perfusion period (330 min from CPB initiation; termed T2).

Given that T1 is the same in condition between CPB and CPB-L groups, as well as CPB + MSC and CPB-L + MSC groups, comparisons at T1 were performed between CPB (n = 8, including CPB and CPB-L groups) and CPB + MSC (n = 8, including CPB + MSC and CPB-L + MSC groups) conditions. This approach increased statistical power while avoiding redundancy.

We measured the levels of 9 porcine cytokines, including interferon-a (IFN-a), interferon-g (IFN-g), interleukin-1b (IL-1b), interleukin-4 (IL-4), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), interleukin-10 (IL-10), interleukin-12p40 (IL-12p40), and tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α). Brain tissue was perfused and harvested at T2 for immunohistochemical analysis (Figure S1B).3 We performed all experiments in compliance with the National Institutes of Health “Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals.”

To characterize the CPB-induced brain microenvironment changes, we used RNA sequencing on premotor cortical tissues isolated from piglet brains at 3-h post-CPB. Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed using the Enrichr web-based tool14–16 with genes found to be differentially expressed between CPB and control piglet cortex.

In addition, we conducted exploratory correlation analyses between circulating cytokines and microglial activation to assess systemic-central interactions.

The primary objective of this study was to assess the impact of prolonged CPB on microglial activation and systemic cytokine profiles, and to determine whether BM-MSC administration mitigates these effects. Secondary exploratory objectives included correlation analyses between cytokines and microglial activation, and regional analyses of cortex versus WM.

Assessments

Antibodies for Iba1, a marker of all resident microglia, and CD11b, a marker of activated microglia, were used to identify total and activated microglia, respectively. Cellular analysis was performed using coronal sections, which included 6 cortical regions and 5 WM regions to evaluate if there are any regional differences (Figure 1C and D). All Images were acquired on a Leica TCS SP8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Exton, PA) as performed previously.17 Total RNA was extracted from premotor cortices and subjected to RNA sequencing.

Statistical analysis

The Shapiro-Wilk test was performed for normality assessment. One-way ANOVA was applied to assess experimental conditions and perioperative blood work. Two-way ANOVA was applied with Bonferroni post-hoc comparisons for microglia analyses (brain regions and treatment groups). Student’s t-tests were used for cytokine/chemokine analysis, with either P < .05 considered statistically significant for single comparisons or P < .025 (0.05/2) considered significant to account for multiple group comparisons (Bonferroni adjustment).18 The outlier was identified by the ROUT method with 1% false discovery rate.19 The association between the number of microglial cells and cytokine/chemokine levels was evaluated by Spearman’s rank correlation analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using the PRISM9 software package (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, United States) or Stata 16.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station, TX, United States). All statistical analyses were reviewed by a statistical expert (D.Z.).

The detailed methods are described in the Supplementary Materials.

RESULTS

Extended CPB period increases the number of white matter microglia

Experimental conditions and perioperative data are described in Tables S1 and S2. The number of Iba1+ microglia and CD11b+Iba1+ activated microglia in both cortex and WM regions significantly increased in both CPB-induced groups compared to control (P < .001 in both regions, Figures S1A-D and S2). While there were no differences in the number of cortical Iba1+ cells and CD11b+Iba1+ cells between 2 CPB-induced insults, the number of Iba1+ microglia and CD11b+Iba1+ in WM after prolonged CPB was significantly higher than the number in the standard CPB group (P = .002 and P = .003, respectively, Figure S1C and D). There were no regional differences between the WM regions analysed, demonstrating a pan-cerebral effect.

BM-MSC treatment reduced extensive microglia expansion and activation induced by prolonged CPB in the cortex and white matter

BM-MSC delivery through CPB significantly improved microglial increases due to prolonged CPB in all cortical grey and WM regions assessed (P = .001 and P < .001, respectively, Figure 2). CPB-induced microglial activation determined by CD11b expression level was also mitigated by BM-MSC delivery (P < .001 for both regions. Figure 2B and D and Figure S2).11,12

When we assessed plasma cytokine levels at T2, the extra-perfusion caused increases in IFN-g and TNF-a levels compared to standard CPB (4.89 vs 1.26 pg/mL, P = .036, 7.74 vs 0.63 pg/mL, P = .039, respectively, Table S3). Notably, we found that BM-MSC delivery through CPB significantly suppressed increases in IFN-g levels resulting from prolonged CPB (4.89 vs 0.55, P < .001). Interestingly, BM-MSC delivery also increased IL-1b levels (2.28 vs 25.46, P = .011). Significant differences were not detected between CPB-L and CPB-L + MSC groups in other cytokine levels assessed in this study.

CPB creates a tumour-like inflammatory microenvironment, which can be rescued by BM-MSC delivery

Among differentially expressed genes (DEGs) downregulated following CPB, we detected enrichment for signatures of IL-1 processing (adjusted P < .1, Figure 3A). In addition, the GSEA revealed a reduction in the expression of transcripts encoding components of the TNF-a/nuclear factor-kappa B (NF-kB) signalling complex in post-CPB tissue (Adjusted P < .1, Figure 3B). Furthermore, querying the latest BioPlex 2017 interactome database, which profiles protein-protein interactions, found RELB and NFKBI proteins to be statistically significant interactors of the downregulated gene set post-CPB (Adjusted P < .1, Figure 3C). Lastly, the upregulation of DEGs following CPB was associated with a decreased number of peripheral myelinated nerve fibres, which was identified as the primary condition in the Human Phenotype Ontology analysis, indicating impairments of WM due to CPB (Adjusted P < .1, Figure 3D).

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates that prolonged CPB duration increases both numbers of microglia cells in the brain and plasma IFN-g levels. In addition to modulation of cytokine levels, the current study shows that BM-MSC treatment inhibits extensive microglia expansion and activation induced by prolonged CPB. Furthermore, we observe a correlation between microglia numbers and cytokine levels during CPB, suggesting an interaction between systemic inflammation and microglial responses resulting from CPB. Finally, results from our RNA sequencing indicate that a tumour-like inflammatory environment occurs in the developing cortex after CPB, which may explain the unique cytokine modulation pattern induced by BM-MSCs.

IFN-g is one of the key pro-inflammatory cytokines responsible for cell-mediated immunity.20 IFN-g primarily signals through the JAK-STAT pathway, which is known to be critical during the inflammatory response.20 Indeed, a longer duration of CPB increased circulating IFN-g levels. Our previous porcine study demonstrated CPB-induced acute and prolonged increase in the number of microglia in WM.6 In the current study, a prolonged CPB period also caused an increase in microglia in WM. Reactive microgliosis can be toxic to neighbouring neurons and glial cells due to the production of pro-inflammatory factors, leading to exacerbated brain injury.21 In this study, CD11b immunoreactivity was used to assess activated microglia. Both short and long CPB significantly increased activated microglia compared to controls in all brain regions analysed, indicating CPB causes a pan-cerebral inflammation.

BM-MSCs possess significant anti-inflammatory properties.22 As observed in our previous studies.11,12 BM-MSC treatment normalized microglia numbers and their activation status, indicating that BM-MSCs retain significant anti-inflammatory properties against extensive gliosis induced by prolonged CPB time. Indeed, we also found a significant reduction in IFN-g levels following treatment. IFN-g, a major product of Th1 cells, activates M1 pro-inflammatory macrophages.20 Thus, BM-MSC-induced microglial phenotype modulation may contribute to the reduction in inflammation. Interestingly, our data indicated BM-MSC-induced increases in circulating IL-8 levels during the very acute phase. While IL-8 is originally recognized as a pro-inflammatory cytokine that activates neutrophil leukocytes,23 it also plays a key role in MSC-mediated CD4+ T-cell recruitment.24 For instance, MSC autophagy increased CD4+ T-cell migration via IL-8 and promotes regulatory T-cell differentiation.24 In addition, IL-1b levels, also known as pro-inflammatory cytokine,25 were significantly increased after BM-MSC induction. Recent studies, however, suggest that both IL-8 and IL-1b can function to promote an immunosuppressive microenvironment.26,27 Consistent with the cytokine data, our DEG results showed IL-1 processing was among the top predicted downregulated pathways in cortical tissue post-CPB, suggestive of impairments to IL-1 turnover. In our RNA-seq studies, the NF-kB pathway was indirectly upregulated via suppression of the NF-kB inhibitor family gene, NFKBIA/B. Activation of the NF-kB pathway can upregulate anti-apoptotic genes and contribute to tumour promotion and progression.28,29 RELB, specific to the non-canonical NF-kB pathway, was also implicated. Moreover, CPB was associated with WM deficits, which supports our microglial results in WM. Altogether, given the critical role of the NF-kB pathway in tumour promotion and progression, it is possible that inflammatory and oxidative stresses induced by CPB produce similar microenvironments to a progressing tumour, whereby BM-MSCs contribute by promoting anti-inflammatory as well as immunosuppressive signalling, serving dual roles that complicate their net effect. In addition, these findings favour immunomodulation as the predominant mechanism of BM-MSC, with some additional direct paracrine contributions in the local microenvironment.

Our results also demonstrated unique relationships between circulating cytokines and microglial expansion during CPB. We observed correlations between several cytokines and activated microglia cells. When 4 cytokines with significant change following BM-MSC induction were assessed, 4 cytokine levels soon after CPB had a significant correlation with either Iba1+ or CD1b+Iba1+ microglia, suggesting that MSC-induced early cytokine modulations may affect microglial responses. In addition, we observed an inverse correlation between microglia cells and IL-1b and IL-8 at later time points, 2 of which contribute to immunosuppressive mechanism under tumour-mediated microenvironment. Cytokines are highly complex regulatory systems, and a proper balance is necessary in tissue repair and regenerative processes. Future in-depth investigations will be required to understand the effect of CPB on the developing immune system and the role of MSCs in CPB-induced immunomodulation.

BM-MSC can regulate microglia activation and participate in the phenotypic switch from a pro-inflammatory state to repair-permissive state.9,30 We have previously shown that BM-MSC delivery through CPB reduces CPB-induced acute microglia expansion.11 Although CPB-induced inflammatory stress was escalated due to prolonged period of CPB time, our results indicate that BM-MSC treatment inhibits extensive microglia activation after cardiac surgery. Since inflammatory responses during CPB vary according to patient characteristics, type of surgical procedures, and/or CPB management strategy, results from the present studies further support the therapeutic capacity of BM-MSCs for neuroprotection in children with congenital heart disease.

The lack of long-term assessment and small sample sizes are limitations of this study. We also acknowledge that the Bonferroni adjustment may be insufficient with multiple groups. It is possible that the small numbers contributed to the lack of relevant changes between groups. Lastly, direct validation of NF-kB pathway activation was not performed, which may have less impact on the study.

CONCLUSION

In conclusion, BM-MSC delivery through CPB has significant potential for mitigating extensive inflammatory stress caused by CPB and reducing neurological impairment in children after cardiac surgery. Our studies also provide new insights into the potential for CPB to cause a tumour-like environment in the developing brain. Further investigations using this animal model will help understand the long-term impacts of unique pathological events resulting from CPB and mechanisms underlying the therapeutic actions of BM-MSCs in children with congenital heart disease.
