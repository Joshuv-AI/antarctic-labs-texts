A combined classification system for preaxial foot polydactyly and its derived surgical strategies
Authors: Yu Chen, Chunhua Yin, Wei Liu, Wentao Yu, Xiaofang Shen, Mimi Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPediatric polydactyly is a relatively common congenital condition of hand and foot, affecting physical and mental development of children. Hand polydactyly has been brought into focus recently. By contrast, the studies on foot polydactyly are limited. Among the types, there is little international research on preaxial foot polydactyly since it is a relatively rare type, and the domestic research on it is fewer. The purpose of our study is to propose a new classification for guiding operation.MethodsWe collected all patients with preaxial foot polydactyly who had preoperative radiographs at our hospital between 2016 and 2024, and analyzed the reported classifications, finally proposed a new classification system based on the preoperative radiographs. The 64 feet were assessed by 5 observers under the help of the kappa statistic. We operated the derived surgery on various types of the classification system, and evaluate the therapeutic efficacy through the relative imaging parameters and scale.ResultsWe developed a combined classification system for preaxial foot polydactyly, describing variation of hallux in longitudinal dimension. In this classification system, we concluded duplication of distal phalanx combined with abnormal/ normal proximal phalanx, duplication of proximal phalanx combined with abnormal/ normal proximal phalanx or metatarsal, duplication of metatarsal combined with abnormal proximal phalanx and duplication of tarsal. Overall inter-observer agreements for this classification system respectively were 0.805 and 0.795 in first and second reading and intra-observer agreements for 5 observers were > 0.9. Under the derived surgery, the scale for the hallux with preaxial foot polydactyly exhibited notable improvement.ConclusionsThe proposed new classification effectively show different features of all categories, the reliability of which was proved good. The derived surgical strategies of the combined classification system improved the surgical outcomes significantly, which can effectively correct hallux varus and prevent hallux valgus to some extent.Keywords: Preaxial ploydactyly, Foot, Classification, Kappa statistic, Surgery

Introduction

Polydactyly is a common congenital anomaly among limb malformations, which includes both hand and foot polydactyly. Based on the location of the duplication, polydactyly can be classified into three types: preaxial, central and postaxial [1]. In foot polydactyly, postaxial polydactyly is the most common type, accounting for 79% of all cases, while central polydactyly is the rarest, and preaxial polydactyly is less common, comprising 15% of foot polydactyly [2,3]. Although some literature exists on foot polydactyly, preaxial polydactyly of the foot has not been extensively studied compared to postaxial polydactyly. Despite reports from various researchers detailing individual cases, and proposing classification systems, there has been no significant progress in this area for nearly five years [4–7]. The latest research is the Rotterdam foot classification [8].

Preaxial polydactyly of the foot, also known as medial polydactyly, refers to the duplication of the hallux, which includes the distal phalanx, proximal phalanx, metatarsal and tarsal bones [1, 7]. The hallux plays an important role in limiting the movement of the first metatarsophalangeal joint and the entire lower extremity kinetic chain [9]. Additionally, preaxial foot polydactyly can be associated with congenital hallux varus and longitudinal epiphyseal bracket (LEB), leading to unsatisfactory aesthetic outcomes and difficulties with shoe fitting problems [6, 10–12]. Consequently, surgical intervention is often necessary to address there issues. Currently, several classification systems are available to guide surgical procedures, including those proposed by Venn-Waston, Blauth and Olason, Masada, Watanabe and Burger (Table 1) [4–8]. Among these classifications, Venn-Waston proposed a classification system based on the anatomic configuration of the metatarsal. This system includes categories such as normal metatarsal with distal duplication, block metatarsal, Y-shaped metatarsal, T-shaped metatarsal, normal metatarsal shaft with a wide head, and ray duplication [4]. This classification system encompasses three types of polydactyly, emphasizing the level of the metatarsal and describing its anatomic configuration while neglecting other levels. Notably, the block metatarsal classification aligns with LEB and is associated with congenital hallux varus, which necessitates intervention to enhance appearance. Based on the bifurcation level of duplication, Blauth and Olason developed an additional classification system to categorize postaxial and preaxial polydactyly. This system includes tarsal type, metatarsal type, proximal phalanx type, middle phalanx type and distal phalanx type [5]. However, it does not adequately consider other characteristics of preaxial polydactyly. In contrast, Watanabe proposed a specific classification system for preaxial polydactyly, which encompasses tarsal type, metatarsal type, proximal phalanx type and distal phalanx type. This system illustrates various morphologic patterns, although its subtypes are too intricate and complex for practical application [7]. Furthermore, the classification of Masada solely differentiates between phalanx and metatarsal, neglecting the distinctions between distal and proximal phalanges [6]. In contrast, the Rotterdam foot classification has integrated additional subtypes within a duplication level framework, including syndactylous, hypoplastic, and deviated components [8]. For syndactyly, surgeons can design various flaps to reconstruct new web space. However, the proximal part of one hallux may be hypoplastic, while the distal part of another hallux could also be hypoplastic, thus on-top plasty may be a viable surgical treatment. The hypoplastic subtype is insufficient to adequately describe the growth conditions of the duplicated hallux. Additionally, deviation may result from abnormalities of the proximal phalanx and metatarsal, yet this subtype does not specify the underlying issue. In our clinical experience, we have observed that the distal phalanx type may be associated with abnormality of the proximal phalanx, while the proximal phalanx type may correspond with abnormality of the metatarsal. To address these abnormalities, we not only focus on the level of duplication but also consider abnormalities at other levels. The classification of musculoskeletal conditions aims to characterize the nature of the problem and guide treatment decision-making, ultimately improving outcomes. Furthermore, a classification system can establish expected outcomes for the natural history of a condition, thereby providing a foundation for uniform reporting of results across various treatments.

In this study, we present the radiographs of our patients and analyze the corresponding features, subsequently establishing a combined classification system. We assessed the reliability of this combined classification by conducting evaluations among five observers, and operated corresponding surgical approaches to treat various types, achieving satisfactory outcomes.

Materials and methods

Population

Preaxial foot polydactyly refers to the duplication of the hallux [5, 8]. The records of children diagnosed with preaxial foot polydactyly from 2016 to 2024 were reviewed. Patients without preoperative radiographs were excluded. We collected data from 43 patients who suffered from preaxial foot polydactyly, accounting for a total of 67 duplicated halluces, for which radiographs and clinical data were available. A total of 30 patients (46 affected feet) who underwent surgery and were followed up for more than 3 months were included (Fig. 1).

The selection process of study subjects

The classification system

We also employed the Watanabe classification, which categorizes preaxial foot polydactyly based on anatomical patterns into four types: (1) tarsal type, (2) metatarsal type, (3) proximal phalanx type and (4) distal phalanx type. It is important to note that the presence of one type does not imply surgical intervention is required solely for the duplication at that particular level. Based on our clinical experience and the literature, we have observed that the proximal phalanx may exhibit abnormal skeletal morphology in the context of distal phalanx type, such as LEB and a wide proximal phalanx (Fig. 2A, B). The mismatch between the base of the distal phalanx and the head of the proximal phalanx can result in the instability of the interphalangeal joint and ankylosis. However, there has been a lack of studies addressing this issue in cases of foot polydactyly. Additionally, proximal phalanx type may be associated with variations in the proximal phalanx and metatarsal, particularly concerning the base of the metatarsal (Fig. 2C, D, E, F). In some instances, we observed a well-formed proximal part and a hypoplastic distal part of the medial hallux, while the lateral hallux exhibited a hypoplastic proximal part and a well-formed distal part (Fig. 2G). As a result, we integrated the Watanabe classification with variations of adjacent bones to refine a new classification system.

Statistic analysis

Due to the inability to visualize partial cartilage and osseous structures in radiographs, along with variations in personal clinical experience, assessments are not identical across observers. An analysis was conducted to evaluate intra-observer and inter-observer agreement of the classification system by five observers: one orthopedic student, two attending orthopedic surgeons, one plastic specialist and one orthopedic specialist. The five observers classified 64 radiographic images according to our classification system while being blinded to the clinical information of all patients. After a one-month interval, the same observers classified the 64 radiographic images again. Intra-observer and inter-observer agreement were assessed using Cohen’s and Fleiss’ kappa statistics. Intra-observer agreements was calculated by comparing the assessments made by each observer after one month, while inter-observer agreement was determined by comparing the assessments made by all five observers during the same period. The strengths of agreement are expressed using kappa values: <0.2 suggests slight agreement, 0.2–0.4 indicates fair agreement, 0.4–0.6 denotes moderate agreement, 0.6–0.8 signifies substantial agreement, and 0.8-1.0 reflects almost perfect agreement. Statistical analyses of kappa values were performed using SPSSAU online software (https://spssau.com).

The children who underwent surgery were followed up. We collected and recorded the preoperative and postoperative scores of Hallux Metatarsophalangeal-interphalangeal Scale score of the American orthopaedic foot and ankle society (AOFAS), [13] and Metadiaphyseal length index (MDLI) for LEB [10]. For the evaluation of surgical efficacy, the Shapiro-Wilk test was employed to assess the normality of small sample data. Normally distributed measurement data were expressed as Mean ± SD and analyzed using paired sample t-tests. Non-normally distributed measurement data were presented as M (Q1, Q3) and analyzed using paired sample Wilcoxon tests.

Results

The total group consisted of 43 children with 67 preaxial duplications (left: 30, right: 37). Twenty-two girls (51%) and twenty-one boys (49%) were included in the study. Bilateral foot involvement was observed in 24 children (56%), while unilateral foot involvement was noted in 19 children (44%). Among these, one child with bilateral preaxial polydactyly lacked preoperative radiographs, and another other child with unilateral preaxial polydactyly did not have preoperative radiographs of the left foot. Ultimately, a total of 64 children were included. The median age of the children was 13 months (range: 5−132 months).

We found that duplication of the phalanx or metatarsal may coexist with variations of adjacent bones in preaxial foot polydactyly, such as LEB. Therefore, surgical intervention for this type of foot polydactyly must address not only the duplication but also any associated abnormalities of the phalanx or metatarsal. To guide surgical treatment, we developed a new classification that combines the Watanabe classification with considerations for the proximal phalanx and metatarsal. Types Ⅰ, Ⅱ, Ⅲ, and Ⅳ respectively represent distal phalanx type, proximal phalanx type, metatarsal type and tarsal type, while ‘p’ and ‘m’ indicate variations of the proximal phalanx and metatarsal, respectively. The classification system includes the following types: (1) Type Ⅰ, duplication of the distal phalanx without variation of the proximal phalanx; (2) Type Ⅰp, duplication of the distal phalanx with variation of the proximal phalanx, such as LEB, wide proximal phalanx; (3) Type Ⅱ, duplication of the proximal phalanx without variation of the proximal phalanx; (4) Type Ⅱp, duplication of the proximal phalanx with variation of the proximal phalanx, such as LEB, phalangeal fusion; (5) Type Ⅱm, duplication of the proximal phalanx with variation of the metatarsal, such as LEB, dislocation of cuneometatasal joint; (6) Type Ⅲ, duplication of the metatarsal, and the one of duplication is dominant in the distal and proximal part; (7) Type Ⅲp, duplication of the metatarsal, while neither hallux is clearly dominant in all areas; (8) Type Ⅳ, duplication of the tarsal (Fig. 3).

The combined classification system for preaxial foot polydactyly employs specific abbreviations to denote deformities at various levels: ‘p’ for proximal phalanx and ‘m’ for metatarsal. Type Ⅰ indicates duplication at the distal phalanx level, while Type Ⅰp signifies Type Ⅰ accompanied by a deformity of the proximal phalanx. Type Ⅱ denotes duplication at the proximal phalanx level, and Type Ⅱp/m indicates Type Ⅱ associated with deformities of the proximal phalanx or metatarsal. Type Ⅲ refers to duplication at the metatarsal level, with Type Ⅲp specifically addressing the mismatch between the distal and proximal components of the duplicated hallux. Type Ⅳ pertains to duplication at the tarsal level

The inter-observer reliability of the classification system among five observers and the intra-observer reliability of the same observers are presented in Table 2 and Table 3, respectively. In terms of inter-observer reliability, the overall kappa values for the classification system indicated substantial to perfect agreement, with Fleiss’ kappa values of 0.805 (95%CI:0.805 ~ 0.806) and 0.795 (95%CI:0.795 ~ 0.796) for the first and second readings, respectively. Regarding intra-observer reliability, kappa values grater than 0.9 from the same observer indicated perfect agreement. The primary reason for discrepancies in assessments was attributed to the young age of most children, which resulted in underdeveloped partial ossification centers and cartilage. In addition, variations in assessments were influenced by differences in the clinical experience of the observers.

Derived surgical strategies

For various types of preaxial foot polydactyly, we adopt corresponding surgical approaches. Typically, the tibial hallux exhibits hypoplasia, while the fibular hallux is often retained. For duplicated phalanges, while amputating (A) the supernumerary phalanges, their collateral ligaments should be preserved to reconstruct (RCL) at the tibial interphalangeal joint of the retained hallux for stabilizing joint. Due to potential size mismatch between the base of the retained hallux’s distal or proximal phalanx and the head of the proximal phalanx or metatarsus, osteotomy of the enlarged phalangeal head (OP) or osteotomy of the enlarged metatarsal head (OM) is required. For tendons, the insertion points of the extensor and flexor tendons of the retained hallux may deviate from the midpoint, and tendon transfer (TT) from the excised hallux should be performed to adjust the force vector and correct deviation of the retained hallux. In cases of proximal phalangeal duplication, the abductor hallucis must be reconstructed at the tibial base of the retained hallux’s proximal phalanx, while the adductor hallucis must be reconstructed at the fibular base. For LEB, correction is achieved by removing the lateral epiphysis and performing opening-wedge osteotomy (OWO) with bone grafting from the excised hallux. In types of metatarsal duplication, besides tendon adjustment, reconstruction of transverse metatarsal ligament (RTML) is necessary, particularly using on-top plasty (OTP) for hallux reconstruction of Type Ⅲp. Discussion

Although preaxial polydactyly is not the most common type of foot polydactyly, several researchers have focused on this congenital malformation. To assist clinicians in treating preaxial foot polydactyly, various classification systems have been proposed. The classical classification of polydactyly is based on the level of duplication, similar to the Wassel classification for hand polydactyly [14]. In previous classification systems, some authors have also suggested several preaxial foot classification systems based on duplication levels [5, 7, 8] This classification approach is practical and easily understood, which has contributed to its popularity and common use for classification purposes [15]. While these classification systems are useful for describing polydactyly and provide some guidance for surgical management, the level of duplication alone is insufficient to capture all relevant features. Although the Rotterdam foot classification is highly descriptive, the potential combinations of its four subtypes can be more extensive than expected. Furthermore, the reporting of the Rotterdam classification may become quite complex due to the nuanced gradations between many categories, which can complicate agreement among observers. In the context of hand polydactyly, Chung and colleagues proposed an alternative classification aimed at more accurately describing joint involvement. This classification categorizes preaxial hand polydactyly into four types based on the anatomical morphology at the origin of the extra digit: Type Ⅰ (joint type), Type Ⅱ (single epiphyseal type), Type Ⅲ (osteochondroma-like type), and Type Ⅳ (hypoplastic type) [15]. However, certain forms of complicated polydactyly present challenges for categorization, complicating surgical decision-making. In cases of radial hand polydactyly, on-top-plasty is preferable when the distal portion of the ulnar thumb and the proximal portion of the radial thumb are well-formed, while the proximal portion of the ulnar thumb and the distal portion of the radial thumb are hypoplastic [16]. Although rarely reported, a similar situation can also occur in preaxial foot polydactyly, as confirmed by our clinical practice [17]. In addition, LEB is another malformation associated with medial foot polydactyly that necessitates surgical intervention [6, 10–12]. This rare anomaly affects the ossification growth center of tubular bones in the hand and foot that possess a proximal epiphysis, characterized by an arcuate secondary ossification center that brackets the diaphysis and metaphysis of a phalanx, metacarpal or metatarsal. When this anomaly impacts the first metatarsal, the longitudinal metatarsal epiphyseal bracket results in a short, broad, trapezoidal metatarsal, which can lead to hallux varus deformity [10–12, 18, 19].

This study evaluated the preoperative radiographs of preaxial foot polydactyly at our hospital and to develop a new combined classification system. This classification takes into account the potential combination of polydactyly with other malformations of the medial and/or lateral hallux. The proposed classification system is based on the duplication of bones and variations in adjacent bone structures. A successful classification system must demonstrate both reliability and validity. Hence, our classification system was assessed by five observers, and the resulting inter-observer and intra-observer agreements were found to be substantial. However, differences among observers are inevitable, as some osseous structures of the foot may be underdeveloped in radiographs of children aged 6 to 18 months [8, 20]. Consequently, observers classified preaxial foot polydactyly based on their personal clinical experience with partial radiographs. Besides, surgeon can utilize adjuvant imaging methods such MRI and/or intraoperative arthrography to distinguish various types. In those doubtful or borderline problems, classification system should be serve as guide but not as absolute solution in surgical decision-making process.

Hu et al. conducted a comparative analysis among the Wassel-Flatt, Rotterdam, and Chung classifications [21]. In this investigation, the Wassel-Flatt classification aligns with the Blauth and Olason classification as well as the Watanabe classification, based on the level of duplication level. The Rotterdam classification is comparable to the Rotterdam foot classification due to its various subtypes, while the Chung classification is based on the origin of polydactyly.[8, 14, 15, 21]. The findings indicate that these classification systems are applied more consistently applied when observers possess greater experience. A common limitation of radiographic classifications is that bone development in skeletally immature patients may be insufficient to visualize synostosis or shared joint epiphyses on radiographs, leading to discrepancies in judgment. This limitation significantly impacts the Chung classification, as shared joint epiphyses cannot be identified. However, it exerts less influence on the Wassel-Flatt and Rotterdam classifications. Although the subtypes of the Rotterdam classification are easily distinguishable, the combinations of all subtypes can be quite complex for users. Our classification system not only differentiates preaxial foot polydactyly based on the level of duplication but also incorporates adjacent bones. By focusing on the common aspect of duplication level, our combined classification can be used to compare with previous classification systems, such as the Blauth and Olason classification and the Watanabe classification. Furthermore, the combined classification, which consists of two adjacent bones, effectively mitigates the issues arising from shared joint epiphyses.

Among the eight types, the common ones are Type Ⅰ, Type Ⅰp, Type Ⅱ, and Type Ⅱm, which belong to the distal phalanx and proximal phalanx types. In Type I, the bifurcation at the distal end of the distal phalanx may present no clinical symptoms or only slight widening of the nail, and surgical intervention is not necessary if no significant abnormalities are observed. However, in cases of complete duplication of the distal phalanx, simple removal of the polydactyly may lead to the absence of collateral ligaments on one side, resulting in instability of the interphalangeal joint and lateral deviation deformity of the distal phalanx. Therefore, while excising the hypoplastic hallux, it is essential to preserve and reconstruct the collateral ligaments to stabilize the interphalangeal joint. Type Ⅰp is associated with proximal phalanges anomalies, including LEB and broad proximal phalanx. LEB manifests as abnormal tubular bone morphology, presenting a trapezoidal or even triangular shape, which is highly correlated with hallux varus. Surgical treatment requires excision of the abnormal longitudinal epiphysis, followed by opening-wedge osteotomy with bone grafting [12]. As for broad proximal phalanx deformity, after resection of the polydactyly, the interphalangeal joint becomes unstable. This necessitates additional osteotomy of the proximal phalangeal head to match the length of the distal phalangeal base with that of the proximal phalangeal head, thereby facilitating collateral ligament repair and stabilizing the interphalangeal joint.

Type Ⅱ mostly resembles Wassel type IV-D in hand polydactyly, characterized by duplicated proximal phalanges diverging bilaterally and distal phalanges converging centrally, presenting a crab-claw appearance [22]. Some cases exhibit a divergent pattern with bifurcated halluces. In normal hallux, the abductor hallucis inserts at the tibial base of the proximal phalanx while the adductor hallucis attaches to the fibular side. In proximal phalanx type, these muscles insert on the tibial and fibular aspects of the respective duplicated phalanges. Under these forces, the duplicated proximal phalanges diverge toward the tibial and fibular sides. Surgical treatment typically involves resection of the hypoplastic tibial digit. Except for repair of collateral ligament, particular attention must be paid to reattaching the abductor hallucis insertion to the tibial side of the preserved proximal phalanx to prevent postoperative hallux valgus [22]. In addition, osteotomy of the enlarged metatarsal head is required to match the metatarsophalangeal joint and prevent postoperative hallux valgus. In one case of Type Ⅱ, the metatarsal head osteotomy was not performed during the initial surgery. At the one-year follow-up, a prominent “bump” was observed at the metatarsophalangeal joint. A secondary surgery was performed to correct the deformity with metatarsal head osteotomy, resulting in significant improvement in postoperative appearance (Fig. 4).

(C) Exploration during the second surgery revealed an abnormal metatarsal head. (D) Follow-up results after osteotomy of the metatarsal head performed in the second surgery

In LEB of Type Ⅱm, the deformity commonly develops on the tibial side of the metatarsal, leading to metatarsophalangeal joint tilting and hallux varus. Surgical treatment involves excision of the abnormal tibial epiphysis of the metatarsal and corrective opening-wedge osteotomy. The contracted abductor hallucis muscle also requires lengthening and reconstruction of its insertion on the tibial base the fibular proximal phalanx. For the dislocation of cuneometatasal joint, there are currently no reported cases in the literature. It manifests as an abnormal origin of the first metatarsal, with typical radiographic features including abnormally increased intermetatarsal angle of first and second metatarsal and dislocation of the first metatarsal and entocuneiform. In this study, 2 cases of this type were treated solely by excising the tibial toe and reconstructing the tendon insertion to stabilize the fibular hallux. The AOFAS scores improved from preoperative 55 to postoperative 77 points, representing the lowest postoperative score among all types (Fig. 5). For this category, the surgical plan requires further refinement to prevent hallux valgus, including osteotomy of the first metatarsal to correct the morphology, simultaneous reduction of the cuneometatasal joint dislocation, adjustment of the first metatarsal position.

Type Ⅱp includes LEB and phalangeal fusion. The LEB in Type Ⅱp is extremely rare, and both duplicated proximal phalanges are LEB. The LEB of the tibial phalanx is located on the fibular side, while the LEB of the fibular phalanx is on the tibial side, forming a back-to-back double LEB configuration, resembling a “kiss”, also known as “kissing delta phalanx” [23]. Based on whether the two phalanges are adjacent, it is further classified into separated and non-separated types. In non-separated types, the epiphyses may fuse to form a complex. The non-separated complex continues to widen, leading to deformity. Both separated and non-separated types require the aforementioned surgery. The phalangeal fusion type is characterized by the distal and proximal phalanges merging into a single phalanx with joint stiffness, previously reported in hand polydactyly. In this study, all three cases exhibited fusion of the tibial proximal and distal phalanges. The fused phalanges showed no significant deviation, while the interphalangeal joint of the fibular hallux displayed fibular angulation. The surgical approach involved resection of the hypoplastic fibular digits and reconstruction of collateral ligaments to stabilize the metatarsophalangeal joint. This type consistently involves phalangeal fusion, manifesting both transversely as fusion of duplicated proximal phalanges and longitudinally as fusion of proximal and distal phalanges.

The distinction between Type Ⅲp and Type Ⅲ lies in whether the proximal and distal development of the duplicated halluces are consistent. Type Ⅲ is characterized by well-developed proximal and distal tibial hallux, hypoplastic fibular hallux with conical-shaped metatarsal bases [8]. Surgical treatment involves resection of the fibular hallux, reconstruction of the adductor tendon insertion, repair of the collateral ligaments, and tightening of the transverse metatarsal ligament to prevent the first metatarsus adductus. Type Ⅲp presents with stable cuneometatarsal joint in the tibial hallux but unstable interphalangeal and metatarsophalangeal joints, severe fibular deviation of the proximal and distal phalanges, while the fibular hallux exhibits unstable cuneometatarsal joint but stable interphalangeal and metatarsophalangeal joints. The surgical approach employs an on-top plasty technique, involving osteotomy at metatarsal neck of the tibial hallux to remove the distal portion, and osteotomy of the fibular hallux to transpose the stable interphalangeal and metatarsophalangeal joints to the tibial metatarsal [24] Similar to Type Ⅲ, this category also requires tightening of the transverse metatarsal ligament. With this surgical approach, 3 cases showed improvement in AOFAS scores from 76, 40, 49 to 93, 85, 82 respectively. A case, while also undergoing on-top plasty, involved osteotomy at the proximal phalanges with transposition of the fibular interphalangeal joint to the tibial proximal phalanx, along with tightening of the collateral ligaments of the metatarsophalangeal joint and reconstruction of tendon to stabilize the joint. A 10-month postoperative follow-up revealed medial angulation of the metatarsophalangeal joint with hallux valgus presentation (Fig. 6). We recommend performing the osteotomy at metatarsal neck with on-top plasty, which can effectively prevent postoperative hallux valgus.

Type Ⅳ should be distinguished from central and postaxial polydactyly. The differentiation can be made based on the presence of two phalanges in the hallux and the characteristic of the metatarsal ossification center located at the base. Surgical treatment involves resection of the excrescent hallux and removal of excess cuneiform bone to prevent persistent widening of the midfoot. This type exhibits relatively well-developed osseous structures and stable joints. With attention to the aforementioned technical details, the prognosis is favorable.

The classification system proposed in this study can to some extent guide surgical decision-making for the treatment of preaxial foot polydactyly. The AOFAS score improved from 61.000 (57.3, 64.3) to 89.000 (87.3, 90.0) after implementing the derived surgical strategies, reaching an excellent level with statistically significant differences between preoperative and postoperative scores. Most of these surgical techniques have been proven effective for deformity correction in literature, while those without prior documentation also demonstrated satisfactory postoperative outcomes in this study. However, the surgical strategies are not set in stone and must be tailored to the individual patient’s condition.

Conclusion

In our study, we proposed a combined classification system for preaxial foot ploydactyly based on the skeletal morphology observed in radiographs. The statistical analysis of this classification system demonstrated substantial agreement among the observers, indicating that it is a valuable and reliable tool for describing preaxial foot polydactyly. Therefore, we recommend the application of this combined classification system for classifying preaxial foot polydactyly. Meanwhile, the derived surgical strategies have also achieved satisfactory therapeutic effects. Although some surgical methods are not yet fully developed, they hold certain reference and guiding significance. This study has limitations as it is a single-center study, and future multi-center research is needed to further validate its reliability and practicality. Since the classification system is based on X-rays and may contain errors, it serves as a guidance recommendation, with the surgical strategies being reference options, and the final decision should be made according to the intraoperative situation.
