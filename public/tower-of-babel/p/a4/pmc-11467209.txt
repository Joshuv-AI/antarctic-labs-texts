Limited evidence for anatomical contacts between intestinal GLP-1 cells and vagal neurons in male mice
Authors: Newton Cao, Warda Merchant, Laurent Gautron
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The communication between intestinal Glucagon like peptide 1 (GLP-1)-producing cells and the peripheral nervous system has garnered renewed interest considering the availability of anti-obesity and anti-diabetic approaches targeting GLP-1 signaling. While it is well-established that intestinal GLP-1 cells can exert influence through paracrine mechanisms, recent evidence suggests the possible existence of synaptic-like connections between GLP-1 cells and peripheral neurons, including those of the vagus nerve. In this study, using a reporter Phox2b-Cre-Tomato mouse model and super-resolution confocal microscopy, we demonstrated that vagal axons made apparent contacts with less than 0.5% of GLP-1 cells. Moreover, immunohistochemistry combined with super-resolution confocal microscopy revealed abundant post-synaptic density 95 (PSD-95) immunoreactivity within the enteric plexus of the lower intestines of C57/BL6 mice, with virtually none in its mucosa. Lastly, utilizing RNAScope in situ hybridization in the lower intestines of mice, we observed that GLP-1 cells expressed generic markers of secretory cells such as Snap25 and Nefm, but neither synaptic markers such as Syn1 and Nrxn2, nor glutamatergic markers such as Slc17a7. Through theoretical considerations and a critical review of the literature, we concluded that intestinal GLP-1 cells primarily communicate with vagal neurons through paracrine mechanisms, rather than synaptic-like contacts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-74000-8.

Introduction

Glucagon-like peptide 1 (GLP-1)-secreting cells, commonly known as L-cells, are specialized enteroendocrine cells primarily located in the lower intestines of mammals, including humans1–7. These cells have been extensively studied for their capacity to release GLP-18, a peptide renowned for its incretin and anorectic effects9–12. The biology of GLP-1 cells has garnered renewed interest due to the current approval of GLP-1 analogs for the pharmacological treatment of type-2 diabetes and obesity13–15. In addition, bariatric surgery is known to be associated with exacerbated GLP-1 release and increase number of intestinal GLP-1 cells16–18. Consequently, a comprehensive understanding of how GLP-1 cells communicate with other cell types and organs in the body holds the potential to elucidate the pathophysiology of chronic metabolic diseases and enhance existing treatments.

GLP-1 is released during the postprandial phase and exerts its effects on organs distant from the circulation19,20. However, due to its short half-life, bioactive GLP-1 is detectable at a greater concentration in intestinal plasma and lymph than in the general circulation21, suggesting that GLP-1 cells exerts local paracrine effects. In particular, the gastrointestinal mucosa is richly innervated by vagal, spinal, and enteric afferent fibers22–25. Importantly, the receptor for GLP-1 (GLP-1R) is abundantly expressed by vagal afferents12,26,27 and physiological studies indicate that peripheral nerves are, at least in part, necessary for both endogenous GLP-1 and administered GLP-1 receptor agonists’ satiating and incretin effects12,28–32. Contrary to this, intraperitoneal administration of GLP-1 agonists does not stimulate vagal afferents33, only a small subset of GLP-1R-expressing vagal afferents innervate the intestinal mucosa where GLP-1 cells reside33,34, and ablation of vagal GLP-1R neurons doesn’t prevent GLP-1R’s satiating effects35. The latter observations suggest that GLP-1R-expressing vagal afferents may not be important mediators of intestinal GLP-1 satiating effects. Instead, it may be that intestinal GLP-1 cells communicate with vagal afferents through the secretion of other peptides and molecules than GLP-1, such as Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)36.

Regardless of which neurotransmitter(s) mediate L-cells’ interaction with local nerves, the conventional view posits that enteroendocrine signals elicit responses in a paracrine manner in neurons innervating the intestinal mucosa37. Supporting this view, many anterograde tracing and immunohistochemistry studies, with the exception of two studies38,39, have consistently shown that axons in the intestinal mucosa closely approach enteroendocrine cells without establishing contacts22,24,27,40–44. Moreover, ultrastructural and electrochemical examination of enteroendocrine cells, in the case of serotonin-producing cells, is consistent with paracrine communication45. Electron microscopy, the sole method capable of ascertaining the presence of a synaptic contact, defines a synapse by a presynaptic compartment containing small-diameter secretory vesicles juxtaposed to a postsynaptic electron-dense zone46. For decades, investigators have sought structures resembling synapses in the intestinal mucosa, but without success47–54. The observations have been limited to axons with varicosities traveling in the lamina propria, occasionally making passing contacts with various cells, but not epithelial and enteroendocrine cells themselves53,54. In 1985, Wade and colleagues reported one instance of contact between a nerve fiber and an enteroendocrine cell, refraining from using the term “synapse” and concluding that “no evidence for direct (i.e., synaptic) innervation of enteroendocrine cells was found in this study of the adult mouse duodenum”55. The only notable exception is the observation made by Newson and colleagues in 197956, where an image of (only) one putative “synapse” with a gut endocrine cell was provided. However, the image quality was poor. Hence, the evidence supporting that enteroendocrine cells are contacted by synapses remains circumstantial.

Given the above literature, it is surprising that a recent proposal suggests certain enteroendocrine cells, including GLP-1 cells, communicate with vagal afferents through synaptic-like transmission38,39. Initially, these authors described a specialized cytoplasmic protrusion from the base of GLP-1 cells termed the “neuropod”57. Subsequent high-quality ultrastructural data revealed that the neuropod is a thin extension of enteroendocrine cells that closely approach axons and glial cells in the intestinal lamina propria58. However, the authors did not provide images of morphologically identifiable synapses at the ultrastructural level. They also presented data suggesting the presence of putative synaptic markers in GLP-1 and enterochromaffin cells39,58,59, as well as viral tracing indicating synaptic-like connections with a small number of vagal afferents38,59. Here, we reassessed the presence of putative synaptic markers in the lower intestines of mice and the anatomical proximity of GLP-1-cells with vagal axons.

Methods

Mice

Phox2b-Cre-Tomato mice were obtained by breeding Phox2b-Cre (stock#016223) and Tomato reporter mice (stock# 007909) animals purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor). Wild-type (WT) mice on a C57BL/6J background mice were also purchased from the Jackson Laboratory (stock# 000664). All mice were housed in ventilated cages (with igloo and nestlets) in a barrier animal facility of the University of Texas Southwestern medical Center (UTSW) with a temperature- (22 °C) and humidity- (55%) controlled environment, under a 12:12 h light–dark cycle. All methods were performed in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations. Procedures were approved by the UTSW Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) beforehand (Protocols# 2016-101605 and 2017-101994). Mice used in this study were group-housed males between the age of 5–10 weeks fed ad libitum on standard chow diet Teklad 2016. We confirm that procedures were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines60. Per the SAGER guidelines61, we would like to report that this study focused on males because, to the best of our knowledge, no previous studies have examined sex differences in gut innervation. Thus, it remains unclear whether sex is relevant to the interpretation of our results.

Genetic tracing study and super-resolution confocal microscopy

Thick intestinal rings were prepared using standard protocol for immunohistochemistry. A total of 4 Phox2b-Cre-Tomato mice were anesthetized with an overdose of ketamine/xylazine (500/80 mg/kg, i.p.), before being immediately transcardiacally perfused with 10% formalin (Sigma) as described before27. Fixed lower intestines (on each side of the cecum, ~2 cm of ileum and colon) were removed and cleaned. After a 2 h postfixation, intestines were incubated in a 30% sucrose solution, frozen on dry ice, and stored at -80 °C. Using a cryostat, free-floating thick transversal sections of the ileum and colon were produced at 50 μm to increase our ability to reconstruct entire L-cells in a 3D manner. Free-floating intestinal rings were stained with primary antibodies against RFP and GLP-1 (See Table 1) diluted in 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS (pH 7.4). Tissue was next incubated with secondary antibodies anti-chicken biotinylated, streptavidin AlexaFluor 594, and anti-rabbit AlexaFluor 488 (See Table 1). After PBS rinses, slices were mounted using CFM3 (Citifluor, a division of Electron Microscopy Sciences, USA), a mounting medium that offers some degree of tissue clearing62. Of note, the immunostaining patterns were consistent with the known distribution of L-cells and vagal axons within the intestinal tract. As shown before23, RFP immunostaining overlapped completely with that of native Tomato, but resulted in a brighter staining and less bleaching. Of note, we chose not use blocking steps during immunohistochemistry because, in our hands and that of others63, it rarely seems to improve the quality of our observations. Using the Zeiss LSM880 confocal microscopy, we collected digital z-stacks of intestinal rings at 20x. On digital images, GLP-1 cells were randomly selected and the percentage of cells with apparent Tomato-positive axonal contacts was evaluated. A subset of GLP-1 cells with suspected contacts were further imaged using the super-resolution Zeiss LSM880 Airyscan module. This imaging modality has a resolution of up to 120 and 350 nm in the lateral and axial dimensions, respectively64. Albeit not sufficient to visualize synapses, this level of resolution allowed us to accurately evaluate the proximity of Tomato axons and the enteroendocrine neuropod of GLP-1 cells. Briefly, the 63x objective was used with 30-degree oil. We used the ILEX setup for multichannel, alignment, and the collection of a z-stack typically consisting of 50 plans across one GLP-1 cell. The same laser power was used for all cells. We reconstructed 24 cells with potential contacts across ileum and colon.

PSD95 immunohistochemistry and super-resolution confocal microscopy

A total of 3 WT mice were perfused exactly as described before27. Brain, pituitary gland, liver, and lower intestines (on each side of the cecum, ~2 cm of ileum and colon) were post-fixed 2 h, before being incubated into 30% sucrose overnight. Intestinal samples were frozen as a Swiss roll preparation and cut with a cryostat at 16 μm. Swiss rolls allowed us to maximize the amount of tissue analyzed. All samples were either incubated with PBS triton alone or PBS triton with a rabbit antiserum against recombinant PSD95 (See Table 1) for 16 h at room temperature. By western blot, this antibody detects a single band at 75 kDa in SH-SY5Y cell lysate, which is absent from knockout cells. On the next day, slides were rinsed and incubated with a goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody coupled to AlexaFluor647 (See Table 1) for 3 h at room temperature. Slides were rinsed, counterstained with DAPI and a coverslip was added on Prolong antifade medium. Of note, because the intestines often contained a lot of autofluorescence and debris easily confounded for synapses, AlexaFluor647 offered near infrared imaging with minimal autofluorescence and background. Moreover, our immunostaining was consistent with a synaptic localization and only detectable in tissues with synapses. Digital images at 20x were collected using a Leica microscope LSM880 with a 633 nm laser. Digital images at 63x (oil) were taken using the LSM980 with an Ayriscan 2 module for enhanced resolution of up to 140 nm in both lateral and axial dimensions. We selected the SR4Y option in Smart-setup. Acquisition parameters were chosen to avoid capturing unwanted background fluorescence and laser power was selected (< 2%) to avoid bleaching and pixel saturation. Once satisfied with the acquisition parameters, they were uniformly applied across samples of the same experiment. A total of 52 field views of the muscle and mucosa (both colon and ileum) were taken from 3 mice. Using ImageJ Fiji (NIH), we measured the mean intensity of signals per field, background from unstained tissue was separately measured. Data were presented as mean intensity of PSD95 immunoreactivity ± S.E.M.

Chromogenic RNAScope of lower intestines

WT mice were perfused exactly as described before. Intestines were postfixed for 24 h at 4 °C before being switched to 30% sucrose for an additional 24 h at 4 °C. Samples were cut as 16 μm thick rings using a cryostat and collected on SuperFrost slides. Iin situ hybridization (ISH) RNAscope procedure was performed on ileum and colon samples exactly as described before27 using the ACD procedure and reagents from the RNAscope® 2.5 HD chromogenic detection Kit (FastRed). This kit was chosen on the basis that it provides excellent sensitivity and allows both fluorescent and brightfield stains. All probes (see Table 1) were applied to the tissue for 2 h at 40 °C using the ACD hybridization oven. Signals were identified as large red dots by either brightfield or fluorescence microscopy. Slides were counterstained with hematoxylin. Brightfield images were acquired with a Leica LAS X microscope equipped with a digital camera. Estimates of gene expression were produced as follows. Using ImageJ Fiji (NIH), the muscle and mucosal layers were outlined, and their respective surface area measured. The relative amount of RNAScope signals in each compartment was also measured using the Mean tool. The ratio of mean signals per surface area was considered the density of gene expression. For each gene, this process was repeated in approximately 5 tissue sections from 3 different mice, and the data were averaged as density of signals (± S.E.M). To combine Snap25 detection with GLP-1 immunostaining, chromogenic RNAScope was performed first, followed by an immunostaining for GLP-1 exactly as described before (see also Table 1). Digital z-stacks were collected with a Zeiss LSM 880 confocal microscope (20x and 63x). The percentage of GLP-1 cells positive for Snap25 was evaluated on digital images of 5 tissue sections from 3 different mice. Data were expressed as percentage of cells coexpressing Snap25 and GLP-1 immunoreactivity (± S.E.M).

Software, database, and statistics

Adobe Photoshop software (version 2021) was used to annotate images, apply scale bars, and adjust image brightness and contrast. Of note, changes in contrast were uniformly applied to all images belonging to the same figure. Per the journal editorial policies, no other digital alterations were allowed. GraphPad Prism 9.0.0 was utilized for graph production. Notably, our semi-quantitative RNAscope data were not subjected to statistical analysis as we were not comparing experimental groups. However, estimates of PSD95 immunoreactivity were analyzed by a one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. ImageJ Fiji (NIH) was employed to convert digital images to TIF format with a 300 dpi resolution, count cells, and evaluate surface area and intensity of immunoreactivity. The Zeiss Zen Black software was utilized to produce 3D renderings and videos and measure the shortest distance between Tomato-positive axons and reconstructed GLP-1 cells. Zeiss Zen Blue was used for super-resolution image acquisition with the Airyscan2 module and for image processing and export as TIF. Leica LAS X microscope software was used for acquiring brightfield digital images. Adobe After Effects (version 2023) was utilized to produce supplementary video files. Tabula muris publicly available transcriptomics data were retrieved from https://tabula-muris.ds.czbiohub.org/. EndNote X9 was employed for reference indexing, and PubMed (NIH) was used for literature search. Lastly, we would like to report that all methods and data analysis were performed in accordance with the editorial policies and relevant guidelines.

Results

Contacts between vagal axons and GLP-1 cells are exceptionally rare

Anterograde tracing was conducted to examine the anatomical relationship between intestinal L-cells and vagal axons using the Phox2b-Cre-tdTomato mouse model (Fig. 1A). This model provides unparalleled labeling of axons in the periphery, including all vagal afferent and motor neurons as well as small subsets of enteric and sympathetic motor neurons38,65. To our knowledge, the innervation in the intestinal mucosa, including the ileum and colon, is predominantly vagal afferents, with minimal motor neurons present66,67. Of note, Glp1r-expressing neurons are all labeled in the Phox2b-Cre-tdTomato mouse model (Supplementary Fig. 1). Thick sections of the ileum and colon stained for GLP-1 showed the anticipated rich innervation by Tomato-positive axons, which ramified within the lamina propria (Fig. 1B). Most GLP-1 cells were not observed near vagal axons (Fig. 1C). However, at low magnification (20x), a subgroup of GLP-1-positive cells appeared to be in proximity to passing axons, suggesting potential “contacts” (Fig. 1C). The percentage of GLP-1 cells in the ileum and colon with apparent contacts approached 17% of all counted cells (Fig. 2A). However, upon reconstructing a sample of GLP-1 cells with apparent contact at high resolution (63x and Airyscan module), Tomato-labeled axons approached L-cells very closely without direct contact (Figs. 1D, E, F and G and 2B). The 3D reconstruction of 24 cells in both the ileum and colon revealed an average distance of approximately 5 μm between GLP-1 cells and the nearest Tomato axons (Fig. 2B, C). A supplemental video illustrates how a cell with apparent contact at low magnification, upon super-resolution reconstruction and 3D rotation, did not truly contact nearby axons (Supplementary video 1). Only in two instances did we observe what resembled an apparent contact between a Tomato fiber and a GLP-1 cell, accounting for less than 0.5% of all counted GLP-1 cells (Fig. 2A; C). We concluded that contacts between vagal axons and GLP-1 cells are exceptionally rare. Furthermore, using confocal microscopy, it is impossible to say whether such rare contacts are of synaptic nature.

PSD95 immunoreactivity is undetectable in the intestinal mucosa

PSD-95, recognized as one of the most specific and commonly used histological markers of synapses68,69, was investigated using a well-validated antibody in the lower intestines of WT mice. As anticipated, PSD95 immunoreactivity was uniformly observed as small puncta across the mouse brain, but not in tissues devoid of synapses such as the anterior pituitary gland and the liver (Supplementary Fig. 2).

When the primary antibody was omitted, intestinal sections showed no immunoreactivity but background staining (Fig. 3A). In contrast, when the antibody was applied, immunoreactivity was apparent in the entire enteric nervous system at the level of the myenteric and submucosal plexuses, as well as bundles within the muscularis (Fig. 3B). At higher magnification and super-resolution confocal microscopy, clusters of small puncta were discernible across all enteric ganglia (Fig. 3C). These PSD95-positive sites presumably correspond to synaptic contacts made by vagal afferents and enteric neurons onto other enteric neurons. In contrast, no obvious immunoreactivity was observed in the entire mucosa (Fig. 3D). Occasional debris and small dots were seen, but they could be distinguished from what is observed in unstained tissues. Estimates of immunoreactivity confirmed that the muscularis contained significant immunoreactivity above background levels, whereas the mucosa was devoid of significant immunoreactivity (Fig. 3E).

GLP-1 cells do not express neuronal-specific markers

It was previously proposed that the enrichment of neuronal markers (Syn1, Nefm, etc.) in enteroendocrine cells demonstrates their ‘synaptic features”38. In WT mice, we next assessed the expression and distribution of select synaptic markers previously found to be enriched in enteroendocrine cells using chromogenic ISH. As expected, the negative probe (DapB) did not produce detectable signals (Fig. 4A, H). Additionally, no Scl17a7 expression was observed in our gut samples (Fig. 4B, H). However, we found selective expression of the receptor for Glp-1 (Glp1r) in a subset of myenteric neurons (Fig. 4C, C’) and within the mucosa (Fig. 4C’’, H). Interestingly, Glp1r was not produced by epithelial cells but rather by clusters of small cells primarily located within the lamina propria, consistent with previous findings70. In the same animals, we observed that the synaptic markers Nefm, Snap25, Syn1, and Nrxn2 were highly enriched in the muscularis and submucosa of both the ileum and colon (Fig. 4D-G, H). Strong ISH signals were present in nearly all enteric ganglia. However, ISH signals for Nrxn2 and Syn1 were completely absent in the mucosa (Fig. 4E, F, H). Only Snap25 and Nefm were observed in sparse cells within the epithelial layers (Fig. 4D, G, G’, H). We further confirmed the enrichment of Snap25 in L-cells using RNAscope combined with GLP-1 immunofluorescence (Fig. 5). In both the ileum and colon, numerous L-cells exhibited signals for Snap25 (Fig. 5A-D’). While most L-cells expressed Snap25, approximately 45% of Snap25-expressing cells were positive for GLP-1, suggesting its production by other epithelial cells (Fig. 5E).

Overall, our histological findings are consistent with publicly available transcriptomic data of the mouse large intestines obtained from the Tabula Muris71(Fig. 6). Notably, transcriptomic data also reveals the expression of many synaptic markers in various asynaptic cell types, including, for instance, Nrnx2 in macrophages and oligodendrocyte precursors (Fig. 6). Hence, we reassessed the expression of the aforementioned markers in tissues with and without synapses. All genes were expressed in neurons of the nodose ganglion (Fig. 7A-E), a tissue known to contain synapses-forming neurons. None of these markers were found in the liver, an asynaptic organ (Fig. 7F-J). However, the anterior lobe of the pituitary gland exhibited high enrichment of all the same markers (Fig. 7K-O). Considering that the mammalian anterior pituitary gland is minimally innervated72, neither Snap25 nor Nefm can be designated as definitive markers of synapses-forming cells.

Discussion

Our study identified presumptive vagal axons closely associated with a small number of L-cells, initially suggesting apparent contacts at low magnification. However, upon closer examination using super-resolution microscopy and 3D rendering, true contacts were found to be exceedingly rare. This serves as a cautionary note that apparent axonal contacts at low magnification may be deceiving, and that enhanced microscopy may be needed to reveal true contacts. It is important to note that the Phox2b-Cre line does not label all extrinsic and enteric neurons supplying the mucosa, raising the possibility that spinal and enteric neurons could still make contacts with L-cells, a facet yet to be definitively established. It is also possible that vagal axons might contact other types of intestinal cells. For instance, it was previously shown that 5HT3R-GFP-positive axons contact intestinal enterochromaffin cells39. Nevertheless, 5HT3R-labeled elements did not convincingly resemble axonal fibers and, furthermore, it must be noted that 5HT3R-GFP doesn’t exclusively labels axons in the intestines73. Instead, two recent works have provided compelling histological evidence that nerve fibers do not truly contact enterochromaffin cells in the mouse colon24,42. Very recently, another anterograde tracing study in the mouse corroborated our own observations by demonstrating the close proximity of vagal afferents to intestinal enterochromaffin cells, without establishing direct contacts44. It is important to acknowledge that using a reporter model for L-cells (e.g., GFP reporter) would have theoretically provided a more precise delineation of their cell bodies. However, GLP-1 immunoreactivity is expected to accumulate closely at potential sites of contact with axons. Additionally, since most enteroendocrine cells are situated far from vagal axons, we anticipate that employing such a model would not have altered the conclusions of our study.

A synapse, defined as a narrow point of contact characterized by asymmetric junctions known as the presynaptic and postsynaptic compartments, is evident through electron microscopy by distinct electron density on either side of the junction68,74. According to the textbook, synapses only exist between neurons, and specialized sensory cells such as gustatory cells75–77 and hair cells78. The synaptic hypothesis of enteroendocrine communication posits that axons within the intestinal lamina propria, presumably of vagal afferent origin, occasionally establish synapse-like connections with the neuropod of enteroendocrine cells including GLP-1 cells38,39,59. However, electron micrographs proving the existence of such synapses are still lacking, presumably due to their rarity. Obtaining definitive proof of synapses would require systematic reconstruction using serial electron microscopy on a large population of enteroendocrine cells. However, this approach has remained unfeasible thus far. Instead, we can only rely on indirect evidence to study these putative synapses including histology and tracing. One prior report described PSD95-positive elements contacting intestinal enterochromaffin cells of the mouse39, but neither provided quantitative data nor proof of staining specificity. In our opinion, the only provided image of PSD95 immunoreactivity didn’t convincingly resemble a synaptic contact due to its very large size (~ 5 μm). Here, we couldn’t find evidence of PSD95 immunoreactivity in the intestinal mucosa of the mouse, despite obvious PSD95-positive synapses in the enteric nervous system. Nonetheless, it may be that synapses are so scarce in the mucosa, that their detection remained nearly impossible by light microscopy.

Whereas we confirmed the expression of Snap25 and Nefm in L-cells, it can easily be argued these are markers of both neurons and asynaptic secretory cell types. For instance, we found abundant Snap25 expression in the anterior pituitary, a tissue without synapses. Importantly, synaptic markers have been described in a wide range of non-neuronal cell types not making synaptic contacts including adipocytes79, thymic cells80, HeLa and NIH/3T3 cells81, microglia82, endocrine cells of the pituitary gland83,84, rat testicular cells85, kidney cells86, and even human sperm87. In other words, the term “synaptic marker” is a misnomer for many of the markers found to be enriched in L-cells since it is not possible to deduce the ability of L-cells to form synapses solely by examining their molecular profile. On the other hand, we failed to confirm the expression of Slc17a7, Nrxn2, and Syn1 in L-cells, which were previously described as markers of enteroendocrine cells38,58,88. Interestingly, the absence of Slc17a7 indicates that glutamate is unlikely to serve as a signaling molecule released by L-cells. In agreement with our data, two recent transcriptomics studies failed to detect vesicular glutamate transporters in any enteroendocrine cell types4,89. This contrasts with GLP-1 producing neurons in in the rat brain, which are glutamatergic90. The discrepancy between our data and previous literature remains unexplained but could be due to either contamination by PYY-positive enteric neurons91 or altered expression profile upon digestion and cell sorting. Perhaps, our RNAScope was not sensitive enough to detect lowly expressed markers. However, even if we had identified some expression, it would not alter our interpretation that there are no molecular markers indicative of whether a cell forms synapses. Further in-depth analysis of the transcriptome of enteroendocrine cells is warranted to evaluate their degree of similarity to other cell types4,92. Lastly, a technical limitation of the current RNAScope work may be relevant, considering that in vitro co-culture experiments have demonstrated the ability of vagal sensory neurons to respond to enteroendocrine-derived glutamate. However, it cannot be excluded that cultured cells may behave differently from their in vivo counterparts.

Of note, previous research demonstrated the travel of retrograde neurotropic viruses from L-cells to vagal afferents in mice, presumably through a synaptic route38. However, the number of virally transduced vagal afferents was small (3 neurons in only 2 mice)37, prompting questions about the significance of such modest results. More importantly, although rabies and pseudorabies particles are known to travel mainly via a trans-synaptic route between neurons93, there is no evidence precluding the possibility that enteroendocrine cells release viral particles in a paracrine manner. Instead, rabies and pseudorabies particles can spread in vitro by exocytosis and budding mechanisms not requiring synaptic connections94–97. This is a likely scenario of transmission between secretory cells and peripheral neurons, especially considering that salivary gland cells release rabies particles, with saliva being the primary mode of rabies transmission between individuals98,99. Likewise, pseudorabies particles are released in nasal secretions by infected epithelial cells98 before entering neuronal free endings of the nasal mucosa99.

One could rightly argue that enteroendocrine synapses are so sparse that they could have been easily missed by us and others. Then, if such synapses are so rare, what could possibly be their functional significance? In fact, many significant theoretical challenges arise from the synaptic model of enteroendocrine communication. Whereas synapses normally form between stationary cells, cells lining the gastrointestinal epithelium undergo continuous migration at a relatively rapid velocity100. Advanced cell migration studies in the mouse intestines indicate a migration velocity ranging from 3 to 8 μm per hour101–103 and an average lifespan of 4 days for enteroendocrine cells104. Other studies have indicated that enteroendocrine cells may migrate at a rate 5 times slower than other epithelial cells105. If this is correct, enteroendocrine could have a migration velocity ranging from 0.6 to 1.6 μm per hour. Regardless, synaptogenesis is a multifaceted and slow process involving intricate molecular scaffolding and anchoring. In vivo studies indicate that new functional excitatory synapses can form within 1 to 2 h under optimal conditions106. Given an optimistic scenario where a synapse develops in roughly one hour, its enteroendocrine target, localized somewhere on the neuropod would have already migrated at least half of one µm away. Hypothetically assuming a synapse rapidly materializes between an axon and the neuropod (considering their perfect alignment), its transient existence would be dictated by this ongoing cellular migration. Lastly, it’s important to acknowledge that synaptic communication operates incomparably faster than paracrine communication. However, the imperative for such rapid signaling seems unnecessary for most gastrointestinal functions, which tend to be inherently slow-paced107.

While the histological data presented here do not definitively prove the absence of synapses between L-cells and vagal fibers, they contribute to the growing body of literature indicating that enteroendocrine cells primarily communicate through paracrine mechanisms.
