Effect of monkeypox virus B14R protein fragments binding to IL-1β on apoptosis in HeLa cells
Authors: Xiaojuan Zhu, Yunfeng Shan, Qiao Qiao, Fengjuan Shi, Kangchen Zhao, Jinlei Guo, Tao Wu, Shuo Ning, Heng Rong, Yuhan Ding, Yiyue Ge, Lunbiao Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInterleukin-1 beta (IL-1β) is a key mediator of antiviral immunity, and B14R proteins encoded by monkeypox virus (MPV) can interfere with its signaling. This study investigates the differences in IL-1β-binding capacity and apoptotic regulatory effects between two B14R protein isoforms encoded by MPV clades: the truncated B14R-180 (West African clade) and the full-length B14R-326 (Central African clade).MethodsThis study employed bioinformatics approaches to predict the binding affinity between B14R and IL-1β, and validated the findings through Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA), flow cytometry, Quantitative RT-PCR (qRT-PCR), and Western blot analyses.Resultsthe research demonstrates that B14R-180 exhibits superior binding efficiency to IL-1β compared to B14R-326, attributed to more hydrogen bonds (9 vs. 7), lower binding free energy (ΔG = -12.4 vs. -2.0 kJ/mol), and optimized binding interfaces. ELISA confirms the significantly higher binding affinity of B14R-180 for IL-1β (P < 0.05), with stronger competitive inhibition by anti-IL-1β antibodies in vaccinia-immune sera. Flow cytometry reveals that the combination of B14R-180 and IL-1β significantly reduces cell apoptosis rates in HeLa cells after 48 h (h) (P < 0.05), whereas B14R-326 has no significant effect. Subsequent qRT-PCR indicates that the B14R-180/IL-1β co-treatment downregulates the transcriptional expression of caspase-3, caspase-8, caspase-9, and caspase-10 (P < 0.001). Notably, caspase-3 exhibited dual-level suppression, with significant reductions in both mRNA and protein abundance.ConclusionsThese results reveal distinct anti-apoptotic profiles of B14R isoforms and suggest that B14R-180 may enhance MPV immune evasion through high-affinity IL-1β binding. These findings provide critical mechanistic insights to guide the development of MPV vaccines and targeted therapeutic strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12985-025-02962-w.Keywords: Monkeypox virus (MPV), Interleukin-1β (IL-1β), B14R-326 protein, B14R-180 protein, Apoptosis

Introduction

Mpox is a zoonotic infectious disease caused by the monkeypox virus (MPV), characterized by symptoms such as fever, lymphadenopathy, and generalized vesiculopustular eruptions. MPV, a member of the genus Orthopoxvirus within the family Poxviridae, is currently divided into two evolutionary clades: Clade I (Congo Basin or Central African clade) and Clade II (West African clade) [1]. Among these, Clade I has a case fatality rate of up to 10.6%, whereas Clade II (including subclades IIa and IIb) has a case fatality rate of approximately 3.6% [2]. Since May 2022, MPV has been spreading globally, prompting the World Health Organization (WHO) to declare the outbreak a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC) on two occasions. Currently, the newly emerging Ib subclade, which is highly transmissible, is primarily circulating in Africa and Europe. In contrast, the predominant global strain is the IIb subclade, mainly transmitted through sexual contact among humans. In China, low-level transmission of the IIb subclade has been observed, while the Ib subclade poses a significant importation risk but has not yet established local transmission [3]. At present, no vaccines have been clinically approved for exclusive use against MPV infection. Although smallpox vaccines confer partial cross-protection against MPV, the cessation of smallpox vaccination campaigns has left a large proportion of the population susceptible to MPV infection [4–6]. The limited understanding of MPV pathogenesis has significantly hindered the development of effective vaccines and antiviral therapeutics.

Like other orthopoxviruses, MPV encodes multiple proteins that target key inflammatory molecules, disrupting host cell signaling pathways and thereby modulating viral recognition and apoptosis [7–9]. Notably, IL-1β-mediated apoptosis plays a crucial role in viral pathogenesis [10]– [11]. Shchelkunova et al. suggested that IL-1β-binding protein of MPV may contribute to its lower pathogenicity compared to variola virus [12]. This finding suggests that MPV could potentially evade the immune system by modulating IL-1β-mediated apoptotic pathways, thereby affecting its pathogenic potential. Previous studies have demonstrated that MPV B14R is a homolog of BR209 in vaccinia virus and B16R in variola virus; these homologs can encode 326-amino-acid (aa) protein that binds IL-1β to suppress downstream signaling and inhibit host cell apoptosis [13–15]. Chen et al. confirmed that the Central African clade encodes a full-length 326-amino-acid B14R protein (B14R-326), capable of binding to IL-1β [14, 16, 17]. In contrast, the West African clade strains that emerged after 2022 exhibit significant alterations in the B14R gene, predominantly encoding a truncated 180-amino-acid protein at the N-terminus (B14R-180) [16, 18]. The potential disparity in IL-1β-binding capacity between the two distinct-length B14R isoforms and its functional consequences on apoptosis mediated by IL-1β remain to be elucidated.

To explore the potential differences in IL-1β binding affinity among various truncated forms of B14R and to examine the effects of B14R-326 and B14R-180 on apoptosis, we employed homology modeling and receptor-ligand docking to predict hydrogen bonding patterns and binding free energy between each protein and IL-1β ligands. The B1 4R-326 protein corresponds to OPG201 (historically designated B14R) encoded by the Central African clade (Clade I) reference genome (GenBank accession number AAL40633.1, Zaire-96 strain), whereas B14R-180 is derived from MPXVgp175 encoded by the West African clade (Clade II) reference genome (GenBank accession number URP85121.1, 2022 USA strain). The 2022 USA strain was chosen for this study due to its well-annotated genome and direct relevance to the recent global mpox outbreak. Using these sequences, we expressed and purified both B14R-326 and B14R-180 to experimentally evaluate their binding interactions with IL-1β and their influence on apoptosis in HeLa cells. These findings provide mechanistic insights into MPV pathogenesis and may facilitate the identification of novel therapeutic and vaccine targets.

Materials and methods

Cell

HeLa cell line (RRID: CVCL_0030) was kindly provided by Nanjing Medical University. HeLa cells were grown in Dulbecco’ s Modified Eagle’ s Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal calf serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA).

Collection of human serum

The study complied with relevant ethical guidelines and was approved by the Jiangsu Provincial Center for Disease Control Ethics Committee (approval number: JSJK2025-005-01). Human serum samples were collected from a local hospital in Jiangsu Province. Serum samples were categorized into two groups based on smallpox vaccination status: individuals potentially vaccinated against smallpox (born before 1980) and those unlikely to have been vaccinated (born after 1980). The samples were isolated by centrifugation of coagulated whole blood, and aliquoted for storage at −80 °C.

Synthesis and purification of target proteins

The amino acid sequences of B14R-326 and B14R-180 were codon-optimized, synthesized by General Biol (Anhui, China), and cloned into an expression vector (pTT5) with a C-terminal His-tag. Recombinant proteins were expressed in 293 F cells transfected with the corresponding plasmids. Following expression, the cells were lysed, and the proteins were purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The column was washed with binding buffer (PBS containing 20 mM imidazole, pH 7.4) to remove nonspecifically bound impurities. Target proteins were subsequently eluted using elution buffer (PBS containing 500 mM imidazole, pH 7.4).

Prediction of binding affinity between B14R and IL-1β

The crystal structure of the IL-1 receptor complex (PDB ID: 3o4o) was obtained from the PDB database (https://www.rcsb.org/structure). Subsequently, the structure was processed using PyMOL software to extract Chain A and Chain B of IL-1β individually. In accordance with the specified protocol, Chain A was designated as the template for IL-1β, while Chain B was selected as the receptor for molecular docking studies. Homology modeling was executed by the network server of SWISS-MODEL (https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive). IL-1 receptor type 2 (PDB 3o4o.1.B) was selected as the preferred template for both 180 AA and 326 AA products encoded by B14R gene. IL-1β (PDB 3o4o.1.A) was extracted as the receptors (B14Rproteins). ZDOCK-SERVER (https://zdock.umassmed.edu) was utilized for receptor-ligand docking. The PyMOL program (version 2.6.0) was utilized for reading, annotating, and plotting the spatial structures of proteins as well as intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

To determine whether the differential binding observed between truncated B14R variants and IL-1β resulted from disparities in molecular quantity or inherent binding affinity, we performed quantitative evaluations using ELISA. Two fragmented His-tag B14R proteins (His-tagB14R-180, His-tagB14R-326) were were diluted to 1 µg/mL and equimolar concentrations (20 nM). The diluted His-tagB14R proteins (100 µl/well) were added into 96-well polystyrene plates and incubated overnight at 4℃. The plates were washed three times with PBST and then blocked with 5% skim milk in PBS for 2 h at 37℃. After blocking, the plate was washed again three times with PBST. Subsequently, 100 µl of IL-1β solution (0.5 mg/mL in PBS) was added to each well and incubated for 2 h at 37℃. Following four washes with PBST, 100 µL per well of IL-1β Mouse Monoclonal Antibody (diluted 1:500 in blocking buffer; Beyotime, China) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. After four additional washes, 100 µL of HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (dilution ratio 1:5000; ORIGENE, China) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. Following a final wash cycle, 100 µL of TMB substrate solution (Biopanda, China) was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 15–20 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of stop solution (Biopanda, China) per well, and the absorbance values were measured at 450 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader.

To detect anti-B14R antibodies in serum, the serum samples were divided into two groups: one group was treated with specific IL-1β antibodies to block IL-1β/B14R protein interaction, while the other group remained untreated as a control. ELISA plates were coated with fragmented His-tagB14R proteins (His-tagB14R-180, His-tagB14R-326) and incubated overnight at 4℃. The plates were washed three times with PBST and then blocked for 2 h at 37℃. After blocking, two serum groups were added to the ELISA plates and incubated at 37 °C for 2 h. Following four washes with PBST, 100 µL of HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (dilution ratio 1:5000) was added to each well and incubated for 1 h at 37 °C. After a final wash, 100 µL of TMB substrate solution was added to each well and incubated in the dark for 15–20 min at room temperature. The reaction was terminated by adding 50 µL of stop solution per well. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay reader.

Cell apoptosis assay

To establish an apoptosis model of HeLa cells induced by IL-1β, we treated HeLa cells with 10 ng/mL of IL-1β. The HeLa cells were treated with the following four ways: (i) DMEM medium (Control); (ii) 10 µM IL-1β (IL-1β); (iii) 10 µM IL-1β + 10 µM B14R-180 (IL-1β + 180); (iv)10 µM IL-1β + 10 µM B14R-326 (IL-1β + 326). After 24 and 48 h, cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin without EDTA. After the digestion was terminated, the cells were collected and centrifuged at 400×g for 5 min at room temperature, after which the supernatant was removed and the cells were washed with PBS. The cells were then rinsed twice with PBS at 400×g for 5 min at room temperature, and an Annexin V-FITC Apoptosis Detection Kit was used to assess the level of cell apoptosis. All of the steps were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were analyzed using a Becton Dickinson FACS Calibur flow cytometer (BD Accuri C6 Plus; BD Biosciences) with ModFitLT software. All experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

RNA extraction and mRNA expression

The HeLa cells were plated in 12-well plates (5 × 104 cells/well) and were treated as described above. We used to monitor the expression levels of caspase-3、Caspase-8, Caspase-9, Caspase-10. Endogenous housekeeping gene β-Actin was used as an internal control. Cells were harvested after 24 h and 48 h and total RNA was extracted from cultured cells using RNeasy Plus Mini kit (Qiagen). RNA was used as the template for one step qRT-PCR reactions with specific primers [19, 20]. All primers were synthesized by standard phosphoramidite chemistry techniques at Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The qRT-PCR was conducted using 2.5×SYBR Green One Step Mix reagent (Sangon Biotechi, China). The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: 15 min at 50 °C for reverse transcription, 2.5 min at 95 °C for predenaturation, followed by 40 cycles of 94 °C for 15 s and 60 °C for 1 min. Fluorescence measurements were taken at 60 °C during each cycle. After the reaction, the comparative threshold cycle (Ct) calculation was used to determine the relative gene expression levels, which were normalized to β-Actin. The data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

The HeLa cells were treated as described above. After 48 h, cellular proteins were extracted in RIPA Lysis Buffer I (Perfect Biotech) containing 100 mg/ml β-mercaptoethanol and quantified using the Modified BCA Protein Assay Reagent (Perfect Biotech, China). Cells were denatured in loading buffer and boiled at 99 °C for 5 min. Protein separated by SDS-PAGE were transferred to PVDF membranes. The membranes were blocked with 6% Rapid Block Buff II (Sangon Biotech, China) at room temperature for 10 min and incubated with anti-Caspase-3-specific monoclonal antibody (1:1000, dilution; single cell) or anti-β-Actin antibody (1:3000, dilution; Sigma) at 4 °C overnight. Then the membranes were incubated with secondary antibody (1:2500, dilution; goat anti-mouse IgG; Sigma) 1 h at room temperature. The protein bands were detected by chemiluminescence using an ECL kit (Sangon Biotech, China).

Statistical analysis

All the experiments were performed three times independently and the data were presented as means ± SD. Statistical significances were assessed by two-tailed Student’s t-test or one-way multivariate analysis of variance (ANOVA) in Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS.26) statistical software. A value of P < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

Results

Binding affinity between B14R protein and IL-1β

Spatial modeling and receptor-ligand docking analyses demonstrated that although the 180-amino acid (AA) protein encoded by the West African clade (B14R-180) shares high structural similarity with the full-length 326-AA protein from the Central African clade (B14R-326), the truncated variant exhibited significantly stronger binding affinity for IL-1β. This enhanced binding affinity was characterized by the formation of more hydrogen bonds (9 vs. 7) and a lower binding free energy (ΔG = − 12.4 kJ/mol vs. −2.0 kJ/mol) (Fig. 1A and B).

Evaluation of the binding capacity of B14R protein to IL-1β

The results demonstrated that the binding affinity of recombinant His-tagged B14R-180 to IL-1β was significantly higher than that of His-tagged B14R-326 when normalized by mass concentration (P < 0.05, Fig. 2A). Similarly, under molarity-normalized conditions, B14R-180 continued to exhibit a significantly stronger binding signal compared to B14R-326 (P < 0.05, Fig. 2B). Based on this, we systematically evaluated whether anti-B14R antibodies present in vaccinia-immune sera exhibit binding affinity toward IL-1β. The serum samples from vaccinia-vaccinated donors were pre-incubated with anti-IL-1β antibodies to block IL-1β/B14R protein interaction before ELISA analysis. Among the recruited donors, approximately 35% (7/20) were born before 1980, with ages ranging from 43 to 60 years and a median age of 55. The remaining 65% (13/20) were born after 1980, aged 24–40 years, with a median age of 32 (Additional file 1: Table S1). We also contacted all of the donors born before 1980 by phone interview (Additional file 2: Figure S1). Among the 7 donors born before 1980, 5 donors were successfully contacted. 4 donors confirmed their smallpox vaccination history (using VACV Tiantan strain), while the rest one donor was not sure. Antibody cross-reactivity analysis demonstrated that pre-absorption with IL-1β completely abolished B14R-180 binding in all vaccinated donors (4/4, 100%), whereas only partial inhibition of B14R-326 binding was observed in a single donor (1/4, 25%) (Fig. 2C and D).

Effect of B14R protein binding to IL-1β on cellular apoptosis

To investigate the effect of B14R binding to IL-1β on cellular apoptosis, HeLa cells were treated with recombinant His-tagged B14R-180 or B14R-326 in combination with IL-1β. Apoptosis was assessed by Annexin V-FITC/propidium iodide (PI) staining and flow cytometry. As shown in Fig. 3, after 24 h of incubation, no significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed in early or late apoptotic rates between cells treated with IL-1β alone and those co-treated with B14R-180/B14R-326 and IL-1β. However, after 48 h, co-treatment with B14R-180 significantly reduced both early and late apoptosis rates relative to IL-1β alone (P < 0.05; Fig. 3B and C). In contrast, the B14R-326 and IL-1β combination showed no significant effect on apoptosis (P > 0.05). While the B14R-180/IL-1β co-treatment consistently yielded lower apoptosis rates than the B14R-326 and IL-1β combination, the difference reached statistical significance only for early apoptosis (P < 0.05).

Impact of B14R protein binding to IL-1β on the expression of apoptosis-related genes

HeLa cells were treated as described for 24–48 h, followed by quantitative analysis of apoptosis-related gene expression using quantitative reverse transcription PCR (qRT-PCR). No significant differences in caspase expression were detected at 24 h. However, after 48 h of treatment, the B14R-180 and IL-1β combination significantly downregulated expression of caspase-3, caspase-8, caspase-9, and caspase-10 compared to IL-1β alone (P < 0.001; Fig. 4). Similarly, the B14R-326 and IL-1β combination also exhibited significant inhibitory effects, reducing expression of caspase-3, −8, −9 and − 10 relative to IL-1β alone (P < 0.05; Fig. 4). Notably, the suppressive effect of B14R-180 with IL-1β was significantly stronger than that of B14R-326 with IL-1β for caspase-3, −8, and − 10 (P < 0.05; Fig. 4A, B, D), whereas no significant difference between the two combinations was observed in caspase-9 expression (P > 0.05; Fig. 4C).

Discussion

Studies have shown that the B14R gene of vaccinia virus (VACV) encodes a 149-amino acid (aa) protein capable of binding IL-1β. This interaction has been shown to attenuate systemic acute-phase responses to infection, modulate disease severity, and inhibit cellular apoptosis [21–23]. However, the functional conservation of this mechanism in fragmented B14R variants of MPV remains to be characterized. This study provides a comprehensive comparative analysis of the truncated B14R-180 protein (2022 USA strain) and the full-length B14R-326 protein (Zaire-96 strain), focusing on their structural determinants of IL-1β binding and functional consequences for apoptotic regulation. Molecular modeling revealed that B14R-180 exhibits superior IL-1β binding affinity compared to B14R-326, attributable to: (1) formation of two additional hydrogen bonds (9 vs. 7), (2) more favorable binding free energy (ΔG = −12.4 vs. −2.0 kJ/mol), and (3) optimized binding interface due to C-terminal truncation-induced reduction of steric hindrance. Structural analysis identified critical interactions between B14R-180 residues (G30, Q31, Y32) and IL-1β domains (N66, P87, E64) with shortened bond distances (2.4–3.5 Å), consistent with previous reports of enhanced ligand-binding efficiency in truncated variants [18]. These findings collectively establish B14R-180 as the more potent IL-1β-binding variant.

To further investigate the IL-1β binding capacity of different B14R variants, we purified recombinant His-tagged B14R-180 and B14R-326 proteins. This study presents the first systematic comparison of B14R-180 and B14R-326 proteins from two major MPV evolutionary clades. ELISA assays confirmed that B14R-180 exhibits significantly stronger binding affinity for IL-1β than B14R-326 under both mass- and molar-normalized conditions, indicating that this difference reflects enhanced intrinsic binding affinity rather than artifactual concentration effects. These findings are consistent with bioinformatic predictions, suggesting that B14R-180 may expose more critical binding domains structurally, reduce steric hindrance, or enhance complementarity with the IL-1β interaction surface. The enhanced binding affinity of B14R-180 may represent a viral adaptation to host immune pressure. As IL-1β is a central mediator of inflammatory and antiviral responses, more effective suppression of IL-1β signaling could facilitate immune evasion and promote viral dissemination. This finding aligns with previous studies showing that vaccinia virus orthologs of B14R inhibit NF-κB signaling via a Bcl-2-like fold [24], and further underscores the importance of natural variation within viral immunomodulatory genes in shaping pathogenicity and host adaptation. Immunological cross-reactivity assessment using sera from vaccinia-vaccinated donors revealed that anti-IL-1β antibodies exhibit stronger competitive inhibition against B14R-180 binding compared to B14R-326. This differential inhibition profile confirms both the superior binding affinity of B14R-180 for IL-1β and the structural conservation of immunodominant epitopes shared with the vaccinia virus B14R ortholog [25, 26]. Mechanistically, these findings suggest that B14R-180 may function as a molecular decoy through structural mimicry of IL-1β, competitively binding to IL-1 receptors and consequently suppressing pro-apoptotic signaling cascades. To functionally validate these observations, we next performed flow cytometric analysis to quantify the apoptotic effects mediated by B14R variant-IL-1β interactions. The findings indicated that the initial apoptotic response to IL-1β was not significantly altered by the presence of these B14R variants within the first 24 h. However, after 48 h, the interaction between B14R-180 and IL-1β significantly regulated apoptosis in HeLa cells, whereas B14R-326 had no such effect. This suggested that B14R-180 exerts a potent anti-apoptotic effect when bound to IL-1β, and this effect became more pronounced over time. This higher binding affinity may enable B14R-180 to sequester IL-1β more effectively, thereby preventing its pro-apoptotic signaling. In contrast, B14R-326, with lower binding affinity, may fail to effectively inhibit IL-1β signaling, resulting in no significant impact on apoptosis.​.

As a key inflammatory factor, IL-1β induces cell apoptosis by activating apoptosis-related caspases, including caspase-3, caspase-8, caspase-9, and caspase-10 [27–29], this study further elucidated the molecular mechanisms underlying the anti-apoptotic effects of B14R-180 and B14R-326. The results demonstrated that combined treatment with B14R and IL-1β for 24 h did not induce significant changes in apoptosis-related factors, whereas a marked downregulation was observed after 48 h. This temporal pattern suggests that the inhibitory mechanism requires prolonged exposure to manifest, exhibiting time-dependent suppression likely involving downstream signaling modulation and/or cumulative inhibitory effects on IL-1β-mediated transcription. Moreover, the significant downregulation of caspase-3, −8, −9, and − 10 after 48 h of treatment with either B14R indicates that both proteins can functionally antagonize IL-1β-induced apoptotic signaling, which aligns with the known role of IL-1β as an inducer of pro-apoptotic pathways and confirms that MPV B14R proteins act as effective decoys mitigating this response. The consistently stronger suppression exerted by B14R-180 across multiple caspases (caspase-3, −8, and − 10) underscores its superior efficacy in apoptotic inhibition compared to B14R-326. This functional advantage is likely attributable to its enhanced binding affinity for IL-1β. Numerous studies have demonstrated that the expression level of Caspase-3 is a direct indicator of apoptotic activity and is indispensable for the completion of cell death, almost all apoptotic pathways (endogenous and exogenous) ultimately exert their effects by activating Caspase-3 [30, 31]. The observed downregulation involved multiple key caspases, most notably caspase-3, which acts as the central executioner protease in apoptotic pathways. Consequently, we focused our protein-level analysis primarily on caspase-3. The concordant reduction in both mRNA and protein levels of caspase-3 underscores the biological significance of these results, demonstrating that transcriptional suppression effectively translates into diminished functional protein. The more pronounced suppression of caspase-3 protein expression by B14R-180 further affirms its enhanced role as a viral immune modulator. This study has several limitations that warrant further investigation in the future. We will focus on additional apoptosis-related proteins—such as the cleaved forms of caspases-8 and − 9, as well as key regulators including Bax and Bcl-2—will provide a more comprehensive view of how B14R–IL-1β binding modulates the functional apoptotic cascade. Additionally, future studies would benefit from incorporating additional loading controls, such as Ponceau S total protein staining, to further validate protein loading consistency. Despite these limitations, the core findings of this study remain robust and reliable. Our results clearly demonstrate that B14R-180 exhibits enhanced potential to suppress apoptosis through its interaction with IL-1β.

Conclusions

In conclusion, fragmented proteins encoded by the B14R gene exhibit significant differences in IL-1β-binding affinity. The binding of B14R proteins to IL-1β inhibits apoptosis-related signaling pathways (e.g., caspase activation), thereby protecting cells from apoptosis. B14R-180 exhibits stronger IL-1β-binding affinity and more potent apoptosis suppression than the full-length B14R-326. This enhanced interaction may facilitate immune evasion by preventing host cell apoptosis, thereby promoting viral persistence and transmission. These findings provide novel perspectives for MPV vaccine development and therapeutic interventions, while offering critical insights into the complex interplay between MPV pathogenesis and host immune regulation.
