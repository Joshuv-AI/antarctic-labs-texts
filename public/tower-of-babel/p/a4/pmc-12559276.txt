Pharmacological profiling in CLL patients during pirtobrutinib therapy and disease progression
Authors: Shady I. Tantawy, Burcu Aslan, Ganiraju Manyam, LaKesla R. Iles, Natalia Timofeeva, Neetu Singh, Nitin Jain, Alessandra Ferrajoli, Philip A. Thompson, Keyur P. Patel, Sai Prasad Desikan, William G. Wierda, Varsha Gandhi
Published: 2025

Introduction

Pirtobrutinib, an ncBTKi that inhibits signaling both from wildtype (WT)- as well as C481 mutant BTK, has been previously investigated in both preclinical [1–3] and clinical [4, 5] settings. A multi-center phase I/II pirtobrutinib (BRUIN) trial reported on results for 247 patients with CLL or small lymphocytic lymphoma (SLL) who were previously treated with a cBTKi. Three critical questions emerge from this trial. First, what are the determining factors that dictate sustained response to pirtobrutinib? Second, does treatment with pirtobrutinib exert selective pressure on the population of CLL cells to allow re-sensitization to cBTKi or other targeted agents or combinations? Third, what are potential therapeutic intensification options for patients who progress on pirtobrutinib, including those who resolve C481S but develop new BTK-mutant clones, and those who have Bcl-2i-resistant disease? To address these questions, we analyzed data from CLL patients on the BRUIN trial treated at UT MD Anderson Cancer Center (MDACC).

Patients and methods

Clinical protocol

All patients in the current study were participants in the BRUIN trial (NCT03740529). Among the 41 patients treated at MDACC, 26 had CLL and were included (Supplementary Fig. 1A−B). All patients were previously treated with cBTKi and were either intolerant or had relapsed/refractory (R/R) CLL (Supplementary Table 1). The progression free survival (PFS) for these patients was 18.53 months (Supplementary Fig. 1C). Patients provided written informed consent to participate in the clinical trial and consent to participate in the accompanying laboratory correlative studies. The clinical trial was approved by the MDACC-IRB and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Results

Baseline clinical/molecular features and patient outcomes on pirtobrutinib

Among the 26 patients at MDACC, 23 were available for these investigations and completed at least two years (24 cycles) of pirtobrutinib monotherapy (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1A). Among these 23 patients, 14 (61%) had PD within first two years (Fig. 1A). The 9 patients still on therapy have received 30–42 cycles and are continuing pirtobrutinib therapy as of manuscript submission (Supplementary Table 5). Age, gender, ECOG performance status and number of prior therapies did not correlate with PD, although there were higher number of patients (64%) in the PD group who had received Bcl-2i (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Among the 14 patients who progressed, at baseline, one had PLCG2 mutation, three had WT-BTK; and 11 patients had C481 BTK mutation; majority variants were C481S or C481R. Two patients also had the gatekeeper T474I alteration. At disease progression, two patients maintained WT-BTK. Five patients developed the L528W clone. Expansion/origination of BTK T474 clones was observed in several patients, while BTK C481S variant was either reduced or eliminated (Fig. 1A). Among 15 attributes evaluated, there were 6 features that associated with PD with an FDR threshold of 15% (Fig. 1B). The total number of patients is relatively small and needs to be validated in a larger cohort. All patients who had PD were R/R to cBTKi, while patients on therapy included both intolerant and R/R to cBTKi (p = 0.030). Consistent with this observation, 80% patients with PD had baseline BTK mutations. Comparatively, only 22% with baseline BTK mutations are still on therapy (p = 0.030). Patients in the PD subgroup showed associations with bulky lymph nodes (p = 0.048) and a higher prevalence (75%) of complex karyotype (p = 0.030). Although all patients who progressed had baseline unmutated IGHV and XPO1 mutation (n = 5), they did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.057 for both) and need to be confirmed. Collectively, the patients with early (≤24 cycles pirtobrutinib) PD were more likely to have progression on prior cBTKi, baseline BTK mutation, bulky lymph nodes and complex karyotype. Patients who are receiving >24 cycles of pirtobrutinib were more likely to have discontinued prior cBTKi due to intolerance, have baseline wildtype-BTK, and mutated IGHV (Fig. 1B).

Association of baseline clinical/molecular features with progression-free survival (PFS)

Some features were significantly associated with PFS. First, the rates of progression during pirtobrutinib therapy in patients who had R/R disease on prior cBTKi were 53% at 18 months. In contrast, all patients who were intolerant to prior cBTKi remained on pirtobrutinib therapy for >24 cycles; median PFS 17 months vs unreached (Fig. 1C). Second, the time-to-progression was shorter for patients with BTK mutation (Fig. 1D). At 18 months, 60% disease progression occurred for complex karyotype and 83% for bulky lymph nodes. TP53/17pdel, NOTCH1, ATM/11qdel, and 13qdel mutations showed similar median PFS compared to WT cohorts; however, XPO1 mutation was associated with a shorter PFS (Supplementary Fig. 2A–F).

Pharmacological profiling of peripheral blood cells during pirtobrutinib therapy

To identify potential partners for pirtobrutinib, cells collected before and after 10–14 cycles of pirtobrutinib were treated ex vivo with targeted agents. With each drug compared to the pre-treatment sample, there was sensitization of cells after pirtobrutinib therapy, which was statistically significant during combination (Fig. 1E–H). These data suggest that different targeted agents could be added or sequenced after several cycles of pirtobrutinib to enhance response.

Disease progression is associated with loss of BTK inhibition by pirtobrutinib

Within a week of pirtobrutinib therapy, there was an observable decline in the levels of both CCL3/CCL4 chemokines, which generally remained down throughout the 3 cycles of therapy. However, at PD, there was a marked, although heterogeneous, increase in the levels of these chemokines, suggesting activation of the BCR signaling pathway. In contrast, for five patients who are still on pirtobrutinib therapy, both CCL3/CCL4 levels were low at baseline and remained low (Supplementary Fig. 3A–D).

Disease progression on pirtobrutinib is associated with enhanced sensitivity to ibrutinib

We then tested peripheral blood cells ex vivo from patients during therapy or at the time of disease progression. Cells from patients on therapy were more sensitive (IC50, 532 nM) to pirtobrutinib compared to those from patients who had PD (IC50, 6358 nM). Thus, ex vivo response to pirtobrutinib mirrored clinical disease progression on pirtobrutinib (Fig. 2A). Compared to pirtobrutinib, cells at PD were 10-fold more sensitive to ibrutinib (IC50, 660 nM) (Fig. 2B). Baseline BTK status and associated cytogenetics are provided (Supplementary Fig. 4A, B). At the time of PD, the VAF value of C481 mutations declined with an increase in non-C481 BTK mutations. Acquired mutations were more frequent in patients with prior BTK mutations and had faster PD (Supplementary Fig. 4C–E). We postulate that these changes in BTK mutations at progression may explain, at least partly, the re-sensitization to ibrutinib.

Disease progression on pirtobrutinib is associated with reactivation of the BCR pathway and upregulation of MCL-1 and BCL-2 proteins

In patients on therapy, BCR pathway proteins were suppressed (Fig. 2D). AKT, however, showed increased phosphorylation at later time points. For patients with PD and accompanied second-site BTK mutations, compared to the baseline sample, there was an increase in pBTK and pNFkB at progression (Fig. 2E). During therapy, there was a decline in the level of total Bcl-2 and Mcl-1 (similar to ibrutinib [6]) (Fig. 2D), while in patients with PD, there was an upregulation of Bcl-2 and Mcl-1 protein levels at the time of progression (Fig. 2E).

Pharmacological profiling of CLL cells at progression on pirtobrutinib

Cells were incubated with different drugs, which were selected for their utility in CLL, targets in the disease, or their clinical availability (Supplementary Table 4). Venetoclax was revealed to be a promising, effective single agent as compared to ibrutinib or AZD5991. However, with double and triple drug combinations, 70%–90% cell death was observed in all cases (Supplementary Fig. 5A–D). The extent of apoptosis was highly independent of the type of 2nd site BTK mutation. In contrast to ibrutinib, the combination of pirtobrutinib with another drug, particularly venetoclax and APR-246, generally induced apoptosis from the second drug, further demonstrating resistance to pirtobrutinib (Supplementary Fig. 6A, B). Cells from two patients with Bcl-2 mutations were more resistant to apoptosis (Supplementary Fig. 6C). Except for the double mutation (BCL-2 and BTK), cells from all patients showed high apoptosis with doublet treatments. Patients with or without complex karyotype had a high apoptotic outcome with combined BTKi and Bcl-2i exposure (Fig. 2F). Different genetic/genomic baseline profiles did not impact the extent of apoptosis when drugs were combined. At a strict p-value, the presence of BCL-2 mutation and double mutations was unfavorable, while XPO1 mutation was more sensitive to ibrutinib. Cells with prior venetoclax and cBTKi exposure tend to be less sensitive to targeted agents (Fig. 2F, G, Supplementary Fig. 7A–C).

Discussion

We address three distinct questions of critical relevance to CLL patients on pirtobrutinib. First, a few features appeared to distinguish patients whose disease progressed or not within first 24 cycles: (a) Prior cBTKi R/R patients appeared to have a higher chance of progression on pirtobrutinib compared to those who were intolerant [7]. (b) Most patients in the intolerant cohort did not have BTK mutations, underscoring the association of wild-type versus mutant BTK and disease progression on pirtobrutinib. (c) Progression on pirtobrutinib was associated with a complex karyotype. Thus, we can also postulate that patients who have baseline BTK aberrations or complex karyotypes have a mutator phenotype and are prone to have additional mutations. These observations underscore a need to further evaluate pirtobrutinib treatment in patients who have been treated before but are BTKi-naïve.

Second, a critical component of the current study was to determine if persistent CLL cells during 10–14 cycles of pirtobrutinib are still sensitive to targeted agents. Similar to cBTKi, pirtobrutinib monotherapy results in partial remissions or develops resistance [8–10]. These results underscore a need for pirtobrutinib to be followed by other agent/s in sequence as consolidation or intensification by combination to achieve a deeper response and to achieve uMRD status. Interestingly, when tested prior to pirtobrutinib and during 10–14 cycles of pirtobrutinib, our data clearly demonstrate that pirtobrutinib-treated cells have a better apoptotic response to multiple targeted agents, especially in combination. These data provide pharmacological rationale to sequence pirtobrutinib monotherapy followed by venetoclax and/or cBTKi consolidation or to intensify therapy by adding cBTKi and/or Bcl-2i after a few cycles of pirtobrutinib. There are prior precedents for both these strategies [11–15].

Third focus of the current study, and perhaps the most significant question that was addressed here, was to determine if cells at the time of disease progression exhibit BCR pathway activation and if these pirtobrutinib-resistant cells would respond to targeted agents. This is an unmet medical need, and from the BRUIN trial [5] data, it is apparent that while pirtobrutinib is very effective in CLL, after 12–18 months, 31%–44% patients demonstrate disease progression. Next-generation sequencing suggested a decrease or elimination of the C481S clone after pirtobrutinib therapy [16]. Consistent with this observation, most patient samples showed increased cell death with ibrutinib alone or when combined with venetoclax. Upregulation of Bcl-2 and Mcl-1 proteins was observed at the time of progression. Bcl-2 is successfully targeted by venetoclax, and Mcl-1 is neutralized by many directly binding agents, including AZD5991 [17]. Patients with BCL-2 mutations, however, showed relative resistance, which is consistent with the observation that those with a prior history of BTKi + Bcl-2i showed shorter PFS [5]. Ongoing clinical trials that combine pirtobrutinib and venetoclax with obinutuzumab (NCT05536349) [18] or with rituximab (NCT04965493) [19] will further elucidate the benefits of these combinations.
