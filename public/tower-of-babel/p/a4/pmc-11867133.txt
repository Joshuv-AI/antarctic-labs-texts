Deep learning models using intracranial and scalp EEG for predicting sedation level during emergence from anaesthesia
Authors: Lichy Han, David A Purger, Sarah L Eagleman, Casey H Halpern, Vivek Buch, Samantha M Gaston, Babak Razavi, Kimford Meador, David R Drover
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMaintaining an appropriate depth of anaesthesia is important for avoiding adverse effects from undermedication or overmedication during surgery. Electroencephalography (EEG) has become increasingly used to achieve this balance. Investigating the predictive power of intracranial EEG (iEEG) and scalp EEG for different levels of sedation could increase the utility of EEG monitoring.MethodsSimultaneous iEEG, scalp EEG, and Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) scores were recorded during emergence from anaesthesia in seven patients undergoing placement of intracranial electrodes for medically refractory epilepsy. A deep learning model was constructed to predict an OAA/S score of 0–2 vs 3–5 using iEEG, scalp EEG, and their combination. An additional five patients with only scalp EEG data were used for independent validation. Models were evaluated using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic curve (AUC).ResultsCombining scalp EEG and iEEG yielded significantly better prediction (AUC=0.795, P<0.001) compared with iEEG only (AUC=0.750, P=0.02) or scalp EEG only (AUC=0.764, P<0.001). The validation scalp EEG only data resulted in an AUC of 0.844. Combining the two modalities appeared to capture spatiotemporal advantages from both modalities.ConclusionsThe combination of iEEG and scalp EEG better predicted sedation level than either modality alone. The scalp EEG only model achieved a similar AUC to the combined model and maintained its performance in additional patients, suggesting that scalp EEG models are likely sufficient for real-time monitoring. Deep learning approaches using multiple leads to capture a wider area of brain activity may help augment existing EEG monitors for prediction of sedation.Keywords: deep learning, depth of sedation, electroencephalography, intracranial electroencephalography, prediction modelling

Every year worldwide, there are more than 200 million major operations under anaesthesia.1 During general anaesthesia, maintaining an appropriate depth of anaesthesia is essential for preventing recall,2,3 modulating the haemodynamic effects of surgical stimuli,4 mitigating potential adverse cognitive effects,5,6 and ensuring timely emergence at the end of the procedure. As a result, methods to monitor brain activity using electroencephalography (EEG) have been developed to optimise the titration of anaesthetic agents.7 However, the use of EEG to monitor the depth of anaesthesia or level of sedation during emergence relies on scalp EEG electrodes, which measure only a limited proportion of frontal lobe activity and aggregate large areas of the nearby subcortical intracranial electrical activity.8 Although the use of commercial scalp EEG intraoperative monitors has been shown to decrease complications such as nausea, delirium, and awareness in some studies,9 they are sensitive to noise and artifact such as from electromyography,10 which can obscure the contribution of the underlying brain activity to the predictive value and be a potential confounder when developing these algorithms when neuromuscular blocking agents are used. The underlying brain activity can be captured by intracranial EEG (iEEG), and simultaneous iEEG and scalp EEG has been studied previously predominantly in patients with epilepsy for seizure localisation.11, 12, 13 However, because of the invasive nature of iEEG, few data exist that enable examining these signals together in the context of varying levels of sedation during emergence from anaesthesia.

The advent of artificial intelligence, driven predominantly by advances in deep learning, has revolutionised data analysis and produced novel technologies such as automated cars14 and generative large language models.15,16 These techniques have been applied to EEG data for tasks such as epileptic foci localisation and sleep staging.17 A few studies have focused on depth of anaesthesia, although mostly for the prediction of commercially available metrics such as the bispectral index (Medtronic, Minneapolis, MN, USA) and the patient state index (Masimo Inc. Irvine, CA, USA).

Our study uses machine learning techniques to analyse a dataset of simultaneously recorded iEEG and scalp EEG to predict the depth of sedation as measured by the modified Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) scale from Glass and colleagues.18 Deep learning is particularly well suited in this instance, with the potential to discover patterns from multidimensional time-series data. In combining these methods with our unique dataset, we aim to evaluate the correlation of iEEG and scalp EEG for the assessment of the level of sedation during emergence from anaesthesia.

Methods

Study design

Patients with medically refractory epilepsy undergoing placement of intracranial electrodes under general anaesthesia for localisation of epileptic foci were recruited for this study. Patients were included if they were 18 yr of age or older, had been previously diagnosed with epilepsy, and required implantation of invasive EEG recordings as part of their care. Exclusion criteria included pregnancy or skin abnormalities precluding scalp electrode application. Patients with severe pre-existing comorbidities such that participation in the study and undergoing general anaesthesia constituted an unacceptable risk to their health were also excluded. The study was approved by the Stanford University Institutional Review Board (IRB-45987, NCT03629743), and informed consent was obtained from all subjects before enrolment in the study.

Data collection

On the day of surgery, the intraoperative neuromonitoring team attached scalp electrodes for scalp EEG acquisition before surgery, recording LE, LT, F7, Fp1, Fz, Fp2, F8, RT, RE (Fig. 1) at 1000 Hz. The leads were connected to the Brain Vision V-Amp acquisition system (Brain Vision LLC, Morrisville, NC, USA) in the preoperative unit where lead placement and impedance (<10 kΩ) were confirmed before proceeding to the operating room for induction of general anaesthesia. General anaesthesia was maintained with propofol and remifentanil for all patients except one where a combination of nitrous oxide, sevoflurane, propofol, and remifentanil was used at the discretion of the anaesthesiologist. The neurosurgical team then commenced the placement of the intracranial leads with routine epilepsy monitoring. The total number and location of intracranial depth electrodes placed were determined clinically according to each patient's seizure foci. Up to 64 channels of iEEG were recorded using the Nihon Kohden (Shinjuku City, Tokyo, Japan) system at 1000 Hz.

Scalp EEG lead placement. AFz was used as reference (R), and A2 was used as the ground (G) lead. LE and RE are located on the mastoid processes behind the ear, and LT and RT are on the zygomatic arches. Nasion refers to the anatomical landmark of the midline anterior point where the frontal and nasal bones join, and inion refers to the posterior midline prominence of the external occipital protuberance.

After the electrode implantation procedure was complete and before emergence from anaesthesia, the intracranial leads were connected to the recording devices, and five electrical pulses in a row were generated simultaneously in both the Brain Vision Amp system and Nihon Kohden system to synchronise the iEEG and scalp EEG. The anaesthesia team assessed the OAA/S score every 30 s during emergence from anaesthesia with simultaneous iEEG and scalp EEG recording throughout. When the patient responded to consecutive verbal commands at least twice, the patient's trachea was extubated. Another series of five simultaneous pulses was generated to align the scalp EEG and iEEG data. Intracranial electrode connections and scalp electrodes were removed, and the patient was taken to the PACU.

After surgery, a CT scan was performed and co-registered with the preoperative MRI to verify the locations of electrodes using Brainlab software (Brainlab, Munich, Germany). The iEEG channels used for downstream analysis were manually selected using these images to confirm their location and siting in grey matter.

Preprocessing EEG data

EEG preprocessing was performed using MATLAB 2021a (MathWorks, Natick, MA, USA) and with Chronux 2.12.19 Scalp EEG and iEEG data were aligned in time using the generated electrical pulses. Data were trimmed to the section between the pulse trains that included simultaneous iEEG and scalp EEG data that corresponded with emergence from anaesthesia.

iEEG data were detrended to remove baseline wandering. Because of the variability of electrode placement (seizure location differed between patients), four electrodes that were the most consistent between patients were selected for analysis. These corresponded to the left and right frontal lobe, most commonly the ventromedial prefrontal cortex, and deeper structures on the left and right, most commonly the posterior cingulate cortex, which are both part of the default mode network.20 When these exact channel locations were not available, alternate recording sites chosen based on location included the ventrolateral prefrontal cortex (n=3), the anterior dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (n=2), the posterior dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (n=1), the superior temporal gyrus (n=1), the superior frontal gyrus (n=1), the hippocampus (n=1), the anterior insula (n=1), and the posterior insula (n=1). One patient had only right-sided intracranial leads, therefore data were replicated to artificially generate left-sided leads to mirror the right side to be compatible with our model architecture. A notch filter was applied at 60 Hz and 120 Hz to the iEEG data to remove electrical noise. Scalp EEG data were similarly detrended to remove baseline wandering. A bandpass filter between 1 and 50 Hz was applied to scalp EEG data to remove high-frequency artifact and noise.

OAA/S prediction model

We constructed a deep learning model to predict the OAA/S score from iEEG, scalp EEG, and combined iEEG and scalp EEG. OAA/S scores were grouped into two categories: 0–2 vs 3–5. We predicted the dichotomised OAA/S score at every second in time, assigning the OAA/S score as the last recorded OAA/S score up until that point in time. For each point, we used the previous 5 s of EEG, consisting of 5000 data points as a series of 13 time-domain signals. Previous studies using machine learning to predict consciousness from EEG data have used 2-s and 4-s time-series epochs based on previous domain knowledge on the length of time needed to capture key features such as epileptiform features and burst suppression.21, 22

The overall model architecture is depicted in Figure. 2. All nine channels of scalp EEG were used together and passed through three 2-D convolutional layers, with batch normalisation, activation, dropout, and then pooling layers between convolutional layers. iEEG were processed as four individual channels, each passed through three 1-D convolutional layers, again with batch normalisation, activation, dropout, and then pooling. The scalp EEG and iEEG encodings from the convolutional layers were then concatenated. This then served as the input for the transformer portion of our model, which consisted of two multi-head attention layers with feed forward layers. The output was then fed into a dense fully connected layer, which produced the output.

Model architecture for prediction of Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) score from scalp EEG and intracranial EEG (iEEG) data. The nine channels of EEG data were combined and passed through three 2-D convolutional (Conv) layers, with batch normalisation, activation, dropout, and then pooling (Pool) layers between convolutional layers. iEEG were processed as four individual channels through a similar architecture. Different shades of purple reflect that each iEEG channel produced individual output, as compared with combining all scalp EEG data together into a single output owing to their spatial relationship. The scalp EEG and iEEG encodings from the convolutional layers were then concatenated and input into a transformer with two multi-head attention layers with feed forward layers. The output was then fed into a dense fully connected layer, which produced the final output. (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the Web version of this article.)

The activation function used was the rectified linear unit. In terms of our hyperparameters, the filter size was 3×3 for 2-D convolutional layers, 3×1 for 1-D convolutional layers, dropout was 0.5, kernel size was 8, four heads were used with 32 units each, and the dense layer contained 32 units (see Supplementary methods for more detail on model layers and hyperparameters). Class weights that were inversely proportional to their frequencies were used to account for class imbalance. For our iEEG and scalp EEG only models, we used the same model architecture; however, the input into the transformer block was either the scalp EEG encoding or the iEEG encodings only.

Statistical analysis

Deep learning models were implemented using Python 3.10 (Python Software Foundation, Wilmington, DE, USA) with Keras 2.11.023 and TensorFlow 2.11.0.24 Model output was analysed using R 3.6.1 (R Core Team, Vienna, Austria). The data were divided into a 70% training group and independent 30% test group by patient number. A subset of patients with incomplete iEEG localisation or non-overlapping locations were used as a secondary independent validation test set for the scalp EEG only model. Models were trained and tuned using leave-one-patient-out cross-validation on the training data, and then subsequently evaluated on the test data. Predictions from the training and test sets were compared with the real value using the area under the receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). The bootstrap method using 2000 iterations of randomly generated ROC curves from each set was used to test significance between ROC curves. A P-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

EEG data during emergence

Twelve patients were enrolled in this study (Table 1). The average age was 39.1 yr at the time of surgery. Eight patients were female, and four were male. Eight patients identified as white. Seizure foci location included right temporal lobe (n=2), bilateral hippocampal (n=1), right frontal lobe (n=1), multifocal (n=2), left insula (n=1), left temporal lobe (n=2), right hemisphere (n=1), and inconclusive (n=2). Five patients were excluded from the iEEG portion of the analysis owing to incomplete electrode localisation or lack of overlapping lead locations used in our model, and these patients were instead used as separate independent validation data for our scalp EEG only model. The remaining seven patients had scalp EEG and iEEG recorded from the completion of iEEG electrode placement and throughout the emergence from anaesthesia with simultaneous assessment of the OAA/S score. An example of a subset of the leads of the overall recorded data is depicted in Figure 3, with the OAA/S score and corresponding spectrograms for visualisation. The OAA/S score of all patients during emergence from anaesthesia is shown in Figure 4. In total, 8361 s of simultaneous EEG recordings were derived from the seven patients. The five scalp EEG only patients generated 7784 s of recording. Predictions were made at each second in time using the previous 5 s of electrophysiological signal using a sliding window. The 8361 s were divided into a training set of 5842 samples and a test set of 2519 samples.

Prediction of dichotomised OAA/S score

Prediction models based on iEEG only, scalp EEG only, and combined iEEG and scalp EEG resulted in test AUCs of 0.764, 0.750, and 0.795, respectively (Fig. 5a, c, and e). The corresponding training AUCs were 0.936, 0.991, and 0.979, respectively. On the independent scalp EEG only validation data set, the scalp EEG only model achieved an AUC of 0.844 (Fig. 5c).

Receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves for training and test set data for intracranial EEG (iEEG) (a), scalp EEG (c), and combined iEEG and scalp EEG (e) models for the prediction of Observer's Assessment of Alertness/Sedation (OAA/S) score of 3–5 for a test set patient. Associated prediction probability trajectories (grey), locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) fits on the probability trajectory (blue), and recorded OAA/S scores (orange) are shown in the corresponding panels b, d, and f. (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the Web version of this article.)

The AUC was significantly better using scalp EEG combined with iEEG than using iEEG only (P=0.02) or scalp EEG only (P<0.001). The predictive power of iEEG only or scalp EEG only was not significantly different (P=0.38). Although the trajectory during emergence from anaesthesia for iEEG showed more variable prediction probabilities over time, fitting a locally estimated scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) curve demonstrates that the probability of a high OAA/S score remains below 0.5 before regaining consciousness (Fig. 5b). The iEEG prediction probabilities also appear to slightly precede the actual emergence in time, although this was not tested because of the small sample size of the test set. In comparison, the scalp EEG prediction probabilities were much less variable when patients were unconscious but nearing emergence, and the predicted timing appeared to overlap with the actual emergence from anaesthesia (Fig. 5d). The combination of iEEG and scalp EEG showed the most reliable (or least variable) prediction while maintaining the temporal relationship associated with iEEG only prediction (Fig. 5f).

Discussion

Analysing iEEG and scalp EEG simultaneously during emergence from anaesthesia provides a rare opportunity to study the interplay between these signals and the depth of sedation. The majority of previous studies using EEG to predict the depth of sedation and commercially available monitors use traditional machine learning techniques, which rely on user derived features such as spectral signatures.25,26 However, these approaches can be biased to the features thought to be significant, and relatively few studies have examined the effects of anaesthesia on simultaneous iEEG for predicting the depth of sedation.27 Thus, our study leverages deep learning techniques, which can extract relevant features from raw data, enabling automated feature engineering, feature selection, and prediction in one model. Furthermore, because of the limited amount of preprocessing and feature creation required, our deep learning model takes less than 1 s to be applied to our moving windows and thus performs fast enough to be used in real time.

Previous studies using deep learning with EEG have predominantly used convolutional neural networks (CNNs), followed by recurrent neural networks (RNNs), or a combination of the two. The advent of transformers28 uses attention mechanisms with input weights to better capture interactions within sequential data and have largely replaced the use of RNNs. Based on previous work and these new advancements, we devised a novel combined approach of generating representations using a CNN first followed by a transformer for classification of OAA/S score (Fig. 2).

When predicting OAA/S score, iEEG only performed similarly to scalp EEG only (Fig. 5). The AUCs of 0.750–0.795 are similar to the AUC of 0.80 reported previously, with a deep learning-based classification of sedation.21 The latter study was in ICU and focused on differentiating deep sedation/unarousable (Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale [RASS]29 of –4 or –5) vs drowsy/alert (RASS of –1 or 0), which may have greater differences in their EEG signals than those in our study, which also includes light and moderate sedation.

Combining scalp EEG and iEEG appears to leverage the improved early prediction from iEEG and decreased variability from scalp EEG to attain a smoother prediction probability trajectory while still accurately identifying level of consciousness (Fig. 5f). The improvement with combining scalp EEG and iEEG is likely multifactorial. The scalp EEG data contain more channels, are more uniform in their location, and have a wider spatial spread. Subsequently, the model can use spatial 2-D convolutions to capture patterns across leads, which was not feasible with the available iEEG leads. Each scalp EEG electrode also samples brain activity from a larger volume of brain tissue than a singular contact from an iEEG lead.8 Conversely, iEEG data have been shown to be less affected by, though not immune to, certain artifacts such as electromyographic (EMG) signals.30,31 Furthermore, in addition to capturing a greater area of the brain, the addition of iEEG data may contribute features specific to iEEG recordings that improve performance. We speculate that combining the two sources potentially enabled improved signal to noise ratio while capturing a larger area of brain activity.

iEEG prediction probabilities exhibited greater variability than scalp EEG prediction probabilities. This may be in part owing to a combination of noise and signal from the frequent assessment of OAA/S score every 30 s, which could result in motion artifact. Alternatively, assessment of the OAA/S score may also result in subtle lightening of sedation from the stimulus required for scoring which would lead to variability during continuous EEG monitoring. Unfortunately, this is a limitation of the OAA/S score and other sedation scores, in that assessment of the score requires a stimulus which may transiently affect the level of sedation. Although we see substantial fluctuations, the smoothed fit continued to predict correctly that the patient had an OAA/S score of 0–2. Thus, an averaging approach, as utilised by many commercial monitors, has the potential to overcome the effects of noise and artifact to continue providing an accurate prediction. The combination of iEEG and scalp EEG had greater predictive power, although the improvement in the AUC was small compared with scalp EEG or iEEG only. Furthermore, the scalp EEG model achieved good performance (AUC=0.844) on our secondary validation test set, indicating promise for generalisability of our model to additional scalp EEG data. Thus, using scalp EEG only prediction can still provide valuable, and in many cases, likely sufficient information about the depth of sedation.

Overall, our data show that deep learning applied to EEG has the capability to differentiate deep sedation from little to no sedation. The combined iEEG and scalp EEG model achieved better performance than either modality alone. Scalp EEG only models were similar and outperformed the initial test set on additional data. Incorporation of more leads that capture additional spatial relationships might further improve scalp EEG models that predict the depth of anaesthesia. Beyond predicting the level of sedation, further studies using deep learning models with a larger dataset and more intracranial recordings could also provide insight into the electrophysiological underpinnings of recovery from disorders of consciousness.

Limitations

The sample size of patients with combined iEEG and scalp EEG recordings was small (n=7), although each patient had a substantial amount of EEG recorded. Other related studies also involved relatively few patients (nine in Antony and colleagues12; 10 in Abel and colleagues,22 and 19 in Li and colleagues13), and iEEG data acquisition is frequently the limiting resource. Overall, the model performance was limited by the total number of subjects, which contributed to overfitting, where the model learns features on the training data that may not apply to additional data. This resulted in the performance of the model on the training dataset to be better than on additional independent data, and might adversely impact generalisability to new patients. Our secondary validation set of scalp EEG data did achieve good performance, indicating there is potential for our model to perform well on new data. In addition, we performed limited preprocessing of the data to avoid introducing bias from filtering or manual artifact detection and to enable the model to be more easily transferrable. However, because of this, there exists artifact such as EMG that might be contributing to the performance of our classifier.

As iEEG is an invasive technique, work like this relies on clinically indicated placement of iEEG leads, limiting the ability to capture data from additional relevant areas of the brain. As iEEG electrodes in this study were placed depending on the location of each patient's seizure foci, the number of overlapping locations among all patients limited our ability to include additional iEEG leads. As iEEG captures very specific locations in the brain, additional electrodes would likely improve the performance of both models and would enable improved understanding of the specific feature contributions of iEEG data. As our study focuses on the emergence of anaesthesia, the patient is deeply sedated most of the time, and thus there is a class imbalance (more OAA/S scores of 0–2 as compared with OAA/S 3–5) which poses a challenge for classification. Additional EEG recordings in the awake state or under light sedation might help mitigate this issue and lend further insight into the transition to consciousness.
