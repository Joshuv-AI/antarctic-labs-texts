Acute effects of high-intensity interval exercise and moderate-intensity continuous training on arterial stiffness and endothelial function in hypertension: A crossover trial
Authors: Wei Feng, Wei-liang Yu, Zhuang Zhao, Yao-dong Guo, Xiao-jie Zhou, Kai-qi Zhai, Xiao Liu, Zhi-wei Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractHypertension contributes to endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness, elevating cardiovascular disease risk. While moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) and high-intensity interval training (HIIT) have demonstrated long-term benefits for these vascular conditions, their acute effects on hypertensive patients are unclear. Thirty participants with essential hypertension completed a baseline testing session, followed by a HIIT session (10 × 1 min at 90% VO2peak) and then a MICT session of equal energy (32 min at 65% VO2peak) after a 1-week washout (no exercise). Measurements were taken at baseline, immediately postexercise, 5 min post-exercise, and 24 h post-exercise. The primary outcome was flow-mediated dilation (FMD), with secondary outcomes including the arterial basal internal diameter, peak diameter, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), pulse pressure (PP), mean arterial pressure (MAP), heart rate (HR), wall shear stress, brachial‒ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), and the ankle‒brachial index (ABI). A total of 30 hypertensive participants (16 males, 14 females; mean age 54 ± 4 years) were included. Significant intervention-specific effects were observed: MICT acutely increased both the arterial basal diameter (4.58 to 4.84 mm, P = 0.036) and peak diameter (4.77 to 5.05 mm, P = 0.025), whereas HIIT induced no significant changes. HIIT provoked a greater immediate increase in SBP (159 ± 9 vs 156 ± 9 mmHg; P < 0.001) and increased PP (64 to 70 mmHg, P < 0.001) than did MICT, in contrast to the reduction in MICT in the PP after 5 min. The immediate postexercise HR increase was significantly greater after HIIT than after MICT (105 ± 9 bpm vs. 101 ± 9 bpm; P < 0.001). BaPWV decreased after MICT (17.56 to 15.75 m/s, P < 0.001) but remained unchanged after HIIT (P > 0.05). No differential effects were found for wall shear stress, FMD or ABI (P > 0.05) between HIIT and MICT. Both types of exercise led to an immediate increase in SBP postexercise, but MICT decreased at 5 min. MICT was more effective in reducing arterial stiffness and improving vasodilation, with its impact on arterial stiffness lasting up to 24 h. Introduction

Hypertension, defined by high blood pressure in blood vessels, is a major risk factor for cardiovascular diseases (CVDs)1. Chronic hypertension leads to increased intravascular pressure, which causes endothelial dysfunction, thickening of the endovascular media, and collagen deposition. These changes result in increased arterial stiffness, peripheral resistance, and the development of atherosclerosis2,3. Therefore, prevention of endothelial dysfunction and increased vascular stiffness are essential for alleviating atherosclerosis in hypertensive patients.

Regular exercise, as a nonpharmacologic intervention, benefits cardiovascular health in hypertensive patients by activating multiple endogenous protective mechanisms4,5. Moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT) is commonly used in the prevention and rehabilitation of hypertensive patients because of its clinical benefits and wide applicability6,7. MICT has antihypertensive benefits in part because it modulates peripheral resistance by improving endothelial dysfunction and reducing arterial stiffness8,9. The potential mechanisms by which MICT promotes vascular health are complex and include inducing laminar shear stress to promote nitric oxide synthase (NOS) expression, increasing the bioavailability of NO, inducing microangiogenesis, and exerting circulating anti-inflammatory and antioxidant effects4–6,8–11. Thus, MICT promotes vascular health in hypertensive patients by integrating multiple biological mechanisms to regulate blood pressure homeostasis.

High-intensity interval training (HIIT) has emerged as a promising alternative to MICT, demonstrating superior benefits for cardiovascular health and endothelial function12,13. This advantage may stem from its unique hemodynamic profile: HIIT generates intermittent oscillatory shear stress with higher peak magnitudes than MICT does, thereby acutely stimulating greater nitric oxide (NO) release and enhancing vasodilation7,14. However, emerging evidence suggests that this oscillatory stress manifests as distinct turbulent shear patterns15,16, unlike the vasculoprotective laminar flow induced by MICT. Critically, while these turbulent patterns may transiently activate oxidative stress pathways and challenge endothelial homeostasis14, the cyclical nature of the mechanical loading of HIIT drives adaptive vascular remodeling over time. This occurs through recurrent eNOS phosphorylation17, despite transient increases in vascular wall stress during acute BP spikes18,19.

Although both MICT and HIIT have demonstrated vascular benefits with long-term training, research on their acute effects on vascular health remains limited, with significant variations across different populations 9,20. Acute sessions of both HIIT and MICT have been shown to improve insulin sensitivity in patients with diabetes, contributing to better control of atherosclerosis20,21. However, in obese adults, acute HIIT and MICT may transiently increase the risk of atherosclerosis22, likely due to sympathetic overactivity, which accelerates atherogenesis through α1-adrenergic-mediated proinflammatory cytokine release (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) and reactive oxygen species bursts23,24.

Understanding the physiological responses to acute exercise is crucial for elucidating the physiological adaptation mechanisms of chronic exercise. This is particularly significant given the notable heterogeneity observed across diverse populations, such as individuals with obesity or diabetes20,22, while the physiological responses in hypertensive patients remain poorly understood. Thus, we aimed to evaluate the acute effects of HIIT and MICT on atherosclerosis and endothelial function in hypertensive patients, providing valuable insights for optimizing exercise interventions.

Materials and methods

Study design

Thirty participants with essential hypertension completed a baseline testing session, followed by a HIIT session (10 × 1 min at 90% VO2peak) on the treadmill and then a MICT session with equal training volume (32 min at 65% VO2peak) after a 1-week washout (no exercise). Measurements were taken during the baseline session, immediately postexercise, 5 min post-exercise, and 24 h post-exercise. The primary outcome was flow-mediated dilation (FMD), with secondary outcomes including peak diameter, arterial basal internal diameter, wall shear stress (WSS), systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), pulse pressure (PP), brachial‒ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV), and the ankle‒brachial index (ABI). All participants taking antihypertensive medications were permitted by their attending physicians to discontinue their medications prior to the exercise test and remained off their medications during the 24-h period following the test, during which all physiological parameters were measured. Professional medical personnel were present throughout the study to respond to any emergencies. All participants signed written informed consent forms after fully understanding the purpose of the study and the potential risks involved. This trial followed the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Normal University (number: 2024–01-27). (see Fig. 1).

Summary diagram of the study design.

Participants and recruitment

Inclusion criteria

Participants were considered eligible on the basis of the following criteria: (1) aged 50–65 years; (2) SBP ranging from 140 to 179 mmHg and DBP ranging from 90 to 109 mmHg; and (3) no regular exercise habits within the 3 months prior to the commencement of the intervention.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: (1) currently participating in other scientific experiments; (2) suffering from musculoskeletal diseases leading to impaired motor function; (3) having cognitive impairments such as dementia; (4) having cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases other than hypertension; (5) being diagnosed with cancer; (6) being diagnosed with diabetes or other metabolic diseases; and (7) being at risk of falling.

Intervention

Exercise intensity, duration, and heart rate (HR) were recorded during exercise, and adverse events that occurred during exercise were recorded. To reduce the risk of bias in the interpretation of our study results, an equal training volume for both the HIIT and MICT protocols was used to match the HIIT and MICT protocols. We used the following calculation25: the average VO2peak of all participants before training was 1.79 L/min. The HIIT condition included 10 1‐min exercises at 1.61 L/min (90% of VO2peak) and 10 2‐min exercises at 1.07 L/min (60% of VO2peak). The total VO2 time relationship for the high‐intensity condition was then divided by the intensity of the moderate‐intensity condition (37.5 L/1.16 L/min = 32 min). To facilitate the regulation of exercise intensity, we converted the percentage of VO2peak into the corresponding percentage of HRpeak for monitoring. For example, an intensity of 50–60% VO2peak typically corresponds to 50–60% HRpeak, thereby enabling indirect control of exercise load. All the participants used an HR monitor (Polar Electro, Kempele, Finland) during each training session. Thus, participants were able to control the corresponding exercise HR relative to the VO2peak (see Fig. 2). Throughout the training sessions, the speed of the treadmill was constantly adjusted to ensure that each session was performed at the prescribed intensity.

The protocol of exercise.

HIIT conditions

The HIIT session consists of a 5‐min warm‐up and cool‐down at a walking pace (50%–60% HRpeak) and 10 1‐min intervals at high intensity (85%–95% HRpeak) interspersed with 10 2‐min intervals at moderate exercise (60%–70% HRpeak). This session is conducted in the form of running, aiming for a Borg RPE scale rating of 16–17.

MICT conditions

Each MICT session comprised a 5‐min warm‐up and a 5‐min cool‐down phase (50%–60% HRpeak), which was completed by walking. This is interspersed with 32‐min moderate exercise (65%–75% HRpeak), which is completed by running.

Measurements

Blinded research assistants collected baseline data, including age, height, weight, BMI, and blood pressure, from the participants. Measurements, including blood pressure, FMD, baPWV, and ABI, were performed by blinded cardiologists and exercise physiologists. Before cardiovascular function testing, all participants were asked to fast for at least 6 h and avoid consuming specific foods (such as high-nitrate foods, alcohol, tea, tobacco, and caffeine) and medications that may affect vascular function within 12 h. This requirement was established on the basis of our previous studies and relevant guidelines26,27. The primary outcome measure was the change from FMD%. The secondary outcomes included the systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, pulse pressure, mean arterial pressure (MAP), arterial basal internal diameter, peak diameter, WSS, baPWV, and ABI.

Flow-mediated dilation

The vascular endothelial function of participants was tested by flow-mediated dilation (FMD), which is considered the gold standard for assessing vascular endothelial function11. FMD was performed by an experienced sonographer who was unaware of the experimental protocol using UNEX (unexp18g, UNEX, Japan). To avoid heterogeneity, all participants underwent right-arm FMD testing in a quiet, temperature-controlled room (22–25 °C). During the test, the participant was kept in the supine position with the arm fixed on the test table, the right arm abducted 90° on the arm pad, and the brachial artery located 2–10 cm above the transverse strip of the elbow was selected as the test point. An ultrasound probe was used to find a clear vascular image, and after obtaining a clear vascular image, the probe test point was marked for each participant (marked with a marker and photographed for documentation). The test points were kept in the same position until subsequent evaluations. The cuff was released after at least 5 min of compression for each trial, and the maximum vessel diameter was recorded during the last 2 min after compression. FMD was expressed as the percentage change in arterial diameter relative to baseline. Absolute diameter change = peak diameter—arterial basal internal diameter. FMD (%) = (peak diameter—arterial basal internal diameter)/(arterial basal internal diameter) × 100%. WSS = kηV/D, where k is a constant that reflects the relationship between the mean flow velocity and the velocity gradient at the vessel wall and is commonly represented by a value of 428. η is the blood viscosity (mPa·s-1), V is the mean carotid blood flow velocity (cm·s-1), and D is the arterial basal internal diameter (cm).

Blood pressure

The BP of the participants was tested with an Omron HEM-907 automated blood pressure device. The test was performed with the participants seated and their nondominant arms used for measurement. Three blood pressure recordings were taken at 1-min intervals, and the average of the last two readings was chosen as the result. The parameters measured included SBP, DBP, MAP, and PP. PP = SBP—DBP. MAP = DBP + 1/3​(SBP-DBP).

Brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity

The arterial stiffness of the participants was assessed via brachial‒ankle pulse wave velocity (baPWV)29, a reliable and well-established index of arterial stiffness. The baPWV test was performed with a BP-203RPE device (Omron, Tokyo, Japan) by an experienced cardiologist who was blinded to the experimental protocol. Four pressure cuffs were attached to both the humerus and the ankle of each participant. The time interval (T) between the humeral wave front and the ankle wave front was calculated. The distance between the heart and the sampling point (L) was calculated automatically on the basis of the height of the participant, where “La” is the length from the heart to the ankle and “Lb” is the length from the heart to the humerus. The BaPWV was calculated via the following formula: BaPWV = (La-Lb)/T. Three measurements on each side were taken and averaged for analysis.

Ankle brachial index

The vascular stiffness of the participants was tested by the ABI, which is considered to be a reliable index for assessing vascular stiffness30. The ABI test was performed by an experienced cardiologist who was unaware of the experimental protocol used (BP-203RPE, Omron, Tokyo, Japan). The ABI was calculated as the ratio of bilateral ankle systolic blood pressure to the highest systolic blood pressure in the arm, using the lower ABI values for both legs.

Cardiopulmonary exercise test

The VO2peak of the participants was evaluated via the incremental ramp-type protocol. Ventilation indices and gas exchange analyses were performed with a Cortex Gas Metabolism analyzer (MetaMax 3B, Italy), which was manually calibrated and completed before each test. The protocol consisted of a 2‐min warm‐up at 4 km/h, followed by an increase of 0.3 km/h every 30 s until physical exhaustion. The treadmill was set at a 1% incline during the test. The recovery period consisted of 4 min, starting at 5 km/h and decreasing by 1 km/h each minute. The CPET takes the average value of the last 30 s of the exercise stage as the VO2peak31. An exercise test was considered maximal with the fulfillment of any two: voluntary exhaustion; a respiratory quotient greater than 1.1; an HR greater than 90% of the age-predicted maximum HR (208—0.7 × age)32; a plateau in oxygen uptake; and a Borg scale score ≥ 1733.

Power calculation

The required sample size was not determined before the study began, and a post hoc power analysis was performed. A paired t test was used to compare flow-mediated dilation between the HIIT and MICT groups. With the 30 participants who completed the crossover trial, the study had a statistical power of 80.3% to detect a between-group mean difference of 0.51 (SD 0.96) in the change in FMD, assuming a two-sided alpha level of 0.05.

Statistical analysis

The normality of continuous variables was assessed via the Shapiro‒Wilk test. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics, reporting means ± standard deviations (SDs) for normally distributed variables and medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) for nonnormally distributed variables. Categorical variables are presented as frequencies and percentages. Comparisons between experimental conditions and time points were conducted via repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni correction. Statistical analyses were performed via IBM SPSS Statistics version 27.0.1 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), with significance set at P < 0.05.

Secondary outcomes

Arterial basal internal diameter

A significant time × trial interaction was identified (F = 3.812, Pc = 0.028). In the MICT trial, the arterial basal internal diameter increased significantly from 4.58 ± 0.66 mm before exercise to 4.84 ± 0.56 mm at 5 min post-exercise (P = 0.036). No significant changes from preexercise levels were observed in the HIIT trial over time (P > 0.05). At 5 min post-exercise, a significant difference between trials was noted (P = 0.008), with MICT increasing to 4.84 ± 0.56 mm and HIIT decreasing to 4.46 ± 0.51 mm from preexercise. (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Peak diameter

A significant time × trial interaction was observed (F = 4.236, Pc = 0.019). In the MICT trial, the peak diameter increased significantly from 4.77 ± 0.67 mm before exercise to 5.05 ± 0.58 mm immediately postexercise (P = 0.025). No significant changes from preexercise levels were observed in the HIIT trial over time (P > 0.05). Immediately postexercise, a significant difference between trials was noted (P = 0.005), with the MICT trial increasing to 5.05 ± 0.58 mm and the HIIT trial decreasing to 4.63 ± 0.54 mm from preexercise. (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Absolute diameter change

No significant differences were observed in the absolute diameter change between the HIIT and MICT groups at either the 5 min post-intervention (P = 0.310) or the 24-h timepoint (P = 0.200).

Systolic blood pressure

Significant time effects (F = 124.783, Pa < 0.001) and time × trial interactions were observed (F = 12.647, Pc < 0.001). In the MICT trial, SBP increased significantly from 154 ± 9 mmHg before exercise to 156 ± 9 mmHg immediately postexercise (P < 0.001). Five minutes later, the SBP decreased to 150 ± 10 mmHg, which was a significant reduction compared with the preexercise value (P < 0.001). In the HIIT trial, SBP increased significantly from 154 ± 9 mmHg to 159 ± 9 mmHg immediately postexercise (P < 0.001). No significant differences in SBP changes were observed between the two trials at any time point (P > 0.05). (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Diastolic blood pressure

No significant time effects (F = 2.754, Pa = 0.051), trial effects (F = 0.002, Pb = 0.967) or time × trial interactions (F = 0.370, Pc = 0.775) were observed, indicating that neither trial resulted in significant changes in DBP from preexercise. Additionally, no significant differences in DBP changes were observed between the two trials at any time point (P > 0.05). (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Mean arterial pressure

A significant main effect of time (F = 22.569, Pa < 0.001) was found for MAP, indicating that the MAP changed significantly over time in both HIIT and MICT. However, there was no significant main effect of trial (F = 0.102, Pb = 0.751) and no significant time × trial interaction (F = 0.904, Pc = 0.445), indicating that the overall levels of MAP did not differ significantly between the two trials. (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Pulse pressure

Significant time effects (F = 37.032, Pa < 0.001) and time × trial interactions (F = 4.328, Pc = 0.008) were observed. In the MICT trial, PP significantly decreased from 64 ± 11 mmHg before exercise to 61 ± 10 mmHg after 5 min (P = 0.008). The HIIT trial revealed a significant increase in PP from 64 ± 11 mmHg before exercise to 70 ± 9 mmHg immediately postexercise (P < 0.001). No significant differences in PP changes were observed between the two trials at any time point (P > 0.05). (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Heart rate

Significant time effects (F = 788.143, Pa < 0.001) and time × trial interactions were observed (F = 4.497, Pc = 0.015). In the MICT trial, the HR increased significantly from 74 ± 10 bpm before exercise to 101 + 9 bpm immediately after exercise (P < 0.001). In the HIIT trial, HR increased significantly from 74 ± 10 bpm before exercise to 105 + 9 bpm immediately after exercise (P < 0.001). Moreover, the immediate postexercise HR increase was significantly greater after HIIT than after MICT (P < 0.001). (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Wall shear stress

A significant main effect of time (F = 48.928, Pa < 0.001) was found for the wall shear stress, indicating that the wall shear stress changed significantly over time in both trials. However, there was no significant main effect of trial (F = 0.001, Pb = 0.972) and no significant time × trial interaction (F = 0.313, Pc = 0.719), indicating that the overall levels of wall shear stress did not differ significantly between the two trials. (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Brachial-a nkle pulse wave velocity

Significant time effects (F = 14.728, Pa < 0.001) and time × trial interactions (F = 7.938, Pc < 0.001) were observed. In the MICT trial, the baPWV decreased significantly from 17.56 ± 4.25 m/s before exercise to 15.75 ± 3.34 m/s immediately after exercise (P < 0.001). Twenty-four hours later, it increased to 16.38 ± 3.55 m/s, remaining significantly lower than the preexercise value (P < 0.001). In the HIIT trial, no significant changes from preexercise occurred over time (P > 0.05). No significant differences in baPWV changes were observed between the two trials at any time point (P > 0.05). (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Ankle–brachial index

No significant time effects (F = 0.669, Pa = 0.511), trial effects (F = 0.681, Pb = 0.413) or time × trial interactions (F = 0.954, Pc = 0.387) were observed for ABI changes, indicating that there were no significant changes from before exercise in either intervention (P > 0.05). In addition, no significant differences were found between the two trials at any time point (P > 0.05). (see Table 2 and Fig. 3).

Discussion

Major findings

This crossover study investigated the acute effects of HIIT and MICT in hypertensive patients. Although neither modality elicited short-term improvements in endothelial function as measured by FMD, MICT demonstrated unique advantages in modulating vascular dynamics. At 5 min post-exercise, MICT induced transient vasodilation, as evidenced by increased arterial basal internal diameter and peak diameter, whereas HIIT demonstrated no significant hemodynamic changes. Notably, MICT reduced stiffness (baPWV reduction), with effects detectable at 24 h, whereas HIIT resulted in no such vascular changes. Blood pressure during the acute recovery phase (0–5 min) differed significantly: MICT triggered an initial SBP elevation followed by a reduction within this 5-min observation window, accompanied by reduced pulse pressure, whereas HIIT provoked more pronounced immediate SBP and PP surges. Notably, subgroup analyses by sex indicated that these observed patterns of vascular and hemodynamic responses did not differ significantly between male and female participants (see Supplemental Table 1). We emphasize that these findings are confined to the measured timepoints (immediate/5 min/24 h); longer-term hemodynamic patterns remain unexplored. Both interventions ultimately normalized hemodynamic parameters within 24 h. Collectively, these acute phase findings suggest that acute MICT results in more favorable vascular modulation than HIIT does, particularly through its dual capacity to reduce arterial stiffness while mitigating postexercise blood pressure elevation during early recovery in hypertensive patients, an effect that was consistently observed across both sexes.

Vascular endothelial cells have received extensive attention as target tissues that respond to endogenous stimuli from exercise. The contrasting acute and chronic vascular responses to HIIT and MICT in hypertensive patients highlight the complex interplay between hemodynamic stimuli, neuroendocrine activation, and endothelial adaptation4,5,8,9,26,34–42. While neither modality elicited comparable acute changes in FMD, the transient peripheral vasodilation observed after MICT—evidenced by increased arterial basal internal diameter and peak diameter—suggests a distinct mechanistic pathway. Contrary to our initial hypotheses, postexercise wall shear stress measurements were not significantly different between the HIIT and MICT trials in the upper (inactive) limbs. However, it is possible that the steady rhythmic nature of MICT may promote shear stress patterns distinct from those of HIIT during exercise in inactive limbs. These differences in endothelial signaling patterns might favor eNOS activation and NO bioavailability during MICT, enhancing acute vasodilation4,5,8,9,43. This aligns with the immediate postexercise reduction in baPWV, which is indicative of reduced arterial stiffness. In contrast, the lack of acute vasodilation in HIIT may reflect its distinct hemodynamic shear profile15,16. Emerging hemodynamic evidence indicates that HIIT generates distinct turbulent shear stress patterns15,16. Unlike the vasculoprotective laminar flow induced by MICT, this oscillatory shear stress may predominantly activate proinflammatory pathways (e.g., NF-κB and ET-1) and suppress eNOS expression/activity, thereby attenuating acute NO-dependent vasodilation4–6,8–11,17.While chronic exercise studies have reported divergent adaptation pathways, our acute data do not address long-term effects44–46. Clinically, the rapid hemodynamic stabilization of MICT is particularly relevant for stage 2–3 hypertensive patients, who face elevated risks of postexercise cardiovascular events7. Unlike recent trials demonstrating the superior chronic endothelial benefits of HIIT in low-risk cohorts47, our acute-phase data reveal its transient hemodynamic disadvantage—highlighting the need for phased exercise prescription: beginning with MICT for immediate stabilization before progressing to HIIT. This approach aligns with the recommendations of the ESC 20237.

The transient postexercise blood pressure fluctuations observed in this study reflect the net effects of competing vasodilatory and vasoconstrictive forces, which are modulated by exercise intensity and duration40,48. The acute increase in SBP following both HIIT and MICT aligns with exercise-induced increases in cardiac output and sympathetic-driven vasoconstriction38. However, the rapid postexercise SBP reduction in MICT—below baseline levels at 5 min—suggests a delayed dominance of NO-mediated vasodilation and parasympathetic reactivation26,40,49,50. This phenomenon may be attributed to the prolonged endothelial activation induced by MICT, which sustains NO release even after exercise cessation, counteracting residual sympathetic activity26,51. In contrast, the abrupt return of SBP to baseline within 5 min in HIIT implies a transient vasodilatory window overwhelmed by persistent sympathetic tone26,38,49,52. Notably, the lack of DBP changes across both modalities underscores that a lack of worsening arterial stiffness is critical for buffering diastolic pressure53. The elevated cardiac output during exercise increases shear stress, stimulating endothelial NO release and peripheral vasodilation, which redistributes blood flow without significantly altering arteriolar resistance44–46,54.Moreover, the acute sustained reduction in HIIT in arterial stiffness, reflected in baPWV reduction, reduces pulse pressure amplification—a key determinant of end‒organ damage in hypertension2,3,53,55,56. All interpretations are confined to acute responses. This mechanistic insight extends beyond theoretical models: the 34% faster SBP normalization with MICT directly addresses clinical concerns about exercise-induced BP surges in guideline-directed therapy7. Whereas meta-analyses confirm the long-term efficacy of HIIT in stable patients (e.g., 12-week HIIT reduces SBP by 12 mmHg)7, our findings necessitate risk stratification—vulnerable patients (SBP > 160 mmHg) may require initial MICT conditioning to avoid hemodynamic decompensation during early exercise adoption7,57.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the present study did not test at multiple time points after exercise, so the time course of the cardiovascular response to acute exercise could not be determined. Second, baseline measurements were conducted on a separate day (Day 1) from postexercise testing (Days 2 and 9), rather than on the same day. This may introduce additional variability due to potential day‒to-day fluctuations under physiological conditions, thereby affecting the reliability of the comparisons. Third, the relatively small sample size may limit the statistical power to detect subtle changes in certain outcomes. Furthermore, the participants were all Asian, which may limit the generalizability of the findings to other regions and ethnic groups. Fourth, the absence of postexercise measurements beyond 5 min (particularly during the 30–60 min peak endothelial adaptation window) precludes comprehensive characterization of cardiovascular recovery dynamics, necessitated by immediate occlusion cuff requirements for flow-mediated dilation assessment and concerns over measurement confounding. Fifth, the study used a fixed order of exercise interventions (all participants performed HIIT first followed by MICT after a one-week washout), rather than a counterbalanced design. While a one-week washout period was implemented to mitigate carryover effects such as learning or stress habituation, the lack of randomization in visit order means that potential sequence effects cannot be fully ruled out as a confounding factor.​​ Sixth, the absence of direct shear stress measurements during exercise precludes definitive conclusions about sustained versus cyclical shear stress patterns between HIIT and MICT. Although we supplemented postexercise shear stress data, the lack of real-time hemodynamic monitoring during active movement remains an important constraint. Finally, this study did not delve into the potential mechanisms of arterial stiffness and endothelial function in response to changes in different modes of exercise. Future studies should include diverse types of recruitment, integrated physiological profiling, ​real-time hemodynamic assessment during exercise, and randomized intervention sequences.

Conclusion

Both HIIT and MICTe led to an immediate increase in SBP postexercise, but MICT decreased at 5 min. MICT was more effective than HIIT in reducing arterial stiffness and improving vasodilation, with its impact on arterial stiffness lasting up to 24 h. The ability of MICT to reduce acute vascular stiffness may be prioritized for hypertensive patients requiring immediate hemodynamic stabilization.
