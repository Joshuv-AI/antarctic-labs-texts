Artificial intelligence in healthcare: a qualitative study exploring consumer perspectives
Authors: Vicki Hobson-Green, Quita Olsen, Clair Sullivan, Leanna Woods
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) holds significant promise for healthcare, yet its real-world adoption remains limited, partly due to uncertainty about healthcare consumer acceptance. While existing ethical frameworks provide high-level principles, there remains a need to understand healthcare consumer perspectives to ensure AI integration reflects their values and preferences. This study addresses the question: What are healthcare consumers’ perspectives on AI in healthcare?MethodsA cross-sectional qualitative research study involving online focus groups was conducted in April 2025. Participants were self-selecting adult healthcare consumers or caregivers who had accessed public healthcare in Queensland, Australia in the preceding 24 months. Analysis was guided by the World Health Organisation (WHO) ethical principles, combining deductive classifications with inductive thematic analysis. Themes were refined through consensus.ResultsConsumers’ perspectives on AI in healthcare were captured in 19 themes across all six WHO principles. The most frequently discussed principles were inclusiveness and equity (Principle 5, five themes) and transparency, explainability, and intelligibility (Principle 3, four themes). Participants emphasised the need for clear communication, equity, transparency and preservation of human interaction in clinical care.ConclusionsSustaining healthcare consumers trust and upholding traditional healthcare values by ensuring human connection in healthcare are critical for successful AI integration. Insights from this study, combined with existing evidence, can inform the future development of a values-based framework and social licence to guide safe, ethical and consumer-centred implementation of AI in healthcare.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01535-y.Keywords: Digital health, Artificial intelligence, Healthcare consumers opinion, Patients, Ethics, Health services research, Qualitative research

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) has the potential to improve the effectiveness, efficiency, and accessibility of healthcare systems worldwide [1]. AI is increasingly being explored to support diagnostics, streamline administrative processes, predict patient outcomes, and personalise treatments [2]. Despite the potential of AI, the real-world implementation and adoption in healthcare remains limited. The slow uptake is not merely a matter of technological readiness but is influenced by the perceptions of patients, carers, and individuals who use the healthcare system (collectively, healthcare consumers [3]) and broader societal concerns surrounding AI in healthcare [4].

Evidence suggests healthcare consumers concerns about AI are multifaceted and commonly include: (i) limited transparency in AI systems often described as “black boxes” where decision making processes are difficult to understand, (ii) fears about data privacy and concerns AI may perpetuate inequities and systemic biases in training data, and (iii) the absence of strong, adaptable government regulation [5]. Consumers have also expressed apprehension that AI could replace human healthcare professionals, erode the human element of care, or introduce unintended bias into clinical decision-making [5]. These concerns may reduce trust, enthusiasm, and contribute to hesitation in participating in AI driven healthcare initiatives [6]. Importantly, recent literature findings indicate healthcare consumers are more likely to support the adoption and implementation of AI when they are consulted in the decision making process and when AI is used to support clinical decision making, rather than a replacement for clinicians [7].

The concept of social licence is increasingly recognised as critical to the responsible implementation of AI in healthcare. Social licence is the ongoing, informal approval granted by the public when they perceive an activity as trustworthy, ethically aligned and responsive to societal expectations. In the context of AI in healthcare, social licence requires transparency, confidence in data governance and evidence the benefits outweigh any potential risk [8]. Without public trust and acceptance, those implementing AI may encounter resistance or integration barriers [9]. In this context, social licence requires ongoing community support, confidence in data security, belief that the benefits outweigh the risks, and trust in the systems that govern AI use [10]. Without this, even the most advanced AI tools may face public pushback or obstacles to implementation [11].

Although global strategies have established regulatory frameworks and protocols, there still lacks comprehensive, consumer-led framework that embeds social licence values and expectations of diverse populations in AI development and implementation [12]. In Australia, national efforts including the Australian Alliance for Artificial Intelligence in Healthcare (AAAiH) and the National AI Centre (NAIC) have contributed important ethical, data governance and regulatory steps, however these are often expert-driven and may not necessarily incorporate values or perspectives of everyday health consumers [13]. Recent studies of healthcare consumers’ perspectives on AI in healthcare, have been mostly gathered through quantitative methods [5, 14, 15], typically focusing on specific constructs such as trust, acceptance or decision‑making [14–16]. Much of this work also includes comparisons with, or input from, other stakeholder groups affected by AI such as managers, researchers and clinicians [17], resulting in comparatively limited attention to consumers’ views on ethical AI as it relates directly to their experience of receiving healthcare services [17].

The gap identified highlights the implementation of AI in healthcare without an ethical framework and meaningful consumer input risks being unregulated, inequitable, and misaligned with consumer values, potentially impacting trust and health outcomes. Existing research highlights that trust, social licence and inclusivity are crucial to the legitimacy of AI in healthcare [9, 10, 18]. Developing an ethical framework would provide practical guidance to policymakers, healthcare professionals, and AI developers by embedding the principles of transparency, accountability, equity and consumer involvement. Such a framework would support the integration of AI across the health system while ensuring patient needs, values and outcomes remain central to care delivery [19].

At present, there is limited qualitative evidence on how healthcare consumers experience, interpret, and evaluate the ethical implications of AI in healthcare. To address this current gap, we conducted a qualitative study using focus groups with healthcare consumers to explore perspectives on the use of AI in healthcare. Guided by the principles from the World Health Organisation ethical AI framework [19], this study aimed to explore how healthcare consumers experience, interpret and evaluate AI in healthcare as it relates to predefined ethical principles. Specifically, the study addressed the research question: “What are healthcare consumers’ perspectives of AI in healthcare?”.

Methods

The study is reported using the consolidated criteria for reporting qualitative research (COREQ) checklist (Additional File 1), followed the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research [20] and received prospective Human Research Ethics Approval from the University of Queensland [ID: 2024/HE002534].

Setting and participants

The research was conducted in the geographically large state of Queensland in Australia (population ~ 5 million), via online videoconferencing to include healthcare consumers regardless of location of residence. Participants were recruited through the research centre’s networks and partner organisations using a convenience sampling approach via a public call for volunteers. Individuals self-selected, meaning they actively chose to participate rather than being purposely selected. Those who decided to take part may have particular interests or experiences related to the research topic and as a result, the sample may not fully represent the broader population, potentially limiting the generalisability of the results. The study did not seek representative samples but rather capture a range of perspectives of AI in healthcare.

Participants were screened for eligibility. To be eligible, participants had to: (i) have accessed the Queensland healthcare system within the past 24 months, either personally or as a primary caregiver, (ii) be aged 18 or older, (iii) be proficient in English, (iv) be able to use Microsoft Teams, and (v) provide informed consent.

For the purpose of this research, healthcare consumers are defined as patients, carers, and individuals who use the healthcare system [3]. This definition recognises individuals and communities affected by healthcare to have an important role in contributing to AI in healthcare policy, research and service delivery [3]. The inclusion of caregivers was deliberate to capture insights and experiences patients alone may not report, contributing to a more comprehensive understanding. Participants were required to have accessed the healthcare system within the past 24 months to ensure experiences were sufficiently recent to reflect current healthcare practices incorporating AI, while allowing an adequate timeframe to capture a broad range of relevant patient and caregiver experiences.

The study intentionally targeted a maximum of four participants per focus group to support more nuanced discussion and ensure participants had the opportunity to contribute. Smaller focus groups are often recommended for complex or emerging topics, as they encourage richer discussion and a more in-depth exploration of participant’s perspectives [21].

Participants were emailed an online survey where they were required to read the patient information sheet and submit their consent if they were willing to proceed. Reasons for non-participation included not meeting the eligibility criteria and withdrawing due to work commitments. During the screening process, it was noted that the interviewers had prior personal or professional relationships with some participants. Participants were advised they could withdraw from the study at any time. Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to data collection.

Data collection

Participants were invited to participate in online focus groups on Microsoft Teams and given four options across a two-week period. A student researcher (VHG, female) led the focus groups following a semi-structured guide (Additional File 2) and was assisted either by a second student researcher (QO, female), or senior researcher with extensive experience in qualitative research (LW, PhD, female). The research team consisted of clinician researchers and public health research students who shared a common research interest in the adoption of AI in healthcare. This interest motivated the study and informed the development of the semi-structured interview guide, which aimed to explore the healthcare consumers perspectives. The questions were developed based on recent work exploring the ethical considerations, consumers preferences and AI implementation [22–24]. Consistent with the study design, demographic information was not collected, with delineation between patients and caregivers not considered. Participants were free to respond to any element of their lived experience of receiving healthcare regardless of health service setting. No individuals other than the participants and researchers were present during the interviews. Focus groups were audio- and video recorded via web-based videoconferencing to ensure that non-verbal body language could be considered. A slide deck was presented to participants, including purpose of the research, focus group processes and a working definition of AI:

Systems designed to perform tasks that typically require human intelligence such as interpreting medical images, recognizing speech, making decisions, and translating languages. In the healthcare context, AI tools can analyse medical scans, test results, and patient records to assist healthcare professionals in diagnosing illnesses, predicting health risks, and selecting appropriate treatments. These systems are intended to support, not replace, healthcare professionals [25].

Repeat sessions were not conducted, and field notes were not created. Data saturation was not discussed as it was not the focus for determining sample size and is less meaningful in studies where themes are developed through the researcher’s interpretation guided by a classification framework, rather than simply emerging from the data itself [26].

Participants were offered remuneration for their time consistent with local consumer involvement rates and university policies. In this study, research participants were offered a AUD$50 gift card as a token of appreciation.

Focus group sessions were recorded with transcripts auto generated by Microsoft Teams, anonymised and corrected for accuracy. Transcripts were not returned to participants for comment or correction. Data were stored securely on the university’s secure research data management system, accessible only to the ethically approved research team.

Data analysis

Transcript data was coded by the research team using NVivo software (Version 14; QSR International). Data from all focus groups was analysed together in a mixed deductive-inductive approach, often referred to as an abductive analysis. First, the focus group transcripts were coded deductively to the six ‘Key Ethical Principles for Use of AI for Health’ authored by the WHO (19) to create a working theoretical framework (Table 1). The WHO framework was globally developed by a multi-disciplinary group of experts and informed by international cross-sector consultations over a two-year timeframe, is comprehensive and recognised, and designed to consider contemporary healthcare models, emphasising universal health coverage, health equity and socio-technical perspectives [19]. It draws on the collective views of an interdisciplinary WHO Expert Group, supporting its broad relevance, credibility and scalability across diverse health systems globally [19]. The deductive approach was intentionally chosen to situate consumer perspectives within a globally recognised and policy-relevant framework, given our aim to understand perspectives in a way that can inform real-world AI implementation. All transcript data were deductively coded by two researchers (VHG and QO) by allocation of text segments to one or more of the six predefined WHO principles based on its relevance.

Second, to obtain a rich and deeper understanding of the data and ensure interpretive depth, an inductive analysis was performed on text segments within each and every ethical principle, following a modified six step method as outlined by Braun and Clarke [27]. Two researchers (VHG and QO) familiarized themselves with the data by reading and rereading text segments deductively coded to the ethical principles (Step 1: Familiarisation with the data). Researchers independently and systematically reviewed WHO coded data for validation purposes (Step 2: Generating initial codes). In the search for themes, researchers examined text segments for patterns of meaning by grouping related concepts, reviewing all data relevant to each potential theme and developing preliminary interpretations of these emerging thematic clusters (Step 3: Searching for themes). As several text segments were coded to multiple principles and themes, overlap in the data became evident, highlighting how consumers perceive these concepts as interconnected. Consequently, in a 2-hour workshop, researchers (VHG, QO, LW) collectively reviewed preliminary interpretations, discussed theoretical assumptions and merged content accordingly (Step 4: Reviewing themes). Themes were then reviewed, defined and named (Step 5: Defining and naming themes). The findings were finalised through consensus [28] and presented in a table and narratively (Step 6: Producing the report). The inductive approach supported greater conceptual clarity, particularly where data overlapped multiple principles, by enabling deeper interpretation and team-based consensus. Collectively, the mixed deductive-inductive approach suitably balanced theoretical relevance with rich interpretation. Participants did not provide feedback on the findings.

Principle 1: protect autonomy

Participants emphasised the value of maintaining human connection when experiencing healthcare. Participants described autonomy deriving from the choice to engage with the care providers, noting AI as a valuable tool, not a replacement for traditional, human care, especially in sensitive or critical health scenarios. One participant shared the concerns for human involvement throughout the healthcare process, emphasising the importance of healthcare professionals retaining human contact:

“If the AI gives them more time to spend with people, that’s fantastic… I certainly wouldn’t like to see it used in a way that takes away that human contact because that has been really important to me”. (P1, FG2)

There was concern about lack of consent processes, overlapping with transparency, when AI was being used in clinical consultations. In this context participants called for clear, informed consent, not ‘buried’ in fine print. One participant described their recent visit to the doctor, whereby the appointment was recorded in its entirety without any prior discussion or consent, causing concern and mistrust.

“[S]hould I have consented to have AI used on me? There’s so many [concerning] things. It opens up a can of worms.” (P3, FG2).

Participants explained the desire for control over their personal health data, expressing a preference to have full understanding of how and when AI may be involved in their healthcare. Participants discussed privacy and data protection in relation to how AI is operationalised in the delivery of healthcare, expressing concerns about data ownership clarity, awareness of data use, and security measures to protect sensitive health information from unauthorised access or misuse.

“And definitely some safety around data usage, that’s a big concern for people and privacy. And yeah, people are sick of finding out that whatever company they’ve given their data to has been breached again, and it would be horrible for people to prey on people’s medical vulnerabilities in that sense of data protection.” (P1, FG1).

Principle 2: promote human wellbeing, safety and public interest

Participants suggested AI implementation in healthcare should incorporate robust safety mechanisms and professional oversight to prevent harm and ensure appropriate clinical decision-making. There was a clear desire for safety protocols when integrating AI into healthcare. Participants expressed concerns about potential risks from AI recommendations, particularly in medication management and diagnosis, emphasising that professional expertise should remain central to clinical decision making. Ensuring clinical safety involves harm prevention from inappropriate AI recommendations and upholding established healthcare clinical safety standards.

“I think ethically would be first and foremost. With under the guise of ‘do no harm’ and prevent harm… So I think there would need to be a level of ethics [to the outcome] in which [the AI is] producing information for.” (P2, FG2).

Participants expressed a preference for AI tools to be embedded within established healthcare systems as this integration could address current service limitations and provide support for patients in settings with limited professional oversight. Participants recognised AI’s potential to address current healthcare problems, such as medication management support, symptom monitoring between appointments, and making complex medical information more accessible and understandable to patients. One participant noted that AI could be particularly helpful in addressing current gaps in care if it were integrated in a way that directly supports patients.

“How just sorting out medication for patients, it would take them [health care professionals] 30 minutes to work out, whereas if they use AI within 7 minutes, they’ve got it. This idea of medication management would be the best for this patient at this time. They [health care professionals] would still need to check it though.” (P3, FG2).

The promotion of AI healthcare tools through social media channels was perceived to create public health risks by encouraging unvalidated self-diagnosis. Participants identified concerning trends where people are using AI chatbots as therapy replacements based on social media endorsements and developing inappropriate confidence in self-diagnosis capabilities. This pathway bypasses professional oversight and represents a threat to public health through misinformation and inappropriate healthcare decision-making. One participant noted they preferred to use AI for counselling, claiming they were their own therapist now. Another shared they know people who are diagnosing themselves with AI.

“Close relations that are older than me. They think they’re like the [Internet] doctor, like they diagnose themselves, they think, oh, I can take this supplement to cure me for this and that. I think there’s a risk that we … think we’re medical practitioners.” (P3, FG3).

Principle 3: ensure transparency, explainability and intelligibility

Participants expressed the preference for healthcare professionals to be transparent with patients in instances when AI is being used in their care or used to supplement treatment processes. Participants expressed surprise when they discovered AI had been used without their knowledge, such as in transcription services or diagnosis. Transparency begins with disclosure, to ensure patients understand when AI is playing a role in their care, from appointment scheduling to diagnostic imaging analysis.

“That is my first experience with AI in terms of face to face [healthcare]… I didn’t get asked if I wanted it done that way. But I can tell by the transcript that I got after, that process [of AI transcription] had taken place. The conversation was almost verbatim, and there’s probably no way that the doctor could have transcribed it that well.” (P1, FG3).

Participants expressed a desire for clear insight into how AI derives results and arrives at decisions. They discussed the importance of transparency and need for explainability when using AI in the decision making processes, and the preference for both healthcare professionals and patients to understand the rationale behind AI generated recommendations. Participants expressed concerns about not understanding how AI reached decisions and emphasised the importance of transparency and understanding the logic behind AI recommendations to ensure trust.

“I think it’s the quality of the data that’s being used to train the AI. You know there needs to be a level …[of] transparency and saying how you’re using it. But I think there also needs to be a level of transparency and how you are training and monitoring your systems as well to ensure that the you know the rigour is there and that they’re being trained on good quality data.” (P1, FG2).

Participants expressed the importance of being provided with clear, easy to understand information about how their personal health data is collected, stored, shared, and utilised within the AI systems. Participants expressed surprise that data was shared across healthcare platforms and concerns about data ownership and usage. They highlighted the importance of transparency about data collection practices, storage locations, sharing protocols, and how patient information contributes to AI training and decision making processes.

“One thing I was kind of mindful about in hospital though was that I had to have a lot of medication, where are these records being kept? Like who has got access to this data. I think it was the realisation [when I was] having a scan at [Centre 1] the neurologist in [Centre 2] could immediately access the information. I was like, wow, you know, so the information is on a shared platform, obviously. It’s like it’s out there in the ether somewhere.” (P3, FG3).

Participants suggested healthcare systems develop accessible communication tools and provide educational support to help patients and providers understand AI and its implications on healthcare delivery. Participants emphasised both patients and healthcare professionals need clear, understandable information about the role of AI in healthcare. Participants highlighted a preference for better education about AI capabilities and limitations, as well as being informed when AI processes are involved in their healthcare.

“And with the transparency, clinicians are going to have to be trained on how to explain it in a way that’s accessible but thorough enough. And that’s again going to be very tricky, that’s an added skill set, they’re going to have to learn.” (P2, FG3).

Participants highlighted the importance of healthcare providers clearly communicating the role of AI in diagnosis and treatment decisions, including its limitations and potential for error. When patients are informed about the specific role AI plays in their care, they are better equipped to make informed treatment decisions. This transparency fosters trust in both the AI systems and the healthcare providers.

“But even if you’re adding AI it says it is reading a scan, so AI has read it first. We then looked over it, both matched. So we’re confident that based on that, this is the diagnosis. … [M]ore explanation would be better.” (P1, FG1).

Principle 4: foster responsibility and accountability

Some participants described clear tasks and conditions that would constitute the responsible use of AI in healthcare. They preferred AI systems were designed for specific tasks and had a clear delineation of the role of AI and healthcare professional.

“I think it’s best it’s used as a tool rather than like a complete replacement of like that human contact”. (P4, FG2)

Participants expressed strong support for clinicians, while raising concern that AI may undermine their role by reducing responsibility and accountability. They advocated for an approach that preserves clinical professionalism.

“To your credit, your credibility as a clinician, … because you don’t you don’t want a patient saying, oh, I could have just googled this”. (P2, FG2)

Participants were concerned about data leaks and noted the importance of accountability and governance. They wished for government reassurance that their data is safe from organisations that may use it for other purposes.

“I would say, like cybersecurity. So recently we’ve had a lot of leaks come through in terms of people’s data being out there. People are buying on the black market, whatever it is. But if that’s able to go out, so is this sort of information. So will that be an issue as well? I think there from a government and a healthcare perspective that needs to be reassured before.” (P1, FG3).

Principle 5: ensure inclusiveness and equity

Participants were concerned if AI would be capable of reflecting the needs of marginalised populations such as culturally and linguistically diverse or First Nations people. Another concern was differences between rural and metropolitan populations but it was unclear if these were pertaining to AI accessibility for those living rurally. It was specifically noted how to make the technology “bespoke” for these populations.

“English as a second language. That’s hard enough as it is with a human, let alone with a piece of technology that you can’t hear the inflexions of or whatever, [also] people with cognitive impairments or things that would lead them to see the technology as a person and put them at risk of over relying on it or saying it’s talking to me. So, I think there’s some groups that we need to be particularly careful of and perhaps have different routes for them.” (P1, FG1).

Participants noted that perceptions of AI varied across age groups, pointing to a “generational technological divide” (FG3) in attitudes toward its use in healthcare. There was consensus that different aged groups would interact with AI in healthcare and have different levels of acceptance. One participant noted they have an elderly friend that has voiced he would refuse health care that involved AI, illustrating the age related concerns of AI in health care.

“Interestingly, I work a lot with teenagers and adolescents and they seem to be a little bit less bothered by it. It’s kind of just a part of their life, but definitely in older demographics. I think they tend to be picking up more of the doom and gloom side of it all.” (P1, FG1).

Concerns were raised about digital and health literacy; for example the elderly and those with English as a second language, might be challenged with or be excluded from, AI integrated healthcare. One participant suggested the importance of an interpreter to help them be comfortable with AI use, and another noted health literacy for many is already low.

“[Ideally, you would have a person to] explain how it’s gonna (sic.) be used to help you. … explain to you how [AI is] used … in all the different procedures and information” (P4, FG2).

Participants expressed concerns AI systems would perpetuate power disparities between industry and consumers with interest in profits rather than the public good. One participant voiced scepticism and noted the lack of a consumer voice.

“What’s hard for me is even though consumers have a lot more power in the system, we are still largely powerless.” (P2, FG3).

Participants demonstrated an awareness of the potential for bias in AI systems. One participant raised concerns that AI was being shaped by Western medical paradigms and wished for broader holistic approach when designing and applying AI in healthcare. Participants advocated for systems that consider the ‘whole person’, including social, psychological, and cultural factors, rather than relying solely on a one-size-fits-all solution.

“That would be my concern. Is the AI appropriate, is the data set appropriate [for individuals], enough to be able to take in the whole of the person.” (P2, FG 3).

Principle 6: promote AI that is responsive and sustainable

As AI is constantly evolving, it was noted that the process to ensure trust was maintained and required robust processes with government oversight. Participants discussed how AI systems would require continuous review and evaluation to ensure they are meeting stakeholder expectations.

“So there’s almost an element of what happens in the Parliament, in government, in introducing some of these legislations or oversights or mechanisms to control for, for the greater good”. (P2, FG3)

Discussion

This study explored healthcare consumers’ perceptions of AI in healthcare, and is, to our knowledge, the first to interpret these perspectives through the lens of the WHO’s six ethical principles [19] within a health consumer context. By mapping consumer-derived themes to an established global ethics framework, this research extends existing AI ethics literature beyond policy, provider and developer viewpoints to highlight consumer values and expectations. The findings suggest consumer acceptance of AI requires transparent communication, equitable access, and active involvement in implementation. While participants recognised the potential benefits of AI, they consistently emphasised the importance of maintaining human centred care and healthcare professional oversight. Insights from this study mapped to the WHO principles, combined with findings from other research, could inform the future development of a values-based framework to guide the safe, ethical, and genuinely consumer centred implementation of AI in healthcare.

Consumer acceptance of AI in healthcare appears to be dependent on clear, accessible communication and transparency about AI’s role in their care. Participants expressed a desire to be informed when AI was being used and what implications this may have on their treatment. This finding aligns with literature demonstrating the importance of clear communication and how it is crucial for building healthcare consumers trust in healthcare AI systems [29]. Research by Zhang et al. similarly found patients expressed discomfort when AI involvement was not openly communicated, even when the technology improved care outcomes [30]. Consequently, healthcare professionals may need to adopt new communication guidelines that enable them to explain AI to consumers in an easily comprehensible manner and encourages a dialogue for addressing questions and concerns.

Participants expressed the importance of transparency, explainability and insight into how AI systems arrived at decisions. A study looking at the transparency of ‘black box’ challenges of AI in medical imaging, highlighted the ethical implications of opaque AI systems [31]. They emphasised the necessity for explainable and transparent use of AI to instill trust [31]. This desire for transparency presents ongoing challenges, as many AI systems operate through complex networks and are inherently difficult to explain in simple terms [31]. There is a need for education tools to assist with the explanation of AI in healthcare for both consumers and clinicians.

Healthcare consumers recognise the risk that AI may reinforce existing health inequities and introduce bias, particularly those impacting marginalised populations. Participants identified potentially vulnerable groups, including those with cultural and linguistic diversity, and those living in rural areas. A study conducted in 2019 demonstrated AI systems trained primarily on data from majority groups often overlook critical factors relevant to minority populations, specifically racial groups, and as such contributed to existing healthcare disparities [18]. The United States based study revealed AI algorithms underestimated the health needs of racial minority groups, compared to their majority counterparts, noting the algorithms were biased. These concerns reflect established evidence that AI systems can perpetuate and amplify existing healthcare disparities if not carefully designed and implemented [32]. The development of algorithms that are inclusive are essential for equitable healthcare delivery [33].

Age was identified as a potential barrier to accepting AI in healthcare by participants. Gordon and Hornbrook found older adults expressed greater concern about healthcare professionals being replaced by AI, preferring traditional models of care and noted a lack of confidence in using technology impacted uptake [34]. Our focus groups acknowledged younger people were more comfortable with AI as they had “grown up” with it, opposed to the older generation who were perceivably more fearful and suspicious. This supports findings from the Canadian Digital Health Survey 2023, which indicated younger Canadians are generally more comfortable with AI in healthcare, with older people expressing concerns surrounding data privacy, lack of human interaction and distrust [35]. The variation in acceptance across age groups is noteworthy, as older adults are among the most frequent users of healthcare services. This finding highlights an important area for future research.

Participants expressed confidence in expert guidance whilst emphasising the importance of consumer involvement in decision-making processes. This finding reinforces the value of participatory design approaches that engage consumers through the development and implementation process [36]. The emphasis on maintaining human oversight as noted in the protect autonomy principle, reflects concerns about preserving patient or caregiver relationships and professional expertise. Participants consistently viewed AI as a supportive tool and not a replacement [37]. Also highlighted was the need for government moderation, regulation and accountability, noting this oversight was considered responsible care and critical to maintaining trust in the AI processes and implementation.

Strengths and limitations

This study is timely as countries worldwide advance their digital health strategy and ethical guidelines, offering practical insights into communication, transparency and human interaction to support consumer-focused AI implementation. It also identifies equity considerations such as age and cultural diversity, as critical areas for ensuring inclusive adoption. Several limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings. The number of participants in this study was relatively small (n = 9), limited by the self-selection convenience sampling approach. Although additional participants had initially been recruited, several were unable to attend the scheduled sessions, resulting in focus groups of less than four participants, potentially limiting the richness of the participant discussion. The participants were self-selective which may have introduced an inclusion bias as they could potentially have strong opinions about AI in healthcare and may not represent the opinion of other healthcare consumers. Future research should focuses specifically on underrepresented subpopulations. Deductive coding to the six ethical principles occasionally resulted in overlap, with some text segments fitting multiple categories. To mitigate this subjectivity, researchers collaborated in workshops for the inductive analysis to more distinctly assign theoretical concepts until consensus was achieved. This process may have limited the extent to which the data could be interpreted beyond the established ethical principles framework and future research could collect more nuanced perspectives.

Conclusion

This study reinforces the importance of understanding healthcare consumers’ views on the implementation of AI in healthcare. Consumer voices in this research consistently emphasised values of clear communication, transparency, equity and the preservation of meaningful human relationships with healthcare professionals. As AI continues to rapidly expand in the healthcare systems globally, there is a critical, time-sensitive opportunity to embed ethical and values-based principles into its design, government and implementation. Action is required to establish globally relevant yet locally adaptable frameworks, processes, and regulatory mechanisms that evolve alongside AI advancement. Upholding consumer trust and aligning AI innovation with core healthcare values by ensuring human connection remains critical as we navigate achieving ethical, equitable and genuinely consumer-centred integration into clinical care.
