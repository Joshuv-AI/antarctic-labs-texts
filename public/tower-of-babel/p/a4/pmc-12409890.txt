Hygrothermal Degradation Behavior and Structural Evolution of Electrospun Poly(ethylene terephthalate) Fiber Mats
Authors: Keigo Kawahara, Hisao Matsuno, Keiji Tanaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Growing environmental concerns over plastic waste have recently spurred interest in degradable polymers. Among these, polyesters are considered promising candidates due to the hydrolyzable nature of their ester linkages. While the degradation behavior of aliphatic polyesters, known for their relatively high degradability, has been extensively studied with a focus on aggregation structures, our understanding of aromatic polyesters, which exhibit much lower degradability, remains limited. In this study, we investigated the time-dependent degradation behavior of electrospun poly­(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) fiber mats exposed to a hygrothermal environment at 403 K and 85% relative humidity. Initially, the weight of the PET fiber mats remained nearly constant, followed by a marked decrease upon longer exposure. Scanning electron microscopy revealed progressive surface roughening, along with the formation of holes and cracks in the fibers. Structural analysis using attenuated-total-reflection Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction analysis indicated that in the early stages, hydrolysis-induced chain scission promoted crystallization. In contrast, during later stages, extensive hydrolysis led to depolymerization of the PET chains into monomeric species, resulting in marked weight loss. These findings provide new insights into the degradation mechanism of aromatic polyesters and may inform the design and development of degradable aromatic polyester-based materials and devices.

Introduction

Synthetic polymer materials play an indispensable role in various industries such as food packaging, construction, and agriculture due to their diverse properties and long-term durability. However, their widespread use has led to growing environmental concerns, particularly due to the accumulation of plastic waste. − A promising strategy to address these issues is the development of degradable polymers to replace currently used nondegradable ones. , Among them, polyesters are attractive candidates, as they can undergo hydrolytic degradation at the ester linkages present in each repeating unit along their main chains. −

Poly­(glycolide) (PGA), which possesses a relatively simple chemical structure, is known to degrade rapidly in aqueous environments. ,− Other aliphatic polyesters, such as poly­(L-lactic acid) (PLLA), , poly­(ε-caprolactone) (PCL), , and their copolymers are also biodegradable in environments containing microorganisms capable of producing hydrolytic enzymes. However, the use of aliphatic polyesters is limited by their relatively poor thermal and mechanical properties. In contrast, aromatic polyesters such as poly­(ethylene terephthalate) (PET) − and poly­(butylene terephthalate) (PBT) offer superior thermal and mechanical performance but generally exhibit poor degradability in natural environments. , To improve the environmental degradability of such materials, a fundamental understanding of their degradation mechanism is essential.

Hydrolytic degradation generally proceeds more readily in amorphous regions than in crystalline domains, as water molecules can more easily diffuse through the former. As such, aliphatic polyesters with low glass transition temperatures (T g) and low hydrophobicity, such as PGA, can be degraded under relatively mild aqueous conditions. In contrast, aromatic polyesters like PET and PBT, which exhibit high T g and hydrophobicity, typically require more aggressive hygrothermal conditions for degradation. Although prior studies have investigated PET degradation under elevated temperature and humidity, these efforts have largely focused on molecular weight reduction and associated deterioration in thermal and mechanical properties. − In contrast, relatively little attention has been paid to changes in aggregation structures during the degradation of aromatic polyesters under such conditions. For example, Dubelley et al. investigated the long-term stability of PET laminate films over several years in a relatively mild hygrothermal environment. Using attenuated-total-reflection Fourier transform infrared (ATR/FT-IR) spectroscopy, they reported the time-dependent formation of chain-end carboxylic acids and an increase in crystallinity. However, the aggregation structure at the stage where monomer-like degradation products are produced has yet to be elucidated.

In this study, we investigated the degradation behavior of PET under hygrothermal conditions using electrospun PET fiber mats as model samples. These mats feature a high surface area-to-volume ratio, which enhances water diffusion and facilitates degradation. We first quantified the weight loss of PET fiber mats as a function of exposure time under elevated temperature and humidity. Morphological changes were then characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Finally, structural changes were evaluated using ATR/FT-IR spectroscopy and X-ray diffraction (XRD). The degradation behavior was analyzed in terms of changes in surface morphology, carboxyl index, and crystallinity of the PET fibers.

Experimental Section

Preparation of Fiber Mats

PET with an inherent viscosity of 0.65 dL·g–1 obtained from TEIJIN Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan) was used as the polymer sample. The glass transition temperature (T g) of PET pellets was determined to be 354 K by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). PET fiber mats were prepared via electrospinning, following previous reported procedures. − A 10 wt % PET solution was prepared using 1,1,1,3,3,3-hexafluoroisopropanol (HFIP) as the solvent. After electrospinning, the resulting fiber mats were dried under vacuum at 374 K for 12 h to remove residual solvent.

Hygrothermal Test

Hygrothermal degradation was conducted by exposing the PET fiber mats to 403 K and 85% relative humidity (%RH) for predetermined durations using a highly accelerated stress test chamber (PC-242HSR2, Hirayama Manufacturing, Saitama, Japan). This test temperature was selected to be sufficiently higher than the T g of PET, and close to the maximum temperature achievable with this test chamber. The chosen relative humidity represents one of the typical conditions used in hygrothermal testing. After exposure, the samples were dried under vacuum at 374 K for 12 h to eliminate absorbed water and low molecular weight degradation products.

Characterization

The morphology of the PET fiber mats was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM SU3500, Hitachi High-Technologies, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. Prior to imaging, each sample was coated with a thin platinum layer using an ion sputter coater (MC1000, Hitachi High-Technologies). The molecular aggregation states of PET chains were examined using an FT-IR-6700 spectrometer (JASCO, Tokyo, Japan), equipped with a ZnSe prism and a triglycine sulfate detector. All spectra were acquired under a nitrogen atmosphere at room temperature in the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm–1 with a resolution of 2 cm–1, averaging 32 scans per measurement. Crystalline structures of PET in the fiber mats were analyzed by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a SmartLab X-ray diffractometer (Rigaku, Tokyo, Japan) with Cu Kα radiation at a wavelength (λ) of 0.154 nm. Diffraction patterns were recorded in the diffraction angle (2θ) range of 5–40° at a scan rate of 0.7°·min–1. The scattering vector (q) was calculated using the equation, q = (4π/λ)·sin θ.

Results and Discussion

Changes in Weight and Morphology

To investigate the effect of hygrothermal conditions on electrospun PET fiber mats, changes in weight and morphology were examined. Prior to weighing, each sample was vacuum-dried to remove low-molecular weight species generated during degradation. Figure shows the normalized weight of the PET fiber mats as a function of exposure time at 403 K and 85%RH. During the first 48 h-exposure, the relative weight remained almost unchanged but as exposure time increased it changed markedly. A gradual decrease began after 96 h, with values of (99.6 ± 0.4)% at 96 h and (95.8 ± 0.7)% at 192 h. After 288 h, the weight further decreased to (92.2 ± 0.8)%. These results suggest that, particularly in the later stages of exposure, low-molecular-weight degradation products were generated and subsequently removed during vacuum drying.

Figure a shows a photographic image of PET fiber mats before and after 288 h of hygrothermal treatment. No apparent macroscopic changes were observed. However, the 288 h-exposed mat became mechanically fragile and required delicate handling. To investigate local morphological changes, SEM observations were conducted. Figure b–f shows SEM images of the mats (b) before exposure, and after (c) 48 h, (d) 96 h, (e) 192 h, and (f) 288 h of hygrothermal treatment. Insets in each panel show higher-magnification images. Prior to exposure, the fiber surfaces appeared smooth (Figure b). After 48 h of exposure, surface roughening was observed (Figure c), even though the weight change was minimal at this stage, as shown in Figure . As the exposure time increased, the fiber surfaces became progressively rougher (Figure d–f). After 192 h, small holes and cracks appeared on the fiber surfaces, as indicated by black and white arrowheads, respectively (Figure e). The number and size of these defects increased further by 288 h (Figure f), possibly contributing to the observed mechanical fragility. In contrast, no marked change in fiber diameter was detected even after 288 h of exposure. Considering the concurrent decrease in overall weight, this suggests the formation of internal voids or low-density regions within the fibers. It should be noted that the sizes of the holes and cracks may have been exaggerated during the sample drying process prior to SEM observations, and therefore these values should be interpreted only in relative terms.

Interestingly, a small amount of white powder was observed near the degraded PET fiber mats after 192 h of exposure. The amount of this powder further increased after 288 h, and it was identified as terephthalic acid by 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, as shown in Figure S1 in the Supporting Information (SI). Given the high sublimation point of terephthalic acid (573 K), , it likely remained after vacuum drying. This confirms that, at least after 192 h, PET chains underwent hydrolysis to form low-molecular-weight compounds, including monomeric units as terephthalic acid and, likely ethylene glycol. However, ethylene glycol was probably removed during the vacuum drying process, as evidenced by the weight loss shown in Figure .

Aggregation States of PET Chains

ATR/FT-IR spectroscopy was used to investigate changes in the aggregation states of PET chains in the fiber mats under hygrothermal conditions. Since FT-IR measurements using the transmission mode are known to exhibit a baseline drift due to light scattering in fibrous samples, an ATR method was used here. Panel a of Figure shows ATR/FT-IR spectra in the 1800 to 1600 cm–1 region for mats exposed for various durations. Spectra were normalized to the intensity of the C–H aromatic ring vibration at 1410 cm–1. The unexposed mat exhibited a strong absorption peak near 1715 cm–1, corresponding to the carbonyl stretching (ν­(CO)) mode of ester groups. ,− This peak gradually decreased in intensity with increasing exposure time. Simultaneously, a shoulder emerged around 1680 cm–1, which can be attributed to the carboxylic acid carbonyl stretching mode, indicating the formation of terminal carboxy groups. −

(a) ATR/FT-IR spectra of PET fiber mats in the wavenumber range from 1800 to 1600 cm–1 after various exposure times. Spectra are normalized to the C–H aromatic ring vibration at 1410 cm–1. (b) Carboxy index (I 1680/I 1715) as a function of exposure time.

Figure b shows the carboxy index, defined as the ratio of peak intensities at 1680 and 1715 cm–1 (I 1680/I 1715), as a function of exposure time. A slight increase was observed at 48 h, followed by a more pronounced rise at a longer exposure time. Since carboxylic acid end groups are known to lower pH and accelerate further PET hydrolysis via autocatalysis, , it appears that this process was minimal in the early stages but became increasingly influential during prolonged exposure.

To assess the conformational changes of PET chains within the fiber mats, we focused on the spectral region from 1440 to 1320 cm–1. Figure a shows the data. Four characteristic peaks in this region are known to be sensitive to changes in crystallinity. ,− The peaks at 1410 and 1386 cm–1 correspond to the in-plane C–C stretching vibration (ν­(C–C)) mode and an in-plane C–H bending (δ­(CH)) mode, respectively, of benzene rings in the terephthalate skeletons. The peaks at 1370 and 1340 cm–1 are assigned to the CH2 wagging mode (ω­(CH2)) in the ethylene skeleton. The segment of (−O–CH2–CH2–O−) in the PET backbone can adopt either trans and gauche conformers due to internal rotation around C–C bonds. In crystalline regions, this segment preferentially adopts a trans conformation, while in amorphous regions, it predominantly adopts a gauche conformation with a minor trans contribution. ,

The integrated intensities of the conformer bands (A 1370 and A 1340) for gauche and trans, respectively, were used to calculate the fraction of the (−O–CH2–CH2–O−) segment in the trans conformation (f T). The initial f T was approximately 26%, which increased rapidly to ∼ 55% after 48 h. With further exposure, f T continued to increase gradually, reaching ∼ 72% at 288 h. These results indicate a progressive increase in the crystalline fraction of the PET fiber mats under hygrothermal conditions. The relationship between f T and weight loss as a function of exposure time will be discussed in a later section.

Furthermore, the crystalline structure of PET chains was characterized by XRD analysis. Figure a shows the XRD profiles of the PET fiber mats obtained at various exposure times. The deconvolution of the diffraction patterns into crystalline and amorphous contributions is presented in Figure S2a–e of the SI. Peaks were observed at q of 11.5, 12.3, 15.3, 16.2, 18.4, and 22.9 nm–1 in all samples, which correspond to (011), (010), (111), (110), (100) and (101) planes, respectively, of the triclinic PET crystalline phase. , Notably, after 192 h of hygrothermal exposure, three additional peaks appeared, indicated by filled triangles in Figure a, that could not be attributed to the PET crystalline structure. These peaks were assigned to the diffraction from crystalline terephthalic acid (see also Figure S2d,e). , This observation indicates that terephthalic acid was produced as a result of extensive hydrolytic degradation of the PET chains and subsequently crystallized (Figure S2f). Some of the resulting terephthalic acid crystals likely detached from the fiber mats and were observed as white powder surrounding the mats, as described earlier.

Degradation Mechanism

We herein discuss the evolution of the aggregation structure of PET fiber mats at each stage of hygrothermal exposure, based on a comprehensive set of results from weight measurements, SEM observations, 1H NMR spectroscopy, ATR/FT-IR spectroscopy, and XRD analysis.

During the initial stage of hygrothermal exposure (0–48 h), the weight of the PET fiber mats remained nearly constant (Figure ), although localized surface roughening of the fibers was observed (Figure b,c), and the carboxy index showed a modest rise (Figure ). At this stage, no formation of monomeric terephthalic acid, which would contribute to mass loss, was yet detected. In contrast, both f T and X c values increased markedly during this period, as shown in Figures and , respectively. These increases can be attributed to enhanced chain mobility under elevated temperature and to chain cleavage-induced crystallization. This crystallization process is understood to involve the formation of new lamellae within amorphous gaps situated between pre-existing lamellae, , which result from heterogeneous and nonparallel stacking of crystalline domains. Indeed, as shown in Figure S4, the long period evaluated by small-angle X-ray scattering (SAXS) analysis decreased with increasing exposure time, whereas the SAXS peak intensity associated with crystallinity clearly increased. Although chain scission had initiated by this point, it had likely not progressed far enough to generate low-molecular-weight compounds such as terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, which would contribute to the weight loss of the fiber mats. In other words, the observed increases in f T and X c reflect a genuine increase in crystallinity, rather than an apparent rise due to selective degradation and loss of amorphous PET chains via hydrolysis. Furthermore, a small and broad peak observed at around 397 K in the DSC thermogram of the initial fiber mat (Figure S3a) indicates the presence of a small amount of thinner and imperfect crystals. In the DSC thermograms of the fiber mats treated under hygrothermal conditions for 48 and 96 h, this peak appeared at higher temperatures, around 436 and 440 K, respectively. This shift suggests that the imperfect crystallites located in the amorphous regions had undergone grown slightly. ,

In the second stage of exposure (48–96 h), the weight of the PET fiber mats began to show a slight decrease (Figure ). A characteristic change during this period was a notable increase in the carboxy index (Figure ), suggesting that the hydrolysis reaction had accelerated. However, the formation of low-molecular-weight compounds capable of separating from the fiber mats remained limited. Both f T and X c, parameters representing the relative crystalline content of the PET chains, continued to increase during this period (Figures b and b). This trend can be attributed to the enhanced chain cleavage-induced crystallization, which is likely facilitated by an autocatalytic mechanism involving carboxylic acid end groups. ,,,

In contrast to the earlier stages, a substantial weight reduction of the PET fiber mats was observed during the third period (96–192 h) (Figure ). In addition to progressive surface roughening, the formation of holes and cracks became evident (Figure e). Also, the SAXS intensity decreased markedly, as shown in Figure S4, suggesting that voids may have formed within the fibers in addition to the reduction in mat thickness. Furthermore, XRD analysis confirmed the appearance of diffraction peaks assignable to crystalline terephthalic acid after 192 h (Figure a). Given the formation of terephthalic acid, the generation of another monomer, ethylene glycol, is also expected as a result of hydrolysis. Although ethylene glycol was not directly detected in this study, it is likely that it was removed during the vacuum drying process prior to weight measurements, thereby contributing to the observed mass loss. During this period, both f T and X c values continued to increase (Figures b and b), with a more pronounced rate of increase compared to the earlier periods. These increases are attributed to a combination of two factors: the continued chain cleavage-induced crystallization and an apparent rise in crystallinity resulting from the preferential degradation and loss of amorphous PET chains.

During the final stage of hygrothermal exposure (192–288 h), a further reduction in the weight of the PET fiber mats was observed (Figure ), accompanied by increased surface roughening and the formation of numerous holes and cracks (Figure f). The accompanying decrease in SAXS intensity (Figure S4) suggests continued void formation within the fibers in addition to overall mat thinning. The remaining fiber mats became extremely fragile and difficult to handle. In addition, a greater amount of terephthalic acid was visibly detected near the residual mats, and the carboxy index continued to rise (Figure b), indicating ongoing degradation of PET chains. In contrast, the f T and X c values showed only slight increases during this period (Figures b and b), suggesting that little to no additional crystalline structures were formed. One possible explanation is that the amorphous regions between pre-existing lamellae had already been fully occupied by newly formed lamellae, leaving no further space for crystallization. This interpretation is consistent with the observed mechanical fragility of the remaining fiber mats. Unfortunately, the extreme fragility of the samples made further structural characterization using the same analytical techniques difficult. Therefore, the application of alternative methods will be necessary to obtain additional insights into the degradation behavior at longer exposure times, a subject that warrants further investigation.

Conclusions

The degradation behavior of PET fiber mats under hygrothermal conditions was examined as a function of exposure time, with a focus on changes in aggregation structure based on SEM, ATR/FT-IR, and XRD analyses. The use of fiber mats enabled accelerated testing, and the results indicated that the degradation process could be divided into several distinct stages. In the initial stage, PET chains underwent hydrolytic cleavage, although the extent of degradation was insufficient to induce measurable weight loss. Nevertheless, the crystallinity of PET in the fiber mats was increased markedly, driven by enhanced chain mobility under high-temperature conditions and chain cleavage-induced crystallization. During the second stage, a slight reduction in weight was observed. This stage was characterized by an acceleration in chain scission due to autocatalytic hydrolysis initiated by terminal carboxylic groups, along with continued increases in crystallinity. In the third stage, extensive chain degradation led to the formation of monomeric species, such as terephthalic acid and ethylene glycol, resulting in marked weight loss. SEM observations revealed the formation of holes and cracks on the fiber surfaces. The increase in crystallinity during this stage was attributed to both chain cleavage-induced crystallization and the selective removal of amorphous regions. In the final stage, further weight loss occurred, however the crystallinity remained nearly unchanged, suggesting that the amorphous regions between pre-existing lamellae had been fully filled by newly formed lamellae. Beyond this point, the remaining PET fiber mats became extremely fragile and difficult to handle, limiting further structural analysis using conventional methods. Alternative approaches, such as, X-ray structural analysis using a microbeam at a synchrotron radiation facility, or molecular weight characterization by size exclusion chromatography using the highly corrosive solvent HFIP, will therefore be required to investigate degradation behavior at more advanced stages. Although the present results were obtained under fixed temperature and relative humidity conditions, future studies under varied conditions and on other aromatic polyesters, such as poly­(butylene terephthalate), will be valuable for assessing the generality of the degradation mechanism and its kinetics. The fundamental insights obtained here contribute to a better understanding of the degradation mechanisms of aromatic polyesters and may support the future development of environmentally sustainable aromatic polyesters-based materials and devices.
