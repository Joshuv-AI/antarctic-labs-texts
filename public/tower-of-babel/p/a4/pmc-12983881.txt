Paediatric inherited arrhythmia clinic: developing a new model of care
Authors: Karen M Robinson, Hiroko Asakai, Christian Turner, Claire Lawley, Lexi Dengler, Gabbie Scarfe, Ansley Morrish, Janette Hayward, Ngai Lam Woo, Janine Smith, Jonathan R Skinner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractsObjectiveTo describe the development and utilisation of a paediatric inherited arrhythmia clinic (IAC) from 2014 to 2024, including service developments following the introduction of cardiac genetic coordinators (CGC).DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingThe IAC at Children’s Hospital Westmead, New South Wales, Australia (approximately 8 million people).PatientsChildren (aged 0–18 years) referred to the IAC.InterventionsClinical and genetic data were extracted and analysed.Main outcome measuresDescription of the development and cohort of the clinic.ResultsA monthly clinic preceded by a team meeting (multidisciplinary team) led by a paediatric cardiologist and clinical geneticist was established. Attendees included a genetic counsellor, senior arrhythmia nurse and clinical psychologist.A total of 301 individuals aged 0–18 years were seen over 11 years: 32 in 2014 and 128 in 2024. 85 probands presented with cardiac arrest (37; 44%), syncope (23; 27%) and others (25; 29%). The most common diagnoses were Long QT syndrome 33 (38%), catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia 24 (28%) and Brugada syndrome 6 (7%). 226/301 children had genetic testing; pathogenic/likely pathogenic variants were found in 173/226 (77%), including 121 family members. The most common genes implicated were KCNQ1 (67), KCNH2 (39), SCN5A (28) and RyR2 (15).Dedicated allied health/nursing CGCs appointed in 2023 improved the collation of clinical/family data (particularly new referrals), links to adult cardiology, forensic and regional genetic and paediatric services.ConclusionsThe multidisciplinary IAC achieves the phenotyping, genotyping and holistic care of large numbers of children, mostly with cardiac ion channelopathies. Dedicated coordinators provided an incremental improvement to the service.

Introduction

The prevention of sudden death due to inherited arrhythmia (IA) conditions such as long QT syndrome (LQTS) and catecholaminergic polymorphic ventricular tachycardia (CPVT) depends on the detection and best management of probands and the detection of presymptomatic individuals within the family.1 Beta-blockers, particularly nadolol, are remarkably protective in most cases of LQTS, and flecainide, in combination with beta-blockers, has revolutionised the management of CPVT.2 Some cases are more difficult to manage, and care is nuanced, with left cardiac sympathetic denervation, permanent pacemakers and implantable cardioverter defibrillators sometimes being required.1

Advances in genetic testing, family screening (clinical and molecular), and the evolving understanding of the genetic basis of IA conditions have led to the detection of large numbers of children and families who are at increased risk of developing these conditions. The precise definition of genotype materially affects management, for example, in subtypes of LQTS.3

Multidisciplinary team (MDT) clinics are now recommended best practice in managing these children and families. This includes the investigation of sudden cardiac death (SCD) and Resuscitated Cardiac Arrest.47

Children with IA are often complex to manage, with the conditions having significant medical, familial and psychosocial implications for patients and families. Additionally, schools and sports clubs may be concerned about a child’s welfare and uncertain about how best to proceed. Managing such cases within the typical busy paediatric cardiology clinic is challenging and does not provide best care for families.8

Multidisciplinary management of children at risk of IA requires critical awareness of the powerful psychosocial impacts of a newly diagnosed condition, which may have already caused a cardiac arrest or SCD in a family member.6 7 9 A cohesive MDT can avoid duplication of resources and can prevent the need for families to visit multiple agencies.9

In 2014, the regional paediatric electrophysiologist (CT) and clinical geneticist (JS) at the Children’s Hospital Westmead (CHW) recognised the difficulty often faced by families where IAs were a possibility or a reality within a family. Not only was accurate phenotyping essential, but genetic testing needed to be discussed in full and arranged expeditiously with appropriate access to pregenetic and postgenetic counselling. In response to this need, they established a monthly paediatric IA clinic (IAC) as described below.

Although the individual conditions and genetic yield have been well described in the literature, to our knowledge, the defined work of a paediatric IAC has not previously been described.

Aims

Other services may be considering establishing such a clinic. With this in mind, we aim to describe how our IAC MDT clinic is run and the clinical spectrum it covers. We will discuss key relationships across multiple disciplines and some practical aspects of data collection and storage. Additionally, we describe the added value of a dedicated cardiac genetic coordinator (CGC) to an IAC clinic, discuss their role and highlight how recent service delivery has changed for the better, and consider areas for further development.

Materials and methods

The clinical setting and MDT are described below. A retrospective cohort study of patients seen at the IAC during the study period of 2014–2024 was performed. Patients were identified through medical records as well as a bespoke departmental database, ‘CardioSync’, designed and built by CT.

Data, including baseline demographics, clinical presentation details, diagnostic methods, genetic testing results, treatment types and symptoms during follow-up, were extracted.

Descriptive statistics are presented as numbers, frequencies and percentages for categorical variables.

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of our research; analysis was retrospective of existing anonymised data.

The state of New South Wales (NSW) has a population of approximately 8 million people covering an area of 800 642 km2.10 In 2023, approximately 78% of the population lived in major cities, 19% in inner regional areas and 5% in outer regional areas. This is closely reflected in the children who attend the IAC, with 239 (79%) residing in a major city, 54 (18%) in inner regional areas and 8 (3%) in outer regional areas.

The ACT (Australian Capital Territory, including Canberra) has a population of approximately 450 000 people.11

Results

This multidisciplinary clinic was established at CHW, a paediatric-only tertiary hospital located in the Western Sydney metropolitan area, in 2014. CHW and the Sydney Children’s Hospital (situated in the Eastern Sydney metropolitan area) operate as a unified network—the Sydney Children’s Hospitals Network (SCHN)—with the same cardiology specialists serving both sites. A third, smaller children’s hospital in Newcastle, NSW, part of the Hunter region, collaborates closely with SCHN. Children requiring specialised paediatric arrhythmia services from the ACT are also predominantly supported by SCHN. The increased use and availability of telehealth services during the COVID-19 pandemic, especially in paediatric electrophysiology (EP), has enhanced access to care.12

Children under the age of 18 years, with a diagnosis of familial history of, or at risk of an IA, are referred to the clinic. Referrals come from a variety of sources, including general practitioners (GPs), other paediatric cardiologists, adult cardiologists, clinical geneticists and general paediatricians. GP referrals were mostly at the suggestion of a cardiologist involved in the care of a relative. Less commonly, a referral may arise from the forensic services following an SCD in the family. Referrals are accepted and scheduled either by clinical genetics or paediatric electrophysiology.

The IAC occurs monthly. Clinically indicated cardiac screening tests are pre-scheduled for the day of the appointment.

From inception, an MDT meeting was held before the start of the clinic. Such an intake meeting is common before clinical genetic clinics but was new to cardiology. Family and clinical history, relevant reports and test results are reviewed.

Attendees include senior medical representation from both paediatric cardiology and clinical genetics, a senior cardiac nurse, a genetic counsellor and a clinical psychologist. An adult care transition nurse is invited as needed. Clinical and family histories are reviewed, and any required supplementary investigations are identified. If a familial genetic variant is known, it is discussed. Any psychological pressure points are raised as appropriate. Patients are allocated to see one or both specialist teams in neighbouring rooms.

We found that a source of recurring difficulty was insufficiency of data defining the phenotype in many referrals coming to cardiology. Reviewing the clinical phenotype in a parent or second-degree relative following their cardiac arrest, for example, may be critical in determining what sort of clinical testing to initiate in the child. Furthermore, links to forensic services were not formalised, and genetic data was not automatically shared with other genetic services across the state. In response to these shortcomings, two dedicated CGCs in a job share were appointed in 2023, one with a senior nursing background in paediatric cardiology and the other in senior oncology, genetic nursing and genetic counselling roles.

The clinical cohort

During the 11-year study period, a total of 301 children attended the IAC clinic (figure 1). The decrease in attendees between 2020 and 2022 was attributed to the COVID-19 pandemic and ensuing restrictions.

Family members

216 of 301 (72%) children attended the clinic for cardiac/genetic assessment, either because of a family history of a proven or suspected inherited heart condition, or an unexplained SCD. Of these 216, 148 (69%) underwent predictive genetic testing for the familial causative gene variant (table 2). Predictive genetic testing was not indicated in the other children because the family proband was gene elusive, had not had genetic testing, or was awaiting genetic testing at the time of this study. The uptake of predictive genetic testing was high, only one family choosing not to proceed when offered it.

Role of the CGC

The CGC role was established in 2023. The focus of this position was to improve communication and data sharing between the various specialist groups and to provide additional support for those attending the clinic (see figure 3).

Diagrammatic representation of key relationships; a proposed model for a paediatric inherited arrhythmia clinic.

Preclinic intake meeting and family contact

A new intake meeting was established, allowing new referrals to cardiology to be discussed among the MDT. The CGC contacts the family to obtain clinical information, including family tree, clinical tests such as ECG, familial information, and the accompanying consent to access clinical details from other hospital records. Previously, referrals from adult cardiology tended to include too little background information to guide triaging. A three-generation pedigree helps identify those at potential risk and who require screening.68 Previously, it was not uncommon to see one child at IAC, only to later identify other siblings who required screening, resulting in a significant delay until all at-risk children were screened.

The CGC also reviews upcoming patients obtaining outstanding information since the patient’s last clinical review. This may include the cardiac or genetic testing results of adult family members seen at other institutions.

Links with other institutions

A regular MDT with a large adult cardiac genetic service in Sydney (at the Royal Prince Alfred Hospital, Sydney) has now been established where new referrals are discussed. This has included collaboration on entering data into the statewide genetic information system (GIS) database (see below). This link greatly facilitates the transfer of diagnostically critical data from other family members, ensures seamless family screening and facilitates the transition of children to an appropriate adult clinical service when they reach the required age.

Data management

Data management has been a significant hurdle to overcome. The clinical genetics services across NSW have access to a statewide GIS. However, resourcing greatly inhibits the entry of complete family history, diagnostic and genetic testing information, resulting in various data storage methods among different genetic services.

The CGC, JS and JRS were able to coordinate and work with NSW Health and the Agency for Clinical Innovation to obtain access to the NSW GIS database. Families are verbally consented by the CGC at the IAC clinic to enter data to this database, and more than 80 families have been entered to this database. This enhances the ability of other regional genetic services to assess family members referred within their catchment.

Variant curation

When a new variant in a gene of interest is found, pathogenicity may be uncertain. While there are rigorous frameworks for assessing pathogenicity in practice,13 there is still variability in the pathogenicity assignment of a variant between laboratories. ‘Variant curation’ requires input from multiple specialties, beyond the paediatric cardiology clinician. Coordinating a variant meeting has become part of the CGC role. This meeting occurs before the IAC MDT meeting and includes molecular geneticists, genetic pathologists and clinical geneticists outside of the routine MDT. This process gives the cardiology and genetic clinicians confidence when advising families how best to proceed.14 15

Discussion

From the late 1990s onwards, several genetic arrhythmia susceptibility genes have been identified.14 Since then, the rate of progress in cardiac genetics has been exponential, and genetic testing, once isolated to research laboratories, is now sophisticated, rapid and affordable. In the field of IAs and cardiomyopathies, there has been the discovery of and ensuing complexity in interpreting newly identified genes and overlap syndromes, such that keeping up with the knowledge is challenging.14

The ‘one-stop shop’

This report describes how one team has focused an effort to transition this new knowledge into patient care and developed, in essence, a ‘one stop shop’ to facilitate accurate phenotyping and best practice genetic testing to families affected by definite or possible IA syndromes. 85 probands were identified, most presented after cardiac arrest or syncope, and ultimately 52 could be accurately genotyped. The clinic then facilitated the detection of 121 presymptomatic children, most with cardiac ion channelopathies, who are now being carefully managed.

Multidisciplinary care

The data presented here show a predominance of children being diagnosed with IAs via predictive genetic testing and cardiac screening, most commonly LQTS and CPVT. Central to the success of this effective clinic is the MDT. The collaboration of health professionals with experience in managing cardiac conditions combined with genetic knowledge provides enhanced, best practice management of those at risk.6 7 9 16

Clinical Genetics

Following the preclinic intake meeting, genetic counsellors contact families referred to the IAC before the appointment. They ensure that the service has the clinical information, investigations and family history required. They seek to address psychosocial concerns and introduce the role of the clinical genetic service and genetic testing. At the clinic, their role includes recognising potential genetic causes, providing genetic counselling, choosing the test strategy for diagnostic testing and facilitating predictive genetic testing. Afterwards, they will liaise with the genomic testing laboratory and assist in the evaluation of the family history to identify potentially at-risk relatives.5 Genetic counsellors are an essential component of ensuring families understand the medical and psychosocial complexity and implications of IA and work with families to assist them in making informed decisions.17

When the genetic cause of an IA is identified, it can inform the management of patients and families.17 18 Yet, the cardiologist has a significant role since variation in the clinical phenotype between patients with the same pathogenic variant also influences risk - the classic example being the length of the QT interval on the ECG in LQTS.19

It is interesting that in our clinic 81% of family members undergoing genetic testing carried the familial genetic variant, higher than the 50% we would normally expect in autosomal dominant conditions. We don’t fully understand this yet, since not all case results are shared on the NSW genetic platform, but we are aware that in some other family members (including adults and some children), genetic testing was done elsewhere. This included via outreach clinics and regional genetic services; and those who were not found to have the familial variant did not attend the IAC.

Psychological support

Having an IA comes with medical and psychological challenges. Dealing with the lifelong risk of sudden death and restrictions on lifestyle behaviours can lead to decreased compliance with medical interventions and lifestyle management.17 Children with an IA are, of course, at increased risk of cardiac-focused anxiety. Routine psychological screening is recommended, noting that adolescents with IA are the most vulnerable to psychosocial risk and consequently may resist medical management.20

It has been shown that patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy who attend specialised cardiac genetic clinics are better adjusted and worry less than those who do not attend. This suggests that an integrated multidisciplinary approach such as we have with this IAC clinic, including a genetic counsellor, is important in the management of families with inherited heart conditions.21

The CGC: data collation, the intake meeting, variant meeting and links to other services

Although this multidisciplinary clinic has served its patients very well for 11 years, the last 2 years have seen a further significant step up in quality of care, following the inclusion of CGC. The CGC could be an expanded role for someone already involved in the clinic—such as a genetic counsellor, arrhythmia nurse or cardiac physiologist. In our case, the position was advertised and competed for, resulting in a shared appointment between a children’s heart nurse and a senior genetic counsellor. These coordinators ensure that critical data are now shared via a common confidential and secure platform across the state of NSW with other genetic services. They have also established a new referral intake meeting, a novel genetic variant curation meeting, and regular meetings with a large adult cardiac genetic service and with forensic services. The latter allows the discussion of recent sudden death cases to facilitate the simultaneous referral of adult relatives to adult services and children to paediatric services. It also advises on the potential value of genetic testing in the deceased (the molecular autopsy).

Conclusions

We describe the work of a dedicated regional paediatric IAC with nearly half of the probands presenting with cardiac arrest. We show that significant numbers of presymptomatic children can be successfully identified, in part due to the high yield of diagnostic genetic tests. We describe our model of care.

Our recent experience of new dedicated CGC, coming from allied health or nursing backgrounds, is very positive. Future research should include study of the health economic benefits and patient experience, satisfaction and quality of life, which we have not reported here. Nevertheless, the clinical impression of the value of the coordinators is very positive, with a number of new initiatives contributing to streamlining multidisciplinary care.
