Mechanical and microstructural enhancement of clayey soils through recycled glass powder stabilization
Authors: Abdollah Tabaroei, Farhad Bozorgvar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
In this paper, we use recycled glass powder (RGP) as a replacement of the soil to enhance the mechanical characteristics of the Mashhad clayey soil. For this purpose, a total of 60 unconfined compression tests and microstructural analyses including scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and x-ray diffraction (XRD) have been carried out on both of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens. The RGP contents used for replacing in the soil were 3% to 25% and the curing time were of 7 and 28 days, respectively. The results indicated that replacing RGP in clayey soil led to an increase in soil stiffness, unconfined compressive strength (UCS), maximum strain energy or energy absorption capacity (Eu), secant modulus (E50), resilient modulus (MR) and a decrease in the failure strain (εf) of the clayey soil mixture. The effect of increasing in the soil strength was more pronounced with RGP = 15% (optimum content) and more amount of curing times. The results obtained from the microstructural analysis were in full agreement with the findings obtained from UCS tests. The XRD tests indicated that the replacing of RGP = 15% with amorphous structure to the clays soil caused reduced the concentration of crystalline phases in the unstabilized soil specimens, leading to reduce in the intensity of the peaks. SEM results further emphasized that the microstructure of the clayey soil stabilized with RGP = 15% became more consolidated, with a noticeable reduction in the number and size of pores.

Introduction

Fine-grained soils with high plasticity typically lack the desirable properties required for use as structural foundations, unless their engineering characteristics are substantially enhanced in a cost-effective manner. In recent years, there has been growing concern regarding the conservation of natural resources, energy efficiency, carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions, and the management of industrial waste. PC, a crucial additive commonly used in the construction, road-building, and geotechnical engineering industries, is produced through a process that consumes significant amounts of energy and results in CO2 emissions. Notably, the CO2 emissions from cement production account for approximately 5% of global CO2 emissions. This underscores the urgent need for research focused on the development of innovative stabilizers and materials that provide effective performance while minimizing adverse environmental impacts. Glass is one of the most prevalent materials found in waste streams, and its production continues to rise globally. Between 2002 and 2008, the volume of glass waste produced in Europe increased from three to 4.1 million tons per year. According to United Nations data, approximately 200 million tons of solid waste were generated globally in 2004, with around 7% (equivalent to 14 million tons) comprising glass waste. Moreover, the relatively low recycling rate of glass, combined with limited landfill space and the occupation of new landfill sites, has made glass waste a significant challenge for global waste management systems1.

In order to solve geotechnical engineering problems, there are various techniques are available including analytical2,3, laboratory4–8 and numerical9–16 methods. A review of the literature reviews showed significant research on stabilizing of problematic soils using PC17–31.

Using RGP in civil engineering application including construction materials, concrete, road construction, pavement and soil stabilization were reported in the previous studies32–37. Arulrajah et al.34 evaluate on the behavior of FRG as a supplementary material in blends with cement stabilized RCA. The repeated load triaxial tests revealed that RCA/FRG blends with 30% FRG content performed adequately, meeting the threshold for bound pavement material. All blends satisfied the minimum unconfined compression strength requirement after 7 days of curing, while 28-day strengths demonstrated notable enhancement with extended curing. Demir and Cabalar37 conducts some experimental tests on mixture of bentonite WG. They showed that that the unconfined compressive strength of clean bentonite rose with WG content, peaking at 30% medium WG with a 56% increase. The highest energy absorption capacity was 11.50 kJ/m³, marking a 76% improvement over clean bentonite. However, there are limited study focusing on the using of RGP alone or as a geopolymer, for the stabilization of soils38–46. In the following, some of these studies has been presented. Maher and Ho38 explored the mechanical characteristics of composite materials made from clay and fibers, using kaolinite clay, polypropylene fibers, and glass. Their results demonstrated that randomly distributed fibers significantly improved the ductility and strength of clayey soil specimens. Olufowobi et al.39 conducted laboratory experiments to assess the geotechnical characteristics of clayey soils stabilized with GP. Canakci et al.40 studied on the influence of replacing WSLGP on the behavior of clayey soil. They observed that the compressive strength of clayey soil increased with curing time and with incorporating 12% of WSLGP to clayey soil reduced the pressure of swelling of clayey soil from 5.50% to 1.65%. Benny et al.41 by performing DS and CBR tests, study on the influence of GP in improving the strength parameters of clayey soil, with a focusing on its use in roadbed stabilization of the soil. The values of GP considered in their study were 2%, 4%, 6%, 8% and 10%. Pourabbas Bilondi et al.42 carried out experimental tests on clayey soil to investigate the use of RGP as a geopolymer to improve the mechanical behavior of clay soils. Also, they used sodium hydroxide as an activator and varied the curing temperature. Their findings revealed that the optimum value of RGP was 15% and the UCS values of soil specimens increased as curing time increased. Sabbagh Gol and Toufigh44 examined the effects of geopolymer stabilization on the performance of sandy soils. They attempted to use GP as a waste material to replace the natural pozzolans. The activator solution they used consisted of a mixture of sodium hydroxide and liquid sodium silicate, with varying concentrations of sodium hydroxide. Blayi et al.45 through experiments on clayey soil stabilized with GP observed that adding GP to clayey soil significantly increased both the shear strength and bearing capacity of clayey soil. Tajaddini et al.46 assessed the microstructural and mechanical behavior of low-plasticity clay stabilized with a mixture of GP and copper slag. They founded that the values of UCS for all specimens stabilized with this combination improved. In addition, the UCS values ​​of clayey specimens stabilized with GP were higher than stabilized with copper overhead.

RGP presents an environmentally promising yet technically uncertain solution for soil stabilization, particularly for the problematic fine-grained clays prevalent in the Mashhad’s central and eastern regions. While RGP’s pozzolanic potential offers theoretical sustainability benefits, critical knowledge gaps persist regarding its actual stabilization mechanisms-specifically, the unresolved interplay between chemical reactivity (that governed by glass composition) and physical filler effects across diverse in clayey soils. These uncertainties are compounded by inconsistent performance observed in high-plasticity clays and arid environments, limiting reliable field application despite laboratory-scale promise. This study systematically evaluates RGP’s efficacy for the Mashhad’s low-strength clayey soils through a comprehensive experimental program including geotechnical characterization, mechanical performance and microstructural analysis. By addressing both the fundamental stabilization mechanisms and region-specific soil challenges, the research aims to bridge the gap between RGP’s waste-recycling advantages and its practical viability as a stabilization alternative for infrastructure applications in the Mashhad city of Iran.

RGP

RGP has the potential to be used as an alternative material in construction applications. Given the widespread use of glass, its waste is readily available and is often considered an environmental challenge due to its non-biodegradability. In addition to reduce the reliance on raw materials, utilizing waste glass in high-demand industries such as cement production and soil stabilization can remarkably contribute to decreasing conserving natural resources and greenhouse gas emissions. In this study, the RGP used was sourced from glass waste managed by the Mashhad municipality’s waste management system. To prepare RGP used in this study, the glass was first broken into pieces ranging from 1 to 2 cm, then ground in a mill, and finally pulverized using a mortar and pestle. Testing method

Testing program

The specifications and testing program for both unconfined and microstructural tests carried out in this study are shown in Fig. 3. The unconfined compression tests were conducted on both of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens based on ASTM D216654 to obtain the RGP optimum value. The RGP contents used for replacing in clayey soil were considered 3%, 5%, 7%, 9%, 11%, 13%, 15%, 20%, and 25%. The range of RGP values considered in this study are in range with those reported in previous studies40–43,46,55. The curing times for the RGP-stabilized clayey soil specimens were 7 and 28 days that similar to range values that reported in others studies55–57. To ensure accuracy tests results, each test was conducted three times. For both of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens, all unconfined compression tests were conducted at 100% standard compaction. It is worth mentioning that RGP was used as a replacement for clayey soil in all tests, contributing to environmental sustainability. Additionally, two sets of microstructural tests, including SEM and XRD were carried out on both of the unstabilized and RGP-stabilized clayey soil specimens with the optimum value of RGP after 28 days of curing time.

Specimen Preparation

To prepare the unstabilized and stabilized clayey soil specimens, both the clayey soil and RGP were first dried in an oven at 60 °C for 3 days. Once the RGP percentage in clayey soil was determined, clayey soil mixture was adjusted to the required OMC according to the standard compaction test and then thoroughly mixed with RGP for 10 min. It is important to note that the weight of each specimen was calculated based on the weight% of RGP and the OMC, using 100% standard compaction. The homogeneous clayey soil mixture was then divided into five equal portions, each placed into a plastic bag to prevent moisture losses. These portions were transferred to the unconfined compression test mold, where the soil specimens were compacted using a hammer. To ensure better bonding between the compacted clayey soil layers, grooves with thickness about 3 mm were created on the surface of the clayey soil specimen with a blade to help the layers bond more effectively. To assess sample uniformity, multiple specimens were sectioned into 6–8 segments using a precision wire saw. Each segment underwent mass and volumetric measurements, enabling unit weight and density calculations. Comparative analysis revealed minimal deviation (< 5%) from target density values across all sections, confirming statistically uniform material distribution. Therefore, the prepared samples can be considered as uniform samples. After compacting five layers of clayey soil, the soil specimen was removed from the mold with the help of a jack. Finally, the soil specimen was wrapped in cellophane and placed inside a resealable plastic bag to ensure it retained moisture during curing time at room temperature before being tested (25 ± 2 °C). Some researchers used this method to prepare the soil-additive mixture specimens58,59. Figure 4 shows how clayey soil specimen were stored to prevent moisture loss. Results and discussion

In this section of the article, the results obtained from the unconfined compression tests on the clay-RGP mixture, as well as the engineering parameters derived from these tests, are reviewed and interpreted. These parameters including UCS, the failure strain (εf), maximum strain energy or energy absorption capacity (Eu), secant modulus (E50) and resilient modulus (MR). Additionally, the results from the microstructural tests are provided.

Compressive strength and engineering soil parameters

UCS curves

In this study, the stress-strain curve for each test was determined by averaging the results of three individual tests conducted for the same soil specimen. In Fig. 6, the stress-strain curves for both of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens for different RGP contents, after curing time of 7 and 28 days. The findings indicated that the incorporation of RGP significantly enhanced the UCS values of the stabilized soil specimens. This improvement can be attributed to pozzolanic reactions and the cementitious products formation within the clay-RGP mixture. Due to the angular shape and higher strength of the RGP particles as compared to clayey soil particles, the interlocking and internal friction between clayey soil particles are increased, thereby contributing to the higher values of USC for soil mixture. The USC value of the clay-RGP mixtures after curing time of 28 days were greater than that of the 7 days specimens, suggesting that the chemical reactions in the soil mixture take time to fully develop. Additionally, the substitution of RGP in clayey soil can caused an increase in the stiffness (slope of the stress-strain curve) of the clay-RGP mixture, with the effect being more pronounced as the RGP content approached the optimum value and the curing time was extended.

Stress-strain curves of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens for different RGP contents at curing time of: (a) 7 days and (b) 28 days.

UCS variations

The variations of the UCS values of both of the unstabilized and stabilized clayey soil specimens for different values of RGP contents and curing time is depicted in Fig. 7. The findings revealed that the UCS values for the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens after curing time of 7 and 28 days ranged from 374 to 593 kPa and 413 to 729 kPa, respectively. This demonstrates that the UCS of the clay-RGP mixtures after 28 days of curing time is greater than that those curing in 7 days.

Replacing RGP content up to 15% in clayey soil caused a 13% increase in the UCS values of 7 days and a 64% increase in the 28 days of clayey soil specimens. Increasing the RGP content from 15% to 20% caused only a slight increase of 2.35% in the UCS values of the 28 days clayey soil specimen, indicating that replacing more than 15% of RGP to the clayey soil is not cost-effective. Therefore, it can be concluded that the optimum value of RGP content in this study is 15%. The optimum RGP content determined in this paper are in accordance with the value reported in the previous studies. Pourabbas Bilondi et al.42 by conducting unconfined compressive tests on clayey soil, studied on the effect of RGP as a geopolymer to increase the physical behavior of clayey soils. They concluded that to get a best performance in soil, the optimum RGP content should be 15%. Mohammadzadeh and Toufigh60 conducted unconfined compression tests on low liquid limit clayey soil specimens and reported the optimum RGP content to be 15%. Hosseini et al.61 also performed unconfined compression tests on a poorly graded sand-clayey soil mixture, with CCR and GP, reporting the optimal CCR and RGP contents as 7% and 15%, respectively.

εf variations

Figure 8 illustrates the variations of the εf values for the unstabilized and stabilized clayey soil specimens at different values of RGP and curing time. It is important to note that εf refers to the strain induced in the soil under maximum value of stress. As shown, the values of εf for the clayey soil specimens range from 4% to 8%. The results showed that the use of RGP decreases the εf value of the clayey-RGP mixture, which indicates that RGP substitution leads to brittleness and an increase in the elastic modulus of the clayey soil specimens. A decreasing trend in εf value is more pronounced for RGP contents ranging from 9% to 20%. The values of εf for the clayey soil stabilized obtained at 7 days is similar to 28 days of curing time.

Eu variations

Some important mechanical indices for clayey soils are Eu, E50 and MR are that their values determined from the stress-strain curve obtained from unconfined compression tests. In the following section, each of these parameters has been thoroughly evaluated. One of the significant parameters for clayey soils is Eu, which indicates the toughness of the specimen and the energy required for its deformation. The value of Eu is obtained by integrating the stress-strain curve of clayey specimen up to the point of maximum stress. The variations of Eu values for the unstabilized and stabilized clayey soil specimens at different values of RGP and curing time are presents in Fig. 9. As depicted, the Eu value for the 7 days of curing time for the unstabilized and RGP-stabilized clayey soil specimens with RGP = 15% are 18 and 12 kJ/m³, respectively. The reason for this decrease in Eu value is attributed to the limited reaction of RGP with clayey soil at 7 days of curing time. As the curing time increases from 7 to 28 days, the Eu values for RGP-stabilized clayey soil also change, with an increase of 34% in Eu when RGP = 15% is replaced to clayey soil, indicating an increase in reaction and better cohesion between RGP and clayey soil particles.

Variations of Eu values for the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens for different values of RGP and curing time.

E50 variations

E50 or elasticity modulus of corresponding to 50% of maximum strength reflects clayey soil specimens stiffness and is equal to the linear slope drawn from the origin of the coordinates to the point corresponding to 50% of maximum stress. Figure 10 shows the variations of E50 values for the unstabilized and stabilized clayey soil specimens at different values of RGP and curing time. The results show that the E50 value for the clay-RGP mixture is higher than that for the unstabilized clayey soil, which can be interpreted as follows: the unstabilized clayey soil shows significant plasticity and deformability, while the clay-RGP mixture behaves in a more brittle manner due to the cementitious products formation in the mixture. This behavior is also clearly visible in the stress-strain curves illustrated in Fig. 6. According to the results obtained for Eu and at a constant RGP substitution percentage, E50 for the clay-RGP mixture increases with curing time (from 7 to 28 days). The E50 value for the 7 and 28 days clayey soil specimens with RGP = 15% increased by 88% and 162%, respectively, indicating a significant increase in the stiffness of clayey soil mixture. As the RGP content increases from 15% to 20%, the rate of increase in E50 values for the 7 and 28 days cured specimens is 4.3% and 28.7%, respectively, indicating a marginal increase in E50 values. The trend in the increase of E50 for clayey soil specimens with RGP substitution aligns with the trend in UCS values (Sect. 4.1.2).

Modes of failure in soil-RGP specimens

Based on Hatibu and Hettiaratchi63 and Liu et al.64 studies, all failure modes for the unstabilized and stabilized clayey soil specimens can be categorized into seven different modes, as shown in the top section of Fig. 12. As depicted, modes (a) to (c) represent brittle-columnar, slabbing, and faulting states. The transitional state from brittle to ductile with a crushed failure surface and cataclastic or shear faulting is shown in mode (d). Modes (e) to (g) represent the ductile deformation of the specimens with flowing curved surface lines, indicating ductile-faulting behavior.

Figure 12 presents the different failure modes observed in the unstabilized and RGP-stabilized clayey soil specimens at different values of RGP and curing time. As seen, the failure mode for the unstabilized clayey soil specimens after curing time of 28 days is ductile (mode f). When RGP is replaced to clayey soil, the failure behavior of clayey soil specimens changes. The RGP particles make clayey soil specimens more brittle, with the failure modes transitioning into brittle-columnar, slabbing, and faulting states. If the curing time increases from 7 to 28 days, vertical cracks along the length of the specimens become more pronounced. For RGP = 11% and 13%, after 28 days of curing time, wedge failure occurs at the top and bottom of clayey soil specimen, and the failure mechanism transitions from faulting states to slabbing. For clayey soil specimen substituted with RGP = 15%, as the curing time increases from 7 to 28 days, the failure mechanism changes from faulting states to brittle-columnar. Finally, in clayey soil specimens substituted with RGP = 20% and 25%, the failure mechanism at 7 and 28 days is diagonal, with smaller vertical and diagonal cracks forming along these primary cracks.

Microstructure analysis

XRD

The XRD spectra of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens at RGP = 15% after 28 days of curing time is illustrated in Fig. 13. As seen in the figure, the primary characteristic peaks in all three graphs include the quartz (SiO2) peak, located at angles of 20.8°, 22°, 26.7°, and 36.7°65–69.

RGP is an entirely amorphous, silicon-rich material, and its XRD spectrum shows no crystalline peaks70. The figure demonstrates that by replacing RGP = 15% in clayey soil, no new peaks appear in clayey soil specimen, and there is no shift in the crystalline peaks. However, the intensity of all the peaks in the unstabilized clayey soil specimen decreases. This suggests that the overall crystalline structure of clayey soil is largely maintained. In conclusion, the replacement of RGP with amorphous structure results in a dilution of the crystalline phase concentration in the unstabilized clayey soil, which in turn leads to a reduction in peak intensities, as peak intensity is directly related to the amount of crystalline material present.

The XRD analyses of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens at RGP = 15% after 28 days of curing time.

SEM

Figure 14 displays SEM analyses of the unstabilized/stabilized RGP clayey soil specimens at RGP = 15% after curing time of 28 days. As observed in the first row images, the natural clayey soil specimen consists of aggregated particles with a disordered structure. Additionally, the microscopic structure of the unstabilized clayey soil specimen is highly porous, with large pores and cracks visible at a magnification of 1000x. The presence of pores in the unstabilized clayey soil reduces its compression strength. Upon the substitution of RGP = 15% in the natural clayey soil (the optimum RGP content, second row images), the microscopic structure of the clay-RGP mixture becomes more uniform, and the number and size of the pores decrease significantly. Specifically, the sharp-edged, angular RGP particles fill the voids between clayey soil particles, leading to a reduction in overall porosity and an increase in mechanical interlocking. The angular shape and high surface area of RGP particles ensure effective bonding during mixing and compaction, resulting in a denser and stronger structure strength to compression. Notably, SEM results are in full alignment with the findings from the XRD analyses and the UCS tests of clayey soil specimens.

Conclusions

This paper investigates the improvement of the mechanical properties of the Mashhad city clayey soil by replacing RGP in to it. A total of 60 UCS tests were conducted on the unstabilized and RGP-stabilized clayey soil specimens, with RGP contents of 3% to 25% and curing time of 7 and 28 days. The main results obtained from the tests are summarized as follows:

The substitution of RGP in clayey soil resulted in an increase in the stiffness of the clay-RGP mixture, with the effect being more pronounced as RGP content approached the optimum value and the curing time was extended. Due to the angular shape and higher strength of the RGP particles as compared to clayey soil particles, the interlocking and internal friction between clayey soil particles are increased, thereby contributing to the higher values of USC for soil mixture. For clayey soil used in this study, the optimum value of RGP content is found to be 15%.Replacing clayey soil with RGP reduced the εf value of the clayey-RGP mixture, indicating brittleness and an increase in the elastic modulus of clayey soil specimens.The E50 value of the clay-RGP mixture was higher than that of the unstabilized clayey soil, primarily because the unstabilized clayey soil showed notable plasticity, while the clay-RGP mixture exhibited a more brittle behavior due to the cementitious products formation. The substitution of RGP = 15% into clayey soil specimen significantly increased the E50 values for both the 7 and 28 days cured specimens.The inclusion of RGP led to increased adhesion, interlocking between particles, a significant rise in MR and a decrease in the flexibility of the soil specimens.
