Interventions to reduce the impact of post-tuberculosis lung disease: a scoping review of the literature
Authors: Anthony L. Byrne, Gunar Günther, Cathrine Tadyanemhandu, Carlos P. Borges de Almeida, Intan MW. Dewi, Carole D. Mitnick, Serena P. Koenig, Kamila Romanowski, Robert S. Wallis
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPost tuberculosis lung disease (PTLD) is an increasingly recognized contributor of long-term morbidity among the estimated 155 million survivors of Mycobacterium tuberculosis disease. While a range of interventions may influence PTLD risk, the full spectrum has not been systematically reviewed.MethodsWe conducted a scoping review following Joanna Briggs Institute methodology to identify interventions delivered during or after TB treatment with potential to affect PTLD outcomes. Embase was searched from inception to March 31, 2025, for seven intervention categories: host-directed therapy (HDT), therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM), inhaled pharmacotherapy, antimicrobials beyond TB treatment, treatment shortening, thoracic surgery, and microbiome-altering interventions. Outcomes were grouped into disability, lung function, radiology, biomarkers, and histopathology, and categorized as showing improvement, worsening, or no effect on PTLD.ResultsOf 7,965 records screened, 87 studies were included. Most were small, uncontrolled, and designed to assess microbiological cure rather than post-TB sequelae. Radiology and unstructured symptom reports were the most common outcomes, while validated disability tools and advanced lung function measures were rarely used. Corticosteroids and several HDTs showed signals of radiological or spirometric benefit, but the findings were inconsistent. Doxycycline and metformin improved cavity resolution in small trials. Inhaled bronchodilators demonstrated short-term spirometric gains in two studies and a survival benefit in one retrospective cohort. Antifungals for chronic pulmonary aspergillosis improved symptoms, radiology, and inflammatory biomarkers, whereas evidence for antibacterial or NTM-directed therapy was lacking. TDM was associated with faster culture conversion in some studies but was not linked to PTLD-specific outcomes. Microbiome studies consistently reported reduced diversity and altered composition during TB treatment, but no direct associations with PTLD.ConclusionEvidence for interventions to prevent or mitigate PTLD remains sparse, heterogeneous, and largely incidental. High-quality studies using standardized, person-centered outcomes are urgently needed to guide care for TB survivors worldwide.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12890-026-04355-3.

Introduction

Many of the estimated 155 million global tuberculosis (TB) survivors continue to experience chronic symptoms despite successful antimicrobial treatment [1]. Post-TB lung disease (PTLD) - defined as chronic respiratory abnormalities, in the presence or absence of symptoms attributable to previous TB disease [2] - significantly contributes to post-TB morbidity and mortality. There is accumulating evidence of reduced long-term survival and chronic disability from respiratory and other sequalae in this population of TB survivors [3, 4]. In low- and middle-income countries, where much of the burden of disease falls, there are an estimated 46.7% of people with abnormal lung function and 41% reporting persistent symptoms [5]. With more than 10 million individuals developing TB annually, the global burden of PTLD is substantial, underscoring the urgent need for strategies to prevent or reduce its severity.

Intervention to mitigate PTLD can target either the pathogen (Mycobacterium tuberculosis) or the host (i.e., the individual with TB). Pathogen-directed strategies include ensuring effective, resistance-appropriate antibiotics, optimizing drug levels through therapeutic drug monitoring, and evaluating treatment-shortening regimen. Host-directed approaches aim to minimize tissue damage, reduce disability, and enhance recovery. These include systemic or inhaled corticosteroids, other immune-modulating or metabolic therapies, and microbiome-altering interventions. Inhaled pharmacotherapies and antimicrobials, including antibiotics and anti-fungals, may also reduce post-TB complications by addressing secondary infections or persistent inflammation. The timing of the intervention is also critical: intervening early, during TB treatment, may help prevent lasting lung impairment, whereas interventions initiated after treatment may have limited potential to prevent chronic impairment.

This scoping review aimed to identify and synthesize the available evidence on a broad range of interventions, administered during or after TB treatment, that may mitigate the development or severity of PTLD.

Methods

This review followed the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Methods Manual for Scoping Reviews and was reported according to the PRISMA-ScR (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews) guidelines. The completed checklist is available in the supporting files (Additional file 1), and the protocol is available upon request. Ethical approval was not required.

Search strategy and data sources

We applied JBI’s recommended three-step approach. First, an initial search of Embase was conducted on July 25, 2023, using the terms: (Tuberculosis) AND (Antimicrobials OR Pharmacotherapy OR Drug Monitoring OR Host directed Therapy OR Surgery). Keywords and indexing terms from retrieved articles informed refinement of the search strategy with a medical librarian from the University of British Columbia. The final Embase search was completed on August 3, 2023, and updated on March 31st, 2025. Reference lists of included studies were also manually screened to identify additional studies.

Because older host-directed therapy studies (e.g., corticosteroids, ACTH, vitamin D) often lacked searchable abstracts, we also performed a targeted review of historical literature known to the authors to capture relevant articles. The complete search strategy is provided in the Additional file 2.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

We included English-language studies evaluating interventions delivered during or after TB treatment to prevent or reduce PTLD. Eligible populations included individuals with confirmed TB (microbiologically or clinically diagnosed), either on treatment or post-treatment. Interventions were categorized as: TB related surgery, treatment shortening, host-directed therapy (HDT), inhaled pharmacotherapy, use of antimicrobials beyond TB treatment, therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) and microbiome-altering interventions.

We excluded narrative reviews, case reports, studies without pre-specified respiratory outcomes, and articles without full-text availability. Studies on pulmonary rehabilitation, smoking cessation, or nutritional support were also excluded, as these are addressed in prior reviews.

Outcomes

Outcomes were grouped into five domains:

For TDM and microbiome interventions, no predefined PTLD-specific outcomes were identified. However, due to their potential relevance, we reported these categories separately. For TDM, we considered culture conversion as a proxy outcome under the assumption that faster clearance may reduce PTLD risk. For microbiome-altering interventions, changes in microbiota composition were treated as potential PTLD-related biomarkers.

Study selection, data extraction, and charting of results

References were managed in Zotero for de-duplication and screened in Rayyan. Abstracts were reviewed independently by three teams of reviewers, with uncertain records advanced to full-text review. Full-text screening was completed by one reviewer and verified by a second; discrepancies were resolved by discussion.

Data extraction used a standardized form and was cross-checked by a second reviewer. We used evidence-mapping to describe the distribution, scope, and methodological features of the literature. Findings were summarized in tables and figures and synthesized narratively.

Results

Overall summary of included studies

We screened 7965 unique titles and abstracts across the seven interventions categories and included 87 studies in the review (Fig. 1, Additional file 3: Prisma charts for individual interventions). Studies were published between 1959 and 2025, and designs varied with the majority being randomized controlled trials (n = 47) and cohort studies (n = 29) (Table 1). Overall, radiological outcomes (n = 49) and respiratory disability assessed through symptom (n = 43) were the most frequently reported endpoints, while biomarkers (n = 18) and lung function (n = 15) were less common. No studies reported on histopathology outcomes. A heatmap of outcomes by intervention category is shown in Figs. 2, 3 and 4.

TB related surgery

Thirteen studies [6–18] evaluated surgical interventions performed during TB treatment for sequelae such as destroyed lung, cavitary lesions, bronchiectasis, or airway stenosis (Fig. 2, Additional file 4). All reported reductions in respiratory symptoms such as cough, haemoptysis, or dyspnea, though none used standardized quality-of-life tools. Two studies documented postoperative spirometry improvements [8, 15], while nine described radiological resolution of cavities [6, 7, 11, 13, 14], hydropneumothorax [13] and bronchiectasis [7, 11]. No studies reported biomarker or histopathology outcomes. Although surgery generally improved symptoms and imaging, postoperative complications such as empyema, bronchopleural fistula, and prolonged air leak were frequently noted.

Tuberculosis treatment shortening

Three studies [19–21] assessed PTLD outcomes with treatment-shortening (Fig. 2, Additional file 4), assuming, that by decreasing the duration of active disease using more effective treatments, related inflammation and cumulative lung injury could be limited. However, the opposite is also possible and shorter TB treatment duration may also translate to less opportunity for TB antimicrobials to mitigate lung injury and its related effect on lung function and respiratory symptoms. Disability was reported in two studies [20, 21], one showing improvement in symptoms and quality of life with shorter therapy [20], though none used validated questionnaires. Lung function was assessed in only one study, which found no difference between short and standard regimens [21]. Radiological outcomes were reported in two studies, with greater improvements observed in standard-duration groups. One study included biomarker measures, favoring standard therapy [19]. Overall, PTLD outcomes were inconsistently reported, often limited to basic imaging or symptom counts, with little use of standardized instruments.

Host-directed therapies

Vitamin D

Vitamin D was studied in 8 randomized controlled trials [22–29] (Fig. 2, Additional file 4). The vitamin D dose varied substantially among these trials, as did the extent of pre-existing vitamin D deficiency. Five reported effects on chest radiology [23–27], of which two found more rapid clearance of lesions [23, 26]. Two studies reported spirometry results, but neither found a benefit of Vitamin D [27, 28]. Four reported on disability using symptom scores [25–27, 29], of which two documented an improvement [26, 29].

Corticosteroids

Twenty trials of ACTH and/or corticosteroids were identified, of which 15 were reported from 1959 to 1969 [30–49] (Fig. 2, Additional file 4). The daily dose and duration of corticosteroid treatment varied substantially among the trials, whereas they were given exclusively during TB treatment. 15 trials reported outcomes on chest radiography, of which 12 showed favorable improvement in the intervention group. Four studies reported lung function outcomes [38–40, 42], of which two found transient improvement [38, 39]. One study reported long-term clinical outcomes. In this trial, which followed 102 people for 5 years, corticosteroid treatment resulted in fewer re-hospitalizations for tuberculosis and fewer deaths due to non-tuberculous respiratory disease [36].

HDT other than corticosteroids and Vitamin D

Ten studies reported on PTLD-related outcomes across a range of agents, including doxycyline, metformin, phenylbutyrate, pentoxifylline, CC-11050, everolimus, and auranofin [25, 28, 50–59] (Fig. 2, Additional file 4). Additional search of references revealed studies using rosuvastatin, atorvastatin, etanercept, N-acetylcysteine, interleukin- 2, recombinant interferon gamma 1b and therapeutic vaccination with Mycobacterium vaccae to alter post TB outcomes [50, 51, 53, 57, 59–61]. Overall, 11 studies reported radiological outcomes, seven reported TB score or symptoms, and three reported lung function outcomes.

CC-11050 (now dovramilast, a type 4 phosphodiesterase-inhibitor), everolimus (an mTOR inhibitor) and N-acetylcysteine (an antioxidant) had a positive effect on lung function (FEV1); everolimus additionally reduced pulmonary inflammation, measured as glucose utilization by 18F-FDG PET-CT scan [28, 58, 59]. In one trial, N-acetylcysteine (1200 mg) given twice daily for 112 days during TB treatment in people with moderate to severe lung damage significantly improved FEV1 and FVC at 6 months, with apparent benefit up to 24 months [59].

In addition to its antimicrobial properties, doxycycline has broad-spectrum activity as an inhibitor of matrix – metalloproteinases (MMPs). These enzymes are responsible for destruction of the extracellular matrix that surrounds cells. In a recent phase II trial, doxycycline significantly improved cavity resolution compared to placebo and reduced type 1 collagenase, elastase in sputum and MMP-1, -8, -9, -12 -13 [54]. In one trial, metformin (1000 mg) led to more rapid clearance of cavities on chest X-ray with a relative risk 0.42 (95%CI, 0.18–0.96) and to significant reduction in acute phase proteins and proinflammatory cytokines at 8 weeks [55]. Two trials have examined adjunctive HMGCo-A reductase inhibitors (statins) in TB. In one, atorvastatin (40 mg) led to faster chest X-ray clearance and cavity resolution in a small trial in Nigeria, while in a second, rosuvastatin (10 mg) did not influence imaging, including total lung glycolysis in PET- CT [50, 51]. Phenylbutyrate, etanercept (a TNF blocker) and M. vaccae (a rapid-growing mycobacterium found in soil) had a mild positive impact on X-ray clearance, while pentoxifylline (a non-specific phosphodiesterase inhibitor) improved symptoms [25, 53, 57]. Interferon gamma had a small positive effect on symptoms resolution, while interleukin 2 did not. Both did not improve clearance of chest imaging [60, 61].

Inhaled therapies

Three studies [62–64] assessed bronchodilators (Fig. 2, Additional file 4). One trial investigated the impact of indacaterol, a long-acting beta mimetic, and two studies investigated tiotropium bromide, a long-acting anticholinergic on lung function outcomes. Indacaterol improved FEV1 after eight weeks [62], while tiotropium was associated with improved survival in a retrospective Korean cohort and greater FEV1 after two months in another study [64]. Disability outcomes were rarely measured and never with validated scales, and no studies reported radiology, biomarkers, or histopathology.

Antimicrobials

Nine studies [65–73] investigated antifungal or antibacterial therapy for chronic aspergillosis (CPA) and secondary infections in post-TB bronchiectasis (Fig. 2, Additional file 4). Across studies, 50–80% of people with CPA had a previous history of TB disease, with chronic cavitary disease being the most common presentation. Treatment with itraconazole or voriconazole led to symptomatic improvement, most notably reduction in haemoptysis, and several studies reported better quality of life, although these were not assessed with standardized questionnaires [65–67]. Radiological responses were documented in one-third to three-quarters of people treated [69, 71], with reduced cavity size, pleural thickening, and resolution of aspergillomas. Biomarkers, including CRP, ESR, and the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio, frequently improved with therapy [66, 70, 71]. Lung function and histopathology outcomes were not evaluated.

Therapeutic drug monitoring

Eight studies [74–81] evaluated therapeutic drug monitoring (TDM) during TB treatment, covering both first-line drugs (isoniazid, rifampicin, ethambutol, pyrazinamide) and second-line agents such as delamanid, clofazimine, bedaquiline, linezolid, kanamycin, and amikacin (Fig. 3, Additional file 4). None directly assessed PTLD outcomes. Instead, culture and smear conversion and time to culture positivity were used as proxy measures, under the assumption that more rapid clearance could reduce long-term lung injury. Five studies demonstrated that higher drug exposure, typically measured by Cmax or area under the concentration curve, was associated with faster conversion [74–77, 81], whereas three found no association [78–80]. One study measured both serum and bronchial lavage concentrations of first-line drugs and showed that drug levels correlated with treatment outcomes using standard dosing [78]. Disability, lung function, radiology, and histopathology outcomes were not reported.

Microbiome

Eleven studies, primarily case-control or small cohort studies, examined the effect of Mycobacterium tuberculosis TB disease and antimicrobial treatment on gut microbiota diversity and composition using 16S rRNA sequencing or metagenomics [82–91] (Fig. 4, Additional file 4). One study assessed non-TB antibiotics and microRNA signaling [65]. Findings consistently indicated reduced diversity and altered composition of commensal species in TB-affected individuals. Specific microbiome profiles, such as dominance of Proteobacteria, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria in TB/HIV co-infection, or Pseudomonas species in recurrent TB, may have relevance to PTLD, given the association between recurrent TB, abnormal lung function, and structural lung disease (e.g., bronchiectasis) [76, 77]. Another study found an increase in Rothia spp. in sputa during treatment was associated with delayed culture conversion and reduced radiological resolution of pulmonary lesions. However, no study directly linked these microbiome biomarkers to PTLD [84].

Discussion

This scoping review identified 87 studies across seven intervention categories aimed at mitigating PTLD. Most were small, heterogenous, and rarely designed with PTLD prevention or treatment as a primary outcome. When outcomes were reported, they were typically limited to chest radiographs, or basic symptom scores. More commonly accepted tools in chronic disease research, such as mMRC dyspnea scale and SGRQ are seldom used. Few studies included lung function, almost exclusively spirometry, and none applied advanced measures such as plethysmography or diffusion capacity. Overall, PTLD-relevant endpoints were under-prioritized, with most studies focused on microbiological cure rather than functional recovery or quality of life.

Surgical interventions were reported in 13 cohort studies, mainly as adjuncts for drug-resistant TB. While improvements in symptoms and radiology were described, the absence of control groups and individualized indications prevent conclusions about their role in PTLD prevention [2, 92, 93]. Similarly, three trials of treatment shortening for drug-sensitive TB found no consistent effects on PTLD outcomes. Despite the large number of TB treatment trials in recent years, PTLD-related endpoints remain largely neglected [94].

Vitamin D was the most frequently studied HDT but showed no consistent benefit for PTLD outcomes [95]. Corticosteroids, mainly evaluated in older studies, often hastened radiographic resolution but provided limited evidence for long-term functional benefit. More recent candidates, including N-acetylcysteine, CC-11050, and everolimus, demonstrated modest improvements in spirometry [28, 59], while doxycycline and metformin improved radiological outcomes. These findings suggest potential promise, but trials remain few, small, and inconsistent, and rarely incorporated person-reported outcomes [54, 55].

Inhaled bronchodilators, evaluated in three studies showed improvements in lung function, and, in one retrospective study, a survival benefit among TB survivors prescribed tiotropium [63]. Antifungal therapy for chronic pulmonary aspergillosis demonstrated benefit in radiology and symptoms [96], but evidence for treating bacterial or NTM infections in PTLD was lacking.

TDM studies focused on microbiological endpoints, with some evidence linking optimal drug exposure to faster culture conversion. Whether this translates into reduced PTLD risk remains unclear. Microbiome studies consistently reported reduced diversity and altered composition during TB treatment but did not assess direct links with PTLD, highlighting a gap for future research.

Despite the diversity of approaches investigated, the evidence base for PTLD interventions remains narrow and fragmented. Most studies were designed to optimize TB cure rather than to prevent or manage long-term sequelae, and PTLD outcomes were usually secondary or incidental. Where benefits were observed, whether with host-directed therapies, inhaled bronchodilators, or antifungals, results were based on small trials or observational studies, often with short follow-up and non-standardized outcome measures. This underscores both the promise of several candidate interventions and the pressing need for larger, rigorously designed studies explicitly focused on PTLD.

This review has several limitations. First, we focused on seven predefined intervention categories, which may have excluded other potentially relevant strategies. Second, we limited our search to Embase and English-language publications, which could have led to missed studies. Third, a widely accepted, specific PTLD definition was not used in research studies [2] and outcomes were self-defined, as no consensus PTLD outcome criteria existed, and reported measures varied widely across studies [93]. This heterogeneity limited comparability and synthesis. Finally, our scope was restricted to pulmonary TB (without specifying between drug-resistant and drug-sensitive TB) and respiratory sequelae, excluding extrapulmonary disease. However, a specific strength of this review was the inclusion of older literature not indexed in contemporary databases, such as corticosteroid trials, which provided historical context often absent in recent reviews.

Conclusion

Evidence for interventions to prevent or treat PTLD remains limited, heterogeneous, and often incidental. Most studies continue to prioritize microbiological cure over clinically meaningful outcomes such as symptoms, lung function, and quality of life. Future research must move beyond cure alone, integrating standardized, person-centered outcomes. Addressing this gap is critical to improving long-term health for the growing population of TB survivors worldwide.
