Circular RNAs in Lotus japonicus Responses to Nutrient Supply and Mesorhizobium Symbiosis
Authors: Delecia Utley, Asa Budnick, Simona Radutoiu, Heike Sederoff
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTSymbiotic interactions between legumes and rhizobia enable nitrogen fixation under low nutrient conditions. The establishment and function of symbiotic interactions require coordinated changes in gene expression in both the host and the microbe. Circular RNAs (circRNAs) are endogenous gene‐specific molecules that can regulate transcription and translation in response to biotic and abiotic stress through various mechanisms. Our objective was to identify circRNAs specifically generated in response to nutrient supply and rhizobial symbiosis. We sequenced nodulated and non‐inoculated roots from Lotus japonicus and identified a total of 11,923 putative circRNAs originating from 5,290 nuclear‐encoded transcripts in Lotus roots under low or high nutrient supply and nodulated roots. Of those, 58 circRNAs were specific and present in most nodulated root samples. We identified circRNAs for more than half of the known symbiosis‐associated genes, including SymRK, CCamK, and Cyclops, and showed that several of those genes also generated circRNAs in Phaseolus vulgaris nodules. We validated select circRNAs potentially involved in regulating symbiosis and predicted miRNA recognition elements (MREs) created only by the backsplice junctions of circRNAs. These putative backsplice‐generated MREs could represent an additional mechanism by which circRNAs may modulate the abundance and translation of mRNAs in competing endogenous RNA‐regulatory networks.Keywords: backsplice‐generated miRNA response element (bsg‐MRE), circular RNA, gene expression, Lotus japonicus, microRNA, rhizobia, symbiosis

Introduction

Harnessing the power of symbiotic relationships formed between plants and beneficial microbes will be key to advancing sustainable agriculture. These symbiotic relationships enhance nutrient uptake, improve soil health, and reduce the reliance on chemical inputs. Legumes are unique in their ability to form symbiotic relationships with both phosphorus‐acquiring arbuscular mycorrhizae and nitrogen‐fixing rhizobia. Lotus japonicus has been studied as a model legume to understand the evolution, signaling and development of those symbiotic interactions.

Perception is the first step in the development of symbiotic relationships. Plants excrete secondary metabolites that are recognized by rhizobia, resulting in the production of Nod factors. In L. japonicus, the Nod factor receptors, NFR1 and NFR5, are responsible for recognizing these signals (Broghammer et al. 2012; Murakami et al. 2018).

Downstream of recognition, the symbiotic relationships between legumes and rhizobia are orchestrated by a shared molecular pathway, known as the common symbiosis pathway (CSP), which includes several genes that are involved in nodulation and colonization of AM fungi. Some CSP genes that are shared among the two symbiotic relationships include the Symbiosis Receptor Like Kinase (SymRK) (Stracke et al. 2002), CASTOR and POLLUX (Charpentier et al. 2008), Calcium and Calmodulin‐dependent protein Kinase (CCaMK) (Gleason et al. 2006), and CYCLOPS (S. Singh et al. 2014; Yano et al. 2008).

While the genetic framework governing these processes is well‐established, emerging research suggests that RNA, particularly non‐coding RNA, plays a crucial role in regulating the expression of some symbiosis‐associated genes. Non‐coding RNAs, such as microRNAs (miRNAs), transfer RNA (tRNA) derived small RNA fragments (tRFs), and circular RNAs (circRNAs), are increasingly recognized as important regulators of gene expression, including the fine‐tuning of plant responses to microbial signaling (Okuma and Kawaguchi 2021; B. Ren et al. 2019; Tsikou et al. 2018). Understanding the role of non‐coding RNA in these pathways offers new insights into how plants manage complex symbiotic relationships post‐transcriptionally. For example, miR2111 is involved in the systemic regulation of nodulation in L. japonicus (Tsikou et al. 2018). Two other miRNAs, miR397 and miR171 are systemically regulated and target a transcription factor involved in organogenesis of nodules in L. japonicus (De Luis et al. 2012). Rhizobial tRFs were found to regulate host genes involved in nodulation by affecting the RNA interference machinery, a novel mechanism showing regulation of host genes by the symbiont (B. Ren et al. 2019). More recently, circRNAs have emerged as crucial players in plant responses to abiotic and biotic conditions (Lv et al. 2020; Ma et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2020; Xia et al. 2023).

CircRNAs are single stranded, covalently closed RNA molecules, distinguishable only by their back‐splice junction (BSJ) (Zhang et al. 2024). The BSJ is formed by the ligation of the 3’ and 5’ ends of linear RNA, making these circular molecules inaccessible to exonucleases. In plants, there are several studies focusing on the discovery of circRNAs under different abiotic and biotic conditions (Lv et al. 2020; Ma et al. 2021; Wu et al. 2020; Xia et al. 2023; Zeng et al. 2023). CircRNAs have been identified in response to nodulation in Phaseolus vulgaris (Wu et al. 2020), and to nitrogen fertilization or availability in bamboo, maize, wheat, and poplar (Liu et al. 2020; Ma et al. 2021; Ren et al. 2018; Zhu et al. 2023). CircRNAs were detected in soybean experiencing low phosphorus conditions (Lv et al. 2020). There are also studies that identify circRNAs involved in plant defense and immunity responses (Fan et al. 2020; Xia et al. 2023). Collectively, these findings provide key insights into the potential role of circRNAs in plant symbiosis and nutrient signaling.

One well‐documented function of circRNAs in both animals and plants is transcriptional regulation. In animals, some circRNAs enhance their own gene expression by interacting with the transcriptional machinery (Guria et al. 2020), while others bind to their cognate mRNA to suppress expression (Guria et al. 2020; Li et al. 2017). In plants, a circRNA from exon 6 of the SEPALLATA3 (SEP3) gene in Arabidopsis thaliana forms an R‐loop on the parent gene. Transgenic overexpression of the circRNA led to increased abundance of the exon‐6‐skipped alternative splice variant and a floral phenotype (Conn et al. 2017).

CircRNAs are well known for their role as miRNA sponges. A classic example in animals is ciRS‐7, which binds to miR‐7, a key regulator in various cancers (Guria et al. 2020; Kefas et al. 2008). CiRS‐7 contains multiple binding sites for miR‐7, many of which are conserved across vertebrates, effectively sequestering miR‐7 and preventing its interaction with mRNA targets (Guria et al. 2020; Zhang et al. 2024). In plants, circR5g05160 enhances Magnaporthe oryzae blast resistance in rice through miRNA sponging (Fan et al. 2020).

CircRNAs' ability to compete with linear mRNA for miRNA binding could form competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks that coordinate expression within a pathway or between networks. The ceRNA model suggests that circular RNAs containing shared miRNA response elements (MREs) can compete for miRNA binding, thereby regulating each other's expression levels (Salmena et al. 2011). This hypothesized mechanism creates a miRNA‐mediated regulatory network linking different RNA species, including mRNAs, long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and circRNAs (Salmena et al. 2011). By sequestering miRNAs, circRNAs could protect target mRNAs from degradation or translational inhibition. Several plant studies have predicted that these ceRNA networks function under different conditions (Y. Wang et al. 2022; Xu et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2022). In common bean, this network was predicted to regulate gene expression during nodule formation and nitrogen fixation. During nitrogen‐fixation, more miRNAs, circRNAs, and mRNAs were predicted to be interacting or in competition with each other compared to during nodule formation (Wu et al. 2020). Understanding ceRNA interactions across different biological systems provides insight into the complexity of gene expression regulation. However, none of these ceRNA networks have been functionally verified.

Our study identified the circRNA landscape of L. japonicus under nutrient stress and symbiotic interaction with rhizobia. A key novelty of our work is the identification of circRNAs originating from select CSP pathway genes during rhizobial symbiosis and the overrepresentation of circRNAs in symbiosis‐associated pathway genes. A broader set of circRNAs was also associated with nutrient signaling. We developed a novel approach to map miRNA to potential new miRNA recognition elements (MREs) generated by the circRNA BSJ, constructing hypothetical competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) networks. By analyzing differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and employing multiple circRNA detection methods, we further demonstrated the impact of nutrient supply and symbiosis on the L. japonicus circRNA landscape. Lastly, we validated key circRNAs, including those from CCaMK, and observed treatment‐specific expression patterns. Our deep sequencing and use of random primers for cDNA synthesis provided simultaneous transcriptomes from lotus and rhizobia, which allows a snapshot of coordinated responses between both organisms.

Materials and Methods

Plant and Inoculant Material

L. japonicus (ecotype Gifu B‐129) seeds were scarified, surface‐sterilized in 7% bleach (10 min), rinsed five times in sterile water, and imbibed overnight at 4°C in the dark. Seeds were stratified on moist sterile filter paper for 3 days at 22°C–23°C in the dark, then transferred to a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle (50–120 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) for 7 days.

Seedlings were grown on vertical plates with ½ MS (high‐nutrient; Hn) or custom low‐nutrient (Ln) MS media (Phytotech Labs) containing 1.4% agar. The Hn media contained 30 mM total nitrogen (N) and 0.625 mM phosphate (PO₄³⁻), while the Ln media contained 2 mM N and 0.0625 mM PO₄³⁻ (see Supplemental File 1 for full recipe). A 30 µm piece of nylon mesh was placed over the agar, and stainless‐steel zip ties covered in black tape with punched holes for seedlings were positioned to block light while holding 10 seedlings per plate. Roots were positioned on the nylon mesh in contact with the media. Plates were sealed with parafilm and micropore tape, wrapped in black sleeves, and incubated vertically in the growth chamber.

After 1 week, seedlings on Ln media were inoculated with Mesorhizobium loti (500 µL; OD₆₀₀ = 0.02 in Ln media). M. loti was cultured in YMB medium for 3 days at 30°C with shaking (100 rpm). Control plates received sterile media. After inoculation, plates rested flat for 1 h before being returned to growth conditions for 3 weeks. Plants were grown in four independent experimental batches. In total, 26 plates were used, with approximately equal numbers of replicates from each batch to ensure balanced representation.

Prior to harvest, plates were imaged for phenotyping. Roots were excised with sterile razor blades, and tissues from five plants per plate were pooled to generate one sample. For each sample, either the left or the right set of five plants from a 10‐plant plate was collected and placed into a tube containing zirconia/silica and metal beads, flash frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80°C.

Phenotyping

Root lengths were measured in FIJI (ImageJ) by tracing the primary root using the segmented line tool and setting the scale based on a 20 × 20 cm grid in the image. Lateral and tertiary roots were counted manually to assess root branching. Shoot height was estimated from the same grid in 0.5 cm increments. For Rhizobia‐treated plants, pink nodules were counted per plant, and averages were calculated per biological replicate. Data were analyzed in R using ANOVA followed by Tukey's HSD post hoc test when p < 0.05.

RNA Extraction and Sequencing

Root tissue from five pooled plants was homogenized twice at 30 Hz for 45 s using a tissue lyser. Total RNA was extracted using the Purelink RNA Mini Kit (Invitrogen) per the manufacturer's instructions. Each biological replicate consisted of pooled RNA from 10 plants (two tubes, combined through a single column). RNA was DNase‐treated (TURBO DNase, Invitrogen), quantified via Qubit fluorometry, and assessed for quality with the Bioanalyzer 2100; only samples with RIN ≥ 8 were sequenced.

Ribosomal RNA was depleted using the QIAseq FastSelect–rRNA Plant Kit (Qiagen). Libraries were prepared with the NEBNext® Ultra™ II Directional RNA Library Prep Kit and multiplexed with NEBNext® Dual Index Primers (NEB). Sequencing was performed at NCSU Genomic Sciences Lab on an Illumina NovaSeq S4 platform (150 bp paired‐end, 40–100 million reads/sample across two runs). Read quality was assessed with FastQC (Andrews, 2010). Trimmomatic (Bolger et al. 2014) was used to remove adapters and low‐quality reads (sliding window: 4 bp, phred ≥ 20, min length 30 bp); unpaired reads were discarded. Resulting fastq files were used for circRNA and linear RNA analysis.

RNA‐Seq Alignment and Gene Expression Analysis

Trimmed paired reads were aligned using BBSplit (Bushnell 2014), against two reference genomes: L. japonicus Gifu v1.2 assembly (Genbank: GCA_012489685.2), Mesorhizobium loti (Genbank: GCA_012913625.1). High stringency settings (minimum identity = 0.97, ambiguous reads removed) ensured accurate organism‐specific read assignments. Ribosomal RNA counts were used to verify symbiont presence in each sample. Reads assigned to each genome were quantified at the gene level using featureCounts (Liao et al. 2014) with default settings. Counts for M. loti 5S, 16S, and 23S ribosomal RNA genes were summed to confirm sample treatment identities.

Reads aligned to the L. japonicus genome were individually counted at the gene level using featureCounts with default settings. Counts for each sample were merged into a single count matrix. Genes with zero counts or low abundance across all samples (< 10 total reads) were filtered out. Differential gene expression (DGE) analysis was performed with edgeR (v4.2.1) (Robinson et al. 2010). Counts were normalized, and a group matrix without an intercept was used to allow more flexible comparisons between treatments (i.e., no pre‐defined control group). A generalized linear model (GLM) was applied, and edgeR's automated dispersion estimation was used to model biological variability in the normalized count data. Likelihood ratio tests were performed for relevant pairwise contrasts. Contrasts followed a “treatment vs control” format, where positive log fold changes indicate upregulation in the treatment relative to the control. All treatments were compared using single pairwise contrasts.

GO Term Analysis

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis for Biological Process terms was performed using the topGO (Adrian Alexa 2017) R package with the L. japonicus Gifu v1.2 GO annotations from Lotus Base. Enrichment was tested using the ‘weight01’ algorithm and Fisher's exact test via the runTest function. The background gene universe included 13,654 annotated genes. Terms with p < 0.001 were considered significant, and the top 50 were reported.

CircRNA Analysis

CircRNAs were identified using two independent pipelines: CIRI2 (v2.1.1) (Gao et al. 2018) and CLEAR (v1.0.1) (X.‐K. Ma et al. 2019). Trimmed reads were aligned to the L. japonicus Gifu v1.2 genome using BWA‐MEM (v0.7.17) with default parameters and a t‐19 alignment score cutoff for CIRI2. For CLEAR, reads were aligned with HISAT2 (v2.2.1) and analyzed using default settings to detect circRNAs and calculate circRNA:linear RNA ratios. Linear and circular reads were normalized by read depth and transcript length and quantified in fragments per billion mapped bases (FPB). The CIRCscore was computed as the FPB ratio of circRNA to its corresponding linear transcript. Outputs from both pipelines were processed with custom R scripts for filtering, clustering, and summarization.

ceRNA Networks

Raw fastq files were processed with CIRI‐Full (v2.0) pipeline (Zheng and Zhao 2018) to assemble full‐length circRNA sequences. To test for potential back‐splice generated miRNA recognition elements (bsgMREs) circRNA sequences were represented in the query both as the original full sequence from CIRI‐full and as an offset version of that sequence made by repositioning the last 40 nt to the sequence start. Both original and ‘offset’ circRNA sequences were analyzed for predicted miRNA recognition elements (MREs) using psRNATargetV2 (Dai and Zhao 2011) with L. japonicus miRNAs from miRBase (Kozomara and Griffiths‐Jones 2014). In cases where the ‘offset’ circRNA sequences were predicted to have distinct putative MREs compared to the original sequence these new MREs were termed bsgMREs. For linear transcripts, we used CDS sequences available from Lotus Base (Lotusjaponicus_Gifu_v1.2_cds) which were then analyzed for miRNA response elements using psRNATargetV2 under the same parameters.

RT‐PCR Validation and Sanger Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from 50 to 100 mg of ground L. japonicus root tissue using a modified CTAB protocol (Jordon‐Thaden et al. 2015) followed by TRIzol purification. Briefly, CTAB extraction buffer (2% (w/v) CTAB, 2% (w/v) polyvinylpyrrolidone K‐40, 25 mM EDTA, 100 mM Tris‐HCL, 1 g/L spermidine; pH 8.0) with 5% β‐mercaptoethanol was pre‐warmed at 65°C, added to the tissue, mixed by vortex, incubated at RT for 5 min, and centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 10 min. The aqueous phase underwent two sequential 1:1 chloroform:isoamyl alcohol (24:1) extractions before isopropanol precipitation at 4°C for 1 h. The RNA pellet was washed twice with 70% ethanol, resuspended in TRIzol (Ambion), and re‐extracted with chloroform:isoamyl alcohol, followed by a second isopropanol precipitation. The final pellet was washed with 70% ethanol, dried, and resuspended in 37°C nuclease‐free water. Each sample represented RNA pooled from 15 plants.

Select validations were performed with RNA treated with RNase R to digest linear RNAs. RNA (∼2 µg) was treated with 20 U RNase R (Lucigen) in a modified LiCl buffer (Xiao and Wilusz 2019) or treated to the same buffer and incubation conditions without the addition of RNase R enzyme (RNase R ‐). RNA reactions were purified with 1.8× SPRI, quantified using the Qubit High Sensitivity RNA kit (Invitrogen), and used for cDNA synthesis.

cDNA synthesis was performed using DNase‐treated total RNA and SuperScript IV Reverse Transcriptase with random hexamers (Thermo Fisher Scientific), following the manufacturer's instructions. Divergent primers were designed to span the back‐splice junction (BSJ) and maximize internal circRNA sequence coverage. PCR amplification was performed using OneTaq Hot Start 2X Master Mix (New England Biolabs) on cDNA (10% of total volume) and on 100 ng of RNase A‐treated genomic DNA (gDNA) as a control for false positives. PCR cycling followed manufacturer's guidelines, with a 30‐second extension for circRNAs. Amplicons were visualized via agarose gel electrophoresis. PCR products of expected size were gel‐excised, purified, and submitted for Sanger sequencing to confirm the BSJ. Primer sequences are listed in Supplemental File 1.

All custom analysis scripts are available at: https://github.com/SederoffLab/Lotus_And_Friend

Results

Experimental Design and Phenotypic Response

L. japonicus was grown under three conditions: High nutrient (Hn) (30 mM total N and 0.625 mM PO₄³⁻), Low nutrient (Ln) (2 mM total N and 0.0625 mM PO₄³⁻), and Ln with Mesorhizobium loti inoculation, Rhizobia (Rhiz). The nutrient regimes were originally designed for an experiment that also included inoculation with the arbuscular mycorrhizal fungus Rhizophagus irregularis, which requires low phosphate concentrations to establish symbiosis. Although the AM treatment was ultimately excluded from this study due to insufficient colonization and lower sequencing depth, the nutrient conditions were retained for the present comparisons. Analysis of the AM samples are described in Utley (2025).

After a 3‐week interaction, plants showed distinct phenotypes (Figure 1A–B). Primary root length differed significantly among treatments (one‐way ANOVA, F(2,10) = 5.78, p = 0.0215; Supplemental Figure 1A). Post hoc Tukey tests revealed that Hn roots were significantly longer than Rhiz roots (p = 0.019), whereas Ln did not differ significantly from either Hn (p = 0.338) or Rhiz (p = 0.137). In contrast, lateral root number did not differ significantly among treatments (one‐way ANOVA, F(2,10) = 0.262, p = 0.775; Supplemental Figure 1B). Shoot height varied significantly across treatments (one‐way ANOVA, F(2,10) = 159.3, p = 2.61 × 10⁻⁸; Supplemental Figure 1C), with all pairwise comparisons significant (Tukey test: Hn vs. Ln, p < 0.001; Hn vs. Rhiz, p = 5.0 × 10⁻⁷; Ln vs. Rhiz, p = 4.69 × 10⁻⁴). Rhizobia‐inoculated plants formed an average of three nodules (Supplemental Figure 1D). Nodulation significantly increased shoot height of the lotus plants, but it did not rescue the lotus phenotype to that of the Hn grown plants. This is likely due to the lower phosphate concentration in the Ln medium, but also a known effect of symbiotic nitrogen fixation (SNF) on root growth and architecture, which suggests resource competition between nodule formation and root growth (Goh et al. 2016).

Treatments Show Distinct Patterns of Differentially Expressed Linear Transcripts

We first assessed how treatment conditions impact the L. japonicus linear transcriptome. Because random primers were used to generate the sequencing libraries, the linear transcriptome not only contained mRNA, but all transcripts > 150 nt, including non‐coding transcripts. PCA revealed clear separation between high and low nutrient conditions along PC2 (23%), with Rhiz between Hn and Ln. The PCA analysis showed clear separation between Rhiz and non‐inoculated Hn, and Ln samples separated along PC1(27%) (Figure 2B). DEG analysis revealed distinct transcriptional responses between treatments (Figure 2C; Supplemental File 3). The Rhiz vs Ln comparison showed a higher number of upregulated DEGs, whereas the Hn vs Ln comparison showed a greater number of downregulated DEGs. Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of DEGs revealed biological processes affected by symbiosis (Figure 2D). In the Rhiz vs Ln comparison, enrichment of processes ‘cellular response to phosphate starvation’ and ‘cell wall modification’ is consistent with nitrogen‐fixing symbiosis. In contrast, the Hn vs Ln comparison showed enrichment in processes such as ‘trehalose metabolic process’, ‘cell wall biogenesis’, and ‘ammonium transmembrane transport’, indicating processes associated with normal plant growth.

Deep sequencing of plant rRNA‐depleted libraries constructed with random primers enabled simultaneous analysis of host and symbiont transcriptomes in our samples. The M. loti transcriptome provided insight into symbiont gene expression, though analysis and comparisons are limited due to the absence of host‐free controls or spatiotemporal treatment conditions. Across all samples, an average of 3.38 × 108 ± 2.40 × 107 reads mapped to L. japonicus. In Rhiz samples, 2.24 × 107 ± 6.51 ×106 reads mapped to M. Loti, representing 5.3%–9.6% of total reads (Figure 2A). In contrast, Hn and Ln samples contained an order of magnitude fewer M. loti reads (mean 2.24 × 105 reads per sample; 0.009%–0.17% of total reads per sample). Those low numbers of reads in Hn and Ln samples are below the mapping accuracy of BBsplit, indicating that rhizobial‐specific transcripts were only present in inoculated samples and no contamination occurred. Most of the genes required for symbiosis in M. loti (strain R7A) are contained in a 502 kb “symbiosis island”, which contains 414 genes, including all the genes required for Nod factor synthesis (Nod), nitrogen fixation (Fix, Nif), regulatory genes, transport genes, and an array of metabolic genes (Sullivan et al. 2001). The nodulation efficiency gene (NfeD) encodes a stomatin‐like protein responsible for the bacterium's nodulation competitiveness (Green et al. 2009; Singh et al. 2021; Thomas and Rahman 2020). We found expression of multiple Nod genes in the M. loti inoculated samples (Supplemental Figures 2A and 3A).

Once symbiosis is established, genes required for N2‐fixation are expressed. The Nif operon is regulated by the NifA‐RpoN‐FixV cascade, with NifA2 being essential for symbiotic nitrogen fixation in L. japonicus. We found expression of both NifA1 and NifA2 (Supplemental Figures 2B and 3B), suggesting active nitrogen fixation pathway in our treatments. In addition to Nif genes, we observed expression of multiple Fix genes, essential for nitrogen fixation in bacteroids (Supplemental Figures 2C and 3C). Expression of genes encoding flagella (FLx, MOT) and pili structures (CPA) were found in both, bacteroids and free‐living bacteria, but are quantitatively higher in free‐living bacteria and during early stage bacteroid development (Roux et al. 2014; Tatsukami et al. 2013; Uchiumi et al. 2004) (Supplemental Figures 2D and 3D). Expression data for all M. loti genes are available in Supplemental File 4.

L. japonicus circRNA Landscape From Two Bioinformatics Pipelines

We identified circRNAs using two bioinformatic tools, CIRI2 and CircExplorer3 (CLEAR) to compensate for the high rate of false‐positive discoveries and differences in the BSJ discovery algorithms (Budnick et al. 2024; Nielsen et al. 2022). Important differences between these programs are that CLEAR identifies circRNAs only within annotated genes (exons and introns) and has no cutoff for the detection of BSJ reads, while CIRI2 can identify circRNAs across the entire genome (exons, introns, and intergenic regions) and requires > 1 read to report a BSJ. To account for the differences in BSJ assignment, circRNAs with similar BSJs (Manhattan distance ≤ 10 across start and end coordinates) were clustered. A total of 11,923 unique nuclear circRNAs from 5,290 unique cognate gene IDs were predicted using the combined data set (Supplemental File 5). CIRI2 and CLEAR detected 3,796 and 9,960 (7,009 unique circRNAs with a BSJ read number > 1) unique circRNAs, respectively. Of the 3,796 and 9,960 unique circRNAs found in CIRI2 and CLEAR, only 1,833 are detected by both (Supplemental Figure 4A). CIRI2 detected circRNA originating from 2,048 distinct gene IDs, of which 450 gene IDs were identified exclusively by CIRI2. CLEAR detected circRNA originating from 4,840 unique gene IDs, of which 3,242 gene IDs were exclusively identified by CLEAR (Supplemental Figure 4B). The majority of circRNAs identified had between 1 and 19 BSJ reads, with 137 circRNAs having more than 81 BSJ reads (Supplemental Figure 4C). The average length of identified circRNAs was 1.6 kb (Supplemental Figure 4D). Populations of circRNAs from both CIRI2 and CLEAR had roughly 1/4th of circRNAs originating from intronic or intergenic regions (Supplemental Figure 4E). Due to our focus on coding RNAs, we have excluded intergenic circRNAs from all downstream analyses. However, we recognized the growing importance and relevance of lncRNA and analyzed if there was a difference in the amount of unique intergenic circRNAs being detected between the treatments (Supplemental Figure 4F). A very small number of circRNAs were identified as organellar (chloroplast and mitochondrial) which will also be excluded from our analysis from henceforth but are included in Supplemental File 5 (Supplemental Figure 4G).

Rhizobia Treatment Produces Specific circRNAs

To explore the qualitative and quantitative distribution of nuclear‐encoded genic circRNAs across treatments, we analyzed circRNAs identified by both CLEAR and CIRI2. Among the three treatments, Hn had the lowest number of circRNAs, while Ln and Rhiz showed higher circRNA abundance (Supplemental Figure 5A). However, when considering the average number of unique circRNAs from each treatment, rhizobia inoculated plants showed the highest average circRNA counts, while Ln had the lowest (Supplemental Figure 5B). A similar pattern was observed for parent gene IDs (Supplemental Figure 5C). We also identified circRNAs that were specific to Rhizobia inoculated samples (3,872) and identified 1,353 circRNAs that are shared among all treatments (Supplemental Figure 5D; Supplemental File 5).

Focusing on circRNAs that are present in at least three replicates of a treatment, we found 411 in Hn, 714 in Ln, and 882 in Rhiz (Figure 3). Comparing these circRNAs that are present in at least three replicates resulted in 284 shared among all treatments, while others were treatment specific (Figure 3). Notably, there were 58 circRNAs specific to Rhiz, 29 circRNAs specific to Ln, and only two circRNAs specific to Hn (Supplemental File 5). To further highlight potentially relevant circRNAs, we examined treatment‐specific circRNAs identified among those consistently detected in at least three biological replicates (Supplemental Table 1; Supplemental File 5). In the 58 circRNAs specific to the Rhiz treatment, a circRNA derived from LotjaGi1g1v0118200 (LjG1.1_chr6:36811083‐36811826), encoding Asparagine Synthetase, consistent with the role of nitrogen assimilation during rhizobial symbiosis. Another Rhiz‐specific circRNA originated from LotjaGi5g1v0169900, annotated as Type I inositol‐1,4,5‐trisphosphate 5‐phosphatase, stood out due to its high expression and potential involvement in phosphate signaling, a pathway known to influence rhizobial symbiosis (Riemer et al. 2022).

CircRNAs Are Overrepresented in Symbiosis‐Associated Genes

To explore transcriptional regulation within symbiotic signaling, we analyzed differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between Rhiz vs Ln and Hn vs Ln treatments. Several genes, including CLE‐RS2 and CLE‐RS3 exhibited dramatic upregulation in the Rhiz vs Ln comparison (logFC ~6), consistent with their roles in nodulation signaling (Supplemental Figure 6; Supplemental File 6). These same genes showed comparatively lower expression in the Hn vs Ln comparison. A similar expression pattern was observed for NIN and TML, genes known to function in nodule organogenesis and autoregulation of nodulation, respectively (Soyano et al. 2014; Tsikou et al. 2018) (Supplemental Figure 6). Together, these patterns indicate that rhizobial inoculation activates symbiotic signaling pathways beyond those induced by differences in nutrient status between the Hn and Ln treatments.

To explore post‐transcriptional regulation in symbiosis, we identified circRNAs associated with symbiosis genes. From literature, genes functionally identified to be involved in the regulation of symbiosis, infection thread development, and organogenesis, we compiled a list of 61 genes we call “symbiosis‐associated” genes (Madsen et al. 2010; Murakami et al. 2018). The list is provided in Supplemental File 6. We detected circRNAs from 37 of the 61 known/predicted symbiosis‐associated genes, highlighting their potential regulatory role in symbiosis (Figure 4; Supplemental Figure 7; Supplemental File 6). More circRNAs are expressed from this set of symbiosis‐associated genes than from randomly sampled sets of the same size, with 101 unique circRNAs expressed from the 61 symbiosis‐associated genes compared to an average of 25 unique circRNAs expressed from randomly sampled gene sets (Supplemental Figure 8). Together these results show symbiosis‐associated genes have an overrepresentation of circRNAs.

In the early perception stage, one circRNA was found in the Hn treatment associated with Nod factor/LCO receptors. In the infection thread pathway (Madsen et al. 2010), two circRNAs were identified for Pir1 (actin rearrangement): one specific to Rhiz, and one shared between Rhiz and Hn. Three circRNAs were also detected for Cerberus, all in Ln. In the CSP pathway, SymRK had six circRNAs: two specific to Hn, two specific to Ln, one specific to Rhiz, and one other present in Rhiz and Hn treatments. Pollux had one Rhiz‐specific circRNA. CCaMK had four circRNAs, with two being treatment‐specific to Rhiz, one treatment‐specific to Ln, and one present in both, Hn and Ln treatments. Cyclops had one circRNA shared between Ln and Rhiz. Continuing with the organogenesis pathway in the establishment of nodules (Madsen et al. 2010), Lhk1 had five circRNAs: two each specific to either Rhiz or Ln, and one in both treatments. Notably, its paralog Lhk1a had 16 circRNAs, some of them specific to a treatment, while others are present in two or all three treatments. Identification of these differences in circRNA expression from symbiosis genes suggests that there may be an additional transcriptional regulatory layer within the symbiotic signaling network during symbiotic interactions.

We compared those CSP and symbiosis associated genes generating circRNAs in Lotus to those identified in Phaseolus vulgaris by Wu et al. (2020), and showed that SymRK, Pollux, Cyclops, LHK1, Cerberus, and PIR1 from both species generate circRNAs during nodulation (Table in Figure 4). This suggests a potential conservation of circRNA in the regulation of symbiosis‐associated genes during nodulation.

Select circRNAs Were Validated With PCR

Validation is essential after the identification of circRNAs via different bioinformatic tools because of the use of different aligners and chance of false positives. RT‐PCR using divergent primers was used to validate putative potential circRNAs. Unlike typical convergent primers used to amplify linear cDNA fragments, divergent primers were oriented away from each other and did not amplify linear RNA but instead captured the BSJ in circular RNA and/or the internal sequence (Figure 5A). Select circRNAs were validated with RT‐PCR starting from RNA that had been RNase R treated to digest linear RNAs. As a control, gDNA was used to ensure there was no tandem duplication in the genome.

We attempted to validate circRNAs from four different genes and were successful in validating at least one circRNA for each gene (Figure 5; Supplemental Figures 9‐13). Notably, circCCR4(4,5), previously confirmed in leaf tissue (Budnick et al. 2024) and derived from Cinnamoyl‐CoA reductase 4 (CCR4; LotjaGi1g1v0115700), was also validated in roots. Here, however, we focus on circRNAs detected from two core CSP genes: SymRK and CCaMK.

For SymRK, six circRNAs were detected: two circRNAs exclusive to Hn, one circRNA detected in Hn and Rhiz, two exclusive to Ln, and one exclusive to Rhiz (Figure 5B). We attempted to validate the circRNA (LjG1.1_chr2:80616495‐80617260) which was exclusive to the rhizobia treatment with divergent RT‐PCR using RNA from the Rhiz treatment. However, an identical band appeared in the gDNA‐template divergent PCR, indicating a false‐positive circRNA (Supplemental Figure 10, 12, 13). Despite a slight mismatch in the apparent BSJ between the cDNA and gDNA, the extensive sequence overlap led us to classify this circRNA as a false‐positive. We validated a circRNA, circSymRK(1 s) (LjG1.1_chr2:80617651‐80618162), which was exclusive to the Ln treatment with RNase R followed by RT‐PCR (Figure 5B).

Four unique circRNAs were identified for CCaMK from deep sequencing: two specific to Rhiz, one specific to Ln, and one occurring in both Hn and Ln treatments (Figure 5C). Divergent RT‐PCR using RNA isolated from Rhiz treatment plants validated circCCaMK(3,4,5,6) and circCCaMK(4,5) originally identified from NGS data. It also provided additional circRNAs circCCaMK(s3,4,5 s), circCCaMK(s3b,4,5 s), and circCCaMK(3,4 L) which were not present in the NGS dataset (Figure 5C, Supplemental Figure 11). We also performed divergent RT‐PCR using RNA from Hn, and Ln treatments alongside Rhiz treatment RNA which validated circCCaMK(3,4,5) in Hn, Ln, and Rhiz treatments, circCCaMK(3,4) in Rhiz only, and provided the additional circRNA circCCaMK(s3a,4,5 sa) (Hn), and showed circCCaMK(s3b,4,5s) presence in Ln (Figure 5C, Supplemental Fig. 11. This validation demonstrates that expression of circRNAs from the CCaMK locus is at least qualitatively different across treatment conditions.

MicroRNA Recognition Elements on circRNAs Form Competing Endogenous RNA Networks

MiRNAs can bind to circRNAs, competing with linear mRNA targets and potentially reducing mRNA degradation or enhancing translation (Figure 6A) (Salmena et al. 2011). To identify putative miRNA recognition elements (MREs) on circRNAs, we reconstructed full‐length circRNA sequences and analyzed them with psRNATargetV2 (Dai and Zhao 2011) using L. japonicus miRNAs from miRBase (Kozomara and Griffiths‐Jones 2014) (see Methods for details).

We identified 2,191 unique circRNAs with putative MREs to 362 of the 365 annotated L. japonicus miRNAs (Supplemental File 7). The majority of the 8,407 circRNAs with sequences from CIRI‐vis had no putative MREs (6,111, 73%) or only a single predicted MRE (1,632, 19%), with only 664 L. japonicus circRNAs containing more than 1 putative MRE (Supplemental Figure 14). Comparing across treatments, we found 46 circRNAs with putative MREs that were expressed in at least three samples of Ln, Rhiz, and Hn treatments and considered them consistently expressed (Supplemental Figure 15A; Supplemental Table 2).

To identify treatment‐specific circRNAs with putative MREs, we compared those present in at least three Hn, Ln, or Rhiz samples against circRNAs present in other treatments. We identified 1 Hn, 5 Ln, and 7 Rhiz‐specific circRNAs with MREs (Supplemental Figure 15B‐D; Supplemental Table 3).

Theoretically, the BSJ could generate novel MREs, backsplice‐generated MREs (bsgMREs), that are not present in cognate linear mRNAs (Figure 6A). We found 286 circRNAs containing putative bsgMREs for 213 unique miRNAs in L. japonicus. Five bsgMRE circRNAs were found in at least three replicates of all three treatments. Only one bsgMRE containing circRNA was consistently expressed in three replicates and specific to Rhizobia treatment samples. Treatment‐specific MREs are available in Supplemental Table 4.

We also looked for bsgMREs in circRNAs of other plant species to identify putative conservation. We used available data of 6,519 circRNAs from Oryza sativa and 4,663 circRNAs from Arabidopsis thaliana (Chu et al. 2022) as well as the microRNAs available for these species from miRBase (Supplemental Figure 16A). We identified 444 circRNAs with bsgMREs for 405 miRNAs in O. sativa and 274 circRNAs with bsgMREs for 223 miRNAs in A. thaliana. CircRNAs from all 3 species showed similar portions of having bsgMREs, any MRE, or no MRE though L. japonicus had a larger portion of circRNAs with no MRE (Supplemental Figure 16B). There were 12 miRNA families in common across all three species (by miRBase name) and 9 of these putatively targeted bsgMREs in all three species. MRE Results for Lj, At, and Os circs are available in Supplemental File 7. These results point towards the potential for bsgMREs to be observed in other plant species.

An established example of a miRNA‐mediated symbiosis regulation is mir2111, which represses its target gene TOO MUCH LOVE (TML), a negative regulator of nodulation (Figure 6B) (Tsikou et al. 2018). Under low nitrogen conditions, mir2111 is upregulated and moves through the phloem to suppress TML, enabling nodulation. Conversely, when nitrogen is sufficient or rhizobia colonization is extensive, mir2111 levels drop, allowing TML to limit further nodulation (Tsikou et al. 2018). The role of mir2111 highlights how miRNAs can act as systemic regulators in symbiosis. Beyond individual interactions, the competing endogenous RNA (ceRNA) hypothesis suggests that different RNA molecules, including mRNAs, long non‐coding RNAs, and circRNAs, can regulate each other by competing for shared miRNAs (Salmena et al. 2011).

To understand potential involvement of circRNAs in symbiosis‐relevant ceRNA networks, we constructed a hypothetical network linking circRNA:miRNA:DEG predictions (Figure 6C). For the creation of the subnetwork four nodes were highlighted based on genes potentially involved in symbiosis: repressor of nodulation, TML, a circRNA from VAG1 (LotjaGi2g1v0179500_LC), a gene important to endoreduplication in growing nodules (Suzaki et al. 2014); a circRNA from CPS (LotjaGi1g1v0228700), a gene involved in gibberellin biosynthesis (Maekawa et al. 2009); and a circRNA from FEN1 (LotjaGi1g1v0613300), a homocitrate synthase gene necessary for symbiotic nitrogen fixation (Hakoyama et al. 2009). Nodes within two connections of these were included. The predicted circRNA:miRNA:DEG network shows how much competition for the miRNA binding to circRNA versus mRNA could be present at the post‐transcriptional level, which may play a role in symbiosis.

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the circRNA landscape and transcriptome of L. japonicus (Gifu) under symbiotic conditions with M. loti (Rhiz), alongside nutrient treatments with high or low N and P in the media. Analysis of the linear transcriptomes revealed gene expression patterns of our host as well as rhizobia. We focused on the identification of circRNAs, particularly those associated with symbiosis, and we explored interactions of identified circRNAs, linear DEGs, and predicted miRNAs in hypothetical ceRNA networks. Additionally, we show that BSJ could potentially generate novel miRNA recognition elements (bsgMREs) to compete with a unique set of miRNA interactions beyond those of their linear transcripts. Lastly, we validated several circRNAs originating from symbiosis and metabolism‐ related genes.

Circular RNAs are only distinguishable from their linear counterparts by their back‐splice junctions (BSJs), making accurate detection technically challenging. Genetic features like repetitive sequences, genomic rearrangements, experimental variability, and technical artifacts during extraction, reverse transcription, sequencing, and bioinformatic analysis can affect the accurate detection of the BSJ, leading to false positives (Budnick et al. 2024; Hansen et al. 2016; Utley et al. 2025).

In our study, we employed both CIRI2 (Gao et al. 2018) and CLEAR (X.‐K. Ma et al. 2019), which are known for their high precision and relatively high sensitivity (Vromman et al. 2023). This multi‐bioinformatic pipeline strategy aligns with best practices in the field and reduces the likelihood of false positives (Budnick et al. 2024; Hansen et al. 2016; Utley et al. 2025).

Beyond computational work, experimental validation is essential for determining true circRNAs and their function. In this study, we validated 14 circRNAs arising from genes of interest, including those involved in the Common Symbiosis Pathway (CSP) (Figure 5, Supplemental Figures 8‐12). For example, the Rhiz‐specific circCCaMK(3,4) could play a role in symbiotic regulation. While a putative circRNA from SymRK was determined to be a false‐positive, another SymRK circRNA specific to Low nutrient treatment was confirmed as a true circRNA (Figure 5B). These results emphasize the importance of experimental validation to identify true circRNAs.

Few plant studies have investigated circRNAs and their role in nodulation. In Phaseolus vulgaris, 3,448 circRNAs were expressed during nodulation, with the highest numbers in the nitrogen‐fixing stage (Wu et al. 2020). In Medicago truncatula, 19 circRNAs were identified under severe drought stress in the presence of rhizobia, pointing to circRNAs potential role in coordinating and regulating abiotic and biotic signaling (Jing et al. 2022).

Our study builds on this work by focusing on circRNA originating from CSP and symbiosis‐associated genes. Comparisons of circRNA‐generating symbiosis‐associated and CSP genes in nodulated roots from Lotus with those from the work in Phaseolus vulgaris (Wu et al. 2020) identified multiple CSP/symbiosis‐associated genes which express circular RNAs in both species, indicating potential conservation of circRNA during nodulation in this pathway (Figure 4). We also demonstrated that circRNAs are overrepresented in symbiosis‐associated genes, suggesting another link between circRNAs and symbiotic gene expression.

While it has been functionally confirmed in plant and animal studies that circRNAs can bind miRNAs and thereby modify the expression of their respective genes of origin (Guria et al. 2020; Kefas et al. 2008; Fan et al. 2020), all competing endogenous RNA models, including ours, are so far hypothetical and only based on bioinformatics and statistical correlation. Other possible functions of circRNAs are still ignored in the creation of these networks. However, these ceRNA networks provide a hypothesis for future studies. While previous studies explore hypothetical ceRNA networks in the context of symbiosis, we expand on this concept by incorporating the potential backsplice‐generated miRNA recognition elements (bsgMREs), which could display novel recognition sites created by the back‐splice junction that are absent from linear transcripts. This novel concept of bsgMREs as potential regulatory sites that can affect transcript stability or translation of genes other than the gene the circRNA originated from. If this is a general potential feature of some circRNAs, it would be expected to be conserved across species. We found that published full circRNA sequences in Oryza sativa and Arabidopsis thaliana also contained putative bsgMREs for miRNA sequences of their respective species. If future validation of this concept confirms bsgMRE functions, this could show that circRNAs have broader potential regulatory relationships than is currently understood.

We placed particular focus on the TML‐mir2111 interaction, a well‐characterized component of the Autoregulation of Nodulation (AON) pathway in L. japonicus, which regulates nodulation through long‐distance signaling (Okuma and Kawaguchi 2021; Tsikou et al. 2018). While we did not identify circRNAs from AON pathway genes, we were able to predict interactions between predicted miRNAs, circRNAs and DEGs in Rhiz vs Ln treatments (Figure 6C).

Moreover, the mobility of mir2111, known to travel from shoot to root to regulate nodulation via TML, raises the possibility that circRNAs could also be mobile. Especially due to the presence of circRNAs in exosomes (Hansen et al. 2013), circRNAs may be able to function beyond their tissue of origin, making implications for function in systemic or even plant‐microbe communication. Additional evidence suggests that circRNAs participate in transcriptional regulation via R‐loop (RNA:DNA hybrid) formation (Conn et al. 2017) or be translated into peptides (Pan et al. 2025).

Future studies will focus on validating circRNAs from key CSP and symbiosis‐associated genes to confirm the regulatory role in symbiosis and nutrient stress. High‐throughput approaches, such as the circPanel method (Budnick et al. 2024; Rahimi et al. 2021) could streamline this process. Integration of deep circular, linear, and miRNA sequencing, normalized with spike‐ins (Laosuntisuk et al. 2024), would provide a more accurate representation of ceRNA networks. Many of the mechanistic functions of circRNA depend on their subcellular localization. Nuclear localized circRNAs can be involved in R‐loop formations, while cytoplasmic localization of circRNA is necessary for miRNA binding or translational regulation. Experimental proof of localization would therefore support potential actions of circRNA in gene regulation (W. Wang et al. 2011). Sampling across nodulation stages and using symbiotic mutants such as ccamk (Gleason et al. 2006), snf (Tirichine et al. 2006), and others, could further clarify the temporal and genetic regulation of circRNAs. Such work would build on our findings and deepen understanding of post‐transcriptional regulation in L. japonicus symbiosis.
