Performance of porous carbons based on waste-printed paper on the CO2 capture by temperature swing adsorption
Authors: Yanier Sánchez-Hechavarría, Jorge Arce-Castro, João Pedro Santos Alves, Tamille Alves Souza, Mauricio Brandão dos Santos, Fernanda Teixeira Cruz, Raildo Alves Fiuza Junior, Karen Valverde Pontes, Artur José Santos Mascarenhas
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of low-cost and sustainable adsorbents is crucial for reducing CO2 emissions associated with climate change. In this context, waste-derived porous carbons have emerged as promising materials for adsorption applications. This work evaluates the CO2 adsorption performance of porous carbons derived from printed paper waste using temperature swing adsorption (TSA). The activated carbons were produced by pretreating printed paper waste, followed by impregnation with KOH, K2CO3, or KHC8H4O4 and chemical activation via pyrolysis at 800 °C. Adsorption tests were conducted using TSA, assessing adsorption capacity as a function of temperature. The results showed that the maximum CO2 adsorption capacity at 30 °C for AC-KOH, AC-KCO, and AC-KHP was 7.64%, 6.28%, and 6.80%, respectively. In adsorption tests at 30 °C at 1 bar, AC-KOH (83.33% micropore volume) demonstrated the best performance with a maximum CO2 adsorption efficiency of 7.64% (2.29 mmol g−1). The average fluctuation after 10 adsorption–desorption cycles was less than 1%, indicating good regeneration and stability of AC-KOH. These results suggest that printed paper is a promising precursor for producing adsorbents for CO2 adsorption.

Introduction

In urban solid waste, paper of different types constitutes one of the most significant fractions. Used paper is an essential component of this waste, with an annual production exceeding 300 million tons worldwide (Putro et al. 2019). The complexity of recycled paper lies in its composition, which involves cellulose fibers (containing polar functional groups such as alcohol and ether) and inorganic fillers, such as kaolin, clay, and calcium carbonate (Mabalane et al. 2024).

Traditionally recycled for the production of new paper, paper has gained new applications in the last decade (Zhang and Zhu 2021), serving as raw material for cellulose derivatives and bioethanol. The production of recycled fibers from paper is mostly carried out through the conventional process of removing ink by chemical flotation, a well-established and extensively documented method. In parallel, alternative approaches such as the use of enzymes, ultrasound, and adsorbents show potential, although their large-scale implementation in paper recycling units is still incipient. The effectiveness of these different methods is directly related to the complex interaction between the characteristics of the paper and the printing inks used (Vukoje and Rožić 2018; Inkoua et al. 2022).

Aiming at the mitigation of CO2 emissions, the application of activated carbons from plant biomass in capture and adsorption processes is an area of growing interest. In this scenario, the valorization of paper and cardboard waste for the production of low-cost and highly selective activated carbons for CO2 presents itself as a promising solution for sustainable waste management and in line with the principles of the circular economy.

Chemical activation is fundamental to enhance the properties and adsorption capacities of typically carbonaceous materials. In this process, the adsorbent reacts with various agents, such as oxidants, acids, bases, inorganic salts, and organic solvents. However, given the significant environmental pollution generated, the use of environmentally friendly and safe solvents for living organisms becomes imperative (Nguyen et al. 2021; Wei et al. 2024). Chemical activation consists of a thermochemical method that transforms carbonaceous material into activated carbon, condensable volatiles (called bio-oil when the raw material is biomass), and fuel gases (Inkoua et al. 2022).

Recent reviews and studies have shown that chemical activation using agents such as KOH, K2CO3, and ZnCl2 can effectively tailor the microporosity and surface chemistry of activated carbons, resulting in high CO2 adsorption performance at low pressures. These methods can produce materials with well-developed narrow micropores and high surface areas suitable for CO2 capture applications and are compatible with scalable production processes, while physical activation with CO2 may require harsher conditions and often yields broader pore size distributions that are less optimal at near-ambient conditions (Cordeiro et al. 2024; Zentou et al. 2025; Dayi et al. 2025; Kundu et al. 2024).

In the chemical activation of porous carbons from paper and cardboard waste, agents such as H3PO4 (Adinaveen et al. 2016; Nowrouzi et al. 2018; Xu et al. 2014), K2CO3 (Ma et al. 2018; Kim et al. 2020; Liu and Huang 2018), ZnCl2 (Chang et al. 2019), and K2C2O4 (Sevilla et al. 2018; Rehman et al. 2022) are used, with KOH being the most prevalent (Tseng and Tseng 2005; Chang et al. 2019; Liu et al. 2018; Durairaj et al. 2019; Nahar et al. 2023; Jinyang et al. 2024). KOH induces internal corrosion in the carbon, forming micropores and increasing the surface area as a function of the KOH/carbon ratio. The formation of micro- and mesopores during activation results from the simultaneity of surface pyrolysis and internal corrosion, processes driven by cracking and gasification reactions that remove significant amounts of oxygen, hydrogen, and carbon (Tseng and Tseng 2005).

Despite the prevalence of KOH, a study by Tay et al. (2009) revealed that K2CO3 provided higher yields in the production of activated carbon from the pyrolysis of soybean oil cake. Furthermore, under the same experimental conditions, the activated carbon derived from K2CO3 exhibited a higher specific surface area than that produced with KOH (Heidarinejad et al. 2020). Other benefits pointed out by Tay et al. (2009) are the lower ash and sulfur content in the activated carbon obtained with K2CO3 compared to that produced with KOH. Foo and Hameed (2012) also found that the adsorptive capacity of activated carbon from orange peel increased with the increase in the proportion of K2CO3 used. Additionally, Adinata et al. (2007) demonstrated that the use of K2CO3 prevented the formation of tar and other liquid byproducts, such as acetic acid and methanol, during the activation process.

In temperature swing adsorption processes for CO2, the search for sustainable carbonaceous materials has directed attention towards biomass waste. Surprisingly, the potential of porous adsorbent carbons produced from paper has not yet been widely investigated in these applications. The studies by Vazhayal et al. (2020) and Jinyang et al. (2024) explored the use of paper and cardboard waste as CO2 adsorbents.

Vazhayal et al. (2020) developed a microporous carbon from silk paper waste and polyvinyl alcohol, using a combination of freeze-drying and carbonization. The resulting aerogels exhibited a highly interconnected three-dimensional porous structure, low density (0.057 g cm−3), high porosity (~ 97%), and significant specific surface area (1384 m2 g−1), with a predominance of ultramicropores. These characteristics conferred great potential for CO2 adsorption (5.14 mmol g−1 at 0 °C) to the material, demonstrating good selectivity and regeneration capacity, without the need for chemical activation, functionalization, or heteroatom doping.

Jinyang et al. (2024) produced hierarchical porous carbon aerogels from various paper wastes, aiming for their application in CO2 adsorption. Activation with KOH was performed in a single step, optimizing energy consumption compared to two-step methods. The aerogels derived from printing paper, napkins, and corrugated paper, pyrolyzed at 700, 800, and 900 °C, exhibited specific surface areas between 958 and 1370 m2 g−1, with a micropore contribution to the surface area ranging from 89.8% to 94.3%. The maximum CO2 adsorption capacity at 25 °C and 1 bar for the activated carbons obtained at 800 °C from different paper types ranged between 143 mg g−1 (3.25 mmol g−1) and 151 mg g−1 (3.43 mmol g−1), with a CO2/N2 selectivity of 8 to 11.

In other studies, in which commercial activated carbon was impregnated with NaOH, the material exhibited an average specific surface area of approximately 1022 m2 g−1, a cumulative pore volume of 0.4123 cm3 g−1, and an average pore width of 1.543 nm (Nwaisr et al. 2024). The adsorption capacity of this activated carbon for a gas mixture containing 10 mol% CO2 in N2O reached 290.62 mg g−1, which is approximately twice that of the commercial activated carbon prior to impregnation, corresponding to an increase of about 150% relative to the raw material, thereby demonstrating the effectiveness of the impregnation process in enhancing adsorptive performance.

In this study, activated carbons were synthesized from printed paper waste using chemical activation with KOH (potassium hydroxide), K2CO3 (potassium carbonate), or KHC8H4O4 (potassium hydrogen phthalate, KHP) as agents. The resulting materials were characterized, and the CO2 adsorption capacity by temperature swing adsorption and the selective capture of CO2 in a N2/CO2 flow were evaluated. Additionally, the effect of activating agents on the structure of the activated carbons was investigated, contributing to the knowledge about the synthesis and application of sustainable carbonaceous adsorbents in CO2 capture.

Materials and methods

Preparation of activated carbons

The raw material used in this study consisted of postconsumer office paper (A4, 80 g m−2). This specific waste stream was selected for its high cellulose content (approx. 60%–70%) and relatively low lignin content compared to newsprint or cardboard. The printed paper waste was previously cut and ground using a knife mill and subsequently sieved through an 80-mesh sieve.

The production of activated carbons started with a three-stage pretreatment of waste-printed paper (W-PP). In the first stage, the shredded cellulosic material was submerged in a hydroalcoholic solution (50% v/v ethanol) at 60 °C, under magnetic stirring for 120 min, aiming to obtain a homogeneous suspension. This suspension was then subjected to centrifugation to separate the solid and liquid phases. The second stage consisted of immersing the resulting solid material in deionized water at 80 °C, with magnetic stirring for 120 min. The third stage involved washing the cellulosic pulp with deionized water at room temperature, followed by vacuum filtration to remove excess liquid. After the pretreatment, the wet pulp was chemically impregnated using an aqueous solution of the activating agents KOH, K2CO3, or KHC8H4O4 in a mass ratio of 1:1 (activator/precursor). This mass ratio was defined based on the suggestion of Gurten et al. (2012) for activated carbons prepared using waste tea leaves and K2CO3. For each activation process, 30 g of paper pulp and 30 g of the activating agent were weighed, corresponding to a 1:1 mass ratio, and dispersed with 38.6 g of deionized water. The subsequent evaporation of water and drying of the impregnated material in an oven at 100 °C for 12 h completed the activation step.

The carbonization step of the three impregnated materials was conducted in a tubular furnace at 800 °C under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 150 mL min−1 and a controlled heating rate of 10 °C min−1. The selection of the carbonization temperature for the paper pulp obtained from treated paper waste followed the recommendations reported by Liu et al. (2018), Ma et al. (2018), Sundriyal et al. (2021), and Son et al. (2024). These studies employed a carbonization temperature of 800 °C and reported excellent results in terms of structural characteristics and adsorption properties of the resulting activated carbons. In addition, the thermogravimetric analysis of the paper waste was considered, showing that at 800 °C the thermogravimetric profile reaches a plateau, which indicates the completion of the carbonization process and the effective removal of unstable volatile matter.

The resulting activated carbons were subjected to a washing process with a 0.1 mol L−1 aqueous HCl solution at 80 °C, aiming at neutralization and removal of residual inorganic species, followed by successive washes with deionized water at room temperature until a neutral effluent (pH = 7.0) was obtained. The final drying of the materials was carried out in an oven at 110 °C for a period of 12 h. The resulting activated carbons were ground and sieved to a particle size range of 100–200 mesh (75–150 μm).

The activated carbons obtained through the described methodology were identified as AC-KOH (KOH), AC-KCO (K2CO3), and AC-KHP (KHC8H4O4). Additionally, a reference carbon (RC-PP) was prepared by carbonizing waste printed paper not subjected to chemical activation, using the same carbonization conditions applied to the activated carbons.

Characterization

The morphology of the precursor materials, the reference carbon, and the activated carbons were studied using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) with a Hitachi S-3400N low-vacuum scanning electron microscope. Samples were dispersed on an aluminum sample holder using carbon tape. Images were collected without previous metallization.

The chemical composition of printed paper waste was determined using an S8 Tiger (Bruker) X-ray fluorescence (XRF) spectrometer, operating in a vacuum atmosphere, a rhodium (Rh) source, a Si(Li) semiconductor detector, and a 10-mm collimator. Measurements were performed using tube voltages of 50 kV for elements Ti–U and 15 kV for Na–Sc, with maximum operating energies of 40 and 4.4 keV, respectively. Analyses were done using pellets of the printed paper supported on boric acid pressed at 5.0 N, and a semiquantitative method (internal calibration) was used to estimate the elements’ concentration.

Fourier transformed infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy was performed at room temperature (25 °C) using a Perkin–Elmer Spectrum 400 spectrometer to characterize the chemical composition of the synthesized carbons, providing information about different chemical bonds and the detection of functional groups. Spectra were collected using 0.1% KBr pellets. The thermal degradation of the precursor material and the synthesized carbons was studied using thermogravimetry (TGA) under a nitrogen and synthetic air flow rate of 50 mL min−1 with a Shimadzu DTG60H thermobalance (accuracy ± 2 °C and 0.001 mg), employing a heating rate of 10 °C min−1, ranging from room temperature to 900 °C. The crystalline structure of the materials was analyzed using X-ray diffraction patterns collected on a Shimadzu model XRD-6000 diffractometer, with a graphite monochromator, operating with CuKα (λ = 0.15406 nm) radiation generated at 40.0 kV and 30.0 mA, ranging from 10 to 80° 2θ at a scan rate of 2° min−1.

Raman spectra were collected in the range of 400–3000 cm−1 with an XY Raman Spectrometer (Jasco 5100), using a 2.8 mW argon laser (532 nm, Spectra Physics), and the analyzed region was directly visualized through a 20× microscope objective. The Raman light was collected using a backscattering configuration. The incident light was consistently polarized perpendicularly to the plane of incidence to increase the intensity of the scattered signal, with the data acquired in reflection mode. Powder samples were placed on a pressed slide directly on the microscope stage, with the specific area for spectral recording selected via optical alignment. Three Raman spectra were recorded for each sample.

The N2 physisorption isotherm was collected at −196°C using an equipment Micromeritics ASAP 2020. Regarding sample preparation, the degassing conditions were meticulously controlled at a temperature of 200 °C for 360 min under a nitrogen atmosphere, ensuring the ideal preparation of the samples for the adsorption tests. Textural properties were calculated by treating the isotherms using the BET, Dubinin-Astakhov, and the non-local density functional theory (NLDFT) methods.

CO2 adsorption and regeneration tests

The adsorption characteristics were evaluated using a Shimadzu DTG-60H thermogravimetric analyzer (TG) with a precision of ± 2 °C and 0.001 mg, for CO2 adsorption at 30 °C and atmospheric pressure (1 bar), using the experimental setup shown in Fig. 1. Sample pretreatment involved heating to 150 °C for 30 min under N2 flow (100 mL min−1) to remove gaseous impurities and moisture, followed by cooling to 30 °C for the CO2 adsorption process. Adsorption was conducted by purging with a CO2:N2 = 1:1 mixture at a flow rate of 100 mL min−1 for 20 min at 30 °C, after the system reached thermal equilibrium. Desorption was performed by switching the gas flow to N2 (100 mL min−1) maintained at 150 °C for 15 min. The best-performing adsorbent was selected to investigate the effect of cyclic capture on its adsorption capacity. The weight loss stabilizes significantly above 750 °C, reaching a constant plateau. Therefore, the thermogravimetric analysis suggests that a pyrolysis temperature of 800 °C is optimal for ensuring the complete carbonization of paper waste and the removal of residual volatile matter. This temperature ensures the formation of a thermally stable carbonaceous matrix, providing a consistent precursor for the subsequent activation stages.

The analysis of the FTIR spectrum, Fig. 5(a) reveals the presence of OH functional groups linked by hydrogen bonds in the 3500–3300 cm−1 band. The low-intensity absorption band at 2200 cm−1 can be attributed to the stretching vibration of the C≡N bond, likely associated with nitrogen-containing surface groups originating from ink residues or additives present in the printed paper precursor, which underwent transformation during the carbonization process. The bands in the 1700–1550 cm−1 and 1500–1250 cm−1 regions are associated with the angular deformation of the H–O–H bond and C–H bond from residual aliphatic groups, respectively. The band in the frequency range of 1024 cm−1, corresponding to C–C groups, is related to the stretching vibration of the carbon skeleton within aromatic or disordered aliphatic structures, commonly found in carbonaceous materials derived from the thermal decomposition of cellulose and lignin present in printed paper waste. The well-defined absorption bands observed at approximately 881 and 697 cm−1 are attributed to the in-plane and out-of-plane bending vibrations of C–H bonds in aromatic rings, respectively, characteristic of the reference carbon sample RC-PP. Additionally, the band at 449 cm−1 is associated with M–O stretching vibrations, indicating the presence of inorganic metal–oxygen species likely originating from residual inorganic components in the precursor or from the activation process. Similar results were found by Zu et al. (2013) and Pirzadeh and Ghoreyshi (2014) in studies of carbons derived from paper waste and paper pulp subjected to pretreatment.

The vibrational modes in the three samples, as studied from the Raman spectra and analyzed in the range of 300–3200 cm−1, are shown in Fig. 5(b). The peaks at 1340 and 1590 cm−1 represent the D and G bands, respectively (Liu et al. 2019; Sundriyal et al. 2021). The G band appears due to in-plane vibrations of sp2 hybridized carbons, representing the graphitic order, while the D band originates from defects and disordered graphite present in the amorphous carbon. The ID/IG ratio for the AC-KCO sample was recorded as 1.00, indicating a balanced relationship between the degree of graphitization and disorder. Meanwhile, for the AC-KOH and AC-KHP samples, the ratio was recorded as 0.97, suggesting a slightly higher degree of graphitization in relation to the disordered phase.

The 2D band, in the range of 2420–2430 cm−1, corresponds to a graphitic lattice mode with E2g symmetry (Al-Jishi et al. 1982; Dresselhaus et al. 2002; Sadezky et al. 2005). The presence of these bands is due to phonon processes that are only possible in graphitic or sheet-like carbons. Phonons are collective excitations that represent the vibrational motion of atoms within a crystal lattice and play a significant role in determining the thermal and electrical properties of solids. This phenomenon has also been observed by other authors (Chen et al. 2017; Singh et al. 2021). It is observed that the AC-KOH carbon shows a significant increase in the 2D band compared to the other carbons. This suggests a turbostratic carbon structure arrangement of non-graphitic carbon related in depth to the abundant composition of sp2 hybridized carbon in a stack of nanometer-sized graphene layers with disordered structural characteristics (Ruhaimi et al. 2024).

Nitrogen physisorption isotherms and NLDFT pore size distribution are shown in Fig. 6(a) and 6(b), respectively. As illustrated in Fig. 6(a), the activated carbons and the reference carbon exhibited Type I/IV hybrid adsorption isotherms, typical of hierarchical pore distribution, i.e., ranging from micropores to mesopores region. These isotherms are characterized by a sharp curvature towards the relative pressure axis (P/P₀), followed by a plateau indicative of saturation, a behavior typical of the Langmuir isotherm (Xu et al. 2024). It is noteworthy that, at low relative pressures (P/P₀ < 0.02), the N₂ adsorption showed a rapid increase (Li et al. 2019). Based on the data presented in Table 2, chemical activation significantly increases the BET specific surface area of activated carbons compared to the reference carbon (RC-PP). The AC-KOH sample showed a surface area of 1091 m2 g−1, which is approximately 2.5 times higher than that of RC-PP (432 m2 g−1). Similarly, the AC-KCO (1059 m2 g−1) and AC-KHP (969 m2 g−1) samples also exhibited BET-specific surface areas more than twice as large as that of the reference carbon. This increase can be attributed to the oxidation of the carbonaceous structure promoted by potassium at temperatures above 700 °C (Fiuza-Junior et al. 2016), as well as the gases released during the thermal decomposition of the activating agents. The release of these gases during carbonization promotes an increase in the BET-specific surface area and in the pore volume of the activated carbons (Xu et al. 2024). These results clearly reveal a notable variation in textural properties when compared to the reference carbon (RC-PP), thus demonstrating the activating potential of KOH, K2CO3, and KHC8H4O4 in the single-step activation process of pretreated paper waste.

A further analysis of the action of activating agents KOH, K2CO3, and KC8H4O4 based on textural properties presented in Table 2 reveals that all were highly effective in creating and expanding the micropore network in activated carbons. The substantial increase in the micropore surface area, calculated by the Dubinin–Astakhov method, evidences this effect: AC-KOH (1329 m2 g−1), AC-KCO (1310 m2 g−1), and AC-KHP (1139 m2 g−1), values more than twice that of the reference carbon RC-PP (518 m2 g−1).

These results suggest that the chemical activation process promoted the development of an extensive network of ultrafine pores. Potassium hydroxide (KOH) is known for its high reactivity and proved to be particularly efficient in generating the largest micropore surface area among the three agents studied. Although the values obtained for AC-KCO and AC-KHP are slightly lower than that of AC-KOH, they still represent a significant increase compared to the reference carbon, also confirming the effectiveness of K2CO3 and KC8H4O4 in generating micropores. The observed variations between the activated materials can be attributed to differences in the thermal decomposition mechanisms of each agent and the release of gases that contribute to porosity development.

Jinyang et al. (2024) propose that this performance may be related to the formation of a higher proportion of micropores or the creation of more accessible and effective structures for adsorption. To confirm which hypothesis is correct, the nonlocal density functional theory approach was used, as recommended by IUPAC (Thommes et al. 2015).

The pore size distribution was determined using the NLDFT method, applying the slit pore model, which allowed for a quantitative assessment of the presence of micropores (diameter < 2 nm) and mesopores (2 nm < diameter < 50 nm). According to the classification proposed by IUPAC, micropores can be further subdivided into ultramicropores (< 0.7 nm), supermicropores (0.7–1.0 nm), and micropores (1.0–2.0 nm) (Cordeiro et al. 2024). The results presented in Table 2 indicate that the AC-KOH sample is predominantly supermicroporous, with most pores ranging from 0.7 to 1.0 nm, accounting for 83.3% of the total pore volume. Additionally, the presence of narrow mesopores, ranging from 5.8 to 13.0 nm (16.7%), was also present. These findings suggest that AC-KOH exhibits a hierarchical porous structure, a desirable characteristic for materials used in adsorption and catalysis applications.

In the activated carbons AC-KCO and AC-KHP, the micropores are also predominantly distributed in the 0.7 to 1.5 nm range, as shown in Table 2. For AC-KCO, supermicropores account for 78.4% of the total pore volume, while 14.8% fall within the 1.0 to 1.5 nm range, indicating a significant distribution within the broadened microporosity. The activation of printed paper waste treated with potassium carbonate caused strong reactions to the carbonaceous structures, inducing carbon oxidation to CO2 and breaking down structures, thus promoting microporosity with a small amount of mesopores. In comparison, AC-KHP, which has a smaller total pore volume, has the largest distribution of micropores in the supermicropore range (85.4%) with a significant contribution in the 1.0–1.5 nm range (4.95%).

All SEM images in Fig. 7 were acquired at consistent magnifications: 100 × for the reference carbon (RC-PP), with a scale bar of 500 µm, and 3000 × for all other samples, with a scale bar of 10 µm. This allows a direct visual comparison of morphological transformations.

As illustrated in Fig. 7(a), the morphology of shredded paper waste is characterized by agglomerates of fibers with a rough surface (sometimes described in the literature as scaly ribbons). These fibers are devoid of pores or cavities, which is consistent with the high concentration of cellulose and hemicellulose in their composition (Vukoje and Rožić 2018).

Figure 7(b) shows that the reference carbon (RC-PP), produced at 800 °C without activation, exhibits a rough texture with multiple cavities, still preserving several carbonized ribbon-shaped fibers that reflect the morphology of the original material. However, this structure appears dense and collapsed in comparison to the activated samples, suggesting a limited contribution to overall porosity and, consequently, a low surface area and adsorption capacity, which necessitates subsequent chemical activation.

The activation with potassium hydroxide (AC-KOH), as demonstrated in Fig. 7(c), leads to the formation of a structure with significant roughness and numerous cavities, indicating the presence of microporosity, not detectable by N2 physisorption analysis.

Visually, the AC-KOH surface exhibits the most severe and extensive erosion among all samples, resulting in an extremely heterogeneous texture. This highly aggressive etching is a direct manifestation of the redox reaction between carbon and KOH.

According to Heidarinejad et al. (2020), the genesis of these cavities in the KOH-activated carbon is attributed to the redox reaction between the carbon and KOH, as well as gasification with the release of H2, H2O, CO, and CO2. During the activation process, the generation of K+ and K2CO3 within the carbonaceous matrix contributes to stability and increases the interlayer spacing, enhancing the efficiency and adsorption capacity of the activated carbon. Furthermore, metallic potassium, originating from KOH during pyrolysis, intercalates into the carbon structure and reacts with the carbon walls.

Figure 7(d) illustrates the AC-KCO sample, whose porosity developed during activation with K2CO3. The obtained material is a result of its decomposition at high temperatures under an inert atmosphere to produce K2O and CO2 and subsequent reduction of K2O by carbon, producing elemental potassium (K) and carbon monoxide (CO) (Lehman et al. 1998). It is important to consider the possibility of reduction of K2O by CO at higher temperatures resulting in elemental potassium and CO2. The highly permeable elemental potassium penetrates the carbon structure, triggering the formation of micropores. Concomitantly, the gases released during the process promote pore expansion (Adinata et al. 2007; Foo and Hameed, 2012). While also highly porous, the AC-KCO morphology appears to feature more defined and potentially larger cavity structures compared to the general roughness seen in AC-KOH. The visual distinction supports the literature suggesting that the decomposition of potassium carbonate generates cavities on the surface of the activated carbon shaped by the locations occupied by the activating agent. These empty spaces act as channels, connecting the micropores and mesopores formed in the activated carbon (Abbas and Ahmed, 2016).

The porosity development and surface roughening observed in the AC-KHP sample, Fig. 7(e) shows clear evidence of activation, presenting an intermediary level of surface destruction between the RC-PP and the highly etched AC-KOH. This morphology is attributed to the influence of potassium species (K2CO3, K2O, and K) formed during the thermal decomposition of KC8H4O4 under a nitrogen atmosphere. The infiltration of these species into the carbonaceous framework during activation leads to the creation of micropores and mesopores, as evidenced by the textural analysis, Fig. 6(a) and 6(b).

Considering that the potassium hydrogen phthalate (KHC8H4O4) was used in an aqueous solution to impregnate the printed paper prior to the pyrolysis stage, the chemical equilibrium can produce low concentrations of phthalate anions (C8H4O42−) and oxonium (H3O+), since hydrogen phthalate is weakly acidic, pKa = 5.41 (Gagliardi et al. 2010). The subsequent thermal decomposition of KHC8H4O4 in N2 results in K2CO3 and char (Belcher et al. 1960; Newkirk and Laware, 1962). Afterward, K2CO3 decomposes into K2O and CO2 and reacts as discussed above.

These results indicate that the activated carbons prepared in one step with a precursor/activating agent ratio of 1:1 (AC-KOH, AC-KCO, and AC-KHP) show acceptable potential, justifying the use of printed paper as a suitable precursor for the preparation of AC for CO2 capture.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that microporous activated carbons can be effectively manufactured from paper waste via a process involving pretreatment, impregnation, and chemical activation coupled with direct carbonization under an inert atmosphere. This strategy bypasses the separate precarbonization stage, leading to a decrease in energy consumption compared to conventional two-step procedures.

The activation of printed paper waste with KOH, K2CO3, and KHC8H4O4 resulted in activated carbons with significant surface areas and a high volume of micropores. AC-KOH exhibited a surface area of 1091 m2 g−1, with 83.33% of the micropore volume in the 0.7–1.0 nm range. In contrast, AC-KCO and AC-KHP displayed a larger volume of micropores in the 0.7–1.5 nm range (93.2% and 90.4%, respectively), with lower microporosity below 1.0 nm compared to AC-KOH. However, the micropore volume of AC-KHP in the 0.7–1.0 nm range (0.345 cm3 g−1) was higher than that of AC-KOH (0.333 cm3 g−1).

In TSA tests conducted at a temperature of 30 °C, a pressure of 1 bar, and a flow rate of 100 mL·min−1 (50% CO2 + 50% N2), the activated carbons AC-KOH, AC-KCO, and AC-KHP, produced from printed paper waste, adsorbed 7.64%, 6.28%, and 6.80% of CO2, respectively. These results demonstrate the effectiveness of activating agents, in contrast with the adsorption of only 1.98% observed in the reference carbon (without activation). The cyclic stability analysis of AC-KOH showed a minimal variation in CO2 adsorption capacity (average of less than 1%, between 7.60% and 7.55%) over ten adsorption–desorption cycles, indicating good cycle stability and regeneration performance. This research provides a valuable reference for the development of solid CO2 adsorbents using different types of paper found in municipal solid waste.

The activated carbons AC-KOH, AC-K2CO3, and AC-KHP exhibit a rapid initial adsorption rate due to the availability of active sites. However, as adsorption progresses, the occupation of these sites and the saturation of the pores by CO2 molecules increase the resistance to diffusion within the carbonaceous adsorbents. Equilibrium is reached in approximately 6 min for the three synthesized carbons, achieving adsorption capacities of 2.29 mmol g−1 (AC-KOH), 1.93 mmol g−1 (AC-K2CO3), and 2.03 mmol g−1 (AC-KHP).
