Whole-exome sequencing reveals the genetic causes and modifiers of moyamoya syndrome
Authors: Akikazu Nakamura, Shunsuke Nomura, Shoko Hara, Thiparpa Thamamongood, Taketoshi Maehara, Tadashi Nariai, Shasha Khairullah, Kay Sin Tan, Kenko Azuma, Ayako Chida‐Nagai, Yoshiyuki Furutani, Takahiro Hori, Koji Yamaguchi, Takakazu Kawamata, Constantin Roder, Hiroyuki Akagawa
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractMoyamoya vasculopathy secondary to various genetic disorders is classified as moyamoya syndrome (MMS). Recent studies indicate MMS occurs due to a combination of genetic modifiers and causative mutations for the primary genetic disorders. We performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) in 13 patients with various genetic disorders who developed MMS. WES successfully revealed the genetic diagnoses of neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF-1), Down syndrome, multisystemic smooth muscle dysfunction syndrome, Noonan syndrome, and alpha thalassemia. The previously reported modifier genes, RNF213 and MRVI1, were confirmed in the NF-1 and Down syndrome cases. Further analysis revealed rare hypomorphic variants in the causative genes of the primary disorders underlying MMS, such as Alagille syndrome and Rasopathies, conferred susceptibility to MMS. Genes involved in the development of pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH), such as ABCC8 and BMPR2, were also identified as potential modifiers. The rare variants in the MMS and PAH genes were significantly enriched in the eight Japanese patients with MMS compared with the 104 Japanese individuals from the 1000 Genomes Project. Disease genes associated with the arterial occlusive conditions represented by those of Rasopathies and PAH may provide novel diagnostic markers and future therapeutic targets for MMS as well as moyamoya disease with an unknown cause.Subject terms: Disease genetics, Cerebrovascular disorders, Stroke

Introduction

Moyamoya vasculopathy is a rare cerebrovascular disorder characterized by progressive steno-occlusion around the terminal internal carotid arteries, which leads to the formation of abnormal collateral networks called moyamoya vessels. The idiopathic form of this condition is termed moyamoya disease (MMD, MIM#607151) whereas the secondary form, which is associated with various genetic and systemic disorders, is termed moyamoya syndrome (MMS) or quasi-moyamoya disease1–3.

Genetic epidemiology studies suggest that MMD is a multifactorial disease, with RNF213 being the most important susceptibility gene in East Asian patients4–6. However, how RNF213 triggers disease onset and other undetermined genetic and environmental factors remain largely unknown7. According to recent studies, moyamoya vasculopathy observed in MMS has a multifactorial etiology. For example, neurofibromatosis type 1 (NF-1, MIM#162200), an autosomal dominant disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the NF1 gene, is a known underlying comorbidity of MMS. A Korean study reported that, compared to patients with NF-1 but without MMS (0%), a significantly higher proportion (18.6%) of patients with NF-1 and MMS harbored the p.R4810K variant in RNF213 (rs112735431), which is the founder susceptibility variant among East Asian patients with MMD8. In two unrelated European families with NF-1, the p.P186S variant in MRVI1 (rs35857561) co-segregated with family members that had MMS9. MRVI1 is a functional partner of GUCY1A3, the causative gene for an autosomal recessive form of MMS with esophageal achalasia (MIM#615750)10. These two genes are involved in nitric oxide signaling, which regulates vascular tone, and participate in platelet function9–12. Therefore, MRVI1 is considered a novel MMS susceptibility gene.

Neurofibromin encoded by NF1 is a negative regulator of Ras, whose haploinsufficiency in patients with NF-1 induces hyperactivation of the Ras pathway, resulting in a risk of MMS due to intimal cell proliferation13,14. As MMS only develops in a small proportion of patients with NF-1, synergic effects with genetic modifiers, such as variants in RNF213 and MRVI1, have been proposed for the pathogenesis of MMS8,9,15.

To confirm the polygenic architecture underlying MMS, we performed whole-exome sequencing (WES) using samples from patients with various genetic disorders, including NF-1, who developed MMS. We first determined the genetic diagnosis of the primary disorders and then attempted to identify genetic modifiers associated with MMS.

Methods

Ethics statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients or a parent/guardian of young patients provided written informed consent. This study was approved by the Ethics Committees of our respective universities: Tokyo Women’s Medical University and Tokyo Medical and Dental University for Asian patients, and Eberhard Karls University of Tübingen for European patients.

Patients

A total of 13 patients with MMS (Table 1) were analyzed: six with NF-1 (MIM#162200), three with Down syndrome (MIM#190685), two with multisystemic smooth muscle dysfunction syndrome (MSMDS; MIM#613834), one with Noonan syndrome (MIM#163950), and one with alpha thalassemia (MIM#604131). Coexisting moyamoya vasculopathy was diagnosed using magnetic resonance angiography, three-dimensional computed tomography angiography, or conventional angiography.

Genetic analysis

After obtaining informed consent, genomic DNA (gDNA) samples were obtained from the patients and subjected to WES using Illumina (San Diego, CA, USA) and Thermo Fisher Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA) platforms. The sequencers and exome enrichment kits used in this study are listed in Supplementary Table 1. Exome-enriched DNA libraries were constructed and sequenced according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Sequencing data were processed using the pipelines described in our previous studies on the Genome Reference Consortium Human Build 3716,17. Detected variants were annotated using ANNOVAR (http://annovar.openbioinformatics.org/)18. Confirmation of the candidate variants identified in WES was performed via standard polymerase chain reaction (PCR)-based amplification, followed by BigDye Terminator cycle sequencing on a 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

We used two bioinformatics tools, eXome-Hidden Markov Model (XHMM, https://zzz.bwh.harvard.edu/xhmm/index.shtml) and EXCAVATOR2 (https://sourceforge.net/projects/excavator2tool/), according to developer instructions to identify copy number aberrations using WES data from Illumina platforms19–21. These tools are read-depth based programs that use principal component analysis and off-targets reads, respectively, and require several tens of BAM files from the same exome enrichment kit as the initial input data. We used at least 39 BAM files from our in-house WES controls in addition to the BAM files of patients. The final copy number results were displayed in the primary chromosomal coordinate.

Functional analyses of the splice-site variants

Variants that could lead to abnormal splicing were first screened using dbscSNV v1.1 in ANNOVAR18,22. Candidate variants were further evaluated using SpliceAI, and those with delta scores above a threshold of 0.5 were subjected to minigene assay23. The exon and its adjacent intronic sequences containing the putative splice-altering variants were amplified via PCR and cloned into the multiple cloning site of the Exontrap pET01 vector (MoBi-Tec GmbH, Göttingen, Germany). Three cell lines were used for the subsequent transfection experiments: HEK293T and COS7 cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), and HeLa cells in Eagle’s minimum essential medium containing non-essential amino acids and 10% FBS. The cells were transfected with the wild-type or mutant vector using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). At 24 h post-transfection, total RNA was extracted, and reverse transcription (RT)-PCR of the region between the 5’ and 3’ exons in the pET01 vector was performed. The RT-PCR products were visualized using agarose gel electrophoresis and sequenced using BigDye Terminator cycle sequencing on a 3130xl Genetic Analyzer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). If the variants exhibited multiple splicing changes that could not be fractionated by agarose gel electrophoresis, they were separated by cloning into the pT7 blue t-vector (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and BamHI-digested pGEM-3Zf(+) vector (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). The primers used for the assay are available on request.

Quantitative analysis of intracellular and cell-surface expression

The effects of candidate variants on the intracellular expression of SMAD9 and PTGIS were evaluated using the Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Detection System (Promega Corporation)24. Human pcDNA3.0-6xMyc-SMAD9 was kindly provided by Dr. K. Miyazono (Tokyo, Japan)25, whose coding sequence (CCDS45032.1) was subcloned into the SgfI/PmeI-digested pFN38K HiBiT CMV-neo Flexi® Vector (Promega Corporation). The transcriptional sequence of PTGIS (NM_000961) was obtained by PCR using human ovary cDNA from multiple tissue cDNA (MTC) Panel II (Takara Bio USA, Inc., San Jose, CA, USA) as a template. Its coding sequence CCDS13419.1 was then obtained using nested-PCR and subcloned into the SgfI/PmeI-digested pFN38K HiBiT CMV-neo Flexi® Vector (Promega Corporation). Variants in SMAD9 and PTGIS were introduced into the vectors using a KOD-Plus Mutagenesis Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) or PrimeSTAR® Mutagenesis Basal Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Otsu, Japan).

In assays for SMAD9 variants, HEK293T cells were transfected with wild-type or mutant vectors expressing SMAD9 fused with the amino-terminal HiBiT tag using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). At 24 h post-transfection, Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Reagent including LgBiT and NanoLuc substrates were added to the culture medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega Corporation), and luminescence was measured using a Plate CHAMELEON V reader (Hidex, Turku, Finland).

In assays for PTGIS variants, Human Carotid Artery Endothelial cells (HCtAECs) were used (Cell Applications, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). The cells were cultured with Human Meso Endo Growth Medium Kit (Cell Applications, Inc.), and transfected with wild-type or mutant vectors expressing HiBiT-tagged PTGIS using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). At 24 h post-transfection, Nano-Glo® HiBiT Lytic Reagent was added to freshly replaced medium, and luminescence was measured in the same manner.

The effects of BMPR2 variants on the cell-surface expression were evaluated using the the Nano-Glo® HiBiT Extracellular Detection System (Promega Corporation)26,27. Human pcDNA3.0-BMPR2-HA was kindly provided by Dr. K. Miyazono (Tokyo, Japan)25, whose coding sequence (CCDS33361.1) was subcloned into the pFN38K-HiBiT vector in the same manner (Promega Corporation). HEK293T were transfected with wild-type or mutant vectors expressing HiBiT-tagged BMPR2 using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). At 24 h post-transfection, Nano-Glo® HiBiT Extracellular Reagent including LgBiT and NanoLuc substrates were added to freshly replaced medium according to the manufacturer’s instructions (Promega Corporation), and luminescence was measured.

Allelic differences in luminescence were compared in at least four experiments and evaluated using Wilcoxon rank sum test or Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the post-hoc Steel–Dwass test. These statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro (version 17; SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA). A P significance was set at P < 0.05. The primers used for the assays are available on request.

Dual luciferase reporter assay

To assess the impact of SMAD9 and BMPR2 variants on the bone morphogenetic protein (BMP) signaling, a reporter plasmid encoding deep-sea shrimp Oplophorus luciferase (NanoLuc®, Nluc) downstream of a BMP response element (BRE) was used as described in our previous study27. The pGL4.53[luc2/PGK] vector, encoding firefly luciferase (Fluc) downstream of the human phosphoglycerate kinase (PGK) promoter, was used as a control (Promega Corporation).

In assays for SMAD9 variants, COS7 cells were transfected in Opti-MEM™ with GlutaMAX™ supplement using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 30 ng of pNL3.1-BRE-Nluc, 6.0 ng of pGL4.53-Fluc, 1.0 ng of wild-type pFN38K-BMPR2-HiBiT, and 1.0 ng of wild-type or mutant pFN38K-SMAD9-HiBiT.

In assays for BMPR2 variants, COS7 cells were similarly transfected with 30 ng of pNL3.1-BRE-Nluc, 6.0 ng of pGL4.53-Fluc, 1.0 ng of wild-type or mutant pFN38K-BMPR2-HiBiT. Immediately after transfection, the cells were stimulated with or without 200 pg/mL human recombinant BMP4 (FUJIFILM WAKO Pure Chemical Corporation, Osaka, Japan).

At 24 h post-transfection, Fluc and Nluc activities were measured sequentially with Nano-Glo® Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega Corporation) using a Plate CHAMELEON V reader (Hidex). Allelic differences in luciferase activities were compared in at least four experiments and evaluated using Kruskal–Wallis test followed by the post-hoc Steel–Dwass test. A P significance was set at P < 0.05.

Prostacyclin synthase activity

To assess the impact of the PTGIS variant on prostacyclin synthesis, HCtAECs were transfected with wild-type or mutant pFN38K-PTGIS-HiBiT vectors using Lipofectamine 3000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific). At 48 h post-transfection, the stable metabolite of prostacyclin, 6-keto-prostaglandin F1alpha, was measured in the culture supernatant using a 6-keto-prostaglandin F1alpha ELISA kit (Cayman Chemical Company, Ann Arbor, MI, USA) and a Plate CHAMELEON II reader (Hidex). Allelic differences in the metabolite concentrations were evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A P significance was set at P < 0.05.

Gene set association analysis of genetic modifiers of MMS

Based on the WES results, two gene groups were selected as potential modifier genes for MMS (Supplementary Table 2). In this study, autosomal recessive loci and sex chromosomes were excluded to assess the effects of single alleles. To identify rare candidate variants for subsequent statistical analysis, a cutoff value for pathogenicity was defined as minor allele frequencies (MAFs) less than 1% in all sub-populations of the 1000 Genomes Project database (https://www.internationalgenome.org/home)28, the Genome Aggregation Database (gnomAD exomes and genomes collection v.2.1; https://gnomad.broadinstitute.org/)29, and the Human Genetic Variation Database (HGVD; https://www.hgvd.genome.med.kyoto-u.ac.jp/index.html) in the Japanese general population30. This cutoff value was based on the MAF of the known modifier, RNF213 p.R4810K (0.97% in HGVD), although that of MRVI1 p.P186S was 5.6% in the gnomAD exomes. Functional effects of the detected variants were also assessed using Sorting Tolerant From Intolerant For Genomes (SIFT 4G)31, MutationTaster32, and Combined Annotation Dependent Depletion (CADD) version 1.633. In this study, putative functional variants were defined as deleterious according to either SIFT 4G or MutationTaster, and CADD C-scores higher than or equal to RNF213 p.R4810K (13.19). Splicing variants were extracted as described in the previous section. Variants registered as “Benign” in the ClinVar database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/clinvar, accessed in July 2023) were excluded.

The genotypic data of 104 Japanese individuals (JPT panel) were obtained from the 1000 Genomes Project using Tabix (http://www.htslib.org/) and VCFtools (https://vcftools.github.io/index.html) and used as the control next-generation sequencing data for Japanese individuals. Subsequently, the association between the detected candidate variants and MMS was tested using a collapsing method described in our previous study34. The P values were obtained using Fisher’s exact test in R ver.4.3.2 (https://www.r-project.org/). Statistical significance was set at P value < 0.05. The present case series consisted of multiple ethnicities from East Asia and Europe. Although many of the patients were of Japanese ethnicity (61.5%), we provided the overall P values in the Supplementary Information. The control genotypic data of 1,007 East Asian (EAS) and European (EUR) individuals were obtained and filtered as described above.

Results

WES of samples from 13 patients yielded an average read depth of 92.8-fold and covered ≥ 96.9% of the targeted region with a read depth of more than ten-fold (Supplementary Table 1). First, we determined the genetic diagnosis of the primary diseases. Heterozygous point mutations in the NF1 gene were detected in each of the six patients with NF-1 (Table 1, Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2). These are known mutations and are registered as ‘Pathogenic’ or ‘Likely pathogenic’ in the ClinVar database. Three of the six mutations, c.3974G>A (p.R1325K), c.4578-1G>A, and c.6704+1G>T, were located at the exon–intron boundaries, and were predicted to cause aberrant splicing by SpliceAI23 (Supplementary Fig. 3). The minigene assay confirmed that these splicing mutations caused partial intron retentions or exon skipping, resulting in premature terminations: p.Y1292RfsX7, p.R1526SfsX53/54, and p.A2215IfsX5, respectively (Fig. 1).

For the two remaining patients, genetic diagnosis was confirmed by detecting the known pathogenic mutations. XHMM and EXCAVATOR2 analyses revealed that the Chinese-Malaysian patient with alpha thalassemia had a hemizygous deletion of the hemoglobin subunit alpha 1 and 2 genes (HBA1 and HBA2) (Fig. 2). This deletion corresponds to the Southeast Asian (SEA) deletion, the most frequently observed alpha thalassemia allele in Southeast Asia and South China37. In the remaining hemizygous allele, HBA2 was disrupted due to a mutation at the termination codon (p.X143Q; Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2), which is also commonly found in these regions and is referred to as Hemoglobin Constant Spring38. Thus, loss of function in three of the four alpha globin alleles led to the diagnosis of Hemoglobin H Constant Spring, a relatively severe form of alpha thalassemia that requires occasional transfusions. The last European patient with Noonan syndrome had the heterozygous p.R265Q mutation in PTPN11, which was reported as ‘Pathogenic’ in ClinVar (Table 1 and Supplementary Fig. 2).

We investigated the potential genetic modifiers involved in the development of MMS in combination with the underlying disease-causing mutations. Consistent with previous reports, the RNF213 p.R4810K (rs112735931) and MRVI1 p.P186S (rs35857561) variants were confirmed in Japanese and European patients with NF-1, respectively (Table 2)8,9.

Other rare missense variants in RNF213 and MRVI1 were also identified in the Japanese and European patients with Down syndrome, respectively, indicating ethnic specificity in the genetic susceptibility to moyamoya vasculopathy between East Asian and European patients.

ClinVar annotations provided further insights into the polygenic architecture underlying MMS. In addition to the causative mutations of the primary diseases, five of the 13 patients harbored ClinVar variants that might be involved in their pathogenesis (Table 2). Although these variants were classified as variants of ‘Uncertain significance’ or ‘Conflicting interpretations of pathogenicity,’ they were all putative functional variants that exceeded the predefined functional threshold based on rarity in the general population and deleterious scores of nucleotide substitutions (Methods). Patient 3, who had NF-1, harbored the p.T174M variant (rs144999773) in JAG1, the causative gene for Alagille syndrome (MIM#118450), an autosomal dominant arteriohepatic dysplasia known to complicate MMS39,40. Patient 4, who also had NF-1, harbored the p.D434H variant in SOS2, which is one of the causative genes of Noonan syndrome (Noonan syndrome 9, MIM#616559, due to SOS2 mutations). Currently, OMIM lists the 12 genes responsible for the autosomal dominant types of this syndrome, which are also known to complicate cardio-cerebrovascular abnormalities, including MMS40,41. Patient 7, who had Down syndrome, harbored the p.R2349L variant (rs864622065) in NF1. Some cases of Noonan syndrome and NF-1 are categorized as Rasopathy as they share the same phenotypic spectrum and genetic cause as abnormalities in the genes that participate in the Ras pathway42,43. For example, Costello syndrome (MIM#218040), a type of Rasopathy caused by HRAS mutations, was reported to complicate MMS40,42–44. Based on the above-mentioned observations, we hypothesized that the following candidates are modifier genetic variants associated with MMS: variants in the known susceptibility genes to MMD and MMS, and those in causative genes of the genetic disorders complicating MMS. A total of 27 candidate genes were selected and defined as MM genes (Supplementary Table 2).

Patient 2, who also had NF-1, harbored the c.4412-2A>G mutation (rs1554904136) in ABCC8, in which loss-of-function mutations were recently reported to cause pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) with incomplete penetrance45 (Table 2). SpliceAI indicated that this mutation disrupts the splice-acceptor site at exon 37 in ABCC8 (NM_000352) (Supplementary Fig. 3), and the mutation was confirmed to cause partial intron retentions and/or exon skipping based on the minigene assay (Fig. 3).

Several common features exist between PAH and moyamoya vasculopathy. PAH is a rare, severe vasculopathy characterized by the obliteration of remodeled pulmonary microvessels that leads to elevated pulmonary arterial pressure and right ventricle failure. Several patients with co-occurring PAH and MMD were identified previously, two of whom had the homozygous p.R4810K variant (rs112735931) in RNF21346,47. This foremost susceptibility variant to MMD was also reported to be a risk factor for poor clinical outcome and the onset of PAH without MMD48,49. Peripheral pulmonary artery stenosis is a major clinical manifestation of Alagille syndrome rather than MMS39,40. These lines of evidence for the common genetic susceptibility between PAH and moyamoya vasculopathy indicate that the known disease genes of PAH could be good candidates for the genetic modifiers of MMS. A total of 13 genes were selected and defined as PAH genes (Supplementary Table 2).

The GDF3 p.R266C variant (rs140926412) from patient 12 was previously reported to be pathogenic in American and European families with Klippel–Feil syndrome (MIM#613702), scoliosis, and colobomatous microphthalmia (MIM#613703)50. However, the interpretation of its pathogenicity is currently being questioned in ClinVar. GDF3 is a member of the transforming growth factor-beta superfamily, belonging to the subclass of bone morphogenetic protein/growth and differentiation factors, and plays a role in ocular and skeletal development50,51. As the clinical features of Noonan syndrome also include spinal deformity52, we concluded that the GDF3 p.R266C variant is a genetic modifier of patient skeletal phenotype rather than MMS (Table 2).

We preferentially reviewed the MM and PAH genes, which overlapped with RNF213, to identify modifier genetic variants for MMS from the WES data (Methods and Supplementary Table 2), and identified 13 potential modifier variants for MMS in eight patients (Table 2). Five of the eight patients harbored two modifiers, which further supports the polygenic mechanism underlying MMS. These modifiers in the MM and PAH genes were significantly overrepresented in the eight Japanese patients (75.0%) compared with the 104 Japanese individuals (31.7%) from the 1000 Genomes Project (P = 0.021, Fisher’s exact test). The association was also significant when PAH and MM were compared separately (Table 3). Overall comparisons in East Asian and European subjects showed similar associations (P < 0.05, Fisher’s exact test, Supplementary Table 3). No potential modifiers were identified in patients with MSMDS and alpha thalassemia.

Discussion

In this study, WES was used to successfully diagnose the primary genetic disorders underlying MMS by detecting both causative point mutations and structural variations (Table 1). In recent years, exome-first approaches have been encouraged to reduce diagnostic costs and time with the aid of advanced bioinformatics tools, such as XHMM and EXCAVATOR2 used in this study54,55. The use of artificial intelligence also helped confirm genetic diagnoses by predicting not only splice site disruption but also cryptic splice site activation with high accuracy23 (Figs. 1 and 3, and Supplementary Fig. 3).

The polygenic disease susceptibility underlying MMS was subsequently examined. In addition to the previously reported RNF213 and MRVI18,9, two morbid gene groups (MM and PAH) were identified as novel modifier susceptibility genes to MMS (Table 3 and Supplementary Table 2). The MM group included the causative genes of the genetic disorders known to complicate MMS, in which rare functional variants exhibit susceptibility to comorbid MMS without developing the corresponding genetic disorders (Table 2). Blair et al. first described this phenomenon as transitive genetic association. Within Mendelian disease loci, highly penetrant mutations not only result in corresponding Mendelian disorders but also play a substantial role in comorbid multifactorial diseases, whereas variants with milder effects only contribute to comorbid multifactorial diseases, such as MMS56. Although this theory was originally demonstrated in the context of common-variant association studies, a subsequent report revealed the similar contribution of rare variants in Mendelian disease loci57.

Identification of the PAH group is of particular interest as it has potential drug targets. For example, ABCC8, mutated in patient 2 with NF-1, encodes sulfonylurea receptor 1 (SUR1), which acts as a regulatory subunit of the ATP-sensitive potassium channel, Kir6.2. Mutant ABCC8 impaired the SUR1/Kit6.2 channel activity, which was recovered by the selective SUR1 activator, diazoxide. SUR1/Kir6.2 channels activated using diazoxide were revealed to relax arteries in lung specimens45,58. PTGIS, which was mutated in patient 6 with NF-1, encodes an enzyme responsible for the biosynthesis of prostacyclin, a potent regulator of vascular tone, intimal proliferation, and platelet aggregation59,60. A synthetic analog of prostacyclin, iloprost, significantly decreased pulmonary vascular resistance of patients with PAH having PTGIS variants60.

In this study, no potential modifier variants for MMS were detected in patients with MSMDS and alpha thalassemia. MMS observed in patients with ACTA2 mutations has distinctive angiographical features, such as an abnormal straight course of intracranial arteries and absence of basal collaterals, as reported previously for patient 10 with MSMDS35. Therefore, MMS associated with MSMDS is currently considered to be a different disease than the classic moyamoya vasculopathy. Moreover, this ACTA2 cerebral vasculopathy is highly penetrant when the Arg179 residue is disrupted, and thus requires no genetic modifier40. In contrast, MMS observed in patients with thalassemia exhibits the typical angiographical pattern (Supplementary Fig. 1), despite the relative scarcity of reports of MMS occurring with thalassemia, particularly with alpha thalassemia61,62. Among hereditary hemoglobinopathies, MMS is more commonly associated with sickle cell disease and is found in 20–35% of pediatric patients with sickle cell disease who undergo cerebral angiography62. Non‑deformable red cells are hypothesized to obstruct blood flow in the vasa vasorum, leading to vessel wall ischemia and subsequent intimal proliferation of the internal carotid artery. The reduction in blood flow due to anemia, together with less deformable blood cells, might also lead to progressive endothelial proliferation61,62. Owing to the higher penetrance of MMS relative to NF-1 (2.5–6%) and direct vascular wall damage caused by abnormal red blood cells, hereditary hemoglobinopathies may require fewer genetic modifiers for MMS development40.

The findings of this study can be extended to MMD and MMS associated with systemic diseases, such as autoimmune diseases, in future replication studies with a larger sample size; this is because we previously revealed that approximately half of Japanese patients with MMS and Graves’ disease had susceptibility variants in RNF21334. Targeted next-generation sequencing covering the MM and PAH genes and in vitro and in vivo functional analyses of susceptibility variants may provide new diagnostic markers and therapeutic avenues for moyamoya vasculopathy.
