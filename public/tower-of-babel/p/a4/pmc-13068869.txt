ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundMarburg virus disease (MVD) is a rare but highly fatal zoonotic illness. Current serological tools are limited by their reliance on glycoprotein (GP)-based antigens or Marburg virus infected cell lysates, which cannot distinguish natural infection induced immune responses from GP-based vaccines. This diagnostic gap complicates the detection of missed cases particularly in areas of high vaccination coverage.MethodsWe developed and validated two complementary assays: a nucleoprotein C-terminal tail (NPct) Mix-and-Read (MR) assay and a recombinant GP1,2ΔTM ELISA. Serum samples used for validation were collected 6–9 months after the 2024 Rwanda MVD outbreak from RT-PCR–confirmed recovered patients (n = 36) and uninfected contacts (n = 46). Assay validation included additional serum controls from pre-outbreak Rwandan samples, Ebola virus disease recovered patients, and Rousettus aegyptiacus bats experimentally infected with MARV. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analyses were used to assess clinical accuracy.FindingsThe MARV anti-NPct MR assay demonstrated near-perfect discrimination between cases and controls with an area under the curve (AUC) = 0.996 (95% CI, 0.990–1.000), and yielding performance of 94.4% clinical sensitivity (95% CI, 81.9–99.0%) and 99.7% specificity (95% CI, 98.1–100.0%), and no cross-reactivity with Ebola convalescent sera. The MARV GP ELISA achieved 94.4% clinical sensitivity (95% CI, 81.9–99.0%) and 100% specificity (95% CI, 92.3–100%) with an AUC 0.973 (95% CI, 0.922–1.000). Both assays produced concordant results for all samples tested. In experimentally infected bats, the MARV anti-NPct MR assay achieved 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity.InterpretationThe MARV anti-NPct MR assay provides a rapid, high-throughput, species-agnostic tool expected to differentiate natural infection from vaccine-induced immunity, addressing a critical gap in the current MVD diagnostic pipeline. Its robust validation in the largest cohort of recovered patients to date and applicability to reservoir hosts highlight its broad utility for outbreak response, follow-up care, and ecological studies.FundingThis work was supported by funding from US Centers for Disease Control and prevention, the Coalition for Epidemic Preparedness Innovations (CEPI), and Rwanda Biomedical Centre.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

Existing knowledge on Marburg virus serology is limited to small cohorts of recovered patients. Available assays rely almost exclusively on glycoprotein (GP) or whole-virus antigens, restricting their ability to distinguish natural infection from GP-based vaccine responses in settings where these vaccines have been deployed. There is a lack of assays that have been validated using a large cohort of RT-PCR–confirmed Marburg virus disease (MVD) recovered patients, and a lack of rapid, high-throughput, or species-agnostic MARV serological tools. As a result, critical gaps have remained in outbreak detection, recovered patients’ follow-up, and One Health surveillance systems.

Added value of this study

Using the largest population of confirmed MVD recovered patients assembled to date, we developed and validated two complementary serological assays: a rapid Mix-and-Read (MR) assay targeting the nucleoprotein C-terminal tail (NPct) and an IgG ELISA based on a prefusion-stabilised GP trimer. The anti-NPct assay demonstrated near-perfect clinical performance (AUC 0.996; sensitivity 94.4% (95% CI, 81.9–99.0%); specificity 99.7% (95% CI, 98.1–100.0%), and no cross-reactivity with Ebola convalescent sera. The GP ELISA independently confirmed natural infection in unvaccinated recovered patients (AUC 0.973 (95% CI, 0.922–1.000); sensitivity 94.4% (95% CI, 81.9–99.0%); specificity 100% (95% CI, 92.3–100%)). Both assays showed complete concordance in the validation cohort. Because nucleoprotein is not encoded by current MARV GP-based vaccine constructs, NPct-based detection is expected to distinguish natural infection from GP vaccine–induced seropositivity; however, this distinction was not directly evaluated in the present study. In experimentally infected Rousettus aegyptiacus bats, the anti-NPct assay achieved 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity, supporting its use for reservoir surveillance and integrated human–animal monitoring.

Implications of all the available evidence

These complementary assays fill essential gaps in MARV diagnostic preparedness by enabling reliable detection of natural infection supporting surveillance across human and reservoir hosts. The MR assay rapid workflow, high throughput, and species-agnostic design address longstanding bottlenecks in filovirus serology and enable scalable integration into outbreak response, ecological monitoring, and One Health early-warning systems. With further longitudinal validation, these tools can strengthen MARV epidemiologic modelling, risk assessment, and public health decision-making, improving readiness for future outbreaks.

Introduction

Marburg virus disease (MVD) is caused by Marburg virus (MARV) and Ravn virus (RAVV), both members of the species Orthomarburgvirus marburgense in the Filoviridae family, with MARV responsible for the vast majority of documented outbreaks.1,2 Since its first recorded occurrence in 1967 in Germany, MVD has caused 21 outbreaks globally, with the majority occurring in sub-Saharan Africa.3, 4, 5, 6 The increasing frequency of outbreaks and size of MVD outbreaks in diverse geographic locations, along with cases linked to international travel, underscores the potential for heightened risk of wider or global spread. Consequently, the World Health Organization (WHO) and Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunisation (Gavi) have classified MARV as a priority pathogen for research and development to enhance pandemic preparedness and prevention capabilities.6, 7, 8

The Egyptian Rosette bat (R. aegyptiacus) serves as a natural reservoir for both Marburg virus (MARV) and Ravn virus (RAVV).9,10 Human exposure to these bats or entry into caves and mines colonised by infected R. aegyptiacus populations are frequently associated with viral spillover events into human populations.11, 12, 13, 14 This association is supported by the geographic overlap between MVD outbreaks and countries with large populations of R. aegyptiacus bats.15

Marburg virus (MARV) encodes seven structural proteins arranged on its ∼19-kb negative-sense RNA genome: nucleoprotein (NP), VP35 (polymerase cofactor and innate-immune antagonist), VP40 (matrix protein and major driver of budding/egress, with additional roles in interferon signalling antagonism), the virion surface glycoprotein GP that is proteolytically cleaved into GP1 and GP2 (mediating attachment/entry via endosomal processing and interaction with host factors such as NPC1), VP30 (a transcription factor with genus-specific functions), VP24 (a nucleocapsid-associated/minor matrix protein important for assembly), and the L (RNA-dependent RNA polymerase). Structural and functional studies have defined the L–VP35 polymerase complex, GP processing and neutralisation epitopes, and nucleocapsid assembly requirements (NP-VP35-VP24), all of which inform antigen design for vaccines and serology as well as targets for antivirals.16 For serological applications in particular, NP and GP are important targets, as naturally infected individuals may generate antibody responses against both proteins.17

Currently, there is no licenced treatment or vaccine for MVD; therefore, timely case detection and preventive measures are critical to mitigate the impact and rapidly control outbreaks. Diagnosis is complicated by nonspecific early symptoms that are clinically similar to other endemic febrile illnesses.18 The diagnosis of MVD relies on detecting viral RNA via nucleic acid amplification tests such as real-time reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), a method that is rarely accessible for the at-risk population, mostly in resource-limited settings.19,20 MARV RNA in blood plasma can be reliably detected by RT-PCR with high sensitivity throughout the acute phase of the disease but becomes undetectable during the convalescence phase. However, MARV may persist in the eye and in body fluids such as semen in males and breast milk in females, posing a potential risk of further transmission after recovery.21, 22, 23 Hence, patient follow-up post-discharge is essential to ensure proper management of complications as well as for public health surveillance. Furthermore, serosurveys among close contacts are recommended to identify potential missed cases, support appropriate care, and inform epidemiological investigations, including contact-tracing, a core component of outbreak response and prevention.24

In 2024, Rwanda experienced an outbreak of MVD from 27 September to 20 December, resulting in 66 confirmed cases, of whom 15 died. The case fatality rate (CFR) of approximately 23% in Rwanda was the second lowest compared to previous outbreaks.25 As part of the robust response to the outbreak, a vaccine trial (PACTR202412708603314) was conducted using a chimpanzee adenovirus serotype 3 (cAd3) vector vaccine encoding the wild-type glycoprotein (GP) from Marburg virus.25, 26, 27 The trial enrolled more than 1700 participants, comprising registered high-risk contacts of MVD cases identified during the outbreak as well as frontline and other healthcare workers.27

In Rwanda, the post-outbreak identification of MARV infections among contacts using serological methods was complicated by vaccine deployments. Both the Marburg vaccine (cAd3-MARV GP) and the Ebola vaccine booster (MVA-BN-Filo) deployed in 2019 produce MARV glycoprotein.28,29 This issue impacts assays utilising total Marburg virus (MARV)-infected lysates, commonly employed in MARV ELISA or glycoprotein-based assays. These assays are currently the primary methods for identifying previously unrecognised serologically positive cases, which may have been missed due to insufficient screening during the acute phase of illness.30, 31, 32, 33 The viral NP serves as a possible alternative antigen for serological detection of natural MARV infection because it is not included in the current vaccine formulations, thereby allowing clear differentiation between vaccine-induced immune responses and antibodies resulting from actual viral infection. However, there is limited evidence that anti-NP antibodies can be used to reliably identify natural MARV infections, in humans and animals.34,35

Here we report the development and validation of two complementary serological assays using an unprecedented large number of RT-PCR confirmed MVD recovered patients: the MARV anti-NP Mix-and-Read (MR) assay and the anti-GP ELISA. The MARV anti-NP MR assay is species agnostic, high-throughput, simple, Ig isotype independent, and rapid method for detecting anti-MARV NP antibodies, which indicate prior infection in humans. The anti-MARV GP ELISA is designed to detect human total IgG antibodies that bind to the cleaved recombinant MARV GP1,2ΔTM trimer. This combination of serological assays using MARV antigens can identify human cases with high accuracy, independent of their vaccination status and discriminate between natural infection-induced antibody responses and vaccine-induced responses.

Methods

Ethics

Human samples used in this study were collected from controls and RT-PCR confirmed MVD recovered patients under protocols approved by the Rwanda National Ethics Committee (RNEC) (Approval No. RNEC 132/2024). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and local regulations governing research with human subjects. Each participant was assigned a unique Participant ID (PTID) which was labelled on the signed consent form and recorded in REDCap without any personal identifiers.

Human sample collection

Venous blood (8.5 mL) was collected using standard aseptic techniques into pre-labelled serum-separator tubes (BD Vacutainer SST Tubes, Becton Dickinson, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA). Samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min, then transported to Rwanda National Reference Laboratory for separation and storage. Serum was separated by centrifugation at 1500 × g for 10 min, then aliquoted into 2 mL sterile cryovials labelled with the PTID and aliquot number. Aliquoted serum samples were stored at −80 °C until tested. For assay validation, control serum and plasma samples included historical MVD recovered patients from three outbreaks: South Africa 1975 (2 recovered patients out of 3 cases), Kenya 1980 (1 recovered patient), and Uganda 2012 (2 recovered patients out of 15 cases). Additional controls comprised pre-outbreak Rwanda samples (2019–2023, n = 243), US negative controls from non-endemic regions (n = 168), low-risk Rwanda outbreak contacts who remained RT-PCR negative, asymptomatic and unvaccinated (n = 46), and Ebola virus disease (EVD) recovered patients from Guinea 2021 (n = 8) for cross-reactivity assessment. For Ebola Virus Disease recovered patient samples obtained following 2021 outbreak in Guinea, serum specimens were collected 14 months after symptom onset. This study was approved by the Comité National d’Ethique pour la Recherche en Santé (CNERS). For validation of the NP MR assay, additional deidentified plasma samples collected in previous years before the MVD outbreak were used as negative control sets. Following validation, all samples collected were tested and systematically repeated to ensure reproducibility of the anti-MARV NPct assay.

Recombinant protein production

The more variable C-terminal tail of MARV-NP was selected over the NP core domain, which exhibits higher sequence conservation between Marburg viruses and Ebola viruses, suggesting that its use as an antigen is more likely to compromise assay specificity due to cross-reactivity (Figure S1). The sequences for expressing MARV nucleoprotein C-terminal tail region (residues 600–695) from Rwanda 2024 strain were designed with N-terminal His-tagged maltose binding protein, HRV 3C cleavage site, and SmBit or LgBit. These sequences were codon-optimised for bacterial expression and cloned into pET28a by Twist Bioscience. The MARV NP sequence was derived from GenBank: XJQ58952. The constructs were transformed in Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) strain (Thermo Fisher), and bacterial cultures were grown in Luria broth with kanamycin. The cultures were induced with 1 mM isopropyl β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside when the optical density was 0.4–0.6. Following induction, the cultures were transferred to 16 °C for overnight incubation. Cells were harvested by centrifugation, resuspended in lysis buffer (500 mM NaCl, 20 mM Tris-Cl (pH 7), 0.1% Triton-X, 5% glycerol), and sonicated. MARV NPct antigens were purified by nickel affinity chromatography (HisTrap Excel column; Cytiva). Elution from the nickel column was performed with PBS containing additional 350 mM NaCl and 500 mM imidazole. The purified proteins were dialysed against PBS pH 7.4 by performing four buffer exchanges using Amicon Ultra centrifugal filters with 30 kDa cutoff membrane (Amicon, Cat. no. UFC903008). The MBP tag was removed by cleavage with HRV 3C protease at 4 °C overnight (AcroBiosystems, Cat. no. 3C-N3133). His-tagged MBP and 3C protease were removed by passing the sample through a HisTrap Excel column, and the flow-through containing SmBit-NPct and LgBit-NPct were collected, aliquoted, stored at −80 °C and purity and integrity was verified by Coomassie staining (Figure S2A). Aliquots of 10X stock mix of MARV SmBit-NPct (25 nM) and LgBit-NPct (50 nM) in MR buffer (PBS pH 7.4, 0.2% bovine serum albumin, and 0.05% Tween-20) were prepared and kept at −80 °C.

The MARV GP ectodomain from Angola 2005 strain (GenBank: DQ447660.1, residues 1–637) with a C-terminal 8X His-tag and AviTag was codon-optimised for human cell expression, synthesised, and cloned into pEEV-Puro vector by Twist Bioscience. Mutations T578P and H589I were introduced to stabilise the MARV-GP trimer.36 The protein was produced by transfecting Expi293F cells (Thermo Fisher Scientific, RRID:CVCL_D615) with the pEEV-MARV GP stable trimer plasmid using ExpiFectamine 293 (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. no. A14524) in Expi293 Expression Medium (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat no. A1435101). Transfected cells were maintained in polycarbonate Erlenmeyer flasks on an orbital shaker in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 8% CO2 for 4–5 days. Cells were removed by centrifugation, and the clarified supernatant was supplemented with 350 mM NaCl. The protein was purified using HisTrap Excel columns (Cytiva, Cat. no. 17371205). The column was washed with PBS supplemented with 350 mM NaCl and 25 mM imidazole, and protein was eluted with PBS supplemented with 350 mM NaCl and 500 mM imidazole. The eluted protein was concentrated and the AviTag was biotinylated using the BirA enzyme (BirA biotin-protein ligase standard reaction kit, Avidity, Cat. no. BirA500). The biotinylated GP trimer was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography on a Superdex 200 pg column (Cytiva, Cat. no. 28989335) in PBS. The purified trimer eluted as a single symmetric peak centred at 55 min (Figure S3). GP trimer fractions were collected, aliquoted, and stored at −80 °C. Protein integrity and purity were verified by SDS-PAGE followed by Coomassie staining (Figure S2B). Under unreduced conditions, the GP antigen migrated as an SDS-resistant high molecular weight species (>200 kDa), consistent with a trimer, whereas under reduced conditions, it resolved into the expected GP1 and GP2ΔTM subunits.

MaxiSorp 96-well plates (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. no. 442404) were coated with 50 ng/well StrepTactin (IBA Lifesciences, Cat. no. 2-1204) diluted in 100 μL PBS by overnight incubation at +4 °C. After three washes with 0.1% Tween-20 in PBS (PBS-T), the plates were blocked with 5% (w/v) nonfat dry milk (Cell Signalling Technology) in the same buffer for 2 h at room temperature. The biotinylated MARV GP trimer was then captured from 100 μL PBS-T milk solution (25 ng/well in all but the first row of each plate) by incubation at room temperature for 1 h. After three washes with PBS-T and one wash with PBS, the plates were incubated with Liquid Plate Sealer (Candour Bioscience, Cat. no. 160 500; 200 μL/well) for 1 h at room temperature, the sealer removed, and the plates dried in a biological safety cabinet for approximately 1 h. Positive and negative controls sera were included on each plate during testing to monitor assay performance throughout the study period. Plates were stored at room temperature with desiccant and used within four weeks of preparation.

NP mix-and-read assay procedure

10X MARV-NPct mix was diluted to 1X in MR buffer. 15 μl of diluted MARV-NPct mix was dispensed into each well of a 96-well half-area white plate (Corning, Cat. no. 3693). 15 μl of serum or plasma sample or control was added to each well and incubated for 20 min at room temperature before adding 30 μl of NanoGlo substrate (Promega, Cat. no. N1120). The plate was read 10 min later using a GloMax® Navigator microplate reader (Promega). Luminescence was recorded as relative light units (RLU; raw instrument output). Because raw RLU is instrument- and settings-dependent, values were normalised to internal plate controls to minimise between-run variability. The RLU signal of samples and positive controls was normalised by dividing the relative luminescence unit (RLU) of each sample by the average RLU of the negative control (standard normal human serum) performed in triplicate. For R. aegyptiacus bat samples, the procedure was identical except that the 10X MARV-NPct mix was diluted to 5X with MR buffer.

MARV GP ELISA procedure

Serum samples were diluted 1:10 with 0.55% Triton-X100 and 0.55% Tween-20 (Sigma Aldrich, X100 and P2287) in PBS and heated to +56 °C for 30 min in a PCR cycler (volume 60 μL, lid temperature +80 °C). The samples were then further diluted into 5% nonfat dry milk so that a 1:100 serum dilution was obtained. A 7-point, 3-fold dilution series was then prepared so that the final serum dilution was 1:72,900. An in-house positive control, a negative control, and a no-sample control were similarly prepared and run on each plate. The strongest dilution of every sample or control, 1:100, was also tested in a well that had been coated with StrepTactin but in which no MARV GP had been captured.

Samples thus prepared (100 μL volumes) were added into dry wells of the pre-prepared ELISA plates and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. After three washes with PBS-T, secondary anti-human IgG antibody conjugated to horseradish peroxidase (Jackson ImmunoResearch, RRID: AB_2337586) was added in 100 μL 5% milk/PBS-T for 1 h, unbound enzyme washed off by three PBS-T cycles, and signals developed by incubation with 100 μL 1-Step Ultra TMB ELISA substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 34029) with development terminated by 100 μL ELISA Stop Solution (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Cat. no. SS04). Absorbances were quantified at 450 nm, the average signals from no-sample wells subtracted, and the 1:100 signals for MARV GP further corrected by subtracting the no-antigen signal from the same dilution. The ratio of no-antigen corrected versus uncorrected signal at 1:100 was then used to correct the signals of further dilutions. Laboratory personnel were not blinded to RT-PCR results and relevant clinical information when performing and interpreting the serological assays, as testing was conducted as part of assay validation using characterised samples.

Statistics

All specimens from RT-PCR-confirmed MVD cases with sufficient volume were included in the analyses; no imputation was performed. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 10). The performance of the anti-NPct MR assay and the GP1,2ΔTM ELISA was characterised using Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis and Area Under the Curve (AUC) values with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Sensitivity and specificity estimates were derived from a panel of 36 PCR-confirmed MVD recovered patients and 289 true-negative controls; the GP ELISA evaluation utilised the same cohort of recovered patients and 46 negative controls. While Youden's J statistic was calculated to explore optimal data-driven thresholds, we retained the validated operational cut-offs (normalised RLU ≥ 24.22 for anti-NPct; OD > 0.3 for GP ELISA at dilution 1:100) to ensure robust signal separation from background noise.

For group comparisons, non-parametric tests were selected due to the non-normal distribution of negative control data and small sample sizes in specific cohorts. Differences in antibody levels between multiple groups (MVD recovered patients, EVD recovered patients, and negative controls) were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Comparisons between two groups (e.g., infected vs. uninfected bats; MVD cases vs. controls in ELISA) were performed using the two-tailed Mann–Whitney U test. No formal sample size calculation was performed; sample sizes were determined by availability of RT-PCR–confirmed MVD convalescent sera and available negative control panels. Laboratory personnel were not blinded to RT-PCR results and relevant clinical information when performing and interpreting the serological assays, as testing was conducted as part of assay validation development.

Agreement between the anti-NPct MR assay and GP ELISA was assessed by calculating the overall percent concordance based on the established cut-offs, and the relationship between assay signals was evaluated using Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Data are presented as mean with standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise noted. P-values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

MARV NP MR assay in humans

Protein complementation assays can be developed to detect protein–protein interactions (PPI) in solution. The NanoBit PPI detection system can be adapted to detect antibody–antigen complexes without the need for washing steps typically required in conventional immunoassays. This approach utilises pairs of biosensors consisting of identical antigens fused to either the Small (SmBit) or Large (LgBit) inactive fragments of NanoLuc, an ultra-bright luciferase. Since most antibody isotypes are at least bivalent, they can simultaneously bind identical epitopes present on both SmBit and LgBit antigen fusions. Antigen-specific antibodies can therefore be detected by mixing identical antigen-LgBit and antigen-SmBit fusions with test sera. Antibody binding brings the SmBit and LgBit fragments into close proximity, enabling reconstitution of NanoLuc activity and generating a measurable luminescent signal upon addition of Nanoglo substrate. To detect anti-MARV antibodies induced by MARV infection independently of vaccination status, SmBit and LgBit fragments were fused to the MARV NP C-terminal tail domain (Fig. 1). To evaluate assay performance and determine thresholds of the MARV MR assay for use in MVD endemic countries, sera from 36 RT-PCR confirmed (32/36 received remdesivir during their hospitalisation), individuals who recovered from the Rwanda MVD outbreak were used. Serum samples from RT-PCR confirmed MVD cases and low-risk contacts of cases were collected between April and June 2025 (∼6–9 months after outbreak declaration). Neither the confirmed cases nor the low-risk contacts had received the chAD3 MARV-GP vaccine. Since previous outbreaks were mostly associated with very high case fatality rates or small numbers of cases, the recovered MVD group from Rwanda is unique due to the relatively high number of recovered individuals.4 In addition, 46 presumed negative samples from the 2024 Rwanda outbreak were added to the validation set. These presumed negative samples included low-risk contacts who were RT-PCR negative during the outbreak, had no symptoms following exposure, had not received Marburg vaccine, and had no history of exposure to bats.

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed by comparing MVD recovered individuals to a control group comprising low-risk contacts as well as pre-MVD outbreak samples, collected prior to 2024. ROC curve analysis demonstrated excellent clinical accuracy of the MARV anti-NP MR assay (Fig. 4A). NP MR assay in bats

MARV is a zoonotic virus that circulates in R. aegyptiacus, a well-characterised natural reservoir, and has been detected across several African countries. Given the species-agnostic nature of the MR assay principle,38, 39, 40 we anticipated it would detect anti-NPct antibodies in any previously infected species, including R. aegyptiacus bats. For bats, blood sample collection, serum preparation and processing were previously performed and described.41,42 Briefly, MARV-convalescent sera were generated from R. aegyptiacus experimentally infected with live MARV isolate 200704852 (Uganda bat origin) using a prime-boost protocol (8-week interval), with sera collected 3 weeks post-boost, as described by Schuh et al.41

The anti-NPct MR assay was therefore optimised and validated using sera from 20 of these experimentally MARV-infected R. aegyptiacus. These samples had been previously characterised using bat-specific anti-MARV ELISAs employing MARV-infected or RAVV-infected cell lysates as antigen sources, with uninfected bats serving as negative controls. The previous ELISAs detected 18/20 infected bats using MARV total antigen and 19/20 using RAVV total antigen.42

Using the anti-NPct MR assay, luminescence signals from infected bat sera were normalised to the mean of 5 uninfected control bats (Fig. 5A). MARV-infected bat sera exhibited normalised RLU values averaging 30.2-fold higher than uninfected controls, with individual signals ranging from 2.9 to 93.7. ROC analysis demonstrated perfect discrimination with an AUC of 1.0. MARV GP ELISA

Next, an ELISA measuring human IgG antibodies against the MARV GP ectodomain was developed to validate the results of the NP MR assay using a method independent in both technical principle and antigenic target. To minimise the risk of exposing cross-reactive epitopes due to plastic ELISA plate adsorption of MARV GP trimer, we used C-terminally biotinylated MARV GP that was captured on StrepTactin-coated plates.43 The same 36 MVD recovered patients and 46 low-risk contacts of the 2024 Rwanda outbreak that were tested with the anti-NPct MR assay were used as the positive and negative sample sets, respectively. The assay achieved an AUC of 0.973 (95% CI, 0.922–1.000) (Fig. 6A). Based on the ROC curve and distribution of positive and negative sample results, a cut-off of 0.3 corrected absorbance units at dilution 1:100 was chosen. This cut-off resulted in 100% specificity (95% CI, 92.3–100) and 94.4% sensitivity (95% CI, 81.9–99.0%) (34/36 RT-PCR-diagnosed cases positive for IgG antibodies at convalescence) (Fig. 6B). Serial sample dilution resulted in the expected dose–response curves, and positive/negative determination is therefore based on the first sample dilution. Discussion

Marburg virus disease (MVD), like Ebola virus disease, represents a rare but high-consequence infectious disease threat. Since its discovery in 1967, only 495 confirmed cases have been documented worldwide, yet the disease carries an approximate 80% fatality rate and has been imported outside of Africa on at least three occasions.4,44, 45, 46 While MARV causes severe disease in diagnosed patients, the true extent of asymptomatic or mildly symptomatic infections remains uncertain.

This knowledge gap exists because contact tracing and testing are typically limited to patients presenting severe symptoms compatible with MVD. Additionally, no serological assays for identifying missed MVD cases have been validated using a significant number of samples from confirmed cases. The substantial collection of convalescent samples from the 2024 Rwanda outbreak (n = 36) provided an unprecedented opportunity to create a robust positive control set that could be compared to locally acquired negative controls, enabling the establishment of assay sensitivity and specificity with the largest validation cohort of RT-PCR confirmed MVD recovered patients used for serological assay development to date.

To ensure robustness, two independent serological assays targeting different viral antigens and using distinct detection principles were developed: the anti-NPct MR assay and the GP ELISA. These methods were designed to cross-validate each other's performance in identifying past MARV infection. The anti-NPct MR assay utility and validation were extended to bats, MARV's natural animal reservoir, demonstrating superior performance and expanding its potential use for ecological studies.

Anti-NPct MR assay: validation and advantages

The MR assay format offers several key advantages over traditional immunoassays, including rapidity (approximately 30 min versus several hours for conventional ELISAs), simplicity through elimination of washing steps, and high-throughput capacity suitable for mass screening. Its diagnostic performance has been validated using the largest cohort of MVD recovery individuals to date, achieving sensitivity: 94.4%; (95% CI, 81.9–99.0%) and 99.7% specificity (95% CI, 98.1–100%). Importantly, the anti-NPct MR assay demonstrates no cross-reactivity with sera from Ebola virus disease recovered patients, ensuring specificity within the filovirus family.

The assay's design as an antibody isotype-nonspecific, species-agnostic method makes it particularly suitable for screening large numbers of samples across different host species. A unique strategic advantage lies in its use of the nucleoprotein C-terminal tail (NPct) as the target antigen. This antigen selection is critically important given that most existing MARV assays (14 of 15) used to establish the first WHO international standard for MARV antibodies rely on glycoprotein (GP) or whole MARV antigens, with only one NP-based immunofluorescence assay included.30 A direct head-to-head comparison with the IFA-based MARV NP assay was not performed in this study and would be valuable in future evaluations.

The NP-based approach enables differentiation between natural infection and vaccination in populations that have received MARV GP-based vaccines. Both the Marburg vaccine (cAd3-MARV GP) and the Ebola vaccine booster (MVA-BN-Filo) deployed in Rwanda produce MARV glycoprotein, making traditional GP-based serological assays principally unable to distinguish vaccine-induced antibodies from those resulting from natural infection. By selecting the viral nucleoprotein C-terminal tail as the target antigen, positive results necessarily indicate MARV infection rather than vaccine-induced immunity from current or previous filovirus vaccination campaigns.

Agreement with traditional anti-GP ELISA results

Since samples from the validation set were obtained from unvaccinated individuals, detection of anti-GP antibodies served as an independent method to confirm past MARV infection. The anti-NPct MR assay performance was characterised by comparing results with those obtained using a traditional ELISA format detecting human IgG antibodies against the viral glycoprotein ectodomain trimer, which achieved 94.4% sensitivity (95% CI, 81.9–99.0%) and 100% specificity (95% CI, 92.3%–100%).

The results demonstrate perfect concordance between both assays in the validation cohort, with both methods reliably detecting MARV-specific antibodies. This agreement provides confidence in the accuracy of both approaches and enables cross-validation when needed. However, in settings where GP-based vaccination has occurred, the anti-NPct MR assay serves as the primary diagnostic method, while anti-GP detection provides only supporting evidence, as GP antibodies could reflect either natural infection or vaccine-induced immunity. It is worth noting that we identified two RT-PCR–confirmed cases (5.4%) with no detectable anti-MARV antibodies on either assay. Both patients received remdesivir during MVD treatment, which may have reduced viral replication and, in turn, limited antigen exposure, potentially resulting in a weaker humoural response. Sample degradation or initial diagnostic errors are unlikely explanations for these findings, as sample integrity was strictly maintained during processing and storage at −80 °C. In addition, we reconfirmed the MVD diagnosis for these cases by RT-PCR, ruling out initial false-positive results. Consequently, the absence of detectable antibodies may reflect biological heterogeneity in immune responses rather than technical limitations. This interpretation is consistent with reports in EVD survivors, where seronegativity rate of 3% has been described.47 The concordance between our NP and GP assays further supports that these represent true serosilent cases or rapid antibody decline compared with others in the cohort.

Ecological applications

Detection of MARV in both human and ecological settings is critical to preventing spillovers and subsequent transmission among humans. The lack of appropriate validated tools has remained a major gap in filovirus preparedness. The MARV anti-NPct MR assay is uniquely suited for large-scale research in both humans and animals due to its species-agnostic design, simplicity, and high-throughput capacity.

The assay's validation in experimentally infected R. aegyptiacus bats demonstrated perfect discrimination with 100% sensitivity and 100% specificity. MARV-infected bat sera exhibited normalised RLU values averaging 30.2-fold higher than uninfected controls, providing clear analytical separation. This performance enables deployment for ecological investigations of MARV circulation in its natural reservoir, supporting spillover risk assessment and targeted prevention strategies. Furthermore, this assay will improve the implementation of integrated One Health research for the early detection of MARV, thus informing policymaking, and guiding the strategic planning and implementation of holistic pandemic preparedness, prevention, and response strategies.

Limitations and future considerations

Important limitations warrant consideration. The validation samples were collected 6–9 months post-infection, limiting understanding of antibody kinetics at different time points. Two RT-PCR confirmed cases (5.6%), both treated with remdesivir, tested negative by both serological assays, suggesting potential rapid antibody waning or deficient antibody response pattern in a small percentage of individuals.

One sample collected two years before the outbreak tested positive in the anti-NPct MR assay, raising questions about potential unrecognised prior exposure or low level (<0.5%) of non-specific reaction. First, Future studies should characterise the kinetic and durability of anti-NPct antibodies through longitudinal investigations and test performance across geographic regions. Second, head-to-head comparisons of NPct versus full-length NP (or NP-core) as ELISA coating antigens to determine the relative contributions of antigen-region selection versus assay format to diagnostic performance. Third, formal stability studies would establish the shelf-life of dried ELISA assay plates. Fourth, validation studies using wild-caught bat samples would also be valuable, as the current bat data were derived from experimentally infected animals. Finally, while this manuscript does not include data from vaccinated participants, subsequent analyses will evaluate and compare both assay performances within vaccinated populations to assess cross-reactivity and specificity.

Conclusions

We developed and validated two complementary serological tools for Marburg virus: a rapid, no-wash, species-agnostic anti-NPct MR assay and a conventional anti-GP ELISA. In RT-PCR–confirmed MVD recovered patients from Rwanda, the anti-NPct MR assay showed near-perfect discrimination (AUC 0.996), with 94.4% and 99.7% specificity, and no reactivity with Ebola convalescent sera. The GP ELISA independently confirmed prior infection in unvaccinated recovered patients (AUC 0.973; 94.4% sensitivity; 100% specificity), yielding concordant results for all the samples tested. In experimentally infected R. aegyptiacus, the anti-NPct MR assay achieved complete separation, supporting ecological applications. Together, these data address a critical diagnostic gap: distinguishing natural infection from GP vaccine-induced seropositivity. Pending longitudinal and multi-setting validation, a two-step algorithm, anti-NPct MR as the primary test with GP ELISA as orthogonal confirmation, can support outbreak response and follow-up of recovered patients, including in vaccinated populations.
