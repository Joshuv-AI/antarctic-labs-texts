Exploiting the potential of microbial consortia against sheath blight in basmati rice caused by Rhizoctonia solani
Authors: Mansi Verma, Shahid Ahmad, Vishal Gupta, Sushil Kumar Gupta, Zakir Amin, Suhail Ashraf, Fayaz Ahmad Mohiddin, Mona Saleh Al Tami, Khalid E Hamed, Mohammed Aladhad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSheath blight, caused by the necrotrophic fungus Rhizoctonia solani, poses a significant threat to Basmati rice production, leading to substantial yield and quality losses. Efforts were made to evaluate a microbial consortium comprising Trichoderma harzianum, Trichoderma asperellum, and Pseudomonas fluorescens against this disease. The compatibility of these strains was confirmed through prior assays, and the consortium was field-tested during the Kharif season of 2023–2024.Seed treatment with the consortium significantly reduced sheath blight incidence (54.08%), intensity (69.99%), lesion length (53.36%), and number of infected tillers (47.30%) relative to untreated controls. These reductions were comparable to those achieved with the chemical fungicide carbendazim, though the fungicide remained the most effective treatment overall. The consortium also improved growth and yield parameters, including panicle length (11.11%), plant fresh weight (15.23%), test weight (20.44%), and yield (49.52%) relative to untreated controls. Additionally, the microbial treatment enhanced key defense-related enzyme activities, with substantial increases observed for phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, total phenolic content, peroxidase, and polyphenol oxidase. Correlation analysis revealed that sheath blight incidence was strongly positively correlated with lesion length (r = + 0.97), disease intensity (r = + 0.98), and number of infected tillers (r = + 0.95), while strongly negatively correlated with yield (r = -0.99), plant fresh weight (r = -0.97), and test weight (r = -0.88). Defense enzyme activities showed moderate negative correlations with disease incidence (PAL: r = -0.57; TPC: r = -0.58; PO: r = -0.56; PPO: r = -0.57), indicating that higher enzyme activity is associated with reduced disease. These findings highlight the potential of microbial consortia as effective, sustainable alternatives to chemical fungicides for managing sheath blight while promoting basmati rice production.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s10123-026-00842-z.Keywords: Bamati, Biocontrol, Antagonism, Sheath blight, Enzyme assays

Introduction

Basmati rice (Oryza sativa L.), belonging to the family Poaceae, is a self-pollinated cereal crop renowned for its slender grains and aromatic qualities. Globally, rice is cultivated on 172.41 million hectares, producing 820.22 million tonnes at an average productivity of 4,757.3 kg/ha (FAOSTAT 2024). It is predominantly cultivated in the subtropical and warm temperate regions of the northwestern Indian subcontinent, including the foothills of the Himalayas. India is the largest exporter of Basmati rice, accounting for 65 per cent of the international trade (Sidhu et al. 2024). As the leading global exporter, India has shipped over 5.24 million metric tons of Basmati rice valued at approximately USD 5.84 billion during the fiscal year 2023–24 (Akhter and Haider 2020; APEDA 2025; Gupta et al. 2021). This rice type is recognized as a Geographical Indication, is predominantly cultivated in the northwestern region and the Indo-Gangetic foothills of the Himalayas, covering an area of approximately 2.13 million hectares with an annual production of 9.84 million tonnes (APEDA 2023; Mahajan et al. 2018). In India, it is traditionally grown in Punjab (812.39 thousand hectares), Haryana (787.60 thousand hectares), Uttar Pradesh (461.74 thousand hectares), Jammu & Kashmir (46.61 thousand hectares), Uttarakhand (19.35 thousand hectares) and Himachal Pradesh (7.62 thousand hectares) (Amin et al. 2023; APEDA 2023). Basmati rice cultivation plays a significant role in sustaining the livelihood of farmers in these regions, owing to its easy digestibility, fluffy texture, extended shelf life, pleasant taste, high elongation upon cooking, and distinctive aromatic qualities that ensure premium market returns (Sundaram et al. 2018). It contains a metallothionein-like protein that facilitates iron absorption, has a low glycaemic index, and is enriched with essential micronutrients such as iron and zinc (Atkinson et al. 2008; Verma et al. 2018).

Sheath blight (ShB) caused by basidiomycetous necrotrophic Rhizoctonia solani Kühn [Teleomorph-Thanatephorus cucumeris Frank (Donk)], is a devasting disease of rice as evident from the substantial losses reported as a result of this disease (Khoshkdaman et al. 2021). The symptoms of the disease typically appear on rice plants from the tillering stage through panicle initiation. The initial symptoms are small, oval, or irregular water-soaked lesions on the leaf sheaths near the water line. As the disease progresses, these lesions enlarge and develop a characteristic greenish-gray, bleached center with an irregular brown or purplish-brown border (Senapati et al. 2022). Yield losses can vary significantly, ranging from 25 to 69%, depending on the growth stage of the crop and the severity of the disease, (Ahamad and Khan 2023). The disease was first reported from Japan in 1910 (Miyake 1910), and has been causing the heavy yield losses in various countries. The disease is accountable for 24% yield loss in Philippines (Hossain et al. 2016), 5–15% in USA (Annou et al. 2005), 14–35% in Bangladesh (Ali et al. 2021), 30–50% in Pakistan (Abbas et al. 2019) and 50–60% in Arkansas (Yellareddygari et al. 2014). In India, ShB was first reported from Gurdaspur, after which it emerged as a major production constraint across several rice-growing states, including Haryana, Bihar, Eastern Uttar Pradesh, Jammu and Kashmir etc. (Singh et al. 2016). The pathogen is soil and water-borne and has a broad host range, infecting plant species across 32 different taxonomic families (Gangopadhyay and Chakrabarti 1982). The progression of sheath blight in rice is favoured by temperatures ranging between 26 and 34 °C and relative humidity around 80%.

Rhizoctonia solani is inherently highly variable and comprises 14 anastomosis groups (AGs), designated AG1 to AG13 and AGB1 (Carling et al. 2002). Among these, AG1-IA has been identified as the primary causal agent of sheath blight in rice (Carling et al. 2002). The genome of R. solani AG1-IA encodes numerous proteins, including small cysteine-rich effector candidates (MTPA), phenylacetic acid (PAA), and its derivatives, which enhance its virulence by disrupting host physical barriers, interfering with host physiological processes, and suppressing defense mechanisms (Costanzo et al. 2011; Liang and Rollins 2018; Sivalingam et al. 2006). During infection, increased activity of chitin deacetylase contributes to degradation of the host cell wall, thereby facilitating disease progression (Sivalingam et al. 2006). Management of sheath blight largely depends on synthetic fungicides and changes in cultural practices, and the lack of resistant rice varieties. However, the use of synthetic fungicides comes with several drawbacks, including residual toxicity (Teló et al. 2017), harm to beneficial microorganisms (Muñoz-Leoz et al. 2013; Teló et al. 2017), disruption of natural ecosystems (Beketov et al. 2013), environmental pollution, risks to human health (Kumar et al. 2015), and negative effects on soil fertility (Mishra et al. 2018). Due to excessive pesticide use, in recent years, export of basmati rice from India has been facing significant challenges due to changing food safety regulations and economic conditions in importing countries (Sidhu et al. 2024). Indian exporters often struggle with the non-recognition of testing methods and compliance standards, particularly for basmati rice, organic spices, and organic tea. Between 2014 and 2017, the United States reported 444 rejections for Indian basmati rice shipments, while the European Union issued over 40 rejection notifications from 2020 to 2023, mostly due to excessive pesticide residues detected in the consignments (Amin et al. 2024a, b; Kumar et al. 2018; Sidhu et al. 2024).

Use of biocontrol agents offers a potential and sustainable approach for management of plant diseases. Successful management of R. solani using antagonistic microorganisms has been reported in several crops including rice (Parveen et al. 2023b, 2025). Biocontrol agents employ diverse modes of action, including competition, antibiosis, hyper-parasitism, and induction of systemic resistance (ISR) by triggering defense-related enzymes such as peroxidase (POD), phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) (Berendsen et al. 2018; Gu et al. 2020; Ramachander Turaga 2020). Further, these beneficial strains activate ISR in host by triggering various antioxidant enzymes such as peroxidase (Podosokorskaya et al. 2013), phenylalanine ammonia lyase (PAL), and polyphenol oxidase (Sharma et al. 2024, 2025). The soil microbiome plays a crucial role in maintaining ecosystem functions, including the cycling of carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus, and iron, as well as promoting plant growth (Deng et al. 2019; Scarlett et al. 2021). Given the limitations of single-strain inoculants, which often show inconsistent field performance, there is a pressing need for multi-strain consortia that can exploit complementary or synergistic interactions (Alori and Babalola 2018; Chen et al. 2000; Mukherjee et al. 2021). The main aim of this study was to develop a liquid microbial consortium formulation composed of indigenous beneficial microbes that have both biostimulant and biocontrol properties for managing sheath blight in basmati rice. The findings from this research will support the development of effective microbial formulations to control sheath blight in this valuable crop.

Material and methods

The study was conducted during the kharif season of 2023 at the Research Farm of SKUAST-Jammu, Chatha (32°65.953’ N latitude; 74°80.6845’ E longitude). A randomized block design (RBD) was employed with three replications, and each plot measured 3 × 4 m2. The row-to-row spacing was maintained at 20 cm, while plant-to-plant spacing within each row was 15 cm. The experiment aimed to evaluate the efficacy of microbial consortia in managing sheath blight disease in rice.

Plant material and pathogen

The rice cultivar used in this study was Basmati 370, sourced from the Mega Seed Unit of SKUAST-Jammu. Rhizoctonia solani and other pure cultures were obtained from the Biocontrol Laboratory at SKUAST-Jammu. For mass multiplication, healthy wheat grains were used as a substrate. One hundred grams of wheat grains were washed, soaked overnight, drained, air-dried, and sterilized by autoclaving at 15 psi for 20 min. After cooling, sclerotia from a pure culture of R. solani were introduced aseptically inoculated on to the wheat grains followed by incubation in 500 mL Erlenmeyer flasks at 24 ± 1 °C for 15 days in a REMI RIS-24 orbital shaking incubator. The final inoculum was prepared by adding sterilized distilled water to the colonized grains.

Pathogenicity assay

Pathogenicity of Rhizoctonia solani was tested on rice cv. Basmati-370 following Koch’s postulates. Two-week-old seedlings were transplanted into plastic pots. At the tillering stage, plants were inoculated by placing a mixture of mycelium and sclerotia between leaf sheaths using a sterilized needle. Inoculated sheaths were covered with absorbent cotton, sealed with cellotape, and covered with butter paper bags to maintain humidity. One pot was kept as uninoculated control. Plants were observed daily for symptom development. After symptom appearance, re-isolation was performed from infected tissues and compared with the original culture.

Microbial inoculants

The biocontrol agents used in this study included Trichoderma harzianum-PF-055 (Accession No. MN721820), Pseudomonas fluorescens 9.F (Accession No. KJ194131), and a microbial consortium comprising T. harzianum-PF-055, T. asperellum- V188 (Accession No. MT395682), and P. fluorescens-9. F (Figs. 1, 2). These agents were pre-characterized for their biocontrol properties. The biocontrol agents were procured from Biocontrol Laboratory at SKUAST-Jammu.

Compatibility test

The compatibility between potential microbes was evaluated on three media, potato dextrose agar (PDA; HiMedia, Catalog No. M096; composition: potato infusion 200 g/L, dextrose 20 g/L, agar 15 g/L, pH 5.6 ± 0.2), nutrient agar (NA; HiMedia, Catalog No. M001; composition: peptone 5 g/L, beef extract 1.5 g/L, yeast extract 1.5 g/L, NaCl 5 g/L, agar 15 g/L, pH 7.4 ± 0.2), and PDA (50%) + NA (50%). These media were aseptically streaked with P. fluorescens vertically at a 90° angle relative to the colonies of B. subtilis. Concurrently, mycelial disc (5 mm) of T. asperellum and T. harzianum was placed in the center of petri plate. Each treatment was replicated three times. The inoculated petri plates were incubated at 28 ± 2 °C in BOD incubator. After incubation, the intersection points between the microbes were examined for the presence of inhibition zones (Royse and Ries 1978). Bacillus subtilis was not included in the final consortium as the study focused specifically on the combination of Trichoderma spp. and P. fluorescens based on their well-documented complementary mechanisms of action.

Sequence alignment and phylogenetic tree construction

Genomic DNA was extracted from pure cultures of Trichoderma harzianum, T. asperellum, and Pseudomonas fluorescens using CTAB method as per the method followed by Bandral et al. (2025). The quality and concentration of DNA were assessed using agarose gel electrophoresis (HiMedia) and spectrophotometry. For fungi, the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region was amplified using ITS1 and ITS4 primers (Parveen et al. 2023a), while for bacteria, the 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 27 F and 1492R (Eurofins Genomics India Pvt. Ltd., Bengaluru, India) (Parveen et al. 2025). The sequences obtained were edited and assembled using BioEdit v7.2.5 (Ibis Biosciences, Carlsbad, CA, USA; developed by Tom Hall) and compared to reference sequences in the NCBI GenBank database using BLASTn (National Center for Biotechnology Information, Bethesda, MD, USA; https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) to confirm species identity. Multiple sequence alignments were performed using ClustalW (European Bioinformatics Institute, Cambridge, UK; version 2.1) implemented in MEGA X (Molecular Evolutionary Genetics Analysis, Pennsylvania State University, State College, PA, USA; version 10.2.6) software (Tamura et al. 2011). Phylogenetic analyses were conducted using the Neighbor-Joining (NJ) method with 1000 bootstrap replicates to evaluate the robustness of tree topology (Felsenstein 1985). Evolutionary distances were computed using the Kimura 2-parameter model (Srivathsan and Meier 2012). The phylogenetic trees for T. harzianum, T. asperellum, and P. fluorescens were constructed separately and visualized as dendrograms with bootstrap values indicated at major branch points. The sequences generated in this study were deposited in GenBank under the accession numbers MN721820.1 (T. harzianum PF-055), MT395682.1 (T. asperellum V188), and KJ194131.1 (P. fluorescens 9. F).

Seed and seedling treatments

Prior to sowing, seeds of Basmati 370 were surface-sterilized and soaked for 20 min in suspensions of T. harzianum (1 × 10⁷ spores/ml), P. fluorescens (1 × 10⁸ cfu/ml), or the microbial consortium before being shade-dried. Seeds were sown on June 24, 2023. For seedling treatments, one-month-old seedlings were uprooted, washed thoroughly with tap water, and immersed in similar biocontrol suspensions for 20 min before transplantation on July 24, 2023.

Preparation of consortium suspensions

For all consortium treatments, the three microbial strains were combined into a single mixed suspension immediately before use. Individual stock suspensions of T. harzianum (1 × 10⁷ spores mL−1), T. asperellum (1 × 10⁷ spores mL−1), and P. fluorescens (1 × 10⁸ CFU mL−1) were prepared separately as described above. Equal volumes of each stock suspension were then mixed together in a sterile container and gently stirred for 2 min to ensure homogeneity. Seeds (T3), seedlings (T6), or foliar spray solution (T9) were then treated with this pre-mixed consortium suspension at the volumes specified in Table 1. Each seed/seedling/plant thus received all three strains simultaneously from a single soaking or spraying solution. Control seeds (T11) were treated with sterile distilled water containing 0.01% Tween 20 (no microbes).

Details of treatments used in the study

Pathogen inoculation

Pathogen inoculation was performed at the tillering stage (30 days post-transplanting) using a knapsack sprayer to apply R. solani inoculum to the sheath area approximately 1.5 cm above the water level.

Foliar applications

Seven days after pathogen inoculation, foliar applications of biocontrol agents on liquid carrier or carbendazim (50 WP) (Crystal Crop Protection,India Ltd.) at a concentration of 0.1% were carried out using a 3WBS-16A2 model electric air-pressure knapsack sprayer (Zhejiang Ousen Machinery Co., Ltd., China) fitted with twin hollow-cone nozzles. The sprayer had a 16‑liter tank capacity and operated with a pressure pump capable of delivering up to 4 bars, maintaining a flow rate of approximately 1.6 L min-−1. The lance measured 81 cm in length, providing a spray swath of nearly 2.5 m. The operation followed a swinging spray pattern with the nozzles positioned 0.5 m above the crop canopy to achieve effective deposition on leaf sheaths and minimize drift. Approximately 250 mL of treatment solution was applied per plot (3 × 4 m2). Detailed information aimed at managing sheath blight in rice is provided in Table 1.

Observations recorded

Observations were recorded concerning various parameters, including per cent disease incidence and % intensity (Rajput and Bartaria 1995), lesion length (cm) (Ravat et al. 2019), and number of infected tillers after 15 days post-inoculation. Panicle length was measured in centimetres before harvest, while plant fresh weight (g), test weight (g), and yield (qha−1) were recorded after harvest.

Effect of microbial consortia on the defence related enzymes

Fresh leaf samples were collected from each plot fifteen days post-inoculation to evaluate the activity of various defense-related enzymes. The assessment included measuring phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity, total phenolic content (TPC), peroxidase activity, and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity, following established protocols (Campos et al. 2004; Kumar and Khan 1982; Swain and Hillis 1959).

Phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity

The activity of phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) was assessed using a method based on Campos et al. (2004). The detailed protocol is discussed in Supplementary File S2. Absorbance at 290 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer, and PAL activity was calculated from a calibration curve using cinnamic acid as a standard, expressed as µmoles of cinnamic acid produced per gram of soluble protein per hour.

Total phenolic content (TPC)

Total phenolic content was determined following the method described by Swain and Hills (1959). The detailed protocol is discussed in Supplementary File S2. Absorbance at 650 nm was measured using a spectrophotometer, and total phenolic content was calculated from a calibration curve using gallic acid as a standard, expressed as mg/g.

Peroxidase activity

Peroxidase activity was evaluated according to Kumar and Khan’s methodology (1982) (Kumar and Khan 1982).The detailed protocol is discussed in Supplementary File S2. Peroxidase activity was calculated based on a calibration curve using pyrogallol as standard, expressed as units per minute per milligram of soluble protein.

Polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity

Polyphenol oxidase activity was measured following Kumar and Khan’s protocol (1982) (Kumar and Khan 1982). The detailed protocol is discussed in Supplementary File S2.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using ANOVA followed by Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT, p ≤ 0.05) in R software. The assumptions of ANOVA (normality and homogeneity of variances) were tested and met (Nasrizal et al. 2021). Additionally, correlation analysis was conducted to investigate the relationships between sheath blight incidence and several parameters, including disease intensity, lesion length, number of infected tillers, panicle length, plant fresh weight, test weight, yield, phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL) activity, total phenolic content (TPC), peroxidase activity, and polyphenol oxidase (PPO) activity. Correlation analysis was conducted using the ‘psych’ package, and figures were created using OriginPro 8.5 (Baranova and Sorokin 2017). Pearson correlation coefficients (r) were calculated to measure the strength and direction of linear relationships between each pair of variables. The analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.1; R Core Team, 2023) (Ahmad et al. 2023) with the following packages: ‘psych’ (version 2.2.5) for correlation coefficient calculation and significance testing (Revelle 2019), ‘corrplot’ (version 0.92) for visualization of the correlation matrix (Wei and Simko 2021), and ‘ggplot2’ (version 3.3.6) for generating scatter plots with fitted regression lines (Wickham 2016). Prior to analysis, data from all 11 treatments (T1–T11) across three replications were compiled (n = 33 per variable). Normality of each variable was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05 considered normal), and linearity was visually inspected using scatter plots (Hanusz et al. 2016). All variables met the assumptions for Pearson correlation analysis. For each correlation, the p-value was computed to test the null hypothesis that the true correlation coefficient is zero. Correlations with p < 0.05 were considered statistically significant, with significance levels indicated as * p < 0.05, ** p < 0.01, and *** p < 0.001.

Results

Pathogenicity assay

Rhizoctonia solani was confirmed as the causal agent of sheath blight. Seven days after inoculation, typical symptoms appeared as irregular, water-soaked lesions with grayish-to-brown margins on leaf sheaths. No symptoms were observed in control plants (Fig. 3). Re-isolation from symptomatic tissues yielded colonies identical to the original culture, fulfilling Koch’s postulates.

Phylogenetic analysis

To determine the evolutionary relationships of the biocontrol agents used in this study, molecular phylogenetic analyses were conducted. For each strain, the corresponding reference sequences were retrieved from GenBank under the following accession numbers: Trichoderma harzianum (MN721820.1), Trichoderma asperellum (MT395682.1), and Pseudomonas fluorescens (KJ194131.1). Sequence alignments were performed using MEGA X software and phylogenetic trees were reconstructed using the Maximum Composite Likelihood model, which accounts for both transitions and transversions. Evolutionary distances were computed as the number of base substitutions per site (Xu et al. 2025). The robustness of each tree topology was assessed by bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replicates, and bootstrap values are indicated at major branch points. The resulting dendrograms illustrating the phylogenetic placement of T. harzianum, T. asperellum, and P. fluorescens are provided in Supplementary Figs. 1, 2, and 3, respectively.

Effect of microbial consortium on incidence, intensity, lesion length and number of infected tillers of sheath blight

All treatments significantly reduced sheath blight incidence, intensity, lesion length, and number of infected tillers compared to the untreated control (T11). Among all treatments, foliar application of carbendazim (T10) was the most effective, recording the lowest disease incidence (19.98%), intensity (10.22%), lesion length (7.54 cm), and number of infected tillers (3.53). Seed treatment with the microbial consortium (T3) was the next most effective treatment. Compared to the untreated control (T11: 54.63% incidence, 44.16% intensity, 23.01 cm lesion length, 11.50 infected tillers), T3 significantly reduced disease incidence by 58.08%, intensity by 69.99%, lesion length by 53.36%, and infected tillers by 47.30%. The performance of T3 was statistically at par with seedling treatment with the consortium (T6) for all disease parameters. Single-strain treatments (T1, T2, T4, T5) and foliar consortium application (T9) were significantly less effective than the seed-treated consortium (T3) (Figs. 5, 6 and 7; Table 3).

Effect of microbial consortium on rice plant growth promotion and yield attributes

All treatments significantly improved growth and yield parameters compared to the untreated control (T11). Foliar application of carbendazim (T10) produced the highest values across all parameters, including panicle length (29.46 cm), plant fresh weight (201.10 g), test weight (22.83 g), and yield (45.13 q ha⁻1).

Seed treatment with the microbial consortium (T3) was the most effective biological treatment. Compared to the untreated control (T11: 25.83 cm panicle length, 168.13 g plant fresh weight, 18.06 g test weight, 21.20 q ha⁻1 yield), T3 significantly increased panicle length by 11.11%, plant fresh weight by 15.23%, test weight by 20.44%, and yield by 49.52%. The performance of T3 was statistically at par with seedling treatment with the consortium (T6) for panicle length, test weight, and yield. Single-strain treatments (T1, T2, T4, T5) and foliar consortium application (T9) were significantly less effective than the seed-applied consortium (T3). These results suggest that the combination of these microorganisms synergistically promoted plant growth by improving nutrient uptake and enhancing physiological processes. The statistical significance of these findings is highlighted by the different letters assigned to each treatment group, indicating that T3 was superior to individual treatments like T1 (seed treatment with T. harzianum) and T2 (seed treatment with P. fluorescens), which yielded lower values across all metrics (Table 4; Fig. 8).

All treatments influenced the activity of defense-related enzymes compared to the untreated control (T11). The consortium treatments (T3 and T6) induced substantially higher defense enzyme activities than the chemical fungicide carbendazim (T10), which recorded the lowest enzyme activities among all treatments except the control. Single-strain treatments (T1, T2, T4, T5) and foliar consortium application (T9) were significantly less effective than the seed-applied consortium (T3) in inducing defense-related enzymes (Table 5; Fig. 9).

Discussion

Basmati is a long, premium aromatic variety of rice grown in the foothills of Northern Himalayas region, with incredible export potential for Jammu & Kashmir. However, its susceptibility to sheath blight is a persistent challenge to its production and productivity causing significant yield losses from 25 to 69% (Ahamad and Khan 2023; Naik et al. 2017). Further, due to strict regulations on the maximum residue levels (MRL) of fungicides for export, alternative options for managing this disease are being explored (Sidhu et al. 2024). A chemical-free management strategy for sheath blight disease was assessed in this study. Compatibility assays performed beforehand demonstrated no antagonism among the bioagents, confirming that these strains were suitable for inclusion in a microbial consortium. Although this assay detects direct, contact-dependent antagonism, however, it does not assess other potential antagonistic mechanisms such as volatile organic compounds, siderophore-mediated iron competition, pH alteration, or temporal colonization order. Therefore, the absence of inhibition zones indicates no strong direct antagonism under these in vitro conditions, but does not guarantee complete absence of more subtle or environment-dependent antagonism in the rhizosphere. The compatibility of Trichoderma spp. with Pseudomonas fluorescens has been previously established (Dandurand and Knudsen 1993; Tomer et al. 2015).While foliar application of carbendazim significantly reduced ShB incidence and enhanced yield, its continuous use poses environmental and health risks (Kumar et al. 2018). The application of microbial consortia appeared to enhance various growth parameters and yield in rice plants which is well supported by earlier reports (Aksarah and Bangkele 2024). Seed treatment with the consortium reduced disease incidence, intensity, lesion length, and the number of infected tillers. Furthermore, it enhanced panicle length, plant fresh weight, test weight, and yield compared to the control. Our findings are consistent with previous reports demonstrating the efficacy of seed treatment with microbial consortia (Rahma et al. 2025). Furthermore, foliar application of microbial consortia has been reported to significantly suppress sheath blight and enhance growth parameters in rice (Singh et al. 2010). Previous studies support these findings by demonstrating that multi-strain bacterial consortia can lead to improved plant growth through synergistic interactions among the microbial strains as reported in earlier studies (Doni et al. 2022). Microbial consortia may improve plant growth through several mechanisms. First, microbes can enhance nutrient solubilization and availability to plants, which is crucial for optimal growth (Panneerselvam et al. 2020). Second, some microbes produce phytohormones such as auxins that can stimulate root elongation and overall plant vigor (Santoyo et al. 2021). Additionally, beneficial microbes can provide a protective effect against pathogens, reducing disease incidence and promoting healthier plants (Aksarah and Bangkele 2024).These results are in line with the earlier studies wherein the microbial bioagents have actively enhanced the growth characteristics of plants, while also reducing the disease incidence and severity (Bora 2019; Khadka and Uphoff 2019; Ram et al. 2022). The application of Trichoderma spp., and its effect in promoting growth and reducing the disease may be because of production of antimicrobial substances such as trichocellins, trichorovins, trichodecenins and trichotoxins A and B as well as cell wall-degrading enzymes (CWDEs) such as chitinases, proteases and glucanases that lead to the breakdown of host cell walls, leads to the parasitism of the target fungus (Pascale et al. 2017). Microbial consortium of Trichoderma spp. and Bacillus subtilis were significantly effective in suppressing sheath blight as compared to single inoculants (Ali and Nadarajah 2013). Further, seed treatment with microbial consortium (T. harzianum + T.asperellum + P. fluorescens) also induced significant enhancement in defence related enzymes in rice plants. Studies have demonstrated that microbial inoculation significantly boosts defense enzyme activities in plants, contributing to systemic resistance against diseases (Muthukumar et al. 2024; Singh et al. 2020).

In our study, the application of Trichoderma spp. enhanced PAL enzyme activity. PAL plays a critical role in the phenylpropanoid pathway, where it catalyzes the formation of cinnamic acid, leading to the synthesis of various phenolic compounds (Ushamalini et al. 2008). Additionally, PAL is a key enzyme in the salicylic acid biosynthesis pathway, which activates systemic acquired resistance against invading pathogens in plants (Das et al. 2023). Beyond PAL, other phenolic compounds and enzymes are integral components of plant defense mechanisms. Phenolic acids (PAA), peroxidase (PO), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), and total phenolic content (TPC) all contribute to resistance against pathogens (Kumari et al. 2023). PAA function as signalling molecules and antimicrobial agents, facilitating communication within the plant and between plants and microorganisms (Bisht et al. 2025; Maciel-Rodríguez et al. 2025). Meanwhile, TPC serves as an indicator of the plant’s overall antimicrobial potential, reflecting its ability to resist pathogenic invasions (Fu et al. 2025). Our findings are consistent with previous reports. Kabdwal et al. (2023) observed that rice plants treated with a combination of Trichoderma harzianum, Pseudomonas fluorescens, and herbal Kunapajala (as seed dressing, drenching, and foliar application) exhibited elevated activities of PAL, PO, and PPO compared to untreated controls (Kabdwal et al. 2023). Similarly, Elsharkawy et al. (2022) reported that different strains of Pseudomonas spp. enhanced PAL activity, PO and PPO production, and the expression of NPR1 genes in rice, which collectively improved growth, yield, and resistance against R. solani (Elsharkawy et al. 2022). Furthermore, Pseudomonas spp. have been shown to oxidize infected tissues into highly toxic quinones via the copper-containing enzyme polyphenol oxidase (PPO), thereby contributing to plant resistance against phytopathogens (Elsharkawy and El-Khateeb 2019).

Moreover, microbial consortia are known to induce systemic resistance more effectively than single microbial strains due to their diverse functional capabilities. This is supported by findings indicating that microbial consortia can enhance plant growth and stimulate defense responses through increased production of phenolic compounds and antioxidant enzymes (Negi et al. 2024; Santoyo et al. 2021; Sarma et al. 2015). The mechanisms underlying these enhancements often involve the activation of signalling pathways that regulate gene expression related to defense responses (Negi et al. 2024; Wu et al. 2023; Zahir et al. 2024). Studies have shown that plants treated with beneficial microbes exhibit elevated expression of genes associated with antioxidant defenses and stress responses, which help mitigate oxidative damage during pathogen attacks (Enebe and Babalola 2019; Kaur et al. 2022; Sahu et al. 2022).

Application of T. harzianum in the rhizosphere has been shown to increase root surface area, enabling roots to explore a larger soil volume and absorb more nutrients, leading to significant enhancement in growth and yield attributes of rice (Yedidia et al. 2001). Nandakumar and his co-workers (2001) also found enhanced plant height and yield using P. fluorescens as seed treatment. Correlation analysis among various factors such as intensity, lesion length and number of infected tillers had significantly positive correlation, whereas, yield, test weight, panicle length and plant fresh weight had also significantly but negative correlation with the incidence of ShB. França et al. (2015), observed that the combined application of T. asperellum isolates resulted in strongly negative correlation between the incidence of sheath blight and yield, panicle length and crop biomass of rice (de França et al. 2015). Further, correlation analysis of defense-related enzymes such as PAL, TPC, PO, and PPO activities with the incidence of sheath blight of rice had significantly negative but moderate correlation, possibly due to the reason that, the biocontrol agents exhibit the activation of several aromatic compounds consisting of phenolics, which play important role in the defense mechanism by strengthening the plant cell wall and altering its physiological and metabolic reactions leading to more resistance in plants towards infections (Mondal et al. 2012). While the present study clearly demonstrates the efficacy of the microbial consortium, it was conducted over a single season at one location. While the results from this study are highly promising, further multi-location and multi-season field trials may boost the understanding of its efficacy across diverse agro-ecological conditions. This was a deliberate first-step validation under controlled conditions, and the results are statistically conclusive. Future molecular studies based on gene expression analysis could complement the robust enzyme activity data reported here.

Conclusion

This study revealed that among the biocontrol formulations, seed treatment with microbial consortia (T. harzianum + T. asperellum + P. fluorescens) proves to be a promising approach to reduce the incidence and intensity of sheath blight and also increased the growth and yield along with the induction of defence related enzymes in rice. These results emphasize the potential role of microbial consortium in enhancing the production of basmati rice in a sustainable way. For farmers, this consortium offers a chemical-free alternative that reduces reliance on synthetic fungicides, lowers input costs over time, and minimizes health and environmental risks. For exporters, the use of this biological product can help meet the stringent maximum residue level (MRL) regulations imposed by major importers such as the European Union and the United States, thereby reducing the risk of shipment rejections due to pesticide residues. The microbial consortium described in this study is currently at the pre-commercial validation stage and will be commercialized after undergoing multi location and multi seasonal trials. While these results provide strong evidence for the effectiveness of the microbial consortia, focus on gene expression studies (such as qPCR or transcriptome analysis) in future would help to clarify which specific genes and signalling pathways are activated in the rice plants in response to these treatments.
