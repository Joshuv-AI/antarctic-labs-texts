Sleep Apnea and Cardiovascular Outcomes in Patients Undergoing Coronary Artery Bypass Grafting
Authors: Yao Hao Teo, Yao Neng Teo, Cai Ling Yong, Justin Chan, Wilson W. Tam, Chieh-Yang Koo, Chi-Hang Lee
Published: 2024

Sleep apnea is a prevalent chronic disorder associated with coronary artery disease and arrhythmia.1,2 Defined as apnea-hypopnea index of ≥15 events per hour, sleep apnea affects over 400 million people across the globe.3 Recent evidence suggests that untreated sleep apnea is associated with adverse outcomes in patients undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention.4 We previously reported that, in a cohort of 1,007 Asian patients undergoing nonemergent coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), untreated sleep apnea was associated with a 1.57-fold higher risk of developing major adverse cardiac and cerebrovascular events (MACCE) during a median follow-up of 2 years.5 In this report, we presented the extended median follow-up of 6.1 years (IQR: 5.0-7.3 years). The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been previously published.5 Recruited patients were aged 18-90 years and were slated for nonemergent CABG. We excluded patients who were already on regular treatment for known sleep apnea or those who were clinically or hemodynamically unstable. Before their CABG procedure, eligible patients underwent an overnight sleep study to assess the presence and severity of sleep apnea. The study was approved by the Domain Specific Review Board-C 2016/01132. All of the participants have provided written informed consent.

From 2013-2018, 1,007 Asian patients underwent a hospital-based overnight sleep study using a wrist-worn portable device approved by the USA Food and Drug Administration (Watch-PAT 200, Itamar Medical) before the CABG. Of these patients, 513 (50.9%) were diagnosed with sleep apnea (based on an apnea-hypopnea index of ≥15 events/hour). The body mass index (25.9 [IQR: 23.3-28.6] kg/m2 vs 23.5 [IQR: 21.8–26.2] kg/m2; P < 0.0001), neck circumference (39 [IQR: 37-41] cm vs 38 [IQR: 36-40] cm; P < 0.0001), and waist circumference (95 [IQR: 88-102] cm vs 90 [IQR: 84-96] cm; P < 0.0001) were higher in the sleep apnea group than the non-sleep apnea group. The extended follow-up was conducted prospectively through a combination of clinic visits, medical record reviews, and telephone contact.

We used SPSS for the Cox regression and then the R package “cmprsk” for the competing risk regression.6 We generated MACCE-free survival curves using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared them between the sleep apnea and non–sleep apnea groups using the log-rank test. To account for potential confounders and compare the time to MACCE occurrence between the 2 groups, we conducted a Cox proportional hazards regression analysis and a competing risk regression. The multivariate Cox regression model included covariates selected based on clinical relevance, such as age, sex, body mass index, left ventricular ejection fraction, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, chronic kidney disease, and excessive daytime sleepiness, and a backward selection method was used to form the final model. All patients were prospectively followed up until January 30, 2024, and statistical significance was defined as a2-sided P value of <0.05.

Over the follow-up period, averaging 6.1 years, a total of 162 (16.1% of 1,007) patients died, with 95 (18.5% of 513) patients in the sleep apnea group and 67 (13.6% of 494) patients in the non–sleep apnea group. Among the entire cohort, comprising both sleep apnea and non–sleep apnea groups, 253 patients (25.1% of 1,007) experienced MACCE, which included 70 cardiovascular deaths (7.0%), 100 (9.9%) nonfatal myocardial infarctions, 100 nonfatal strokes (9.9%), and 75 (7.45%) unplanned revascularizations.

In the MACCE-free survival analysis, compared with non–sleep apnea, sleep apnea was associated with a lower mean MACCE-free survival time (7.6 vs. 8.1; P = 0.015) (Figure 1), and a lower overall survival time (8.7 vs. 9.0; P = 0.028) (Figure 1). In the Cox regression analysis, compared with non–sleep apnea, sleep apnea was found to be associated with a higher incidence of MACCE, with an unadjusted HR of 1.30 (95% CI: 1.01-1.67; P = 0.042). In the competing risk regression, sleep apnea similarly demonstrated an increased hazard rate of 1.36 (95% CI: 1.06-1.74; P = 0.016) for MACCE. In the competing risk regression analysis with adjustment for covariates, sleep apnea remained independently associated with MACCE (adjusted HR: 1.29; 95% CI: 1.00-1.66; P = 0.046). Furthermore, in comparing heart failure hospitalization, compared with non–sleep apnea, sleep apnea was associated with a lower mean heart failure hospitalization-free survival time (8.3 vs 9.2; P < 0.001). Compared with non–sleep apnea, sleep apnea was associated with an unadjusted HR of 1.62 (95% CI: 1.13-2.33; P = 0.009) in the Cox regression, and 1.98 (95% CI: 1.41-2.77; P < 0.001) competing risk regression, for heart failure hospitalization. In the competing risk regression with adjustment for covariates, sleep apnea remained independently associated with heart failure hospitalization (adjusted HR: 1.64; 95% CI: 1.14-2.35; P = 0.007). These data suggest a large effect size between sleep apnea and heart failure hospitalization.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the largest cohort study with the longest follow-up duration on the association between sleep apnea and cardiovascular outcomes after CABG. Our study demonstrates that in patients undergoing nonemergent CABG, sleep apnea is associated with a 30% increased hazard rate for MACCE and a 60% increased hazard rate for heart failure hospitalization. To date, the totality of the evidence strongly suggests that untreated sleep apnea is associated with adverse cardiovascular outcomes in patients undergoing percutaneous or surgical coronary revascularization.7 Although continuous positive airway pressure has not delivered promising outcomes so far, previous trials have limitations, and there are non–positive airway pressure treatments available.8
