Prebiotic Roles of Formaldehyde as an Activating Agent and a Building Block in Solid-State Peptide Modification
Authors: Jamie C. Thuan, Anuja Koirala, James Pirez, Jun Ohata
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Signal transduction by gaseous small molecules is an essential process in modern living systems, where the gasotransmitters relay cellular signals to bio­(macro)­molecules through covalent bond formation. However, the origin or a primordial version of such signaling events in abiotic worlds has been poorly understood to date. Through examination of chemical reactivities between formaldehyde and cyclic dipeptide/diketopiperazine derivatives in prebiotically relevant solid-state environments, this study demonstrates that the gaseous small molecule may serve not only as a mere building block for the abiotic construction of biomolecules but also as an activating agent that transforms the inert peptide into reactive, prebiotically important chemical species. In addition, superiority of solid-state reactions or mechanochemistry to solution-based reaction conditions described in this article may be an indication of potential significance of the mechanical force-induced chemistry for chemical evolution, in particular for abiotic emergence of polypeptides.

Introduction

Gaseous signaling molecules are essential in all of the modern life forms, and their diverse roles in the abiotic world have been also increasingly studied to decipher potential pathways for origin of biomolecules. Notwithstanding their well-known toxicity at high concentration, numerous physiological signaling pathways in modern cells are regulated by gaseous molecules called gasotransmitters such as nitric oxide, carbon monoxide, hydrogen sulfide, and hydrogen cyanide. , At the same time, a series of astrochemical and geochemical evidence suggested the ubiquity of such gaseous molecules in various environments, and those molecules have been extensively studied in the context of prebiotic chemistry (e.g., observation of NO, CO, H2S, and HCN in abiotic conditions). Notably, the carbon-containing gasotransmitters are also known to be transformed to another class of gaseous carbon building block, formaldehyde. ,

Formaldehyde was recently identified as another class of gaseous molecules for cell signaling with precise control of its production and consumption in cellular environments, while its unregulated reactivities in prebiotically relevant aqueous conditions were shown to often produce complex mixtures (Figure ). With its concentration highly regulated by enzymes and other cellular machinery, the electrophilic nature of the simplest carbonyl compound has been revealed to enable diverse physiological and pathological events including folate metabolism, , interactions with amino acids, , cysteine hydroxylmethylation on polypeptides, , and nucleotide methylation. , The abundance of formaldehyde in the modern biological systems (e.g., its intracellular and blood concentrations can reach 0.2–0.5 and 0.05–0.1 mM, respectively) mirrors its presence in numerous abiotic environments spanning geochemical conditions, meteorites, , a comet, interstellar medium, and another planet. Because a carbon building block would be indispensable in conceivable origin-of-life scenarios regarding carbon-based life forms like those on the Earth, a number of studies has examined formaldehyde in prebiotically relevant contexts, demonstrating its various adduct formation and oligomerization products (Figure A). − A seminal example includes formation of ribose that is a key component of RNA, although the aqueous self-condensation of formaldehyde (i.e., formose reaction) was also shown to produce a number of other noncanonical saccharides such as xylose and threose. As such, while there is a report of a unique catalytic activity of formaldehyde in prebiotically relevant aqueous conditions, , formaldehyde reactivity in solution states tends to lead to multiple mixtures of products if there is no enzymatic or cellular control. Given that formaldehyde could be a key molecule not only in modern biological systems but also in an origin-of-bio­(macro)­molecule scenario, we hypothesized that there may be a natural mechanism for formaldehyde to react and behave in a more controlled manner such as solid-state conditions.

Among many plausible chemical-evolution scenarios, mechanical force-mediated reactions under solid-state conditions may account for the nonenzymatic, prebiotic emergence of biomolecules (Figure A). By recognizing the abovementioned difficulty of selective formation of canonical biomolecule in simple aqueous conditions, unique reaction environments were proposed as media facilitating the origin of the biomolecules, including hydrothermal vents, wet–dry cycles, coacervates, heat flows, and so on. Mechanochemistry is another prebiotically plausible process under solid-state environments, where chemical reactions are driven by external mechanical force. , In other words, mechanochemical reactions do not always necessitate solvents for chemical transformation to occur, which may be beneficial to suppress decomposition of constructed biomolecules or biomacromolecules under aqueous conditions (i.e., hydrolysis) in abiotic worlds. Such mechanical force would include extraterrestrial impacts that were probably common in the early Earth during the Late Heavy Bombardment period − as well as in other planets. , Meteorite impacts are known to create hydrothermal vents as well, and such extraterrestrial impacts might have been crucial for laying the foundation of hydrothermal vent-driven chemical-evolution events. Lithospheric activity would be another type of prebiotically plausible events generating mechanical force (e.g., plate tectonics and other lithospheric events in the Earth and other planets). − This work investigates the solid-state reactivity of formaldehyde with a peptide substrate frequently found in prebiotic samples/conditions.

Cyclic dipeptide or diketopiperazine (DKP) derivatives are observed in many prebiotically relevant conditions and might be potential building blocks for abiotic biomacromolecule formation (Figure B,C). The molecular scaffold of DKP is common in bioactive compounds of the modern prokaryotes, − and the parent DKP or cyclic glycine dimer 1a (also called glycine anhydride) was observed under numerous abiotic samples as well as simulated prebiotic conditions (e.g., various aqueous conditions, − simulated interstellar ice, and solid-state reactions , ). Noteworthy, cyclic glycine dimer is one of the few peptides observed in astrochemical samples to date. , Even if formation of cyclic dipeptides is often regarded as an unproductive pathway of polypeptide synthesis through coupling of amino acid building blocks, its productive actions potentially related to prebiotic chemistry have been recently reported including its oligomerization to form acyclic polypeptide, ,, photodereamization, and formation of dehydroalanine from acetylated derivatives (1b). Yet, studies of DKP derivatives as building blocks in chemical evolution have not been pursued extensively so far.

We discovered that, through dual actions of formaldehyde, cyclic glycine dimer can be converted to prebiotically valuable peptide building blocks, dehydroalanine derivatives, under solid-state reaction conditions. Because dehydroalanine reactivities have been increasingly recognized as key to abiotic polypeptide formation during the past few years, − the rapid formation of dehydroalanine derivatives on the DKP scaffolds reported in numerous synthetic chemistry literature (Figure C, shaded yellow) may be indicative of its importance and possibility in prebiotic chemistry. However, it is worth noting that a literature search using Reaxys showed no examples of such condensation reactions to form the parent dehydroalanine cyclic dimer with formaldehyde (Figures S1–S5). Our hypothesis was that the promiscuous nature of formaldehyde in a solution state may be attributed to the lack of the precedents, and indeed, our results demonstrated that solid-state reaction conditions offered more efficient, selective formation of the dehydroalanine-based DKP than solution-state reactions. This dehydroalanine formation requires the presence of acyl groups on the amide nitrogens of DKP, and our finding implied that formaldehyde may be acting as an activating group of the cyclic glycine dimer to eventually facilitate the process through the N-to-O-acyl transfer mechanism (Figure C) and decrease of pK a of the α protons.

Experimental Section

General Information

Materials and Reagents

All chemicals were purchased from commercial suppliers unless otherwise noted, including paraformaldehyde, salts, acyclic glycine derivatives, acetamide, N-hydroxylacetamide, and glyoxylic acid. All chemical synthesis procedures were performed in air unless otherwise noted. Cyclic serine dimer (#A296222) and N-acetyl cyclic glycine dimer (1b, #A157857) were purchased from Ambeed. Cyclic glycine dimer (or glycine anhydride, 1a, no. 120080050) was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Paraformaldehyde-d 2 (#394513) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. 16% paraformaldehyde solution (#15710S) was purchased from Electron Microscopy Sciences.

Instrumentation

Solid-State Milling

Mechanochemical reactions were performed on an MM500 Vario (Retsch). For reaction screening purposes, stainless steel balls (from Amazon vendors such as uxcell and QCQIANG) and a 1.7 mL Eppendorf tube (Axygen, MCT175C) with tube holders (#22.008.0008, Retsch) were used. For preparative synthesis purposes, 5 mL stainless steel jars (Retsch, #01.462.0231) with stainless steel balls were used instead of the Eppendorf tube and tube holder.

NMR

NMR was performed on Bruker Avance NEO 500, 600, and 700.

IR

Infrared spectroscopy was performed on an Agilent Technologies FTIR Cary 630.

X-ray Crystallography

The crystal structures of C6H6N2O2 (cyclic dehydroalanine dimer, 1c) and C6H8N2O4 (N-hydroxylmethylated DKP, 1d) were analyzed using single-crystal X-ray diffraction at 100(2) K on a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer. Data for each crystal were gathered with a Photon III detector operating in mixed mode, utilizing Mo Kα radiation (λ = 0.71073 Å) sourced from a graphite monochromator. Individual crystals were carefully selected, mounted on a transparent loop coated with Paratone-N oil, and secured on a goniometer head for data collection. A preliminary 180-frame scan was conducted to evaluate the crystal quality. The APEX3 software suite was employed for unit cell determination and data collection. Collection parameters included a voltage of 50 kV, a current of 1.4 mA, 2 s frame exposures, a 0.5° frame width, and a 50 mm detector-to-crystal distance. APEX3 was also used for lattice parameter refinement and data integration, while absorption corrections were applied using the multiscan method via SADABS software.

The crystal structure of C7H8N2O3 (cyclic N-acetylglycine dehydroalanine, 1e) was analyzed using single-crystal X-ray diffraction at 150(2) K on a Bruker D8 Venture diffractometer. Data for each crystal were gathered with a Photon III detector operating in mixed mode, utilizing Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54178 Å) sourced from a graphite monochromator. Individual crystals were carefully selected, mounted on a transparent loop coated with Paratone-N oil, and secured on a goniometer head for data collection. A preliminary 180-frame scan was conducted to evaluate crystal quality. The APEX4 software suite was employed for unit cell determination and data collection. Collection parameters included a voltage of 50 kV, a current of 1.4 mA, 10 to 80 s theta-dependent frame exposures, a 2.0° frame width, and a 37 mm detector-to-crystal distance. APEX3 was used for lattice parameter refinement and data integration, while absorption corrections were applied using the multiscan method via SADABS software.

Lyophilization

Lyophilization was performed on a Labconco Freeze Zone 4.5 L Benchtop Freezer Dryer.

Experimental Procedures

General Procedure for Solid-State Reaction Using a Ball Mill

Details of the equipment, accessories, and consumables are described in the instrumentation section. To a 1.7 mL Eppendorf tube, one 3.0 mm ball and two 2.0 mm balls were placed using tweezers. Powders of reactants such as diketopiperazine derivatives (typically 5 mg) were placed in the tube by using a weighing paper. For the reaction with potassium thioacetate, potassium thioacetate was placed in the tube using a fume hood before other powders because of its foul odor. The tubes with all of the powders were placed in a tube rack and then into a ball mill. The reactions were milled at 30 Hz without any temperature control. After the milling process, solvents such as deuterated solvents for NMR analysis were added, solid and liquid were separated using a countertop microcentrifuge, and the supernatant was used for analysis. Negligible reactions of powders in DMSO-d 6, which is a prebiotically irrelevant medium, without milling processes were confirmed to make sure that the observed chemical transformation is due to the milling process rather than solution incubation in the deuterated solvent before/during the NMR analysis.

Aqueous Reactions Using Paraformaldehyde Solution

A diketopiperazine derivative (5.0 mg, 0.025 mmol) and sodium acetate (5.0 mg, 0.061 mmol) were placed in a 4 mL glass vial equipped with a stir bar. Water (0.7 mL) was added, and the powders were dissolved using sonication. 16% paraformaldehyde solution (0.0287 mL, 0.167 mmol) was added using a mechanical pipette, and the reaction mixture was stirred at rt for a given time. At the end of the time point, the reaction mixture was frozen using liquid nitrogen, and all of the volatiles were removed through lyophilization overnight. To the dried reaction mixture, DMSO-d 6 containing internal standard (0.75 mL total. 6.67 mM of 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene) was added, solid and liquid were separated using a countertop microcentrifuge, and the supernatant was analyzed by NMR.

Reactions in DMSO Using Solid Paraformaldehyde

A diketopiperazine derivative (5.0 mg, 0.025 mmol), solid paraformaldehyde (5.0 mg, 0.167 mmol), and sodium acetate (5.0 mg, 0.061 mmol) were placed in a 2 mL plastic Eppendorf tube equipped with a stir bar. DMSO-d 6 containing internal standard (0.75 mL total, 6.67 mM of 1,3,5-trimethoxybenzene) was added, and the suspension was stirred at rt for a given time. At the end of the time point, the solid (including paraformaldehyde) was separated by a countertop microcentrifuge, and the supernatant was analyzed by NMR.

Results and Discussion

The parent diketopiperazine (DKP) or cyclic glycine dimer (1a) was found to undergo N-hydroxylmethylation reactions with paraformaldehyde in solid-state conditions (Figure A–C), which would be a key first step for the activation of the DKP scaffold. We first performed reactions between the two prebiotically relevant molecules DKP 1a and paraformaldehyde under solid-state conditions by using a ball mill. The ball mill has been used as a mimic of mechanical force-causing prebiotic events, ,, and we adopted a similar reaction setup as the precedents (i.e., use of stainless steel balls at 30 Hz frequency). Milling of DKP 1a and paraformaldehyde in the presence of a carbonate salt (Na2CO3) at rt for 8 h afforded N-hydroxymethylated DKP (1d). Except the mass spectrometry (MS)-based characterization of the product as a literature precedent also indicated challenges of MS analysis of DKP derivatives, we were able to characterize the molecule through various methods such as NMR spectroscopy (1H, 13C, and 1H–13C HSQC), infrared (IR) spectroscopy, and X-ray crystallography. In addition, NMR analysis (in D2O) of the reaction mixture with deuterated paraformaldehyde demonstrated the disappearance of the peak corresponding to the hydroxylmethyl group in the product, and this observation is also corroborative evidence of product formation (Figure C and Figure S6). Even though hemiaminal derivatives tend to be hydrolytically quite unstable in general, the product showed stability in aqueous conditions (e.g., NMR experiments in D2O showing no signs of rapid decomposition, use of aqueous solution during purification, and crystallization using aqueous methanol), which is consistent with a previously reported a peptide containing N-hydroxymethylated amide. No detectable level of the aldol condensation products (i.e., dehydroalanine-based DKP 1c) was observed in NMR analysis of reaction mixtures (Figure A), highlighting the importance of the acyl-transfer mechanism (Figure C) and installation of the hydroxylmethyl group as an acyl moiety precursor.

The solid-state hydroxylmethylation processes are effective with various salt compositions, in particular, toward the cyclic dipeptide over acetylated amino acids and peptides (Figure D–F). Because of the lack of significant UV absorbance and their hydrophilic nature, liquid chromatography-based analysis proved to be unfruitful for reactions of the DKP derivatives described in this paper, and 1H NMR was employed as a major analytical method by using peak areas of a starting material and product in spectra. While a carbonate salt initially selected as a base would be present in many prebiotically relevant environments, − we also examined different types of sodium salts to assess the generality of the hydroxylmethylation reaction. In the tested conditions at rt for 8 h, all the salts increased the reactivity compared to the one without any salt additives (Figure D and Figure S7). The reactivity enhancement was especially articulated with stronger bases such as carbonate, acetate, and phosphate, which is presumably due to the necessity of deprotonation of the amide N–H proton at a certain point of the reaction mechanism. Even a relatively strong acid, bisulfite, was able to produce the expected product in this time scale, and it may be possible that this reaction could occur in various prebiotic conditions as well. We did not observe substantial effects by different counter cations (Figure E and Figure S8). The hydroxylmethylation reaction proved to be possible even under aqueous conditions as well (Figure S9). A complex mixture was produced with glycine amino acid bearing an amine group (2), as one of the product was identified as glyoxylic acid, which would be a result of an aldehyde-mediated demination reaction (Figure S10). Whereas the simpler amide compound acetamide (3) was amenable to the solid-state hydroxylmethylation reaction (Figure F and Figure S11), NMR analysis of reaction mixtures for acetylated glycine (4) and linear glycine dimer (5) showed peaks of the intact glycine derivatives, underscoring the potential significance of the cyclic dipeptide structure over the acyclic derivatives (Figure S12).

Oxidation of the hydroxymethylated amide to the corresponding imide (i.e., acylated DKP) would be prebiotically feasible based on literature precedents, but we faced technical challenges to experimentally prove the postulation due to safety concerns. Redox conversion between the hydroxymethylated amide to acyl amide has been reported in a modern peptide report, and an oxidative process has been proposed in origin-of-life literature as well. In fact, oxidation processes of methanol are considered one of the formaldehyde generation mechanisms under hydrothermal vents, and a recent report also described plausible transformation of methanol to formaldehyde in simulated interstellar conditions. Though concentrations of oxygen and oxidizing molecules were traditionally considered quite low in many prebiotically relevant conditions, a multitude of recent literature suggest the importance of oxygen and oxidation in abiotic worlds. − In particular, mechanochemical generation of oxygen species including hydrogen peroxide under abiotic conditions was demonstrated, , and the findings also suggest the mechanical force-induced oxidation of the hydroxymethylated DKP in a solid state would be possible. When we attempted to experimentally validate the hypothesis of oxidation of a DKP derivative with a solid oxidant (urea hydrogen peroxide and/or iron sources) using the ball mill, violent reactions with breakage of the reaction container/lid were observed even at a small scale (10 mg of DKP and 40 mg of oxidant). Indeed, safety concerns of ball milling was described recently including processes with peroxide-based molecules. With a wealth of evidence about the plausibility of prebiotic oxidation processes and safety considerations, we did not further pursue experimental validations of any oxidative conversion of the N-hydroxylmethyl DKP (1d) to the acyl derivative.

Acylated DKP (1b)the potential oxidation product from N-hydroxylalkylated DKPcan cause rapid condensation reactions with paraformaldehyde in the solid state forming a dehydroalanine peptide (Figure ). In order to test the hypothesis of prebiotically plausible formation of dehydroalanine assisted by the acyl-transfer mechanism (Figure C), we used a commercially available N-acetyl cyclic glycine dimer (1b) as a model substrate. While the parent diketopiperazine (cyclic glycine dimer, 1a) did not show any signs of condensation reaction products after the extended reaction time (8 h as the reaction scheme shown in Figure A), acylated DKP 1b underwent rapid formation of dehydroalanine with paraformaldehyde within 10 min based on NMR analysis of a reaction mixture. The cyclic dipeptide dimer with dehydroalanine and N-acetylglycine units (1e) was purified from the reaction mixture through silica gel column chromatography and characterized by NMR spectroscopy, IR spectroscopy, and preliminary X-ray crystallography (Figure A). For instance, the formation of the single α,β-unsaturated carbonyl unit on the diketopiperazine scaffold was indicated by the 1H NMR spectrum where two peaks in the alkene region and amide NH proton emerged with conservation of the glycine methylene and acetyl protons in the upfield region (Figure B). Similar to the results of the abovementioned hydroxylmethylation reaction, this condensation reaction also proved efficient with different basic salts such as carbonates and acetate, which may be implying likelihood of occurrence of those reactions in various prebiotically possible conditions (Figure C and Figure S13). It is unclear as to why the reaction did not proceed efficiently with the phosphate base (Na2HPO4), and possibly, this observation may be related to physical properties of phosphate salts. Even though the condensation reaction can also proceed in aqueous solution to a certain extent, multiple unidentified peaks were observed in the NMR analysis, and the overall NMR yields of aqueous conditions as well as prebiotically irrelevant organic solvent conditions were inferior to that by the mechanochemical/solid-state conditions (Figure D and Figures S14–S16). This observation is consistent with our hypothesis that promiscuous and uncontrollable nature of formaldehyde behaviors in solution could be suppressed through solid-state chemical events.

In consistency with known reactivity of acyclic dehydroalanine derivatives in prebiotically plausible conditions, a dehydroalanine-based cyclic dipeptide was capable of forming a cysteine derivative with a sulfur nucleophile (Figure A). Encouraged by the formation of dehydroalanine through the acyl-transfer-driven condensation reaction, we next investigated whether the dehydroalanine unit on the diketopiperazine scaffold would be transformed to a derivative of a canonical amino acid/peptide. Cyclic dehydroalanine dimer (1c) was used for the model study instead of the monodehydroalanine derivative (1e), owing to the difficulty for preparative scale synthesis of the monodehydroalanine derivative (1e) that is not very soluble in solvents for purification. Using a previously described method with technical modifications, cyclic dehydroalanine dimer (1c) was prepared through standard, solution-state organic synthesis from commercially available cyclic serine dimer and characterized by NMR, IR and X-ray crystallography (Figure B). While the organic synthesis afforded a sufficient amount of the compound, this dehydroalanine dimer (1c) is also poorly soluble in aqueous solution (Figure A), and even though the low solubility represents challenges for synthetic purposes, the characteristics of those dehydroalanine-based DKPs may have been important in abiotic conditions to prevent unwanted decomposition or side reactions in aqueous primordial soup. In other words, the dehydroalanine-based DKPs may preferably undergo solid-state reactions over solution-state ones in abiotic worlds, perhaps offering the class of molecules for more selective, controlled chemical transformation opportunities in a solid state. Thioacetate was chosen as a prebiotically relevant nucleophile for the proof-of-concept examination, , as its reaction with dehydroalanine derivatives should afford acetylated cysteine that would be readily converted to cysteine due to hydrolytic instability of thioester groups. Initial reaction screening with various salts under solid-state conditions showed that formation of the expected product (1f) is particularly effective with monobasic phosphate (Figure C orange bars and Figures S16 and S17). This observation may be consistent with existing literature of organic chemistry and chemical biology that also preferably used phosphate salts for dehydroalanine derivative reactions. , The conditions with other types of salts were able to be facilitated by the addition of the monobasic phosphate as well (Figure C green bars and Figures S17 and S18). It is notable that the reactions proceeded cleanly as the major peaks correspond only to the starting material (1c) and expected product (1f) in the NMR spectra for reaction mixtures (Figure E). A set of phosphate salts were then tested, and reaction conversions based on NMR analysis suggested the preference of monobasic phosphate salts regardless of counter cations (Figure D and Figure S19). For example, the striking difference of reaction conversions was observed between monobasic sodium phosphate and dibasic sodium phosphate (or monobasic aluminum phosphate vs tribasic aluminum phosphate). The reactivity trend with a set of phosphate salts may be related to the importance of the acidity of salts during the reaction.

Previous literature on DKP derivatives demonstrated that the cyclic dipeptides can be converted to corresponding acyclic peptides, and chemical species described in this work would be reasonably capable of conversion into acyclic canonical amino acids and peptides as well. Hydrolysis of the backbones of biomolecules is often considered common abiotic events, and hydrolysis of cyclic dipeptides into linear dipeptides or monomeric amino acids in aqueous solutions has been commonly practiced. − Even solid-state or mechanochemical conditions can induce the ring-opening process, which, in fact, demonstrated not only formation of the simple hydrolysis product (i.e., linear glycine dimer) but also oligomerization of cyclic glycine dimer into a linear 10-mer peptide. In addition, potentials of cysteine and its peptides for prebiotically plausible peptide formation have been recently demonstrated, and therefore, the diketopiperazine derivatives in this report (e.g., cysteine DKP derivative 1f) may be crucial in several prebiotically plausible scenarios of polypeptide formation.

Based on the experimental results and literature body mentioned above, we propose a putative mechanism for prebiotically plausible activation of cyclic dipeptides and subsequent formation of acyclic peptides, wherein mechanical force-induced processes might have played pivotal roles (Figure ). While the parent DKP (1a) was not shown to undergo facile condensation reaction with formaldehyde to produce corresponding dehydroalanine derivatives (Figure A), the N-hydroxylmethylation of cyclic glycine dimer with formaldehyde and subsequent potential oxidation process would generate acylated DKP, as the processes would serve as an activation of DKP by decreasing pK a of its alpha proton as well as facilitating the following condensation step through the acyl-transfer mechanism (Figure C). Facile formation of cyclic dehydroalanine peptides from the acylated DKP with another molecule of formaldehyde would occur, and such dehydroalanine peptides may have been protected from harsh hydrolysis conditions of the abiotic world due to its poor solubility (Figure A). To our knowledge, dehydroalanine derivatives have not been detected in prebiotically relevant samples such as astrochemical and geochemical materials, but perhaps, known prebiotic importance of its hydrolysis product and water adduct (i.e., pyruvic acid , and serine, respectively) may be implying its potential existence in abiotic worlds. Likewise, the paucity of prebiotic evidence of acylated DKP may be because the removal of acyl groups by hydrolysis could occur under abiotic conditions or during sample analysis of astrochemical samples. Although the dehydroalanine formation from the formylated DKP was not experimentally demonstrated because of the abovementioned safety reason to oxidize the hydroxylmethyl group, the acyl-accelerated condensation reactions were reported to occur with various substitutions (e.g., isobutyryl, valeryl, benzoyl, and carbamoyl) in a set of synthetic chemistry literature, − supporting the possibility for the formyl variant to undergo the condensation process in a similar fashion. Conjugate addition of nucleuophiles to dehydroalanine-based DKP would form a canonical amino acid–based peptide or their precursor (depicted as 1j and 1i, for example), as experimentally demonstrated with thioacetate in Figure . Ring-opening processes either by hydrolysis or mechanochemistry would finally produce linear peptides and amino acids potentially with canonical structures/compositions required for the life forms on the current Earth. With a proper nucleophile for the reaction with dehydroalanine and subsequent hydrolysis, the proposed mechanism can account for emergence of many amino acids and peptides; for example, tryptophan (as well as its precursor indole), was detected in serpentine, and this formation could be through a reaction of indole with dehydroalanine-based cyclic dipeptide (as such a reaction is proposed in the report as well). Similarly, abiotic generation of tyrosine and aspartic acidwhich were detected in prebiotically relevant samplescan be explained by a reaction of a dehydroalanine cyclic dipeptide with phenol and cyanide (followed by nitrile hydrolysis to carboxylic acid), respectively.

Conclusions

The prebiotically plausible interplay between formaldehyde and cyclic dipeptides (diketopiperazine or DKP derivatives) under solid-state conditions described in this paper may imply the existence of primitive communication networks of biomolecules in prebiotic environments, as the modern cellular signal transduction is possible through sophisticated versions of such transient covalent bond-forming processes. DKP derivatives are also known to possess unique supramolecular capabilities including interaction with RNA, and potential formation of various DKP derivatives from the dehydroalanine variants may be of importance in this contexts too. Moreover, linear dipeptide or polypeptide formation from DKP would be more likely to produce the canonical backbone structures because of the α-amino acid-based scaffold of DKPs, unlike previously observed noncanonial peptides. The presented work also overcame the synthetic challenge of dehydroalanine-based DKP through a mechanochemical approach showing its superiority over aqueous processes and demonstrated the solid-state reactions of DKP as valuable synthetic tools for modern chemistry research, as DKP derivatives are useful in a variety of contexts. , In the field of mechanochemistry, a variety of reactions is known to be enhanced through liquid-assisted grinding processes, and such a reaction promotion mechanism may be conducive for chemistry presented in this work as well as many prebiotically plausible solid-state reactions where liquids would be virtually omnipresent. While there is a plethora of known signal transduction mechanisms of modern life forms where small molecules act as not only building blocks but also signaling molecules, the origin or even the primordial form of signal transduction in abiotic worlds has not been understood. Perhaps akin to the activation of biomolecules in cellular environments by gasotransmitters, the activating role of formaldehyde in DKP modification may be a primitive version of nonenzymatic signaling events in which the stable cyclic glycine dimer can be transformed to a productive building block. As canonical polypeptides are coated with nucleophilic amino acid side chains, the pronounced electrophilicity of formaldehyde and prebiotically relevant aldehyde species (compared to the other gaseous signaling molecules such as H2S and HCN that are nucleophilic) may be key for such primitive communication networks in a scenario of abiotic emergence of peptides, proteins, protocells, and beyond.
