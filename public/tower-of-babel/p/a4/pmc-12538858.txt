Assessing knowledge and attitudes towards sports-related concussion among university students in Saudi Arabia: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Sarah Bajuaifer, Bodor Bin Sheeha, Reem M. Alwhaibi, Abdulaziz A. Alkathiry, Abdulaziz Alomereni, Michael J. Grey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSports-related concussions (SRC) are a significant public health concern, especially among younger athletes. SRCs can lead to prolonged symptoms that interfere with academic performance and are frequently underreported, particularly in regions with limited awareness. This study aimed to assess knowledge and attitudes towards concussions among university students in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted with 425 university students in Saudi Arabia. The Arabic version of the Rosenbaum Concussion Knowledge and Attitudes Survey (RoCKAS-ST-A) was used to assess students’ understanding of concussion symptoms and their attitudes towards concussion safety. Chi-square tests were used to analyse associations between sex, sports participation, and previous concussion education with the Concussion Knowledge Index (CKI) and Concussion Attitude Index (CAI).Results38% of students demonstrated very high knowledge of concussion symptoms, while 48% showed moderate to high knowledge levels. 14% had limited awareness, with particular difficulty identifying symptoms such as sensitivity to light and “feeling in a fog.” Chi-square analyses revealed significant associations between CKI scores and sex (φ = 0.154, p = 0.018) and sports participation (φ = 0.180, p = 0.003). CAI scores were significantly associated with sports participation (φ = 0.163, p = 0.010). Most students expressed cautious attitudes about concussions, but over 60% indicated they would continue playing sport even if experiencing symptoms.ConclusionAlthough university students in Saudi Arabia generally have good knowledge and attitudes towards concussion, there are notable gaps in symptom recognition and safe concussion behaviours. These findings highlight the need for targeted, region-specific concussion education programmes.

Introduction

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) is a common condition worldwide and is recognised as a major cause of death and disability, with an estimated 64–74 million people sustaining a Traumatic brain injury each year [1]. Mild traumatic brain injury, commonly known as concussion, accounts for 75–90% of these cases, and many go untreated or unreported [2, 3]. Concussion symptoms typically manifest across four major categories: somatic (physical), cognitive, sleep-related and emotional [4]. Common symptoms include headache, dizziness, blurred vision, fatigue, and difficulty concentrating [5]. Most individuals recover within a few weeks; however, overexertion during recovery prolongs symptoms [4]. Adhering to a graded return-to-play and return-to-learn protocol is considered the safest approach to recovery [3], yet such protocols are often neglected due to lack of awareness [4]. Notably, 25–30% of poorly managed concussions lead to persistent symptoms lasting months or years, a condition known as post-concussion syndrome [4]. Recent international consensus recommendations have further highlighted that traditional classifications of Traumatic brain injury often underestimate the impact of mild injuries such as concussion, emphasising the need for more nuanced characterisation, improved surveillance, and greater awareness [6].

Sports-related concussions (SRC) are a subtype of mild Traumatic brain injury caused by biomechanical forces and characterised by complex pathophysiological changes [3]. SRCs are an increasing global public health concern [7], particularly among young athletes who are at heightened risk of both initial injury and long-term effects [8, 9]. Effective prevention and management require both injury reduction strategies and timely recognition of symptoms [10]. Awareness of concussion signs is essential to enable early detection, intervention, and safe return to sport and study. Within educational institutions, especially those with active sports programmes, improving concussion knowledge is critical to promote safer environments and address misconceptions [11].

Although comprehensive national data on concussion are limited in Saudi Arabia, available evidence suggests that sports-related mild traumatic brain injuries, including concussion, are substantially underreported [12]. A regional scoping review found that concussion awareness and surveillance systems remain poorly developed across the Middle East and North Africa, contributing to significant data gaps [12]. Local studies in Saudi Arabia have primarily focused on moderate and severe Traumatic brain injurys, with sports injuries representing a small proportion of documented cases [13, 14]. This lack of systematic data highlights the need for improved concussion monitoring, education, and prevention efforts tailored to the Saudi context.

In Saudi Arabia, the national Vision 2030 initiative has prioritised sports development, increasing opportunities for participation among young people and expanding recreational and competitive sports programmes [15]. Football is the most widely practised sport and accounts for a large proportion of sports-related injuries, followed by basketball, martial arts, and volleyball [16, 17]. However, limited knowledge about concussion recognition, reporting, and management persists among young athletes. While some studies have explored concussion awareness among Saudi university students [18–21], they have been limited to specific institutions [21], focused only on male participants [18], or lacked methodological clarity [20]. Capturing awareness levels among diverse student populations across the country is essential to advancing health and safety, as it enables early concussion detection and timely intervention, and informs targeted educational strategies to address these gaps.

Methods

We used a cross-sectional study design, adhering to the STROBE guidelines [22]. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was obtained from the Princess Nourah University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (application number 24–0722), and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation. Participants who were 18 years of age or older and were students at any Saudi university were eligible to participate. The survey assessed knowledge and attitudes about concussion among university students. The study was conducted from May 2024 to August 2024. The survey link was distributed to university students across Saudi Arabia via official university emails—circulated by university gatekeepers—and social media platforms.

The Arabic version of the Rosenbaum Concussion Knowledge and Attitudes Survey—Student Version (RoCKAS-ST-A) was used to evaluate knowledge and attitudes toward concussion among university students in Saudi Arabia [23, 24]. The psychometric properties for the Arabic version was internal consistency was α = 0.31 for the Concussion Knowledge Index (CKI) and α = 0.97 for the Concussion Attitude Index (CAI); test–retest reliability ICC = 0.79 (95% CI 0.42–0.92) for CKI and ICC = 0.86 (95% CI 0.66–0.94) for CAI [23]. This tool has been validated for the assessment of awareness of concussion causes, signs, and symptoms, as well as attitudes toward concussion. It comprises 55 items across five sections, from which the Concussion Knowledge Index (CKI) and Concussion Attitude Index (CAI) scores are derived. For CKI, Section One comprises 18 true/false questions (three of which are validity index questions and one not scored), Section Two comprises three true/false questions, and Section Five includes 16 symptom recognition items (eight legitimate symptoms and eight distractors). Correct answers received 1 point, and incorrect responses received 0 points, with a total of 25 possible points for the knowledge section. Sections Three and Four relate to CAI and comprise 18 statements, with 15 included in scoring on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree to 5 = strongly agree), including reverse-scored items to assess unsafe attitudes. The total CAI score ranges from 15 to 75, with higher scores indicating safer attitudes.

In accordance with Rosenbaum and Arnett [24], we used a validity index (VI) to assess participants’ attentiveness, based on three true/false questions (correct = 1, incorrect = 0). To ensure attentiveness, we used an a priori VI threshold of less than 2; however, no participants fell below this cutoff.

The sample size calculation was based on the Ministry of Education’s report indicating that the total number of university students in Saudi Arabia was 1,297,426 in 2023. Using OpenEpi (version 3) with a 95% confidence interval (p ≤ 0.05), the estimated required sample size was 385 participants.

For descriptive purposes, CKI and CAI results were reported as means and standard deviations. To enable comparison across participant characteristics, CKI and CAI scores were further classified into four ordinal categories—very high (VH; >80%), high (H; 60–79%), moderate (M; 40–59%), and low (L; 20–39%)—based on recommended percentage thresholds [25]. Chi-square tests were then used to assess associations between these categorical knowledge and attitude levels and nominal variables including sex, geographic region, sports participation, concussion education, and previous concussion history. Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

Results

A total of 425 students completed the RoCKAS-ST-A survey. All participants scored at least 2 on the validity index, confirming their eligibility for inclusion. The sample included 293 females (68.9%) and 132 males (31.1%) with a mean age of 21 ± 2 years (range: 18–33). Participants were drawn from many universities across all five main regions of Saudi Arabia, though representation varied by region. The mean CKI score was 13.9 ± 3.1 out of 25, and the mean CAI score was 53.4 ± 10.5 out of 75. Based on performance in Sections One and Two, 38% of participants demonstrated very high knowledge, 48% showed moderate to high knowledge, and 14% exhibited low knowledge. Regarding attitudes, 11% demonstrated very high scores, 61% high, 22% moderate, and 5% low.

Analysis of symptom recognition indicated that the most consistently identified symptoms were difficulty concentrating (99.5%), dizziness (99.7%), headache (97.1%), and difficulty remembering (91.5%). In contrast, sensitivity to light (45.6%), feeling slowed down (56.7%), feeling in a “fog” (72.9%) and drowsiness (32.2%) were less frequently recognised.

Discussion

This study assessed concussion knowledge and attitudes among university students in Saudi Arabia, providing one of the first large-scale, multi-region analyses of this topic. While university students show a generally moderate understanding of concussion and positive attitudes toward management, significant gaps remain in recognizing subtle but critical symptoms like drowsiness and light sensitivity. These overlooked signs may contribute to underreporting and delayed care, underscoring an urgent need for targeted education emphasizing these less obvious symptoms.

Our results corroborate prior evidence revealing inconsistent concussion awareness among young adults, suggesting that despite growing public health efforts, knowledge dissemination has yet to fully penetrate this population [7, 8]. This indicates that current educational strategies may need to be re-evaluated and tailored to better address persistent knowledge gaps. Similar to King et al. [11] while most students could identify headache and dizziness as symptoms, fewer recognised cognitive and emotional indicators. Register-Mihalik et al. [26] suggested that many athletes underestimate the seriousness of concussions and are reluctant to report them, often due to cultural or peer pressures. Basch et al. [27] highlighted the important role of faculty and staff in supporting concussion education and improving university students’ concussion knowledge, beliefs and reporting behaviours.

In the current study, sex and athlete status were significantly associated with knowledge and attitudes. The observed higher knowledge levels among female students highlight the importance of leveraging gender-sensitive approaches in concussion education [28]. Tailored interventions that engage male students more effectively could therefore reduce disparities and improve overall safety outcomes. Previous studies have suggested that male athletes may internalise cultural norms around resilience and toughness, potentially leading to riskier behaviours, including underreporting concussion symptoms and prematurely returning to play [29]. This cultural expectation can partially explain the pattern observed in our study. However, this narrative must be balanced by acknowledging that female athletes also encounter unique stressors, such as gender stereotyping, lack of institutional support, or fear of being perceived as weak, that can equally affect their willingness to report injuries or advocate for themselves. Therefore, differences in concussion-related behaviours are not solely a function of sex, but rather shaped by complex societal, psychological, and structural factors.

Athletes demonstrated safer attitudes, suggesting that exposure to sports environments may increase awareness of concussion risks, though this relationship is likely influenced by prior education and personal experience [24]. Concussion knowledge and attitudes can vary widely even among non-varsity athlete populations, such as intramural and club sport participants, who may have less formal education and awareness programs than varsity athletes [28, 30], highlighting the need for tailored educational interventions beyond elite sport contexts. Furthermore, understanding decision-making behaviours among athletes and coaches is crucial, as it influences concussion recognition and reporting. For example, Tsao et al. [31] highlighted that NCAA Division I soccer coaches and athletes often faced challenges in concussion decision-making, that impacted injury management outcomes. In contrast, peer-led concussion education programs have demonstrated effectiveness in improving knowledge and reporting behaviours among collegiate athletes [32] and multimedia, theory-based concussion education interventions have been successful in enhancing concussion reporting across athletic divisions [33].

The absence of a significant effect from prior concussion education suggests potential limitations in the design or implementation of current programs. This aligns with O’Reilly et al. [25], who highlighted the necessity for more interactive and ongoing educational interventions to promote lasting knowledge retention and behavioural change. Therefore, there is a clear need for engaging, sustained, and evidence-based concussion education that effectively converts awareness into safer practices.

This study’s reliance on voluntary online participation likely introduced selection bias, with greater representation from students interested in sports or health topics. Additionally, regional participation was uneven, particularly underrepresenting areas with smaller university populations, which limits generalisability. The self-reported nature of the data may also have been influenced by social desirability bias. Finally, the cross-sectional design prevents causal inferences between demographic factors and concussion knowledge or attitudes. Future research should use stratified sampling to improve representativeness, incorporate objective measures to reduce bias, and adopt longitudinal designs to better understand knowledge and attitude changes over time.

Despite these limitations, this study provides important baseline data to guide future educational campaigns and policy efforts aligned with Saudi Arabia’s Vision 2030 sports development goals. Increasing targeted concussion education, improving symptom recognition, and addressing gaps in awareness remain essential priorities. Future research should incorporate longitudinal designs and objective assessments to strengthen understanding of how knowledge and attitudes evolve over time.
