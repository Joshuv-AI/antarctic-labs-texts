Multi-family wavelet-based feature engineering method for short-term time series forecasting
Authors: Kyrylo Yemets, Ivan Izonin, Stergios Aristoteles Mitoulis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate short-term forecasting of natural phenomena—such as temperature, electricity demand, and wind-power output—is critical for reliable planning in climate science and energy management. Forecasting accuracy can be enhanced by developing advanced feature engineering techniques, particularly through the use of wavelet transformation. This paper introduces a novel method to feature construction for short-term time series forecasting of natural phenomena, which is based on the use of the Stationary Wavelet Transform (SWT) and multi-family wavelets. The developed method allows for the extraction of signal characteristics while preserving the original data dimensionality, so each time series observation is supplemented with detailed coefficients from the wavelets. This approach includes the application of several wavelet families (e.g., Daubechies, Symlets, Coiflets, Haar, and Meyer), which increases the informativeness of the time series and improves the forecasting accuracy of neural network models, in particular LSTM. Experimental evaluation on three open datasets—meteorological variables, residential electricity demand, and wind-farm output. Relative to identically configured LSTMs trained on raw observations, the wavelet-augmented models cut error consistently: MAE by 13.6%, MSE by 17.7%, RMSE by 9.5%, and SMAPE by 13.9%. These improvements confirm that multi-family SWT features offer a dataset-agnostic route to higher short-term forecasting accuracy.Keywords: Wavelet transform, Time series forecasting, LSTM, Feature engineering, Feature extraction, Feature extension, ANN, Forecasting accuracy, Stationary wavelet transformSubject terms: Computer science, Information technology

Introduction

Time-series forecasting has become an indispensable tool across domains such as energy, finance, logistics, and environmental management, where reliable insight into future behaviour is critical for planning and decision-making1,2. In particular, short-term forecasts of natural phenomena—such as wind speed, temperature, solar activity (sunspots), and solar energy generation—are becoming increasingly important in light of societal, economic, and environmental needs3,4. Accurate short-term forecasts of natural phenomena—wind speed, ambient temperature5, solar irradiance, and related energy-production variables—underpin critical operational decisions in power-system scheduling, climate-risk mitigation, and public safety and logistics6. Yet the underlying processes are noisy, non-stationary, and manifest dynamics over multiple temporal scales, making reliable prediction inherently difficult.

Despite the existence of established forecasting methods, predicting such time series remains a challenging task, as natural processes are often characterized by a high level of noise, non-stationarity, and complex temporal patterns at different scales7. Traditional modeling approaches — such as autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA), exponential smoothing methods, or neural network architectures — may struggle to capture these complex multi-scale dynamics8. Consequently, there is a growing interest in more advanced feature construction techniques and decomposition methods that better isolate, analyze, and utilize the relevant information hidden in the data9,10.

Today, forecasting methods for time series using feature engineering have gained significant popularity11. However, these approaches are mainly focused on long-term forecasting. Another promising option for extracting additional features in the form of frequency characteristics is the use of wavelet transforms12. Methods based on wavelet transforms13 have demonstrated particular promise in time series analysis due to their ability to decompose signals into frequency and time components. By separating the original series into several sub-bands, the wavelet transform is capable of highlighting short-term details, long-term trends, and cyclic patterns that might otherwise remain unnoticed14,15. These methods already recommended themselves in geotechnical areas16. Also applying wavelet transform with neural networks shows great results in meteorological sphere17,18. When wavelet features are paired with deep learners (e.g., BiLSTM, CNN-GRU or hybrid Transformers), measurable accuracy gains have been reported in financial, environmental and energy-price series alike19.

In addition, the Stationary Wavelet Transform (SWT) provides shift invariance, which enables a more detailed analysis of local patterns. Nevertheless, despite the proven effectiveness of wavelet transforms, many existing approaches do not fully exploit the potential of extended feature construction based on these decompositions.

To address these limitations, we formulate the following central research question concerning the largely unexplored design space of multi-wavelet feature representations: How does the selection—and possible combination—of different mother wavelets (Daubechies, Symlet, Coiflet, Haar, Meyer) influence the predictive accuracy of neural forecasters trained on stationary-wavelet–based feature representations?

By framing the study around this question, we move beyond the conventional practice of choosing one “default” wavelet and instead examine whether complementary families, used individually or in concert, can supply richer multi-scale information and thereby improve short-horizon forecasts.

In this article, the authors have developed a new feature engineering method based on stationary wavelet transforms that is not limited to a single wavelet family, aimed at improving short-term forecasting of time series of natural phenomena. By systematically extracting multiscale features, the proposed method is designed to preserve both sensitive and critical information that enables more accurate forecasts in the near future.

Literature review

The class of methods for pre-processing time series using frequency characteristics includes many approaches. In this article, we focused on a detailed review and analysis of existing methods that are based on the use of wavelet transforms. A common feature of most of the analyzed studies is the use of the wavelet transform for approximating the original time series data. For this purpose, Daubechies wavelets are most frequently employed. For example, in12 a discrete wavelet transform using a Daubechies wavelet is applied to detect the multi-level structure of electricity generation data and to forecast wind energy. This decomposition helps to isolate the trend from high-frequency noise, allowing subsequent models such as CNN-LSTM and LSTM to focus on the most informative aspects of the signal, thereby improving forecasting accuracy.

In20, the authors develop a hybrid forecasting model that performs hierarchical multiscale decomposition via the DWT, using orthogonal basis functions to split the signal into approximation, that reflect low-frequency trends, and detail coefficients, corresponding to high-frequency fluctuations. Separate LSTM networks are trained for each sub-series to model dependencies at the corresponding scale, and the final forecast is obtained by aggregating all sub-models, integrating multiscale dynamics within a combined architecture.

Discrete wavelet transform is applied to analyze financial time series with the aim of extracting the main trends and reducing the influence of high-frequency noise21.The obtained frequency components are further used in an LSTM model, which is combined with the Singular Spectrum Analysis method to improve forecasting accuracy. The use of Daubechies wavelets in this case ensures effective localization of signal characteristics for the task at hand.

In22, the authors apply a discrete wavelet transform to analyze signals from power networks for predicting faults that occur during their operation. The decomposition of the signal allows key frequency components to be isolated, which can then be used to detect anomalies that indicate potential failures. A Daubechies mother wavelet was used for the decomposition.

In23, the authors examine LSTM-based forecasting together with strategies for attenuating noise in the input series. Wavelet transforms serve as a pre-processing step to remove high-frequency components, improving the extraction of essential trends and features. Both Daubechies and Symlet wavelets are discussed for signal analysis.

In24,the authors propose a Wavelet-Seq2Seq-LSTM model with an attention mechanism for forecasting water levels in hydroelectric-dam reservoirs. A discrete wavelet transform is applied to decompose the water level signal, which reduces the influence of high-frequency noise and extracts the main frequency components. The obtained features are then fed into the Seq2Seq-LSTM model, where the attention mechanism improves forecasting accuracy.

In25 ,the authors present a hybrid model that combines a wavelet transform with an LSTM neural network to forecast groundwater depths. The main goal of this approach is to reduce noise in the groundwater depth time series and to improve forecasting accuracy by decomposing the original signal into several sub-time series components. For this purpose, the researchers apply a continuous wavelet transform using a Meyer wavelet, which allows the time series to be decomposed into three sub-time series components, each reflecting distinct frequency characteristics of the signal. In addition, the authors compute a scalogram—a graphical representation of the energy distribution of the signal over time and frequency, which is essentially similar to an audio signal spectrogram. In this way, the time series is processed using methods analogous to those applied for audio signal analysis. The obtained sub-time series components and the scalogram are used as input for the LSTM model, which significantly improves forecasting accuracy, as evidenced by a substantial increase in the NSE coefficient.

In26,a continuous wavelet transform with a Daubechies wavelet is used in combination with a simulated-annealing algorithm. Decomposing the signal using a continuous Wavelet transform allows the input data to be split into basic frequency components, which reduces the influence of noise and improves the training process of the LSTM-based neural network model. The obtained basic components are then used for forecasting, and the time series is reconstructed using an inverse continuous wavelet transform.

In27, the authors apply wavelet denoising followed by a stacked denoising autoencoder, achieving 97.5% precision in unmanned-aerial-vehicle (UAV) fault detection and demonstrating the effectiveness of wavelets in highly noisy flight-parameter streams. Paper introduced TEMPO, a time-evolving multi-period observational framework for space-probe telemetry that fuses wavelet analysis with periodicity mining to capture cross-scale anomalies28.

Recent work has also shown that hybrid decomposition–combination frameworks—though not always wavelet-based—can leverage frequency-oriented signal splits to boost forecasting accuracy. Iftikhar et al. propose a novel decomposition–combination method that separates hourly electricity-demand series from the Nord Pool market into long-run trend, seasonal, and stochastic components, then forecasts each component with an exhaustive set of univariate/multivariate models whose outputs are finally re-combined; the DC pipeline outperforms classic single-model baselines for one-day-ahead load prediction29. Using the same DC idea, the authors extend the approach to day-ahead price forecasting for the Italian power market, again demonstrating that multi-resolution decomposition followed by component-specific modelling yields lower MAE/MAPE than benchmark statistical, machine-learning, and hybrid alternatives30. Beyond the energy domain, Cuba et al. introduce a time-series ensemble technique that filters cumulative monkeypox-case counts (variance stabilisation, detrending, normalisation), fits five single models plus three weighted ensembles, and achieves superior 28-day forecasts for four highly affected countries; the authors argue that such decomposition-plus-ensemble logic is portable to other epidemic settings31. These studies reinforce the value of first isolating distinct frequency or structural blocks—even when wavelets are not the chosen basis—before training specialised forecasters and aggregating their outputs, a strategy that parallels the multiscale intuition behind wavelet-LSTM hybrids.

In general, the main objective of almost all existing studies is to use wavelet transforms for smoothing the time series, which helps combat noise, or to decompose the series into frequencies, as if it were an audio signal. However, almost always a single wavelet family and the discrete wavelet transform are used, without justifying their use or exploring the possibility of employing other wavelet families to achieve more accurate results.

Methods

In this section, the fundamental concepts of the wavelet transform are presented, including its types—such as the discrete wavelet transform and the stationary wavelet transform—as well as an analysis of the limitations in their practical applications. In addition, the method of feature engineering based on the wavelet transform is described in detail, along with its various algorithmic implementations and its integration with LSTM.

Wavelet transformations

The wavelet transform32 is a powerful mathematical tool that decomposes a signal into components defined in both time and frequency. Unlike the Fourier transform18, which provides only an overall overview of the frequency content, the wavelet transform focuses on localized features by representing the signal as a combination of shifted and scaled versions of a single basic function, known as the mother wavelet.

The core operations of the wavelet transform are scaling and translation. The scaling operation stretches or compresses the wavelet, allowing for the analysis of different frequency components—high-frequency details appear at smaller scales, while low-frequency trends and overall signal characteristics emerge at larger scales. The translation operation shifts the wavelet along the time or spatial axis, enabling the examination of local features in different parts of the signal. Mathematically, this process involves computing the scalar products between the signal and these scaled and shifted versions of the wavelet. The result is a set of coefficients that indicate the presence of specific wavelet shapes at various scales and positions, yielding a detailed time-scale representation that is especially useful for analyzing non-stationary signals whose properties change over time.

One of the key advantages of the wavelet transform is its ability to perform multiresolution analysis. It simultaneously provides a general overview of the signal and a detailed depiction of its structure, making it effective for detecting both global trends and local anomalies. Thanks to the inherent time and frequency localization of wavelets, this transform accurately identifies transient or abrupt changes in the signal—an attribute that is particularly beneficial for short-term forecasting with neural network methods.

Next, we review the wavelets that will be employed in further analysis, which can be applied with both discrete and stationary wavelet transforms.

The classical Haar wavelet33 is the simplest and one of the earliest examples of a wavelet. Proposed by Alfred Haar in 1909, it provides a basic tool for multiresolution signal analysis. Its simple, step-like structure makes it easy to understand and compute, especially compared to more complex, smooth wavelets. It can be scaled to identify components at various frequencies.

This family of orthogonal wavelets, introduced by Ingrid Daubechiesі34. Is widely used due to its compact support and its ability to achieve the maximum number of vanishing moments for a given support width. These properties make Daubechies wavelets very effective at representing the polynomial characteristics of signals.

Developed to improve approximation and symmetry compared to other wavelets32. Coiflets have the distinctive property that both the wavelet function , and the scaling function satisfy additional moment conditions. This means that the integrals of these functions multiplied by powers of t vanish up to a certain order, enabling efficient representation of polynomial trends and derivatives. These features lead to better approximation of smooth functions, minimized phase shifts, and preserved symmetry—critical in analyzing signals with abrupt transitions.

Symlets are a family of nearly symmetric, compactly supported orthogonal wavelets that serve as a variation of Daubechies wavelets32. Their design focuses on enhancing symmetry while maintaining properties like the maximum number of vanishing moments for a given filter length. The nearly symmetric form of Symlets yields a more balanced phase response, which is important in tasks where phase distortions can be problematic. Their coefficients are selected to ensure orthogonality, a specified number of vanishing moments, and maximum symmetry.

The Meyer wavelet35 – is an infinitely differentiable wavelet defined primarily in the frequency domain. Unlike some other wavelets, it does not have compact support in the time domain; however, its Fourier transform is compactly supported. This results in high smoothness and a smooth transition between frequency bands, achieved through a special transition function defined for values in the interval [0,1]. Due to these characteristics, the Meyer wavelet is widely applied in signal analysis tasks that require precise frequency localization and minimal artifacts.

Discrete wavelet transform

DWT finds wide application due to its ability for multiresolution signal analysis. It is used for noise removal, where the useful parts of the signal are isolated by thresholding the detail coefficients. The method is also applied for data compression—for example, in the JPEG2000 image compression standard37, where the important features of the signal are preserved despite a significant reduction in data volume. Moreover, DWT is used for feature extraction in biomedical signals such as ECG or EEG38, as well as for detecting defects in the analysis of machine vibration signals.

DWT decomposes the signal using a two-channel filter bank, which consists of a low-pass filter (LPF) and a high-pass filter (HPF). The low-pass filter extracts the smooth, general components of the signal (approximation coefficients), while the high-pass filter extracts the fine, detailed elements (detail coefficients). After filtering, a downsampling process with a factor of 2 is performed, which means reducing the number of data points at each level of decomposition. For example, in a two-level decomposition, the initial signal is first divided into the approximation and detail coefficients of the first level, and then the approximation coefficients of the first level are further decomposed to obtain the coefficients of the second level.

Although such compression provides a compact representation of the signal, it results in output coefficients that do not correspond to every sample of the original signal, which can be critical for some applications. The dimensionality reduction inherent in DWT can create issues for feature construction tasks when it is important to preserve the original resolution of the signal. Since the output coefficients are obtained by discretization, important characteristics tied to specific time points may be lost. In cases where preserving the original dimensionality is critical, it is advisable to use alternative methods. For example, the Stationary Wavelet Transform (SWT) does not include the downsampling stage, and therefore provides coefficients of the same length as the original signal.

Stationary wavelet transform

Stationary Wavelet Transform (SWT)39 is a variant of the wavelet transform which, unlike DWT, does not perform the discretization operation after filtering. This allows the coefficients to retain the same length as the original signal. SWT is used for many tasks similar to those addressed by DWT, including noise removal, data compression, and feature extraction in biomedical signals. The main advantage of SWT is that it provides time localization without loss of dimensionality, making it useful for tasks where an exact correspondence of features to the original signal is important. When applying SWT, the signal is decomposed using filter banks, similar to DWT. However, instead of decimation, after passing through the low-pass and high-pass filters, the resulting coefficients retain the same length as the original signal. This allows one to obtain, for each level, approximation and detail coefficients where all vectors have the same size.

Although this preserves the time association of each point, SWT has a drawback related to enhanced boundary artifacts. Such artifacts often arise due to the signal extension methods at the boundaries (for example, zero-padding or symmetric extension) that are applied to preserve the length of the output coefficients. When the window size or wavelet scale decreases, a sharp transition becomes more noticeable at the edges of the signal, creating an “anomaly” at the end of the time series. Figure 1 illustrates this effect for Symlet-based features extracted with window lengths of 20, 50, and 100 samples. The 20-sample window reproduces the original series closely, whereas the 50- and 100-sample windows show a noticeable accumulation of error toward the end of the sequence.

Demonstration of boundary effects arising in features extracted via wavelet decomposition.

In cases where the number of features is large, the convolution or filter window decreases significantly, which amplifies the effect of artificial extension, making the sharp jumps at the boundaries more noticeable. For smaller numbers of features, the window becomes wider, so the average value at the boundaries smooths the jump, and boundary anomalies are not an issue. This property of SWT is a disadvantage, because when processing long signals or signals with a complex structure, such boundary effects can distort the true dynamics and complicate the interpretation of the results.

In anomaly detection algorithms, such as autoencoders (AE) and variational autoencoders (VAE)40, data is reconstructed, which allows for obtaining an approximation of the original signal. The reconstruction error, calculated as the difference between the original data and its reconstruction, serves as a criterion for identifying anomalies: a high level of loss indicates a deviation from the normal behavior of the signal. This process can be compared with the use of the Stationary Wavelet Transform (SWT), where the time series is decomposed into approximation coefficients that reflect the overall trend of the signal and detail coefficients that represent the deviation or difference between the original signal and its approximation. Thus, the detail coefficients from SWT from first level of decomposition can be viewed as analogous to the reconstruction error in AE/VAE: they allow the detection of small but significant deviations that may indicate the presence of anomalies in the time series.

Modeling results and discussion

Datasets and modelling details

The Australian weather dataset (Weather)42 contains 32,072 daily time series of temperature observations and precipitation forecasts, collected by the Australian Bureau of Meteorology from 422 meteorological stations across Australia, covering the period from 2 May 2015 to 26 April 2017. In this dataset, missing values were simply replaced with zeros. Notably, the time series are balanced among trend, seasonal, and residual components, providing a reliable foundation for studying time series with diverse underlying behavioral characteristics.

The electricity consumption dataset (Electricity)43 represents data from 370 clients, initially recorded at 15-minute intervals in kilowatts during the period from 2011 to 2014. The dataset available is an aggregated version of an hourly electricity dataset, resulting in 321 weekly time series covering the period from 2012 to 2014. This dataset is characterized by pronounced seasonal patterns, making it particularly useful for forecasting models that emphasize seasonality.

The wind energy production dataset (Wind)44 contains a single, very long daily time series reflecting wind energy production in megawatts, recorded every 4 s starting from 1 August 2019, with a total of 7,397,147 data points. This high-frequency dataset is predominantly trend-driven, making it extremely valuable for applications focused on long-term trend forecasting and analyzing gradual changes in renewable energy production.

To better understand the type of data we are working with, we will use the STL (Seasonal and Trend decomposition using Loess) method to decompose the time series into their individual components.

Each model was trained for 50 epochs with early stopping. As optimizer were chosen Adam with learning rate 0.001 with MSE as loss function for model. LSTM consist of 2 layers with 128 hidden size.

In the case of a time series without a clear dominance of any component, the best results were obtained with Daubechies and Haar wavelets. In addition, we chose a context window of 20 time steps, which is the largest length for which boundary effects were not observed.

Future work

Despite the demonstrated effectiveness of the proposed method for time series forecasting, several directions for future research are worth exploring to address practical deployment challenges and extend the applicability of the approach.

First, a key aspect of real-world deployment is the reliability of sensor data. The quality, resolution, and stability of sensor measurements can have a significant impact on forecasting accuracy, particularly in domains with high noise levels or incomplete data streams. Therefore, future studies will consider integrating preprocessing pipelines for denoising, missing data imputation, and uncertainty quantification, to enhance robustness in low-quality input scenarios.

Second, although the LSTM-based inference is computationally efficient on standard hardware, the Stationary Wavelet Transform (SWT) and multi-wavelet feature construction may impose notable preprocessing costs. These may limit the feasibility of the method in edge-computing or real-time environments. To overcome this, future work will focus on developing lightweight variants.

Third, while the current study is focused on natural phenomena, the interpretability of the forecasting model becomes especially important in high-stakes domains such as healthcare, and critical infrastructure. The combination of multiscale wavelet features and deep LSTM architectures increases model complexity, potentially limiting transparency. As part of future research, we plan to investigate model-agnostic interpretability techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations and Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations to quantify the contributions of each feature. In addition, we aim to explore hybrid forecasting architectures that integrate interpretable components (e.g., attention mechanisms, symbolic rule-based modules) to ensure explainability without sacrificing predictive performance.

Fourth, the boundary effect introduced by SWT decomposition, particularly in high-resolution or long time series, remains a limiting factor for extending the method to long-term forecasting. Future work will be directed toward developing techniques to mitigate boundary artifacts, such as improved signal extension schemes, adaptive windowing, or wavelet fusion strategies that reduce cumulative edge distortions.

Finally, we also plan to compare the proposed SWT-based approach with alternative signal decomposition techniques, including the DWT, CWT, and classical Fourier-based methods. Such comparative analyses will help determine the most effective decomposition strategy for a given class of forecasting tasks and application domains.

Conclusions

Short-term time series forecasting is a critical component in numerous real-world applications, as it provides the foundation for timely decision-making and resource management optimization.

In this paper, we proposed a novel approach to feature construction based on wavelet transforms, which utilizes stationary wavelet transforms together with several wavelet families to enhance LSTM-based forecasting models.

The method is based on the use of detail coefficients obtained at the first level of the stationary wavelet transform for different wavelet families. By utilizing these coefficients, we form a feature matrix that provides a deep, informative representation of the time series and contributes to improved forecasting performance in the LSTM model.

The developed method was rigorously tested on the Weather, Electricity, and Wind datasets. For example, for the Weather dataset, the integration of the Symlet, Daubechies, and Meyer wavelets resulted in an approximate reduction of MSE by 14.5%, RMSE by 7.5%, and MAE by 7.5%, with an overall SMAPE improvement of about 7.5%—which together yield an average efficiency increase of nearly 9.3% across all metrics. Similar improvements were observed for the Electricity dataset (with an average improvement of approximately 7.8%) and the Wind dataset (around 24.0%). These quantitative results indicate that our approach to feature construction using wavelets not only enhances forecasting accuracy compared to the baseline LSTM model, but also provides significant robustness when dealing with various signal characteristics—be they seasonal, trend-driven, or balanced.

Future research will be aimed at reducing the influence of edge effects associated with wavelet transforms and expanding the approach by incorporating other types of transforms, such as discrete and continuous wavelet transforms, to further improve both short-term and long-term forecasting.
