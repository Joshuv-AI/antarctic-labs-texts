Delayed Presentation of Common Peroneal Nerve Schwannoma: Nerve-preserving Enucleation Under Loupe Magnification in a Resource-limited Setting
Authors: Simeon Mulugeta Mengistu, Getaw Alamne Gebeyehu, Dagmawi Geremew Zinaw, Daniel Hailemichael Aboye, Harom Gari Wakjira, Azeb Gezahegn, Mehret Sisay, Mekonnen Eshete
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Summary:Schwannomas are benign peripheral nerve sheath tumors that can arise anywhere along the peripheral nerves. Schwannomas of the common peroneal nerve are rare and may present with nonspecific chronic leg pain, often resulting in delayed diagnosis, particularly in resource-limited settings. A 37-year-old man presented with a 2-year history of progressively worsening posterior knee pain radiating distally, described as sharp and electric-like. Physical examination revealed a tender mass in the popliteal region with a positive Tinel sign but no neurological deficits. MRI demonstrated an encapsulated lesion arising from the nerve at the sciatic bifurcation involving the common peroneal division. Fine-needle aspiration cytology was consistent with a benign spindle cell neoplasm. Microsurgical enucleation was performed under loupe magnification with preservation of nerve continuity. Histopathology confirmed schwannoma. Postoperatively, the patient had complete resolution of pain and preserved neurological function. Common peroneal nerve schwannomas, though rare, should be considered in patients with chronic neuropathic leg pain. Early imaging and meticulous microsurgical excision provide excellent functional outcomes.

Schwannomas are benign, encapsulated tumors derived from Schwann cells of peripheral nerves. They typically present as solitary, slow-growing lesions and may cause pain, paresthesia, or mass effect as they enlarge. Schwannomas can occur along any peripheral nerve but are most frequently reported in the head, neck, and upper extremities, with lower extremity involvement being less common.1,2

Schwannomas arising specifically from the common peroneal nerve are rare. Due to their deep anatomical location and often indolent growth, they frequently present with nonspecific symptoms such as chronic leg pain radiating along the nerve distribution, electric-like sensations, or activity-related discomfort, which can be misinterpreted as radiculopathy or other musculoskeletal conditions, leading to diagnostic delays.3 A positive Tinel sign over the course of the affected nerve raises suspicion for a nerve sheath tumor. MRI is the imaging modality of choice, reliably demonstrating a well-circumscribed, encapsulated lesion associated with the nerve.4

Although many schwannomas are asymptomatic, surgical excision is indicated for symptomatic lesions, typically through microsurgical enucleation that preserves nerve fascicles and minimizes postoperative deficits. Recent surgical series indicate that schwannomas of the peroneal nerves around the knee can be safely resected with a low risk of permanent deficits, especially when the tumors are relatively small.5

In resource-limited settings, delayed presentation of peripheral nerve tumors is not uncommon due to limited access to specialized care. Additionally, the lack of advanced microsurgical equipment necessitates reliance on fundamental surgical principles to achieve optimal outcomes. Here, we describe a rare presentation of a schwannoma arising from the common peroneal nerve at the level of the sciatic nerve bifurcation, manifesting as chronic neuropathic leg pain without neurological deficit, and highlight the diagnostic and surgical considerations that led to a favorable outcome in resource-limited settings.

CASE PRESENTATION

A 37-year-old man presented with a 2-year history of progressively increasing pain in the posterior aspect of his left knee. The pain was initially mild but gradually worsened, becoming sharp and electric-like, radiating to the leg and foot. Symptoms were exacerbated by squatting and standing on cold surfaces. For this complaint, the patient went to different clinics and was treated with analgesics without a proper diagnosis or significant symptom improvement.

There was no history of recent trauma to the knee. The patient had a remote history of a bullet injury to the left third and fourth digits 19 years prior, without residual neurological deficits. There was no significant medical or family history related to similar symptoms.

Physical examination: There was no visible swelling, discoloration, or scar in the popliteal region. Deep palpation elicited marked tenderness over a firm, round mass, with a positive Tinel sign over the course of the affected nerve. There were no sensory or motor deficits, and the patient’s gait was normal.

Laboratory investigations, including complete blood count and serum electrolytes, were within normal limits. MRI of the left lower limb revealed a well-defined, encapsulated lesion at the popliteal level arising from the sciatic nerve bifurcation and involving the common peroneal nerve, suggestive of a peripheral nerve sheath tumor. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 1, which displays an MRI of the left lower limb showing a well-defined, encapsulated lesion at the posterior lower leg suggestive of sciatic nerve schwannoma, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F82.)

After obtaining written informed consent, the patient underwent excision under spinal anesthesia in the prone position. A lazy-S incision was made above the popliteal crease. The sciatic nerve and its bifurcation were identified. A firm, nodular 4 × 4 cm mass was found arising from the common peroneal division at its origin. The nerve fascicles were draped over the mass without discontinuity, giving an “onion-skin” appearance (Fig. 1).

Intraoperative images showing a yellowish nodular mass arising from the origin of the common peroneal nerve. Nerve fascicles are draped over the mass, giving it an onion-like appearance.

The procedure was performed under loupe magnification in the absence of an operating microscope and nerve stimulator; instead, visual identification guided fascicular preservation. Care was taken to avoid traction and minimize the use of cautery near neural structures. The integrity of the parent nerve was maintained throughout the procedure. Meticulous dissection allowed identification and preservation of nerve fascicles, and intracapsular enucleation was successfully achieved without iatrogenic injury. Strategies such as minimal cautery, gentle dissection, and preservation of the epineurium were implemented to reduce postoperative scarring. The wound was closed in layers, and the excised specimen was sent for histopathologic examination (Fig. 2).

Gross image of the mass after complete enucleation.

Histopathology: microscopic examination revealed a well-encapsulated schwannoma composed of alternating hypercellular Antoni A areas and hypocellular Antoni B areas with Verocay body formation, confirming the diagnosis of schwannoma. (See figure, Supplemental Digital Content 2, which displays the hypocellular and hypercellular areas of spindle cells with elongated nuclei forming nuclear palisading and Verocay bodies. Antoni B areas with myxoid change, whereas Antoni A areas demonstrate nuclear palisading and Verocay bodies, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/F83.)

The postoperative course was uneventful. At 1-week follow-up, the wound had healed well, and the patient reported mild discomfort when walking but complete resolution of preoperative pain. The patient had significant symptom improvement, and there were no motor or sensory deficits on physical examination at the 3- and 6-month follow-up.

DISCUSSION

Schwannomas of the peripheral nerves can cause chronic neuropathic pain due to mechanical compression of nerve fascicles or adjacent structures. Although most schwannomas involve the head, neck, or upper limb nerves, lower extremity presentations, particularly involving the common peroneal nerve, are unusual.1,2 Common peroneal nerve schwannomas may mimic other etiologies of leg pain, including radiculopathy, tendonitis, or meniscal injury, leading to diagnostic delay.

Delayed presentation, as seen in this case, is a recognized challenge in low-resource settings and may lead to larger tumor size, prolonged nerve compression, and increased surgical complexity.3 Despite this, favorable functional outcomes can still be achieved with careful operative planning and technique. This highlights the importance of maintaining a high index of suspicion for peripheral nerve tumors in patients presenting with long-standing neuropathic symptoms.

MRI remains the gold standard for preoperative diagnosis, providing excellent delineation of tumor size, location, and relationship to the nerve, which facilitates surgical planning.4 Characteristic MRI features include a well-circumscribed, encapsulated lesion with eccentric growth along the nerve.

Recent clinical series have demonstrated that schwannomas of the peroneal nerves around the knee can be excised safely with low risk of permanent neurological deficit, especially when diagnosis and surgery occur before tumors become large.5 Tumor size has been correlated with the likelihood of preoperative neurological deficits; larger tumors (>5 cm) are more likely to result in permanent motor deficits.6

Although microsurgical techniques with operating microscopes and nerve stimulators are considered the gold standard in peripheral nerve surgery, such resources are not universally available. This case demonstrates that adherence to core microsurgical principles—including gentle tissue handling, precise dissection, and preservation of fascicular anatomy—can allow safe schwannoma excision even under loupe magnification. Comparable favorable outcomes following careful nerve-sparing excision have also been reported in previous surgical series of lower extremity schwannomas and extremity neurilemmomas.7,8 These findings have important implications for surgeons practicing in low-resource settings.

CONCLUSIONS

This case highlights that safe and effective excision of peripheral nerve schwannomas can be achieved under loupe magnification, even in resource-limited settings. Furthermore, favorable outcomes are possible despite delayed presentation, provided meticulous surgical technique is used.
