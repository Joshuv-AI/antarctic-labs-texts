Congenital malformation risk following prenatal antipsychotic exposure: a systematic safety surveillance approach
Authors: Loreen Straub, Shirley V Wang, Sonia Hernandez-Diaz, Brian T Bateman, Seanna M Vine, Massimiliano Russo, Yanmin Zhu, Lee Cohen, Carolyn E Cesta, Kathryn J Gray, Krista F Huybrechts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedication safety studies in pregnancy typically focus on selected or composite outcomes (eg, any malformation) to test specific hypotheses or verify suspected safety signals, potentially overlooking or masking other clinically significant associations (eg, individual malformations or malformation types).ObjectiveTo conduct a comprehensive, systematic screening for potential teratogenic safety alerts associated with first-trimester exposure to individual antipsychotics, using a tree-based scan statistic (TBSS) approach for simultaneous evaluation of a broad range of specific malformations and malformation groupings.MethodsUsing a US-nationwide cohort of >4.2 million mother–child dyads (2000–2020), pregnancies with ≥1 first-trimester antipsychotic dispensing were compared with antipsychotic-unexposed pregnancies. Individual congenital malformations were identified via International Classification of Diseases codes, grouped into increasingly aggregated higher-level clinically related categories. Leveraging this hierarchical classification tree, TBSS was used to scan for associations with individual malformation codes and code categories while tightly controlling type 1 error. Confounding was adjusted for via propensity score fine-stratification, and relative risks (RRs) were estimated using an unconditional Poisson scan statistic. The p-values were used to prioritise alerts for further investigation, and follow-up analyses were conducted to refine the understanding of statistical alerts.FindingsExposed pregnancies ranged from 68 (fluphenazine) to 18 366 (prochlorperazine). Antipsychotic-exposed versus antipsychotic-unexposed women tended to be older and to have a higher comorbidity burden and more healthcare encounters. Alerts for an increased risk (with p<0.1) were observed for skin anomaly after haloperidol (RR=1.88) and polydactyly after ziprasidone (RR=3.06) exposure. Results were consistent in sensitivity analyses. The data in the two external data sources were too sparse to confirm a safety alert.ConclusionsTBSS identified two potential alerts previously unreported for prenatal antipsychotic exposure. Importantly, no alerts for severe or life-threatening malformations were detected. Findings from this screening-based approach, therefore, align with existing evidence suggesting that antipsychotics are unlikely to be major teratogens.Clinical implicationsThe potential risk increase observed for some less-severe malformations and some antipsychotics needs to be weighed against the high potential for relapse and mental health deterioration following treatment discontinuation. While no consistent patterns suggesting a strong teratogenic effect have emerged thus far for newer antipsychotics, continued monitoring of these medications is important.

Background

Antipsychotic medications are widely used during pregnancy, particularly for schizophrenia, bipolar disorder and other conditions such as treatment-resistant depression and anxiety.1 Teratogenicity is often the primary concern in pregnancy due to the significant morbidity and, in very severe cases, childhood mortality associated with major congenital malformations. Despite a relatively extensive literature,28 most studies on prenatal antipsychotic exposure have focused on composite outcomes or selected malformations and considered exposure to any antipsychotic or exposure at the drug class level (ie, atypical and typical antipsychotics, respectively). Information for individual antipsychotics, particularly more recently approved medications (eg, asenapine, lurasidone and brexpiprazole), is still limited. This lack of evidence poses a major challenge for pregnant patients (hereafter referred to as pregnant women) and their providers who need to make treatment decisions.

Studies evaluating medication safety in pregnancy typically focus on composite outcomes, often to conserve statistical power.9 For instance, researchers may assess the presence of any major congenital malformation or of organ-specific groups such as cardiovascular malformations. However, given their aetiologic heterogeneity, combining multiple malformations into a single outcome—even within the same organ system (eg, cardiac septal closure and aortic arch anomalies)—may lack a sound embryologic basis.10 Furthermore, studies assessing individual outcomes often focus on selected outcomes that have been suggested to be associated with prenatal exposure to the medication of interest based on prior human data (eg, case studies or spontaneous reports) or animal studies. Yet, the potential adverse effects of a medication are not always readily apparent based on its biological action or based on adverse effects observed in animal studies. To make an informed decision of whether to initiate, continue or discontinue a certain medication in pregnancy, pregnant women and their providers must balance the treatment benefits against all risks for the woman and her unborn child.

There is a need to implement systematic safety surveillance approaches that enable a comprehensive evaluation of adverse maternal and child outcomes. One such approach is the tree-based scan statistic (TBSS) method, which uses a hierarchical outcome classification tree to systematically scan for associations with the exposure of interest across a broad range of potential adverse effects.11 Both the US Food and Drug Administration and the Centres for Disease Control and Prevention have used this method for several medication and vaccine safety surveillance activities outside of pregnancy.12 13 While its use for safety studies in pregnancy is still novel,1416 our group has previously shown its promise for identifying potential teratogenic risks based on test cases.14 16 In this study, we leverage the TBSS to screen for potential teratogenic signals associated with individual antipsychotic medications.

Objective

The objective of this project was, therefore, to conduct a comprehensive systematic safety screening of potential teratogenic signals associated with first-trimester exposure to individual antipsychotic medications, using a TBSS approach within two large, US-nationwide cohorts of publicly and commercially insured pregnant women linked to their liveborn children.

Methods

Data sources and study cohorts

Using predefined mother–infant linked pregnancy cohorts nested within the Medicaid Analytic eXtract/Transformed Medicaid Statistical Information System Analytic Files (MAX/TAF, 2000–2018) and the Merative MarketScan Commercial Claims Database (MarketScan, 2003–2020),17 18 we restricted our study to pregnant women aged 12–55 years, with continuous insurance coverage from ≥3 months before the first day of the last menstrual period (LMP) (to allow for a complete medication and health status history) to ≥1 month after delivery (to capture relevant delivery and perinatal information (eg, codes indicating preterm birth to establish gestational length)) and their liveborn children, with insurance coverage for ≥3 months after birth (to ensure comprehensive identification of congenital malformations, many of which may not be diagnosed immediately at birth but during early paediatric care) unless the child died sooner. Although the cohort development algorithms were originally established using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-9 codes, all definitions (including delivery identification and gestational age algorithms) were systematically converted to ICD-10 following published guidance, and internal validity assessments confirmed consistent cohort capture and gestational age distribution across both coding eras. Pregnancies with foetal chromosomal anomalies were excluded, as the cause of the outcomes of interest in these pregnancies is unlikely to be related to antipsychotic exposure; likewise, pregnancies with exposure to known teratogenic medications were excluded, as these medications present a known increased risk of adverse pregnancy or birth outcomes (figure 1 and online supplemental eFigure 1). Both datasets contain rich patient-level information on demographics, insurance enrolment, outpatient medication dispensings, outpatient, inpatient and emergency department visits and their accompanying diagnostic and procedure codes.

Exposure

Exposure was based on the presence of ≥1 outpatient antipsychotic dispensing during the first trimester as the aetiologically relevant window for congenital malformations. We focused on individual antipsychotics, restricting them to medications with ≥50 exposed pregnancies per cohort (MAX/TAF and MarketScan, respectively), as estimates would be too imprecise to be meaningful for lower numbers. If ≥50 exposed pregnancies were only observed in one but not both cohorts (eg, in MAX/TAF but not in MarketScan), analyses for the individual medication were only conducted in the cohort meeting the requirement. While prochlorperazine, a typical antipsychotic, is primarily used to control severe nausea and vomiting (unlike the other antipsychotic medications), given its common use in women of childbearing age, it was still considered an exposure of interest. Pregnancies with no antipsychotic dispensing from 90 days before LMP to 90 days thereafter were considered unexposed (reference). This 90-day pre-LMP restriction was applied to reduce the risk that residual supply from an earlier dispensing might be used in early pregnancy (figure 1 and online supplemental eTable 1).

Congenital malformations

We identified all children with ≥1 recorded congenital malformation based on the ICD-9/10 coding system (ICD-9: 740.x–759.x; ICD-10: Q00.x–Q99.x) either in the child records within the first 3 months after birth or in the maternal records within the first month after delivery. Maternal records were considered because insurance claims are sometimes recorded under the mother while the child’s eligibility is being processed.

Covariates

We considered a broad range of potential confounders, including maternal demographic characteristics (race/ethnicity (available in MAX/TAF only), US state, year of delivery and age), treatment indications and other mental disorders (eg, anxiety, attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder, bipolar disorder, depression, psychosis and schizophrenia), other maternal conditions (eg, diabetes, hypertension, multiple gestation and obstetric comorbidity index19), substance use (eg, alcohol use disorder, drug use disorder and smoking), concomitant medication exposure (eg, antiseizure medications, antidepressants and prescription opioids) and proxies for healthcare utilisation (eg, number of outpatient visits, hospitalisations and emergency department visits) (online supplemental eTable 2).

TBSS approach

The TBSS approach used for this project scans a congenital malformation tree—which we previously developed based on the hierarchical ICD coding system16—for associations with the individual antipsychotic medication of interest (online supplemental eFigure 2). By grouping clinical concepts into increasingly aggregated higher-level clinically related categories, it therefore allows for the evaluation of individual malformations, as well as of malformation groupings (more details on this approach, including the number of outcome categories tested at each level of the tree, are provided in online supplemental eFigure 2 and previous work16). TBSS tightly controls the type 1 error rate and tests the null hypothesis that exposure to antipsychotics does not increase the risk of any congenital malformation. The alternative hypothesis is that there is at least one outcome node (ie, individual malformation code or clinically related code grouping) in the tree for which risk is elevated with exposure. The outcome nodes are ranked according to their p-values. These p-values were not used to establish causality; rather, they were used to identify and prioritise statistical alerts for further investigation. Consistent with the exploratory nature of TBSS and to avoid ambiguity, we refer to TBSS-identified associations as ‘statistical alerts’ throughout the manuscript. Such observed statistical alerts must therefore be interpreted cautiously with careful consideration from clinical and methodological perspectives.

Statistical analyses

Separately for each exposure-reference contrast and each cohort (ie, MAX/TAF and MarketScan), propensity scores (PS) were estimated based on logistic regression models which included all prespecified covariates. After trimming observations from the non-overlapping regions of the PS distribution, PS fine stratification (with 50 strata) was used for confounding control, with weighting of the unexposed based on the distribution of the exposed among PS strata.20

After pooling the weighted PS strata from both cohorts, an unconditional Poisson TBSS approach was used to calculate log likelihood ratios, relative risks (RR) and p values for each outcome node.11 15 21 Only outcome nodes with >3 exposed cases were tested to reduce the risk of chance findings (ie, false positives) based on very small numbers. We prespecified two thresholds to define statistical alerts. In addition to the traditionally used p-value threshold of 0.05 to identify statistical alerts, which provides stricter type 1 error control but lower power to detect alerts, we also considered a more lenient threshold of p<0.1 to reduce the risk of missing potential alerts (ie, false negatives). We additionally provide summary statistics on all nodes in the tree with p<1 in online supplemental file 1.

A range of sensitivity analyses was conducted, using TBSS. First, to address potential outcome misclassification owing to maternal rather than infant conditions, we restricted it to malformation codes recorded only in the infant claims. Second, while the primary outcome definition required only one malformation code—prioritising sensitivity over specificity and statistical power—such a definition might reflect a rule-out diagnosis rather than a confirmed condition. To reduce potential outcome misclassification, we therefore required ≥2 diagnostic codes for the respective malformation to be recorded on separate dates.22 Third, since filling a prescription does not mean that the medication was taken as prescribed, to reduce the risk of exposure misclassification, we redefined exposure as having ≥2 dispensings within the first trimester, under the assumption that if a prescription is refilled, consumption of the medication is more likely. Fourth, to reduce the risk of potential alerts being caused by another antipsychotic drug taken during the same assessment period, we restricted our analyses to pregnancies exposed to only the medication of interest (monotherapy). Fifth, to account for potential residual confounding, a high-dimensional PS approach was used, which included 200 empirically identified covariates in addition to the predefined covariates.23 Lastly, to mitigate confounding by indication, we restricted our analyses to pregnancies with a recorded diagnosis of bipolar disorder, psychosis or schizophrenia.

Furthermore, we conducted two post hoc exploratory assessments to follow up on potential alerts outside of TBSS. First, we stratified analyses by race/ethnicity given that prior evidence suggests variations in the prevalence of the outcomes for which alerts were observed (see the Findings section for details). Second, we evaluated whether the associations were replicated, assessing crude exposure-specific risks of the observed alerts within two external data sources: (1) the National Pregnancy Registry for Atypical Antipsychotics (NPRAA),24 established in 2008 and (2) nationwide health register data from three Nordic countries (Iceland (2004–2017), Norway (2005–2020) and Sweden (2006–2019)).3 25 Both data sources have previously been used extensively for medication safety studies in pregnancy.

Analyses were conducted using TreeScan21 and SAS V.9.4 (SAS Institute). This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines for observational studies (online supplemental eAppendix 1).

Findings

Cohort characteristics

Both cohorts combined included 4 228 783 unexposed pregnancies (with MAX/TAF contributing 2 456 929 and MarketScan 1 778 342 pregnancies). Quetiapine was the most commonly used atypical antipsychotic (n=10 209 pregnancies), followed by aripiprazole (n=5992). Among typical antipsychotics, prochlorperazine was most commonly used (n=18 366), followed by haloperidol (n=679) (figure 1, online supplemental eTable 1).

Compared with antipsychotic-unexposed women, women with antipsychotic exposure tended to be older, of White race (MAX/TAF only), to have a higher burden of mental disorders, other comorbidities and substance use, to be dispensed other medications, and to have more healthcare encounters (table 1 and online supplemental eTable 3). After PS weighting, characteristics were generally well balanced (with absolute standardised mean differences <10%26) between exposed and unexposed groups (online supplemental eTable 3).

Main analysis

When focusing on the traditional p-value threshold of <0.05, the only statistical alert detected was for anomalies of the integument after haloperidol exposure (RR=1.88, based on 37 exposed cases in both cohorts combined), driven by skin anomalies (RR=1.88, 35 exposed cases). When extending the threshold to include alerts with p<0.1, we additionally identified an increased risk in polydactyly of fingers after ziprasidone exposure (RR=3.06, 12 exposed cases) (table 2). Although there were no alerts for these malformations for other individual drugs at the prespecified threshold, RRs were elevated for several drugs, yet with high p-values due to the generally small numbers of exposed cases (online supplemental eTable 4).

Sensitivity analyses

While the magnitude of the two associations observed in the main analysis remained or even strengthened in sensitivity analyses, for some analyses, the p-value threshold (<0.1) was no longer met due to smaller sample sizes (eg, when requiring ≥2 diagnostic codes for skin anomalies). Other potential alerts identified across multiple sensitivity analyses (but not in the main analysis) with p<0.1 were limb anomalies after perphenazine exposure (RR range=4.56–5.30, <11 exposed cases; driven by polydactyly) and an unspecific code for multiple congenital anomalies after ziprasidone exposure (RR range=3.67–9.70, <11 exposed cases) (table 2 and online supplemental eTable 4). A claims profile review of ziprasidone-exposed children with this multiple congenital anomaly code did not reveal any consistent malformation patterns.

Post hoc exploratory analyses to refine understanding of statistical alerts for potential safety concerns

Given that the incidence of the two alerts from the main analysis—skin anomaly and polydactyly—might vary across racial/ethnic groups27 28 (eg, Black compared with White individuals have a higher risk for polydactyly, particularly postaxial type B polydactyly,27 as well as for certain skin disorders, such as pigmentary disorders28), we conducted exploratory analyses stratified by Black and White individuals, respectively, using the MAX/TAF cohort. Exposed counts were too sparse to conduct analyses stratified by other racial/ethnic groups. For skin anomaly, RRs after haloperidol exposure were 1.70 (95% CI 1.07 to 2.72) among Black individuals and 2.26 (95% CI 1.25 to 4.10) among White individuals; for polydactyly, respective RRs were 3.72 (95% CI 1.90 to 7.25) and 1.63 (95% CI 0.40 to 6.70) (online supplemental eFigure 3).

Within the NPRAA data source, only one polydactyly case (after aripiprazole exposure) was identified among >3000 pregnancies exposed to an atypical antipsychotic medication in the first trimester. No polydactyly cases were found among ziprasidone-exposed pregnancies. Skin anomalies were considered minor congenital malformations and therefore not documented in the registry. Within the Nordic register data, which included information on >2.4 million pregnancies—12 654 with first-trimester antipsychotic exposure—no skin anomaly cases were identified among 128 haloperidol-exposed pregnancies and no polydactyly cases among 61 ziprasidone-exposed pregnancies. There were several other individual antipsychotics, however, for which an increased risk of these two outcomes was observed in unadjusted analyses, although based on a small number of exposed cases (online supplemental eFigure 4).

Discussion

In this large US-nationwide cohort study including information for >4.2 million pregnancies, we applied a systematic safety surveillance approach to screen for potential alerts associated with individual antipsychotic medications across a comprehensive range of individual congenital malformations and malformation groupings. There were two statistical alerts for potential safety concerns that had not previously been reported. Importantly, no alerts for severe or life-threatening malformations were detected. While these findings should not be interpreted as confirmatory of safety or risk given the hypothesis-generating nature of this method, they align with existing evidence suggesting that antipsychotics are unlikely to be major teratogens.

Several studies have assessed the risk of congenital malformations following in utero antipsychotic medication exposure.28 Results generally suggest limited or no teratogenic effect when considering malformations as a composite outcome and focusing on antipsychotics overall or antipsychotic classes. For individual malformations or malformation subtypes, potential safety alerts have previously been reported for cardiovascular malformations, oral clefts, neural tube defects, hip dysplasia, limb reduction defects, anorectal atresia/stenosis, gastroschisis, hydrocephalus, other specific brain anomalies and oesophageal disorders. In a previous study, we focused on individual antipsychotics and malformations that had previously been suggested to be associated with antipsychotics and presented results in a way that accounted for the amount of available information to convey the level of confidence in our findings: for exposure-outcome combinations with a substantial amount of information available, no consistent alerts emerged; for combinations with less information, some alerts were observed, but all were based on very imprecise estimates.3 The current study extends this approach by evaluating a much broader malformation range while controlling the overall rate of false-positive alerts. In this study, only two potential alerts were identified: skin anomalies associated with haloperidol and polydactyly following ziprasidone exposure. Although neither of these alerts has been reported before, systematic bias is unlikely. First, findings were consistent across several sensitivity analyses, emphasising their robustness within our study cohorts. Second, observing an increased risk for only specific malformations, but not others, argues against residual confounding, as such confounding is expected to result in broader, non-specific associations. Third, although the potential mechanism for polydactyly remains unclear, an association with skin anomalies is possible given what is known about antipsychotic-induced side effects: antipsychotics are known to cause adverse skin reactions, which are mostly benign (eg, exanthematous eruptions, skin pigmentation changes and urticaria).29 While these adverse effects relate to individuals taking the medication rather than infants indirectly exposed in utero, similar effects through transplacental transmission are plausible. However, it is unclear why such an effect would be preferentially seen among haloperidol-exposed infants. The finding that most infants had the skin anomaly only recorded once within the first 3 months of life suggests that these anomalies are likely minor and might resolve spontaneously, similar to the adverse skin reactions described for patients directly exposed to these medications. Since we focused on malformations after first-trimester antipsychotic exposure, future work could explore other skin conditions beyond malformations (eg, skin rash, urticaria and others) and different prenatal exposure periods (eg, closer to delivery). Future work could also focus on potentially shared pharmacological mechanisms across multiple antipsychotics once more data accumulate. While point estimates for skin anomaly and polydactyly were elevated for several antipsychotics (in our study as well as the Nordic cohort), these were all based on very few exposed cases, and we therefore cannot draw conclusions about the presence of a broader-level antipsychotic effect at this time.

Even if the observed alerts were causal, they should be viewed in the context of their clinical relevance: skin anomalies are considered minor malformations (and as such, were not documented in the NPRAA), with, therefore, likely minimal to no medical, functional or cosmetic significance. Similarly, although polydactyly is often considered a major malformation, given its low prevalence (~0.3% among unexposed), even a threefold increase in risk would still only affect <1% of prenatally antipsychotic-exposed children. More importantly, polydactyly, as an isolated condition, does not affect the child’s overall health, development or survival and can typically be addressed through a surgical procedure.

Although exploratory analyses stratified by race were conducted to assess potential effect modification based on differences in background prevalence of these outcomes, the small numbers and wide CIs preclude any meaningful interpretation, and no clear evidence of differential risk by race emerged.

A major strength of the study is its use of two large, nationwide pregnancy cohorts, which together are expected to be representative of the US pregnant population. Additionally, the application of advanced statistical methods and multiple sensitivity analyses helps mitigate potential biases such as confounding, exposure and outcome misclassification, increasing confidence in the reliability of findings.

Nevertheless, despite the large cohort size, the evaluation of newer medications remains limited due to the generally small number of women exposed to these medications in pregnancy (eg, asenapine and brexpiprazole). For some medications (eg, iloperidone), numbers were too small (<50 exposed pregnancies) for any formal analysis. Continued monitoring of these medications is therefore important. There were additional challenges that deserved further comment. The intention of using a hierarchical outcome tree that groups individual malformations into increasingly aggregated higher-level clinically related categories was to reduce the risk of missing important alerts (ie, false negatives) due to limited power, particularly for rare exposures or outcomes. We further minimised the risk of false negatives by using a more lenient p-value threshold (<0.1), rather than focusing only on the traditional 0.05 cut-off. Nevertheless, a trade-off when opting for fewer false negatives is the increased risk of chance findings (ie, false positives). While the TBSS approach formally adjusts for multiple testing, when screening across such a broad range of outcomes and focusing on individual medications, false positive findings cannot be completely ruled out. In addition, we imposed cohort restrictions, such as requiring continuous enrolment throughout pregnancy and restricting to pregnancies with successful linkage to the liveborn infant. These restrictions reduced the overall cohort size but were essential to ensure data completeness and the internal validity of our findings. Although such enrolment may result in the selection of a specific subpopulation, the selection criteria are unlikely to alter the underlying biological relationships under study, and the findings should remain generalisable to the broader US obstetric population. Lastly, although not a limitation of the study, it should be recognised that while malformations are typically the outcomes of most immediate concern, other important outcomes were not considered (eg, spontaneous abortion, stillbirth and preterm birth). The TBSS approach can be extended to these outcomes but requires the creation of a hierarchical tree that includes clinically meaningful groupings for these adverse events.

A key aspect of this study was the effort to replicate identified alerts across multiple data sources to distinguish true safety concerns from chance findings. Each data source and study design has relative strengths and weaknesses. Our approach combined the power of a large-scale screening method applied to two large US-based pregnancy cohorts to identify potential safety concerns with the evaluation of identified statistical alerts in an independent but similar health record-based data source, as well as a cohort from a distinct prospective registry study, allowing us to capitalise on the complementary strengths of these different data sources. Although the two alerts observed in our data were not confirmed in the external data, it should be noted that low use of these specific drugs and no recording of skin anomalies in the registry make it challenging to draw firm conclusions.

Conclusions and clinical implications

In summary, by applying advanced statistical methods in a systematic screening approach to large pregnancy cohorts, we generated important insights into potential alerts for the teratogenicity of individual antipsychotics. No alerts were identified for more severe or even life-threatening malformations, consistent with existing evidence suggesting that antipsychotics are unlikely to be major teratogens. The potential risk increase observed for some less-severe malformations and some antipsychotics needs to be weighed against the high potential for relapse and mental health deterioration following treatment discontinuation.30 While there are limited data for newer medications, no consistent patterns have emerged thus far, and continued monitoring will be important. Moving forward, expanding this framework to screen for associations with other pregnancy outcomes could further enhance our understanding of antipsychotic safety in pregnancy. As pregnant women and their providers must balance the benefits of treatment against all possible adverse effects, such efforts would help ensure access to the most comprehensive evidence to guide treatment decisions.
