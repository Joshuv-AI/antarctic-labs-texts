Nephrocalcinosis Following Roux-en-Y Gastric Bypass Secondary to Suspected Enteric Hyperoxaluria: A Case Report
Authors: Marissa Castronovo, Alejandro Biglione
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nephrocalcinosis refers to the deposition of calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate in the kidney, most commonly within the renal medulla, and is frequently linked with underlying metabolic abnormalities. Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) is a well-established risk factor for enteric hyperoxaluria secondary to fat malabsorption, thereby increasing the risk of nephrolithiasis, oxalate nephropathy, and nephrocalcinosis. Despite this association, nephrocalcinosis following RYGB remains an uncommon and underrecognized clinical entity. A 36-year-old male with a history of complicated peptic ulcer disease, multiple prior abdominal surgeries, and previous RYGB presented with acute abdominal pain and distention. Laboratory evaluation revealed an elevated creatinine of 2.27 mg/dL, a suppressed parathyroid hormone level of 6.3 pg/mL, and hypocalcemia with serum calcium of 8.1 mg/dL. Imaging studies revealed bilateral renal medullary calcifications, consistent with nephrocalcinosis. Given the patient’s history of RYGB, chronic pancreatitis, multiple GI surgeries, and evidence of chronic malnutrition, chronic malabsorptive physiology leading to enteric hyperoxaluria was hypothesized to be the most plausible explanation. However, measurements of 24-hour urinary oxalate, calcium, citrate, and vitamin D, as well as a renal biopsy, were not obtained, thereby precluding definitive confirmation of this proposed mechanism. This case highlights the importance of considering metabolic and renal complications in patients with complex GI disease following bariatric surgery and emphasizes the role of comprehensive metabolic evaluation and renal imaging in improving early recognition, diagnostic accuracy, and long-term kidney outcomes.

Introduction

Nephrocalcinosis is defined as the deposition of calcium oxalate or calcium phosphate in the kidney [1]. It is broadly categorized into microscopic and macroscopic forms, reflecting the severity of renal involvement. Microscopic nephrocalcinosis is diagnosed by mineral deposits on light microscopy but not on radiographs, and it often represents early disease. In contrast, macroscopic nephrocalcinosis is diagnosed by calcification visible on abdominal radiographs, ultrasound, or CT scans. The renal medulla is most frequently involved, whereas cortical involvement is less common [2]. Clinically, most individuals are asymptomatic, with findings often discovered incidentally on abdominal and chest radiographs. Progression is typically indolent, which may delay diagnosis until more advanced disease develops.

Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) is increasingly recognized as a risk factor for enteric hyperoxaluria because fat malabsorption promotes calcium saponification within the intestinal lumen. As calcium binds to free fatty acids, less calcium is available to bind dietary oxalate, thereby increasing colonic oxalate absorption and urinary oxalate excretion. Persistent hyperoxaluria may ultimately contribute to a spectrum of renal complications, including nephrolithiasis and nephrocalcinosis [3]. Similar mechanisms may also occur in other GI disorders associated with chronic fat malabsorption, including chronic pancreatitis, which can further promote enteric hyperoxaluria and oxalate nephropathy [4].

Patients undergoing RYGB have an approximately twofold increased risk of nephrolithiasis, and hyperoxaluria develops in an estimated 20-75% of patients, with nearly 20% exhibiting markedly elevated urinary oxalate levels [5]. Despite the relatively high prevalence of hyperoxaluria, nephrocalcinosis remains uncommon and is largely described in isolated case reports. Because these metabolic abnormalities may remain clinically silent until renal injury develops, early recognition of high-risk patients is important.

This report describes a patient with prior RYGB, chronic pancreatitis, and extensive GI surgery who developed nephrocalcinosis in the setting of chronic malabsorptive physiology. Although objective metabolic confirmation of enteric hyperoxaluria was unavailable, this case emphasizes the importance of recognizing renal complications in patients with chronic GI disease and highlights the value of a comprehensive metabolic evaluation in improving diagnostic accuracy.

Case presentation

A 36-year-old male with a history of duodenal ulcer disease, bowel stricture, chronic pancreatitis, and prior RYGB presented with acute abdominal pain and distension. His surgical history was significant for perforated duodenal ulcers requiring partial gastric resection approximately 10 years prior, followed by antrectomy with RYGB. Postoperative complications included small bowel obstruction requiring exploratory laparotomy with lysis of adhesions. Additional interventions included gastric outlet stricture requiring endoscopic balloon dilation, cholecystectomy, gastrojejunal anastomotic ulcer, refractory esophagitis, and necrotizing pancreatitis managed with endoscopic retrograde cholangiopancreatography for pancreatic fistula. Recent endoscopy demonstrated recurrent duodenal ulcer disease.

The patient presented to the emergency department with acute-onset abdominal pain and bloating that started approximately three hours before arrival. Symptoms progressed to marked abdominal distension with diffuse, achy, non-radiating, fluctuating pain that was worse with light palpation. The pain was localized to the periumbilical area and was associated with nausea. He reported normal bowel movements the day prior and continued passage of flatus. He reported chronic GI symptoms since prior abdominal surgery, including intolerance of large meals and the need for small, frequent meals to avoid exacerbation of his symptoms. Home medications included pantoprazole and sucralfate.

Vital signs were stable upon arrival. BMI was 19.58 kg/m². The patient appeared uncomfortable secondary to pain. Abdominal examination revealed a midline laparotomy scar, with notable abdominal distension, diffuse tenderness, and abdominal firmness. Rebound tenderness and guarding were absent, and bowel sounds were present.

Initial laboratory evaluation included a comprehensive metabolic panel and complete blood count. Cardiac troponins, urine toxicology, and urinalysis were unremarkable. Laboratory findings are summarized in Table 1.

Notable abnormalities included elevated creatinine of 2.27 mg/dL, suppressed parathyroid hormone level of 6.3 pg/mL, and hypocalcemia measuring 8.1 mg/dL. Additional findings included hypoalbuminemia (1.6 g/dL) and microcytic anemia with a hemoglobin of 9.6 g/dL. Iron studies were consistent with iron deficiency, including a serum iron of 26 mcg/dL, total iron-binding capacity of 188 mcg/dL, and transferrin level of 150.0 mg/dL. Renal function improved during hospitalization, with creatinine decreasing to 1.19 mg/dL.

The constellation of electrolyte abnormalities, malnutrition, chronic GI disease, and radiologic evidence of renal medullary calcifications raised concern for an underlying metabolic disorder related to chronic malabsorption following RYGB. However, urinary oxalate testing, 24-hour urine calcium, oxalate, and citrate studies, vitamin D measurements, and renal biopsy were not obtained, limiting biochemical confirmation of enteric hyperoxaluria and oxalate nephropathy. Therefore, enteric hyperoxaluria remained a suspected mechanism rather than a biochemically confirmed diagnosis, representing an important limitation of this case report.

CT of the abdomen and pelvis without contrast revealed significant fecal material within bowel loops consistent with constipation. No evidence of bowel obstruction or pneumoperitoneum was identified. There was no retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy. Both kidneys demonstrated multiple hyperdense heterogeneous areas within the medullary regions, consistent with nephrocalcinosis (Figure 1).

Renal ultrasound revealed right and left kidney measurements of 9.7 × 4.8 × 4.2 cm and 10.0 × 5.7 × 4.1 cm, respectively. Both kidneys exhibited normal morphology and cortical echogenicity without hydronephrosis, masses, or discrete calculi. The renal medullary regions appeared hyperechoic bilaterally (Figure 2).

Management was conservative and centered on supportive measures. Treatment included bowel regimen support, continuation of pantoprazole and sucralfate, intravenous hydration, and pain control. Renal function improved with supportive care, fluid resuscitation, and monitoring. Hypoalbuminemia was addressed with nutritional support, dietary counseling, and protein supplementation, while iron deficiency anemia was managed with oral iron replacement.

Symptoms gradually improved during the hospital course. No surgical intervention was indicated, as imaging revealed no acute abdominal pathology or evidence of intestinal obstruction. The patient remained hemodynamically stable and tolerated oral intake prior to discharge. He was discharged on day 5 in stable condition with continuation of proton pump inhibitor therapy, sucralfate, and iron supplementation, along with recommendations for further nutritional support. Outpatient follow-up was recommended with primary care, gastroenterology, nephrology, and pain management for further evaluation of chronic abdominal symptoms and renal findings.

Overall, the history of RYGB, chronic pancreatitis, chronic nutritional deficiencies, and bilateral renal medullary calcifications suggested an acquired metabolic process related to chronic GI malabsorption. Because comprehensive metabolic testing and renal biopsy were not performed, the proposed mechanism of enteric hyperoxaluria could not be confirmed.

Discussion

Renal medullary calcification arises from dysregulated mineral metabolism involving calcium, phosphate, and oxalate in the kidney [6]. Etiologies span a spectrum of disorders associated with hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, hyperphosphatemia, and hyperoxaluria [2,6]. Notable associated conditions include primary hyperparathyroidism, distal renal tubular acidosis, and medullary sponge kidney [7]. Incidental recognition on imaging should prompt further diagnostic evaluation for systemic and metabolic etiologies [6].

A key mechanism of nephrocalcinosis is enteric hyperoxaluria, which arises when fat malabsorption increases intestinal oxalate absorption by reducing calcium binding in the GI tract, leading to elevated urinary oxalate excretion [8-10]. Chronic hyperoxaluria may subsequently contribute to oxalate stone formation, oxalate nephropathy, and nephrocalcinosis [10].

Most notably, RYGB is a well-recognized risk factor and cause of enteric hyperoxaluria due to its malabsorptive effects. It has been associated with hyperoxaluria, nephrolithiasis, and oxalate nephropathy [10-13]. Multiple studies have documented cases of worsening renal function, acute kidney injury, and oxalate nephropathy months to years following RYGB [14-16]. Similar findings have been reported following other bariatric procedures, reinforcing the critical role of fat malabsorption in causing hyperoxaluria [17,18].

In this patient, the combination of prior RYGB, chronic pancreatitis, multiple GI surgeries, hypoalbuminemia, iron deficiency anemia, low BMI, hypocalcemia, and bilateral renal medullary calcifications on CT and ultrasound supported chronic malabsorptive physiology as a plausible explanation for nephrocalcinosis. Although these clinical, laboratory, and imaging findings were consistent with the proposed mechanism, the absence of 24-hour urinary measurements of oxalate, calcium, citrate, and vitamin D, as well as a renal biopsy, precluded definitive biochemical confirmation of enteric hyperoxaluria.

Several features of this case support chronic malabsorptive physiology as a plausible contributor to the patient's nephrocalcinosis. RYGB alters normal GI anatomy and promotes fat malabsorption. Furthermore, the patient's history of chronic pancreatitis may have further impaired fat digestion through pancreatic exocrine dysfunction [4]. Increased intraluminal free fatty acids bind intestinal calcium, reducing the amount of calcium available to complex with dietary oxalate and thereby increasing intestinal oxalate absorption [10,12].

Alternative etiologies were considered but deemed less likely based on the available clinical findings and laboratory data. Interestingly, the suppressed parathyroid hormone level despite hypocalcemia was atypical and argued against primary hyperparathyroidism as the cause of nephrocalcinosis. Although the etiology of this laboratory abnormality could not be determined without additional endocrine evaluation, it made hyperparathyroidism less likely. The absence of vitamin D measurements and additional endocrine evaluation limited further interpretation of this finding. Distal renal tubular acidosis was not supported by clinical or laboratory evidence. Although medullary sponge kidney remained in the differential diagnosis, the patient’s history of RYGB, chronic pancreatitis, and chronic malabsorptive features favored an acquired metabolic process.

Current management focuses on treating the underlying cause, reducing intestinal oxalate absorption, and minimizing calcium oxalate crystallization. Therapeutic strategies include increased fluid intake, reduction of dietary fat and oxalate intake, and calcium and citrate supplementation [19,20]. Long-term nephrology follow-up is critical for monitoring renal function and preventing progression to chronic kidney disease. Dietary and lifestyle modifications are essential to support long-term kidney health.

This report is limited by the absence of a comprehensive metabolic evaluation, including 24-hour urinary oxalate, calcium, and citrate measurements, vitamin D assessment, and histopathologic confirmation via renal biopsy, which limited causal inference regarding the proposed mechanism of nephrocalcinosis. Collectively, this case highlights the diagnostic challenges of nephrocalcinosis in patients with complex GI disease and the need to consider enteric hyperoxaluria in post-bariatric patients presenting with renal calcifications.

Conclusions

Nephrocalcinosis should prompt evaluation for underlying metabolic and GI disorders, particularly in patients with a history of bariatric surgery. This case highlights RYGB as a potential contributor to nephrocalcinosis through chronic malabsorptive physiology. Comprehensive biochemical evaluation and appropriate renal imaging may facilitate earlier recognition and intervention to reduce the risk of progression to chronic kidney disease and nephrolithiasis.
