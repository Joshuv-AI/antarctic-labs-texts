Incidence of acute heart failure with and without concomitant pulmonary disease
Authors: Ida Arentz Taraldsen, Jasmin Dam Lukoschewitz, Aginsha Kandiah, Frederik Dencker Wisborg, Mohammed El-Sheikh, Nora Olsen El Caidi, Ove Andersen, Jens Dahlgaard Hove, Johannes Grand
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Acute heart failure, Incidence, Prevalence, Comorbidity, Prognosis

Introduction

Acute heart failure (AHF) is the primary diagnosis for over one million emergency department (ED) hospitalizations each year in the United States (USA) and is a major cause of hospitalization in the elderly population [1,2]. The numbers are believed to be comparable in Europe, but to our knowledge there are few reports from a Scandinavian setting and none comparing patients with and without concomitant pulmonary disease [3]. Due to demographic changes, such as an ageing population, this burden is expected to continue to increase [4]. Within three months approximately one-third of all patients admitted with AHF will either die or be re-hospitalized [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Contrary to the great progress in other cardiovascular diseases, AHF is still associated with a poor outcome [9].

Heart failure is defined as a syndrome consisting of symptoms such as dyspnea or fatigue, combined with clinical findings that indicate pulmonary or systemic congestion [10]. There are many potential etiologies for heart failure, but in common, it leads to forward and/or backward failure [10]. AHF is defined as an episode of worsening or new-onset symptoms of heart failure leading to a hospital admission [11]. Immediate evaluation and treatment should be initiated within the first minutes or hours, depending on the severity [10].

Traditionally, AHF has been stratified according to perfusion status (warm or cold) and by signs of congestion (dry or wet), but this is primarily interesting in an academic context, as its clinical presentation can vary greatly. Identification and treatment of the patients may be easier based on the clinical presentation. Four different clinical presentations are described in the 2021 European guidelines for heart failure, separated by main mechanisms and cause of symptoms [10]. The most common form of AHF is acute decompensated heart failure (50–70 %), characterized by a gradual onset with left ventricular dysfunction, leading to fluid accumulation and increased intraventricular pressure. The second most common form of AHF is acute pulmonary edema, which has a rapid onset, typically within hours and accounts for about 13–25 % of all cases [12]. The main mechanism can be increased afterload and/or left ventricular diastolic dysfunction, in some cases due to valvular disease [10]. Cardiogenic shock and isolated right ventricular failure accounts for less than 5 % of the population [12].

Patients admitted with acute heart failure are often comorbid and might have several competing causes for their symptoms, especially pulmonary diseases, such as a chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) exacerbation, pneumonia or other infections such as COVID-19. In COPD exacerbations, it was demonstrated that 70 % of readmissions resulted from decompensation or other morbidities, reinforcing both how often these conditions co exists, and how important it is to identify and treat all causes leading to a hospital admission [13].

The prevalence and characteristics of patients with AHF in Denmark, is thought to be similar to those in the rest of Europe and USA, however, to our knowledge, no Danish studies have described this.

The purpose of this study is to investigate the incidence and characteristics of patients admitted with AHF with and without concomitant pulmonary disease, compared to patients without AHF, in a large hospital in Denmark. Secondly, to investigate the mortality rate in AHF compared to patients admitted without AHF and how it is influenced by concomitant pulmonary disease.

Methods

Study design and population

This is a prospective cohort study, where all non-traumatic adult (≥18 years old) patients, admitted at the ED at Copenhagen University Hospital, Amager-Hvidovre, Denmark, between March 10th, 2020, and March 31st, 2022, were included consecutively. Patients without contact to the primary sector within the follow up period and patients without a social security number were excluded, as follow up was not possible. Patients were also excluded if they had no blood samples taken within 24 h, to ensure an evaluation of acutely admitted patients. As this was during the Corona Virus Disease 2019 (COVID-19) pandemic, all patients admitted to the ED were at risk of having infection with severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus (SARS-Cov) and were tested by point-of-care tests.

Study procedures

Upon arrival in the ED, a routine blood sample panel including hemoglobin, leukocytes with differential count, electrolytes, and markers of organ function (creatinine and alanine aminotransferase) were measured. In addition, the biomarker soluble urokinase Plasminogen Activator Receptor (suPAR) was obtained by a team of investigators at admission, which is reported elsewhere [14]. Biomarkers were analysed at the Department of Clinical Biochemistry, Copenhagen University Hospital, Hvidovre. Symptoms and baseline comorbidities were recorded from the electronic health records (EHR). The EHR contains all medical records from hospital admissions. Furthermore, it contains data on drug prescriptions and mortality.

Patients were treated according to the directions of the physician in charge, and the study protocol did not specify any specific guidelines for treatment or diagnostics. Patients were in general treated according to international guidelines for emergency medicine and cardiology. After inclusion was completed, clinical data was transferred from the EHR to the scientific database Research Electronic Data Capture System (REDCap) (Consortium, Nashville, TN, USA).

Diagnosis of acute heart failure

The electronic health record of included patients was assessed by trained medical doctors specialized within cardiology. Initial presentation, medical history, signs of congestion either systemically (such as edema or presence of S3 heart sound) or pulmonary assessed by either chest x-ray or lung ultrasound, echocardiography and natriuretic biomarkers, were assessed when making the diagnosis of AHF or not. In cases where the diagnosis was not clear, or when lab work and clinical presentation pointed in different directions, a consensus was made at a conference call of the entire investigator group. Furthermore, if patients had the admission diagnosis “lung oedema” or patients fulfilled the following criteria: (1) Oxygen demand >1 Liter/minute (L/min); (2) pro-brain natriuretic peptide (pro-BNP) >150 pmol/L at admission; and (3) administration of intravenous furosemide during the first 24 h of admission, they were assigned the AHF diagnosis.

The cohort was stratified into two groups depending on the presence of AHF. The group with AHF was further stratified according to the presence of concomitant pulmonary disease, defined as either COPD exacerbation, pneumonia or COVID-19 infection.

To estimate the prevalence and incidence of AHF, all readmissions for patients with AHF were defined as AHF-admissions and all readmissions for patients without AHF were defined as non-AHF-admissions. The follow up period was up to 2.2 years with a minimum of 60 days, until June 1, 2022.

Ethics

The study was approved by the Danish Data Protection Agency (record no. P-2020-513) and by the Patient Safety Authorities (record no. 31-1521-319) to allow the use of anonymized data for research purposes without informed consent and is in conformity with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki.

Outcomes

Outcome measurements were readmission-rate within one year, length of in-hospital stay during index admission and all-cause mortality.

Statistical analysis

Normally distributed data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), non-normally distributed data are presented as median (interquartile range (IQR)). Categorical data are presented as number (n) and percentages (%). Categorical data was compared with Fishers exact test. Continuous variables were tested for normality and analyzed with unpaired t-test when normally distributed, or Wilcoxon-Mann-Whitney test, in case of a non-normal distribution.

Logistic regression models were used to calculate odds ratios for having AHF depending on different baseline characteristics. All regression analyses were adjusted for age, sex, weight, and comorbidities, defined as one or more of the following: COPD, ischemic heart failure (IHD), diabetes mellitus type 1 or 2, hypertension, stroke or supraventricular tachycardia. Smoking and excessive alcohol consumption were defined as either active use or previous use. Kaplan Maier mortality curves were produced to illustrate crude mortality rate.

Statistical analyses and figures were made using R, version 4.3.0. All tests were 2-tailed, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

We included 7258 patients (1188 patients were excluded, see Fig. 1 for patient flowchart with exclusion criteria). Mean follow-up time was 506 ± 186 days. The mean age was 61 ± 20 years old and 52 % were female. The prevalence of AHF was 5.6 % for unique patients and 7.3 % including readmissions. The incidence rate for admission with AHF was 91/100.000 inhabitants/year, and the incidence rate per year is demonstrated in Supplemental Fig. I. When we included readmissions, the incidence rate was 302/100.000 inhabitants/year. The patients with AHF were older and had more comorbidities, compared to patients without AHF (Table 1). Baseline characteristics for patients with and without AHF are listed in Table 1. In the group with AHF, 106 patients (26 %) had a competing concomitant pulmonary cause of admission; 13 % had a COPD exacerbation, 8 % had pneumonia and 5 % had COVID-19. Baseline characteristics in the group with concomitant pulmonary disease was comparable with the group without, except for their medical history, where COPD was more frequent in the group with concomitant pulmonary disease, while ischemic heart failure, hypertension and heart failure were more frequent in the group without concomitant pulmonary disease (Supplemental Table I).

Outcome for patients with AHF

In the group with AHF, 176 patients (43.1 %) died within the follow-up period, compared with 1354 patients (19.8 %) in the group without AHF (see Supplemental Table II). Fig. 2a demonstrates the 1-year all-cause mortality rate for patients with and without AHF.

Kaplan meier mortality curves COPD, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease exacerbation; AHF, acute heart failure.

AHF was significantly associated with increased mortality: HR 2.6 (2.2–3.1, p < 0.001) compared to ED patients without AHF. Using Multivariable Cox regression, AHF was independently associated with mortality: HRadjusted was 1.34 (1.13–1.58, p-value < 0.001). See Supplemental Table IIa for the complete multivariable cox regression-model with all hazard ratios.

Patients with AHF had a significantly higher readmission rate within one year, as well as a longer mean length of in-hospital stay (Supplemental Table III), compared with patients without AHF.

Outcome for patients with AHF and concomitant pulmonary disease

Fig. 2b-c demonstrates the 1-year all-cause mortality rate for patients with AHF, stratified according to the presence of concomitant pulmonary disease. Concomitant pulmonary disease was significantly associated with increased mortality, both as one group (Fig. 2b), and stratified according to the different pulmonary diseases (Fig. 2c). Using Multivariable Cox regression, concomitant pulmonary disease was associated with a worse prognosis, as compared with AHF without concomitant pulmonary disease (HRadj 1.74 (1.24–2.44), p-value < 0.001). Discussion

In this large prospective trial, we provide insight into the clinical characteristics of typical Danish patients admitted with AHF. Multiple complementary methods were used to identify eligible patients, minimizing the risk of missed cases, including those with concomitant pulmonary disease. One in four patients had a coexisting pulmonary disease, contributing to the admission. Concomitant pulmonary disease was associated with increased mortality rates, highlighting the importance of intensive treatment in this multimorbid patient group.

Prevalence of acute heart failure

AHF was the reason for admission in more than 1 in 20 patients a substantially higher numbers than a Portuguese retrospective cohort from 2017, in which 1.1 % of ED admissions were due to acute decompensated heart failure [15]. In that study inclusion required ICD-9 coding for heart failure, as well as clinical sign of congestion, objective findings of systolic dysfunction or structural heart disease and increased levels of B-type natriuretic peptide. Furthermore, their population included all ED admission, whereas our study was restricted to non-traumatic patients. The population had similar baseline characteristics, but significant better prognosis. The prevalence is in great accordance with a previous Danish study, where 6 % of patients admitted with a medical diagnosis had AHF [3].

We found an incidence of 91/100,000 AHF hospitalizations per year. This is substantially lower than hospitalization rates in the Netherlands between 1980 and 1999, where the incidence was approximately 200–280/100,000 per year [16]. This may be partly be explained by the significant advances in chronic heart failure treatment over the past decades, as well as by the exclusion of readmissions [17].

Characteristics of acute heart failure

Patients age (75 vs. 77 years) and gender (44 % vs. 50 % women) were similar to a population in the United Kingdom and the Netherlands respectively, as well as history of heart failure (57 % vs. 56 %) [18,19]. Notably, these studies were both retrospective. Compared to patients with known heart failure in a large Swedish registry, the patients in our study admitted with AHF had a similar profile of comorbidities, with same prevalence of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, supraventricular tachycardia and stroke [20]. However, the prevalence of COPD was higher in our population (27 % vs. 13 %) [20]. The mean left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was similar to a Portuguese cohort (44 ± 15.1 vs. 43 ± 13 %) [15].

We found an increased odds ratio for AHF with increasing suPAR levels, a sensitive marker for inflammation, but not for CRP [25]. This remained significant in the multivariate model. Increased inflammatory markers is associated with adverse events in AHF and may be a contributing factor in the increasing mortality rates in patients with AHF with and without concomitant pulmonary disease [[26], [27], [28]].

Prognosis following an acute heart failure admission

Within the follow-up period 43 % of patients with AHF died, with most death occurring within the first year. This is higher than reported in the Portuguese cohort from 2017, in which only 28 % died [15]. However, their median follow-up was shorter − 5 months − compared to a mean follow-up of approximately 17 months in our study which might explain this difference. In a large prospective cohort with patients with all phenotypes of AHF the overall 1-year mortality rate was 26.7 %, substantially lower than our overall mortality (34.8 %), but closer to our mortality rate in patients with AHF without concomitant pulmonary disease (30.7 %) [21]. Notably, this cohort also included patients with cardiogenic shock, patients that in our region typically is hospitalized directly to a tertiary center. Our findings are very similar to the American cohort, ADHERE, which found a 36 % 1-year mortality [22].

A Dutch registry of patients with AHF as the primary diagnosis reported a mean hospital length of stay of 8.7 days. [19] Several large trials have similarly reported median lengths of stay ranging from 4 to 11 days, which aligns with our findings with a median of 5 days and a mean of 9 days. [[22], [23], [24]]. In the Dutch study 13.8 % of the patients were readmitted, in great contrast to our findings, where 75 % were readmitted once or more during the first year after their index admission [19].

Limitations

There are some limitations in this study that should be addressed. The population only consists of patients from one single hospital, in the Capital Region of Denmark with a high number of COVID-19 positive patients admitted during the pandemic, thus it might not be representative for the whole country. Patients without social security number and patients without contact to the primary sector were excluded, as follow up was not possible, which might induce a selection bias. As this is an observational study, we cannot conclude on causality and despite adjustments in the multivariable regression models, there will always be a risk of bias and residual confounding. Another limitation of this study is the time of inclusion, which as during the COVID-19-pandemic, this may have had a substantial impact on the characteristics and prevalence of AHF. The low rate of concomitant infection with COVID-19, might indicate that it has not. Further, we identified patients by several methods, one of these consisted of pro-BNP-levels, which was not taken for all patients, so there is a risk of underreporting. There is a risk of misclassification of AHF-admissions, especially in the calculations where we included readmissions, as we do not have data from these admissions. However, we believe the misclassification is probably equally distributed both ways and therefor evens out.

Conclusion

In this prospective observational cohort study, we found that 5 % of all admissions to the medical ED is caused by AHF. The incidence rate for admission with AHF was 91/100.000 inhabitants/year. AHF was associated with worse outcomes than patients without AHF. Concomitant pulmonary admission disease was associated with worse prognosis among patients with AHF, compared with patients admitted with AHF without pulmonary comorbidity.
