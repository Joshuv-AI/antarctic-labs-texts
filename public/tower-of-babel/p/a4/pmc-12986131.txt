From Polar Clusters to Active Nematics: Experimental Signatures of Swarming Dynamics in Bacterial Monolayers
Authors: Xiao Chen, Yaner Yan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial swarms provide a tractable natural model of active matter, where their dynamics illuminate the principles of collective behavior and self-organization phenomena. In particular, the mechanistic and dynamical features of monolayer swarming are critical in driving the transition to multilayer structures at the onset of biofilm formation. Here, we investigate monolayer swarms of Serratia marcescens across varying cell body aspect ratios and area fractions. The results show that at intermediate-to-low densities, bacteria form local dynamic clusters, with the distribution of cluster sizes determined by aspect ratio and area fraction. At higher densities, elongated bacteria align into active nematic states with half-integer topological defects, which point to a potential nucleation mechanism for multilayer formation. These findings provide new physical insights into how cellular morphology and density govern bacterial swarming dynamics and drive the early transition from monolayer swarming to multilayered biofilm development.

1. Introduction

Active matter, broadly defined as non-equilibrium systems of self-propelled agents, has emerged as a unifying framework for understanding a wide range of biological [1,2,3,4,5,6,7] and synthetic [8,9,10] phenomena. As a widely studied natural model of active matter, bacterial swarms display large-scale coherent flows and rich dynamics beyond equilibrium materials. Their accessibility and tunability through cellular and environmental parameters make them an ideal platform for exploring the physical principles of collective behavior and self-organization [7,11,12,13,14].

Previous studies in bacterial colonies [3,7,11,15,16] suggest that the short-range steric and long-range hydrodynamic interactions dominating swarming dynamics both strongly depend on bacterial population density and aspect ratio of cell body. Ilkanaiv et al. [11] demonstrated that cell shape has a more important effect on the statistics of swarm dynamics than theoretical prediction. Recently, Be’er et al. [14] derived a two-dimensional phase diagram of Bacillus subtilis swarming as a function of packing fraction and aspect ratio, where each phase exhibits distinct collective dynamical features, including mean squared displacements, cluster-size distribution, and correlation length. Complementary to statistical characterizations, mechanistic dynamical approaches based on nonlinear continuum theories and discrete-element modeling have been employed to explore the physical origins of collective organization in bacterial colonies. These models account for growth-induced stresses and anisotropic mechanical interactions and provide dynamical descriptions of streaming instabilities and buckling-induced ordering [17,18].

Flagellated bacteria rapidly spread and colonize surfaces via monolayer swarming and subsequently transition into three-dimensional biofilms as an adaptive response to environmental stresses [19,20,21]. Previous experimental and numerical studies have shown that the physical mechanisms and dynamical characteristics that underlie monolayer bacterial swarming are crucial for multilayer formation at the early stage of biofilm transition [21,22,23,24,25,26]. It is speculated that processes such as Motility Induced Phase Separation (MIPS) [21,27] or the nucleation of immotile cells triggered by a combination of mechanical effects and extracellular substance secretion [28] initiate bacterial aggregation and the transition from monolayer swarms to multilayered biofilm. Liu et al. [29] attributed mono- to bilayer transition to an interfacial process denoted as activity-induced interface bulging. The formation of bilayer cells in the liquid bulging areas arises from an increase in interfacial energy supported by activity-induced pressure, without requiring an immotile subpopulation of cells. Two reports on colonies of Myxococcus xanthus [30] and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [31] reveal that an influx of cells towards the +1/2 topological defects formed within densely populated monolayers promotes cell accumulation and vertical extrusion, driving the layering process. However, Yashunsky et al. [32] observed that −1/2 defects serve as sinks for bacterial flow in single-layer swarms of Bacillus subtilis, potentially facilitating vertical mixing within a multilayered bacterial colony.

Although significant progress has been made in understanding bacterial swarming, a comprehensive grasp of how cellular properties such as the morphology of cells influence the statistical characterization of swarming behavior and layering processes remains elusive. In this work, we investigated monolayer swarming of Serratia marcescens across a range of cell body aspect ratios and area fractions. Our findings highlight how cellular morphology and density regulate distinct collective dynamics in bacterial monolayer swarms. Importantly, topological defects formed in dense swarms of elongated bacteria may act as nucleation sites for multilayer formation, offering new insights into the mechanisms underlying biofilm development.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Experimental Methods

Frozen stocks of wild-type Serratia marcescens (ATCC 274) were inoculated into 4 mL of Luria Broth (LB) medium and incubated overnight at 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm. A 5 µL aliquot of the overnight culture (optical density OD650 ≈ 1.0) was then spotted at the center of a 0.5% Eiken agar plate containing 25 g/L LB, supplemented with 18 µg/mL Brij-35—an exogenous surfactant that promotes colony expansion by facilitating monolayer formation at the advancing front [13]. To modulate the average cell length, we added the antibiotic Cephalexin at low concentrations (40–80 µg/mL) to the agar medium. At these concentrations, Cephalexin inhibits cell division while permitting cell growth, thereby inducing cell elongation [33,34]. Agar plates were incubated at 30 °C under 90% relative humidity. After a lag phase of approximately 3 h, colonies expanded outward in a branching pattern with a front velocity of approximately 2.5 µm/s, about 10% of the mean swimming speed of isolated cells. Bacterial dynamics in the monolayer regions of varying area fractions were recorded within a 554×277 µm2 field of view using a 40× Nikon S Plan Fluor objective (Tokyo, Japan). The spatial calibration was performed using a Basler industrial camera (Ahrensburg, Germany) (4080×2048 pixels). Under a 40× objective, a calibrated micrometer scale was imaged, and the full field of view (4080×2048 pixels) corresponded to 554×277 µm2, yielding a conversion factor of approximately 7.4 pixels per micrometer. Videos were acquired at 90 fps for 24 s, during which the number of bacteria remained nearly constant, indicating a quasi–steady state.

2.2. Image Analysis and Cluster Identification

Custom MATLAB scripts were used for data analysis, with single-cell detection and tracking following established procedures [35]. Particle Image Velocimetry (PIV) was performed using PIVLab [36] with an interrogation window of 32×32 pixel2 (4.3×4.3 µm2) to extract velocity fields of dense populations.

Clusters were defined by spatial and orientational proximity: two neighboring cells were assigned to the same cluster if the distance between their centers of mass was within R and their orientation differed by less than θ, as described in ref. [35]. Empirical measurements showed that, across all densities, the average angular deviation between a cell and its neighbors within half a cell length was <15°. Accordingly, we set θ=15° and set R to be 80% times the mean cell length at each area fraction to identify clusters. Supplementary analyses of cluster size distributions and characteristic lengths confirmed that the qualitative results are robust with respect to small variations in R and θ. The average speed of isolated bacteria not engaged in clusters remained 20–30 µm/s, independent of both area fraction and aspect ratio.

2.3. Defect Detection and Analysis

To identify topological defects, we first constructed the bacterial orientation field using the Structure Tensor Algorithm [34], an edge-recognition method based on grayscale intensity gradients. As this algorithm relies on sharp object boundaries, the raw grayscale images were pre-processed with a Gaussian high-pass filter. The structure tensor of the enhanced images was then smoothed with a Gaussian kernel of width 16 pixels (2.2 µm), and the eigenvalues and eigenvectors were calculated at each pixel. The local orientation of bacterial bodies was defined as the direction of the eigenvector corresponding to the smaller eigenvalue. This procedure yielded a robust orientation field for each video frame.

Defect detection was performed by further smoothing the orientation field with a Gaussian filter of 32×32 pixel2. The resulting field was interpolated onto a computational grid of 512×256 grid points, with a spatial resolution of 1.1 µm. At each grid point, the net change of the director angle along a closed clockwise loop was evaluated. Locations with a net orientation change of π (or −π) were identified as the cores of +1/2 (or −1/2) defects.

To quantify the velocity field in the vicinity of topological defects, velocity vectors surrounding isolated defects at different times and spatial locations were counter-rotated to align with the defect orientation angle and subsequently mapped onto a common reference frame. The final velocity field was obtained by averaging over all defects. A similar approach was applied to analyze area fraction fluctuations: local fluctuation fields around individual defects were rotated to align with the defect orientation and subsequently ensemble-averaged to obtain the mean fluctuation field.

3. Results

Wild-type Serratia marcescens are rod-shaped, flagellated bacteria. At the tips of the branching S. marcescens colony grown on semi-solid agar surfaces, we observed an active swarming zone consisting of a monolayer of cells (Figure 1a,b). Within this zone, the bacterial density—defined as the area fraction ϕ—gradually increased from the colony edge toward its interior. As the concentration of Cephalexin increases, the aspect ratio distributions not only shift toward larger values but also broaden considerably, reflecting enhanced variability in cell morphology and the inclusion of highly elongated cells (see Figure 1c). Consistent with this trend, the mean aspect ratio of cell bodies generally increases from 6 to 10 as the concentration of cephalexin rises from 0 to 80 µg/mL. Therefore, in addition to the bacterial area fraction, ϕ, we consider the mean aspect ratio of cell bodies, α, as an independent and directly measurable parameter for characterizing system dynamics in the following analysis. In our experiments, α typically lies in the range 6⩽α⩽10, while ϕ varies between 0.2⩽ϕ⩽0.65, where ϕ≈0.2 corresponds to the population density near the leading edge of the colony. As the branches extended outward, the basal regions of neighboring branches eventually merged, and the densely packed monolayer (ϕ⩾0.65) progressively developed into a multilayer (Figure 1b).

It is observed that, when ϕ⩽0.5, irrespective of cell length, bacteria spontaneously assemble into dynamical clusters with local polar order and migrate collectively (Figure 2a). As the area fraction increases and the cell bodies elongate, larger clusters emerge due to the strengthening of alignment interactions, consistent with previous direct measurements of spatial correlation lengths in similar systems [37]. To facilitate a more quantitative investigation of the distribution of cluster size under varying cell lengths and bacterial densities, we introduce a definition for cluster size: the maximum distance between two bacteria belonging to the same cluster, L. The probability distributions of L corroborate the experimental observation that, overall, higher densities and longer cell bodies are prone to the formation of larger clusters (see Figure 2c,d).

Figure 2b shows L scales as a power law L=Cna where n is the total number of bacteria in the same cluster, yielding a=0.65±0.05, close to the scaling exponent 0.6 measured in clusters formed within B. subtilis colonies [35]. It is found that, the distributions of n can be well fitted by a power law with an exponential cutoff of larger clusters (insets in Figure 2c,d), which has been observed in the cluster-forming process of various bacteria [7,14,15] or active colloids systems [38,39]. From this, it is inferred that the distribution of L, P(L), shall be described by [35](1)P(L)=BL−be−(L/Lc)1/a, where B, b, and Lc are treated as fitting parameters, and a as a known parameter obtained by fitting the data of L vs. n with L=Cna as plotted in Figure 2b. As shown in Figure 2c,d and Figure S1, P(L) can be well fitted by Equation (1). The cut-off length Lc in Equation (1) represents the truncated size of the power-law decay for small clusters with an exponential suppression tail of larger clusters, akin to the characteristic size of the system.

To verify this, we introduce the correlation length within clusters as an additional directly measurable quantity to characterize cluster size. Specifically, we compute the two-dimensional pair correlation function g(x,y) for each cluster, and then average these intracluster correlations over the ensemble of all clusters under the same ϕ and α. The 2-d pair correlation function g(x,y) is defined as [7](2)g(x,y)=1ρ∑j≠iδxx^i+yy^i−(ri−rj)i, where δ is a Dirac function, ρ is the area number density of bacteria, 〈⋯〉i represents averaging over all reference bacteria i, and the position difference Δr=xx^i+yy^i is expressed in the ith local frame, which is centered at the center of mass ri of the ith bacterium in the laboratory coordinate system. The unit vectors x^i and y^i point along the transverse and longitudinal axes of the bacterial body, respectively. We identify the contour for g(x,y)=0.1 as the “statistical” edge of a cluster and define the longitudinal length λy, which is extracted by calculating the distance between (x=0,y1) and (x=0,y2) (g(x=0,y1>0)=0.1, g(x=0,y2<0)=0.1), as the average size of clusters (Figure 2e). The pair correlation function g(x,y) was calculated for different combinations of ϕ and α, and the corresponding longitudinal correlation length λy was extracted as defined above. We found a good quasilinear relation between Lc and λy, both of which increase with increasing ϕ and α, and can effectively characterize the statistical average size of the system (Figure 2f, Figures S2 and S3).

As ϕ reaches above 0.5, densely packed short cells (α⩽7.0) transition from forming local polar clusters to generating vortex-like dynamic structures reminiscent of classical turbulence, and the distribution of bacteria becomes approximately homogeneous. However, at similarly high densities, but with increase of cell length, crowded elongated bacteria (α>9.0 and ϕ>0.55) exhibit bipolar motion and an active nematic state characterized by the emergence of half-integer topological defects (Figure 3a). As expected [40], +1/2 defects display higher activity than −1/2 defects, as reflected in the bacterial velocity field around positive and negative defects. The +1/2 defect core gives rise to a forward-directed jet flow of approximately 6 μm/s (Figure 3b), while the −1/2 defect core is surrounded by 3 symmetric vortices and jets, leading to a more balanced, less motile configuration (Figure 3c).

Such observations are consistent with previous reports in wet, densely packed S. marcescens multilayer colonies [34]. However, a significant difference from the result calculated on multilayer colonies is that there exists a clear clockwise (CW) bias in the vortex near the defects. This bias originates from the single-cell CW-rotating chirality of bacteria near the solid surface [41]. This effect shows a larger influence on the monolayer colony due to the thin water film. In addition to this, unlike bacteria in the multilayered colony surrounded by an incompressible water film, the divergence of bacteria velocity is not necessarily zero (Figure 3b,c), reflecting the fact that the current bacterial system is compressible and associated with density segregation. Our further research shows that the local density fluctuation, defined as ΔΦ(x,y)=Φ(x,y)−ϕ, where Φ(x,y) and ϕ represent the local and global average area fractions, respectively, is correlated with defect location (Figure 3d,e). Local aggregation and dispersion of bacteria can be characterized by the divergence of their velocity field. A negative divergence at the head of the jet flow of the +1/2 defect is observed (Figure 3b), resulting in a net inward flux and the accumulation of cells in the same region (Figure 3d). In contrast, the −1/2 defect core corresponds to a positive divergence area (Figure 3c), and bacteria are escaping out of the negative defect core (Figure 3e). Bacterial aggregation at +1/2 defects suggests that this site may be associated with colony growth to the second layer [30,31].

4. Discussion and Conclusions

The anisotropic friction is considered to be the mechanism that triggers the instability of density and the accumulation of cells at +1/2 defects formed in active nematic materials [30,42]. Due to the anisotropy of cellular friction and the alignment of cells in front of the +1/2 defect core perpendicular to the flow, the friction experienced by the front cells is stronger than that experienced by the cells behind the +1/2 defect core. This anisotropic friction promotes a net inward flow, so that the cells are more prone to being trapped at +1/2 defects. A region of negative divergence of the cellular flow field and density aggregation is indeed observed at the head of the +1/2 defect core in our experiments (Figure 3c,e). Such a net inflow towards +1/2 defects may lead to the extrusion of cells into the third dimension and the formation of multilayered cells [30,31]. The effect of anisotropic friction becomes more pronounced as cell length increases. We did observe the process of elongated bacteria becoming clogged and squeezed into a second layer (see Figure S4). This phenomenon was not observed in colonies of highly concentrated, unelongated cells in the turbulent state of active polar fluids. We speculate that, in this system, the trapping and aggregation of cells induced by topological defects may contribute to the formation of multilayers of elongated bacteria, likely distinct from the layering process of unelongated short bacteria. The mechanism promoting the transitions from a single layer to a multi-layer biofilm may depend on the collective dynamics of bacterial swarming, which is a subject that requires further investigation.

To conclude, cellular morphology and density exert profound influences on the dynamics of bacterial swarming. At intermediate-to-low densities, bacteria exhibit a dynamic clustering state. We identify two independent, directly measurable parameters—the longitudinal correlation length within clusters, λy, and the cut-off parameter of the cluster size distribution function, Lc, both of which consistently capture the characteristic size of the system. By contrast, densely packed elongated bacteria align into an active nematic state, accompanied by the formation of half-integer topological defects. Notably, cell accumulation near +1/2 defects further suggests a possible connection to the initiation of mono- to multilayer transition during bacterial colony development.
