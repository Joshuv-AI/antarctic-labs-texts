Clinical, radiological, and CSF features distinguishing spinal dural arteriovenous fistula from idiopathic transverse myelitis and seropositive NMOSD-/MOGAD-associated myelopathy: a retrospective observational study
Authors: Furkan Sarıdaş, Rifat Özpar, Dursun Ceylan, Ali Özhan Sıvacı, Yasemin Dinç, Emel Oğuz Akarsu, Meral Seferoğlu, Emine Rabia Koç, Bahattin Hakyemez, Hamdi Necdet Karlı, Ömer Faruk Turan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Misdiagnosis is common in cases of spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (SDAVF), and immune-related myelopathies constitute one of the most frequent diagnostic pitfalls. This study aims to identify the clinical, radiological, and cerebrospinal fluid features that distinguish idiopathic transverse myelitis, seropositive neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), and myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody–associated disease (MOGAD)—the immune-mediated myelopathies most often confused with SDAVF. The study included 15 patients with SDAVF, 18 with idiopathic transverse myelitis, 12 seropositive NMOSD patients, and 4 patients with MOGAD, all with spinal cord involvement. Demographic, clinical, cerebrospinal fluid, and radiological imaging data were retrospectively reviewed from medical records. A secondary analysis was performed for patients whose initial clinical presentation was myelitis/myelopathy. In SDAVF, the male-to-female ratio was 3:1, and the mean age at first symptom onset was 56.8 ± 12.11 years. The initial presentation typically involved a progressive (40%) or fluctuating (40%) course, most commonly manifesting as paraparesis (73.3%). Compared with the other three myelopathies, SDAVF was characterized by a higher frequency of male sex (p = 0.032), a longer symptom-to-admission time (p < 0.001), a greater burden of comorbidities (p = 0.038), the presence of provocative factors (p < 0.001), fluctuating symptom onset (p < 0.001), more frequent urinary dysfunction (p < 0.001), absence of lower cervical–upper thoracic involvement (p = 0.012), and involvement of the lower thoracic spinal cord (p < 0.001). No distinguishing differences were observed in CSF characteristics or in other clinical or radiological features. The secondary analysis, limited to patients with the first clinical event being myelitis/myelopathy (excluding MOGAD), showed that these important differences were maintained. SDAVF is a rare but treatable cause of progressive myelopathy that can closely mimic immune-related myelitis/myelopathies. Fluctuating symptom onset, provoking factors, urinary dysfunction, and characteristic lower thoracic involvement may help distinguish SDAVF from seropositive NMOSD, MOGAD, and idiopathic transverse myelitis, supporting earlier diagnosis and improved clinical outcomes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-43104-8.

Introduction

Spinal cord vascular malformations are extremely rare and represent a very small proportion of all vascular neurological pathologies1. The most common type is spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (SDAVF). Fistulas can present as emergencies such as spontaneous hemorrhage or spinal cord infarction, but the typical clinical presentation is progressive myelopathy that usually presents as sphincter dysfunction with sensory or motor dysfunction in the lower limbs. Although it is more common in the early stages of the disease, the possibility of misdiagnosis is quite high2, ranging from 17.5 to 100.0%.Differential diagnosis most commonly includes many neurological conditions such as spondylosis/degenerative spinal disease, immune-related myelitis/myelopathy, and peripheral neuropathies2. The most confusing and misdiagnosed conditions, clinically and radiologically, are immune-related disorders such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease (MOGAD), or acute idiopathic transverse myelitis (ITM), which cause longitudinal transverse myelitis/myelopathy3–6. Inappropriate treatment approaches and diagnostic delays that can extend up to two years may adversely affect the prognosis of SDAVF, potentially resulting in irreversible permanent neurological sequelae7,8. Therefore, SDAVF, which is considered a treatable disease of the spinal cord, should be considered for differential diagnosis in the presence of some findings in the early period9.

Studies focusing on the differential diagnosis of SDAVF with immune-related myelitis are quite limited and are in the form of case reports/series2. This study aims to highlight the differences in the basic clinical, radiological, and cerebrospinal fluid characteristics of SDAVF by comparing it with ITM, Seropositive (AQP4 IgG +) NMOSD, and MOGAD, which are the most commonly misdiagnosed immune-related disorders. For this reason, we emphasize the diagnostic features that should be considered during the initial assessment of SDAVF using a holistic approach.

Material and methods

Patient selection and data assessment

Between 1 January 2012 and 1 October 2024, the medical records of patients diagnosed with SDAVF using magnetic resonance angiography and/or digital subtraction angiography at three different centres, and patients with a preliminary diagnosis of transverse myelitis, were retrospectively evaluated in the hospital information system using ICD diagnosis codes. Multiple sclerosis cases, which can be relatively more clearly distinguished from SDAVF based on radiological features (typically short-segment and focal involvement); Guillain–Barré syndrome, which can be relatively more readily differentiated from SDAVF through laboratory assessments such as cerebrospinal fluid analysis or electrodiagnostic studies; and lupus myelitis cases that did not meet the required sample size threshold (n = 2) were not included in the study. Subsequently, patients with insufficient medical records or incomplete radiological imaging were excluded. After these exclusions, patients with a definitive diagnosis of SDAVF, ITM, seropositive NMOSD, or MOGAD were included in the final study cohort. The demographic, clinical, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) test results of the patients included in the study were reviewed and recorded by five independent neurologists from three different centers, and radiological findings were reviewed and recorded by two independent radiologists experienced in neuroradiology. The data obtained were analysed both by comparing SDAVF with immun-related disorders defined in this study, which are most commonly confused with it diagnostically and by comparing all patient groups separately. This study was conducted with the approval of the Bursa Uludağ University Clinical Research Ethics Committee, and informed consent was waived. (Ethics Approval Date and Number: 04.12.2024, 2024-19/6). All methods were applied in accordance with the relevant guidelines and regulations.

Statistical analysis

The data were examined using the Shapiro–Wilk test to determine whether or not they present a normal distribution. The results were presented as mean ± standard deviation, median (minimum–maximum), or frequency and percentage values. Kruskal Wallis and Mann Whitney U tests were used for non-normally distributed data. The Bonferroni test was used as a multiple comparison test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson chi-square, Fisher’s exact, and Fisher-Freeman-Halton tests between groups. Statistically, the significance level was accepted as α = 0.05. Results

A total of 49 patients (15 with SDAVF, 12 with NMOSD, four with MOGAD, and 18 with ITM), 25 women and 24 men, were included in the study. The mean age of onset was 56.8 ± 12.11 years for SDAVF and 48.52 ± 17.55 years for the remaining patients. SDAVF was more common in males compared with ITM and NMOSD. There was no significant difference in the age at onset of the first symptom. The time from symptom onset to hospital admission was longest in patients with SDAVF (median 92 days). SDAVF and ITM patients presented with initial clinical events, whereas more than half of NMOSD patients and one MOGAD patient had experienced another neurological episode prior to myelitis/myelopathy in their medical history. Although this did not appear to reach statistical significance in the overall analyses for distinguishing SDAVF from the other groups, in the subgroup analyses a history of a prior neurological episode was significant for NMOSD (p = 0.001). Comorbid conditions were more prevalent in patients with SDAVF and NMOSD. Among the precipitating causes (within 3 months before symptom onset), there were patients with a history of infection, trauma, surgery, stress, vaccination, and drug abuse prior to symptoms. However, there were no differences observed between the groups. More than half of SDAVF cases were exacerbated by a provocative causes (nine patients had exacerbations with 14 provocative causes; seven from exercise, four from use of high-dose corticosteroids, two from plasma exchange, and one from the valsalva maneuver). In contrast, none of the other groups had such cases. A fluctuating course of symptoms was more common in the SDAVF group than in the other groups. In subgroup analyses, a stable course after an acute exacerbation was more specific to ITM (p < 0.001). Although paraparesis was the most common clinical presentation among patients with SDAVF, there was no significant difference between the groups. Discussion

Spinal cord vascular malformations, representing 1–2% of all vascular neurological pathologies, are extremely rare with an1,10 incidence of 0.5–1.0 per 100,000. The most common is SDAVF, characterized by an abnormal connection between the branches of the radiculomeningeal artery and the intramedullary venous system, which accounts for 60–80% of all spinal cord vascular malformations. The main mechanism underlying the etiopathogenesis of SDAVF is thought to involve venous obstruction11. With arterialization of the radicular vessels, hydrostatic pressure may increase locally in the venous plexus or cause cord/root compression by direct compression12. The result is chronic myelopathy due to venous hypertension, which, if left untreated, slowly leads to permanent paraplegia13. This insidious onset can often lead to diagnostic confusion and misdiagnosis. About four out of five patients were initially misdiagnosed with glioma, spinal ischemia, spinal stenosis, Guillain–Barre syndrome, multiple sclerosis, NMOSD, transverse myelitis or inflammatory myelopathy9,13–15. Clinically and radiologically, the most confusing and misdiagnosed conditions are immune-related disorders such as neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder causing longitudinal transverse myelitis/myelopathy, oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody-associated disease, or acute idiopathic transverse myelitis3–6. It is more common after the fifth decade in males (5:1) and in patients with a history of surgery or trauma9,16,17. In our present study, the age at onset was consistent with that reported in the literature, and the male sex was slightly less predominant than expected (3:1). A definitive diagnosis may take up to 2 years due to diagnostic difficulties7,18. In this study, the mean time from disease onset to final diagnosis was ~ 1 year. Although SDAVF is a rare condition, it should be considered during differential diagnosis and the findings should be carefully integrated, as a delay in diagnosis may result in permanent neurological sequelae9. In our study, none of the patients with ITM and SDAVF had a history of clinical events that may have indicated prior CNS involvement. In particular, for cases with a history of clinical events in the CNS including symptomatology from various topographic regions, the evaluation of SDAVF during differential diagnosis may not be immediately necessary. However, if long-segment transverse myelitis is present, especially if it is the first clinical episode, it should be prioritized.

The typical clinical presentation is progressive myelopathy, usually presenting as sphincter signs with lower-extremity sensory or motor dysfunction. Thoracic spinal cord involvement is most common, affecting approximately two-thirds of patients. Lumbar spinal cord involvement affects approximately one-fifth of patients1,19. Sacral and cervical involvement follow less frequently. The most commonly affected regions segmentally are the lower thoracic and upper lumbar segments, with T6 being the most frequently observed individual level19. It usually has a bilateral onset and, rarely, complaints may begin with unilateral symptoms20. The initial symptoms are often nonspecific and may mimic other forms of myelopathy, polyradiculopathy, peripheral neuropathy, or cauda equina syndrome, and may include motor, sensory, gait, and bowel/bladder dysfunction21. Periods of spontaneous recovery and acute exacerbation may occur during the course of chronic disease20,22. The pathogenesis and clinical course of sDAVFs vary due to differences in venous anatomy in the cervical region. Most asymptomatic sDAVFs are found in this region23. This situation has been linked to inadequate diagnosis and treatment. Consequently, over time, a chronic, oligo/asymptomatic course with a poor response to treatment may be observed19. As expected, non-specific paraparesis/paraplegia was the most common initial clinical presentation in our study. However, this finding was not useful for differentiating SDAVF. Other clinically significant features that helped differentiate SDAVF included the high prevalence of accompanying urinary dysfunction and the absence of a fluctuating course or pattern of acute deterioration. There may be a number of provocative factors that can have an effect on vascular pressure and venous return mechanisms, with the result that symptoms may worsen. These provocative factors include Valsalva maneuvers, exercise, corticosteroid therapy, and lumbar puncture. On the other hand, symptoms may improve with rest17,24. We found no significant differences among various precipitating causes for SDAVF. On the other hand, only among SDAVF cases did we identify provocative causes that worsened symptoms, and the most common of these was exercise followed by high-dose steroid therapy. Since a time delay is common for the diagnosis of SDAVF, corticosteroid treatment is likely to be administered before a definitive diagnosis can be achieved. This intervention is likely to further compromise venous return. Cases of exacerbation after corticosteroid therapy have been reported, but our knowledge on this subject is primarily based on case series and future large-scale studies are required25.

Studies reporting CSF findings are limited. There may be mild lymphocytic pleocytosis and mild protein elevation due to the transudative flow of protein and cells into the CSF with increasing intravenous pressure, which may be misinterpreted as an infiltrative process17,26. CSF glucose level is usually normal, although mild hypoglycorrhachia has been reported27. In our study, almost half of the patients had hypoglycorrhachia and mild protein elevation, and the cell count was primarily normal. However, these findings were not statistically significant for diagnostic differentiation. In conclusion, we did not find any CSF findings characteristic of SDAVF.

MRI is the first radiologic diagnostic modality to be used after clinical suspicion. Particularly in the lower thoracic and thoracolumbar regions, where both gray and white matter are involved, and multiple spinal segments may be affected, longitudinal wide T2 hyperintensity, T1 hypointensity, and cord swelling should be carefully evaluated for differential diagnosis, even in the absence of subarachnoid flow cavities typical of fistulas or dilated vessels on the cord surface (up to half of which may be seen)28–30. A differential diagnosis with inflammatory myelopathy becomes difficult in the presence of contrast enhancement, which is indicative of an impaired blood-spinal cord barrier due to venous congestion or ischemia as in inflammation. In this study, we did not identify any features that distinguish SDAVF from ITM, NMOSD, or MOGAD in terms of lesion characterization, such as T2 hyperintensity or contrast-enhancing patterns. However, lower thoracic involvement or the absence of mid/lower cervical-upper thoracic involvement was significant for SDAVF in terms of lesion localization.

Additionally, except for the presence of mid-cervical lesions, the other demographic, clinical, and radiological features that differentiated SDAVF from the immune-related myelopathy/myelitis entities, namely ITM, seropositive NMOSD and MOGAD, remained statistically significant even after excluding individuals with prior neurological episodes. This also demonstrates that the results are consistent in the evaluation of naïve cases.

Strengths and limitations

First, our study provides a systematic comparison between SDAVF and the immune-mediated myelopathies most commonly mistaken for it in clinical practice—idiopathic transverse myelitis, seropositive NMOSD, and MOGAD. Unlike many previous reports, which are typically limited to case series or single-cohort descriptions in these rare conditions, our study evaluates clinical, radiological, and CSF parameters across multiple etiologies within a unified analytic framework. Second, the additional secondary analysis restricted to patients whose initial clinical event was myelitis or myelopathy further strengthens the internal validity of the findings.

On the other hand, the main limitation of the study is the modest statistical power associated with group comparisons. As is common in small-cohort research on rare diseases, our analyses necessarily rely on exploratory statistical approaches. The post hoc power analysis conducted for comparing two independent proportions yielded a critical z value of − 1.96 for a two-tailed test at the α = 0.05 significance level. Based on the observed proportions (p₁ = 0.529 and p₂ = 0.200) and sample sizes (group 1: n = 15; group 2: n = 34), the statistical power of the test was calculated as 63.7%. This indicates that the study had a limited ability to detect the observed effect size with the available sample, and therefore the findings should be interpreted with caution.

Conclusion

SDAVF is a rare, treatable cause of slowly progressive spinal cord dysfunction that can mimic inflammatory myelopathies. There is a need for increased awareness among clinicians as diagnostic difficulties delay its identification and treatment. Fluctuating symptom onset, the presence of provoking factors, more frequent urinary dysfunction, absence of lower cervical–upper thoracic involvement, and preferential involvement of the lower thoracic segments may provide valuable clues in distinguishing SDAVF-related myelopathy from longitudinally extensive immune-related myelitis/myelopathy due to seropositive NMOSD, MOGAD, or idiopathic transverse myelitis. When recognized in a timely manner, these features may help reduce diagnostic delays and facilitate earlier treatment of SDAVF, ultimately improving neurological outcomes.
