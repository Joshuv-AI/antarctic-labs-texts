Long-term Outcome of Vital Pulp Therapy and Regenerative Endodontic Procedures in Immature Permanent Maxillary Incisors: A 7-year Follow-up Case Report
Authors: Mulham Almaliki, Motaz Almaghraby, Abdulaziz Bakhsh

ABSTRACT

Vital pulp therapy (VPT) and regenerative endodontic procedures (REP) are effective approaches for preserving tooth structure and function. Pulpotomy with calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) supports apexogenesis, whereas REP facilitates root maturation in necrotic immature teeth. Introduction

Preserving traumatized teeth is a cornerstone of modern dental practice, especially in young patients, where the implications of tooth loss affect oral health.1 Maintaining the natural permanent tooth promotes functional integrity, including chewing efficiency and speech clarity, while also supporting the esthetic appearance which benefits self-esteem and social interactions.1 Furthermore, natural tooth preservation reduces long-term complications associated with tooth loss, including bone resorption, migration of adjacent teeth, and the need for complex restorative procedures.1 Vital and functioning dental pulp is essential for initiating defense mechanisms that protect the tooth and surrounding tissues from bacterial invasion.2 Preservation of pulp vitality after exposure is considered superior to the root canal treatment, as maintaining the vitality of the tooth promotes natural healing and protective responses of the pulp-dentin complex.2 Therefore, vital pulp therapy (VPT), which is a treatment approach, aims to preserve and/or restore pulp function, which is critical for the tooth's long-term vitality and integrity, and protects the pulp from further injury.3 It is used when the pulp is exposed or affected by trauma or caries.3 Treatments under VPT include indirect pulp capping, direct pulp capping, partial pulpotomy, and full pulpotomy.3 Regenerative endodontic procedures (REP) constitutes advancements in retaining and restoring pulp health at the same time as prioritizing minimally invasive interventions.4 Pulpotomy, one of the most common forms of VPT, involves the removal of the infected coronal pulp while maintaining the healthful radicular pulp, followed by application of a medicament such as calcium hydroxide (Ca(OH)₂) to promote healing and induce root development.5 Ca(OH)₂ has been broadly used for its biocompatibility, capability to stimulate dentin formation, and antimicrobial properties.5 Pulpotomy is especially beneficial in immature permanent teeth, where continued root development and apexogenesis are crucial. It helps maintain pulp vitality while promoting the formation of a dentin bridge.6 Pulp revascularization using materials such as calcium silicate-based materials has emerged as a prospective management for necrotic immature teeth, promoting continued root development and apexogenesis through tissue regeneration.7,8 This technique depends on the recruitment of stem cells, scaffold materials, and bioactive molecules to restore a functional pulp-dentin complex.9 The European Society of Endodontology (ESE) has released guidelines designed to standardize the protocols for treatments such as VPT and REP. These guidelines emphasize VPT as a method for maintaining pulp vitality, while also focusing on REP techniques to facilitate tissue regeneration in necrotic immature teeth.10,11 Despite the growing body of evidence supporting these methods, long-term clinical outcomes remain underreported, particularly in cases requiring extended root maturation periods. Therefore, this case report aims to highlight two distinct endodontic interventions: pulpotomy with Ca(OH)₂ and pulp revascularization with mineral trioxide aggregate (MTA), documenting a remarkable 7-year follow-up period that demonstrates sustained pulp vitality and complete root development in both cases. Such long-term success underscores the potential of these techniques to provide durable and biologically favorable outcomes in challenging clinical scenarios.

Case Description

An 8-year-old medically fit female patient attended an endodontic consultation with her father with a chief complaint of trauma on anterior teeth by falling out of stairs at home 2 days prior to the appointment. The clinical examination revealed that teeth #11 and #21 had fractured incisal edges (Fig. 1A). Tooth #21 had undergone initial root canal treatment by the general dentist, while tooth #11 had a near pulp exposure but was not treated. Clinical examination revealed that both teeth were not tender to percussion or palpation tests. Sensibility testing using the cold test (Endo-Ice; Coltène, Germany) for tooth #11 produced a delayed response, while tooth #21 had no response of cold test. Therefore, based on the findings, tooth #11 was diagnosed as asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis with normal periapical tissues. Given its history of initiated root canal treatment, tooth #21 was diagnosed with a previously initiated therapy with normal periapical tissues. Periodontal examination revealed a normal range of pocket depth (2–3 mm) and normal physiological tooth mobility. A periapical radiograph was taken and revealed that tooth #11 had incomplete root formation, and the crown shows an irregular incisomesial outline due to the fracture, while tooth #21 had incomplete root formation, and the crown showed an irregular incisal outline due to the fracture (Fig. 2A). After discussing various treatment options with the patient's father, VPT in the form of pulpotomy was chosen for tooth #11, while pulp revascularization was selected for tooth #21. Procedural details, along with the benefits, risks, and potential complications, were discussed and consent was signed prior to the start of the treatment. All treatments were performed using a dental operating microscope over two visits.

First Visit

Teeth #11 and #21 were anesthetized using one cartridge (1.8 mL) of 2% lidocaine without epinephrine using buccal and palatal infiltration technique, followed by rubber dam isolation. After accessing tooth #11, signs of vital tissue were observed. The coronal pulpal tissue was removed, and the access cavity was irrigated using cotton pellets soaked in 1.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl; 5M; Biotischen, Saudi Arabia), followed by irrigation with normal saline (Becton, Dickinson and Company, United States) to remove debris. Cotton pellets soaked in 1.5% sodium hypochlorite were reapplied for 4 minutes to achieve hemostasis. Once bleeding was controlled, dried cotton pellets were applied, and Ca(OH)2 (Promedica Dental Material GmbH, Germany) was placed as an intracanal medication in the coronal part of the tooth. Finally, the pulp chamber was sealed with glass-ionomer cement (GIC) (Southern Dental Industries, Australia) as a liner, and a temporary restoration.

For tooth #21, no signs of vital tissue were observed. Minimal instrumentation was performed using a #10 K-file (Dentsply, Germany) 1 mm shorter than the working length. Irrigation was carried out using a side-vented needle attached to a 10-mL syringe, adjusted to be 2 mm shorter than the working length. The canals were irrigated with 20 mL of 1.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl; 5M; Biotischen, Saudi Arabia) using a back-and-forth motion, followed by irrigation with normal saline (Becton, Dickinson and Company, United States). The canals were then dried with paper points, and Ca(OH)2 was placed as intracanal medication. The pulp chamber was sealed with GIC as a temporary restoration.

Second Visit

After 4 weeks, periapical radiograph was taken and revealed that tooth #11 had continuous formation of root and absence of signs and symptoms. It was decided to leave the Ca(OH)2 and put the tooth on follow-up visits and replace part of the GIC restoration with a final composite restoration (Fig. 2B). The periapical radiograph on tooth #21 showed no improvement in the level of root formation and absence of signs and symptoms. It was decided to proceed with the treatment plan which was REP. The tooth was anesthetized with 1.8 mL of 2% lidocaine without epinephrine. A rubber dam was placed, and the temporary restoration was removed. The canal was then irrigated with 1.5% sodium hypochlorite (NaOCl; 5M; Biotischen, Saudi Arabia) and dried with sterile paper points. A number 20 sterile K-file (Dentsply, Germany) was introduced 2 mm past the apical foramen to have the canal filled with blood 3 mm from the cementoenamel junction. After blood clot formation, 3–4 mm of MTA (Dentsply Tulsa Dental, United States) was placed, followed by GIC base and a composite (Filtek Z-250; 3M ESPE, Germany) restoration (Fig. 2B).

Follow-up Visits

After 4 months, periapical radiograph was taken, revealing improvement in root formation for tooth #11 and no changes in tooth #21 (Fig. 2C). Clinically, both central incisors were within normal limits regarding responses to percussion, palpation, and pocket probing depths when compared with control teeth. Tooth #11 was responsive to stimulation with (Endo-Ice; Coltène, Germany). At the 14-month follow-up, radiographs showed improved root formation in tooth #11 and minimal change in tooth #21 (Fig. 2D). At the 28-month follow-up, radiographs showed pulp space calcification and complete root formation in tooth #11, with radiopaque deposits in the canal space (Fig. 2E). The tooth was responsive to stimulation with (Endo-Ice; Coltène, Germany) and was within normal limits when compared to control teeth. At the 44-month follow-up, radiographs revealed improvement and further calcification in tooth #21 (Fig. 2F). Clinically, the tooth was still responsive to stimulation with (Endo-Ice; Coltène, Germany). At the 64-month follow-up, radiographs revealed complete root formation in tooth #21 (Fig. 2G). At the final visit, 7 years and 5 months after treatment, radiographs showed complete root formation with obliterated canal space in tooth #11 and complete root formation in tooth #21 with no signs or symptoms in either tooth (Figs 1B and 2H).

Discussion

This case highlights the successful management of immature permanent maxillary central incisors with distinct pulpal statuses following trauma. Tooth #11 diagnosed with asymptomatic irreversible pulpitis, underwent pulpotomy, whilst tooth #21 with necrotic pulp was treated using REP. The medical decisions were guided by the need to preserve the vitality and promote root development in tooth #11 and to facilitate tissue regeneration in tooth #21. These outcomes spotlight the importance of planning treatment strategies for the precise clinical and pulpal conditions of each tooth.

Pulpotomy is a conservative pulp therapy which focuses on preserving healthy pulp tissue.5 In this case, Ca(OH)₂ was decided to be used as the pulp-capping agent because of its biocompatibility, antimicrobial properties, and capacity to stimulate reparative dentinogenesis.2,3 Numerous studies have revealed that Ca(OH)₂ promotes the release of bioactive molecules from dentin, such as transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β), which is essential in the differentiation of odontoblast-like cells and the formation of reparative dentin.2,3 The results of this case report aligns with prior studies advocating pulpotomy as an efficient therapy for immature permanent teeth with vital pulp tissue.12 To illustrate, a study by Mejàre and Cvek reported high success rates of pulpotomy in immature teeth, focusing on the promotion of continued root development when utilizing a biocompatible material like Ca(OH)₂.12 However, several studies have raised concerns about the long-term consequences of Ca(OH)₂, especially its tendency to induce dystrophic calcification in the pulp chamber.13,14 While Ca(OH)₂ is widely considered the ideal material for apexogenesis, recent literature suggests that bioactive materials such as MTA provide a superior effect, specifically in maintaining pulp vitality and minimizing pulp canal obliteration.15 For instance, a study by Silva et al. compared Ca(OH)₂ and MTA in pulpotomy procedures and found that MTA resulted in a more organized and predictable dentinal bridge formation with fewer signs of pulp chamber calcification. Nonetheless, despite these advances, Ca(OH)₂ remains a well-established and accessible option, particularly in cases where cost and availability are factors.15 The absence of periapical pathology and continued responsiveness to cold testing in tooth #11 over 7 years further emphasizes the long-term efficacy of pulpotomy. These outcomes align with reports by Bogen et al., demonstrating the reliability of Ca(OH)₂ in promoting apexogenesis and maintaining pulp vitality.16 The REP performed on tooth #21 utilized a minimally invasive approach aimed at regenerating a functional pulp-dentin complex. The use of Ca(OH)₂ paste for disinfection and stimulation of apical bleeding aids the induction of stem cells from the apical papilla16 MTA served as a coronal seal, creating an environment favorable for tissue regeneration.16 These standards align with the clinical suggestions outlined by the American Association of Endodontists for REP, which emphasize the significance of tissue regeneration over the conventional apexification approach.17 Despite the slower progression of root maturation compared to tooth #11, tooth #21 demonstrated gradual radiographic evidence of canal obliteration and complete root formation over the 7-year follow-up. These findings are consistent with prospective study,17 which report successful outcomes of REP in necrotic immature teeth, albeit with variability in the timeline for root maturation. This case emphasizes the importance of early intervention and appropriate case selection in managing immature teeth with trauma. While both treatments were successful, the slow progression of root development in tooth #21 raises questions about the variability of outcomes in REP. Factors such as the extent of apical bleeding, scaffold integrity, and patient adherence can significantly impact the success of REP. Moreover, long-term follow-up is essential for assessing the consistency of clinical and radiographic outcomes. This study is limited by its single case, which restricted the reproducibility of its findings. While long-term follow-up was performed, the findings may differ among different patients and clinical situations. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to validate the stability and broader clinical applicability of VPT and REP.

Conclusion

This case report demonstrates the successful management of two immature permanent maxillary incisors using VPT and REP. The 7-year follow-up highlights the long-term viability of Ca(OH)₂ in apexogenesis for vital teeth and the potential of REP with MTA to regenerate functional pulp-dentin complexes in necrotic teeth. The outcomes highlighted the importance of individualized treatment planning based on clinical and pulpal status, as well as the role of extended follow-up in evaluating treatment success. While both interventions achieved favorable results, the variability in root development timelines emphasizes the need for further research and standardization in regenerative endodontics to optimize patient outcomes.
