Update: Is exercise-induced oxidative stress a friend or foe?
Authors: Scott K. Powers, Mari Carmen Gomez-Cabrera
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Free radicals (radicals) are highly reactive atoms or molecules that contain one or more unpaired electrons in their outermost shell. The first evidence that muscular exercise increases radical production and promotes oxidative damage to tissues was reported almost five decades ago. Following this milestone discovery, many studies have corroborated the finding that exercise increases the production of radicals and other reactive oxygen species (ROS) resulting in oxidative damage to macromolecules in muscles and other tissues. Although exercise-induced ROS production is associated with oxidative damage in many tissues, growing evidence reveals that ROS produced in contracting muscles act as signaling molecules to promote healthy exercise-induced adaptations in skeletal muscles and other tissues. These adaptive responses include increased mitochondrial volume, improved antioxidant capacity, and expression of cytoprotective proteins. Therefore, a key question emerges: “Is exercise-induced ROS production a friend or foe?” This review provides a state-of-the-art discussion of both the positive and negative effects of exercise-induced ROS production by examining the consequences of both oxidative damage to cellular macromolecules and the redox signaling-induced adaptations that occur in muscle fibers. To address the question of whether exercise-induced ROS production is a friend or foe we conclude with a risk/benefit analysis of the biological effects of exercise-induced production of ROS.

Introduction

Reactive oxygen species (ROS) refers to several reactive byproducts of molecular oxygen that promote oxidation to macromolecules. It is well-known that muscular exercise increases the production of ROS in contracting skeletal muscles resulting in oxidative damage to cellular macromolecules.1, 2, 3 The term “oxidative stress” was coined by Sies in 1985 and has been commonly used for > 40 years to describe an increase in oxidation of cellular components resulting from an imbalance between the production of ROS and cellular antioxidants.4 This definition has been subsequently redefined as “an imbalance between oxidants and antioxidants in favor of the oxidants, leading to a disruption of redox signaling and control and/or molecular damage.”5

Following the discovery that prolonged exercise results in oxidative stress,2,6,7 many studies have demonstrated that exercise promotes increased ROS production in the contracting muscles; this increase in radical production promotes oxidation of both proteins and lipids within muscle fibers and muscle fatigue (reviewed in references8, 9, 10). Further, abundant evidence shows that independent of physical exercise, chronic oxidative stress is associated with numerous pathologies including heart disease, cancer, and diabetes.11, 12, 13 Thus, the fact that muscular exercise promotes oxidative stress in skeletal muscles and other organs is enigmatic because regular exercise is reported to provide numerous health benefits in humans including a reduced risk of heart disease, cancer, and type 2 diabetes.14, 15, 16, 17, 18 We addressed this fascinating exercise and oxidative stress paradox in a previous review that discussed the cellular consequences of exercise-induced ROS production.8 In the years following this earlier report, recent studies using emerging technologies have provided new and important information on this topic. Indeed, these innovative studies permit an in-depth analysis of exercise and oxidant production to better understand why exercise-induced increases in ROS production provides cellular protection against a variety of chronic diseases. Thus, given the rapid increase in new information on this topic, it is appropriate to provide an up-to-date discussion of the pros and cons of exercise-induced ROS production in skeletal muscles. Therefore, this review will provide a state-of-the-art analysis of the risks and benefits associated with exercise-induced ROS production in contracting skeletal muscles. To provide context for this discussion, we begin with a historical overview of the discovery that exercise is associated with oxidative stress and highlight our growing knowledge about the cellular sites responsible for ROS production during exercise. Further, before discussing the pros and cons of exercise-induced production of ROS, we also provide a brief overview of how the levels of ROS are regulated in cells for readers unfamiliar with this area of redox biology.

Historical overview of exercise-induced oxidative stress

The discovery that mammalian cells produce free radicals occurred over 70 years ago.19 Two decades after this seminal finding, it was revealed that human endurance exercise is associated with oxidative stress as evidenced by increases in a biomarker of lipid peroxidation.7 The major source(s) of radical production during exercise remained unknown until 1982 when Davies et al. reported that exhaustive exercise results in a 2-3 fold increase in radical production in both contracting skeletal muscles and the liver.2 Notably, this exercise-induced increase in radical production was also associated with oxidative damage to macromolecules in both muscle and liver tissue.2 Following this important finding, numerous studies confirmed that exercise increases radical production in contracting muscles.1,3,20, 21, 22, 23, 24

As discussed previously, the concept of oxidative stress was first defined over 40 years ago.4 Although the definition of oxidative stress has been widely accepted for decades, to better define differing levels of oxidation in cells, the terms “oxidative eustress” and “oxidative distress” have been proposed to label physiological levels of ROS and supraphysiological levels of ROS, respectively. Specifically, the term “oxidative eustress” refers to a physiological steady-state production of ROS in cells. Oxidative eustress results in a targeted and reversible level of protein oxidation that is responsible for activation of redox signaling pathways that orchestrate numerous normal cellular processes.25 In contrast, supraphysiological levels of cellular ROS production promote widespread oxidation and damage to biomolecules leading to cellular dysfunction and in some cases, cell death.25 This high level of ROS-mediated cellular damage has been defined as “oxidative distress”.25

Because of the published reports that exercise is associated with oxidative stress, ROS were viewed as damaging molecules throughout the 1980's and 1990's. The concept that radicals are harmful entities within skeletal muscle fibers gained further support when it was discovered that radicals contribute to the muscle fatigue that occurs during prolonged exercise.26,27 Three years following the discovery that radicals contribute to muscle fatigue, Reid et al. demonstrated that the redox status of muscle fibers play an important role in determining muscle force production in unfatigued muscle.28 This concept originated from experiments demonstrating that skeletal muscles exhibit maximal force production at an optimal redox state and movement away from this optimal state results in a lower force production. These studies led to the now well-known “U” shaped curve describing the relationship between fiber redox status and muscle force production (Fig. 1).28 Briefly, Fig. 1 illustrates that an optimal cellular redox state exists for muscle force production and deviation from the optimal redox state results in a reduction in muscle force production. Again, during the 1980's-1990's, exercise-induced production of radicals in skeletal muscle was considered a damaging phenomenon. However, studies during 2000-2009 resulted in a paradigm shift away from the concept that ROS production in contracting muscles is purely a damaging process without physiological benefit.29 Indeed, it is now recognized that ROS are key signaling molecules that promote numerous exercise-induced adaptations in skeletal muscles that provide health benefits.30 In the next two sections we discuss the sources of ROS production in contracting muscles and how ROS levels are regulated in muscle fibers.

Sources of ROS in production in contracting muscles

Although a variety of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species are found within skeletal muscle fibers, only superoxide radicals and nitric oxide are directly produced within the fiber. To refine the scope of our discussion, we will focus on ROS within this report. As introduced earlier, ROS is an umbrella term that refers to both radical and non-radical byproducts of molecular oxygen that promote oxidation to macromolecules. It follows that the name ROS does not refer to a specific molecule. Nonetheless, because of the methodological difficulties in the detection of specific ROS in cells, it remains common in the redox literature to use the term ROS to refer to all ROS; this label includes both radicals and non-radicals 30, 31, 32, 33. The parent molecule of all ROS is the superoxide radical (O2.-) and while several by-products of O2.- exist, the non-radical ROS, hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) is recognized as the major ROS signaling by-product of O2.-.25,34 One of the ongoing challenges in studying the impact of radicals in biology is the difficulty of detecting and quantifying the abundance of radicals due to their short half-life and high reactivity.

Knowledge about the sources of ROS production in contracting muscles continues to evolve. It is now clear that ROS are produced at multiple locations within resting and contracting skeletal muscle fibers. Indeed, at least 41 different cellular enzymes can produce either O2.- or H2O2.35 Of the numerous ROS producing enzymes, the enzyme nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase (NOX) is the only family of enzymes known to produce ROS as its primary function.36,37 Interestingly, in addition to the production of O2.- and H2O2 inside the muscle fiber, both O2.- and H2O2 can be generated from NOX family members on the extracellular side of the sarcolemma and released into the interstitial space.36,38

The primary sites of contraction-induced ROS production in skeletal muscle fibers have been debated for over 40 years. Although methodological advances have improved our ability to detect ROS in cells, the fact that ROS are rapidly eliminated following formation continues to pose challenges in the study of the subcellular sites of ROS production. Nonetheless, recent technological advances have significantly improved our understanding of the locations of ROS production in contracting muscles. Because several detailed reviews exist on this topic,30,34,39 only a summary of the sites of ROS production in contracting muscles will be provided here.

During muscular contractions, ROS levels increase in both the cytosol and within the muscle interstitial space.34 Mitochondria were first proposed as a major source of ROS production in contracting skeletal muscles over 40 years ago.2,23 This postulate emerged from a report suggesting that ∼2% of all oxygen consumed by mitochondria is converted to O2.- anions during state 4 respiration.40 However, more recent evidence reveals that only 0.1%-0.15% of total oxygen consumption is converted to ROS within the mitochondria.41,42 Moreover, abundant evidence now shows that mitochondria produce more ROS in basal, state 4 respiration compared to maximal ADP stimulated respiration (state 3).43, 44, 45 Indeed, using a redox-sensitive mitochondrial-targeted probe, Henriquez-Olguin et al. demonstrated that mitochondrial ROS production in skeletal muscle is decreased during exercise.46 Hence, these findings confirm that mitochondria are not the primary source of ROS in contracting skeletal muscles.

Currently, it is generally agreed that NOX enzymes play a primary role in exercise-induced ROS production in skeletal muscles.34,39 The NOX family of enzymes contains seven homologs (NOX1-5, DUOX1, and DUOX2) which are differentially expressed across various tissues in the body.37 Again, the sole purpose of all members of the NOX family is ROS production. As stated earlier, accumulating evidence indicates NOX enzymes play a dominant role in exercise-induced ROS production in skeletal muscle fibers. For example, two independent studies investigating contraction-induced ROS production in isolated single muscle fibers conclude that NOX enzymes play a major role in exercise-induced production of ROS in the cytosol.47,48 Moreover, technological advances in recent years have refined the process of identifying the cellular location of ROS production and therefore, the sites of ROS production. Specifically, the application of a redox-sensitive probe reveals that the exercise-induced accumulation of ROS in the cytosol is primarily due to activation of NOX2.46 Interestingly, the NOX2 production of O2.- results in the release of O2.- into the extracellular space (Fig. 2).37 However, the O2.- released to the extracellular space is rapidly converted to H2O2 which enters the cytosol of muscle fibers through aquaporin channels (AQP) (Fig. 2).34 These AQPs are a family of integral membrane proteins that are often described as “facilitators of diffusion” to enable the movement of H2O2 into the cytosol of muscle fibers.49

To summarize, muscle contractions increase ROS accumulation in the active muscle fibers. Current evidence indicates that this exercise-mediated ROS production is dominated by the NOX2 production of O2.- that is released into interstitial space. Following release of O2.- into the extracellular space, O2.- is rapidly converted to H2O2 which moves into the muscle cytosol via AQP channels. An overview of how muscle fibers cope with this increase in cytosolic ROS follows.

Regulation of redox homeostasis in skeletal muscle

As discussed, ROS are produced in both resting and contracting muscle fibers with higher levels of ROS being produced within the active muscle during exercise. To maintain ROS at physiological and non-damaging levels (i.e., eustress), muscle fibers and other cells are equipped with numerous molecules that act as antioxidants. By definition, antioxidants are molecules that prevent or lower the rate of oxidation of a substrate.9 In this regard, cells are equipped with two layers of antioxidants to defend against oxidation; these include both small non-enzymatic molecules and antioxidant enzymes.50 The first layer of protection against cellular oxidation consists of small molecular weight, non-enzymatic antioxidants; common non-enzymatic antioxidants include uric acid, glutathione, and dietary compounds such as vitamins C and E.50 While many of these small molecular weight non-enzymatic antioxidants can eliminate ROS in cells via one-electron reactions, it is unlikely that ROS can be effectively scavenged via non-enzymatic antioxidants alone.51 Therefore, a second line of defense against oxidants is required. This second layer of protection against oxidants is a collection of antioxidant enzymes that are strategically located within various cellular compartments to regulate ROS at steady-state eustress levels to avoid excessive oxidation. A brief overview of key cellular antioxidant enzymes and their activities in different skeletal fiber types follows. For a more detailed discussion of cellular antioxidant systems, the reader is referred to reviews on this subject.9,50,52

Because of the wide range of physical activities performed by humans and other mammals, the contractile and metabolic properties of mammalian skeletal muscle fibers are heterogenous. In humans, two major muscle fiber types exist: 1) type I, slow muscle fibers; and 2) type II, fast muscle fibers. Importantly, type II fibers exist in two subtypes labeled as type IIa and type IIx.53 Type I (slow) muscle fibers are characterized by a slow myosin heavy chain isoform, large mitochondrial volumes, high capillary densities, and high resistance to fatigue. In contrast, type II muscle fibers contain fast myosin heavy chain isoforms, low mitochondrial/capillary densities, and limited resistance to fatigue.

As mentioned earlier, O2.- is the parent molecule of all ROS. Although O2.− radicals are not highly reactive, these radicals can lead to the production of highly reactive radicals (e.g., hydroxyl radicals and/or peroxynitrate) as well as the production of the oxidant, H2O2. Therefore, elimination of O2.- anions is important to prevent cellular distress. The antioxidant enzyme superoxide dismutase (SOD) is responsible for the dismutation of O2.- into H2O2. To eliminate superoxide production across body compartments, SOD exists in three isoforms located in the cytosol (SOD1), the mitochondrion (SOD2), and the extracellular space (SOD3).9 This compartmentalization of SOD is essential to ensure that O2.- radicals are removed within the various cellular locations where they are produced.

It is established that significant differences exist in the activities of both SOD1 and SOD2 between slow and fast fiber types (reviewed in reference54). For example, in rat limb muscle, SOD1 activity is approximately 235% higher in the soleus muscle (∼80% slow, type 1 fibers) compared to the gastrocnemius (predominantly fast, type II fiber muscles).55 Similarly, SOD1 mRNA levels are ∼130% higher in the soleus muscle compared to the gastrocnemius.56 Not surprisingly, given the greater mitochondrial volume in slow muscle fibers compared to fast fibers, SOD2 activities are ∼150% higher in the soleus muscle compared to the fast gastrocnemius muscle in rats.55

Again, SOD dismutates O2.- into H2O2 and although H2O2 is not a radical, H2O2 is an oxidant that can diffuse long distances to promote oxidation. It follows that removal of both the O2.− radical and H2O2 are important to protect cellular macromolecules against excessive oxidation. Given the significance of regulating the H2O2 levels in cells, several antioxidant enzymes exist across cellular compartments to achieve this objective. An overview of these antioxidant enzymes follows.

Glutathione peroxidase (GPX) exists in eight isoforms located across many cellular compartments; each isoform uses reducing equivalents from glutathione to reduce both H2O2 and organic hydroperoxides to form water or alcohol.57 This compartmentalization of the GPX family of enzymes is strategic to reduce peroxide levels in all compartments of the cell. Similar to SOD, GPX activity can be > 100% higher in slow, type I muscle fibers compared to fast, type II muscle fibers.54

Catalase (CAT) is an additional antioxidant enzyme that degrades H2O2 into water and oxygen.58 In contrast to GPX, CAT does not require a reducing equivalent to remove H2O2.9 Therefore, the depletion of reduced glutathione in the cell is not a hindrance to CAT's function as an antioxidant enzyme. Identical to both SOD and GPX, CAT activity is significantly greater (+300%-745%) in muscles dominated by slow, type I fibers compared to muscles containing predominantly fast, type II muscle fibers (reviewed in reference54).

Until the mid-1990's, CAT and GPXs were considered the major peroxide reducing enzymes in cells.59 This concept changed after another group of peroxidases were discovered in 1994.60 The breakthrough discovery of a family of enzymes known peroxiredoxins (PRDXs) generated a paradigm shift leading to the consensus that PRDXs are the most prominent antioxidant enzymes in the removal of peroxides in cells.59,61 Peroxiredoxins are enzymes that catalyze the reduction of H2O2 using thioredoxin as the electron donor.61 Six isoforms of PRDXs exist in cells at various locations including the cytosol, mitochondrion, and nucleus.61 Currently, limited information exists about whether PRDX activity differs markedly across muscle fiber types. The belief that PRDXs play a central role in eliminating peroxide emerged following the observation that these enzymes are efficient catalysts (e.g., similar rate constants to GPX and CAT) but are expressed in cells at significantly higher levels than CAT or GPX. Indeed, research reveals that > 99% of peroxide in the cytosol and > 90% of H2O2 in the mitochondria are eliminated by PRDX enzymes.61 Therefore, H2O2 elimination from cells is the result of three enzymes (i.e., CAT, GPX, PRDXs) with the PRDX family of enzymes playing the central role. Thus, together, these antioxidant enzymes serve as sinks for H2O2.

In addition to controlling H2O2 levels by removal of H2O2, peroxiredoxins also regulate intracellular redox balance by acting as redox relays.25,34 Specifically, peroxiredoxins can transmit the oxidizing equivalents from H2O2 to other target proteins.34 This transfer of oxidizing equivalents acts as a redox relay to promote cell signaling. Indeed, it is now clear that a major role for contraction-induced production of H2O2 is to provide signals for muscle adaptation in response to the contractile activity.34 The major ROS responsible for this cell signaling process is H2O2 and this signaling process is known as redox signaling.34 The next segment discusses redox signaling and highlights some key cellular targets of redox signaling.

Targets of redox signaling

The exercise-induced redox signaling process appears to be mediated by oxidation of specific thiol groups on target proteins that facilitate the exercise-induced adaptations to skeletal muscle. The number of target proteins in cells is estimated to be 10%-20% of the 214 000 easily oxidized thiols within the cellular cysteine proteome.62 This large number of potential target proteins highlights the pleiotrophy of ROS signaling in biology. These oxidizable proteins include enzymes, receptors, and transcriptional activators that lead to increased gene transcription.25 A detailed discussion of the targets of redox-signaling exceeds the scope of this review and therefore, only a few prototypical examples of redox signaling targets will be provided to illustrate important exercise-induced redox-signaling events in skeletal muscle.

A hallmark of endurance exercise training is the increased abundance of numerous antioxidant enzymes in the trained skeletal muscles.50 This exercise-induced increase in the expression of antioxidant enzymes occurs, in part, due to the activation of nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2). Nrf2 is a transcriptional activating factor responsible for the expression of > 250 genes including key stress proteins and antioxidants providing protection against oxidants and other stressors.63, 64, 65 In fact, Nrf2 is often labeled as the master regulator of antioxidant defenses in cells.66 See Powers et al. for a detailed discussion of exercise-induced activation of Nrf2 in muscle fibers.67

Another hallmark of endurance exercise training is the increase in mitochondrial volume within the trained muscles.68,69 Mitochondrial biogenesis is regulated by a complex series of signaling pathways leading to the activation of the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis, peroxisome proliferator-activated gamma coactivator 1 alpha (PGC-1α).68,70 While numerous exercise-generated signals work cooperatively to activate PGC-1α and promote mitochondrial biogenesis, the production of ROS in contracting muscle contributes to PGC-1α activation, in part, via activation of p38 MAP kinase.68, 69, 70, 71 Moreover, ROS are important signals to trigger mitophagy resulting in the selective removal of damaged mitochondria.68 Thus, mitochondrial biogenesis and mitophagy work cooperatively to maintain healthy and functional mitochondria in cells.

Exercise-induced production of ROS also contributes to the signaling cascade required to express numerous stress proteins including heat shock protein 72 (HSP72).30 In this regard, endurance exercise training increases the abundance of several stress proteins in both heart and skeletal muscles. Importantly, an increased abundance of HSP72 in skeletal muscle fibers can slow the progression of muscular dystrophy, increase insulin sensitivity, and protect against inactivity-induced muscle atrophy.72, 73, 74, 75, 76 The primary transcription factor for the expression of HSP72 in all mammals is heat shock factor 1 (HSF1).77,78 Although several factors can contribute to the activation of HSF1, redox signaling plays a role in the expression of HSP72 in response to exercise and other stressors.79, 80, 81, 82 For more details about exercise-induced redox signaling the reader is referred to several comprehensive reviews on this topic.25,30,34,67,83

Evidence that exercise-induced ROS production is a foe

As discussed earlier, the first evidence that exercise promotes oxidative stress in humans was reported almost five decades ago.7 Since this original observation, hundreds of studies have confirmed that bouts of resistance or endurance exercise results in increases in both lipid and protein oxidation in a variety of tissues (reviewed in references8,9,29). Let's discuss the potential negative impact that this exercise-induced oxidative stress has on cellular health and muscle fatigue.

Exercise-induced oxidation of cellular lipids and proteins

It is established that oxidation of important macromolecules (i.e., oxidative distress) can impair cellular function. For example, oxidation of lipids (i.e., lipid peroxidation) occurs when oxidants interact with polyunsaturated fatty acids in the cell membrane.84 Oxidative damage to lipids within cell membranes can increase membrane permeability and disrupt ion gradients across the membrane resulting in a disruption of normal cellular processes.85 In fact, in extreme cases, significant damage to membrane structure can result in cell death.86

Similarly, oxidation of cellular proteins can impair protein function within the muscle fiber. To illustrate, oxidation of amino acids associated with the active site of enzymes can reduce enzyme activity and increase susceptibility of this oxidized protein to degradation by proteases.87,88 Moreover, oxidation of muscle proteins involved in excitation-contraction coupling can impact muscle force production; this topic will be discussed in detail in the next section.89

Given that high levels of ROS can damage both membranes and numerous proteins in the cell, what effect does exposure to ROS have on cellular health? To address this question, numerous in vitro studies have investigated the impact that exposure to high levels of ROS has on myotubes. In general, these studies reveal that prolonged exposure of myotubes to oxidants results in myotube atrophy and in extreme cases, myotube death.90, 91, 92, 93, 94 Note, however, that many of these studies have exposed cells to high levels of oxidants in the culture media and have also treated cells with toxins to stimulate oxidant generation. To further complicate the interpretation of these experiments, the high partial pressures of oxygen within the culture media markedly exceeds the normal partial pressures of oxygen that cells are exposed to in vivo. This is significant because hyperoxic environments promote the rapid formation of radicals in cells.95, 96, 97, 98 Therefore, it remains unclear if the experimental conditions used in these in vitro cell culture experiments mimic the levels of ROS-mediated cell damage that occurs in muscle fibers in vivo during muscular exercise.

Exercise-induced ROS production contributes to performance fatigability

Historically, muscle fatigue has been defined as a reduced ability of the muscle to generate maximal force.99 However, it has been suggested that the term “muscle fatigue” should be limited to in vitro studies of muscle function and not exercise performance involving whole body exercise. Specifically, it has been proposed that a more appropriate term to describe exercise-induced fatigue during whole body exercise experiments is “performance fatiguability”-defined as the decline in an objective measure of performance (e.g., power output) over time.100 The first evidence that radical production contributes to endurance exercise-induced performance fatiguability appeared in 1990.26,27 The observation that radicals contribute to performance fatiguability during prolonged endurance exercise has been supported by numerous studies during the past 30 years.101, 102, 103, 104, 105 At present, it is unclear if exercise-induced ROS production contributes to performance fatiguability during all intensities of exercise, but abundant evidence exists that ROS production contributes to fatigue development in heavy and very heavy exercise (i.e., 70%-90% V.O2 max). For reviews on the impact of oxidants on performance fatiguability and in vitro muscle fatigue the reader is referred to reviews on this topic.9,10,89,99,106, 107, 108, 109

In theory, high intramuscular levels of ROS can negatively impact muscle force production in numerous ways (reviewed in references107,108,110). Although numerous studies have explored the molecular mechanisms responsible for ROS-mediated performance fatiguability, many questions remain. Nonetheless, current evidence suggests that ROS mediated fatigue is not due to a loss of membrane excitability or decreased calcium release from the sarcoplasmic reticulum.104,109 Current evidence suggests that ROS-mediated performance fatiguability is likely due to both a loss of calcium sensitivity in the muscle and altered myosin function during muscle contraction. In regard to ROS and calcium sensitivity, studies in intact muscle fibers indicate that ROS-mediated oxidation of a cysteine residue on troponin C may contribute to the commonly observed reduction in calcium sensitivity in muscles exposed to ROS.104,105,109,111 Oxidation of a cysteine (98) on troponin C results in a decreased affinity for calcium which results in a reduced binding of myosin cross bridges to actin.111 Similarly, evidence exists that oxidation of cysteine residues on the S1 portion of the myosin crossbridge (i.e., myosin head) is associated with a reduction in force generation presumably due to a reduced number of cross bridge formation and/or reduced myosin ATPase activity.102

Evidence that exercise-induced ROS production can be a friend

Although exercise-induced ROS production in skeletal muscle was widely viewed as a detrimental effect of prolonged exercise in the 1980-90's, mounting evidence during the past several decades suggests that the production of ROS in contracting muscles acts as a pleiotrophic signaling agent to promote numerous healthy exercise-induced adaptations to muscles and perhaps other organs.25,30,34 The next segments illustrate several potential health and performance benefits associated with contraction-induced ROS production in skeletal muscles.

Exercise-induced redox signaling and mitochondrial biogenesis

An increase in mitochondrial volume in skeletal muscles is a hallmark of endurance training adaptation.30 Further, regular exercise also promotes an increase in mitophagy which eliminates damaged and fragmented mitochondria that produce a disproportionate level of ROS.68 Therefore, exercise is an important way of boosting the quality of the mitochondrial pool in skeletal muscle to sustain muscle health.68

The exercise-induced increase in mitochondrial biogenesis is regulated by transcriptional activators that promote gene expression of both mitochondrial and nuclear encoded genes.112 These genes express over 1 200 gene products that comprise the mitochondrial proteome.112 As mentioned previously, PGC-1α is regarded to be the master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis.113 Specifically, PGC-1α propels the expression of many mitochondrial proteins including mitochondrial import machinery and respiratory complexes by interacting with several transcription factors including nuclear respiratory factors 1 and 2.112

Exercise-induced activation of PGC-1α in skeletal muscle requires the cooperative work of several kinases; these calcium/calmodulin-dependent protein kinases (e.g., CaMKII), adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) and p38 kinase.112,114 Importantly, contraction-induced production of ROS in skeletal muscle fibers can activate all these kinases.115,116 In this regard, research reveals that pharmacological blockade of ROS production in contracting muscles or blunting the oxidizing effects of ROS by antioxidant supplementation can prevent the activation of both AMPK and PGC-1α in the working muscles.117, 118, 119 Hence, this observation supports the concept that exercise-induced ROS production plays a key role in exercise-induced mitochondrial biogenesis.

ROS signaling is essential for exercise-induced increases in antioxidant defenses in skeletal muscle

Another hallmark of endurance exercise training is an increase in the abundance of antioxidant enzymes in the trained muscles. Specifically, regular exercise training increases muscle levels of SOD, CAT, GPX, and PRDX (reviewed in reference50). In fact, because exercise training upregulates antioxidant genes, moderate exercise has been termed an antioxidant by some investigators (reviewed in reference118).

As discussed earlier, Nrf2 is a transcriptional activating factor responsible for the control of over 250 genes that protect against a variety of stressors including oxidative stress.63, 64, 65 In this regard, research demonstrates that the exercise-induced activation of Nrf2 results in an increased expression of key antioxidant enzymes including SOD1, GPX, CAT, and PRDX1 in skeletal muscles and other tissues.50,120

Although the regulation of Nrf2 is complex and involves more than 45 control points, it is clear that both ROS and electrophiles contribute to the activation of Nrf2.121 In particular, both clinical and preclinical studies confirm that oral administration of antioxidants (e.g., vitamin E and C) prevent exercise training-induced increases in Nrf2 linked antioxidant enzymes.117,119 This finding is consistent with the concept that ROS-mediated activation of Nrf2 is important for exercise-induced increases in the expression of crucial antioxidant enzymes in skeletal muscles.119

ROS signaling promotes exercise-induced increases in heat shock protein 72

Exercise training increases the abundance of several stress proteins in skeletal muscle including HSP72.79,122, 123, 124 This is significant because an increased abundance of HSP72 in skeletal muscles can contribute to increased insulin sensitivity and protect muscle fibers against specific stresses including muscle atrophy due to prolonged disuse.73,74

Again, HSF1 is the primary transcription factor driving the expression of HSP72 in mammals.77 Activation of HSF1 by exercise is a multistep process that requires redox signaling.77,81,82 Indeed, dietary supplementation with a diet high in antioxidants significantly blunts the exercise-induced expression in skeletal muscles in rodents.79

Regular exercise decreases insulin resistance in type 2 diabetics

Type 2 diabetes mellitus is characterized by dysregulation of carbohydrate, lipid, and protein metabolism resulting from impaired insulin secretion, insulin resistance, or a combination of both.125 Compared to both type 1 diabetes and gestational diabetes, type 2 diabetes is far more common and accounts for ∼90% of all cases of diabetes.125 Importantly, it is widely agreed that both resistance exercise and endurance exercise training increase skeletal muscle and whole body insulin sensitivity in type 2 diabetics.126 Although several unresolved questions remain in elucidating the mechanisms responsible for the benefits of exercise on improving insulin resistance, evidence indicates that ROS production in cells enhances insulin sensitivity127 and mounting evidence indicates that production of ROS by NOX4 is required for the exercise-induced adaptive responses that improve insulin resistance in muscle fibers.128, 129, 130, 131

Regulation of muscle force production

As discussed earlier, contraction-induced production of ROS contributes to the development of muscle fatigue. However, in unfatigued muscle, low levels of ROS act to enhance muscle force production.28 This point was illustrated earlier in Fig. 1. Specifically, notice in Fig. 1 that at rest, the muscle redox state is not optimal to generate maximal force production. Indeed, early work by Reid et al. demonstrated ROS present in unfatigued skeletal muscle has a positive impact on muscle force.28 Close examination of Fig. 1 reveals that ROS have a biphasic effect on contractile function of unfatigued muscle.28,132 That is, basal levels of ROS do not represent the optimal cellular redox state to achieve maximal muscle force production. However, the commencement of muscle contractions results in small increase in ROS levels in muscle that are required to achieve optimal force production. However, as illustrated in Fig. 1, this positive effect of ROS is reversed at higher ROS concentrations as illustrated by the decline in muscle force production.132

How does a low level of endogenous ROS increase muscle force production? A complete answer to this question is currently unknown but it appears that low levels of ROS increase calcium sensitivity of the muscle without increasing intracellular levels of free calcium.133,134 Therefore, an increase in calcium sensitivity increases the number of crossbridge binding and therefore, force generation is increased. For more details the reader is referred to reviews on this topic.89,104,107

Conclusion: is exercise-induced ROS a friend or a foe?

As discussed throughout this report, evidence exists that exercise-induce ROS production can be both a friend and a foe depending upon the context of the exercise intensity and duration. Notably, understanding this duality requires recognition of the fact that redox signaling in skeletal muscle is highly compartmentalized and context dependent. Exercise-induced ROS are not generated as a homogeneous cellular signal; rather, they arise from distinct subcellular sources, including mitochondria, NADPH oxidases, and other redox enzymes, with spatial and temporal control. Consequently, the physiological impact of exercise-induced ROS is dictated not only by their overall magnitude but also by their site of production, duration, and chemical identity, which together determine whether ROS act as targeted signaling molecules that promote beneficial adaptations or, alternatively, contribute to oxidative distress and impaired function.

We conclude the debate of whether exercise-induced ROS production is a friend or foe with a point-by-point critical analysis of the risk/benefits associated with exercise-induced ROS production. Let's begin with an analysis of the risk associated with exercise-induced ROS production.

As discussed previously, the two major arguments that exercise-induced ROS production in skeletal muscle is a foe emerges from the evidence that contracting muscles produce ROS that oxidize cellular macromolecules and contribute to muscle fatigue (Fig. 5A). Although prolonged moderate and heavy intensity exercise does increase lipid peroxidation and oxidation of proteins in muscle fibers, there is limited evidence that this level of macromolecular oxidation is detrimental to health. In fact, a voluminous body of evidence establishes that regular moderate or heavy intensity aerobic exercise and/or regular resistance exercise training (i.e., 2-3 times/week) is associated with a reduced risk of all-cause mortality and a lowered risk of developing several chronic diseases including coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and cancer.14,16,17,135, 136, 137 Thus, this robust evidence indicates that the level of ROS-mediated damage to macromolecules that occur during exercise does not appear to be detrimental to health.Fig. 5Summary of the (A) potential detrimental effects of exercise-induced ROS production in skeletal muscles versus (B) the potential benefits of exercise induced ROS production in the muscle (right). Further, as illustrated in Fig. 5A, a second, and persuasive argument to support the notion that exercise-induced ROS production is a foe is the evidence that during high or very high intensity exercise, contraction-induced ROS production contributes to muscle fatigue. In contrast, there is limited evidence that exercise-induced ROS production negatively impacts exercise performance during moderate or extreme exercise intensities.

Although muscle fatigue hinders endurance exercise performance, it can also be reasoned that muscle fatigue during prolonged or high intensity exercise can be protective to prevent harm to the body during prolonged high intensity exercise (e.g., hyperthermia, dehydration, and/or muscle damage).138 If this is accurate, a case can be made that the muscle fatigue that occurs during a prolonged/high intensity bout of endurance exercise is a friend and not a foe.

As highlighted in section 7, there are at least five reasons to support the position that exercise-induced ROS production in skeletal muscle is a friend (Fig. 5B). Four of the arguments that back the position that exercise-induced ROS production is a friend focus on the concept that relatively small and acute increases in ROS production (e.g., H2O2) provide an important signal to promote health-related physiological adaptations; these include increased mitochondrial biogenesis, increases the expression of stress proteins (e.g., HSP72), improved insulin sensitivity, and increased expression of numerous antioxidant enzymes. Further, at onset of exercise, small increases in contraction-induced ROS production change the cellular redox state from a reduced state to a more oxidized state; this alteration in redox status is associated with increased skeletal muscle force production (Fig. 1, Fig. 5B). Together, these positive benefits of exercise-induced ROS production make a strong case for the stance that exercise-induced ROS production can be a friend during both moderate and heavy exercise.

Fig. 6 illustrates a qualitative “see-saw” analysis of the desired versus the undesired effects of acute increases in muscle ROS production during exercise. Obviously, this simple qualitative analysis does not permit a firm conclusion about whether exercise-induced ROS production is a friend or foe. Nonetheless, based on this simple see-saw analysis, it appears that, except for muscle fatigue during prolonged heavy exercise, the benefits of an acute increase in exercise-induced ROS production outweigh the undesired effects. Indeed, the four key health benefits of regular exercise are likely driven by ROS-induced signaling pathways. It follows that the established health benefits of regular exercise alone indicate that the level of exercise-induced oxidation of macromolecules in the muscle is not detrimental to health. Moreover, it can also be argued that exercise-induced muscle fatigue during prolonged and high intensity exercise can also be beneficial as a “safety value” to reduce the risk of exercise-induced harm to the body. Therefore, based on current evidence, a scientifically supported argument can be made that exercise-induced ROS production is a friend and not a foe. Nonetheless, a definitive answer to the question, “is exercise-induced ROS production a friend or foe” requires additional research that investigates a wide range of exercise modalities, durations, and intensities using multiple techniques to evaluate the cellular impact of various levels of exercise-induced ROS production on a variety of cell types. Finally, it is important to note that much of this review has focused on ROS production and signaling within skeletal muscle fibers themselves. However, skeletal muscle is a highly complex and heterogeneous tissue, and its redox responses cannot be fully understood by considering myofibers in isolation. This complexity is further amplified by the remarkable cellular and nuclear heterogeneity of skeletal muscle, in which fiber-type-specific myonuclei and resident non-myogenic cells exhibit distinct redox programs and vulnerabilities.139
