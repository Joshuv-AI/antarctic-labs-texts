Sugar-sweetened beverage consumption and risk of premature coronary artery disease in a multi-ethnic Iranian case–control study
Authors: Noushin Mohammadifard, Negar Ostadsharif, Ghazaleh Bahrami, Motahare Bateni, Ehsan Zarepur, Fatemeh Nouri, Fereidoon Nouhi, Nahid Azdaki, Nahid Salehi, Masoud Lotfizadeh, Samad Ghaffari, Arsalan Salari, Mostafa Dehghani, Mostafa Cheraghi, Ahmadreza Assareh, Hassan Alikhasi, Fahimeh Haghighatdoost, Nizal Sarrafzadegan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe association of sugar sweetened beverages (SSBs) and coronary artery disease (CAD) has not been well-established in Asians, where SSBs are the leading ultra-processed food product.ObjectiveWe aim to examine the association between SSBs and premature CAD (PCAD) in Iranian adults.DesignCase-control.ParticipantsA multi-centric study of Iranians including 2006 PCAD and 1131 healthy individuals as control group.Main outcome measuresDietary intakes were assessed using a validated food frequency questionnaire (FFQ). SSBs consist of artificial juice and sugar -sweetened drinks. The PCAD was determined based on the results of angiography and the occlusion percent of vessels.Statistical analysisThe odds of PCAD across the quartiles of SSBs were assessed by binary logistic regression.ResultsThe mean (SD) age of participants and SSB consumption was 51.5 years and 46.9 g/d, respectively. In the fully-adjusted model, compared with participants in the first quartile, those in the fourth quartile had higher risk of PCAD (OR = 1.50, 95% CI: 1.12, 2.00; P trend = 0.044). Consistently, SSB consumption was directly associated with the severity of PCAD. The higher SSB consumption, the greater risk for the severe PCAD (OR Q4 vs. Q1 = 1.34, 95% CI: 1.06, 1.68; P < 0.001).ConclusionThis study demonstrated that higher consumption of SSB might be associated with higher risk of PCAD. However, more prospective cohort studies are necessary to confirm this association.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) is the third leading cause of death in both developed and developing countries and the most common type of heart disease [1]. Approximately, 50% of all deaths in Iran are due to CAD annually [2]. Although age is one of the most important CAD risk factors [3], premature CAD (PCAD), which occurs in younger ages [4], is increasing worldwide, especially in developing countries [5, 6]. This may highlight the relevance and growing trend of some risk factors related to lifestyle [7].

In most countries, dietary habits have changed to containing more sweet foods including desserts and sugar-sweetened beverages (SSB) [8]. Similar trend is observed in Asia as a result of Westernized lifestyle, urbanization and intensive beverage marketing. SSB sales more than doubled in South Asia between 2005 and 2018 [9]. High amounts of sugar may adversely affect cardio metabolic health through inflammation, dyslipidemia and increased insulin resistance beside negative effects on excess energy intake and fat mass [10]. Furthermore, due to the high carbohydrate content and glycemic load of these inexpensive beverages [11], SSBs may increase the risk of obesity and diabetes mellitus [12, 13].

Depending on the pattern and amount of SSB consumption beside other lifestyle factors, the association between SSB and various health outcomes may differ between countries. Several studies have demonstrated beneficial effects for decreasing sugar and sweet food intake on the cardio-metabolic risk factor profile [11, 14]. Nevertheless, epidemiological studies have mainly focused on cardiometabolic risk factors and only a few studies have assessed the risk of cardiovascular disease, in particular coronary artery disease with inconsistent findings. In the NHLBI Family Heart Study, conducted on 1991 participants without coronary artery disease at baseline, no association was found between SSBs consumption and subclinical atherosclerosis, determined by coronary artery calcium (CAC) [11]. In another cohort study among Swedish adults, sugar-rich beverages did not significantly influence intima media thickness (IMT), as a measure of subclinical atherosclerosis [15]. In contrast, in a large cross-sectional study on Korean adults, higher levels of SSBs were directly associated with both prevalence and degree of CAC [16]. In a recent review, a dose-response association was suggested between SSBs consumption and the risk of cardiovascular diseases [17]. To date, limited studies have been conducted concerning this association within Iranian population, whereas certain research studies were conducted regarding the association between SSB and stroke, metabolic syndrome, and general obesity among Iranians, but these studies found no significant association [18, 19]. Despite traditional sugar-sweetened drinks (e.g., artificially flavored syrups) remaining a staple drink across all socioeconomic strata, no study has examined SSB intake in relation to angiographically confirmed premature CAD in Iranians. Therefore, our hypothesis in this study is that higher consumption of SSB is associated with increased odds and severity of PCAD among Iranian adults in the framework of IPAD study.

Materials and methods

Design and participants

This investigation is a multi-center, cross-sectional analysis of data collected from patients undergoing coronary angiography. We evaluated the association between sugar-sweetened beverage intake with the risk of PCAD and its severity. The study population was performed in the framework of the Iran premature coronary artery disease (IPAD) study, which is a multi-centric, case-control study on different ethnicities of Iranians. Participants were recruited from eight ethnicities (Fars, Azari, Kurd, Arab, Lor, Gilak, Qashqaei, and Bakhtiari) across the country. The participants underwent angiography based on clinical indications such as acute coronary syndrome (ACS), chronic coronary syndrome (stable angina), and positive stress test. The participants with an occlusion of at least single coronary artery ≥ 75% or left main coronary ≥ 50% were included case group and those who had normal coronary artery was regarded as control group. Both case and control groups were outpatients with elective angiography. Given that the age cut-off for PCAD varies between studies [20–23], the cut-off age in this study was considered ≤ 70 years for women and ≤ 60 years for men. A wider range was considered due to the limitation for the enrolment of participants. All data were collected from hospitals with catheterization laboratories in 13 cities from January 2019 to October 2021. Previous history of documented CAD such as coronary artery bypass surgery, balloon angioplasty, or percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) was regarded as exclusion criterion. The details of the study were published elsewhere [24]. In addition, the severity of PCAD was categorized into three groups including normal coronary artery, stenosis in one vessel, stenosis in two vessels or left main coronary artery.

This study was approved by the ethics committee of Isfahan University of Medical Sciences (IR.MUI.REC.1396.2.055). A written informed consent was obtained from all patients and the Declaration of Helsinki was considered.

Data collection

According to the study protocol [24], the information about socioeconomic status, demographics, and lifestyle factors such as smoking status, alcohol use, dietary intake, health status, ethnicity, and medical history was collected through fully structured interviews conducted by trained health professionals. Physical activity in this study was examined by a validated, Persian version of the international physical activity questionnaire (IPAQ) [25]. Body weight and height were assessed while participants were barefoot and light clothing using standard protocols [26]. Body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight in kilogram divided by square of height in meter (kg/m2). After a 5-minute rest and in a sitting position, blood pressure was measured twice by trained nurses manually using a mercury sphygmomanometer [27].

Dietary assessment

Dietary intake was evaluated using a validated 110-item FFQ [28]. In a face-to-face interview conducted by trained interviewers, participants were requested to report their frequency of consumption of each food item over the preceding year. The FFQ provided the frequency in ten-option categories (‘seldom/never, once per month, 2–3 per month, once per week, 2–3 per week, 4–6 per week, 1 per day, 2–3 per day, 4–5 per day, and 6 or more per day). In addition, they were asked to record the portion size of each food and beverage item. Using household measurements, the frequency was converted into daily intake and multiplied by the portion sizes to estimate daily amount of consumption (g/d) for each food item. SSBs included various types of beverages sweetened with sugar including artificially juice and cola or other carbonated beverages. Low-calorie drinks were not considered in the estimation of SSBs intake. Low-calorie drinks were defined as beverages containing ≤ 40 kcal per 240 mL (8 oz), typically sweetened with non-nutritive sweeteners such as aspartame, sucralose, or stevia [29]. Moreover, since sweetened tea drinks and sports drinks are not common in Iran, we did not assess their intake in the FFQ. Based on the frequency and the amount of consumption and using the household measures [30], all food items were converted to g/d. Daily energy intake of each participant was estimated according to the US Department of Agriculture food-composition database [31]. Pearson correlation for sweets (including sweets, drink and deserts) estimated though FFQ and dietary recalls was 0.53.

Biochemical analysis

A 10 ml 12-h fasting blood sample was taken from participants. Blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min and froze in -80°C. Serum lipids and blood sugar were measured in each center. Fasting blood sugar (FBS), serum total cholesterol (TC) and triglyceride (TG) were measured by the enzymatic method and using commercial kits (Pars Azmoon Co., Tehran, Iran). High-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) was determined enzymatically after precipitating other lipoproteins with dextran sulfate magnesium chloride [32]. The Friedewald formula was utilized to calculate Low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) in individuals with TG < 400 mg/ dL. However, direct measurement of LDL-C was performed with a turbidimetric method for those with TG ≥ 400 mg/dL [33]. Dyslipidemia was defined according to the National Cholesterol Education Panel Adult Treatment Panel III (NCEP-ATP III) [34]. Diabetes was defined based on the American Diabetes Association Criteria [35]. The participants with any of the TC ≥ 200 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/ dL, LDL-C ≥ 130 mg/dL and/ or HDL-C < 40 mg/dL for men and < 50 mg/dL for women were diagnosed with dyslipidemia [34]. Diabetes mellitus was defined as FBG ≥ 126 mg/dL and/ or taking hypoglycemic medications [36].

Statistical analysis

SSB was adjusted for average daily energy intake by the residual method and its quartiles were used in statistical analysis [37]. Since it is possible that those with higher energy intake consume more SSB, we adjust energy intake using residual method and then categorized participants to avoid any possible misclassification due to energy intake. The analyses were done in two descriptive and analytical sections. Quantitative variables are reported as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Univariate comparisons between case and control groups were performed by independent t-test or Mann-Whitney based on statistical assumptions or chi-square test. To evaluate the odds (OR and 95% CI) of PCAD across the quartiles of SSB, multiple logistic regression was performed in the crude and various adjusted models. Age (continuous), sex (men/ women) and ethnicity were adjusted in model 1; further adjustment was applied for education (illiterate, primary school and higher than primary school), smoking status (never, ex-smoker, current smoker), alcohol consumption (g/day) and daily physical activity (continuous, METs-min/day) in model 2; additionally energy (Kcal/ day), fruits (g/ day), vegetables (g/ day), and sugar (g/ days) were adjusted in model 3. Additional adjustment for mediators including BMI (Kg/ m2), hypertension (yes/ no), diabetes mellitus (yes/ no), hypercholesterolemia (yes/ no), and aspirin use (yes/ no) was performed in the fourth model. We also explored the associations stratified by the ethnicity. However, because of low samples, only crude and model 1 were done in ethnicities other than Fars. All analyses were performed at 5% error level, using Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS, Inc., Chicago IL, USA; version 20).

Results

A total of 3,137 individuals were enrolled in the study, consisting of 2,006 cases and 1,131 controls. The mean (SD) age of participants was 54.73 (7.17) years in the case group and 51.97 (8.21) years in the control group, and 1669 (54.4%) and 401 (35.8%) of participants were male in the case and control groups, respectively. Additionally, the mean (SD) BMI was 28.12 (5.04) in the case group and 28.79 (5.43) in the control group. There were statistically significant differences in age, sex, and BMI between the case and control groups (all p-value < 0.001).

In Table 1, general and demographic variables in subjects with and without PCAD based on the quartiles of SSB consumption are demonstrated. In both case and control groups, those who consumed more SSB tended to consume more alcohol and exhibit lower levels of physical activity suggesting a clustering of unhealthy lifestyle behaviors. There was no significant association between SSBs and SBP, DBP, cholesterol, LDL, HDL, and FBS or the use of any type of medication. (Table 1).

Discussion

This case-control study, conducted among different ethnicities of Iranians, revealed a positive association between SSB consumption and the risk of PCAD. The results changed slightly after adjustment for potential confounders but remained statistically significant. However, in the sensitivity analysis by the ethnicity, the association was statistically significant in Fars and Kurd ethnicities, but not others.

The findings of our study corroborate the findings of previous studies that have demonstrated a positive association between SSB consumption and clinically manifest CVD events. The Nurses’ Health Study showed that SSB consumption of 1 and 2 serving(s) per day was respectively associated with a 23% and 35% higher risk of incident coronary heart disease [38]. The Health Professional Follow-up study also indicated that individuals in the top quartile of SSB consumption had a 20% higher risk for coronary heart disease in comparison with those in the bottom quartile [39]. Likewise, in the Japan Public Health Center-based study cohort, participants who consumed more than one soft drink per day had an 83% higher risk for ischemic stroke as compared to individuals who never consumed soft drinks [40]. The results of a recent systematic review revealed that with each additional SSB and artificially sweetened beverages (ASB) serving per day, CVD risk increased by 9% and 8%, respectively [41]. In contrast, there are several studies suggesting no association between SSB and CVDs. For instance, Patel et al. found no association between SSB consumption and prevalent CAC, even after adjusting for CVD risk factors. This may be due to the study design and the use of a single FFQ at baseline that cannot consider changes in SSB consumption over time [11]. Similarly, another study reported no significant association between added sugar intake and intima media thickness, as a measurement of subclinical atherosclerosis [15]. These differences may stem from reliance on subclinical endpoints (CAC, IMT), single FFQ assessments lacking repeat measures, and heterogeneity in beverage definitions. Our angiographic PCAD endpoint and multi-ethnic sampling likely capture more severe disease and broader behavioral patterns, potentially accounting for stronger observed associations. It is worth to note that in our study there was an increasing trend across the quartiles of SSB for PCAD risk before adjustment for dietary intakes. However, differences in dietary intakes between those in the third quartile and others specially in terms of energy and fruit intake may explain the higher risk of PCAD in quartile 3 vs. quartile 4. In other words, those in the third quartile had lower energy intake than other quartiles. It means that with increasing SSB consumption across the quartiles of SSB, those with lower energy intake had higher proportion of energy intake from SSBs, and this, in turn can worsen the risk of PCAD in this group.

The possible mechanisms involved in the direct relationship between SSBs consumption and the risks of CVDs might be explained by several reasons. SSBs contain great amounts of energy-containing sweeteners, such as sucrose (50% glucose, 50% fructose), high-fructose corn syrup (45% glucose, 55% fructose, or 58% glucose and 42% fructose), and fruit juice concentrates [42], which have adverse effect on health status. Various studies have shown that consumption of fructose-sweetened beverages contributes to ectopic lipid deposition through increasing triglyceride load and LDL and decreasing HDL levels [11]. On the other hand, the adverse effects of simple sugars are predominantly mediated through their impacts on serum lipids but not blood sugar. This effect is influenced by various factors, such as subjects’ serum triglyceride and LDL-c levels, body weight and physical activity [43]. Moreover, SSBs may disrupt beta cell function in the pancreas through increasing serum glucose level directly and inflammatory markers indirectly [10, 11, 16, 42], and induce insulin resistance. Additionally, a diet high in simple carbohydrates can increase the pericardial and visceral adipose tissue contributing to CAC and CVD. This association can be explained by the dietary carbohydrate conversion into fat, or de novo lipogenesis that can be enhanced by excessive intake of simple carbohydrates [44]. SSBs also stimulate inflammatory response and various inflammatory biomarkers secretion, such as intercellular adhesion molecule (ICAM), interleukin 6 (IL6), c-reactive protein (CRP), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) [26] and Fetuin-A via activating the electron transport chain [42]. This inflammation factors leads to atherosclerosis in coronary arteries and endothelial damage through the release of superoxide radicals [11, 42]. However, genetic susceptibility may explain, at least to some extent, the discrepancies between studies. There is some evidence showing that the association between SSB and obesity is more pronounced among subjects with genetic predisposition to adiposity [45]. Moreover, unhealthy dietary habits, like high intake of SSB, may interact with genetic factors and exacerbate their impacts on CAD [46]. Therefore, differences in genetics should be taken into account when examining CAD-SSB relationship.

Although findings are inconsistent, there is some evidence suggesting that consumption of sweeteners in foods and beverages over time may alter taste preferences towards sweet-tasting foods, increase appetite, and modify gut hormone secretion, which altogether lead to excess energy consumption and fat mass accumulation [47]. It is also possible that high intake of SSB be associated with lower diet quality which have unfavorable effects on health status [48].

The lower consumption of whole grain, red and processed meat, sweet, HVO, and non-HVO alongside increased consumption of vegetable, fruit, legume, dairy product, and meat in subjects who consumed more SSB suggested a dietary pattern that may be influenced more by cultural and economic factors than by SSB consumption.

This study has several limitations. Due to the inherent limitations of observational studies, it is not possible to establish a causal relationship and determine the temporal sequence of events and SSB consumption. Another limitation that warrants consideration is the potential for recall bias. This may arise due to differential recall of dietary history between patients and healthy volunteers, with patients potentially exhibiting greater accuracy in their recollection of nutritional intake. Measurement errors in the intake of SSB and other dietary factors are inevitable since they are memory-dependent. We did not assess the intake of other sources of sugar drinks such as tea, coffee, and sport drinks and assessed the validity of SSB intake estimation in combination with sweets and deserts. However, we adjusted our models for sugar intake to control consumption of sugar beside tea and coffee. Furthermore, it should be accounted that packed sweetened tea, coffee and sport drinks are not common among Iranians, and the main source of SSBs just were assessed in this study. Estimating energy intake using the US Department of Agriculture’s food composition table in this population is another possible limitation of our study, though it has been adapted for Iranians. Second, despite using a validated FFQ to reduce bias, recall bias is inevitable. Third, we considered various variables confounders; however, the effect of residual variables cannot be completed ruled out. Fourth, energy intake estimates based on the USDA composition table may not perfectly reflect Iranian food nutrient profiles. Fifth, our study individuals were elective outpatients for angiography and therefore our findings might not be generalizable to all. Finally, although the highest vs. lowest SSB quartiles were significantly associated with an increased risk of PCAD, the moderate effect size and width of the confidence interval indicate some imprecision, underscoring the need for larger prospective studies to refine risk estimates. Our study also has some strengths, such as recruiting participants from different ethnic groups, which provide a large external validity for our findings. Second, we assessed SSB-PCAD association stratified by ethnicity which allows us to consider the residual confounding effects of lifestyle habits and socioeconomic status caused by variations in ethnicity. Third, all cases and controls had angiography and their disease was approved by physicians which minimize ascertainment bias.

The findings of our study in the IPAD indicate that there may be an association between SSB consumption and the risk of PCAD. However, further studies are needed to confirm this relationship. Implementation of policies by policymakers such as taxes on sugar-sweetened beverages, public education campaigns, and encouragement of healthier beverage choices could help curb rising rates of premature coronary artery disease.
