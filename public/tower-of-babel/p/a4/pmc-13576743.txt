Adapting to a Shrunken World: A Grounded Theory of Resident Adaptation to Life in Residential Aged Care
Authors: Jarrah FitzGerald, Yvonne D Wells, Jane Mills
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo develop a grounded theory explaining the psychosocial processes older adults use when adapting to life in residential aged care.DesignAn evolved grounded theory methodology was used.MethodsSemi‐structured interviews were conducted with 20 residents and three staff members across four residential aged care homes in Victoria, Australia, supplemented with ethnographic observations. Introduction

Global population ageing will see increasing numbers of older people requiring residential aged care services, a form of long‐term care provided to people who are unable to live independently. In many countries, residential aged care services are delivered within purpose‐built facilities, variously referred to as residential aged care homes (RACHs), nursing homes or long‐term care homes. Older adults entering RACHs often have complex needs, including physical dependency, cognitive impairment or challenges with independent living, which contributes to growing demand for, and pressure on, such facilities worldwide (World Health Organization 2015). In response, new approaches to care delivery are needed to ensure resources and services align with the needs and preferences of this growing and diverse population. Understanding the lived experiences of those in RACHs can help direct services appropriately.

Background

Transitions into RACHs represent significant life events requiring psychoemotional adjustment (Costlow and Parmelee 2020). Distress associated with such moves contributes to already high rates of mental health issues among long‐term care residents (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023a). While older people generally prefer to age in place, the probability of entering residential aged care after age 65 is substantial. In Australia, close to 40% of people over 65 will enter a RACH at some stage (Broad et al. 2015), with risk increasing substantially with each decade beyond 65 years. For most older people, a move into a RACH is likely to be their last—the primary reason for exits from permanent residential care (86% of exits) in Australia is death (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b)—making it essential to ensure that a life lived in care is as fulfilling and meaningful as possible.

Ideally, aged care systems enable older people to live self‐determined and meaningful lives in safe, caring environments. Contemporary aged care policy and practice increasingly emphasise healthy ageing and person‐centred care (PCC). Person‐centred approaches acknowledge the importance of historical, environmental and personal aspects of health and ageing, linking these with resident autonomy. However, systemic issues continue to affect the sector internationally (Sturmberg et al. 2024), and the connection between promoting resident autonomy and improving health outcomes is not always recognised by staff (Tuckett 2007). This is especially evident in settings where care practices are organised around fixed daily schedules, requiring residents to adapt to institutional routines rather than enabling care to be structured around their own rhythms and preferences (Hauge and Kristin 2008; Nygaard et al. 2020). Policies and practices informed by residents' experiences are needed.

While growing evidence draws upon older people's experiences in care, transitions have often been explored only in short‐term periods post‐admission. Most articles identified in systematic reviews focus on initial transitions, with underlying concepts presented thematically rather than as explanatory processes (Brownie et al. 2014; Fitzpatrick and Tzouvara 2019). The average length of stay in care in Australian RACHs is 24 months (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2022), with many residents staying considerably longer. Understanding how residents adapt beyond initial periods is essential.

The Study

Aim

This study aimed to develop a grounded theory explaining the psychosocial processes older adults use when adapting to life in residential aged care.

Methods

Design

Given the need to incorporate residents' voices and develop theory, an evolved grounded theory design was implemented, drawing on classical grounded theory methods and underpinned by symbolic interactionism (Birks et al. 2019; Blumer 1969; Corbin and Strauss 2008; Glaser and Strauss 1967). Grounded theory is particularly suited to generating process‐oriented theories that capture what is important in participants' lives. As an inductive and iterative methodology, grounded theory relies on constant comparison of data, codes and concepts. This process helps researchers identify core concerns of participants and develop explanatory frameworks grounded in their experiences. A symbolic interactionist lens was applied to the grounded theory methods used in this study to direct analytic attention towards how residents interpreted and responded to their social and physical environments over time. Ethnographic observations were incorporated to contextualise these interactions.

Study Setting and Recruitment

The study was conducted across four RACHs in Victoria, Australia. Details on the four RACHs have been provided as Table S1. The populations from which the sample was drawn comprised (a) all residents of participating RACHs who were sufficiently cognitively intact to be able to provide informed consent to participate, and (b) key clinical staff at each participating site.

Recruitment followed a two‐step process. First, residents could volunteer using contact details provided on informational flyers and posters displayed at participating RACHs. Second, to provide contextual information and their perceptions of how residents adapted to life in a RACH, senior staff (e.g., managers, directors of nursing and/or lifestyle coordinators) were recruited. These staff were provided with information about the study and any participation requirements, making clear that participation was voluntary. In addition to providing their own insights, staff also helped identify potential residents who might not access the recruitment posters (e.g., those who were bedbound or visually impaired) or were uncomfortable contacting an external party. Staff assisted by providing residents with information and consent forms and asking if they were willing to be contacted by the researcher. Contact occurred by phone or in person at least 1 week after staff supplied the researcher with residents' details, allowing time for residents to review the materials and consider any questions. To assess eligibility in light of potential cognitive decline, residents were asked to describe the study and participant responsibilities in their own words as part of the consent process. Staff identification of potential participants also supported this assessment. All participants (both staff and residents) received information and consent forms relevant to their participation prior to recruitment.

Data Collection

Semi‐structured interviews and ethnographic observations conducted between September 2021 and March 2023 generated data for analysis. These were undertaken by the first author (PhD candidate at the time), a male researcher with prior qualitative research experience and formal training in grounded theory methods.

A purposive sample of five residents from a single RACH generated initial data. These interviews were conducted via an online platform due to COVID‐19 restrictions on facility access. This sample covered basic demographic variation including age, gender, marital status and length of stay. The remaining 18 interviews (including follow‐up interviews) were conducted face‐to‐face in participants' private rooms once restrictions were eased. Iterative rounds of concurrent data generation and analysis informed theoretical sampling of additional participants to define characteristics, boundaries and relationships of codes and categories.

Initial interviews used a basic interview guide focusing on residents' transitions into, and experiences in, their RACH. Initial questions included: ‘Can you tell me about how you came to live here?’, ‘What was that process like?’, and ‘Has your life changed since moving here?’. Subsequent interviews were informed by previous responses and updated as codes and concepts developed. Participants were given space to respond in their own time, with follow‐up questions used to probe and clarify topics raised. All interviews were transcribed verbatim before being entered into NVivo for coding and categorization.

Multiple periods of ethnographic observation were undertaken at each facility at different times and spaces after COVID‐19 related restrictions were lifted to observe location character and interactions. Field notes and analytic memos explored emerging ideas and were analysed as data, informing theory development.

Data Analysis

Initial coding was conducted line‐by‐line for each transcript by the first author, using short, descriptive labels (codes) to describe actions, events or ideas in the data. Intermediate coding collapsed codes into conceptual categories at higher levels of abstraction. This was more selective than initial coding, with the most common or salient codes used to sort, synthesise, integrate and organise data. Advanced coding methods, including storylining, diagramming and theoretical coding, were used to develop a core category (Birks and Mills 2023). A core category explains the most variation within data and represents the phenomenon around which all other categories integrate. Theoretical coding integrated developed categories into a coherent theoretical framework, with reference to Glaser's description of processes (Glaser 1978). The framework was discussed with the second and third authors to refine interpretations and ensure consensus. This framework is an abstraction proposed as an explanation for tacit processes at the heart of the final substantive grounded theory (Birks and Mills 2023).

Once the core category was determined, theoretical sampling focused more narrowly on exploring this category, its subsumed categories, and their properties and dimensions. This involved re‐interviewing some participants and identifying new participants who could shed light on aspects requiring further exploration and seeking cases that challenged or contradicted developing interpretations. Data collection continued until categories were sufficiently conceptually rich and deep and, having sought out a range of participants and experiences, new data simply reaffirmed major categories and their properties, requiring no major theoretical revisions (Birks and Mills 2023).

Ethical Considerations

Institutional ethics approval was obtained from the La Trobe University Human Research Ethics Committee prior to commencement (project number: HEC21010, approved 14th May 2021). All efforts ensured research complied with principles in the Australian Code for the Responsible Conduct of Research (2018). No prior relationship was established with participants, although they were provided with information about the researcher's role, study aims, and interest in understanding residents' experiences in aged care. All prospective participants received participation information and consent forms and were given 1 week to consider their decision. Verbal consent was given to the first author prior to interviews. Given topics had potential to cause distress, participants were reassured they need not answer anything they did not want to and could stop interviews at any time. The option of having a support person present was offered. Signs of distress were monitored throughout. While some participants became emotional, none became distressed. Assurances of confidentiality and anonymity were provided and ensured using pseudonyms for all participants and RACHs.

Another key risk is related to the potential identification of abuse. A list of advocacy groups was provided to participants, and the mandatory obligation to report violence and abuse was outlined in study documentation. However, no instances of abuse were observed or narrated by participants.

Rigour and Reflexivity

The quality of grounded theory research was reinforced through methodological strategies and evaluative criteria. Strict adherence to essential grounded theory methods ensured procedural quality, preventing unwarranted intrusion of pre‐existing theories and helping researchers view the world from participants' perspectives (Birks and Mills 2023). In evaluating the final output, Glaser's (1998) four criteria were applied: workability, relevance, fit and modifiability. Workability was achieved through a clear explanation of participants' behaviours, actions and outcomes. The final theory is relevant to both residents and those working within RACHs, helping explain residents' actions and motivations while offering insights into requirements for positive adaptation. High fit was achieved through the application of grounded theory methods to ensure that the theory was grounded in data. Modifiability was achieved through the comparison of multiple accounts across facilities, supported by observations, and the theory's ability to incorporate new data.

Ethnographic observation aided development of theoretical sensitivity and improved credibility and reliability of findings (FitzGerald and Mills 2022). Extensive memoing served as a tool for reflexive practice—identifying and exploring implications of the first author's prior experiences, assumptions and beliefs—and as an audit trail of concept and category evolution, safeguarding against unwarranted inclusion of a priori ideas. Frequent discussion with peers and regular team debriefing contributed to quality, as analytical developments were regularly scrutinised and debated (Birks and Mills 2023), with analytical decisions agreed upon by consensus between the three authors.

Findings

The Theory: Adapting to a Shrunken World

The theory presented is a novel theory of resident adaptation to life in a RACH. This process comprises three main elements: (1) realising the need for care, (2) facing a life in residential aged care (with subcategories: turning towards life in care and turning away from life in care), and (3) living with loss. Combined, these categories form the theory of Adapting to a shrunken world (Figure 1). To illustrate the analytic progression, initial line‐by‐line codes such as ‘finding ways to get on with things’, ‘working as a team’, and ‘sticking to what one can do’ were synthesised into the subcategory turning towards life in care, while codes such as ‘resisting or resenting care’ and ‘focusing on losses’ contributed to turning away from life in care. These subcategories combined to form the category facing a life in care. Similar analytic consolidation occurred for the categories realising the need for care and living with loss, each developed through iterative comparison and integration of codes. Together, these categories integrated into the core category adapting to a shrunken world, capturing how residents work to adjust as their worlds contract.

A visual model depicting the process of adapting to a shrunken world.

This grounded theory model depicts the process of adapting to a shrunken world. Reading from left to right, the diagram starts with realising the need for care and captures dualities present in this concept. The proactive line is longer, representing greater lead time and planning in a more considered move. Line smoothness indicates the easier, more positive transition occurring when individuals adequately realise and accept their need for care. The shorter, reactive pathway represents more rapid moves, likely resulting from health crises. The jagged line symbolises lack of acceptance and suggests a more difficult pathway. The arrows converge at a single point (i.e., the move into a RACH), illustrating that the move into a RACH represents both an external, physical relocation and an internal, personal realisation. This convergence occurs regardless of whether the transition has been smooth or difficult, or of the individual's level of acceptance.

Benefits of smooth transitions are illustrated by the flowing green arrowed line continuing in the same direction as the overall process, representing the pathway taken by those turning towards life in care. Its gentle outward undulations illustrate that, although a person's world inevitably shrinks with accumulating losses (the blue dotted line), those who accept support, adapt to changing circumstances, and engage with life in care can still extend their boundaries as far as possible. The line is placed on the outside of the spiral's curve to show that this orientation is unobstructed, that is, acceptance, collaboration and willingness to receive care allow the person's world to remain as expansive as their circumstances permit. Alternatively, turning away from life in care is represented by a broken line placed on the inside of the spiral's curve. This line depicts an orientation characterised by resistance and resentment, efforts to maintain previous ways of living despite irreversible changes, and reluctance to accept the support offered in response to these changes. Its placement on the inside of the spiral signifies that this stance further constrains the person's world: by resisting care and support, individuals limit the potential benefits that might otherwise help them maintain or extend their boundaries. The broken quality of the line reflects the disruptions, frustrations and repeated efforts to hold onto what has already been lost.

Losses provide motion within the process of adapting to a shrunken world, determining the diagram's overall structure. Living with loss is visualised as the spiralled line at the diagram's centre. The spiral form is used because it visually conveys both continuity (losses unfold over time) and constriction (each loss narrows the person's world, drawing the line inward). This line is punctuated by losses—of function, independence, roles, or capacities—that reduce the boundaries of what is possible. Because losses occur regardless of one's orientation, both turning towards and away from life in care follow this central line.

In reality, opposing concepts of turning towards or away from life in care exist on a continuum. People may acknowledge and accept some aspects while rejecting others. Although adapting to a shrunken world is conceptualised and presented linearly, it does not necessarily occur in perfectly discrete phases as ultimately, adapting to a shrunken world is considered an active, ongoing, psychosocial process shaped by continual change and accumulating loss across residents' lives in care.

Realising the Need for Care

Realising the need for care captures both meanings of ‘realise’: making something real (the physical move into a RACH) and becoming fully aware (recognising and accepting the need for care). Residents arrived either reactively—as a result of health crises and subsequent hospital stays or rapid changes in circumstances (e.g., death or declining health of partner)—or proactively, where older people self‐identified the need to move. In both staff and resident accounts, proactive arrivals were uncommon, with most admissions occurring after hospital or respite care stays. A Director of Care Services (Kelly) highlighted that, over 2 years, she had admitted only one resident (out of 60–80 total admissions) she would describe as ‘textbook’—someone whose move was entirely their choice, who was not in crisis, and who had finances and other matters in order—while the majority arrived in crisis. Relocation into a RACH represented a physical manifestation of care needs and acted as a catalyst for adapting to a shrunken world.

However, care needs must also be realised within oneself. Individuals' personal realisations (or lack thereof) most influenced their adaptation. Despite undergoing eligibility assessments, many individuals did not recognise or accept their care needs. From staff perspectives, arriving in crisis without accepting care needs made transitions additionally difficult:

The first couple of days are very unsettling for them, and resistive residents that don't really want to be here, they've come from hospital to here. (Luella)

I think what used to happen was that they went through all of those normal emotions out in the community. You know, that realization that ‘I need to consider aged care’… then all of a sudden they're in here… And so they're living that cycle here. (Kelly)

One consequence of arriving in crisis was delayed settling. Staff conversations about settling, once undertaken within weeks of admission, were now often postponed for months until staff felt new entrants were ready emotionally.

Another consequence was limited resident input into facility selection, with many entering as a result of decisions predominantly made by others (e.g., adult children, doctors). In such instances, moves could feel like being ‘thrown into the deep end’ (Vanessa) and ‘devastating’ (Mark), as though control over life had been lost. This type of transition, where lack of preplanning meant no pre‐existing acceptance, could exacerbate adaptation difficulties. When asked about their move and subsequent life, one person responded strongly:

[I was] dreading it. Because I don't think I'm unfit enough to be here. (Jason)

This assessment contrasted strongly with accounts in which participants realised and accepted their care needs:

I couldn't be anywhere else. I couldn't be at home, I couldn't manage. So, I had to be somewhere to be looked after. (Ellie)

Personal realisation of care needs was critical because, whether they liked it or not, these residents would likely remain until death—a fact openly acknowledged by participants regardless of their acceptance. How people faced this final phase laid the foundation for how positively they would adapt.

Facing a Life in Care

It was generally beyond residents' control to decide which daily activities were available, what food was offered, or to choose rooms or neighbours. In many cases, lack of control regarding what happens extended to when things happened. While senior staff recognised the importance of individual input regarding ‘what they want to do and how they want to do it’ (Brenton), inflexible care routines could exacerbate loss experiences, especially regarding ‘unintentional exclusion’ (Kelly). Where individuals relied on staff for transport (e.g., waiting in wheelchairs) or personal care (e.g., help with activities or toileting), rigid routines could result in negative outcomes:

I do believe that's what hurts a lot of the residents… It's when they're in wheelchairs having to wait to be taken back from the dining room… It's a routine and they might end up being the last one every time… A lot of times they'll say, “[You've] forgotten me,” you know, and that must feel terrible for them to feel forgotten. (Luella)

Delays in staff responses and having to ‘wait your turn’ (Ava‐Rose) were commonly discussed. Despite negative ramifications for control, residents maintained control over personal responses and how they chose to face life in care. While participants commonly perceived RACHs as terminus points, most who had accepted care needs discussed intentions to make the most of their remaining time despite limitations:

I want to enjoy what part of my life I've still got ready for me. (Ava‐Rose)

Turning Towards Life in Care

For those willing to accept life in care, this active determination to make the most of things meant that control was redefined rather than lost. Inevitabilities of ageing were acknowledged by residents who, having fully realised and accepted their care needs, turned towards life in care. Ceding levels of independence, autonomy and control regarding daily life (including personal privacy) was seen as necessary for securing care and assistance, and this exchange was willingly accepted:

Well, my degree of stress is less. I think that's the principal thing. Less stress, more routine. Regular tucker [food], regular lifestyle, without any need to be worried about things. (David)

Turning towards life in care allowed residents to focus on what they could still achieve. By willingly transferring tasks they could no longer do and responsibilities they could no longer carry, these participants directed time and energy towards personally rewarding pursuits and actively engaged in facility activities:

So, that's where I landed here. It's got a beautiful view out there. And I must say that it's enabled me to do things that I wanted to do for years. (Jim)

By and large, such individuals saw the ceding of independence, control and responsibility as partial rather than complete. For them, these aspects were not being taken away but could be shared with staff. This often took the form of helping with smaller tasks, as even ‘small decisions in their day‐to‐day life’ could be ‘incredibly important’ (Kelly). David helped with dishes, Tabitha did cleaning, and other residents undertook as many self‐care activities as possible. This helped residents feel part of a larger team jointly responsible for their care. Fluctuating capabilities still allowed residents, when able, to lessen staff workload by doing things themselves despite no requirement to do so. By actively participating, those turning towards life maximised their independence and control.

Turning Away From Life in Care

Those not accepting care needs risked taking more oppositional paths. While people in each facility faced the same policies and practices, what differed was how individuals perceived and responded to these. Turning away from life in care often led to taking issue with specific practices. Some participants said staff needed ‘training’ on making beds (Vanessa) or giving tablets (Jason). At more extreme ends, verbal and physical altercations with staff occurred:

I blew up one day and I told [the staff], “Get out and go away.” Then someone said, “Oh, we've got to come in to see whether you're alive.” I said, “Well, you know, I'm out of bed… so!.” (Mark)

Being unable or unwilling to accept care needs was key to turning away from life in care. Individuals who turned away were less likely to accept diminished abilities and less willing to share responsibility or control with others. Instead, they focused on negative aspects, where facility practices (e.g., bedtimes, schedules) and policies (e.g., risk management) were seen as overbearing, and lack of participation in daily care requirements (e.g., toileting, showering, dressing) represented potential challenges to identity.

Those turning away were inclined to interpret relinquishment of responsibility as loss rather than opportunity for teamwork, and to view any reduction in independence as a threat to their sense of self rather than part of a positive, beneficial exchange. Acceptance in this context was one of resignation rather than an active, negotiated adaptation, with one participant (Tim) repeatedly stating that he had resigned himself to his fate of living in care.

Living With Loss

Losses experienced on entry included loss of place (e.g., family home), physical space (downsizing to single rooms), and freedom of movement. These often compounded losses already experienced in community, such as loss of spouse or partner. Loss of personal possessions was a further consequence of downsizing, leading some residents to feel they had arrived with ‘nothing’ (Tabitha). Being removed from familiar tasks and routines, however small, represented yet more losses acting as reminders of roles and responsibilities no longer required:

I think it's the simple things, like they can never go and check their mailbox… You know, the simple things that we just take for granted—put the bin out on a Wednesday night—that's gone for them. (Luella)

Moves into a RACH could feel like compounding losses:

You have to give up the autonomy that went with running your own house. You have to give up, to a degree, your autonomy of the people you can see whenever you want to. So, you're more compromised in terms of your freedom. I think that becomes limited, and you have to accept that. (Scarlett)

Another important loss was social connections. Several participants spoke of losing friends when entering a RACH and lacking opportunities for deeper, more satisfying relationships once in care. Moves could cause social losses (e.g., moving away from friends), but difficulties in making new friendships related to friends and acquaintances inevitably ‘disappear[ing]’ (Justin). While some developed meaningful relationships in care, for others lack of time and proximity to death presented real barriers to cultivating new relationships.

Social dislocation and other necessary compromises regarding living arrangements all speak to living with loss. However, accepting ongoing losses was required for turning towards life in care:

I think, when you first start to accept help with showering, those personal things, at first that was hard and then you just accept it, as it saves a lot of energy, and it makes life easier. (Anna)

Given the inevitability of age‐related declines, accumulating losses provides motion within adapting to a shrunken world. With each loss, as physical or cognitive abilities further diminish, so too may opportunities for direct involvement in activities and one's ability to maintain personal responsibility for self‐care. Turning towards life required acknowledging the inevitability of such declines as part of life's “winding down phase” (Justin). Positive adaptation to this winding down involved accepting the care and assistance offered:

It's different because when you come into a place like this you lose your sense of independence… like joining the army. You're told what to do, and when to do it, and how to do it. But you get used to that…. I'm living here with my wife. We're together all the time. It's as normal a life as I can possibly have. (Russell)

Those turning towards life willingly used services and engaged with activities and facility life to positively manage the emotional ramifications of successive losses and implications for a sense of self. Turning away hindered this process, as potential remedies were instead perceived negatively—as unnecessary surrendering of control and selfhood in exchange for unneeded care—leading to resentment or bitterness and the potential for negative or resistive behaviour:

Well, [I] sit here and feel sorry for myself. Which is the wrong thing to do, I suppose. But it has become a habit… I was determined when I [came] in, the only thing they weren't going to get [was] my individuality. Turns out, they've got that too. (Wilfred)

Residents who turned away from life in care tended to focus on losses rather than potential gains. Resentment and resistance could obscure the opportunities that those turning towards care recognised. Moreover, such a position ignored the near inevitability of future declines. While both groups lived with loss and would encounter more, one group lived alongside them, moving with the current, while the other fought against them. Turning away risked a constant returning to loss(es)—the result of declining opportunities to participate, for teamwork, and shared responsibility—further fueling resentments, resistance and disengagement.

Summary of Adapting to a Shrunken World

Losses in the outside world triggered transitions to care, initiating the process of adapting to a shrunken world. In the physical or external sense, care needs were realised at the time of a move into a RACH. However, full realisation required individuals to personally acknowledge and accept their need. Participants who embraced this and turned towards life in care exhibited greater willingness to relinquish degrees of independence, control and personal responsibility, recognising such concessions as integral to the caregiving process. In doing so, they managed the physical and emotional consequences of ongoing losses better. Turning towards life in care helped these individuals counter the detrimental ramifications of shrinking worlds and adapt positively. Despite the inevitability of future declines, positive adaptation helped ensure shrunken worlds need not mean unhappy or unfulfilling ones:

Here, I'm having a life again. A different life, certainly, but a life. And I enjoy [it] and I'm grateful. (Erin)

The difference between this approach and one representing turning away from life in care was stark, exemplified by Wilfred's experience. Except for small acts of kindness by one staff member temporarily relieving boredom, he felt nothing positive about life in care:

Only one thing I could honestly say about this place, in all truth, and you can't do it: get me the f#@ck out of here! The last 4 years that I've been here, I haven't disliked the place. Honestly. I have bloody hated it. I can feel, see, feel, the walls closing in on me. (Wilfred)

The likelihood of successive losses means that adapting to a shrunken world is an ongoing process. This creates opportunities for individuals to reinforce or weaken their orientation over time in response to interactions (with staff and others) or losses. Some residents identified current abilities they would ‘hate to lose’, such as eyesight (Ava‐Rose) or memory (Erin). While the loss of such abilities could act as a catalyst for turning away from life in care, the opposite response is also plausible. This is illustrated by the positive effects of even small acts of kindness. One individual was encouraged to help with mail deliveries, giving them a valued role. Another staff member supplied DVDs from their personal collection to a disgruntled resident, resulting in that individual feeling as though ‘all [his Christmases] had come at once’ (Wilfred). The glimmer of positivity in this otherwise negative description suggests that softening of resistance is possible, if not complete reversal. Accordingly, how individuals choose to face life in care is conceived as ultimately within their control, and the capacity for change is ever present.

Discussion

This study explored residents' experiences living within RACHs in Victoria, Australia. What is required for positive adaptation to life in care has been presented through the grounded theory and visual model of adapting to a shrunken world. Venturing beyond initial transition periods, this theory contributes to knowledge of what it means to live in RACHs. Being grounded in residents' own experiences, findings draw upon values and actions of those whom systems are meant to serve, but whose voices and interests are often overlooked (Martinson and Berridge 2015). The main findings are that adaptation to life in a RACH is both active and ongoing, requiring continual adjustment by residents and staff; that residents' acceptance of care needs is critical for a positive adaptation, as loss of independence and control can otherwise lead to resistance and resentment; and that routinization of daily activities may have an homogenising effects on life in care, reducing person‐centredness. These findings have practical implications for the delivery of residential aged care in facilities around the world.

Adaptation as an Active Process

Adapting to a shrunken world in a RACH is an active process, heavily influenced by how residents engage with the RACH environment. Positive adaptation requires residents to engage with care needs, turning towards life as a satisfactory remedy to declining health and increasing care needs. Active engagement in meaningful activities has been linked to residents' ability to establish connections, foster relationships, and create a sense of belonging and purpose, enhancing wellbeing (Brownie et al. 2014; Fitzpatrick and Tzouvara 2019). Residents' active engagement in care routines maximises choice and control over daily lives while helping maintain independence and autonomy (Davison et al. 2019; Vaismoradi et al. 2016). Participants emphasised the importance of activities for positive adaptation and expressed desires to make the most of remaining time. Adaptation should be seen as an interactive process, where staff have power to promote independence by involving residents, despite tasks potentially taking longer. These findings have international relevance as aged care systems globally move towards person‐centred models that prioritise resident autonomy.

Adaptation as an Ongoing Process

Adapting to a shrunken world is an ongoing process requiring continual adjustment over time by both residents and staff. However, research regarding life in a RACH has often focused on short‐term timeframes, impeding development of coherent concepts of adjustment (Lee et al. 2002). Of 53 studies in two systematic reviews on transitions, only 13 reported participants' length of stay exceeding 18 months (Brownie et al. 2014; Fitzpatrick and Tzouvara 2019). In Australia, the median length of stay in a RACH is 22 months (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare 2023b), while the average length of stay of participants in this study was just over 3 years (37.8 months), ranging from 2 weeks to 10 years (median 29 months).

Studies with shorter observational periods and studies with a transition‐focused perspective have often conceptualised adjustment as a largely time‐limited process culminating in acceptance (Brandburg 2007; O'Neill et al. 2022; Sun et al. 2021; Wilson 1997). For example, O'Neill et al. (2022) focus explicitly on early post‐placement experiences rather than seeking to theorise adaptation across the full duration of residents' stays. In summarising various transition theories, they draw on definitions that treat adaptation as the endpoint of transition. Such formulations implicitly position adaptation as an endpoint of transition and may obscure the ongoing, iterative nature of adaptation across a resident's time in care.

Adapting to a shrunken world is a dynamic, ongoing process throughout a resident's life in care, in which residents respond to continual interactions with staff and fellow residents, as well as ongoing losses (Brandburg 2007; Wilson 1997). The present findings suggest that acceptance does not signal closure of adaptation but coexists with and informs a person's continuing adaptation in response to ongoing change.

Losses acting as push factors for participants entering a RACH included failing health or the death of partners. These same losses, along with loss of social connections, place, privacy, possessions, independence, autonomy, personal responsibility and sense of purpose, are well documented in literature on ageing and residential care internationally (Brownie et al. 2014; Costlow and Parmelee 2020; Davison et al. 2019). Given that maintaining independence and control are considered essential to identity (Cleland et al. 2021), any perceived or actual losses of agency can challenge a resident's sense of self (Paddock et al. 2018; Sullivan and Williams 2017). These findings underscore the crucial role of ongoing losses in the theory of Adapting to a shrunken world. In response to these losses, residents may alter views or preferences regarding care. Staff and facilities must be flexible enough to accommodate changes in a resident's preferences and abilities over time. Staff must remain sensitive to the potential impact of losses on a resident's sense of self and be proactive in providing or organising support where needed to reduce the risk of depression developing.

The Critical Role of Acceptance

How participants approached their need for care was directly linked to their acceptance of losses. Other studies have highlighted the critical role of acceptance for successful transitions, finding that acceptance enhanced residents' engagement in decisions to move, fostered proactive decision‐making, and contributed to smooth, positive transitions (Larkey et al. 2024; Polacsek and Woolford 2022). Accepting trade‐offs (e.g., of independence and control) as part of receiving care is critical for positive adaptation (Walker and Paliadelis 2016). Where acceptance is absent or insufficient, this can lead to residents' continuous maladaptation (Chenitz 1983), resulting in either resigned resistance—leading to withdrawal or helplessness—or aggressive behaviours, both of which were encountered in this study. However, acceptance has been presented in the literature as an outcome of transition processes, occurring between 6‐ and 12‐month post‐admission and representing an end to transition phases (Brandburg 2007). Such conceptualizations overlook that acceptance plays a role from the outset, that adaptation is ongoing, and that some residents proactively intend to adapt positively. In this study, acceptance was not an endpoint but rather a foundation for continued positive adaptation. As such, interventions and practices aimed at fostering acceptance in RACH settings should be explored in future research.

The Routinization of Life

Routinization of daily activities was described by some participants as having an homogenising effects on life in care. Staff routines include waking residents, assisting with dressing and toileting, mealtimes, medication rounds and formal activities. While daily routines are sources of safety and stability appreciated by some residents (Walker et al. 2007), such routines cannot or do not always provide flexibility required to cater for individual preferences. Both situations were evident among participants. Ingrained routines and professionalisation of interactions can disregard residents' everyday choices regarding basic activities, such as selecting clothing, meals, or activities, thereby limiting expression of choice and undermining spontaneity (Mondaca et al. 2020). Rigid routines and standardised caring systems can restrict independence and autonomy (Paddock et al. 2018) and have been suggested as risk factors for loneliness and boredom in RACHs (Panthi 2022). Loneliness and boredom can result from lack of meaningful social and personal activity and have been linked with negative physical and mental health outcomes, such as cognitive decline, frailty, depressive symptoms and dementia (Baumeister et al. 2013; Gonyea et al. 2018; Wright‐St Clair et al. 2017). However, flexibility in daily routines can minimise the negative effects of a structured life in care (Heid et al. 2020; Polacsek and Woolford 2022; Roberts et al. 2018). As such, flexible routines should be seen as promoting health and wellbeing rather than viewed myopically in the context of reducing service efficiency alone, and care providers should be skilled in identifying indicators of boredom to facilitate early intervention. These findings have relevance for aged care systems internationally as they balance efficiency with person‐centred approaches.

Strengths and Limitations

The limitations of this study include COVID‐related difficulties in accessing RACHs, which required initial interviews to be conducted via an internet platform using laptops, potentially excluding residents uncomfortable with technology. Residents identified as turning away from life in care were far fewer than those turning towards life in care, possibly because less‐positive residents opted not to participate. Nevertheless, the low number of negative views was not considered a major limitation, and the process of turning away was deemed adequately explored.

Only one participant was from a culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) background. Residents from CALD backgrounds may experience care differently or face additional challenges not captured here, but the theory is considered sufficiently flexible to accommodate such differences.

None of the participating residents presented with noticeable cognitive impairment and none of the staff highlighted this issue. As such, the experiences of people living with cognitive impairment were not explicitly addressed in this study. Given the distinct cognitive, communicative and relational changes associated with dementia, particularly in later stages, this population may require a study specifically designed for their circumstances. Whether and how cognitive impairment would modify the grounded theory developed here remains an empirical question requiring focused investigation.

Staff perspectives were limited to lifestyle coordinators and one senior manager. These interviews, conducted in the latter half of the study, largely reinforced existing categories.

Conclusion

This study used a grounded theory design to understand how residents in RACHs adapted to living in care. The theory of Adapting to a shrunken world was developed to explain this phenomenon and was presented alongside a visual model of the process. The findings from this study demonstrate that adapting to a RACH is a complex and dynamic process. Active and ongoing engagement in this process is necessary for both those making the move and those supporting them. The findings here challenge aspects of previous research into transitions and adjustments to life in RACHs by asserting that recognition and acceptance of circumstances play a critical role from the outset of the adaptation process—a process shaped by losses and continuing throughout a person's life in care.
