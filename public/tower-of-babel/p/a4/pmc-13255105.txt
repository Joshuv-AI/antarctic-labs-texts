Patient-Specific 3D-Printed Reference Rod for “Smiley Face Rod” Repair of Lumbar Spondylolysis in a Patient with High Body Mass Index
Authors: Takayuki Kitahara, Masatoshi Morimoto, Naoto Ono, Takafumi Ohshima, Yuji Nagao, Saori Soeda, Daiki Nakajima, Makoto Takeuchi, Hiroshi Kageyama, Junzo Fujitani, Hiroaki Manabe, Fumitake Tezuka, Koichi Sairyo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction The “Smiley Face Rod” (SFR) method is an effective direct repair technique for lumbar spondylolysis. However, three-dimensional rod contouring is technically demanding, particularly in patients with a high body mass index (BMI), where deep soft tissue obscures anatomical landmarks. Technical Note We report a technique using a patient-specific three-dimensional (3D)-printed model to create a sterilized “Reference Rod” template. A 27-year-old male with bilateral L5 spondylolysis and severe obesity (BMI 39.8 kg/m2) underwent SFR repair. A 3D model was printed from preoperative computed tomography data. A malleable template rod was contoured on this model to perfectly fit the lamina and spinous process and then sterilized. Intraoperatively, the final titanium rod was bent to match this sterile Reference Rod. This allowed for a “one-pass” insertion through the interspinous ligament with optimal bony contact, eliminating intraoperative trial-and-error. Conclusions The 3D-printed Reference Rod technique is a feasible approach that may facilitate rod contouring for SFR in challenging high-BMI cases by providing a tangible template to address the visualization and geometric limitations of conventional fluoroscopy.

Introduction

Lumbar spondylolysis is a fatigue fracture of the pars interarticularis that often affects young, active individuals. For symptomatic cases refractory to conservative management, direct repair of the pars defect is preferred over intersegmental fusion to preserve the motion of the functional spinal unit and prevent adjacent segment disease1). Among various repair techniques, the “Smiley Face Rod” (SFR) method, utilizing a U-shaped rod connected to pedicle screws beneath the spinous process2), has demonstrated favorable clinical outcomes and high fusion rates3-6).

However, the SFR technique presents a unique technical challenge: the rod must be contoured three-dimensionally to fit the specific laminar anatomy and apply a compressive vector perpendicular to the pars defect. This contouring is particularly difficult in patients with a high body mass index (BMI), in whom the deep surgical field and thick soft tissue envelope obscure direct visualization. In such cases, relying solely on two-dimensional intraoperative fluoroscopy can lead to repeated trial-and-error bending, prolonged operative time, and increased radiation exposure. While navigation systems can assist in screw placement7), they do not physically guide the rod contouring process.

Three-dimensional (3D) printing technology has gained traction in spine surgery for preoperative planning and for creating patient-specific guides8). Although 3D-printed templates have been used for screw trajectory planning, their application for creating a sterile “Reference Rod” to facilitate in situ rod contouring for SFR has not been widely reported.

In this technical note, we describe a novel workflow using a patient-specific 3D-printed model to create a sterilized Reference Rod template. This technique serves as a useful adjunctive tool to facilitate rod fitting, as demonstrated in a challenging case of a patient with severe obesity.

Technical Note

Patient presentation

A 27-year-old male presented with chronic low back pain and numbness in the lateral aspect of both thighs (Right>Left). He had been diagnosed with lumbar spondylolysis at age eight. Conservative treatments, including pars defect blocks, provided only temporary relief. Physical examination revealed a height of 163.9 cm and a weight of 106.8 kg, resulting in a BMI of 39.8 kg/m2. Preoperative radiographs showed bilateral L5 spondylolysis (Figure 1A and B). Computed tomography (CT) scans clearly demonstrated the bilateral pars defects (Figure 1C-E). Magnetic Resonance Imaging indicated no high signal intensity on Short Tau Inversion Recovery sequences at the pars defect, suggesting a pseudoarthrotic state (Figure 1E). Given the patient's refractory symptoms, direct repair using the SFR technique was planned.

Preoperative planning and Reference Rod preparation

Preoperative CT scans were obtained. The Digital Imaging and Communications in Medicine data were used to generate a 3D surface model of the L5 vertebra. A life-sized 3D anatomical model was printed using a standard desktop 3D printer (AnkerMake M5, Anker Innovations, Changsha, China) with polylactic acid (PLA) filament (AnkerMake PLA, Anker Innovations) (Figure 2A). The time required for segmentation and 3D modeling was approximately 30 minutes, and the printing time was approximately two hours. The material cost for the PLA filament was negligible, estimated at less than 1.00 US dollar.

Reference rod preparation and usage. (A) Manual contouring of the template rod on the 3D-printed model. (B) Intraoperative contouring of the final titanium rod to match the sterilized Reference Rod. (C) Final titanium rod (bottom) contoured to match the reference rod (top).

3D: three-dimensional

In a non-sterile setting prior to surgery, a malleable aluminum template rod was manually contoured on the 3D model. The rod was carefully bent to form the ideal “Smiley Face” U-shape, ensuring it:

1. Fit snugly against the caudal aspect of the L5 spinous process and the dorsal surface of the lamina.

2. Aligned perfectly with the planned trajectory of the pedicle screws (Figure 2B).

This pre-contoured rod, designated as the “Reference Rod,” was then sterilized via standard autoclaving protocols for use as a master template in the sterile field.

Surgical procedure

The patient was positioned prone. A hybrid approach was utilized to minimize soft tissue trauma while accommodating the patient's body habitus. A 5 cm midline incision was made to expose the L4-S1 spinous processes, and bilateral paramedian skin incisions (2 cm on the right, 3 cm on the left) were made approximately 2 cm lateral to the pedicle projection for percutaneous pedicle screw insertion. The L4 inferior articular process was partially resected to expose the pars defect, which was then debrided and decorticated using a 4 mm air-tome. Bilateral pedicle screws (7.0 mm diameter, 40 mm length) were inserted percutaneously under fluoroscopic guidance using the Weinstein method9).

The sterilized Reference Rod was introduced into the sterile field. Using this Reference Rod as a guide, the final titanium rod (5.5 mm diameter, 11 cm length) was contoured to match the Reference Rod (Figure 2B and C). The contoured titanium rod was inserted from the left paramedian incision, passed subcutaneously through a tunnel created in the L5/S1 interspinous ligament, and guided to the right pedicle screw head. The rod achieved an optimal fit on the first attempt (“one-pass”), perfectly conforming to the dorsal aspect of the lamina without impingement (Figure 3A-C). No additional intraoperative bending was required. Compression was applied to close the pars defects, and set screws were tightened. Local bone graft harvested from the iliac crest was placed at the defect sites. The total operative time was 141 minutes, and the estimated blood loss was 35 mL.

Postoperative course

Postoperative radiographs and CT demonstrated optimal rod positioning. The pars defects were successfully reduced and closed, and vertebral alignment was maintained without any slippage or screw loosening (Figure 4A-B). The patient reported significant relief, with low back pain reducing to 10%-20% of the preoperative level immediately after surgery. At the three-month follow-up, the patient was doing well with no implant failure or loss of reduction observed on plain radiographs. Clinical outcomes showed significant improvement: the Low Back Pain Visual Analog Scale decreased from 84 mm to 6 mm, and the Oswestry Disability Index improved from 68% to 8%. He has returned to his daily activities without requiring analgesic medication.

Discussion

We successfully performed SFR repair in a patient with severe obesity (BMI 39.8 kg/m2) using a 3D-printed Reference Rod technique. The deep surgical field in obese patients often makes it difficult to assess the 3D laminar anatomy and contour the rod appropriately. An ill-fitting rod can lead to insufficient compression, implant prominence, or prolonged operative time due to repeated adjustments.

Our technique offers distinct advantages by overcoming the geometric limitations of conventional fluoroscopic estimation. A common intraoperative method involves placing a template rod on the patient's skin to estimate the required shape. However, this approach is fundamentally limited by the magnification mismatch caused by the X-ray beam divergence. In a standard C-arm setup (X-ray source below the table), the spine is closer to the source and is magnified more than objects at the skin level (Figure 5A). This discrepancy is exacerbated in patients with high BMI, where the soft tissue envelope is thick (Figure 5B).

Schematic of fluoroscopic magnification discrepancy. (A) Standard BMI: Minimal skin-to-spine distance results in negligible magnification difference. (B) High BMI: Thick soft tissue increases the distance, causing a significant magnification mismatch due to X-ray beam divergence.

BMI: body mass index

If a surgeon attempts to visually match the rod's shadow on the skin to the spine on the monitor, they may inadvertently contour a rod that is physically larger than necessary, leading to implant prominence. Moreover, fluoroscopy only provides a two-dimensional silhouette; it fails to guide the 3D sagittal and axial bending required for a snug laminar fit. Our technique resolves these issues by enabling direct physical contouring on the 3D model. This ensures the rod perfectly matches the patient's specific multi-planar anatomy, regardless of soft tissue thickness or fluoroscopic parallax.

Additionally, our workflow ensures safety and efficiency in sterilization. While some previous reports describe sterilizing the 3D model itself, standard 3D printing materials (e.g., PLA) are heat-sensitive and may deform under autoclave sterilization. Our technique involves sterilizing only the metal Reference Rod. This allows for standard autoclaving, ensuring sterility while avoiding the logistical challenge of bringing a bulky or non-heat-resistant 3D model into the sterile field. Preoperative 3D planning has been reported to achieve high accuracy in instrumentation even without intraoperative navigation10). In this case, the use of the Reference Rod allowed for “one-pass” insertion. Although a comparative study is necessary to draw definitive conclusions, this streamlined workflow suggests a potential for reducing operative time and lower radiation exposure, consistent with findings by Öztürk et al.8), who reported that 3D modeling reduces instrumentation time in trauma cases.

We acknowledge several limitations and potential pitfalls of this study and technique. First, the follow-up period in this case was limited to three months. While early clinical outcomes were favorable and implant position was maintained, this duration is insufficient to assess bony fusion or long-term implant stability. Long-term follow-up is required to validate the ultimate efficacy of this technique. Second, regarding indications and anatomical variations, this method is most beneficial for patients with high BMI or complex anatomy where visualization is compromised. However, surgical difficulty may increase at higher vertebral levels (e.g., L4). As the interpedicular distance narrows cranially, the rod requires a more acute curvature, making passage through the interspinous ligament more challenging. In such cases, surgeons should strive for a more lateral entry point for pedicle screws to maximize the available space and facilitate rod placement. Fortunately, since lumbar spondylolysis predominantly affects the L5 level, this technique remains applicable to most patients. In standard cases with good visualization, conventional techniques may suffice. Third, the workflow requires preoperative preparation time for segmentation and printing, making it unsuitable for emergency surgeries. Finally, preoperative CT scans must be of sufficient quality; significant metal artifacts from previous implants could compromise the accuracy of the 3D model, and access to 3D printing technology is a prerequisite.

The use of a patient-specific 3D-printed Reference Rod demonstrated feasibility and potential utility in facilitating rod contouring for SFR repair. This technique is particularly valuable in patients with high BMI where anatomical landmarks are obscured, converting a challenging contouring step into a reproducible procedure.
