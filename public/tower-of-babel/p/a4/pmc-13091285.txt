“I make my own decisions”: Views on precision medicine research participation decisions from adults with intellectual disability
Authors: Katherine E McDonald, Eulena Banzer, Mark Cooley, Rijul Singhal, Tawara D Goode, Krzysztof Kiryluk, Maya Sabatello, The Project ENGAGE Consortium
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAutonomy is a leading indicator of well-being and a cornerstone principal of research ethics. Yet adults with intellectual disability are routinely denied autonomy in research settings, affecting participation and generalizability. To inform health research policy and practice, we conducted a national survey of 150 adults with intellectual disability to explore their views on autonomy and decision-making in precision medicine research (PMR), a leading area of health research. Participants expressed strong interest in participating in PMR and controlling their participation decisions, sometimes with support. Findings highlight the need for policies and practices that honor these interests and facilitate autonomy. Over time, such efforts can improve health outcomes for this underserved population.Keywords: precision medicine research, consent to research, intellectual disability, ethical implications, legal implications, social implications, autonomy, decision-making, research ethics, responsible inclusion, research policy

Introduction

Autonomy—the ability and opportunity to direct one’s life—is a leading indicator of well-being and critical to thriving.1,2 Given its standing as fundamental to human rights and self-determination,3 autonomy is a cornerstone principle in research ethics and regulations.4 Aligned with international principles, US federal regulations emphasize that individuals must be treated as autonomous agents capable of considering personal goals, evaluating risks and benefits, and acting accordingly. Expectations that researchers provide reasonable, understandable consent disclosures emerge from this emphasis. Concurrently, researchers have a responsibility to ensure that those with diminished autonomy (or decreased self-determination) are protected and not unknowingly or unwillingly enrolled; diminished autonomy does not necessarily result in an inability to make decisions.4,5

These commitments to autonomy and protections have long been in tension for groups with questionable consent capacity (i.e., those embedded in social contexts or experiencing cognitive impairments that could impede consent capacity).5,6 Research with adults with intellectual disability exemplifies this strain.7,8 As adults with intellectual disability experience limitations in intellectual functioning and adaptative behavior,9 they are often assumed to lack the ability to act autonomously—commonly, without study-specific determinations of consent capacity—and are denied the right to make decisions.10 In research, efforts to protect them have resulted in their widespread exclusion or overreliance on surrogate consent.8,11,12 Both practices limit autonomy, even as these practices may be appropriate in some studies or cases.

Adults with intellectual disability experience significant health disparities8,13,14,15 and can benefit from scientific discoveries. Fostering health equity requires their inclusion in research, including the reversal of exclusion and overreliance on surrogate consent as appropriate protections and instead framing responsible inclusion as a right and a necessary step to improve health. As with other communities underrepresented in research, efforts to enhance inclusion by considering community views are valuable.16,17,18,19

Emerging evidence indicates strong interest among adults with intellectual disability to be in social-behavioral research,20,21,22,23,24,25 to receive disability accommodations, and to control participation decisions.26 Advances in upholding rights to autonomy for persons with disabilities are accelerating, including by recognizing the value of taking risks27 and minimizing reliance on restrictions such as guardianship (which can yield surrogate consent) writ large or by inaccurate assumptions that guardianship orders remove all decision-making rights from the individual.3,28,29 These advances highlight the moral requirement to transform research practices, including via an emphasis on responsible inclusion and strategies to enhance autonomy via universal design and disability accommodations. One relevant accommodation already commonly practiced in healthcare12 is supported decision-making. Using supported decision-making, an individual selects someone of their choosing to help them understand information and communicate their decisions30; importantly, the individual remains the decision-maker.31

Precision medicine has potential to transform health by developing individually tailored prevention and treatment that consider biological, environmental, and lifestyle factors.32 As a leading area of clinical research, precision medicine’s emphasis on patient empowerment resonates with principles of autonomy; precision medicine’s precursor—precision medicine research (PMR)—requires diverse cohorts for findings to emerge and calls for broad inclusion.33 Adults with intellectual disability may benefit from insights derived from PMR given their high rates of health conditions, some of which may have genetic underpinnings, as well as from emerging knowledge relating to how genetic makeup may affect responses to treatments.34,35 Prior research found that adults with intellectual disability express high support for, and interest in participating in PMR, including desire to receive results from such studies36 and to be treated with respect37 by disability competent researchers.37 Yet these studies are grappling with whether and how to include those with questionable consent capacity.38,39 PMR studies that enroll participants from the general public often prioritize safeguards over autonomy, resulting in a failure to provide accommodations to facilitate first-person consent, the exclusion of those lacking consent capacity, and, consequently, the underrepresentation of adults with intellectual disability. For example, while the All of Us research program has successfully enrolled participants from diverse backgrounds,39 adults with intellectual disability are not yet intentionally enrolled into the study, including because some may require surrogate consent to participate.

New insight into the views of adults with intellectual disability can shed light on how to ethically navigate long-standing tensions between autonomy and protections, facilitate responsiveness to community views, and enhance cohort diversity. For this purpose, we studied preferences among adults with intellectual disability in making PMR participation decisions and factors related to research decisions. We predicted that adults with intellectual disability would show a strong preference for autonomy, often with support, and that this preference will be particularly strong among those not subject to guardianship, who are older, are involved in disability rights or have living arrangements associated with greater self-determination. While we anticipated gender and racial/ethnic identification to influence views, we did not have specific hypotheses.

Subjects and methods

Project ENGAGE is an academic-community research partnership of people with different personal and professional expertise working on the responsible inclusion of adults with intellectual disability in PMR. Our multi-partner Steering Committee of five adults with intellectual disability, a family member, three support providers, and five experts in genetics, genetic research, and decision-making worked together to design and conduct a national survey of adults with intellectual disability; Steering Committee members co-authored this manuscript.

Subjects

We invited adults 18 years of age or older who self-identified as having an intellectual disability with and without guardians, living in the US, and who were able to understand basic information about the study, make a participation decision, and express their opinions with or without accommodations.

Survey

We developed a survey of adapted and de novo items based on existing measures, scholarship,40 and decisions with the Steering Committee to enhance relevance, comprehensiveness, and disability-accessibility.41,42 We confirmed content validity and understandability of the survey with cognitive interviews (n = 6), iteratively making refinements with the Steering Committee and additional cognitive interviews until we achieved data saturation.43 Here, we used survey items on participation decisions (e.g., usual decision-making practices decisions; preferences on receipt of research invitation, participation discussions, participation decision-making; and open-ended questions about decisions) and prior experiences and social background. The survey is available by request to the corresponding authors.

Procedures

We received IRB approvals from our universities. With the Steering Committee, we created all study materials and procedures and recruited nationally via networks and organizations by and for people with intellectual disability (e.g., self-advocacy groups, social service organizations, and inclusive postsecondary education programs) via word-of-mouth, listserv and online announcements, and in-person and online events. We implemented measures to prevent and detect fraudulent responses, taking care to not introduce access barriers. We collected data from June, 2023, to January, 2024, inviting individuals to complete the survey as they desired: online (53%), via videoconference (22%), in person (19%) or over the telephone with research staff (4%), or on paper (1%); across all modalities, 76% participated with support (most notable, having survey questions read aloud, having another person record their responses, or having a question rephrased for great clarity); we did not find differences in survey responses by participation modality or support. Participants answered screening questions to determine eligibility and provided consent or assent. Participants could take breaks and return to the survey and received a $50 gift card.

Analysis

After removing fraudulent responses via case-by-case examination, we recoded key variables to consolidate into larger categories for more power. We refactored participants’ race into four categories (i.e., Black, Hispanic/Latinx, White, and Other) due to power analysis limitations, participants’ age (under or above 25 years), and living arrangement into three groups. We performed omnibus analyses on several key autonomy-focused questions treated as outcome variables and built linear regression models with demographic and behavioral questions as predictor variables. This allowed for the evaluation of each predictor variable’s significance controlled for the effect of all other predictors in the model. Within each analysis question, participants who responded “I do not know” or “I do not wish to say” were considered as missing data and excluded. We built regression models using the survey package44 in R version 4.4.245 with a quasibinomial distribution for binary outcomes, and an ordered logistic regression for ordinal/categorical outcome variables. We performed significance testing using Wald-type tests in the regTermTest function. For outcome variables analyzed across multiple predictors, we applied FDR correction across significance tests for all predictor variables included. We report p values as FDR-corrected unless otherwise specified. We thematically coded open-ended data, using inductive codes and multiple coders to bolster dependability.46 The Steering Committee provided ideas for analyses, reviewed findings, and discussed interpretation of findings.

Sample

We enrolled 150 adults with intellectual disability from 27 US states, of whom 104 were not subject to guardianship. Most received developmental disabilities services (n = 121) and had either earned a high school diploma/GED (n = 101) or attended but did not complete high school (n = 22); about one-third were employed part- or full-time, and three-quarters were involved in disability rights or advocacy (n = 113).

Interest in PMR

Participants expressed strong interest in participating in PMR (83%); nearly all participants not subject to guardianship (n = 100) were either neutral (n = 13) or interested (n = 83) in being in a PMR study (only n = 1 disagreed). We did not observe differences in level of interest by race/ethnicity, gender, age, involvement in disability advocacy, guardianship status, or residence type (omnibus test across predictors was non-significant: p = 0.164, FDR p = 0.422).

Open-ended responses confirm a strong desire to be included and add insight into motivations. Motivations described desire to better understand self, learn about risks for family members including future children, preparing for future health needs, and opportunities to be included and help others:

It is very important to be in research because not many people with disability are in research.

I am grateful for the chance to do this, because we are not always “allowed” to do this type of thing.

I think it will help people learn about me and people who have disability.

Finding out about and making decisions about being in PMR

When asked to select three of the people they would most want to tell them about an opportunity to be in a PMR study, participants noted strong preferences for learning about the invitation from a family member (51%), someone who supports them (41%), their healthcare provider (40%), and/or the researcher (37%), with similar patterns for preferences across guardianship status (p = 0.45, FDR p = 0.59), except that those subject to guardianship also included their guardians (54%).

When asked how they want to make decisions about being in PMR, participants expressed equal preference for making the decision themselves (54% not subject to guardianship; 28% subject to guardianship) or with help from someone they choose (46% not subject to guardianship; 30.6% subject to guardianship). Among those subject to guardianship (an individual typically not chosen by them), 36% wanted to decide with their guardian and 6% wanted their guardian to decide for them.

We evaluated several variables for association with responses to how they want to make decisions, including demographic variables (age, gender identity, race/ethnicity, residence type, and involvement in advocacy), as well as questions about general and healthcare decision-making (a composite of four questions about general decision-making, confidence in ability to ask for help to take care of their health, confidence in being able to take part in healthcare decisions, and trust in health researchers).

Among participants not subject to guardianship, participants with higher confidence in healthcare decision-making were more likely to want to decide on their own (p < 0.0001). The effect of confidence in healthcare decision-making was significantly modulated by race/ethnicity (p < 0.0001) and gender identity (p < 0.0001). The signal is strongest in participants self-reporting being “Black” or “Other,” and in female participants compared with male participants, suggesting that healthcare decision-making confidence is more strongly associated with desire to make one’s own decisions among these groups. Those with higher confidence in asking for help with their healthcare needs were similarly more likely to want to decide on their own (p < 0.0001).

Among participants subject to guardianship, two variables were associated with how they wanted to make decisions about being in PMR: the composite self-determination score (p = 0.021) and residence type (p < 0.0001). Participants with a higher self-determination score were more likely to want to decide on their own or jointly with their guardian or someone they choose. Those who live with family were more likely to want their legal guardian to decide for them. The effect of the composite score was found to be significantly modulated by participants’ race/ethnicity in an interaction model; specifically: Black participants report a stronger effect from self-determination (p = 0.037).

Open-ended responses confirmed interest in having control over decisions, and extended to note interests in receiving information that is understandable, and having the opportunity to ask questions and the time necessary to make a decision:

A clear idea about why the study is being done and have a clear idea about who is doing the study.

For the researchers to explain things clearly and to have a lot of thinking and listening before I decide and that it is my decision. I do not want to be rushed.

Open-ended responses also shed light on age-related differences:

I started making my own decisions around 18 and it was difficult at first but then I got better and more confident at making my own decisions.

When asked who they would want to talk to about being in PMR, most participants who answered this question (n = 134) wanted to talk with someone of their choosing (58%), followed by their healthcare provider (53%), and/or a healthcare provider who can explain genetic information (35%). Only 11% of participants did not want to talk to someone, and only 1% wanted to talk to the researcher. The prevalence of preferences for whom to talk to about being in PMR was not significantly associated with age (25 or younger vs. above 25) or guardianship status.

Relationships between finding out and making decisions about being in PMR

Interest in being in PMR was significantly associated with responses for how they want to make decisions to be in PMR, in both those with and without guardians; stronger interest in being in PMR is associated with stronger interest in wanting to decide on their own (respectively, p < 0.0001 and p < 0.002).

Looking across different decision-making contexts (i.e., approaches for important daily life decisions generally compared with preferences for decisions about being in PMR), participants demonstrated a fairly even split in whether their practices and preferences were the same (n = 63) or different (n = 65). Compared with those who expressed inconsistency in preferences, individuals with consistent preferences were more likely to report confidence in taking part in healthcare decisions (p < 0.0001), ability to follow-up after visits (p < 0.0001), finding reliable information about their health (p = 0.002), and self-advocacy during medical visits (p < 0.0001). Participants’ residency type, financial hardship, race/ethnicity, gender, and age were not significantly associated with consistency between these preferences.

Discussion

Recognizing the foundational role that representation in research plays in fostering improved health knowledge for everyone, efforts are underway to enhance inclusion of understudied populations. In part, successes in these endeavors are enabled by efforts to (re)build trust in research and responsiveness to public views. Such efforts are more nascent for adults with intellectual disability. Following legal and social shifts toward greater recognition of the value of inclusion and rights,28,47 the responsible inclusion of adults with intellectual disability requires reform from exclusion and overreliance on proxy consent to strategies that facilitate autonomy via inclusion and first-person consent when appropriate. Our national survey with adults with intellectual disability on making decisions in PMR studies provides a glimpse into how these broader shifts may impact research participation.

Not surprisingly, we identified strong interest in being a part of PMR studies and desire to control participation decisions, sometimes with support.30 These preferences for agency and support persisted across guardianship status and align with those of other populations. Of note, these preferences did not vary by other participant demographics, although higher confidence in healthcare decision-making was associated with greater interest in making independent decisions among participants reporting being Black or Other and among female participants. These findings are important. First, similar to findings from the general public,48 adults with intellectual disability uphold autonomy and the behaviors associated with it (e.g., expressing views, deciding for oneself, settings limits, and asserting one’s own goals) as important values, with research as an important context for the exercise of autonomy. Second, there is a need to create opportunities to include adults with intellectual disability in general population clinical research and identify ways to facilitate their autonomy and inclusion via first-person consent or when necessary, surrogate consent coupled with meaningful assent. This could include, for example, genuine efforts to inform the individual about the study and soliciting their preferences and allowing for dissent. This further requires consideration of cultural nuances that affect decision-making such as who decides and how (e.g., individually, with others),49 and it is pivotal in genomic research, as perceptions and value ascribed to genetics may differ.50 Related, since guardianship does not always translate into lack of ability—or legal power—to make research decisions,30 researchers need to carefully examine whether individuals are subject to guardianship orders that include the research decision. When they do not, valid consent capacity determinations calibrated to the complexity of the participation decision are essential autonomy-protecting safeguards. When they do, autonomy-protecting safeguards require ensuring meaningful assent and that guardians’ decisions are based on substituted judgment and guided by their knowledge of preferences of the person.

Second, bioethical discourse has historically been focused on rational decision-making made by individuals claiming their autonomy.51 While many of our participants endorsed this approach, a similarly strong preference for support in decision-making reflects the feminist idea of “ethics of care.”52,53 That is, in reality, individuals develop and make decisions within matrices of relationships and contextual responsibilities.52,54 This approach centers care and our inherent interdependence, where autonomy is supported by relationships and exercised in relations with others (“relational autonomy” or “autonomy with others”55,56). Reconceptualizing autonomy in this way is especially relevant for decisions about participation in PMR given the emphasis on empowerment and recognition of human agency in related decisions.32 It also befits common practices in genetic testing and research, specifically, the recommendation to consult family members and others before making a decision about participation in genomic research and return of genetic results, even as the informed consent form is signed by an individual.

Findings further suggest the need to plan for the involvement of another person to facilitate autonomous, first-person decision-making; for some adults with intellectual disability, they may be more likely to choose support when the decision is more complex or significant. Federal disability laws and research-related provisions in the Affordable Care Act highlight the importance of accessibility and reasonable accommodations. Providing adults with intellectual disability the opportunity of involving a supporter as they make their decision about research is aligned with legal requirements and research policies57,58 and could help maximize understanding and choice. Choice of support is especially important to safeguarding the autonomy of adults with intellectual disability as they may have people in their lives who do not believe in their abilities, seek to change their minds, or dissuade them from opportunities to take calculated risks.

Findings should be interpreted alongside limitations. We relied on a convenience sample of individuals who self-identified as having an intellectual disability, and participants likely had a favorable perspective of research, or at least research designed with adults with intellectual disability; it is possible participants have stronger views or behaviors linked to autonomy than others. Since there is no national surveillance,56 we cannot determine whether our sample reflected population characteristics. However, we enrolled a sample with gender balance, and of various ages, racial, and ethnic groups, and economic situations—sample characteristics often not achieved in samples of adults with disabilities.59 Importantly, we asked participants to imagine PMR decisions. It is possible that when presented with actual decisions, preferences may vary based on critical study characteristics such as their specific risks and benefits. Lastly, our focus did not include considerations of all research contexts.

Our findings add further evidence that adults with intellectual disability want to be included in a broad range of societal functions, and to exercise autonomy, with or without supports of their choice. Importantly, these preferences extend to decisions that may involve new and uncertain risks. Whereas exclusion or inclusion via surrogate consent has largely been viewed as appropriate, we increasingly understand the social and health risks of exclusion and unnecessarily restricting autonomy. Moreover, advances in how to enhance understanding (e.g., via cognitive accessibility, allowing time) and recognition that decision-making confidence and skills grow with experience can be leveraged. Adults with intellectual disability have the right to take risks,27,60 including by making choices that are novel, scary or uncertain—and measures to enhance such abilities should be viewed as part of researchers’ responsibility to create diverse cohorts. It is through these choices, and the subsequent opportunities to learn from them, that individuals can experience autonomy, a critical condition to enhancing well-being and thriving. Researchers should use these findings to inform their actions to presume competence, promote understanding, allow support, and facilitate first-person consent to the maximum extent possible.
