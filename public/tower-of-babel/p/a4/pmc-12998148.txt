Epigenetic biomarkers of progestin-resistance in endometriosis
Authors: E Cansu Cevik, Ramanaiah Mamillapalli, Giacomo Sferruzza, Padmavathi Mamillapalli, Hugh S Taylor
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveEndometriosis is a heterogeneous, estrogen-dependent inflammatory disorder that affects up to 15% of reproductive age women. Progestin-based therapies are the most commonly prescribed initial treatment; however, approximately one-third of patients exhibit progestin resistance, leading to inadequate symptom relief and discontinuation. Given the role of epigenetic dysregulation in endometriosis and its impact on hormonal responsiveness, we aimed to identify if circulating leukocyte DNA methylation signatures were associated with progestin treatment response and could serve as a non-invasive predictive biomarker.MethodsWe conducted a prospective cohort study of 31 women with surgically confirmed endometriosis, categorized as progestin responders (n = 10) or non-responders (n = 21) based on clinical outcomes. Buffy coat-derived leukocytes were processed for whole-genome methylation analysis using enzymatic methyl-seq. Differentially methylated CpG sites were identified using logistic regression, and candidate genes were subject to Receiver Operating Curve analysis. A stepwise logistic regression model was developed to identify the minimal methylation gene set predictive of treatment response. Internal validation included permutation and bootstrap testing.ResultsResponders and non-responders did not differ significantly in baseline demographics or clinical variables. There were 1,439 genes that were significantly differentially methylated between responders and non-responders. A three-gene methylation signature, MMP20, NRXN1, and RNA5-8SN5, distinguished responders from non-responders with high accuracy (ROC AUC = 0·952). Internal validation confirmed model robustness (bootstrap AUC = 0·907, 95% CI: 0·80–0·957; permutation p < 0·001).ConclusionCirculating leukocyte methylation profiles can serve as noninvasive biomarkers of progestin responsiveness in endometriosis. Our findings suggest that blood-based epigenetic profiling may inform personalized treatment decisions, avoiding prolonged treatment with ineffective therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40364-026-00907-1.Keywords: Endometriosis, Epigenetics, Methylation, Progesterone, Progesterone resistance, Treatment-resistant endometriosis

To the editor,

Endometriosis is a heterogeneous, estrogen-dependent disease affecting 10%–15% of reproductive age women and a leading cause of pelvic pain and infertility [1]. Although well-studied, its clinical heterogeneity and unpredictable response to hormonal therapy continue to be a challenge in patient care. Epigenetic dysregulation, across DNA methylation, histone modification, and non-coding RNAs has been repeatedly implicated in endometriosis biology and may underlie variability in lesion behavior and treatment outcomes [2–6]. The role of inflammation and immune dysfunction has become increasingly recognized as a defining feature of endometriosis. The disease is now understood as a chronic inflammatory disorder characterized by both local and systemic immune dysregulation [1]. Chronic inflammation associated with endometriosis is not limited to the pelvic environment, rather it exerts systemic effects, potentially leading to epigenetic reprogramming across multiple tissues, including immune cells [7]. Systemic immune abnormalities, involving altered leukocyte activation and cytotoxicity, further suggest a disease that extends beyond ectopic lesions and lacks reliable biomarkers to guide therapy [1, 7–9].

Progestins, as part of the oral contraceptives (OCs) are the standard first-line treatment, yet approximately one-third of patients fail to respond and 34% of women with endometriosis discontinue OCs due to mood lability or depressive side effects [1, 10–12]. Clinical decisions therefore rely on prolonged trial-and-error. Because impaired progesterone responsiveness is partly mediated through epigenetic alterations, including aberrant methylation of the progesterone receptor and downstream targets such as Homeobox protein A10 (Hoxa10), peripheral methylation signatures may serve as predictive biomarkers [1, 10, 13]. Importantly, unlike surgical lesion sampling, blood-based assays are minimally invasive, scalable, and also widely accessible [9, 14].

Predicting treatment response using peripheral epigenetic markers offers a novel opportunity to enable personalized therapy for endometriosis [14]. Therefore, we profiled genome-wide DNA methylation in circulating leukocytes from well-characterized endometriosis cohorts, aiming to predict progestin response. Baseline demographic and clinical features were comparable between responders and non-responders (Table 1). Differential methylation analysis identified 1,439 genes with significant between-group differences; the 25 most discriminating CpG sites showed clear separation in distribution (Fig. 1A and B). Neither menstrual phase (follicular vs. luteal) nor hormonal suppression status was associated with changes in methylation patterns. Each of three loci, MMP20, NRXN1, and RNA5-8SN5, displayed individual classification utility (AUC > 0.60) (Fig. 1B). A multivariable logistic model combining these three markers achieved excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.95) (Fig. 1C). Internal validation supported robustness, as null permutations produced much lower AUCs than the model and bootstrap resampling yielded a narrow AUC distribution centered on the original estimate, indicating minimal optimism (Supp. Figure 1, 2).

The use of this biomarker has the potential to guide treatment selection by identifying women who are likely to respond to progestin therapy. Progesterone resistance represents one component of a broader severe endometriosis phenotype, characterized by more aggressive disease biology, for which not all underlying molecular mechanisms have been fully elucidated [15]. Accordingly, rather than limiting our analysis to known progesterone signaling or inflammatory pathways, we employed an unbiased approach to identify markers with the strongest discriminatory power. Currently, therapeutic decisions often follow a trial-and-error model, cycling through agents such as progestins, combined OCs, danazol, and GnRH agonists or antagonists. During this process, women may continue to experience significant pain and other debilitating symptoms, leading to prolonged physical and emotional suffering, diminished quality of life, and frustration with care. Moreover, the delay in receiving an effective treatment, often compounded by diagnostic delays, contributes to increased healthcare utilization and costs [16]. Precision-based strategies, such as predictive epigenetic testing could improve therapeutic efficacy, minimize unnecessary interventions, and better align patient management with individual molecular profiles. Beyond optimizing treatment outcomes, such tools could also reduce the personal and clinical burden associated with ineffective therapies, supporting a more compassionate and efficient standard of care.

This study provides a novel epigenetic signature with promising implications for predicting treatment response in endometriosis. We controlled for key confounders and demonstrated robustness through permutation and bootstrap validation; however, external validation in independent and larger prospective cohorts is needed to confirm generalizability and to determine whether these signatures can predict treatment response prior to therapy. In future studies, we plan mechanistic follow-up analyses to link methylation changes to gene expression, identify immune cell–type specificity, and test functional relevance using targeted assays.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that DNA methylation profiles in circulating leukocytes can distinguish progestin responders from non-responders with high accuracy. The identification of a minimal three-gene methylation signature, MMP20, NRXN1, and RNA5-8SN5, with robust predictive performance highlights the potential for developing a clinically applicable, blood-based test to guide individualized treatment decisions in endometriosis. Unlike invasive tissue-based diagnostics, this approach offers a noninvasive, scalable, and rapid tool that could be implemented immediately to improve therapeutic outcomes and reduce unnecessary exposure to ineffective hormonal therapies.
