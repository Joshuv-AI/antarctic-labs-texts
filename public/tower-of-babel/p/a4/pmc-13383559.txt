Network analysis and directed acyclic graph of smartphone addiction and multiple mental health factors in youths
Authors: Minggang Zhang, Lili Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence indicates that smartphone addiction is associated with a series of psychological problems. However, there is still a lack of sufficient understanding regarding smartphone addiction that occurs in both negative and positive mental health conditions in youths. We aimed to map the network linking smartphone addiction (SMA) with negative (depression, anxiety, stress, loneliness) and positive (life satisfaction, social support, resilience, and self-efficacy) mental health factors in youths.MethodsIn a cross-sectional sample of 701 youths (aged 18.27 ± 1.57 years, 16–20 years old, 47.6% male, Chinese), participants completed validated self-report scales. A regularized Gaussian graphical model was estimated to identify conditional associations and central nodes. Bootstrapped exploratory graph analysis was used to detect communities, and a PC-algorithm-based directed acyclic graph (DAG) was used to explore potential directional associations.ResultsCompared with non-SMA individuals, youths with SMA reported higher depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness, and lower life satisfaction, social support, resilience, and self-efficacy (p < 0.05). The strongest edges were self-efficacy–resilience (weight = 0.47) and depression–anxiety (weight = 0.42), SMA was most strongly linked to stress (0.23). Anxiety (Expected Influence = 0.80) and depression (EI = 0.77) showed the highest centrality with good stability. The DAG suggested potential directional pathways from SMA to stress and depression, stress was statistically associated with subsequent nodes of depression and loneliness.ConclusionsSMA is embedded within a tightly connected cluster of negative mental health factors and diminished positive resources. Stress and depression appear to be key pathways from SMA to broader psychopathology, highlighting intervention targets (stress regulation and depression management) alongside strengthening mental health promotion in SMA youths in the future.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04824-8.Keywords: Smartphone addiction, Mental health, Youth, Network analysis, Directed acyclic graph

Background

The widespread adoption of smartphones has profoundly influenced the daily lives of adolescents and young adults. While smartphones provide undeniable benefits for communication, learning, and entertainment, their excessive and uncontrolled use has raised concerns regarding smartphone addiction. Recent evidence suggests that this phenomenon has become a pressing public health issue worldwide. A comprehensive meta-analysis covering more than two million individuals across 64 countries estimated the global prevalence of smartphone addiction at approximately 27% [1]. Another meta-analysis across 24 countries reported an estimated prevalence of problematic smartphone use of 23.3%, with substantial cross-national variation [2]. In China, where smartphone penetration is among the highest globally, according to the 56th “Statistical Report on the Development of China’s Internet” released by the China Internet Society (CNNIC) in July 2025, the number of mobile Internet users has reached 1.105 billion [3]. Recent studies among Chinese college students have reported relatively high rates of smartphone addiction/problematic smartphone use, with estimates varying across samples and measurement cutoffs [4, 5].

Accumulating evidence shows that smartphone addiction is associated with a wide range of psychological problems. Numerous studies have linked smartphone addiction with elevated levels of depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness in youths [6–10]. Smartphone addiction has also been found to undermine protective psychological resources. Individuals with higher levels of smartphone addiction often report lower self-efficacy, reduced life satisfaction, weaker social support, and diminished resilience [11–14]. This high prevalence among Chinese college students may be particularly linked to unique socio-cultural stressors, including intense academic competition, high familial expectations, and the crucial role of social media in peer interaction, which can foster the development of problematic smartphone use as a dysfunctional coping mechanism. Collectively, these findings underscore that smartphone addiction is not a standalone behavioral issue but is embedded within a broader psychological ecosystem involving both risk factors and protective resources.

Despite these advances, several limitations in the existing literature remain. First, most studies have focused narrowly on one or a few variables, such as depression or anxiety, without simultaneously considering the interplay between positive resources (e.g., resilience, life satisfaction) and negative mental health outcomes (e.g., stress, loneliness). Second, research has predominantly relied on correlational or mediation models. While informative, these approaches cannot adequately capture the complex interdependencies among multiple psychological factors, nor do they clarify which variables may play the most central roles in the network of smartphone addiction and mental health. Third, although network analysis has begun to be applied to problematic smartphone use, prior studies have primarily focused on symptom-level associations, such as withdrawal or preoccupation symptoms, and have rarely integrated broader psychosocial constructs. As a result, the structural configuration of smartphone addiction within the context of both positive and negative mental health indicators remains poorly understood.

To address these gaps, the present study applies a network analysis framework to examine the associations between smartphone addiction and multiple psychological factors in Chinese youths. By integrating both positive indicators (self-efficacy, life satisfaction, social support, resilience) and negative indicators (depression, anxiety, loneliness, stress) into a unified model, this study aims to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the psychological mechanisms and smartphone addiction. Unlike traditional regression or mediation models that estimate isolated associations, network analysis allows for the simultaneous estimation of conditional dependencies among all variables, identifies the most central (and potentially influential) nodes within the system, and can detect community structures. This approach provides a holistic and systems-level understanding of the interplay between smartphone addiction and mental health. Moreover, by employing directed acyclic graph (DAG) modeling, the study further explores potential directional influences among these variables, generating hypotheses about directional pathways.

Findings from this study may inform the development of more precise and multifaceted intervention strategies to mitigate the adverse impact of smartphone addiction. We hypothesized: (1) SMA is positively correlated with depression, anxiety, stress, and loneliness, and negatively correlated with positive factors; (2) Depression/anxiety/stress might be the central node; (3) Positive factors and negative factors form different communities, and SMA is more likely to be classified into the negative community; (4) DAG may indicate that there is a potential directional relationship between SMA and stress/depression.

Methods

Participants

In the initial sample, 902 youths were initially recruited using the online platform Wenjuanxing in September, 2024. But 201 did not finish all the scales (including lack of information, incorrect filling, etc.), so a total of 701 youths participated in the present study (mean age = 18.27 ± 1.57 years, 47.6% male), which met the sample requirements for network analysis. Prior to data collection, all participants were fully informed about the study objectives and questionnaire content. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant, and they were assured that their responses would be used solely for research purposes and handled confidentially. The Approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of Shanghai University of Sport (No. 102772024RT171).

Measures

Smartphone addiction (SMA)

Smartphone addiction was assessed using the Chinese version of the Smartphone Addiction Scale–Short Version (SAS-SV) [15]. The original SAS-SV was developed by Kwon et al. as a brief screening instrument for smartphone addiction among adolescents. The Chinese SAS-SV has subsequently been translated and psychometrically evaluated in Chinese-speaking populations, including Hong Kong adults and Chinese college students, supporting its reliability and validity for assessing problematic smartphone use in young Chinese samples. The scale consists of 10 items rated on a 6-point Likert scale ranging from 1, “strongly disagree,” to 6, “strongly agree.” For example: “I will find that I spend more time using my mobile phone than I originally expected”. Higher total scores indicate more severe smartphone addiction. Following the established sex-specific cut-off scores, scores ≥ 31 for males and ≥ 33 for females were classified as indicative of smartphone addiction. In the present sample, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, Cronbach’s α = 0.84.

Mental health factors

Depression

Depressive symptoms were measured using the Chinese version of the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) [16]. The original CES-D was developed by Radloff as a 20-item self-report measure designed to assess depressive symptomatology in the general population. The Chinese CES-D has been widely validated in Chinese populations, including university students and adolescents, and has demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity for assessing depressive symptoms. The abbreviated 9-item version assesses the frequency of depressive symptoms experienced during the past week, with items scored from 0 to 3. For example: “Feeling down and hopeless”, “I feel very tired and listless”. Higher scores reflect greater depressive symptom severity. The internal consistency in this study was satisfactory (Cronbach’s α = 0.85).

Anxiety

Anxiety symptoms were evaluated with the Generalized Anxiety Disorder 7-item questionnaire (GAD-7) [17]. The GAD-7 was originally developed by Spitzer, Kroenke, Williams, and Löwe as a brief self-report measure for screening generalized anxiety disorder and assessing anxiety severity in clinical practice and research. The Chinese version has shown good psychometric properties among Chinese adolescents and has also been validated in adolescent and young adult samples aged 15–24 years in Hong Kong. Items are rated on a 4-point Likert scale, with higher total scores indicating more severe anxiety. For example: “Feeling uneasy, worried and irritable”, “I’m very nervous and it’s hard to relax”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient in the current sample was 0.83.

Perceived stress

Perceived stress was assessed using the Chinese version of the Perceived Stress Scale (CPSS) [18]. The original Perceived Stress Scale was developed by Cohen, Kamarck, and Mermelstein to measure the degree to which individuals appraise situations in their lives as unpredictable, uncontrollable, and overloaded. The original validation included college student and community samples. The Chinese version of the PSS was translated and culturally adapted by Yang and Huang, forming the Chinese Perceived Stress Scale, CPSS, and has been used in Chinese urban residents aged 15 years and above. This 14-item instrument employs a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” to “always,” with higher scores representing higher levels of perceived stress. For example: “Feeling nervous, uneasy and stressed”, “I feel that everything is going smoothly”. The scale showed good reliability in this study (Cronbach’s α = 0.81).

Loneliness

Loneliness was measured using the 3-item short form of the UCLA Loneliness Scale [19]. The original UCLA Loneliness Scale was developed by Russell and colleagues and was initially validated among college students. Chinese versions of the UCLA Loneliness Scale and its short forms have demonstrated acceptable psychometric properties in Chinese-speaking adolescent and young adult samples. Responses range from 1 (“rarely”) to 3 (“often”), with higher scores indicating greater perceived loneliness. For example: “Do you often feel that you lack companions?”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.86.

Life satisfaction

Life satisfaction was assessed using the Chinese version of the Satisfaction With Life Scale (SWLS) [20]. The SWLS was originally developed by Diener, Emmons, Larsen, and Griffin. The Chinese version has been psychometrically evaluated in a nationally representative Chinese sample. The scale consists of five items rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1 = “completely inconsistent” to 7 = “completely consistent”). Higher scores indicate greater overall life satisfaction. For example: “I’m satisfied with my life”, “My life roughly conforms to my ideals”. Cronbach’s α coefficient in the present sample was 0.86.

Perceived social support

Perceived social support was measured with the Perceived Social Support Scale (PSSS-12) [21]. The original MSPSS was developed by Zimet, Dahlem, Zimet, and Farley. The Chinese version has been validated among Chinese adolescents and college students, supporting its reliability and construct validity in youth and young adult samples. This 12-item scale uses a 7-point Likert response format ranging from “strongly disagree” to “strongly agree,” with higher total scores reflecting greater perceived social support. For example: “I can share happiness and joys with some people”, “My friends can truly help me”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.81.

Psychological resilience

Psychological resilience was assessed using the Chinese revised 10-item Connor–Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC-10) [22]. The original 25-item CD-RISC was developed by Connor and Davidson to assess resilience as the ability to cope with stress and adversity. The 10-item version was later refined and validated by Campbell-Sills and Stein using undergraduate samples. The Chinese CD-RISC-10 was validated in Chinese adolescent and young adult samples. Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale from 0 (“never”) to 4 (“always”), and higher summed scores indicate greater resilience. For example: “Going through hardships will make me more powerful”, “I won’t be easily defeated by failure”. The Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.80.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was measured using the revised General Self-Efficacy Scale (GSES-10) [23]. The GSES was originally developed by Schwarzer and Jerusalem to assess general perceived self-efficacy. The Chinese version was adapted by Wang, Hu, and Liu, who reported evidence for its reliability and validity in Chinese samples. The scale consists of 10 items rated on a 4-point Likert scale (1 = “completely incorrect” to 4 = “completely correct”). Higher total scores represent stronger perceived self-efficacy. For example: “I am confident that I can deal with any unexpected events effectively”, “No matter what happens to me, can I handle it with ease”. Cronbach’s α coefficient was 0.82 in the current sample.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed for all study variables. The Shapiro–Wilk test was applied to examine normality assumptions. Differences of SMA and non-SMA were evaluated using independent-samples t tests, and Bonferroni correction was applied.

Network analysis

Network analyses were performed to investigate the relationships between smartphone addiction and mental health variables using the qgraph package in R (version 4.2.2). A Gaussian graphical model (GGM) was estimated to characterize the conditional dependence structure among variables [24]. We estimated the GGM using Spearman’s rank-order correlations. This non-parametric approach, which employs a non-paranormal transformation, provides more robust and reliable network estimates when data are skewed. Although the GGM framework is conventionally based on multivariate normality, Spearman-based regularized partial correlation networks are commonly used as a robust approximation for psychological questionnaire data. In this framework, nodes represent observed variables and edges correspond to partial correlations after controlling for all other variables in the network. Edge thickness reflects association strength, with blue and red edges indicating positive and negative relationships, respectively. Node centrality was primarily quantified using expected influence (EI), which captures the overall influence of a node by summing the signed edge weights connected to it [25]. Higher EI values indicate greater importance within the network [26]. In addition, although studies argue that these traditional indicators like strength, closeness, and betweenness cannot accurately capture the centrality of nodes when one node has both negative and positive edges and their tendency to be unstable [27], however, we also calculate the traditional centrality indicators as a supplement of centrality reference [26]. To reduce spurious associations and enhance model parsimony, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) [28, 29] combined with the extended Bayesian information criterion (EBIC) was applied [30, 31]. The EBIC tuning parameter γ was set to 0.5, which favors a parsimonious network. The stability and accuracy of the network were evaluated using non-parametric bootstrapping procedures implemented in the bootnet package [32, 33]. Edge-weight accuracy was assessed using 95% confidence intervals, and the stability of centrality estimates was evaluated using the correlation stability (CS) coefficient, with values above 0.25 considered acceptable and values above 0.50 indicating good stability [32, 34]. Lastly, non-parametric bootstrapping approach (n = 2000) to generate a series of plausible datasets based on the original observations to evaluate edge accuracy, and a narrower 95% CI indicates a more reliable network [32].

Community detection

Community structure was examined using bootstrapped exploratory graph analysis, EGA, with 1,000 resamples. To maintain methodological consistency with the primary network estimation, the EGA was conducted using the same Spearman correlation matrix as the GGM. The Walktrap algorithm was applied to identify clusters of strongly interconnected nodes within the network. This algorithm uses short random walks to detect groups of nodes that are more densely connected with one another than with the rest of the network. The stability of the community solution was evaluated by examining the consistency of the number and composition of communities across bootstrap samples. All variables included in the community detection analysis were identical to those in the primary network model [35, 36].

Directed acyclic graph

To investigate possible directional relationships among the network variables, we employed a directed acyclic graph (DAG) approach using the pcalg package [37]. DAGs, a form of Bayesian network, estimate potential directional links by modeling conditional independence among variables. This method enables the identification of plausible directional pathways within cross-sectional data and can highlight potential risk factors as well as candidate targets for preventive or therapeutic interventions [38, 39]. The significance level α threshold (0.05) determined whether conditional associations were retained or removed during skeleton estimation. Edge orientation was then determined according to the orientation rules of the PC algorithm. Given the cross-sectional and observational nature of the data, the DAG findings were used only to generate preliminary directional hypotheses.

Network comparison test

To further examine whether the network structure differed between participants with and without smartphone addiction, we conducted a Network Comparison Test, NCT, using the NetworkComparisonTest package [40]. The NCT was used to compare the SMA and non-SMA groups in terms of overall network structure invariance and global strength invariance. The number of permutations was set to 1000.

Results

Sample characteristics

The cross-sectional study included 701 youths who reported smartphone use scores, 294 youths had smartphone addiction, and 407 youths did not. Table 1 presented the entire sample information and differences of all variables between SMA and non-SMA sub-samples. The results showed that there are significant differences among SMA and all mental health factors between groups, that is, the SMA youths had higher scores of negative mental health factors and lower scores of positive mental health factors than non-SMA youths. Furthermore, Shapiro–Wilk tests indicated that several variables deviated from normality; therefore, Spearman correlations were used for bivariate correlation. The results of correlation analysis suggested that SMA is significantly correlated with all negative mental health factors positively and with all positive mental health negatively (Supplementary Figure S1). All the above results suggested that the SMA youths encountered serious mental health problems than non-SMA youths.

Network analysis

Nodes connections and centrality

Figure 1 presents the regularized partial correlation network of SMA and mental health variables. After EBICglasso regularization, 21 non-zero edges were retained, corresponding to a network density of 0.58. No isolated nodes were observed, indicating that all variables were connected to the broader network. Among the retained edges, 14 were positive and 7 were negative. According to the edge weight difference test (Supplementary Figure S2), the strongest positive edge was observed between self-efficacy and psychological resilience (weight = 0.47), followed by depression and anxiety, (weight = 0.42) (Fig. 2). The strongest negative edge was observed between self-efficacy and stress (weight = -0.28), followed by psychological resilience and stress (weight = -0.22). Among the edges involving SMA, the strongest connection was observed between SMA and stress (weight = 0.22), followed by SMA–depression (weight = 0.09). The results showed that the self-efficacy and psychological resilience edge and the depression and anxiety edge were among the strongest and most statistically distinguishable edges in the network. Both edges were significantly stronger than many weaker edges, the complete edge-weight matrix is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Expected influence values are presented in Fig. 2. Anxiety showed the highest expected influence (EI = 0.80), followed closely by depression (EI = 0.77). Self-efficacy (EI = 0.53), and psychological resilience (EI = 0.48), showed the highest expected influence among the positive mental health factors. The bootstrapped centrality difference test indicated that anxiety and depression had significantly higher expected influence than several lower-centrality nodes, particularly loneliness and stress. However, anxiety and depression did not show a clearly meaningful difference from each other (Supplementary Figure S3).

Network stability and accuracy

Regarding the stability of node centrality, the correlation stability coefficient for EI was 0.75, indicating that the node centrality index of the network had good stability (Supplementary Figure S4). Meanwhile, the accuracy of the network edge weights was tested (Supplementary Figure S5), the narrow 95% confidence interval of the edge weights in this survey and the high degree of overlap between the two lines indicate that the evaluation of the edge weights is accurate.

The results of centrality of strength, betweenness and closeness are also present in Supplementary Figure S6, and their stability test are shown in Supplementary Figure S7, which indicating lower stability compared with centrality of EI.

Community detection

Figure 3 presents the detected communities using bootstrapped EGA. The analysis revealed two community structures in 1000 bootstrapped samples (100%), community 1 was composed by all positive mental health factors, and community 2 was composed of all negative mental health items and SMA. Within Community 2, which included SMA and negative mental health factors, SMA showed its strongest connections with stress and depression.

Directed acyclic graph

Although the results from network analysis and community detection offer us the interactions between SMA and mental health factors, however, we further conducted the analysis of directed acyclic graph to explore the potential direction from SMA to all mental health factors. Figure 4 presents the CPDAG of the included variables. According to the graph, SMA showed potential directional links with stress and depression. Stress was further linked with depression and loneliness, while depression was linked with life satisfaction and loneliness. In summary, the CPDAG results suggested that SMA may be directionally associated with stress and depression and may also be indirectly linked with depression, loneliness, and life satisfaction through stress and depression. These findings are consistent with the network analysis results and provide preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence regarding the possible directional organization among SMA and mental health-related variables. Given the cross-sectional nature of the data, these directional patterns should not be interpreted as evidence of causal effects.

Network comparison test

To determine whether the network differed between participants with and without SMA, we conducted a Network Comparison Test. The network structure invariance test was not significant, M = 0.13, p = 0.73, indicating that the overall pattern of edge weights did not differ significantly between the SMA and non-SMA groups. The global strength was 3.84 in the SMA group and 3.78 in the non-SMA group. The global strength invariance test was also not significant, S = 0.06, p = 0.89, suggesting that the overall level of network connectivity was comparable between groups.

Discussion

Summary of findings

The present study investigated the complex associations between smartphone addiction (SMA) and multiple dimensions of mental health among youths, applying a network analytic framework complemented by community detection and directed acyclic graph (DAG) analyses. According to our results, SMA was significantly linked to higher levels of negative mental health indicators and lower levels of positive indicators. Network analysis further highlighted that SMA was most strongly connected to stress, and that anxiety and depression emerged as the most central nodes within the mental health network. Community detection revealed a clear segregation between positive and negative psychological factors, with SMA clustering within the negative mental health community. Finally, DAG analysis suggested that SMA may be directionally linked to stress and depression, and indirect influence on loneliness and life satisfaction via stress and depression. Taken together, these findings underscore the detrimental role of SMA in shaping youth mental health.

Our network analysis indicates that in the psychological networks of adolescents with SMA, anxiety and depression have become the most critical nodes, suggesting that these two symptoms may serve as key hubs for maintaining or exacerbating the psychological pathological state. This is consistent with previous research findings, which state that depression and anxiety are the most common and destructive consequences of smartphone addiction [41, 42]. Additionally, the edge weight between depression-anxiety and resilience–self-efficacy is the strongest, highlighting that the negative and positive psychological structures form a closely connected binary combination, which may be the focus of intervention. These findings expand upon previous cross-sectional studies, demonstrating how SMA is embedded within a broader psychological system. The close connection between SMA and stress is consistent with previous research results, which indicate that smartphone addiction is not only a strategy for coping with stress but also, due to excessive social demands, sleep disruption, and reduced offline recovery time, it itself may also become a stressor [43, 44]. Individuals with stronger self-efficacy may be more likely to mobilize coping strategies when facing academic or interpersonal stressors, whereas resilience may help maintain adaptive functioning under stress. This protective cluster may buffer the association between SMA and negative mental health outcomes. Therefore, interventions for SMA should not only target stress and depressive symptoms but also strengthen positive psychological resources, particularly self-efficacy and resilience.

The community analysis consistently identifies two distinct groups: one group includes all positive psychological health factors, while the other group includes SMA and its negative factors. The separation of these groups indicates that smartphone addiction is essentially closely related to negative psychological states, while protective resources independently form a group. The clustering of SMA with negative mental health indicators further indicates that poor smartphone usage behavior is deeply rooted in unhealthy psychological processes rather than being merely an independent behavioral pattern [45]. We have further expanded upon these findings, revealing the structural role of specific symptoms.

Finally, the DAG analysis suggested a potential directional association from SMA to stress and depression, and simultaneously indirectly influenced loneliness and life satisfaction through stress and depression. At the same time, we noticed that depression might also be influenced by stress. These directional correlations are consistent with some theoretical models, which suggest that SMA will intensify stress due to continuous connectivity and stressful tasks, thereby exacerbating depressive symptoms [46]. This finding also aligns with the network theory in psychopathology, which posits that certain symptoms have a greater impact on maintaining or exacerbating comorbid conditions [47]. Stress becomes a key mediating factor on the path from SMA to depression and loneliness. This finding complements previous research results, which indicate that stress may be closely associated with problematic smartphone use and subsequent depressive or loneliness-related outcomes [48]. Moreover, depression further weakened positive indicators of well-being, such as life satisfaction, and increased social withdrawal and loneliness. Overall, these results suggest that intervention measures aimed at alleviating SMA-related psychological problems should prioritize stress regulation and depression management, given that they may have cascading effects in other areas of mental health.

The current study extends previous network research in several important ways. First, whereas prior studies have mainly focused on symptom-level associations between problematic smartphone use, anxiety, and depression, the present study incorporated both negative mental health indicators and positive psychological resources, including life satisfaction, social support, resilience, and self-efficacy. This broader construct-level network allowed us to identify not only risk-related nodes, such as stress and depression, but also a protective resource community. Second, by combining GGM, bootstrapped EGA, and DAG analysis, the present study provided complementary evidence regarding conditional associations, community organization, and preliminary directional patterns. Third, the strong edge between self-efficacy and resilience highlights a positive psychological resource dyad that has received limited attention in previous SMA network studies. Therefore, the incremental contribution of this study lies in shifting the network perspective from a narrow symptom-centered model to a more protection framework of SMA and mental health.

Theoretical and practical implications

This study has several implications. First, this study deepens the understanding of SMA by transcending binary associations and adopting a multivariate network perspective. Identifying anxiety and depression as core symptoms highlights potential intervention targets, while DAG analysis provides clues to the sequence of psychological processes starting from SMA. Importantly, the clustering of protective factors suggests that strengthening positive psychological resources such as resilience and self-efficacy may help balance this network by reducing the central position of anxiety and depression. Secondly, our research results emphasize the importance of early screening for stress and depressive symptoms in adolescents with problematic smartphone use, as these factors may indicate broader mental health issues. Therefore, given that perceived stress and depression were identified as key nodes in the pathway from SMA to other psychopathology, we specifically recommend Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy (CBT) for stress management and Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR) as potential interventions to break this cascade.

Limitations and future directions

Despite its strengths, including a moderately sized sample of 701 youth sample and the application of advanced network and DAG methods, this study has several limitations. First, although DAG analysis provides preliminary directional insights. we explicitly state that these directed edges are exploratory, based on conditional independence patterns in cross-sectional data, and do not confirm temporal precedence or definitive causality. Longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to verify the temporal ordering of SMA and mental health symptoms. Second, self-report measures may be subject to recall or social desirability bias, future research could integrate objective smartphone usage data. Third, while we included multiple positive and negative mental health factors, other relevant domains (e.g., sleep, attention, academic performance) were not assessed. Moreover, we note that the potential for Common Method Bias (CMB) due to the exclusive reliance on self-report measures, and future studies should consider incorporating multi-method, multi-informant data. Finally, the generalizability of findings is limited to youth populations, replication in other age groups and cultural contexts is warranted. The prevalence of SMA in the current sample was 41.9%, which is higher than many global estimates. This discrepancy may be attributable to the use of the SAS-SV sex-specific cut-offs, the college student sample, the online recruitment strategy, and the specific timing of data collection. Therefore, the prevalence estimate should be interpreted as sample-specific rather than representative of all Chinese young adults.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study, which combines network analysis, community detection, and directed acyclic graph modeling, indicates that youth smartphone addiction is closely related to adverse mental health outcomes, which are key pathways leading to broader psychological problems. Interventions targeting stress and depression may break the vicious cycle and promote an overall improvement in well-being. This study emphasizes the importance of a comprehensive intervention strategy, which should combine the reduction of addictive smartphone behaviors with the enhancement of psychological resilience and protective resources.
