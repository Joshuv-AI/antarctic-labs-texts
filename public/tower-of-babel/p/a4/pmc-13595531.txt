Self-reported availability of research data in American Psychological Association journal articles: a cross-sectional investigation
Authors: Claire C Guyatt, Gert Storms
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrior evidence suggests that journals requiring open data are associated with higher levels of data sharing in the published psychology literature. Data sharing policies are not, however, consistently implemented or enforced. The American Psychological Association (APA), in 2020, signed onto the Transparency and Openness Promotion Guidelines, which promote increasingly stringent data sharing policies. The current study examined self-reported data sharing in APA journals and whether stricter policies are linked to higher levels of self-reported data sharing.MethodsWe assessed self-reported data sharing practices in 1,250 articles published between 2023 and 2025 in 25 APA journals. Using logistic regression, we examined the association between journal policy level and self-reported data sharing. We then applied post-stratification weighting, based on the actual distribution of policy levels across APA journals and their associated percentages of data sharing, to estimate the overall percentage of self-reported data sharing.ResultsWe estimated overall self-reported data sharing to be 30.3% (95% CI [27.7, 33.0]). Journal policy stringency was strongly associated with self-reported data sharing: among journals with no data sharing policy, 15.0% of articles reported shared data (30 out of 200; 95% CI [10.7, 20.6]); among journals that mandate that authors reveal whether they shared their data, 26.4% of articles reported shared data (145 out of 550; 95% CI [22.8, 30.2]), and among journals that mandate data sharing, 70.4% of articles reported shared data (352 out of 500; 95% CI [66.2, 74.2]). At the same time, even journals with more stringent data sharing requirements did not consistently enforce their policies.ConclusionsThese results indicate that despite widespread endorsement of open science practices, data sharing is not yet a customary practice among psychology researchers. The observed self-reported data sharing practices do, however, show that more stringent journal policy requirements are associated with increased openness. While the observational nature of the present study precludes strong causal inferences, stricter journal policies may represent one potential factor relating to the adoption of data sharing practices, including the use of data embargoes and greater transparency around decisions not to share data. Overall, findings highlight a promising direction for ongoing efforts to promote openness in data sharing, while underscoring the need for future research to clarify the mechanisms that link policy to practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s41073-026-00235-w.Keywords: Data sharing, Transparency and openness promotion guidelines, Journal policies, Open data, Open science

Background

Open science and data sharing

Open science is an umbrella term that encompasses the theoretical perspective, and the associated practical applications, that scientific knowledge should be freely available to all [1, 2]. A growing scepticism regarding the credibility of findings, the widespread failure to replicate psychology findings – known as the replicability crisis – together with increasing concern about questionable and even fraudulent research practices, has fueled calls for open science in the psychological field [3, 4]. Such calls assert that open science can help to reform research norms and improve the robustness of scientific findings.

A key aspect of open science is data sharing, defined by Martone et al. as “the publication of the primary data and any supporting materials required to interpret the data acquired as part of a research study” ([5], p. 111). The term ‘open data’ similarly refers to data published under this definition. The American Psychological Association (APA) asserts that data sharing encourages a culture of openness and accountability; allows those with limited resources to do research; enables replication of analyses; and facilitates new more powerful analyses of extant data [6].

Meta researchers have provided evidence for the advantages of data sharing. Researchers who share data benefit from increased visibility and citation counts, independent of journal impact factor [7]. Moreover, data sharing practices are associated with increased certainty of evidence and quality of reporting statistical results [8]. In addition to the advantages to researchers, both self-report and experimental investigations have reported positive associations between data sharing and the general population’s trust in scientists and their findings [9, 10].

Attention to and endorsement of data sharing has motivated a plethora of tools and services that facilitate the practice [11]. Public repositories are the often-recommended route for sharing research products, such as the Open Science Framework (OSF), a free and open-sourced management tool that houses a popular repository [11].

Previous findings on data sharing in psychology

Although appeals for data sharing in psychology have grown in recent years, psychology researchers have long advocated for the open science practice. Wolins, in 1962, was the first to quantitatively assess data sharing practices in psychology, finding that only 9 out of 37 APA journal authors (24%) willingly shared their data upon request [12]. Close to fifty years after this initial investigation, Wicherts et al. requested data from 249 papers published in four APA journals, intending to conduct reanalyses to assess the robustness of the studies’ findings to outliers in their data [13]. Despite the decades that had passed, Wicherts et al. were unable to conduct their intended reanalyses, as they received only 26% (64 out of 249) of the requested datasets – a similar percentage to that of Wolins. In a follow up study six years later, Vanpaemel et al. requested data from 394 papers published in four APA journals, reporting that only 38% of researchers sent their data upon request (n = 148) [14].

Open science advocates, however, encourage data sharing not upon request, but by open access provided as part of the original article publication. Openly published data circumvents the often-futile efforts to contact authors in order to access data [15]. Nevertheless, only 2% of a random sample of 250 psychology articles published between 2014–2017 had publicly shared data (4 out of 188) [16]. The most recent exploration reported that 14% of a sample of 200 psychology articles published in 2022 had publicly accessible data (n = 28), with a marginally higher percentage of 16% of 200 articles that came from the most prominent psychology journals ranked by impact factor (n = 33) [17].

Levels of data sharing also vary in response to journal publication requirements and open science initiatives. In 2014, Psychological Science implemented open science badges, which reward published articles with badges that signify transparency, and saw self-reported data sharing rise from 3% in 2014 to 39% in 2015 [18]. There were no changes over time in the low levels of self-reported data sharing among comparison journals, and the authors concluded that the badges increased the availability of open data. Rochios and Richmond conducted an updated investigation of Psychological Science’s data sharing, and found that while the journal’s data sharing policy remained unchanged between 2014 and 2020, the increasingly open data trend continued: based on authors’ open data scoring system including ratings of self-reported and actual open data in four psychology subfields, data sharing rose from an average of 23% just after the badges were introduced to 56% six years later [19]. Despite the badge system’s success, Psychological Science ceased awarding ‘open data’ badges in 2024 following their 2023 mandate that authors make primary data available or justify any failure to do so [20]. This change reflects the outlook that data sharing should be a standard practice, rather than an optional exercise.

Researchers have investigated the potential power of compulsory data sharing policies that require authors to make relevant research data publicly available upon publication or, if data sharing is impossible, include a statement explaining why the data cannot be shared. The journal Cognition introduced such a policy and saw, in a sample of 591 articles from 2014–2017, an increase in self-reported open data from 25% (104 out of 417) to 78% (136 out of 174) [21]. Similarly, the Journal of Memory and Language introduced a mandatory open data policy and saw an increase in shared data from 34% (20 out of 59) to 86% (51 out of 59) [22].

From these case studies it appears that journal policies such as badges intended to promote open data do improve data sharing, but to a much lesser degree than journal policies that mandate data sharing. Even so, data sharing policy adherence does not rise to 100% even under mandatory open data policies, revealing that authors do not always follow journal regulations and journals do not always check compliance [7, 23].

TOP Guidelines

While some psychology journals mandate their own open science policies, the Center for Open Science (COS) has created Transparency and Openness Promotion (TOP) Guidelines from which journals can draw from and/or explicitly implement [24]. The TOP Guidelines cover eight standards of modular, complementary open science practices, including one standard for open data labelled “Data Transparency”. TOP further quantifies the degree to which journals implement these standards with levels ranging from Level 0 to 3 for each standard with increasing stringency (Table 1). COS updated their TOP Guidelines in 2025 but made no significant changes to the Data Transparency Guideline.

Center for Open Science Data Transparency and Openness Promotion Guideline levels

The APA is, since 2020, a signatory of the TOP Guidelines from the Center for Open Science [25, 26]. When APA endorsed the TOP Guidelines, the COS wrote that APA agreed to officially begin implementing standardized disclosure requirements of data and underlying research materials (TOP Level 1) in its associated journals and that APA encourages editors of those journals to move to higher TOP levels. It is, however, up to each individual journal to determine which and to what degree it mandates open science practices, and currently only a subset of APA journals explicitly refer to data sharing TOP Guidelines or have corresponding open data policies. In the remainder of this paper ‘TOP levels’ refers specifically to the COS Data Transparency Guideline.

Current investigation

Journal guidelines promoting or requiring open data increase data sharing in the published psychology literature [18, 19, 21, 22]. Therefore, one might anticipate that APA journal editions published in the four years since APA signed on to TOP Guidelines may have high levels of self-reported data sharing, and that higher TOP level journal mandates may show higher levels of self-reported open data in empirical articles. We therefore hypothesized that higher TOP levels (ranging from 0–2 in psychology journals) as specified in APA journal submission policies are associated with higher levels of self-reported data sharing, with a moderate effect size of TOP level on data sharing.

To investigate this possibility, we determined TOP level requirements and the frequency of self-reported data sharing in articles published from 2023 to 2025 in a sample of APA journals. We also investigated if APA journals have indeed implemented TOP data sharing guidelines and the extent to which implementation is associated with self-reported data sharing. We also explored whether differences in policies or data sharing exist between psychology subdisciplines as well as between qualitative and quantitative articles. Finally, we explored how authors choose to share data, and if they choose not to share data, what reasons they provide for doing so.

Methods

Sample

We limited our search to APA-published journals. Our objectives were to 1) obtain a large enough dataset to glean meaningful results and 2) draw conclusions about recent developments and the current data sharing landscape. To achieve the first objective, we selected 50 articles per journal. To address the second, recognizing that APA endorsed TOP Guidelines in 2020 and that it takes time to implement new policies, we limited our search to articles published since the beginning of 2023. Moreover, APA is a leading scientific organization representing the field of psychology, publishing a broad, impactful, and substantial number of psychology journals that include research from around the world; therefore, APA journals provide a good indication of overarching data sharing trends within the psychology field [27].

We excluded APA journals that are no longer taking submissions, journals that do not publish original studies, and journals that have been or are set to transfer to a publisher different than APA, resulting in a set of 88 possible APA journals. APA divides their journals into ten subdisciplines, and we included a sample of at least 20% of the journals within each one, with 4 journals from TOP Level 0, 11 from TOP Level 1, and 10 from TOP Level 2 (no APA journals require TOP Level 3). The greater number of TOP Level 1 and Level 2 journals is due to the sampling procedure: data collectors were master’s students using their respective subsets for their thesis projects, all centred around some aspect of data sharing. Thus, their own interests and subjective importance of the journals motivated their journal selection.

We began data collection in November 2024 and finished in July 2025. From each journal, we selected the 50 most recently published articles that presented findings on empirical data. We excluded reviews, commentaries, and other non-empirical articles. This resulted in a final dataset of N = 1,250 articles from 25 APA journals.

Data collection

Five researchers independently collected data, each from three to eight journals. Researchers searched directly on the APA website to view the most recent journal article publications. Data collection included the following meta-data for each article: corresponding journal TOP level; data type (quantitative – including meta-analyses containing original empirical data – qualitative, or mixed methods); if and how authors reported to have publicly shared their data; if the author supplied a reason for omitted data, and if so, what reason they provided. Coders first sought meta-data by examining parts of articles in which open data information is often found (e.g. transparency and openness or methods sections). If the initial search was unsuccessful, coders then searched the article’s full text for keywords such as ‘available’ and ‘dataset’.

We coded ‘upon request’ as data not shared. This is consistent both with previous similar work and reports that data availability upon request is an insufficient method to adequately facilitate data sharing [15, 16, 23]. We specially coded simulated data, found in psychology methods articles, as shared if authors made available either the raw data or all the code and parameters for data-generating. We accepted code and parameters as open data as these materials provide sufficient information to reproduce the data-generating-process [28].

Coders noted anomalies or uncertainties, and the lead researcher addressed the uncertainties and, if necessary, conducted a conversation with the senior investigator. For example, some articles had multiple datasets in which one or more was not shared; we coded such cases as data not shared. To ensure accuracy, two additional researchers double coded a unique random subset of 10 articles from each journal (n = 250), resolving coding differences in the same manner as initial uncertainties. We calculated a Cohen's Kappa reliability analysis for each duplicate coder’s data against the original data. Our first coder had moderate reliability (k = 0.61) and our second coder had high reliability (k = 0.80) according to standard guidelines [29]. Given the moderate reliability of the first coder, we recruited a third researcher to perform another round of duplicate coding on an additional unique random subset of 10 articles from each journal (n = 250), resulting in high reliability (k = 0.82). These inter-rater reliability statistics are based on the data collected for data availability (if and where authors shared their data, reported in a single cell) and whether authors gave a reason for sharing their data. When we estimated reliability based also on data type and year, inter-rater reliability statistics improved. Our duplicate coder data collection protocol is available at https://osf.io/pru3c/files/y7p68.

Data analysis

We report raw counts and percentages of self-reported data sharing for each TOP level. For overall self-reported data sharing in psychology, we report a 95% confidence interval calculated with the Wilson method for binomial variables. To address sample bias arising from the unequal representation of journal TOP levels within our dataset, we applied a post-stratification weighting procedure to obtain a less biased estimate of overall self-reported data sharing: we first calculated the mean self-reported data sharing of the collection of journals in each TOP level based on our sample and then weighted these stratum-specific means by the actual distribution of TOP levels across all 88 empirical APA journals. Additionally, we attempted post-hoc sensitivity analyses to account for this bias: we ran a propensity score matching analysis and a logistic regression with covariates using additional journal-level variables (impact factor and number of articles published). While the propensity analysis proved unfeasible due to sample size restrictions, the additional logistic regression showed that TOP Level remained a significant predictor of data sharing for Level 2 journals. Additional file 1 presents these results.

We conducted a binary logistic regression to estimate odds ratios of self-reported data sharing as a function of TOP level, with Level 0 as the reference category. We used Cramer’s V, calculated from the chi square statistic, to measure the effect size [30]. We ran a post-hoc Wald test to evaluate the difference between Level 1 and Level 2. We report counts and percentages of qualitative versus quantitative open data; open data by subdiscipline; how data were shared and reasons given as to why data were not shared.

Overall data sharing

Of the 1,250 empirical articles, 527 (42.2%, 95% CI [39.5%, 44.9%]) reported to give access to their data. Of the 723 articles that did not, 112 (15.5%) offered a reason for not sharing data: 107 pointed to legal or ethical concerns (95.5%), four had data embargoes (3.6%) (i.e., a temporary restriction placed on the sharing of a dataset until a specified future date or the termination of ongoing analyses), and 1 cited a technological reason (1.0%). Excluding the 112 articles whose authors provided a reason for not sharing data, overall self-reported data sharing rose to 46.3% (527 out of 1,138; 95% CI [43.4%, 49.2%]).

Data sharing by TOP level

Of the 200 articles in Level 0 journals, 30 (15.0%, 95% CI [10.7, 20.6]) reported shared data. This was true of 145 (26.4%; 95% CI [22.8, 30.2]) of all 550 articles in Level 1 journals, and 352 (70.4%; 95% CI [66.2, 74.2]) of all 500 articles in Level 2 journals (Fig. 1). The effect size was moderate (Cramer’s V = 0.33) [29]. Table 3 presents a logistic regression of TOP Level on self-reported data sharing with Level 0 as a baseline. A post-hoc investigation additionally showed a significant difference between Level 1 journals and Level 2 journals (χ2 (1, N = 1,250) = 189.10; p < 0.001).

Discussion

Data sharing by TOP level

Our analysis of the relationship between TOP levels and self-reported data sharing practices shows a strong correlation. Very few articles (30 out of 200, 15.0%) from journals with no data availability requirements (TOP Level 0) reported shared data; just over a quarter (145 out of 550, 26.4%) of articles from journals that mandate authors to state whether or not data are available (TOP Level 1) reported shared data; and the majority (352 out of 500, 70.4%) of articles from journals that mandate authors to share their data or give a reason for why they do not share their data (TOP Level 2) reported shared data. These percentages align with previous work showing that the data sharing at the journal Cognition rose from 25% (104 out of 417) to 78% (136 out of 174) after mandating that journal submissions share their data or else include an explanation as to why data cannot be shared [21].

Given the strong association between journal TOP level and data sharing and the overrepresentation of higher TOP level journals in our sample (Fig. 2), the observed self-reported data-sharing percentage of 46.3% is likely an upwardly biased estimate of the true percentage. To address this sampling bias, we derived a weighted estimate by mapping TOP level-specific self-reported data sharing percentages onto the distribution of TOP levels across the full set of 88 APA journals. The resulting estimate of 30.3% self-reported shared data is likely a more accurate approximation of the true prevalence of data sharing across APA journals.

The significant association between journal data sharing requirement as delineated by the TOP framework and corresponding levels of self-reported data sharing suggests that stringent journal submission transparency requirements may benefit the open science movement. This association may be a result of journals’ requirements for article submission that motivate authors to share their data prior to journal submission. Alternatively, it may be that subsequent journal editor responses during the peer-review process motivate data sharing. It is plausible that both occur; moreover, the former may lead to the latter: e.g., journals ask authors for data in response to an article submission, and in a subsequent article submission authors pre-emptively share their data. In this way, journal data sharing policies may shift the norms and academic expectations around open data.

In a similar fashion, simply requiring authors to state whether or not data are shared may increase data sharing by introducing authors to, or reminding them of, the practice. Alternatively, authors may recognize that readers generally support open science practices and data sharing and thus may prefer to make their data available rather than acknowledge its withholding and risk criticism from their peers [31, 32]. In support of the latter explanation for the higher data sharing percentage, only 0.01% of articles without open data in TOP Level 0 journals provided a reason for not sharing data (2 out of 170); rising to 14.1% of articles without open data in TOP Level 1 journals (57 out of 405). One possible explanation for this finding is that requiring authors to disclose whether or not they shared their data motivates authors to defend their reasoning if they did not.

Of the articles published in TOP Level 2 journals that did not share their data, a majority (95 out of 148; 64.2%) gave no reason as to why not, which is in direct violation of these journals’ submission policy. This reveals a failure of some academic journals to enforce their own submission policies. Some TOP Level 2 and 3 journal editors have acknowledged their own lack of policy enforcement, explaining that full implementation of strict data sharing policies requires additional staff and funding [33]. Silverstein et al. recognize these challenges, noting that verifying authors’ compliance with open data and materials policies is resource-intensive [34]. To mitigate this barrier, they suggest providing authors with information around data and materials transparency and clear instructions that outline authors’ responsibility to meet open data and materials requirements.

Data sharing by subdiscipline and data type

Given the association between TOP level and self-reported data sharing, it is revealing to examine the spread across APA subdisciplines’ journal guidelines. Figure 3 presents the wide ranges in proportion of TOP levels among subdisciplines’ journals. Within all subdisciplines, however, either Level 0 or Level 1 is the most common journal policy level.

Journals may also follow broader data sharing trends within their respective subdisciplines. A TOP Level 2 journal situated in a subdiscipline with a majority of low TOP level journals – and thus generally low self-reported data sharing – may have a lower percentage than would be expected based solely on its stringent policy. We observe this phenomenon in Sport, Exercise, and Performance Psychology and Journal of Psychopathology and Clinical Science. Both are TOP Level 2 journals, yet their self-reported data sharing percentages are relatively low compared to the mean Level 2 data sharing percentage (Table 2). Both journals belong to the Clinical Psychology subdiscipline, which has majority TOP Level 0 journals, and relatively few Level 2 journals (Fig. 3). Nonetheless, they still outperform Clinical Psychology journals at Level 0 and Level 1 (see Additional file 2 for more information on subdisciplines’ sharing percentages in the current sample). Similarly, we also see the reverse: The Canadian Journal of Experimental Psychology is a Level 0 journal with a relatively high level of self-reported data sharing compared to the average for Level 0 journals, aligning with the journal belonging to the Basic and Experimental Psychology subdiscipline, which has the largest proportion of Level 2 journals among all subdisciplines. These examples suggest that the broader data-sharing culture within a subdiscipline – reflected in the distribution of TOP levels across its journals – interacts with TOP level in its relationship to a journal’s level of self-reported data sharing.

Studies with qualitative data had a much lower level of self-reported data sharing, compared to those with solely quantitative data. Some argue that qualitative data poses particular ethical and technological barriers to data sharing, such as the impossibility of separating the data from its unique context, and the detailed nature of the data that may put participants’ privacy at elevated risk [35, 36]. Others, however, contend that in practice, qualitative data involves unique sharing advantages, such as the opportunity for researchers to discuss and negotiate informed consent with participants [37]. The challenges to sharing qualitative data may explain the lower level of data sharing for qualitative articles; traditions may also play a role.

Reasons provided for not sharing data

Only a minority of articles that did not share data provided a reason. Of the articles that included a reason, a large majority gave a legal and/or ethical explanation, while just a few articles cited data embargoes, and one gave a technical reason. This conflicts with the findings of a survey study investigating barriers to data sharing: scientists from a variety of fields most often report the “desire to publish results before releasing data” as a major barrier, followed by “legal constraints” and “loss of credit or recognition” [38]. Another survey targeting psychology researchers found that the most common barriers that kept researchers from sharing their data were “sharing data is not a common practice in my field”, followed by “I prefer to share my data upon request”, and “preparing data is too time-consuming” [30]. A recent survey of psychology and law researchers reported that those expressing concerns with data sharing most often mentioned inappropriate or uncredited use of their data, followed by privacy or confidentiality issues and ‘being scooped’, i.e. forfeiting the chance to do all analyses first [39]. Qualitative interviews with researchers have reported similar sentiments, as well as concern regarding vulnerability to accusations of misconduct [40]. Reconciling these survey and interview results and our own findings, it appears that authors are comfortable citing legal constraints but would otherwise prefer to omit an explanation than state their true concerns.

When encouraging open science practices, authors’ legitimate concerns and potential drawbacks must also be taken seriously. Beyond requiring data, data sharing mandates also serve a crucial function in encouraging transparency by prompting authors to provide justifications when data cannot be shared. When researchers have valid reasons to withhold data, including intending to collaborate on secondary analyses and seeking appropriate credit, they should openly state their reasons in the publication without fear of penalty.

Finally, only four studies cited a data embargo, meaning a temporary restriction on the sharing of data, as their reason for withholding open data. Embargo periods can range in length from months to years, and during this time, researchers who collected the data continue to control others’ access [5]. In this way, researchers may continue to solely analyze and interpret their data or share their data with selected collaborators. Data embargoes thus offer a compromise to researchers’ concerns about sharing their data before fully publishing their own results [41].

Limitations

Our data collection procedure did not involve a random selection of APA journals. Instead, the choice of the journals was motivated by the subjective importance of the journals in their respective field and data collectors’ own interests based on each one’s research project. The lack of random selection introduced sample bias into our investigation, particularly with respect to the number of TOP Level 0 (n = 4), Level 1 (n = 11), and Level 2 (n = 10) journals in our sample. This bias warrants caution in interpreting our results. Nonetheless, our large sample demonstrated a significant association between journal data sharing policies and self-reported data sharing. This, in turn, allowed us to obtain a credible estimate of data sharing in APA journals by mapping self-reported data sharing percentages onto the actual distribution of data sharing policies across all APA journals. Similarly, readers may gain insight into data sharing differences between subdisciplines by focusing on the distribution of TOP levels across journals within each subdiscipline.

Additionally, articles published in 2024 (n = 916) are, in comparison to 2023 (n = 189) and 2025 (n = 145) overrepresented. We recognize that journal policies, as well as data transparency norms, change over time and there may be more open data in articles published in 2025 than in 2023. Our selected timeline nevertheless gives an overall picture of self-reported data sharing in the years following APA’s endorsement of TOP Guidelines in 2020. Moreover, we addressed the timing of when our sample of journals implemented their data sharing policies. We were able to definitively confirm for all but three journals (all Level 1) that their data sharing policies were in effect during the years of article publication for their set of articles. An excel file listing sources for proof of policy implementations is uploaded to OSF at https://osf.io/pru3c/files/53j6y.

Given these limitations, future research could employ random selection to glean a less biased account of current data sharing practices among APA journals and APA subdisciplines. This would be especially useful if combined with a difference-in-differences analysis investigating open data in journals before and after stringent policy implementation compared to journals that remained with low-level or no data sharing policy.

We also did not verify the functionality of the links provided for accessing open data; some articles coded as sharing data may therefore not have had truly accessible datasets. Some studies show that the majority of links do function and data are accessible when advertised as such, however, other investigations have found upwards of 10% decreases in sharing when analyzing reusable shared data rather than claimed shared data [17, 21, 22, 42]. The value of shared datasets depends not only on availability but also on comprehensibility – for example, complete datasets with accompanying metadata – which is essential for replication and reuse [43]. For this reason, we specify ‘self-reported’ data sharing rather than actual open data.

Conclusion

These results indicate that despite widespread endorsement of open science practices, data sharing is not yet a customary practice among psychology researchers. Our findings do, however, show that level of self-reported data sharing is associated with journal submission requirements, such that open data policies strongly correlate with increased data sharing. While the observational nature of the present study precludes strong causal inferences, stricter journal policies may represent one potential factor relating to the adoption of data sharing practices, including the use of data embargoes and greater transparency around decisions not to share data. Overall, findings highlight a promising direction for ongoing efforts to promote openness in data sharing, while underscoring the need for future research to clarify the mechanisms that link policy to practice.
