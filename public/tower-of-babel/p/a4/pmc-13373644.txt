Sustainable and efficient silver nano-catalysed C–N bond-forming approaches in organic synthesis
Authors: Anagh Sai K, Adarsh Krishna T. P.
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Nanocatalysts have become popular due to their efficient catalytic properties and eco-friendly synthesis processes compared with traditional catalysts. The use of catalysts in the nano-sized form is an alternative methodology for combining the advantages of heterogeneous and homogeneous catalysis. Various transition metals are used as nanocatalysts, among which the catalytic efficiency of silver-based nanocatalysts has been widely investigated for various organic transformations and the synthesis of fine chemicals and intermediates. This review comprehensively emphasizes the catalytic efficiency and mechanistic aspects of silver-based nanocatalysts in the diverse C–N bond-forming transformations for the synthesis of heterocyclic compounds, with particular focus on N–H/C–X cross-coupling reactions. Moreover, we highlight the role of silver nanocatalysts in enhancing the reaction selectivity, catalytic activity, and sustainability in heterocyclic synthesis. This review provides an insightful strategy for the design of next-generation AgNPs with enhanced catalytic properties.

Introduction

Catalysis occupies a central position in organic chemistry, and nanotechnology and nanoscience are generating significant advances in nano-catalysis, which has clearly emerged as a domain at the interface between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis.1 Using precisely designed nano-catalysts, catalytic reactions and transformations are improved.2 Notably, an aluminum-enhanced graphene oxide nanocomposite was used as an eco-friendly and sustainable catalyst for electrochemical Friedel–crafts reactions.3a Moreover, the use of nano-particles in catalysis is becoming more attractive, and these nano-particles are alternatives to conventional metal catalysts3b–g due to their eco-friendly nature, simplicity, and re-usability as well as extremely high activity and selectivity, low energy consumption and long lifetime.4a

However, the particle size and shape, chemical composition, metal-support interaction, and metal-reactant/solvent interaction can have significant influences on the catalytic properties of nano-catalysts.4b Therefore, the catalytic ability can easily be altered based on the stabilizing agents used during the nano-catalyst preparation, which has increased the popularity of novel nano-catalysts. The last decade has witnessed tremendous applications of different metal nanoparticles as efficient heterogeneous catalysts for a diverse range of chemical reactions, including oxidation,5 reduction,6 hydrogenation,7 epoxidation,8 condensation,9 cyclization,10 cross-coupling,11 and hetero-functionalization, including multicomponent reactions,12 facilitating the production of numerous physiologically and therapeutically important molecules.

In this category, silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) have gained much interest among synthetic chemists due to their valuable properties such as high atom economy, low cost, simple preparation, easy availability, and recyclability. Moreover, the AgNPs have unique chemical, optical, biological, and electronic properties.13,14 Chemical reduction, thermal decomposition, electrochemical, photochemical, microwave, and microbial processes are popular choices for synthesizing AgNPs.15 Moreover, plant extracts are significantly used as reducing or capping agents for silver-based nano-catalysts and gaining popularity due to biologically active secondary metabolites that aid in green synthesis.16

Molecules containing nitrogen, sulphur, and oxygen occupied a prominent position in the area of medicinal chemistry. Among this, heterocyclic compounds with a nitrogen ring are commonly employed in medicine and functional materials.17,18 The construction of the C–N bond has great importance because it opens up nitrogen into organic molecules19 and the synthetic intermediates are essential, highly versatile building blocks in organic synthesis, especially for the production of pharmaceuticals. It is estimated that 80% of marketed drugs contain at least one C–N bond (Fig. 1).20,21 Moreover, top 25 most frequent FDA-approved drugs are nitrogen heterocycles.22 Due to diverse biological activities, the construction of C–N bonds has always been an attractive target to synthetic chemists. The utilization of catalysts for developing novel C–N bond is an art and constitutes a challenging process. Several strategies are available for C–N bonds, including metal- and non-metal-catalyzed reactions, for example, Pd-catalyzed Buchwald–Hartwig amination,23 Cu-catalyzed coupling,24 direct C–H amination,25 and nitrogen-centered radical methods.26 Nowadays, metal-catalyzed C–N cross-coupling reactions have been established as one of the most general protocols. Despite significant advancements, this field has many challenges due to the involvement of harsh reaction conditions, requirement of stoichiometric external oxidants or the use of expensive catalysts in many cases.27–30 Moreover, the reactivity and selectivity of reactive intermediates in the catalyst system are another area of concern.

Nano-catalysis

Nanotechnology and nanoscience emerged in various fields including catalysis (Fig. 2),31 which plays a central role in chemical transformations and lies at the heart of countless chemical protocols, and in the absence of catalysts, most of the organic transformations would not be feasible. Globally, more than 80% of valuable chemicals are made through a catalytic transformation.32 Therefore, designing and developing a suitable catalytic system is very important, and the enzymatic catalysis is the most efficient and greenest catalysis found in nature.33,34 The homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis has its own merits and demerits due to which there is an urgent need for a new catalytic system, which should be active like homogeneous catalysis, and should also be easily recoverable like a heterogeneous catalyst.

Selected progress areas in nanotechnology.

Nano-catalysts have combined advantages of both the homogeneous and heterogeneous catalytic systems. The global market for NPs used in catalytic applications is growing. The nano-catalytic system allows the rapid, selective chemical transformations with excellent product yield. Typically, they are <100 nm in size, which accelerates the chemical reactions by lowering activation energy, offering superior reactivity, selectivity, and stability compared to bulk counterparts.35 Due to their high surface-to-volume ratio (due to nano size), they bridge the gap between homogeneous and heterogeneous catalysis. Generally, nano-catalysts can be classified based on their composition, dimension and origin, primarily categorized as (i) metal nanoparticles/metal oxides; (ii) polymeric or resin nano-catalyst and (iii) carbon-based nanomaterials that include graphic materials. In recent years, several metal and metal oxide nanoparticles/complexes have been utilized for the fabrication of nano-catalysts for various organic transformations, and this highlights several advantages over metal salt-catalyzed reactions. In other words, due to their high surface area/volume ratio with a number of unsaturation sites, tiny metal nano-particles are often preferred over the bulk metal precursors in catalysis. Among various metal-based nanoparticles, AgNPs play an important role in catalysis due to high surface area, stability and the reusability, which has been widely explored in many organic transformations,36 and the progress of research in this field is very rapid.

Nano-particle formation

Silver, known and utilized since ancient times, is a less expensive noble metal and considered as a versatile, sustainable, and powerful tool in organic synthesis.37 Ag(i), combined with oxidants, facilitates efficient C–C and C–N, C–O and C–S bond formation.38–40 Silver can form many different inorganic and organic complexes, and its most stable oxidation states are 0 and +1, although others (+2, +3) exist as well. From the various Ag(i) salts (AgOTf, AgAOc, AgNO3, and AgX), silver nitrate is the most frequently employed precursor for the synthesis of AgNPs,41 due to its high water solubility, cost-effectiveness, and ease of reduction. It acts as the source of silver ions (Ag+) which are reduced to metallic silver (Ag0) under suitable conditions to form AgNPs. In general, NPs can be fabricated by various chemical, physical, and biological methods (Fig. 3),15 each with distinct benefits and challenges.42 An environmentally friendly method for the synthesis of NPs is a very important consideration in this field. Natural reducing agents from plants, bacteria, fungi, and algae are commonly used for the synthesis of NPs. During reduction, the silver ions accept electrons from reducing agents, forming elemental silver atoms that aggregate into nano-sized particles. The nucleation and growth processes determine the shape, size, and morphology of the NPs. Moreover, many AgNPs were found to be supported materials to increase the catalytic property.

Fabrication of AgNPs. [i] Reduction: (conversion of silver ions into metallic silver atoms); [ii] nucleation: formation of small, stable silver nuclei through aggregation of Ag0 atoms and [iii] growth: enlargement of nuclei into AgNPs via further deposition of Ag0 atoms, regulated by capping or stabilizing agents.

Silver nano-catalysed C–N bond formation

Nitrogen constitutes a large family of molecules, and development of various catalytic systems for the formation of C–N bonds is a very important and challenging task for organic chemists, as the final products are widely used in pharmaceuticals, biologically active compounds, and natural products. Metal nanoparticles are currently being employed as catalysts for a number of classical chemical transformations.43 The main advantages of nanoparticle catalysts include (i) high selectivity, (ii) high yield and less byproducts, (iii) high reactivity due to high surface area, (iv) ease of recovery and recyclability, (v) significantly faster reaction rate and (vi) large number of active sites (Fig. 4).

Advantages of nanoparticle catalysts over conventional catalysts. (1) High selectivity, (2) high yield and less byproducts, (3) high reactivity due to high surface area, (4) ease of recovery and recyclability, (5) significantly faster reaction rate and (6) large number of active sites.

Nano-catalysts have demonstrated excellent performance in various organic transformations,43c,d and their utilization in the synthesis of structurally complex natural products remains insufficiently explored. Since many natural products contain essential C–N bonds43e that contribute to their biological activity, the development of nanoparticle-based catalytic systems for selective and efficient C–N bond formation represents an important research direction, due to their excellent catalytic performance, high yields of products, the ability to perform reactions under relatively mild and ligand-free conditions, low toxicity to the environment, and recovery and reusability. In this section, we summarize the recent progress in the fabrication of silver nano-catalysts and their application to the construction of carbon-nitrogen bonds, including heterocycle synthesis, cross-coupling, nitrile hydration, nitro group reduction, and CO2 fixation, with particular emphasis on mechanistic insights and green chemistry principles.

Heterocycle synthesis via C–N bonds

Heterocyclic chemistry is an ever-expanding field without boundaries, and these groups of molecules are essential building blocks in pharmaceutical agents. Top 25 most frequent FDA-approved drugs are nitrogen heterocycles,22 which includes common aromatic heterocyclic compounds such as pyrrole, imidazole, pyrazole, isoxazole, pyridine, and pyrimidine, as well as aliphatic heterocyclic compounds like caprolactam, piperazine, and piperidine.20 Conventional strategies of synthesizing nitrogenous heterocycles rely on hazardous solvents and high energy requirements, which result in a significant amount of waste. In recent years, sustainable chemistry approaches have provided safer and more effective alternatives, which makes them an endless topic for research. Therefore, it is necessary to conduct research towards the synthesis of various heterocyclic compounds using simpler, greener and economical approaches. Using a simple process, multicomponent reactions (MCRs) can increase structural diversity and molecular complexity. As the molecular units become more structurally diverse, MCRs become more valuable. Here, we discuss the silver nano-catalysts for the construction of heterocycles, since most of the C–N bond formation in MCRs leads to biologically important heterocycle scaffolds. This strategy has several advantages over other methods, including reduced number of reaction steps, increased product yields, use of available raw materials, energy efficiency, low cost, ease of using, and a wide range of products. The use of diverse nano-catalysts is one of the sustainable and efficient approaches in organic synthesis, and the main attraction is the recovery and reusability of catalysts.

The utilization or modification of existing methods drives modern science. Jamili and co-workers (2019)44 adopted a previous method45 for a chitosan-supported heterogeneous AgNP catalyst, and they successfully utilized it for the synthesis of imidazole (9) and pyrazine (13) ring systems via the MCR pathway. This catalyst is applicable to the facile synthesis of biologically active molecules. This green and efficient heterogeneous Cs–AgNP system was easily catalyzed by benzylamines (BnNH2) and benzaldehyde (PhCHO) in the presence of NH4OAc (as nitrogen surrogates), affording fully functionalized imidazoles 13 (Scheme 1). Similarly, the reactive diamines (10), ketones (11) and isocyanides (12) could also be used to synthesize the pyrazine (13) derivatives under this same catalytic system. This heterogeneous catalyst showed many advantages such as environmental friendliness, catalyst reusability (4 times), short reaction time (70–100 min), and increased product yield (up to 94%).

Under this catalytic system, the final products are imidazolones (15). The propargylic urea 14 is produced from tosyl isocyanate or phenyl isocyanate in the presence of BM-2%Ag@Al-SBA15. The reaction was carried out at 40–80 °C for 1–20 h. The reaction's exo/endo double-bond changes depend upon the solvent system and their associated reaction conditions (Scheme 2).

Interestingly, a reusable heterogeneous catalyst (recyclable over 5 times) with AgNPs embedded in a porous polymeric framework as a catalytic system for the synthesis of oxazolidinones (19) via chemical fixation of CO2 was developed by Islam and co-workers (Scheme 3).47 The catalysts can be easily recycled to generate the desired oxazolidinone (19) compounds under solvent-free conditions. Moreover, in most instances, high-pressure CO2 has been utilized as part of the process. This approach highlights ambient conditions using noble metals like Ag without the need for supercritical or high-pressure CO2, solvents, or additives. Notably, CO2 fixation occurs in an environment-friendly manner. In 2020, Islam et Al.54 demonstrated the synthesis of AgNPs via an eco-friendly method, and these NPs were successfully incorporated into a covalent organic framework (COF). This stable and versatile catalyst, AgN@COF, can convert CO2 (18) into terminal propargylic amine (32) for the production of 2-oxazolidinones (33). The catalytic efficiency was maintained even after six consecutive catalytic cycles. The Lantana camara leaf extract was used for the synthesis of AgNPs, and the construction of the COF moiety was carried out by the p-TSA catalyst, and AgN@COF was prepared by a dispersion method. Here, Lantana leaf extract was used as a capping and stabilizing agent (Ag+ to Ag0). The pore size of the COF material was 1.72 nm, while the average size of the AgNPs was 20–35 nm. This catalyst efficiently catalyzed the carboxylative cyclization of terminal propargylic amines 32 (Scheme 5). The proposed plausible mechanism is very interesting. Initially, the catalyst reacts with the base 20 to form the adduct 34. Here, the catalyst coordinates to the alkyne bond of the terminal propargyl amine 32. Furthermore, the base molecules released from intermediate-I (35) activate CO2via the zwitterionic adduct. Later, this adduct reacts with intermediate 35 to form another intermediate-II (36) by incorporating CO2 (18) into the N–H bond of the propargyl amine 32. Next, intramolecular nucleophilic cyclization converts alkynyl-AgN@COF into intermediate-III (37). Finally, the desired product was provided by the proton-demetalation of oxazolidinone intermediate 37.

Five-membered nitrogen-containing heterocycles, particularly imidazoles and tetrazoles, have been shown to offer therapeutic potential and serve as a unique basis in drug discovery.58,59 Interestingly, Bala and co-workers60 developed a facile synthesis strategy to couple porous nanocube (NCs) of CeO2 with AgNPs for the click reaction (Scheme 7). The NCs were synthesized through a hot injection method, and the surface of the NCs was modified with 2,4-dimethylphenol (DMP). The reducing and capping abilities of DMP to form AgNPs were explored to form these noble MNPs over a porous CeO2 NC support at room temperature. The CeO2–Ag nanocomposite was found to have enhanced catalytic activities in the click reaction as compared to unsupported AgNPs and porous CeO2 NCs. This one-pot synthetic system has many advantages over other varieties of catalysts.61–65 Notably, this system provided higher yields within a shorter reaction time and the reactions were conducted in water at room temperature. Moreover, the successive recyclable performance of the catalyst was found to be five times.

The transition metal/complex catalysts for the synthesis of tetrazoles have been extensively studied in the past.69–72 Satish and co-workers (2014),73 reported that heterogeneous AgNPs catalyzed the one-pot synthesis of 1H-tetrazoles 59. Previously, this methodology was established using 10 mol% AgNO3.69 In this work, they reported the first attempt using AgNPs for the synthesis of tetrazoles, via the [3 + 2] cycloaddition of nitriles with sodium azide (46). This method has no significant advantage over the AgNO3-catalyzed reaction except for the reusability of AgNPs. The AgNPs were prepared using an established method74 which involved reducing AgNO3 with NaBH4 under mild conditions. A characteristic absorption band at 399 nm was observed, confirming the formation of AgNPs. The average size of the nanoparticles was found to be 40–42 nm, and they exhibited no aggregation. Furthermore, the HRTEM image of the AgNPs revealed an interplanar spacing of approximately 0.96 nm. After successful characterization, the AgNPs were employed as an efficient and convenient catalyst for the one-pot synthesis of 5-substituted 1H-tetrazoles 59. The reaction conditions were optimized across various solvents, temperatures, and catalyst loading percentages. The nature of the solvents significantly influenced the reaction, with the best isolated yield achieved using 20 mol% AgNPs as a catalyst in the DMF system at 120 °C. A total of thirteen derivatives was synthesized from various substituted aryl and heteroaryl nitriles 58b, yielding excellent results. It was observed that electron-donating groups tended to provide slightly lower yields in comparison to electron-withdrawing groups. Additionally, this methodology was applicable to the transformation of benzimidazole, which bore an additional tetrazole moiety. The proposed reaction mechanism is illustrated in Scheme 9. In this context, AgNPs acted as Lewis acids, enhancing the electrophilic characteristic of the aromatic nitrile 58 group through complexation 60, which subsequently reacted with azide to form substituted tetrazoles 59.

A six-membered ring with at least one nitrogen atom served as the basic building block in many medicines, natural products, and agrochemicals.75 Sami's group successfully synthesized a new and reusable silver immobilized on hydroxyapatite-core-shell γ-Fe2O3 NPs (γ-Fe2O3@HAp-Ag) in 2017.76 A detailed representation of the catalyst synthesis was depicted at the end of Scheme 10. This Lewis acid magnetic nanocatalyst was used for an atom-economical, very facile and eco-friendly procedure for the synthesis of highly functional tetrahydropyridine derivatives 64. Compared with existing methods,77–79 this catalytic system has several advantages, mainly that the products are obtained in good and high yields within a short reaction time, and the easy recoverability and reusability of the catalyst is up to six times. Moreover, this reaction is easy to operate, avoids toxic reagents and solvents, and occurs under mild reaction conditions. The reaction mechanism is intermolecular Mannich-type, catalyzed by γ-Fe2O3@HAp-Ag, which involves β-enaminone (65) and Schiff base (66, imine) intermediates.

Recently, Singh and co-workers (2023) have developed a magnetic silver-based nanocatalyst, Fe3O4@SiO2-Ag, for the synthesis of quinoline derivatives (Scheme 11).80 The AgNPs are immobilized on Fe3O4, so that they can be easily recovered from the reaction mixture. The preparation of catalyst involves a two-step process: first is the synthesis of Fe3O4@SiO2 particles and second is AgNP loading or composite preparation. The Fe3O4 NPs are almost spherical in shape with a diameter of about 20 nm. The catalytic model reaction system was constructed using aldehyde (62), amine (70), and 1,3-indanedione (71) in a solvent of water and ethanol at 60 °C. The desired product quinoline was obtained in excellent yields within a short time. Then, 25 mg of Fe3O4@SiO2-Ag was used for each reaction. The catalyst was successfully recycled six times in succession, with little loss in activity. In this way, the product was easily obtained by filtering the reaction mixture, washing with ethanol, ethyl acetate, and chloroform, and purifying. The separation of nanocomposites from the solution using magnetic force gives the catalyst the advantages of reusability, saves money from an economic perspective, and prevents the introduction of iron and silver compounds.

Fe3O4@SiO2-Ag-catalyzed synthesis of quinolines.

Maleki and colleagues (2016)81 proposed magnetic cellulose/Ag as a novel eco-friendly nano-bio-composite (Scheme 12). Here, the magnetic cellulose/Ag nano-bio-composite consisted of γ-Fe2O3 NPs supported on cellulose layers and AgNPs immobilized on the matrix surface. The average size of AgNPs was 27.56 nm. This magnetic heterogeneous catalyst was successfully used for the synthesis of chromene-linked nicotinonitrile derivatives 76. Ammonium acetate (75) is a nitrogen source, which is attached to the keto group of acetylcoumarin (73), and the reaction was improved by imine-enamine tautomerization. Under optimized conditions, different benzaldehydes 62 substituted with electron-donating or electron-withdrawing groups gave the corresponding products in good to excellent yields. Notably, aldehydes 62 bearing electron-withdrawing substituents underwent the reaction faster. This approach has many advantages of low-cost catalysts, catalyst reusability (up to five times), easy separation of the catalyst from the reaction mixture, environmentally friendly procedure, short reaction time, avoidance of hazardous organic solvents, and high yields.

Designing a simple and practical protocol for the green synthesis of heterocycles is indeed a fascinating challenge in modern organic chemistry. To achieve an eco-friendly and sustainable methodology, chemists rely on specific green chemistry principles to maximize the atom economy, reduce hazardous waste, and lower energy consumption. In another remarkably study by Dandia et al. (2015),87 a straightforward process for AgNP decoration with reduced graphene oxide (AgNPs/rGO) composites through an innovative chemical route was presented. The particle crystallite size of Ag NPs in the Ag NP/rGO composite was estimated to be 20 nm. The AgNP/rGO composite possesses assets of both AgNPs and graphene-based materials, thus providing enhanced surface acidity with good dispersion capability that is usually lacking in AgNPs alone. This nanocomposite can be easily recovered via simple filtration and can be reused up to seven times without any significant loss in catalytic activity. Furthermore, the efficiency of the composite increased with microwave irradiation compared to traditional techniques. The plausible mechanism is described in Scheme 15. Here, the AgNP/rGO composite served as an acidic catalyst, and the mechanism was initiated with the formation of enaminone 95. Further, enaminone reacts with isatin to give an intermediate 97. The loss of an H2O molecule from the intermediate 97 gives a new intermediate 98. The amidic C–N bond cleavage or ring-opening sequence of isatin leads to another intermediate 99. Finally, the cyclization reaction leads to the formation of desired products 94.

In order to explore the generality and scope of nano-catalyst, Safari and Ravandi (2014)88 proposed silver-decorated multi-walled carbon nanotubes as heterogeneous catalysts for the synthesis of quinazolinones 101 from the sonication of anthranilamide 100 and an aldehyde 62 (Scheme 16). The Ag-CNT catalyst was compared with other catalysts reported in the literature for the synthesis of quinazolinones;89–93 this method was more convenient and easily controlled. The advantages of ultrasound procedures consist of good yields, short reaction times, high selectivity and mild reaction conditions. Here, the electrophilicity of the carbonyl group in aldehyde 62 has occurred using Ag-CNTs. The intermediate 103 was formed by the reaction of 2-aminobenzamide (100) and activated aldehyde 102. Later, dehydration of intermediate 103 affords another key intermediate 104. Further, 1,5 hydrogen atom transfer affords desired products 101. This catalytic system will be useful to design quinazolinone-related bioactive molecules.

Ag-CNT-catalyzed synthesis of quinazolinones.

In another study, Sadjadi and colleagues (2017) designed the Ag@CDNS-SBA-15 heterologous catalyst for the synthesis of benzopyranopyrimidine derivatives under ultrasonic irradiation.94 The core SBA-15/cyclodextrin nano-sponge adduct was synthesized by the reaction of Cl-functionalized SBA-15 and amine-functionalized cyclodextrin nanosponge (CDNS), which was used for the immobilization of AgNPs encapsulated with cannabis extract (bio-based approach). The catalyst contains a very low silver loading (less than 0.5 wt%) and can be considered as an efficient catalyst with an efficiency higher or comparable to classic toxic and homogeneous catalysts. Moreover, compared to the previously reported catalysts,95–98 Ag@CDNS-SBA-15 led to higher or comparable yields, and the recyclability studies indicated that the catalyst was reusable and could be recovered and reused for up to four reactions. The reaction process was initiated by the activation of aldehyde 62 by the catalyst and subsequent Knoevenagel condensation reaction with urea (108a) to form an imine intermediate 112. Further, addition with 4-hydroxycumarin (107), followed by cyclization, furnished the final product 109 (Scheme 17).

In order to explore their expertise in catalysis, Rout et al. (2019)99a developed a core–shell bimetallic Cu@Ag nanocatalyst via a two-step thermal decomposition and galvanic displacement reaction. A plausible mechanism involving (i) carbonyl oxygen activation of benzaldehyde 62via Cu@Ag, (ii) Knoevenagel condensation (iii) Michael addition, (iv) cyclization, and (v) catalyst regeneration. This catalyst has high thermal stability and recyclability, and is magnetic in nature such that it can be easily separated with the help of an external magnet. The Knoevenagel condensation between a molecule of salicylaldehyde (121) and malononitrile (74) is the starting point of the reaction, leading to the formation of intermediate 125. The attack of malononitrile on salicylaldehyde is facilitated by AgNPs via electrophilic activation of the carbonyl oxygen of salicylaldehyde (121). Furthermore, the nucleophilic attack on the cyano group of intermediate 125 gave intermediate 126. Finally, the intermediate 126 reacts with another molecule of activated salicylaldehyde 128, which is activated by the catalyst to generate a cyclized intermediate 129, followed by proton transfer to afford the final product 123. Considering the recently reported method for the preparation of benzopyranopyrimidine 123 derivatives, the product formation takes place over a long reaction time, and most of the reported catalysts were homogeneous and lacked recyclability.105–108 Therefore, this catalytic system offers significant advantages in terms of excellent catalytic activity, green catalysis, short reaction time, and excellent recyclability with desirable yields.

Among the various types of AgNPs discussed above with their catalytic properties, phyto-fabricated NPs have gained more interest because they utilize plant extracts as environmentally friendly, non-toxic reducing and capping agents, avoiding the hazardous chemicals used in traditional chemical synthesis methods. Plants are rich sources of diverse secondary metabolites,111 which possess the ability to reduce metal ions into their corresponding nanoparticles (NPs) through eco-friendly and sustainable bio-reduction processes. There are limited reports available for phyto-fabricated AgNPs utilized as heterogeneous catalysts in organic reactions, especially for the C–N bond. Notably, Balwe et al. This method offers several advantages, including an eco-friendly catalyst preparation, a one-pot, solvent-free, three-component reaction, straightforward operation, rapid reaction rates, and the catalyst's reusability. The characteristic peak of AgNPs was detected at 430 nm, revealing monodisperse nanoparticles with spherical and oval shapes and an average particle size ranging from 10 to 35 nm. Furthermore, the X-ray diffraction pattern confirmed the crystalline nature of the AgNPs. The catalytic activity of AgNPs for the A3-coupling reaction was investigated using 3-aminoindazoles 135, an aldehyde 62, and ethynylbenzene 48. Initially, the researchers explored the influence of various solvents on the model reaction employing 0.5 mol% AgNP catalyst under reflux conditions. The desired product 136 was achieved with a 96% yield under solvent-free conditions using the same catalyst concentration. This transformation is broadly applicable across a variety of aldehydes 62, facilitating straightforward access to substituted pyrimido[1,2-b]indazole 135 derivatives with good to excellent yields. The electronic effect of the substrate did not significantly influence the overall yields. Notably, aliphatic alkynes such as n-hexane and n-pentyne failed to yield the desired products. Interestingly, it was observed that the catalyst system could be reused for up to three consecutive runs. Overall, they synthesized 31 diverse and desirable products, successfully demonstrating the large-scale synthetic utility of this approach.

Cross coupling via C–N bonds

The A3 coupling reaction is well known for its higher atom economy to form C–C and C–N bonds via a one-pot process, and the output of the reaction is highly functionalized propargylamine 157. These are main intermediates and building blocks for many pharmaceutically active compounds.117–119 The traditional methods for the synthesis of propargylamine need multiple steps, toxic reagents, and harsh reaction conditions. The green chemistry-aligned one-pot synthesis of propargylamine via nano-catalysis is more attractive. Several approaches are introduced in this field, the details of which are given below.

Sun and co-workers (2012)127 utilized a silver oxide nanoparticle (Ag2ONPs) for one-pot A3 coupling reactions. The Ag2ONPs were readily prepared according to the reported procedure.120,121 The optimal catalyst amount was 5 mol%, and this efficient and recyclable silver oxide nanoparticle catalyst worked at room temperature in air without additional co-catalysts or ligands. Moreover, the Ag2ONPs could be recycled at least 6 times without any significant decrease in yields. This system is more suitable for aliphatic cyclic aldehydes 62 (Scheme 26). The simple preparation and reusability of the catalyst, which show significant advantages, make this reaction more economical and environmentally acceptable. Later, Salam and colleagues66 synthesized a graphene-based composite with silver nanoparticles via a simple chemical method, and this Ag-graphene (Ag-G) nano-catalyst was used for A3 coupling. The main advantages of this catalyst are that it is air-stable, inexpensive, easy to prepare, and can be easily recovered and reused five times without any significant loss in activity and selectivity. The details are illustrated in Scheme 26.

Ag2ONP- and Ag-G-catalyzed A3 coupling.

Javad and co-workers (2013)122 introduced silver iodide nanoparticles (AgINPs) as efficient and reusable catalysts for the one-pot synthesis of 3-amino-substituted benzofurans 159 under aqueous conditions (Scheme 27). The reaction yields are excellent, and biologically important secondary amines such as morpholine 122a and piperidine 122b have been successfully used under optimized conditions. The main advantages of this method are short reaction times and low catalyst loading. Spherical NPs with particle sizes of 45–50 nm are obtained, and they are synthesized from AgNO3 treated with KI. Here, AgI NPs promote the formation of a silver acetylide intermediate 161 from the alkyne 48, which can be easily coupled to the iminium 160 prepared in situ by the reaction between salicylaldehyde 158 and the amine 122. Furthermore, AgI NPs act as a Lewis acid and lead to the activation of the triple bond to promote the cyclization reaction via nucleophilic attack of the hydroxyl group 162. After the course of the reaction, the AgI NPs release the catalyst for further reaction.

AgINP-catalyzed synthesis of 3-amino-substituted benzofurans.

Notably, Sadjadi et al. (2017)123 reported a novel magnetic hybrid system for the synthesis of propargylamines 157 and benzo[b]furans 159. The hybrid system consists of nano-magnetic Fe2O3 hollow spheres, a silica shell, [pmim]Cl ionic liquid, and AgNPs. Interestingly, AgNPs were prepared via a biosynthesis method, and Achillea millefolium flower extract was used as a reducing and stabilizing agent. This hybrid catalyst was successfully used as an efficient and reusable catalyst to promote green ultrasonically assisted A3 and KA2 coupling reactions and benzo[b]furan 159 synthesis (Scheme 28). The main advantages of this catalytic system were the use of aqueous medium, high yield, short reaction time, broad substrate tolerance, and low catalyst requirement. This mechanism involves the initial activation of the terminal C–H bond of acetylene 165 and the formation of iminium ion 168 through the reaction of amine 122 and aldehyde 158 by h-Fe2O3@SiO2-IL/Ag. Further, the iminium ion 168 undergoes a reaction with silver acetylide 165 to afford the corresponding propargylamine 157 as well as h-Fe2O3@SiO2-IL/Ag.

Arylamines are essential intermediates for the production of a wide variety of commercial and industrial products. One of the main strategies for the preparation of aryl amines is the conversion of an aromatic nitro (NO2) or nitrile (CN) group to an amine. Traditionally, nitro compounds are reduced by high-pressure hydrogenation.147 Several silver-based nano catalysts have been reported as efficient catalysts for the reduction of various aryl nitro compounds to the corresponding products.148–158 Interestingly, mesoporous titania-supported silver nanoparticles (Ag/MTA) effectively catalysed the reduction of nitroarenes into the corresponding aryl amines 45 and N-aryl hydroxylamines 199 with NaBH4 and NH3BH3, respectively.159 Further, 10 mg of catalyst Ag/MTA (4%) was found to be more effective for chemoselective reduction (Scheme 40). The mesoporous TiO2 nanoparticle assemblies (MTA) were prepared based on the previous method.160 After that, different amounts of AgNPs were loaded into MTA. The nitroarenes 194 bearing electron-withdrawing groups reduced faster than those with electron-donating groups.

Ag/MTA-catalyzed chemoselective reduction of nitroarenes.

Notably, Laikakis and co-workers161 investigated the catalytic efficiency of silica-supported AgNPs for the chemoselective reduction of amino acid-substituted derivatives or nitrotetrazoles to the corresponding amines using NaBH4 as the reducing agent (Scheme 41). AgNP-catalyzed reduction of aromatic nitro compounds to amines.

Usually, the hydration of heteroaromatic nitriles is more difficult and the reaction rates are much lower than those of common nitriles because of their strong coordination to the metal centres. Kaneda and co-workers (2009)87 introduced the AgNP catalyst for the selective hydration of nitriles 213 to amides 214 in water (Scheme 43). Hydroxyapatite-supported silver nanoparticles (AgHAP) acted as highly efficient reusable heterogeneous catalysts. This catalyst was efficient for the hydration of nitriles 213, except for aliphatic ones. Moreover, the substituents bearing electron-withdrawing groups were more reactive than those bearing electron-donating groups. The catalyst can be easily separated by centrifugation and could be reused four times without any loss of its catalytic activity and selectivity. The plausible mechanism involves the dual activation of cyano and aromatic groups by AgHAP and the final attack via an iminol transition state.

Conclusion and future perspectives

Nitrogen chemistry has emerged as an ever-expanding domain in organic synthesis owing to the structural diversity and broad prevalence of nitrogen-containing compounds, such as amines, amides, imines, azides, nitriles, and nitrogen heterocycles, which play pivotal roles in pharmaceuticals. The formation of C–N bonds is of great importance, as it opens up pathways for the introduction of nitrogen into organic molecules. Transition metals are well known for C–N catalysis (Ullmann, Chan–Lam and Buchwald–Hartwig), and many C–N bond-forming strategies have been developed in this field,177–179 which face many challenges, including catalyst stability, selectivity, recyclability, and harsh reaction conditions. Recently, several metal-based nanoparticles (MNPs) have been widely used as catalysts in various organic transformations180,181 such as coupling, reduction, and multicomponent reactions, and most of these strategies are low-cost and sustainable methodologies for the formation of C–N bonds.

Silver catalysts are efficient for many organic transformations through a various mechanistic pathway. Over the past decades, significant advances have been made in the fabrication of a silver-based nano catalyst (AgNC) as an active heterogeneous catalyst. This review mainly focused on the catalytic efficiency and mechanistic aspects of AgNCs in diverse C–N bond-forming transformations for the synthesis of heterocyclic compounds, with particular focus on N–H/C–X cross-coupling reactions. The catalytic properties (efficiency, selectivity, and recyclability) of AgNCs depend on the size, shape, composition, and their assembly. From the literature, it was found that the AgNC has a very small particle size, various morphological features, and most of the AgNC support by various composite, bimetallic, and magnetic materials, which are synthesized through various chemical and physical approaches. One of the most important aspects of green chemistry is reusable nano catalysts. Notably, N-heterocyclic carbene-protected AgNPs immobilized on PANF were found to be efficient catalysts for AHA coupling. The recyclability of catalyst was eight times without any loss of significant activity. Likewise, several composites such as CNT,88,126 graphene,66,87 C3N3-Alginate,67 and core–shell,76 supported AgNC are showed efficient catalytic properties and reused several times without any loss of its catalytic activity.

Combining AgNC with metals such as Cu, Pd, Ni, Ti, Al, and Fe would be highly desirable because the synergistic effects between the metals accelerate the electron flow from the catalyst surface to the reactants, leading to the development of efficient nano catalysts. From this study, it was revealed that most of the reported nano catalysts for C–N cross-coupling reactions are monometallic catalysts, and there are very few reports on bimetallic catalysts. In recent decades, several silver-based bimetallic nano catalysts have been reported for a variety of organic transformations.182–185 Therefore, we can adopt or modify such bimetallic nano catalysts for efficient C–N bond formation reactions in the future. Furthermore, the incorporation of magnetic properties into AgNC facilitates easy recovery of the nano-catalyst from the reaction medium by applying an external magnetic field. This facile magnetic separation significantly improves catalyst recovery, recyclability, and sustainability while minimizing the catalyst loss and post-reaction purification steps. Finally, to prepare nano-catalysts for efficient catalytic properties, it is necessary to control the specific size and well-defined surface structure of NCs.

Furthermore, phytofabricated AgNPs have emerged as promising green catalysts for C–N bond formation, and their catalytic properties depend on the particle size and the presence of bioactive phytochemicals adsorbed on their surface. There are limited reports on phytofabricated AgNP-catalyzed C–N bond formation. Notably, AgNPs mediated by L. barbarum fruit extract were found to be efficient catalysts for the synthesis of pyrimido[1,2-b]indazole derivatives, and the recyclability of the catalyst increased sevenfold without any significant loss in its efficiency. A detailed comparison of phytofabricated AgNPs is shown in Fig. 5. Despite their good catalytic properties, phytofabricated AgNPs have several drawbacks, including poor reproducibility, limited control over particle size and morphology, batch-to-batch variations due to differences in phytochemical composition, purification difficulties, and challenges in large-scale production. In this scenario, we believe that the use of single or combination of phyto-constituents (like curcumin, quercetin, and gallic acids) for the fabrication of AgNPs overcomes the above-mentioned drawbacks. From this perspective, these studies may provide further insights into a stable and efficient silver nano-catalyst for C–N bond formation in organic synthesis.
