Streamlined synthetic regulatory cassette for efficient and photoreceptor-enriched retinal gene expression
Authors: Jae-Eun Kwon, Ye Ji Kim, Seung-Hyun Kim, Ok-Seon Kwon, Hyeon-Ki Jang, Yong-min Choi, Hyeon-Jin Na, Namshik Han, Yibin Chun, Jung Woo Han, Tae Kwann Park, Kyung-Sook Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) are major causes of vision loss and often associated with the degeneration of retinal neurons, such as photoreceptors. Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors hold therapeutic potential for IRDs; however, their off-target expression has prompted the development of refined engineering strategies to enhance cell-type preference without compromising transgene expression. Here, we present G5mP, a streamlined 315-bp synthetic promoter-enhancer construct designed for robust retinal gene expression. G5mP integrates three components: G5, a G protein-coupled receptor kinase 1-derived enhancer; mP, a minimal promoter from phosphodiesterase 6B; and a 5′-untranslated region (UTR) derived from retinoschisin 1 (RS1). Compared with the clinically used 742-bp RIR cassette—comprising the RS1 promoter, interphotoreceptor retinoid-binding protein enhancer, and RS1-derived 5′-UTR—G5mP drove stronger overall retinal expression and showed enhanced activity within photoreceptor cells in retinal cell lines, human retinal organoids, and mouse retina without inducing detectable cytotoxicity in vivo. Notably, across retinal cell lines, human retinal organoids, and mouse retina, G5mP induced more robust and distinct photoreceptor-preferential transgene expression than the ubiquitous CAG promoter did. These results highlight its potential as a compact and efficient regulatory element suitable for AAV-mediated gene delivery across retinal cell types, including the effective targeting of photoreceptors.

Introduction

Inherited retinal diseases (IRDs) are a diverse group of genetic disorders that cause progressive vision loss largely driven by degeneration of the retina.1,2 Among these, retinitis pigmentosa (RP) and cone-rod dystrophy are the most prevalent IRD subtypes, comprising the majority of reported cases.3 As IRDs involve progressive photoreceptor damage, gene therapy targeting these cells offers a promising therapeutic approach to address their underlying genetic causes.4,5

Adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors have shown clinical and commercial potential in retinal gene therapy, exemplified by voretigene neparvovec-rzyl (Luxturna), the first and only FDA-approved AAV gene therapy for an inherited retinal disease.6,7,8 While Luxturna has demonstrated clinical proof of concept for AAV-mediated retinal gene therapy using a broadly active CAG to drive transgene expression in retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), post-marketing data have noted adverse effects, including retinal atrophy following vector administration.9,10,11 Although the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely understood, broadly active promoters, such as chicken beta-acin (CBA), cytomegalovirus (CMV), and short CAG (sCAG) drive transgene expression across multiple retinal cell layers following ocular AAV delivery—including non-target cells such as Müller glia and astrocytes12—whereas photoreceptor-specific promoters remain well tolerated even at high doses.13 These findings suggest that restricting transgene expression to target cell types is a key determinant of the retinal safety profile, underscoring the unmet need for highly selective regulatory elements in retinal gene therapy.

Beyond cell-type preference, AAV-based gene delivery faces additional technical challenges, including the limited packaging capacity of the vector—particularly restrictive for self-complementary AAVs (∼2.3 kb)14,15—as well as potential immune responses to AAV capsids16,17 and physical barriers, such as the blood-retinal barrier and inner limiting membrane.18 Engineering compact promoter-enhancer cassettes with strong, cell-type-preferred activity are, therefore, essential to maximize packaging efficiency, enhance therapeutic precision, and reduce immunotoxicity.

In this study, we developed G5mP, a streamlined 315-bp synthetic promoter-enhancer module composed of a G protein-coupled receptor kinase 1 (GRK1)-derived enhancer (G5), minimal phosphodiesterase 6 B (PDE6B) promoter (mP), and 5′-untranslated region (UTR) derived from retinoschisin 1 (RS1), which was identified through stepwise screening of enhancer-promoter–5′-UTR combinations. G5mP was optimized as a compact and potent regulatory element that drives robust and photoreceptor-enriched transgene expression in retinal cell lines, human retinal organoids, and mouse retina. G5mP represents a broadly applicable regulatory element for gene therapy targeting photoreceptor-associated retinal diseases, including RP, cone-rod dystrophy, and Leber congenital amaurosis.

With its compact size, strong activity, and enhanced specificity, G5mP is a versatile regulatory module compatible not only with AAV vectors but also with a range of emerging therapeutic platforms, including nonviral vectors, mRNA, and DNA-based delivery systems.

Results

Selection and validation of minimal promoters for photoreceptor-enriched expression

Seven genes highly enriched in retinal tissues and photoreceptors (rods and cones) were selected from the Human Protein Atlas database (https://www.proteinatlas.org) to identify promoter candidates capable of driving photoreceptor-preferred transgene expression19,20 (Figure S1). The selected genes included PDE6B, GRK1, RS1, guanylate cyclase 2 F (GUCY2F), ATP-binding cassette subfamily A member 4 (ABCA4), interphotoreceptor retinoid-binding protein (IRBP), and rhodopsin (RHO). The promoter regions of these genes were assessed using a Gaussia luciferase (Gluc) reporter system and co-transfected with a firefly luciferase (Fluc) internal control into six representative cell lines. These included five retinal-derived cell lines—661 W (mouse cone photoreceptor-like), Y79, WERI-RB1 (human retinoblastoma), rMC-1 (rat Müller glia), and ARPE-19 (human retinal pigment epithelium)—and one non-retinal cell line, HEK293FT (human embryonic kidney) (Figure 1A).Figure 1Identification and optimization of retinal-specific promoters for enhanced transcriptional efficiency(A) Schematic of the dual-luciferase reporter constructs comparing retinal-specific promoter activity. In this study, a photoreceptor-enriched promoter derived from RS1 was used as a clinically relevant baseline owing to its prior application in AAV-based gene therapy for X-linked retinoschisis (XLRS; ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02317887).21,22 Among the seven candidate promoters tested, the PDE6B promoter consistently induced the strongest levels of Gluc expression across all tested cell lines, outperforming the RS1 promoter (Figure 1B). In 661 W photoreceptor-like cells, the PDE6B promoter induced a 32.6-fold increase in Gluc expression compared with that of RS1, whereas GRK1 showed a 3.4-fold increase. A similar trend was observed in other retinal and non-retinal models. In ARPE-19 cells, PDE6B and GRK1 exhibited 15- and 6.7-fold increases in expression, respectively. In Y79 and WERI-RB1 cells, the observed increases were modest, with PDE6B showing only 1.1–1.5-fold changes and GRK1 showing 0.4–1.3-fold changes relative to that of RS1. In non-retinal HEK293FT cells, PDE6B and GRK1 yielded 15.6- and 1.5-fold enhancements, respectively. The largest differential was observed in rMC-1 cells, where PDE6B induced a marked 67-fold increase relative to that of RS1, whereas GRK1 resulted in a 6.7-fold elevation. In contrast, other promoters, such as IRBP, ABCA4, and RHO, exhibited only modest or limited activity, and GUCY2F consistently showed the weakest expression across all evaluated cell lines. Cell-type selectivity was further assessed by evaluating the same panel of promoters in four additional non-retinal human cell lines—DLD-1 (colorectal adenocarcinoma), Hep3B (hepatocellular carcinoma), HeLa (cervical adenocarcinoma), and SNU216 (gastric carcinoma). Across all non-retinal cell lines tested, promoter activity was substantially lower than that observed in ARPE-19 cells, with relative expression ranging from approximately 2%–55% of ARPE-19 levels (Figure S2). These results indicate that the evaluated promoters exhibit preferential activity in retinal-derived cells over diverse non-retinal cell types.

To generate compact promoter elements suitable for AAV packaging constraints, truncated “minimal” versions of the GRK1 and PDE6B promoters were engineered by reducing them to the shortest regions known to retain transcriptional activity23,24 (Figure 1C). The resulting minimal GRK1 (mG) and PDE6B (mP) promoters corresponded to the −215 to −1 and −114 to −1 regions upstream of their respective transcription start sites. When tested for their functional activity, both minimal promoters outperformed their full-length versions across multiple retinal and non-retinal cell types (Figure 1D). In 661 W cells, mG and mP increased reporter expression by 6.9- and 2.4-fold, respectively. Similar increases were observed in Y79 (5.4-fold for mG and 1.94-fold for mP) and HEK293FT cells (7.5-fold for mG and 2.0-fold for mP). Although mG showed a higher fold increase than that of its full-length promoter, mP consistently drove superior absolute expression levels (Figure 1D). These results demonstrated that minimal promoter engineering can yield compact regulatory elements that preserve or enhance transcriptional activity, supporting their utility as foundational components for developing artificial synthetic promoters.

Optimization of photoreceptor-selective vector construction with promoter and 5′-UTR combinations

Efficient and photoreceptor-enriched transgene expression relies on carefully optimizing promoter and 5′-UTR combinations, as 5′-UTRs influence mRNA stability and translational efficiency.25,26,27 This study evaluated the performance of two previously identified minimal promoters, mG and mP, combined with their respective 5′-UTRs and a reference 5′-UTR derived from RS1. As a benchmark construct, the RIR RU vector, originally used in the AAV8-hRS1 vector for preclinical and clinical XLRS studies (ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT02317887), was employed.21,22 This construct is composed of an 5′-RS1 promoter, IRBP enhancer, 3′-RS1 promoter (collectively referred to as RIR), and a 40-bp RS1-derived 5′-UTR (RU) (Figure 2A). To improve compactness and transgene expression, the 3′-RS1 promoter in RIR was replaced with mG or mP, generating the RImG and RImP constructs, respectively. In all three retinal cell lines, RImG paired with RU induced significantly higher Gluc expression than that of the RIR RU control, with fold increases of 6.0 in 661 W, 2.3 in Y79, and 2.8 in WERI-RB1 cells (Figure 2E). Replacing RU with either GU1 or GU2 reduced expression in all cell lines, particularly in Y79 and WERI-RB1 cells. A similar pattern was observed with RImP: RU consistently supported the highest expression, yielding 11.4-, 10.4-, and 12.2-fold increases over those measured for RIR RU in 661 W, Y79, and WERI-RB1 cells, respectively (Figure 2F). In contrast, the PU variant showed significantly reduced or abolished expression in Y79 and WERI-RB1 cells. Consequently, the RU element was selected as the default 5′-UTR for all subsequent enhancer–promoter combinations owing to its superior performance across promoters and cell types.

Evaluation and optimization of enhancers derived from the retina-specific genes, GRK1 and PDE6B

Optimizing enhancer elements is essential for achieving robust and specific gene expression in photoreceptor-targeted gene therapy.29,30,31 To identify compact and effective enhancers, candidates derived from two retina-specific genes, GRK1 and PDE6B, were screened. Six GRK1-derived (G1–G6; Figure 3A) and two PDE6B-derived enhancers (P1 and P2; Figure S3A) were selected based on their proximity to the transcription start site (±600 kb) and compact size (≤600 bp), as curated from the GeneCards database (https://www.genecards.org/).32,33 Each enhancer was placed upstream of either the mG or mP promoter to generate various enhancer-promoter combinations. To understand molecular features underlying the strong activity of RG5mP and guide further optimization, the transcription factor (TF) motif content of its three core components—RS1 5′-promoter, G5 enhancer, and mP 3′-promoter—was analyzed, and their potential influence on compactness and transcriptional efficiency assessed. Motif analysis revealed that the RS1 5′-promoter harbors substantially fewer TF-binding sites compared with those of G5 and mP (Figures S4A and S4B).34 Annotation of detected motifs using Human Protein Atlas retinal expression data and TRRUST-curated regulatory relationships showed that G5 is enriched for binding motifs of retina-relevant activating TFs, most notably SP1 and SP4—TF with established roles in activating photoreceptor-specific gene promoters including rod opsin35—as well as RXRG, a cone-enriched nuclear receptor involved in cone photoreceptor subtype specification.36 Similarly, mP contained motifs for MEIS2, a TF upregulated during early cone photoreceptor development.37 In contrast, while the RS1 5′-promoter fragment harbors motifs for retina-expressed TFs such as ESRRB and RORA, the overall density of retina-relevant activating TF motifs was substantially lower than that of G5 or mP. DNA secondary structure analysis further indicated that constructs containing the RS1 5′-promoter (RIR) adopted a more stable and rigid conformation, showing increased folding height and overlapping mountain plot traces. In contrast, constructs lacking the RS1 5′-promoter (IR) exhibited more relaxed and flexible structural profiles (Figure S4C).38

These findings led to the hypothesis that the RS1 5′-promoter may interfere with downstream promoter function. To directly test this hypothesis, constructs with and without the RS1 5′-promoter were compared across four cell lines using luciferase assays (Figure 3C). Removal of the RS1 5′-promoter (IR vs. RIR) resulted in 3.3- and 3.7-fold increases in Y79 and HEK293FT cells, respectively, whereas 661 W and rMC-1 cells showed no significant change. Substituting the RS1 3′-promoter with mP (ImP vs. IR) further enhanced expression in all cell lines—12.0-fold in 661 W, 4.6-fold in Y79, 12.0-fold in rMC-1, and 24.7-fold in HEK293FT. Addition of the G5 enhancer (G5mP vs. ImP) yielded further gains: 3.3-fold in 661 W, 1.6-fold in Y79, 7.8-fold in rMC-1, and 8.0-fold in HEK293FT. Based on its potential inhibitory effects, the RS1 5′-promoter was excluded from subsequent evaluations of PDE6B-derived enhancers. Under this optimized promoter context, P1 exhibited minimal activity across all cell lines (Figure S3B). P2 showed moderate activity when paired with mG (P2mG), yielding a 3.0-fold increase over that of the IRBP enhancer (ImG) in 661 W and modest gains in HEK293FT. However, this advantage diminished when P2 was paired with mP (P2mP), as it did not outperform ImP under any tested condition. When directly compared, P2mG was markedly inferior to G5mP, with G5mP exhibiting a 35-fold increase in HEK293FT and 1.4-fold increase in 661 W (Figure S3C).

Collectively, these results establish G5mP as the most compact regulatory unit, consistently achieving the highest expression across cell types and supporting selectively enriched expression in photoreceptors.

Compact and efficient G5mP cassette for AAV-mediated retinal gene delivery

To evaluate AAV-mediated transgene expression, performance of the G5mP cassette was evaluated in AAV vector formats and compared with that of the previously used RIR configuration. These constructs (pscAAV.RIR and pscAAV.G5mP) were introduced into photoreceptor-like 661 W and RPE-like ARPE-19 cells via plasmid transfection and AAV transduction. Following plasmid transfection, G5mP showed a 1.6-fold increase in mCherry fluorescence in 661 W cells and a 3.4-fold increase in ARPE-19 cells compared with that of RIR (Figures S5A and S5B). Next, these cassettes were assessed in the context of scAAV2/8 (scAAV8) vectors: scAAV8.RIR and scAAV8.G5mP. Transductions were performed at 5 × 105 and 1 × 106 viral genomes (vg) per cell. The proportion of mCherry-positive cells increased from 1.0% to 6.3% (5 × 105 vg/cell) and to 8.6% (1 × 106 vg/cell). In ARPE-19 cells, G5mP increased fluorescence by 1.4-fold over that of RIR at 5 × 105 vg/cell and by 1.5-fold at 1 × 106 vg/cell, with mCherry-positive cells rising from 2.3% to 7.6% and from 3.1% to 18.9%, respectively (Figure 4E).

Additionally, the G5mP cassette reduces vector size by more than half compared with that of the RIR configuration. Expression of the mCherry reporter was monitored in live retinal organoids for up to 30 days post-transduction (Figure S6A). Among all the tested constructs, scAAV8.G5mP consistently exhibited the strongest expression, markedly exceeding that of scAAV8.RIR (Figure 5B). Quantitative analysis showed that the G5mP cassette improved transduction efficiency by 34.4-fold compared with that of the clinically used RIR cassette (Figure 5C). G5mP-driven expression was preferentially enriched in photoreceptors, as evidenced by strong co-localization with recoverin (RCVRN), RHO, and blue opsin, with minimal signal in non-photoreceptor cells (Figures 5D and S6B). In contrast, CAG-driven expression was widespread across multiple retinal cell types, including bipolar and amacrine cells. Quantitative analysis confirmed significantly higher co-localization of G5mP with RCVRN-positive photoreceptors, whereas CAG showed greater co-localization with CHX10- and calbindin-positive cells, with no significant difference observed for nestin-positive Müller glia (Figure S6C).

Collectively, these results establish that G5mP surpasses the clinically used CAG promoter in expression strength while maintaining a compact size and improved photoreceptor-enriched expression, supporting its potential as a next-generation regulatory element for photoreceptor-targeted gene therapy.

Immunostaining showed that G5mP-driven mCherry expression co-localized with both peanut agglutinin (PNA)-positive cone and RHO-positive rod photoreceptors (Figures 6E and 6F). Quantification analysis confirmed significantly higher co-localization of scAAV8.G5mP with RHO-positive rods (12.12 ± 1.29% vs. 5.72 ± 3.06%) and PNA-positive cones (7.64 ± 1.37% vs. 3.97 ± 1.41%) compared with scAAV8.CAG (Figure S7A). Fluorescence intensity profile analysis further revealed that G5mP-driven mCherry expression closely tracked the RHO-positive photoreceptor layer, primarily concentrated in the inner/outer segments (IS/OS) and extending slightly into the outer nuclear layer (ONL), whereas CAG-driven expression was broadly distributed across multiple retinal layers (Figure S7B). Terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assays showed detectable apoptotic cells in retinas treated with scAAV8.CAG, whereas no cytotoxicity was observed following scAAV8.RIR or scAAV8.G5mP treatment (Figure S8), confirming that photoreceptor-enriched cell-type selectivity confers a retinal safety advantage over broadly active promoters.13

Discussion

Achieving efficient and cell-specific transgene expression remains a major challenge in IRD gene therapy, particularly when targeting photoreceptor cells. Although AAV-mediated approaches, such as Luxturna, have achieved clinical success, the widespread use of strong ubiquitously active promoters (e.g., CAG promoter) can compromise cell-type selectivity and raise concerns about unintended transgene expression in non-target retinal cell layers.

To overcome these limitations, we developed G5mP, a compact 315-bp regulatory cassette engineered for potent and photoreceptor-enriched expression. G5mP integrates a GRK1-derived enhancer (G5) with a minimal PDE6B promoter (mP), assembled through a stepwise screening of enhancers, promoters, and 5′-UTR combinations. Unlike prior studies that used these elements alongside endogenous 5′-UTR, our approach defines these short regions as independent minimal modules.23,24 This modular strategy enabled a dissection of each component’s contribution to retinal transgene expression and guided the rational assembly of G5mP, which consistently outperformed conventional promoter-enhancer combinations—including the RS1/IRBP cassette and CAG promoter—in photoreceptor-like cell lines, human retinal organoids, and mouse retinas. Across all tested systems, G5mP drove strong transgene expression while maintaining clear photoreceptor-enriched selectivity. In human retinal organoids, G5mP not only achieved stronger expression than the larger CAG promoter but also demonstrated enhanced photoreceptor specificity, with preferential co-localization with RCVRN-positive photoreceptors, whereas CAG exhibited broader expression across bipolar and amacrine cell populations. This cell-type selectivity was consistently observed in vivo, where G5mP-driven expression was robustly detected across both rod and cone photoreceptors and showed significantly higher co-localization with RHO- and PNA-positive cells compared with CAG. Importantly, the absence of TUNEL-positive cells following G5mP or RIR delivery, in contrast to the detectable cytotoxicity observed with CAG, supports the notion that photoreceptor-selective expression confers a meaningful safety advantage.

These findings emphasize the value of rational enhancer–promoter engineering tailored to tissue and cell-type contexts. Motif and DNA secondary structure analyses, supported by experimental evidence, suggest that the RS1 5′-promoter segment may suppress downstream promoter function through structural hindrance or transcriptional interference.42,43 Removing this segment enhanced transgene expression, likely by improving promoter accessibility and facilitating RNA polymerase recruitment.44 Interestingly, the RS1-derived RU element—despite its context-dependent negative influence—reliably supported high expression across different promoters and retinal cell types. This suggests that RU contributes post-transcriptional benefits, such as enhancing mRNA stability,45 facilitating efficient translation through favorable RNA structures,46 or interacting with retina-enriched RNA-binding proteins.47 Together, these results highlight the benefit of a modular design strategy that preserves functional elements, such as RU, while removing inhibitory sequences.

For retinal gene therapy applications, the small size and photoreceptor-enriched selectivity of G5mP make it a practical regulatory module compatible with diverse gene delivery systems. The compact 315-bp cassette preserves substantially more payload space than larger regulatory elements such as the RIR cassette (742 bp), an advantage that becomes particularly critical given the packaging constraints of AAV vectors. While single-stranded AAV (ssAAV) vectors accommodate up to ∼4.7 kb, self-complementary AAV (scAAV) vectors—despite enabling faster and stronger expression—are further restricted to ∼2.3 kb.14,15 These size limitations often constrain the use of conventional promoters and reduce the available payload for therapeutic genes. By minimizing the regulatory footprint while simultaneously maximizing transcriptional potency, G5mP offers a distinct advantage over existing compact photoreceptor regulatory elements. Although short endogenous promoters, such as the hGRK1 promoter, have been successfully utilized in clinical gene therapy for RPGR-associated X-linked retinitis pigmentosa,48 G5mP combines comparable compactness with enhanced transcriptional activity and broader pan-photoreceptor coverage spanning both rods and cones—properties consistently demonstrated across retinal cell lines, human retinal organoids, and the mouse retina in this study. This broad activity profile may be particularly advantageous for pan-photoreceptor disorders such as RPGR-linked XLRP and GUCY2D-associated LCA1,49 where the preservation of both rod and cone function is therapeutically important.50,51 Beyond these expression properties, the compact 315-bp cassette provides additional packaging headroom that may be beneficial in size-constrained vector systems. While scAAV vectors enable faster and more efficient transgene expression, they are limited to approximately 2.3 kb of effective payload capacity, and thus minimizing regulatory element size improves flexibility for accommodating large coding sequences or additional therapeutic components. This added capacity may facilitate the design of dual-AAV or trans-splicing approaches for large retinal disease genes such as CEP290 (∼8.0 kb)52 or ABCA4 (∼6.8 kb),53 as well as emerging multi-component genome-editing platforms.

Ultimately, its potential compatibility with both viral and nonviral delivery platforms further broadens its utility as a versatile regulatory module adaptable to emerging therapeutic modalities. Moreover, the stepwise screening and miniaturization workflow establishes a generalizable framework for designing compact, cell-type-enriched expression cassettes applicable well beyond the retina.

Despite these promising findings, certain limitations remain. Human retinal organoids and wild-type mouse models provide valuable insights but do not fully recapitulate the architecture or disease environment of the degenerating human retina. Further evaluation in retinal degeneration models, including large animals, will be essential to confirm the translatability and therapeutic relevance of our findings. Long-term studies are also required to evaluate the durability of expression, potential immune responses, and safety under clinically relevant dosing conditions. Although G5mP successfully drove robust reporter expression using mCherry, future work should confirm its ability to support therapeutic gene expression and restore function in disease models. Additionally, while the present study evaluated G5mP in the context of subretinal delivery, its compact size and photoreceptor-enriched activity may also render it suitable for intravitreal delivery approaches, which warrant further investigation.

In summary, G5mP is a compact and photoreceptor-selective regulatory cassette that enables efficient gene expression while preserving AAV vector capacity. As such, it represents a versatile foundation for developing AAV-based and nonviral gene therapy strategies targeting IRDs and other cell-type-focused applications.

Materials and Methods

Cell culture

HEK293FT (R70007; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 661 W photoreceptor-like (generously provided by Prof. Muayyad Al-Ubaidi; University of Houston, TX, USA), rMC-1 Müller glial cells (ENW001; Kerafast, Boston, MA, USA), Hep3B (HB-8064; ATCC), and HeLa (CRM-CCL-2; ATCC) cells were cultured in high-glucose DMEM (11965-092; Gibco, Billings, MT, USA). Y79 (HTB-18; ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), WERI-RB1 (HTB-169; ATCC), DLD-1 (CCL-221; ATCC), and SNU216 (00216; Korean Cell Line Bank, Seoul, South Korea) cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (11875-093; Gibco), and ARPE-19 retinal pigment epithelial cells (CRL-2302; ATCC) grown in DMEM/F12 medium (11320-033; Gibco). All media were supplemented with 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS) (S001-01; Welgene, Gyeongsan, South Korea) and 1% (v/v) penicillin-streptomycin (15140-122; Gibco). All cell lines were maintained at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2.

Plasmid cloning

The synthetic firefly luciferase expression vector, pGL4.13(luc2/SV40) (E668A; Promega, Madison, WI, USA), was used as a backbone for initial reporter assays. Promoter-reporter plasmids were purchased from Genecopoeia (Rockville, MD, USA) and included the following: pABCA4-Gluc (HPRM30328-PG02), pGUCY2F-Gluc (HPRM43732-PG02), pRBP3-Gluc (HPRM44587-PG02), pRHO-Gluc (HPRM30507-PG02), pGRK1-Gluc (HRPM44605-PG02), pRS1-Gluc (HPRM30308-PG02), and pPDE6B-Gluc (HPRM54815-PG02). Short promoter and enhancer sequences derived from GRK1 and PDE6B were PCR-amplified using KOD-Multi & Epi DNA polymerase (KME-101; Toyobo, Osaka, Japan) and purified with the AccuPrep Kit (K-3037; Bioneer, Daejeon, South Korea). Promoter, enhancer, and UTR fragments were assembled into luciferase or mCherry reporter backbones using NEBuilder HiFi DNA Assembly Master Mix (E2621; New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA), according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Assembly reactions were incubated at 50°C for 15 min, transformed into competent Escherichia coli (C3040H; New England Biolabs), and screened via colony PCR. Positive clones were confirmed through Sanger sequencing prior to use in transfection or AAV vector production.

Dual-luciferase reporter assay for quantification of transgene expression

Cells were seeded in 48-well plates (2 × 104 cells/well) and co-transfected with 150 ng each of Gluc and Fluc plasmids using Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019; Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 32 h, cells were lysed, and luciferase activities measured using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay System (E1910; Promega) on a GloMax 96 Luminometer (Promega). Gluc activity was normalized to Fluc activity to control for transfection efficiency.

TF-binding Motifs and DNA secondary Structure analysis

TF-binding motifs within the RS1 5′-promoter, mP promoter, and G5 enhancer regions were identified using FIMO54 from the MEME Suite (v.5.5.7)55 by scanning the input sequences against the JASPAR 2022 CORE vertebrate non-redundant motif database56 with a significance threshold of p < 1 × 10−4. Motif locations were visualized in strand-specific maps using custom Python scripts, with the position and orientation of each motif represented as color-coded horizontal bars. Identified TFs were annotated with retinal expression status using the Human Protein Atlas database and with transcriptional regulatory directionality using the TRRUST v.2 database.

DNA secondary structure analysis was performed on sequences spanning the promoter or enhancer region through to the start codon of the GLuc coding sequence. Secondary structure predictions and mountain plots were generated using the RNAfold web server (ViennaRNA Package v.2.0) with DNA parameters (Matthews model, 2004).34,38 Predicted minimum free energy (MFE) structures were visualized with base-pair probabilities indicated by a color scale ranging from 0 to 1. MFE, partition function, and centroid traces from mountain plots were used to assess global DNA secondary structure analysis. The sequences used for this analysis are listed in Table S2.

AAV production and purification

Self-complementary AAV8 vectors were produced in AAVpro 293 T cells (632273; Takara Bio, Shiga, Japan) using calcium phosphate-mediated triple plasmid transfection with (1) an inverted terminal repeat-flanked mCherry transfer plasmid, (2) Rep2/Cap8 packaging plasmid (112864; Addgene, Cambridge, MA, USA), and (3) helper plasmid (Takara Bio).57 Cells were harvested 48–72 h post-transfection and lysed via freeze-thaw cycles. Viral particles were purified using iodixanol gradient ultracentrifugation (OptiPrep; Serumwerk Bernburg AG, Bernburg, Germany), and the 40% iodixanol fraction buffer-exchanged into phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.001% Pluronic F-68 (24040-032; Thermo Fisher Scientific). Final aliquots were stored at −80°C. Viral genome titers were determined via quantitative PCR (qPCR) targeting the mCherry coding sequence using the following primers: forward, 5′-CACTACGACGCTGAGGTCAA-3′; reverse, 5′-TAGTCCTCGTTGTGGGAGGT-3′. Titers were reported as vg/mL.

In vitro transfection and transduction

661 W and ARPE-19 cells (1 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 48-well plates. For transfection, 500 ng mCherry plasmid was delivered using Lipofectamine 2000. For AAV transduction, cells were infected with scAAV8.RIR, scAAV8.RG5mP, or scAAV8.G5mP at 5 × 105 or 1 × 106 vg/cell in serum-free medium for 6 h, followed by medium replacement. After 72 h, mCherry expression was imaged using a Cytation 5 fluorescence microscope (Agilent BioTek, Winooski, VT, USA), and both the percentage of mCherry-positive cells and mean fluorescence intensity were quantified using an Attune NxT Flow Cytometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Total RNA extraction and real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol Reagent (15596026; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 500 ng RNA reverse-transcribed into cDNA using PrimeScript RT Master Mix (RR036A; Takara Bio). The qPCR assay was performed using PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (A25741; Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA), with β-actin as an internal control. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the comparative Ct (ΔΔCt) method. Primer sequences used in this study are listed in Table S3.

Generation of retinal organoids and AAV transduction

Human embryonic stem cells (hESCs; H9) were maintained in mTeSR medium (85850; STEMCELL Technologies, Vancouver, Canada) on Matrigel-coated plates. Experiments involving hESCs were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Korea Center for Disease Prevention (P01-201409-ES-01). To generate human retinal organoids, hESCs were pretreated with 5 μM SB431542 (1614; Tocris Bioscience, Bristol, UK) and 300 nM smoothened agonist (SAG) (S7779; Selleck Chemicals, Houston, TX, USA) for 18–30 h. Colonies were dissociated with Gentle Cell Dissociation Reagent (07174; STEMCELL Technologies), centrifuged (1,200 rpm, 3 min), and washed three times. Single cells were aggregated in 96-well ultralow attachment V-bottom plates (10,000 cells/well) in differentiation medium containing 10 μM Y-27632 (1293823; BioGems, Westlake Village, CA, USA) and 30 nM SAG (d0-SAG method). Cultures were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2. Differentiation medium consisted of growth factor-free chemically defined medium (gfCDM) supplemented with 10% KnockOut Serum Replacement (10828028; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The gfCDM included 45% IMDM (12440053; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 45% Ham’s F12 (11765054; Thermo Fisher Scientific), GlutaMAX (35030061; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% lipid concentrate (11905031; Thermo Fisher Scientific), and 450 μM monothioglycerol (M6145; Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA).

Day 0 marked the start of SFEBq culture. Bone morphogenetic protein 4 (1.5 nM or 55 ng/mL; AF-120-05ET, PeproTech, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA) was added on day 7 and reduced by half at each medium change. On day 18, cultures were switched to neural retina induction medium: DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS, 1% N2 supplement (17502048; Thermo Fisher Scientific), 1% GlutaMAX, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 0.5 μM retinoic acid (R2625; Sigma-Aldrich), and 100 μM taurine (T8691; Sigma-Aldrich). Media were refreshed every other day. On day 140, retinal organoids were transduced with scAAV8 vectors encoding mCherry under the indicated regulatory elements. Transduction followed established protocols,58 and reporter expression was monitored for up to 30 days using live fluorescence imaging (Cytation 5; Agilent BioTek).

Immunofluorescence and histological analysis of retinal organoids and mouse retina

Retinal organoids were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA; Biosesang, South Korea) for 2 h at room temperature, cryoprotected overnight in 30% sucrose at 4°C, embedded in optimal cutting temperature (OCT) compound (3801480; Leica Biosystems, Richmond, IL, USA), and sectioned at a 10-μm thickness using a cryostat. Sections were rinsed, blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies diluted in 5% BSA. After PBS washes, the sections were incubated with secondary antibodies and Hoechst 33342 (H3569; Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 1 h, washed, mounted, and imaged using an LSM800 confocal microscope (Carl Zeiss, Oberkochen, Germany). Antibodies used are listed in Table S4. For quantification, cell numbers were determined based on Hoechst 33342 nuclear staining, and mCherry- and cell-type marker-positive cells were identified from confocal images. Co-localization with cell-type markers (RCVRN, CHX10, calbindin, and nestin) was quantified as the percentage of double-positive cells among total mCherry-positive cells.

Mouse eyes were fixed, embedded in OCT, and cryosectioned at 10 μm. Sections were permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS for 15 min, blocked with 5% donkey serum (017-000-121; Jackson ImmunoResearch, West Grove, PA, USA) for 1 h, and incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies. After washing, Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were applied for 4 h, and nuclei counterstained with Hoechst 33342. Antibodies used are listed in Table S5.

Apoptotic cells were detected using the TUNEL assay (11684795910; Roche, Basel, Switzerland), with DNase I-treated sections serving as positive controls. Slides were mounted in Fluorescence Mounting Medium (S302380; Dako, Santa Clara, CA, USA) and imaged with a Leica DMi8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

In vivo AAV injection

Twelve 8-week-old male C57BL/6J mice (DBL, Seoul, South Korea) were housed under a 12-h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were acclimated for one week prior to experimentation and maintained at a controlled temperature of 23°C–26°C with 50% relative humidity.

Subretinal injections were performed under general anesthesia induced through the intraperitoneal injection of Zoletil 50 (Virbac, Carros Cedex, France) and Rompun (Bayer Healthcare, Leverkusen, Germany). Topical anesthesia was achieved by applying 0.5% proparacaine hydrochloride (Hanmi Pharmaceutical, Seoul, South Korea), and pupil dilation induced using a mixture of 0.5% tropicamide and 0.5% phenylephrine (Tropherine; Hanmi Pharmaceutical). A 2% hypromellose solution (Samil Pharmaceutical, Seoul, South Korea) was placed inside a plastic ring on the cornea to maintain optical clarity.59,60 A small sclerotomy was created approximately 0.5 mm posterior to the limbus using a 30-gauge needle, and 1.5 μL AAV (3 × 109 vg) delivered into the subretinal space using a NanoFil syringe fitted with a 35-gauge blunt needle (NF35BL; World Precision Instruments, Sarasota, FL, USA). All experimental animal procedures complied with relevant ethical guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Soonchunhyang University Hospital Bucheon (approval number: SCHBCA 2024-09-02). All procedures were conducted in accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee (IBC no. SCHBCIBC 2024-005).

Tissue processing and image analysis

Four weeks after subretinal injection, the mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 4% PFA. Mouse eyes were post-fixed for 2 h at room temperature, then cryoprotected in 30% sucrose overnight at 4°C. Retinas were mounted according to the protocol described by Pang et al. (2021)61 to ensure consistent orientation.

Fluorescence images of retinal whole mounts were acquired using a Zeiss Axio Observer A1 fluorescence microscope (Carl Zeiss), and expression was quantified using ImageJ software (NIH, Bethesda, MD, USA). 2.5D surface rendering was performed using ZEN Lite software (Carl Zeiss).

Furthermore, the overlap of mCherry with cell-type-specific markers (PNA and RHO) was analyzed using ImageJ (NIH) to quantify the expression ratio in CAG and G5mP. Images were thresholded with consistent parameters across all groups, and overlap was determined using the Image Calculator in ImageJ.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (v.10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way or two-way analysis of variance, followed by Tukey’s, Dunnett’s, or Šídák’s multiple comparisons test, as appropriate. For pairwise comparisons, unpaired two-tailed t tests or Welch’s t tests were applied, as appropriate based on variance equality. Quantitative data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean, with p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. The number of biological replicates (n) is specified in each figure legend.
