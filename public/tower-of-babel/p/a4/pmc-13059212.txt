The role of trust and relationship building in the ethical recruitment of youth living with HIV in research: perspectives from Kenyan youth living with HIV, their caregivers and subject matter experts
Authors: Ashley Chory, Ava Boal, Winstone Nyandiko, Josephine Aluoch, Michael Scanlon, Emma Gillette, Hillary Koros, Celestine Ashimosi, Whitney Beigon, Dennis Munyoro, Janet Lidweye, Jack Nyagaya, Allison DeLong, Rami Kantor, Violet Naanyu, Rachel Vreeman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInclusion of youth (10–24 years) living with HIV (YLWH) in clinical research is critical to addressing their unique vulnerabilities and improving care outcomes. Examining the role of trust and relationship building in research decision-making is critical to designing ethical recruitment and engagement strategies.MethodsWe conducted in-depth, semi-structured interviews with YLWH (10–24 years, enrolled in HIV care at Academic Model Providing Access to Healthcare (AMPATH) in western Kenya), caregivers (parents and guardians) of YLWH, and other subject matter experts (SMEs). Interviews focused on barriers, facilitators, and strategies to improve participant-researcher trust and relationship building, with a particular focus on recruitment and engagement strategies. Interviews were conducted with 99 participants (53% male): 40 YLWH [median age 17.5, (range 11–24), 50% female], 20 caregivers of enrolled YLWH (70% female), and 39 SMEs (33% female; 46% community leaders, 26% healthcare providers, 15% clinical researchers, 8% social scientists, 3% international research experts, 2% laboratory experts).ResultsAll groups indicated trust could be built and broken through research processes. YLWH and SMEs viewed participant identification and study recruitment through medical records as a violation of trust, indicating that their HIV status and health information should remain confidential between themselves and their clinical team. All groups preferred recruitment through existing clinician-YLWH relationships, emphasizing the importance of privacy and confidentially; this strategy was viewed as stronger and more ethical. Losses of confidentiality and mistakes in sample collection that require participants to attend additional, unnecessary research visits or provide additional samples were identified by all groups as additional barriers to relationship-building. YLWH and caregivers discussed researcher characteristics that support relationship-building, emphasizing the importance of positive demeanors and non-stigmatizing behaviors by research personnel. YLWH and SMEs discussed operational needs that foster relationship-building, including proper communication about study procedures, reliably reporting results to participants, and receiving future benefits.ConclusionsTrusting participant-researcher relationships plays an important role in YLWH research decision making and greatly influence the development of ethical recruitment strategies. Study participants highlighted more appropriate and ethical recruitment strategies, stemming from strong participant-researcher relationships.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01426-2.Keywords: Bioethics, Youth, HIV, Kenya, Trust, Research participation, Ethical research practices, Research methods, Research recruitment

Background

Adolescence and young adulthood is a crucial period of development marked by increases in health-related autonomy, shifts away from traditional support systems like caregivers and toward peers, and the advent of risk-taking behaviors [1]. This period is psychologically, biologically and socioemotionally distinct [2], requiring both adolescent and youth-friendly health services and specific attention within health research for maximum health benefits to accrue [2]. Youth (young people 10–19 years old) participation in research is therefore critically important, but key ethical and operational questions remain. In this paper, we approach ethics through a relational and care-based lens, drawing on MacIntyre’s virtue ethics and the ethics of care tradition, which emphasize the moral significance of relationships, trust, and mutual responsibility between researchers and participants [3–6]. This framing recognizes that ethical engagement is not limited to adherence to rules or procedures but involves the cultivation of trust, attentiveness, and respect within the research encounter.

Youth populations with chronic and stigmatized conditions, like HIV, require particular consideration for health services and research. Worldwide, 1.7 million adolescents are living with HIV, the majority of which live in African Countries; HIV remains a major cause of death for this population [7]. This generation of perinatally infected adolescents living with HIV are among the first to reach adulthood, largely because of advances in research and the subsequent development and rollout of highly active antiretroviral therapy (ART); this constitutes a new population with unique and specific needs that warrant further exploration and protections.

While broad guidelines for the protection of children in research exist, there is less that considers the unique vulnerabilities and ethical considerations of engaging youth, especially youth living with HIV (YLWH). Previous studies show that ethical and regulatory safeguards meant to protect youth may also lead to their exclusion in clinical care access; for example, their lack of inclusion in HIV clinical research has been shown to have caused delays in regulatory approvals for pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) use in this age group [8]. Justifications for the exclusion of YLWH in research are often tied to concerns related to the deepening of vulnerabilities, but excluding YLWH may further marginalize this group, limiting access to care advancements [9]. Increased guidance on the ethical engagement of youth populations may bridge these concerns, but additional research is needed to determine what constitutes and facilitates appropriate engagement, and what serves as a barrier.

Our objective is to explore how participant–researcher trust and relationships influence the participation of YLWH in research, with a particular focus on barriers to recruitment and engagement, and strategies to improve them. Understanding the nature of ethical relationships between researchers and youth is critical, as caring and trusting relationships—those that allow for patient vulnerability—have been identified as key motivators for youth engagement in research [8, 10, 11]. Moreover, perceiving researchers as honest and straightforward can further enhance recruitment and retention [11]. Building on this, there is a need to deepen our understanding of how to cultivate ethical, trusting relationships through both interpersonal researcher–participant dynamics and broader operational practices, such as recruitment strategies. Despite existing evidence underscoring the importance of researcher characteristics in engaging YLWH, no studies to date have specifically examined these relationships in low-income country contexts.

Methods

Study setting

This study was conducted at the Academic Model Providing Access to Healthcare (AMPATH), a long-standing partnership between Moi University, Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital and a consortium of North American Academic Institutions [12, 13]. In partnership with the Kenyan Ministry of Health, AMPATH provides comprehensive care for over 160,000 people living with HIV, including over 15,000 children and adolescents across western Kenya [14]. This study is part of a broader, ongoing cohort study (R01 AI120792), that was originally assembled in 2010–2013 (subsequently called the “parent study”), now followed for more than ten years, enrolled in two completed longitudinal studies, and one ongoing study to investigate ART adherence and drug resistance in YLWH in western Kenya.

Study design, sampling and recruitment

Study recruitment details have been published extensively, elsewhere [15–19]. Semi-structured interviews were conducted from January to May 2021, with four categories of participants: (1) YLWH currently enrolled in the parent study, (2) caregivers of YLWH enrolled in the parent study, (3) research naïve YLWH, and (4) additional subject matter experts (SMEs). The interview guides (supplemental files one and two) were developed for this study and informed by both the literature and the study team’s long-standing experience enrolling YLWH in research in this setting [15–17].

YLWH participants were recruited through random sampling selected from scheduled study visits for the parent study and were included only if they were (1) 10–24 years of age, (2) aware of their HIV status, and (3) enrolled in the parent study. Caregivers were child pairs similarly recruited through random sampling from scheduled visits. Caregiver participants were eligible if they were (1) a caregiver of a YLWH enrolled in the parent study, (2) 18 years of age or older, (3) aware of the HIV status of the youth participant, and (4) knowledgeable about the care and research participation of the youth participant. A description of the parent study and its recruitment criteria are published elsewhere [15–19]. Research-naïve YLWH were randomly sampled from the Rafiki Center of Excellence in Adolescent Health, one of the largest adolescent health centers in Africa that provides comprehensive, youth-friendly care to nearly 1,500 YLWH. Participants were eligible if they were (1) 10–24 years of age, (2) living with HIV and aware of their status, (3) receive HIV care at the Rafiki Center and (4) reported no history of research participation. Finally, additional SMEs were identified by the research team and were eligible if they were: (1) 18 years or older, (2) a member of the following groups: community leaders (village elders and chiefs), AMPATH adolescent or community advisory board member, healthcare providers that treat youth, members of IRBs (both local and national), researchers from the AMPATH Research Network and other institutions conducting research with YLWH in Kenya, research laboratory leadership, and government or policy representatives. The only exclusion criteria for all participant groups was the inability to provide written informed consent to participate, or in the case of those under 18 years of age, the inability to provide assent themselves and consent by an appropriate caregiver.

Data collection and analysis

Interviews were conducted by experienced Kenyan facilitators in either Kiswahili or English, depending on the participant’s preference. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed, and those conducted in Kiswahili were translated into English for analysis. This analysis includes a subset of data from a larger study that have not previously been reported, and contributes specifically to questions around relationship building and recruitment [15–17]. Interviewers probed topics related to study recruitment and engagement, researcher-participant relationships, and privacy and confidentiality in research operations. The questions remained the same for all participants but were framed according to each group’s expertise and position in the research process. Deductive thematic analysis was led by two researchers (HK and EG) based on an initial coding framework derived from the interview questions and reviewed with JA and AC. The researchers independently extracted data using the qualitative software program NVivo, version 12 [28]. The same investigators (HK and EG) led inductive analysis, and extracted emerging themes independently before comparing results and reaching consensus with JA and AC on relevant themes. AB provided additional analysis on the sub-set of data related to the themes presented in this study. Research-experienced and research naive YLWH participants were combined as a homogenous group as preliminary analyses showed no differences in perspectives.

Ethical approvals

This study was approved by the Lifespan IRB in Providence, RI, USA (Package number 1452141), the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai Institutional Review Board in New York, NY, USA (study number 20-01177), and the Moi University/Moi Teaching and Referral Hospital’s Institutional Research and Ethics Committee in Eldoret, Kenya (approval number 0003689). Additional approval was received by the National Commission for Science, Technology and Innovation (NACOSTI), a Kenyan government research regulatory body (reference number NACOSTI/HW/3/1/16). All participants over 18 years of age provided written informed consent to participate in study interviews, and participants below 18 provided written assent and caregiver consent. A detailed, standardized informed consent/assent form written in either Kiswahili or English was read aloud with adequate time for questions and clarifications, and written consent/assent was obtained by trained, bilingual research assistants with experience in recruiting youth for research.

Results

Participant demographics

We enrolled 99 participants in this study; 40 YLWH, 20 caregivers, and 39 SMEs: 5 community advisory board members, 10 health care providers, 5 village chiefs, 5 Mtaa leads (community leaders), 5 IRB experts, 5 clinical researchers, 3 social science researchers, and 1 laboratory lead. The majority (52%) of all participants were female: 50% of YLWH (median age 17.5), 70% of caregivers, and 44% of SMEs. Thematic saturation was found across the domains of inquiry.

Barriers to ethical recruitment and engagement

All participant groups identified trust and researcher-participant relationship components as barriers to the engagement of YLWH in research (Table 1). Both youth and SME participants discussed concerns related to identifying participants through their medical records, specifically viewing it as a privacy violation and a sign that their records were not protected. This additionally brought up fears around potential public HIV disclosure and subsequent, associated stigma and discrimination. In-clinic recruitment by staff not otherwise on their care team was also viewed as a violation of confidentiality by youth participants, noting a loss of trust in their doctors and staff secondary to the perception that their medical history was externally shared. All participant groups held concerns around privacy and confidentiality; these concerns were identified as potentially occurring at the point of research recruitment and at each study visit. Participants shared that if they felt their data would not be kept private, they may be forced to share false information to maintain their privacy. SME and youth participants alike agreed that research sample collection mistakes negatively impact the participant’s trust in the research team. Both groups mentioned the importance of correct sample storage, giving the example of a youth participant being called in to provide an additional sample due to researcher error or improper storage; participants share that this experience impacted the level of trust a participant would have in the researcher. Overall, participants expressed that if they could trust that their research experience would be private, and without error, and that their health records kept confidential, they would be more likely to participate.

Facilitators of ethical recruitment and engagement

All groups discussed participant-researcher relationship components that may facilitate the decision to participate in research (Table 2). All three participant groups spoke on the importance of youth feeling well informed on procedures and outcomes and feeling understood by the research team – specifically noting that researchers should understand the specific needs of youth populations – in order to facilitate a more honest, trusting and open participant–researcher relationship. All participant groups highlighted the importance of and concerns related to privacy; SMEs indicated the possibility that youth may ‘give false information’ if they felt their privacy wouldn’t be maintained. Youth participants indicated that “the most important aspect” they consider when approached to participate in research is whether or not their privacy would be protected.

Facilitators to ethical recruitment and engagement

Researcher disposition – specifically being friendly, straightforward and positive – was noted by youth and caregiver participants as an important factor in participants’ willingness to ‘open up’ during research evaluations. Both groups suggest that the researcher should be “friendly,” communicate well, and encourage “positive thoughts” about their HIV status to encourage youth to “be true to themselves.” Perceived benefits related to the perception and acceptance of ones’ status, a sense of belonging and being able to ask questions they may not feel comfortable discussing at home were noted by caregivers as important facilitators of research recruitment and engagement.

Ethical recruitment strategies

Participants viewed common recruitment strategies as violations of privacy and trust; this prompted further inquiry on more acceptable and appropriate recruitment approaches (Table 3). All three participant groups discussed the benefit of having their trusted clinician introduce the research study in clinic prior to a separate research team introduction. Having a clinician whom the potential research participant already knows and trusts introduce the study in a setting where HIV discussions were already likely to happen was thought to limit privacy violation concerns previously mentioned. Finally, youth and SME participants viewed the use of peer navigators – youth living with HIV themselves, who provide social supports and help patients navigate the health care system -- as a productive recruitment strategy.

Discussion

This study explored the importance of establishing participant-researcher trust and relationships on research participation decision making in Kenyan youth. We demonstrate that a strong, trusting relationship between a youth participant and their research team is critical to both recruitment and ongoing engagement of YLWH. Recruitment through the medical record, errors in data collection and perceived breaches of confidentiality were identified as key barriers to participation. Study participants also identified important facilitators, including youth participants being kept well-informed on study procedures and outcomes, feeling respected and valued by researchers, and feeling as though their health information were kept confidential. Researcher characteristics – such as friendliness, honesty, and a positive disposition – were also viewed as facilitating research engagement for YLWH. As such, research teams have a unique opportunity to establish supportive environments, led by principles of youth autonomy [20] that can strengthen participant-researcher relationships, improve key HIV-related outcomes [20], and generate importance evidence to advance HIV medicine.

Privacy and confidentiality were emphasized by every participant – noting that privacy and confidentiality concerns would lead one to decline enrollment and participation, but confidence in privacy and confidentiality measures would increase likelihood for enrollment. Once already enrolled in a study, participants shared that new or ongoing privacy concerns may lead to youth providing false information to the study team in order to maintain their privacy. Confidentiality is a pillar of all medical research but is of additional importance when working with individuals with highly stigmatized health conditions. Unintended public disclosure of one’s HIV status has serious consequences, and may come with social ostracism, violence, shame, and poor mental and HIV related outcomes [21–23]. For youth, these concerns are compounded by other typical developmental challenges, like peer group identity [22]. These concerns are exacerbated by recruitment strategies and study procedures, like recruitment through medical record review, when viewed by participants as not a breach of confidentiality.

Participants provided insights into common recruitment methods and their impact on the decision to participate in research studies. Participants viewed recruitment through an existing medical relationship as the most appropriate and effective strategy when working with YLWH. In many cases, the existing relationship was with a fellow youth, or ‘peer navigator’ who was also viewed as an appropriate person to facilitate research recruitment. Peer navigators in this context are YLWH themselves, who provide patients with social support, strategies to improve ART adherence, mental health outcomes, and engagement and retention in care [24–27]. Peer navigators have also been found to improve communication with clinicians and reduce stress [28]. The use of peers in the recruitment process is a strong strategy to both ensure privacy and facilitate relationship building and trust with traditional, adult researchers. These data demonstrate that trusting relationships between YLWH and their research team lead to both increased research uptake and more meaningful engagement in studies.

For this analysis, research-experienced participants and research-naive participants were analyzed together, as no significant difference was found between the two groups. Significantly, this points to points to consensus around what factors are important in building trust in the researcher-participant relationship. Regardless of research experience, participants identified being kept informed, being treated with respect, and confidence that their medical information would be kept confidential as facilitators of research participation, and a lack of trust in researchers as a barrier to research participation.

This study also has a number of limitations. The study was conducted in a single country, and may not be generalizable to many other contexts, but is likely applicable to other African countries considering the significant research and care for YLWH conducted in Kenya. The number of participants in each group was relatively small but adequate for achieving thematic saturation in the relevant topic areas. This study has a number of strengths. Using qualitative methodology allowed for an in-depth understanding of participants’ perspectives and experiences, and enrolling participants from a variety of backgrounds provided a broad spectrum of views on the involvement of YLWH in research. Particularly, involving YLWH who have participated in research for multiple years provided unique and highly relevant insights. Inclusion of participant caregivers allowed for an additional layer of context to the experiences of YLWH in research. Additionally, we included YLWH who were uniquely experienced in research and highly studied, and we mitigated this bias by purposively sampling YLWH who had not participated in the research. The analysis was conducted by two sets of independent analysts, both with one Kenyan and one American investigator, which mitigated the risk of bias introduced by personal reflexivity and cultural backgrounds.

Conclusions

Our data demonstrate how the dynamics of participant–researcher relationships for YLWH can both break and build trust, and how this trust shapes the quality and depth of research engagement. These findings indicate that thoughtful recruitment methods are a crucial aspect of ethical research engagement; recruitment must be kept confidential and is most effective when conducted through healthcare workers already known to YLWH, such as primary clinicians or peer navigators. Within a virtue- and care-ethics framework, trust is not merely instrumental but moral: it reflects respect for participants’ vulnerability, recognition of their agency, and commitment to their well-being. In this sense, trust becomes an ethical practice—an outcome of researcher integrity, honesty, and attentiveness to relationships—rather than simply a means of facilitating participation. Further research is warranted on bioethical issues related to trust in vulnerable populations and low-resource settings, where challenges to sustaining ethical and trusting researcher–participant relationships may be heightened.
