Pathogenesis and clinical management of chronic multilobar segmental bronchial stenosis: a literature review
Authors: Mingjun Wu, Qian Yang, Yan Hou, Qingliang Xue
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic multilobar segmental bronchial stenosis (CMBS) represents a chronic airway inflammatory disorder characterized by fibrotic narrowing of multiple lobar and segmental bronchi. Its pathogenesis is closely associated with chronic exposure to biomass smoke and tuberculosis infection. CMBS demonstrates high morbidity and poor prognosis, representing a condition of growing clinical significance. However, its underlying pathophysiological mechanisms remain incompletely elucidated. Epidemiological studies reveal a notably higher disease prevalence in developing countries, with particular susceptibility observed in female populations and tuberculosis-endemic cohorts. The pathogenic cascade involves an “inflammatory–oxidative damage–fibrosis” axis, wherein biomass smoke induces airway remodeling through epigenetic reprogramming and mitochondrial dysfunction. Characteristic clinical manifestations include obstructive ventilatory impairment accompanied by pulmonary hypertension. Current therapeutic strategies predominantly rely on bronchodilators and antitubercular agents, yet fail to reverse established fibrotic lesions. Future directions necessitate coordinated public health interventions for early disease detection, deeper exploration of molecular mechanisms, and the development of targeted therapeutics.Keywords: Chronic multilobar segmental bronchial stenosis, Biomass smoke, Tuberculosis infection, Airway remodeling, Epigenetic regulation

Introduction

Chronic airway inflammatory diseases are primarily characterized by persistent inflammatory responses and progressive airway remodeling [1]. In recent years, a distinct phenotype of chronic airway inflammation marked by chronic multilobar segmental bronchial stenosis has garnered increasing clinical attention. Current research on this condition remains limited, with insufficient understanding and a lack of standardized nomenclature. Chung et al. [2] initially designated this entity as “bronchial anthracofibrosis”, defining diagnostic criteria based on bronchoscopic findings of lobar/segmental bronchial stenosis or occlusion accompanied by mucosal pigmentation. To date, most studies have adhered to Chung’s diagnostic criteria and nomenclature. However, clinical observations reveal that not all patients exhibit pigmentation alongside bronchial stenosis, suggesting that pigmentation should be considered a concomitant feature rather than a diagnostic requirement, thereby exposing limitations in prior research frameworks. Consequently, we propose the term “Chronic multilobar segmental bronchial stenosis (CMBS)” to characterize this disorder (see Table 1 for a comparison between CMBS and bronchial anthracofibrosis).

CMBS manifests as multifocal fibrotic thickening and luminal narrowing of lobar/segmental bronchi, often accompanied by mucosal pigmentation, progressive dyspnea, recurrent infections, and irreversible pulmonary function decline. Epidemiological data indicate a disproportionately high incidence in developing countries, strongly correlating with household biomass smoke exposure and post-tuberculosis complications, particularly among females and populations in tuberculosis-endemic regions [3–5]. Recent evidence indicates that the pathogenesis of CMBS stems from the synergistic interaction of environmental exposures, immune dysregulation, and genetic susceptibility. Specifically, biomass smoke drives the “inflammation-damage-fibrosis” axis through oxidative stress and epigenetic reprogramming [3–8].

CMBS poses a growing clinical burden worldwide. However, its pathophysiology remains incompletely understood. Current therapeutic options, including bronchodilators and anti-inflammatory agents, provide only temporary symptomatic relief. Importantly, these treatments fail to modify the underlying disease progression. This critical gap underscores the imperative for elucidating molecular mechanisms and developing targeted interventions. This review comprehensively synthesizes current knowledge on CMBS epidemiology, etiology, pathogenesis, and clinical management, aiming to provide a theoretical foundation and translational perspectives for overcoming existing therapeutic limitations.

Methods

This study conducted a systematic search of PubMed, MEDLINE, and Web of Science databases covering the period from January 1998 to December 2023. The search strategy combined subject headings and free-text terms including “bronchial stenosis”, “anthracofibrosis”, “bronchial anthracofibrosis”, “COPD-BS”, “BSCOPD”, “biomass smoke”, “biomass fuel”, “PM2.5”, “biomass smoke AND bronchial stenosis”, “tuberculosis AND bronchial stenosis”, “biomass fuel AND bronchial stenosis”, and “wood smoke AND bronchial stenosis”. The inclusion criteria comprised: (1) clinical or basic research studies explicitly reporting CMBS or bronchial anthracofibrosis; (2) studies containing data related to biomass smoke exposure and/or tuberculosis infection; (3) studies providing extractable pathological mechanisms or treatment data. The exclusion criteria were: (1) observational studies without control groups; (2) studies with non-extractable data; (3) low-quality studies. The initial search yielded 10,808 records, which were reduced to 8429 after duplicate removal using EndNote. Title and abstract screening excluded 6812 articles including non-CMBS-related studies, case reports, and conference abstracts. Following full-text review of 617 articles, 51 core studies were ultimately included for analysis.

Epidemiological characteristics

CMBS exhibits an incidence rate ranging from 0.1% to 22.5%, demonstrating marked geographical heterogeneity and population clustering. Its development is closely linked to environmental pollution, infections, and socioeconomic determinants. The disease prevalence is significantly higher in developing countries compared to developed nations, with rural areas showing elevated incidence rates over urban regions [5]. Notably, countries with high tuberculosis burdens (e.g., India, South Africa) report disproportionately increased CMBS cases, predominantly secondary to lesion reactivation within 5 years post-tuberculosis treatment [4, 9].

Chronic use of biomass fuels (e.g., wood, coal) for cooking or heating constitutes a major risk factor for CMBS. Tuberculosis infection markedly elevates CMBS susceptibility, particularly in cases of drug-resistant tuberculosis and poor treatment adherence. Occupational exposures further contribute to disease risk, with textile and mining workers exhibiting higher CMBS prevalence than the general population, primarily due to inhalational dust (e.g., silicates) triggering chronic airway inflammatory damage [10, 11].

CMBS displays distinct gender-specific patterns. Epidemiological surveys indicate a female predominance, attributable to women’s predominant role in household cooking/heating activities. Females frequently endure prolonged biomass smoke exposure beginning in childhood or earlier, usually in poorly ventilated indoor settings. Additionally, female CMBS patients frequently present with shorter stature and a higher body mass index (BMI) [10].

The multifocal bronchial stenosis in CMBS impairs airway secretion drainage, predisposing patients to recurrent infections, acute exacerbations, and hospitalizations. Progressive lung function decline due to disease flares severely compromises quality of life, escalates direct medical costs, and imposes substantial socioeconomic burdens. Consequently, implementing early screening and intervention programs in biomass fuel-dependent communities and tuberculosis-endemic regions is urgently required to mitigate disease progression.

Etiology and risk factors

Biomass smoke

Chronic biomass smoke exposure represents the primary etiological driver of CMBS. Substantial evidence demonstrates that individuals with prolonged use of biomass fuels for cooking or heating exhibit significantly elevated CMBS risk compared to non-exposed populations, with risk proportional to exposure duration [3, 6, 7]. Intervention studies further validate this causal relationship: households transitioning from biomass to clean energy sources show reduced annual CMBS incidence rates and slower declines in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) [12].

Biomass smoke comprises a complex mixture of inhalable particulate matter (PM), carbon monoxide, sulfur oxides, and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs). In vitro studies confirm its mutagenic potential, containing irritants, ciliotoxic agents, and pro-inflammatory compounds that compromise respiratory defense mechanisms [13]. Under physiological conditions, mucociliary clearance efficiently removes carbon particles from biomass smoke. However, chronic exposure impairs airway clearance, allowing carbon particles to adhere preferentially at bronchial bifurcations—regions with weaker mucociliary activity. Phagocytosed particles by macrophages trigger fibrotic repair processes within damaged bronchial mucosa, resulting in wall thickening and luminal narrowing. Unphagocytosed deposits induce macrophage lysis, releasing inflammatory cytokines that promote mucosal hyperplasia, submucosal fibrosis, and peribronchial vascular/lymphatic involvement, manifesting as chronic nonspecific inflammation or granulomatous changes [3, 14, 15].

Tuberculosis infection

Epidemiological data indicate that 27%–60% of CMBS patients have concurrent tuberculosis infections [4]. High-tuberculosis-burden countries report elevated CMBS incidence, often linked to post-tuberculosis complications or lesion reactivation within 5 years post-treatment. Notably, antitubercular therapy improves bronchial stenosis and mucosal pigmentation in some cases [4, 9]. Anatomically, CMBS-related stenotic and pigmented sites overlap with tuberculosis predilection zones. Intriguingly, most CMBS patients lack classical tuberculosis symptoms, prompting two mechanistic hypotheses [4, 16]: (1) strain-specific pathogenicity: molecular epidemiological analyses reveal the predominance of Mycobacterium tuberculosis strains (e.g., East African-Indian and Central Asian genotypes) in CMBS patients, suggesting strain-specific interactions with CMBS pathogenesis. (2) Hypoxia-mediated immune evasion: hypoxic microenvironments within bronchial mucosa may suppress bacterial growth, delaying immune recognition and attenuating inflammatory responses.

Despite ongoing debate regarding the tuberculosis–CMBS nexus, clinicians must maintain high suspicion for tuberculosis coinfection in CMBS patients due to their potential interplay.

Genetic factors

Beyond environmental exposure factors (such as biomass smoke and tuberculosis infection), genetic predisposition may contribute to CMBS pathogenesis. Based on evidence inferred from related diseases, interleukin-17A (IL-17A) gene polymorphisms (rs2275913 and rs8193036) identified in biomass smoke-exposed populations may potentially increase CMBS susceptibility by modulating T helper 17 cell (Th17) inflammatory responses [17]. Conversely, HSP90B1 rs2070908 and HHIP rs13118928/rs1828591 variants have been shown to exhibit airway protective effects [18, 19]. It is hypothesized, based on related evidence, that these genetic variants might participate in CMBS airway remodeling through their influence on oxidative stress and fibrotic pathways. However, their direct mechanistic roles in CMBS require validation through disease-specific investigations.

Epigenetic factors

Current evidence suggests epigenetic regulation may contribute to CMBS progression, though its specific mechanisms require further validation [10]. Based on findings inferred from biomass smoke exposure studies, DNA methylation and miRNA regulation are hypothesized as potential key mechanisms [20, 21]. In biomass smoke-exposed populations, observed downregulation of miR-22 shows a significant correlation with increased histone deacetylase 4 (HDAC4) expression and decreased lung diffusion capacity [22], suggesting this pathway may play a role in CMBS. In vitro experimental data indicate that biomass smoke might influence airway epithelial differentiation through the miR-34a-5p/Notch-1 axis [23], though this requires confirmation in clinical CMBS samples. Additionally, specific miRNA expression alterations identified in CMBS patients (e.g., miR-374a-5p downregulation and miR-191-5p upregulation) may reflect disease subtypes [24], but these findings need more direct evidence. It should be noted that some mechanistic insights are primarily derived from in vitro or animal models of related conditions rather than being CMBS-specific. Future studies combining multi-omics analyses with clinical cohorts are needed to clarify the precise role of epigenetic regulation in CMBS pathogenesis.

Airway inflammation and oxidative stress

Chronic exposure to PM2.5 from biomass smoke may impair respiratory function through multiple pathophysiological mechanisms. Based on evidence inferred from related disease studies, at the airway cellular level, these fine particles could potentially induce mitochondrial dysfunction, manifested by reduced membrane potential, enhanced dynamin-related protein 1 (DRP1)-dependent fission, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and energy metabolism disturbances—alterations observed in 16 human bronchial epithelial (HBE) cell models and long-term mouse exposure experiments [21, 27]. Current data suggest, though not yet confirmed in CMBS specifically, that PM2.5 might trigger sustained ROS and pro-inflammatory factor production in airway epithelial cells, compromising epithelial barrier integrity and activating innate immune responses [25]. Experimental evidence from related conditions indicates alveolar macrophages may exhibit stronger responses to particulates, displaying elevated pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion patterns [25]. This inflammatory profile finds partial validation in population studies, with exposed groups showing increased systemic inflammatory markers (IL-6, IL-8, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), C-reactive protein (CRP)) and impaired antioxidant defenses (reduced superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, elevated ROS) [26]. Research data demonstrate wood-derived PM2.5 can dose-dependently regulate expression of various inflammatory mediators while potentially promoting pulmonary chemokine release [27, 28]. Importantly, it must be noted that many of these mechanistic insights are derived from non-CMBS models. Current evidence suggests PM2.5’s biological effects likely depend on its physicochemical properties, where particle size, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbon content, exposure duration, and target cell heterogeneity may collectively determine pathological outcomes [29, 30]. While these findings offer potential explanations for biomass smoke-induced respiratory damage, CMBS-specific validation studies remain imperative.

Extracellular matrix remodeling

The characteristic airway remodeling in CMBS manifests as bronchial wall thickening, marked basement membrane hyperplasia, and progressive peribronchiolar and perivascular fibrosis [7]. The molecular mechanisms underlying these pathological changes primarily stem from inflammation induced by the aforementioned etiological factors, which trigger subsequent fibrotic cascades leading to irreversible airway structural remodeling. At the molecular level, the persistent inflammatory microenvironment induced by PM2.5 upregulates transforming growth factor-beta 1(TGF-β1) expression, thereby activating its downstream signaling network. TGF-β1 coordinately regulates the fibrotic process through dual signaling transduction pathways [31–35]: (1) the canonical Smads-dependent pathway that mediates transcriptional activation of extracellular matrix genes; and (2) non-canonical pathways (including phosphoinositide 3-kinase(PI3K)/protein kinase B(AKT) and mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) signaling axes) that promote fibroblast-to-myofibroblast transition while inducing epithelial–mesenchymal transition (EMT) and local immune microenvironment dysregulation, collectively contributing to irreversible airway remodeling. Notably, biomass smoke exposure also persistently activates the nuclear factor-kappa B(NF-κB)/activator protein-1(AP-1) signaling pathway, disrupting the protease–antiprotease balance. This manifests as excessive activation of matrix metalloproteinases (MMP-9/MMP-12) coupled with relative deficiency of tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1(TIMP-1), ultimately leading to disruption of extracellular matrix (ECM) homeostasis and abnormal deposition [36].

Clinical features

Pulmonary function

Clinical studies demonstrate that CMBS patients exhibit declines in multiple spirometric parameters (FVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, FEF25-75%, and FEF25-75/FVC), predominantly manifesting as mild obstructive ventilatory impairment without significant diffusion capacity abnormalities [37–40] (see Table 2). Notably, pulmonary function decline in CMBS progresses slowly, with rare cases of rapid deterioration [21]. Compared to chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), CMBS patients show markedly reduced responsiveness to bronchodilators [37], attributable to distinct pathophysiological features: (1) anatomic heterogeneity: COPD primarily involves progressive narrowing of small airways (terminal/respiratory bronchioles), which lack cartilage and exhibit high compliance. In contrast, CMBS predominantly affects larger airways (lobar/segmental bronchi) with complex multilayered structures (cartilage rings, smooth muscle layers, mucosal folds). Pathological remodeling (smooth muscle hyperplasia, subepithelial fibrosis) reduces airway compliance [41, 42]. (2) β2-adrenergic receptor distribution: receptor density increases from central to peripheral airways, peaking in alveolar regions. Bronchodilators primarily target small airways, whereas CMBS lesions in larger airways—with lower β2-receptor density—result in diminished therapeutic efficacy [41, 42].

Cardiac ultrasound

CMBS patients exhibit higher pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) and pulmonary artery-to-aorta diameter ratios (PA/AA) than COPD patients [43], indicating greater pulmonary vascular pathology. Mechanistic contributors include: (1) hypoxia-driven vascular remodeling: chronic hypoxemia activates hypoxia-inducible factor-1 alpha (HIF-1α), inducing vasoconstriction and upregulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)/platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF) to promote vascular smooth muscle proliferation, elastic/collagen deposition, and irreversible pulmonary hypertension(PH) [45]. (2) Bronchovascular coupling: peribronchial fibrosis mechanically compresses adjacent vessels, increasing pulmonary vascular resistance and perpetuating a hemodynamic–inflammatory cycle [44]. (3) Vasotoxic effects of biomass smoke: histopathological studies confirm endothelial injury (intimal fibrosis, medial hypertrophy) mediated by direct particulate damage and IL-6/TNF-α-driven inflammation, elevating vascular resistance [36]. These mechanisms distinguish CMBS-associated PH from COPD-related vascular pathology.

Chest CT

Chest CT holds significant diagnostic value in CMBS [5]. Multiple studies demonstrate that characteristic CT findings in CMBS patients include bronchial stenosis or occlusion with wall thickening, most frequently observed in the right middle lobe bronchus (51%), followed by bilateral upper lobe bronchi [14]. Notably, approximately 56% of cases exhibit calcified mediastinal or hilar lymphadenopathy adjacent to stenotic bronchi, a feature with differential diagnostic implications [14]. A retrospective analysis by Park et al. of 167 CMBS patients identified atelectasis, calcified mediastinal/hilar lymph nodes, and peribronchial lymphadenopathy as the most distinctive imaging manifestations. Multivariate logistic regression confirmed the high diagnostic accuracy of these CT features for CMBS (AUC = 0.939, 95% CI 0.911–0.960) [46] (see Table 2).

Regarding parenchymal alterations, CMBS patients exhibit a lower prevalence of emphysema [43]. Comparative histopathological studies by Rivera et al. [7] revealed that chronic biomass smoke exposure predisposes to interstitial fibrosis more prominently than cigarette smoke. While histopathology remains definitive for characterizing parenchymal damage (e.g., fibrosis, emphysema), clinical challenges in obtaining lung biopsies underscore the potential of CT-based quantitative analysis (e.g., Hounsfield unit metrics) for noninvasive assessment. However, current evidence linking CT radiomic features to CMBS-associated parenchymal pathology remains insufficient. Future multicenter, large-scale cohort studies are warranted to establish standardized imaging evaluation frameworks, thereby enhancing the clinical utility of CT in CMBS-related lung injury assessment.

Bronchoscopy

Bronchoscopy remains the diagnostic gold standard. CMBS features luminal stenosis/obstruction with mucosal pigmentation, edema, and structural distortion, categorized into three subtypes: (1) flat subtype: normal lumen morphology with smooth mucosa. (2) Retraction subtype: mild extrinsic narrowing with mucosal folds. (3) Pigmented protrusive subtype: severe luminal distortion, ulceration, and hemorrhagic mucosa reflecting aberrant angiogenesis [38, 47] (see Table 2).

Histopathology reveals chronic inflammation (macrophage infiltration, subepithelial fibrosis, vascular hyperplasia) and black particulate deposits in biomass-exposed individuals [4]. For high-risk patients, Park et al. [16] developed a clinical-CT predictive model (incorporating gender, age, tuberculosis history, and imaging features), though its utility is limited by overlapping radiological findings with lung cancer. High-resolution CT with 3D reconstruction offers noninvasive alternatives, yet comparative studies on diagnostic efficacy remain scarce.

Current therapeutic approaches

Currently, there is no standardized treatment protocol for CMBS, with clinical management primarily focusing on symptomatic and supportive care. Pharmacological interventions using bronchodilators combined with inhaled corticosteroids can partially alleviate symptoms but fail to reverse established airway remodeling [4]. The study by Mirsadraee et al. [48] demonstrated that 5-month treatment with salmeterol/fluticasone plus tiotropium significantly improved COPD assessment test(CAT) scores in 57% of patients and enhanced small airway function parameters, though without changes in FEV1/FVC ratios and with limited short-term efficacy (evidence level B). Notably, patients with biomass smoke exposure showed better therapeutic responses, highlighting the importance of etiological classification in treatment selection. Importantly, corticosteroid use in tuberculosis-endemic regions may increase the risk of tuberculosis reactivation, and their standalone efficacy remains unconfirmed (evidence level C) [49].

For tuberculosis-associated CMBS, empirical anti-tuberculosis treatment may improve bronchial stenosis and mucosal pigmentation even in the absence of etiological evidence. Zhao et al.’s case report [50] showed that diagnostic anti-tuberculosis therapy combined with cryotherapy produced favorable outcomes, but the evidence was rated as level C because the study lacked control groups, did not use standardized latent infection testing, and the potential confounding effects of adjunct cryotherapy could not be excluded. Importantly, while anti-tuberculosis treatment can improve radiographic findings, it rarely fully reverses established bronchial deformities. In high tuberculosis burden regions, diagnostic anti-tuberculosis therapy is recommended for refractory cases, with concurrent implementation of molecular testing to enhance diagnostic accuracy and treatment duration optimization.

For patients with severe stenosis refractory to medication, bronchial stent placement may serve as a palliative option [51]. Current evidence shows metal stents provide short-term ventilatory improvement but carry > 60% long-term restenosis rates with risks of granulation and infection, thus recommended only for patients with limited life expectancy. Tuberculosis-associated cases require complete anti-tuberculosis therapy before stenting. Clinical decisions should integrate stenosis location, disease activity, and patient status, with endobronchial ultrasound assessment of distal lung function. Future development of anti-fibrotic coated stents and standardized protocols is needed. Current stent therapies carry level C evidence and require cautious application.

Overall, CMBS management requires individualized approaches based on etiology classification, disease severity, and complication risks. Future research priorities include: optimizing drug regimens through parallel-controlled trials; investigating predictive biomarkers like sputum miRNAs; developing novel anti-fibrotic drugs and stent technologies; and establishing multicenter networks for evidence-based guidelines. Large-scale clinical studies are urgently needed to address current limitations in treatment options and standardized outcome measures.

Conclusions

CMBS is an inflammatory airway disorder characterized by fibrotic narrowing of multiple lobar/segmental bronchi, driven by a complex interplay of biomass smoke exposure and tuberculosis infection. The disease progresses through an “inflammatory–oxidative damage–fibrosis” cascade, culminating in irreversible airway remodeling. Epidemiological data highlight its clustering in female populations and tuberculosis-endemic cohorts in developing countries, strongly associated with chronic indoor biomass fuel use. Clinically, CMBS manifests as obstructive ventilatory impairment with PH, accompanied by characteristic bronchial stenosis and mucosal pigmentation on imaging and bronchoscopy. Current therapeutic strategies—primarily bronchodilators, antitubercular agents, and interventional procedures—offer symptomatic palliation but fail to halt fibrotic progression. Critical knowledge gaps persist, particularly regarding core mechanisms such as TGF-β1/Smads–PI3K/AKT crosstalk and epigenetic reprogramming-mediated airway remodeling, which hinder the development of targeted therapies.

Future research priorities include: (1) public health interventions: government-led initiatives to promote clean energy adoption, develop low-emission biomass stoves, and reduce PM2.5 exposure. (2) Early screening protocols: integrated pulmonary function and imaging surveillance in high-risk populations (Tuberculosis-exposed, chronic biomass smoke users). (3) Biomarker discovery: validation of plasma exosomal miRNAs (e.g., miR-34a-5p, miR-374a-5p) as noninvasive diagnostic markers. (4) Mechanistic exploration: elucidation of molecular pathways to enable targeted drug development for fibrosis reversal. (5) Stratified therapeutics: clinical management should adopt a stepwise treatment algorithm based on bronchial stenosis staging (see Table 2 and Fig. 2). It should be noted that due to overlapping clinical manifestations and the current lack of disease-specific diagnostic criteria, the existing staging system references the COPD classification criteria, which may not fully capture the distinctive characteristics of CMBS. Therefore, future revisions and refinements of the staging system should incorporate new evidence from ongoing cohort studies.

Graded treatment strategy for CMBS

Multidisciplinary efforts integrating epidemiological, molecular, and clinical research are imperative to address this underrecognized yet impactful respiratory disease.
