CKDs at the Crossroads: From Failures to Future Therapies
Authors: Farah Wehbe, Mark Elliott, Adeera Levin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractChronic kidney disease (CKD) is a public health emergency because it is common and carries an increasing burden of morbidity and mortality. Until recently, treatment options for CKD and its adverse systemic effects were limited; however, we now have multiple approved therapies and a growing pipeline of promising treatments under evaluation. Despite the strong evidence and guideline recommendations supporting the broad use of approved therapies, uptake in practice remains lower than expected, potentially limiting the benefits of these advances. Early identification of CKD remains a prerequisite for therapy, yet screening and cost-effectiveness continue to be debated globally. In this review, we discuss the currently available pharmacologic treatments for CKD that have proven kidney and cardiac benefits with a specific focus on addressing barriers to implementation and ongoing trials that will inform their routine clinical use. We then discuss treatments that are in late-phase clinical trials that may expand the therapeutic options for CKD in the next few years. This will necessitate a personalized approach to management to determine which therapies will work best for which patients. Finally, we touch on therapeutic strategies that did not demonstrate clinical benefits despite rational physiologic support.This review addresses the know-do gap that exists in applying proven therapies for people with CKD and provides clinicians with practical tools and knowledge to improve clinical uptake, while acknowledging global challenges of access and affordability.Keywords: chronic kidney disease, diabetic kidney disease, endothelin receptor antagonists, mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists, SGLT2 inhibitors, treatment

CKD is a global health challenge, affecting > 850 million people worldwide, with prevalence increasing by ∼30% over the past 2 decades.1, 2, 3 CKD is projected to become the fifth leading cause of death worldwide by 2040, representing one of the most significant increases among all major causes of mortality.4 This alarming trend is driven by aging populations, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, and persistent disparities in access to effective treatments.5 Equally important, many eligible patients remain untreated with therapies proven to slow CKD progression and reduce cardiovascular (CV) risk—an implementation “know–do gap” this review addresses.

CKD is a multisystem condition, with impacts on CV, endocrine, immune, reproductive and nervous systems.6 CV disease (CVD) accounts for 39% of CKD-related deaths.1 The pathophysiological link between CKD and CVD highlights the urgent need for therapies that address both kidney and CV health.

The 2024 Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines define CKD as a persistent abnormality in kidney structure or function for > 3 months, with health implications.7 This inclusive definition differs from the classification system, which is based on cause, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) and albuminuria.7 Because CKD is an umbrella term, comprising distinct kidney diseases, establishing etiology is important for optimizing care and guiding future research priorities. Because the disease is often silent, case-finding and screening programs are necessary for early detection and diagnosis.8,9

Since 2017, CKD care has shifted toward earlier and multitargeted interventions.10 Renin-angiotensin-system inhibitors (RASi) remain foundational, and sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors (SGLT2i) have redefined care, offering cardio-renal protection, independent of glycemic control. Other novel agents, including nonsteroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonists (MRAs, nsMRAs) and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs), further expand the therapeutic options for people with CKD.

Despite progress, developing new therapeutics for CKD and its complications remains challenging. Several promising agents, such as bardoxolone methyl and veverimer failed because of safety and efficacy concerns, highlighting the complexity of CKD pathophysiology and the need for precision-based approaches. Within this paradigm, we continue to develop more effective management strategies and a clearer understanding of when, and in whom, to start specific therapies. Although much has been learned, patients with CKD still face residual risk of kidney disease progression and CV events.1 In major outcome trials, substantial events persisted despite therapy: EMPA-KIDNEY (13.1% on empagliflozin vs. 16.9% on placebo over ∼2 years),11 DAPA-CKD (9.2% on dapagliflozin vs. 14.5% on placebo over ∼2.4 years),12 and FIDELIO-DKD (17.8% on finerenone vs. 21.1% on placebo over ∼ 2.6 years).13 These illustrate incomplete risk reduction. A pooled modeling analysis estimated that layering SGLT2i, GLP-1RA, and an nsMRA onto standard care yields a 4.4% absolute risk reduction over 3 years and multi-year gains (∼ 3.2 years major adverse CV event–free; 5.5 years CKD-progression–free), underscoring the need for combination, guideline-directed therapy alongside better implementation.14 However, many patients still do not receive proven therapies, reflecting a persistent evidence-practice gap.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of the current and emerging therapeutic strategies in CKD, summarizing the efficacy, safety, and limitations of approved and investigational treatments. We also examine key successes and setbacks in drug development, with attention to the persistent “know–do gap”; the disconnect between evidence and everyday practice. Too often, patients miss out on therapies that could slow kidney decline or protect their hearts. We explore why this exists and how it might be closed (Supplementary Table S1), aiming to provide practical insights to help more patients benefit from proven treatments.

Early Identification And Screening

Early identification of CKD remains a significant challenge. Although most cases are diagnosed opportunistically, evidence favors case finding within high-risk groups (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, CVD, or family history of kidney disease). Routine eGFR and urine albumin-creatinine ratio (uACR) testing in these populations can permit earlier intervention, yet implementation varies across health care systems.

The role of general population screening is more controversial. Cost-effectiveness depends on local prevalence, infrastructure, and capacity to follow-up with abnormal results. Efforts to place testing into diabetes and hypertension care, reflex reporting of eGFR and of uACR, and case-finding of high-risk communities may be more feasible in most regions. Ultimately, early diagnosis and case-finding are prerequisites for administering the treatments reviewed here. Unless patients are first identified, closing the evidence–practice gap is impossible.15,16

Current Therapies: What We Know And How We Use Them

Managing CKD requires a comprehensive strategy: lifestyle modification (diet, physical activity, smoking cessation) combined with pharmacological therapies targeting blood pressure (BP), glycemic control, proteinuria, and kidney injury key pathways. Although these approaches are well-established, there have been major advancements in pharmacologic treatments within the past decade that remain underutilized in clinical care. Here, we focus on pharmacological interventions central to CKD management.

RASi—Foundation of CKD Treatment

RASi, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), revolutionized CKD treatment, with decades of robust evidence supporting their efficacy in slowing disease progression, reducing proteinuria, and improving CV outcomes.17, 18, 19

Angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors such as captopril, enalapril, and lisinopril were among the first to demonstrate significant benefit, especially in diabetic nephropathy.20, 21, 22 Although direct renin inhibitors such as aliskiren aimed to provide more complete RAS blockade, studies such as ALTITUDE,23 when tested on top of RASi, showed increased risks of hyperkalemia, hypotension, and stroke, without added benefits, excluding them from CKD management.5

RASi are primarily indicated for patients with proteinuria or hypertensive nephropathies, but their protective effects go beyond BP control. They attenuate intrarenal inflammation and fibrosis, key drivers of progression, and offer CV benefits in CKD populations.17,24,25 The 2024 KDIGO guidelines recommend initiating RASi as first-line therapy in patients with CKD, high BP, and moderate to severe albuminuria (A2–A3), regardless of diabetes status.7 Early initiation has been shown to reduce the risk of CKD progression. However, the residual risk of kidney function decline and CV events remains.26,27

Despite robust evidence, low costs and easy access to RASi medications over the past 30 years, their use remains suboptimal in clinical practice.5 One of the main barriers is hyperkalemia, a common but manageable side effect. Although guidelines emphasize that hyperkalemia should be managed with potassium-lowering strategies (diuretics or binders [patiromer, sodium zirconium cyclosilicate]) rather than discontinuing RASi, in practice, they are often reduced or stopped, because of potassium or eGFR changes. Dose reduction or cessation has been shown to result in worse kidney and cardiac outcomes without any clinical benefit.28 Current guidance suggests that one should avoid stopping RASi when possible.7 However, patients are often not rechallenged with RASi once the transient issue resolves.29 The reluctance to restart therapy, despite recommendations and available strategies, highlights a critical disconnect between evidence-based practice and real-world CKD care.28,30

RASi benefit patients with normal to mildly elevated albuminuria (A1), particularly when treating hypertension or heart failure (HF) with reduced ejection fraction.7 Maximizing doses of RASi to recommended levels offers the greatest benefit for patients to reduce kidney and cardiac events.29 Clinicians should be more confident in adhering to these guidelines, maintaining RASi therapy even when eGFR falls below 30 ml/min per 1.73 m2, unless clear contraindications exist.7,31 These issues highlight a “know-do gap” in one of our oldest and most established treatments for people with kidney disease that remains persistent with newer therapies. Closing this gap is a low-cost, high-impact opportunity to improve outcomes for millions worldwide, and remind us about how much progress is possible when evidence is translated into everyday care.32 Practical resources, such as the ISN’s Optimization of RASi Therapy Toolkit and KIDGO’s hyperkalemia guidelines, support broader prescribing and clinician confidence.7,33

SGLT2i—A Breakthrough in CKD Management

SGLT2i are now a cornerstone of CKD management. Large trials and meta-analyses demonstrate slowing eGFR decline, decreasing need for kidney replacement therapies, and lowering CV mortality, independent of diabetes. Benefits extend to patients with CVD, reducing CKD progression risk, end-stage kidney disease, HF hospitalizations, and death.11,12,34

Originally developed for glycemic control, SGLT2i block glucose reabsorption in the proximal tubule, causing glycosuria and natriuresis, restoring tubuloglomerular feedback, reducing intraglomerular pressure, and preventing hyperfiltration-induced damage.35 SGLT2i have direct cardioprotective actions: they modulate myocardial energy metabolism and reduce oxidative stress in cardiomyocytes, mechanisms that may contribute to the identified improved cardiac function.36 They also lower weight; increase sodium, potassium, phosphate, and uric acid excretion; lower BP; and modulate inflammation and fibrosis, together offering broad kidney and CV system protection.37

Clinical Evidence and Recommendations

SGLT2i slow CKD progression, regardless of albuminuria.38 Although data in advanced CKD (G4/G5) remain limited, evidence consistently points toward a positive therapeutic impact.39, 40, 41 Indications have expanded significantly: SGLT2i are recommended for patients with or without diabetes, CKD, HF, and atherosclerotic CVD, irrespective of CKD status.42 A meta-analysis of 10 trials has demonstrated a reduction in CV death or hypertensive HF among high-risk patients with diabetes.43,44

Based on the totality of evidence, the 2024 KDIGO guidelines recommend starting SGLT2i in patients with CKD with eGFR ≥ 20 ml/min per 1.73 m2, regardless of diabetes status.7,12,45, 46, 47 This includes patients with diabetes, HF, or an eGFR between 20 and 45 ml/min per 1.73 m2 irrespective of albuminuria, and any patient with a uACR ≥ 20 mg/mmol (≥ 200 mg/g).7,46,48 This eGFR initiation threshold for SGLT2i may represent clinical conservatism based on trial inclusion criteria, as clinical trials have demonstrated continued benefit with SGLT2i treatment until transplant or dialysis initiation.11,12,34 Consequently, the eGFR limits specified in current product labeling are generally consistent with these KDIGO recommendations. However, once therapy is initiated, discontinuation is not required if eGFR later falls below 20 ml/min per 1.73 m2 unless not tolerated or dialysis started.7

Two ongoing studies address this limitation: RENAL LIFECYCLE is exploring the potential benefits of dapagliflozin in advanced CKD, including those on dialysis or with kidney transplants49 and DAPA-HD (patients on hemodialysis).50 Until results emerge, it remains uncertain whether and to what extent the CV and kidney benefits seen in early CKD stages extend to patients on dialysis.

In glomerular diseases (e.g., IgA nephropathy), progression to kidney failure occurs despite optimal RASi and immunosuppression.39 SGLT2i offer complementary benefits, and their use should not be delayed by RASi titration.48 Notably, certain groups of patients, including those with autosomal dominant polycystic disease, type 1 diabetes (T1D), antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody–associated vasculitis, lupus nephritis, and patients with solid organ transplants, were excluded from major trials, so no recommendations exist in these populations. Safety Profile and Common Side Effects

SGLT2i are generally well-tolerated, though patients should be counselled on potential adverse effects. Euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis is often the most concerning but is rare and typically precipitated by cessation of insulin or a severe intercurrent illness. The most cited adverse effect is genital mycotic infections, particularly in females, which carry low absolute risk despite a higher relative risk and are often manageable without stopping the SGLT2i.68,69

Other effects include volume depletion and, rarely, Fournier’s gangrene. Education on hygiene and early infection signs is key; discontinuation may be needed for persistent, severe infections.48 Reassuringly, newer data suggest that these risks are lower than originally documented; and contrary to belief, no study has documented any increase in urinary tract infections.35,69 Other potential “positive” effects of SGLT2i include an increase in hemoglobin, fewer kidney stones, and lower new-onset atrial fibrillation.37,70

Managing the Initial eGFR Dip: What Clinicians Should Know

Early concerns about acute kidney injury (AKI) from SGLT2i were largely because of the now well-described transient eGFR decline of 3 to 5 ml/min per 1.73 m2 after initiation. These hemodynamic changes, similar to RASi,71 likely underlie some of the kidney protective benefits observed with SGLT2i, because patients with dips show slower chronic eGFR decline.72,73

This “dip” is expected, benign, typically reversible, and not a sign of injury or toxicity. Therefore, routine eGFR checks within the first 2 weeks are unnecessary. Follow-up in keeping with the usual blood workup (1–3 months of commencement) is adequate. A sustained eGFR decline > 30% warrants further evaluation. KDIGO guidelines recommend “sick day” guidance: pausing SGLT2i use if patients are unwell, febrile, hypotensive, dehydrated, or fasting, and before surgery to lower euglycemic diabetic ketoacidosis risk, particularly if insulin is withheld. Of note, the evidence supporting sick days rules remains limited, and in fact, the harm possible by not resuming key medications needs to be mitigated.7,74 Thus, physicians should communicate a clear plan with their patients and clearly document when to restart therapy.

Unlike RASi, SGLT2i do not increase hyperkalemia; rather, by enhancing distal sodium delivery and aldosterone activation, they promote kaliuresis, and may decrease it, minimizing monitoring needs.42,75 This enables RASi optimization as part of a combined treatment strategy.76 In addition, glycosuria aids glycemic control and serves as an adherence marker, rather than being misinterpreted as a problematic sign.

Bridging the Gap: Real-World Barriers

Despite strong evidence and guideline recommendations alongside RASi as first-line therapy, SGLT2i remain underused in CKD care globally. A review found SGLT2i were used in 6% to 28% of eligible patients, peaking at 32.9% in Korean patients with CKD.77 Australian CKD data showed that 44% of CKD adults would be eligible to use an SGLT2i based on clinical trial inclusions; however, only 4.1% received prescriptions for these medications. The impact of this underutilization is profound, because optimal SGLT2i use could prevent thousands of cardiac and kidney events annually in this population of 230,000 individuals.78 Barriers include limited patient awareness, cost, and system-level challenges like formulary restrictions and limited specialist access. Provider barriers remain as well, with knowledge translation and clinical inertia being the major identified factors, highlighting the important role nephrologists play in modelling optimal use of SGLT2i for kidney and cardiac protection.42,79

MRAs—Enhancing Kidney and Heart Protection

Aldosterone-driven mineralocorticoid receptor overactivation contributes to CKD progression and CV complications through proinflammatory and profibrotic effects, leading to proteinuria, glomerulosclerosis, and accelerated eGFR decline.80 In addition, ongoing glomerular hyperfiltration may persist despite adequate RASi, further driving CKD progression. Thus, there is a mechanistic basis in CKD for further inhibiting the mineralocorticoid receptor beyond standard RASi.

Two main classes of MRAs exist: steroidal (e.g., spironolactone, eplerenone) and nonsteroidal (nsMRAs). Developed nsMRAs (finerenone, esaxerenone, apararenone) are more selective for mineralocorticoid receptor than steroidal MRAs and have fewer off-target hormonal side effects (e.g., gynecomastia, menstrual irregularities, sexual dysfunction).81,82 The effects of steroidal MRA on kidney and cardiac outcomes in people with CKD have been inconsistent. An umbrella review by Amornritvanich et al.83 found that steroidal MRAs reduced all-cause and CV mortality primarily in dialysis patients, but showed no effect on eGFR decline or dialysis initiation despite lowering uACR. This led to 2 large randomized control trials (ALCHEMIST and ACHIEVE) evaluating the effect of spironolactone in patients receiving maintenance hemodialysis, which failed to show CV benefits, while significantly increasing hyperkalemia risks.84,85 In contrast, nsMRAs showed superior efficacy in improving kidney and CV outcomes in nondialysis CKD, especially with diabetes.

Within nsMRAs, we focus on finerenone because of the plethora of trial data available. Finerenone showed significant improvement in uACR and eGFR decline, along with composite kidney and CV outcomes in clinical trials of patients with diabetic kidney disease (DKD), including FIDELIO-DKD and FIGARO-DKD.13,86 This came with a modest serum potassium level rise (median increase: 0.24 mmol/l) without sexual side effects.80,87 In addition to its proven effects in people with diabetes, finerenone may benefit those without diabetes. The FIND-CKD trial (completion 2026) is enrolling such patients.55 FINE-ONE54 and FINE-REAL57 are assessing their use in individuals with T1D and real-world populations, respectively (in Table 1, we present a list of ongoing trials). If proven effective, finerenone could represent the first approved therapy for CKD in T1D in nearly 3 decades.

Beyond nephrology, FINEARTS-HF found that patients with HF with preserved or mildly reduced ejection fraction benefited from lower rates of cardiac events.88 The ongoing REDEFINE-HF (NCT06008197)89 and FINALITY-HF (NCT06033950)90 will better define its role in managing cardiac dysfunction. Collectively, these studies highlight the expanding role of nsMRAs in cardiorenal therapeutics. Unfortunately, the cost of these medications remains high, which will be a barrier for uptake globally across income levels, despite potential long-term cost savings because of reduced kidney and cardiac events.91 This is a barrier to the uptake of many new therapies and one that will likely require system-level changes to address.92 Other nsMRAs have shown promise in CKD, although their availability varies by country.93,94

Despite advantages, finerenone requires monitoring for hyperkalemia, which may necessitate dose adjustments or discontinuation. Acute kidney injury is another risk, especially with volume depletion. The initial eGFR dip reflects altered glomerular hemodynamics, similar to RASi and SGLT2i, and is generally reversible and not harmful if within 30% of baseline.95 Clinicians are advised to monitor serum creatinine and potassium within 4 weeks of initiating MRAs, especially in high-risk patients.

To optimize outcomes in people with diabetes and CKD, a comprehensive strategy incorporating multiple nephroprotective agents is proposed. Combining finerenone with SGLT2i is promising, because SGLT2i may lower serum potassium and reduce hyperkalemia risk associated with MRAs.96 Coadministering diuretics or potassium binders may help to maintain potassium homeostasis and preserve kidney and heart protective therapy.97 The CONFIDENCE trial found that the combination of empagliflozin and finerenone in patients with DKD led to a larger reduction in uACR at 180 days compared with either alone, without safety concerns, supporting the push towards combining therapies for CKD.98

Current evidence supports using finerenone as a foundational treatment in patients with type 2 diabetes, eGFR > 25 ml/min per 1.73 m2, moderate to severe albuminuria (uACR > 3 mg/mmol [∼ 30 mg/g]), and serum potassium < 4.8 mmol/l. Guidelines recommend that people with DKD should receive triple therapy: maximally tolerated RASi, SGLT2i, and finerenone. Although no definitive guidance regarding which agent should be initiated first exists, either concomitant initiation of SGLT2i and finerenone (supported by CONFIDENCE) or rapid sequential initiation with 2 to 6 weeks, with BP monitoring, kidney function, and potassium levels, is reasonable.99 There should be some sense of urgency in getting these agents started and appropriately titrated, given their synergistic effects.100,101 In practice, MRA uptake remains low, with potassium and cost concerns limiting real-world use despite robust trial evidence. This illustrates an important implementation gap that continues to hold back the full benefits of this therapy for patients with CKD.

GLP-1RAs—Beyond Glycemic Control

Mechanism of Action

GLP1-RAs stimulate insulin release and inhibit glucagon via the cAMP pathway, improving glycemic control. In addition, they delay gastric emptying; promote satiety, and lower weight, BP, and lipid parameters. GLP-1 receptors are expressed in glomerular, tubular, and vascular cells, though the exact mechanisms of kidney benefit remain unclear.102 They may influence extracellular matrix remodeling and RAS modulation, with trials such as REMODEL evaluating mechanistic pathways to help improve our understanding.58,103,104

GLP-1RAs are pharmacologically distinct from dipeptidyl peptidase 4 inhibitors, because they directly activate GLP-1 rather than preventing its degradation, providing additional metabolic and CV benefits. Data suggest that oral semaglutide may achieve comparable glycemic, weight, and safety outcomes as the subcutaneous formulation in patients with CKD, though larger trials are needed to confirm these findings.105, 106, 107

Cardiac and Kidney Protection and Clinical Outcomes

Nephroprotection has been observed in trials such as LEADER (liraglutide)108 and FLOW (semaglutide in high-risk CKD)109; the latter is the first randomized control trials evaluating a primary kidney outcome in DKD. FLOW (> 3000 patients) demonstrated the efficacy of semaglutide in improving kidney, cardiac, and mortality benefits. The SELECT trial showed that semaglutide reduced albuminuria and slowed kidney function decline in > 17,500 individuals with obesity and CVD but without diabetes.110 These results were extended by the SMART trial, the first in nondiabetic patients with CKD, which demonstrated a ∼52% reduction in albuminuria among obese and overweight patients, a landmark finding despite its small cohort (100 patients).111 Beyond these studies, data supporting GLP1-RAs in nondiabetic CKD populations continues to grow and represents an important area of ongoing study.

Beyond semaglutide, liraglutide, and dulaglutide lower albuminuria, slow eGFR decline, and reduce the risk of kidney function loss, as demonstrated in LEADER and REWIND trials.108,112 Tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and GLP1-RA, also shows promise in slowing CKD progression in type 2 diabetes, per prespecified SUPRASS-4 trials analysis.113, 114, 115

GLP-1RAs provide well-established CV benefits. Trials such as SUSTAIN 6 and PIONEER 6 confirmed reductions in CV events in diabetic patients with CKD, with a unique advantage in stroke reduction, not seen with SGLT2i.116,117 Thus, GLP-1RAs may be valuable for specific high-risk patients (e.g., with atherosclerotic CVD), aligning with patient-specific risks and clinical circumstances. The nuances and considerations of both the order of pharmacological intervention and the totality of use remain unstudied.

Compared with SGLT2i, GLP1-RAs demonstrate superior glycemic control and weight loss, especially at lower eGFR, without dose adjustment in kidney failure.106 Trials such as DURATION-8 highlight potential for combined use with SGLT2i, but further research is needed to confirm additive CV and kidney benefits.118

Practical Challenges and Unmet Needs

Despite strong evidence and endorsements from the European Society of Cardiology, KDIGO, and the American Diabetes Association, GLP1-RAs remain underused in clinical practice. A review of > 21,000 patients with type 2 diabetes and CVD (2013–2019) found that only 1.6% of patients received a GLP1-RA.119 A 2020 update showed modest improvement to 6.8%, reflecting gradual uptake as supporting data evolved.120 Cost remains a major barrier, particularly for uninsured or in resource-limited settings, where out-of-pocket expenses for combination therapies can be prohibitive. The American Diabetes Association recommends reserving these agents for patients with albuminuria, atherosclerotic CVD, HF, or poorly controlled metabolic risks when affordability is a concern.121 This persistent underuse, despite strong trial evidence and broad guideline endorsement, reflects a persistent “know-do” gap in translating research findings into clinical practice.

Current Limitations

Evidence for GLP-1RAs in dialysis, T1D, and transplant populations remains limited. There have not been studies selecting specific phenotypes (i.e., overweight with diabetes and CKD vs. lean individuals) for preferential commencement of GLP1-RAs over SLGT2i; however, recommendations have focused on aligning secondary effects with patient needs. For example, patients aiming to lose weight or require more glycemic control may reasonably prioritize GLP1-RAs. Overall, GLP-1RAs represent a transformative shift in CKD management, broadening outcomes beyond glucose control, but future studies will clarify their full potential in diabetic and nondiabetic CKD.

Endothelin Receptor Antagonists—The Promise in CKD

Endothelin-1, a potent vasoconstrictive and proinflammatory peptide, plays an important role in CKD progression. In CKD, endothelin-1 is overexpressed and acts mainly via ETA and ETB receptors in endothelial cells, mesangial cells, and podocytes, promoting vasoconstriction, inflammation, fibrosis, and kidney damage.122,123

Thus, selective endothelin-1 blockade with endothelin receptor antagonists (ERAs) may reduce inflammation and proteinuria, and improve kidney function in CKD and diabetes, especially in patients with persistent proteinuria despite optimized RAS inhibition.

Selective ETA receptor antagonists, including atrasentan and avosentan, demonstrated promising benefits in reducing albuminuria (35%–58%) and slowed DKD progression in RADAR, ASCEND, and SONAR trials.124, 125, 126, 127, 128 Effects extend beyond diabetic nephropathy, with positive results in IgA nephropathy, focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, and Alport syndrome.63,64,129, 130, 131 In CKD and T1D, where patients are younger with fewer CV comorbidities, ERAs may be beneficial, though no dedicated studies exist in this subgroup.

Despite encouraging findings, ERA use is limited by adverse effects, specifically fluid retention, HF (notably in ASCEND), and cost.126 These risks underscore the importance of carefully balancing efficacy with safety in high CV risk patients. Thus, the 2022 KDIGO Guidelines for Diabetes Management in CKD make no recommendations for ERAs, and routine use in DKD remains unlikely at this time.132

Combining ERAs with SGLT2i offers a promising strategy to mitigate safety concerns. With SGLT2i’s diuretic effect and their robust data in HF, combining with ERAs may reduce fluid retention while enhancing kidney protection. In the ZENITH-CKD trial, adding zibotentan to dapagliflozin in patients with CKD (with or without diabetes, on optimized RAS blockade) further reduced albuminuria compared to dapagliflozin alone.133 This suggests that ERAs are safe, promising add-on therapy for patients with residual albuminuria. The ongoing ZENITH-HP trial is evaluating long-term kidney benefit.62 Further insights into this combination of RASi, SGLT2i, and ERAs are anticipated from upcoming trials, ALIGN,63 AFFINITY,64 and ASSIST,65 especially in glomerular diseases like IgA nephropathy.

As data emerge showing that ERAs slow CKD progression, they may become a valuable part of the nephrology toolkit, though cost and accessibility may limit global uptake.

Emerging Therapies

Despite advances in CKD therapies, residual risk remains, irrespective of prescribing practices, highlighting the need for approaches that address additional mechanisms and drivers. These include novel antiinflammatory, antifibrotic, and aldosterone-suppressive agents as well as interleukin-6 inhibitors, hypoxia-inducible factor inhibitors, antibody-based therapies, mesenchymal stromal cell therapies in DKD, along with combination treatments (Figure 1).

Aldosterone Synthase Inhibitors: A Promising Target

Aldosterone synthase inhibitors block aldosterone production directly, potentially reducing inflammation and fibrosis without the hyperkalemia risk seen with MRAs. Given the recent positive trials of nsMRA on kidney and cardiac outcomes, these may offer an alternative therapeutic class for patients with CKD and are being studied in broad populations. Ongoing trials, including EASi-KIDNEY66 and BaxDuo,67 will clarify their safety and role in CKD care.

Therapeutic Approaches That Failed To Slow Ckd Progression

Despite theoretical benefits, several therapies have failed to consistently show efficacy in slowing CKD progression in clinical trials.

Uric Acid-Lowering Therapy

Although hyperuricemia has been associated with CKD progression, trials evaluating urate-lowering agents have not shown clear kidney benefits.134 The CKD-FIX trial evaluated allopurinol in asymptomatic patients; despite achieving reductions in serum uric acid levels, there was no meaningful difference in eGFR decline versus placebo.135 The PERL trial, a 3-year trial in T1D, reported no kidney benefit from uric acid reduction.136

Given that the data do not support lowering serum uric acid to benefit kidney progression, KDIGO 2024 guidelines do not recommend initiating urate-lowering therapy in patients with asymptomatic CKD.7 However, in individuals with clinical indications (e.g., gout or tophus deposition), treatment for that indication remains.137

Bardoxolone

Bardoxolone methyl is a synthetic triterpenoid that activates nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor, a transcription factor regulating oxidative stress, inflammation, and metabolism.138 Early phase studies in DKD suggested that bardoxolone could improve eGFR.139 However, the phase 3 BEACON trial was terminated early because of higher risk of hospitalization and death from HF in CKD stage G4 and type 2 diabetes. Despite an initial eGFR rise, bardoxolone did not reduce kidney failure events and was associated with increased proteinuria and BP, unlike RASi and SGLT2i, likely from maladaptive glomerular hyperfiltration.140

The CARDINAL trial in Alport syndrome similarly failed to show a meaningful benefit.141 Although the AYAME trial in diabetic CKD met its primary end points without significant safety issues, it failed to lower kidney failure events, prompting discontinuation of FALCON (autosomal dominant polycystic disease) and EAGLE (extension study).142, 143, 144 In 2021, the US Food and Drug Administration advisory committee unanimously voted against approval in Alport syndrome, citing limited kidney benefit and safety concerns (hypertension, HF, liver enzyme abnormalities, hyperfiltration injury). Although bardoxolone did not reduce kidney failure events, its effects on eGFR and oxidative stress sustain ongoing interest, and research may define if it has a role in delaying CKD progression.145

Applying Precision Medicine To Ckd Care

The combination of improved mechanistic insights into CKD pathophysiology and expanding therapeutic options holds promise for personalized medicine in nephrology. Integrating phenotypic and multiomics data may optimize care by matching treatment intensity to a patient’s risk of disease progression, identifying who will respond best, those at risk for adverse effects, and potentially providing primary prevention of kidney diseases.146, 147, 148 Currently available biomarkers (eGFR, uACR, and kidney biopsy data) are useful in providing individualized prognosis for people with CKD, and often guiding treatments, but fall short of providing the detail and specificity seen in oncology, where biomarkers guide targeted therapies.144,149, 150, 151

Within DKD specifically, precision medicine is emerging from the need to match the right treatment for the right patient. This is starting to be reflected in guidelines, where recommendations for therapeutic choices are being made based on secondary considerations of treatment such as glycemic effects, impacts on weight loss, and risks of adverse events such as hyperkalemia.152 This is a necessary step toward broader application of precision medicine and incorporation of newer biomarkers and genetic data into our decision-making. These advances may ultimately lead to both the development and subsequent use of targeted therapies that cure kidney disease rather than addressing downstream sequelae.153

One of the key biomarkers going forward will be genetic testing, which is increasingly used as a key step in CKD diagnosis. Even with limited targeted therapies available, genetic diagnoses lead to meaningful care changes, including avoiding ineffective treatments, enabling family cascade testing, and guiding targeted therapies.154,155 Beyond diagnostics, genetic data can enable safer medication prescribing and dosing through pharmacogenomics at multiple stages of a patient’s journey with CKD.156,157 Throughout nephrology, we are seeing rapid growth in studies evaluating disease-modifying targeted treatments across glomerular disorders (IgA nephropathy, lupus nephritis), genetic kidney disorders (APOL1-associated kidney disease, TRPC6-associated focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, primary hyperoxaluria), and others.158, 159, 160, 161 If successful, these may represent a paradigm shift toward personalized approach to CKD treatment and ultimately improve patients’ health.

Reimagining Ckd Care: From Silence To Strategy

CKD remains a silent burden, marked by low patient and clinician awareness despite its high prevalence and serious complications. Early recognition and timely interventions by clinicians are critical to slowing progression, preventing metabolic issues, and improving CV outcomes. Nephrologists play a key role in prescribing nephroprotective agents and guiding primary care providers; however, gaps in knowledge and implementation can delay diagnosis and management, leading to adverse patient outcomes.

We advocate for a proactive, individualized treatment approach that integrates evidence-informed guidance and considers each patient’s unique needs. Multidisciplinary models involving nephrologists, cardiologists, endocrinologists, primary care physicians, family medicine physicians, pharmacists, and dietitians offer benefits in reducing care fragmentation and improving outcomes. However, health care systems and funding models impact the ability to implement such care and should be taken into consideration.

Since 2017, therapeutic advances have reshaped CKD management. Future research will focus on optimizing combination therapy, sequencing and timing, while ensuring long-term safety and efficacy. Combination strategies are emerging as a key strategy in all causes of CKD, with evidence showing that SGLT2i, MRAs, GLP-1RAs, and ERAs provide additive kidney and CV benefits while minimizing risks. Targeted, disease-modifying treatments may become more widely available, allowing us to effectively treat specific causes of CKD rather than just the sequelae and offering a unique approach to addressing the residual multi-system risks.

Despite multiple effective therapies available, use in routine clinical practice remains suboptimal. To support clinicians in implementing the evidence into action, we offer a clinical checklist (Table 2), global system-level barriers with strategies (Supplementary Table S1), and practical key messages (Supplementary Table S2) to guide optimal decision-making for patients.
