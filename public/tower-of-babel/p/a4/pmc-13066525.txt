Construction of virtual whole eye model based on ultra-widefield optical coherence tomography in myopia
Authors: Xiangcheng Tang, Na Luo, Caiyi Chen, Pengsen Wu, Wenjun Guo, Yitong Shen, Juntao Jiang, Mingzhu FU, Li Huo, Jingfeng Mu, Meizhou Liu, Guiqin Liu, Shaochong Zhang, Xingzheng Pan, Wei Chi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Recognizing the inconvenience of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in evaluating the ocular morphology, this study developed a three-dimensional virtual whole eye model (CET-1) using ultra-widefield swept-source optical coherence tomography. The CET-1 were validated with MRI models in 80 eyes from 40 individuals with different extent of myopia. The result showed these two models have high consistency across different axial lengths (22.19 to 32.56 mm) —calculated as the mean point-to-surface distance, measuring 0.0183 ± 0.0025 normalized unit (439.57 ± 59.87 μm). Compared to the normal group, both of the posterior segment Gaussian curvature and anterior segment parameters demonstrated a significant change in myopic groups (P < 0.05). The CET-1 model provides a simple and effective tool that enables the establishment of a digital twin ocular model, facilitating the monitoring and quantitative analysis of myopia development and progression. Thus, supports optimized treatment strategies and contributes to the advancement of personalized medicine.

Introduction

Myopia currently affects about one-third of the global population and is projected to increase to one-half by 20501. Among this group, high myopia accounts for 10%2, which can lead to various ocular complications, including glaucoma, cataracts, macular degeneration, and retinal detachment. These conditions can result in a gradual decline in visual quality and, in severe cases, potential blindness3. Myopia has now been recognized as a global eye health threat. Although the pathological mechanisms of high myopia are still not fully understood, early prediction and prevention of myopia progression and its complications are primary goals in myopia research.

Elongation of the axial length and expansion of the posterior sclera are the leading sources of myopia progression, sometimes resulting in ocular deformities like scleral staphyloma. As early as 1854, Von Graefe was the first to observe the morphological changes in the fundus of myopic eyes—posterior staphyloma, while he did not pursue further research on the subject4. In 1977, Brian J. Curtin used indirect ophthalmoscopy to outline a classification of 10 different morphologies5. However, it was not until 1990 that magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was first applied to ocular imaging, providing the most intuitive data on eyeball morphology. MRI uses hydrogen atoms intrinsically present in ocular tissues to generate signals and is able to visualize the entire eye globe thanks to its non-optical nature6–8. Using volumetric three-dimensional (3D) MRI technology, Guo et al. correlated eyeball shape with myopic complications and visual prognosis, discovering that barrel-shaped and temporally tilted eyes exhibited significant myopic maculopathy9. However, due to limitations of MRI, such as high costs and accessibility, it is challenging to conduct large-scale and longitudinal studies, resulting in a lack of further guiding results.

Digital twins (DTs) are digital and virtual representations of physical entities that are widely used in the engineering and manufacturing industries for monitoring and modeling processes and optimizing efficiency10,11,12. The introduction and application of DTs in the medical field has become a hot topic in recent years13, potentially opening new avenues for myopia research. Given the close relationship between myopia and the morphological structure of the eye, establishing an ocular digital eye twin model that incorporates ocular structural parameters allows for the simulation of the ocular development trajectory of myopic eyes, facilitating quantitative analysis of myopic progression. This approach not only enhances our understanding of the mechanisms behind myopia but also aids in identifying high-risk factors for pathological myopia, ultimately contributing to personalized prevention and control of high myopia. Theoretically, creating anatomically accurate ocular DTs based on MRI models is the ideal method. However, due to the challenges of conducting large-scale examinations and follow-ups with MRI, there is an urgent need for clinically accessible ophthalmological imaging.

Recently, advancements in ultra-widefield swept-source optical coherence tomography (SS-OCT) technology and equipment have enabled 3D scanning of the posterior retina, covering a range of 120–150°. Using this new tool, several studies have calculated the Gauss curvature of the posterior retina to assess high myopia retinoschisis and myopic complications14–16, making it applicable to research on the early onset of myopia in children17. However, these studies have overlooked information in the anterior segment. Moreover, while they claim to use MRI data for comparison, they have not achieved true quantitative comparisons with MRI models, leaving the accuracy of the OCT models unvalidated. To achieve a more comprehensive and precise measurement of ocular morphology, our research team developed a 3D whole eye virtual model (Chi Eye Twins, CET-1) based on a novel radial scanning mode utilizing SS-OCT. Over the past two decades, several research teams have made substantial contributions in the field of whole-eye OCT, optimizing OCT assessments of the retinal periphery and improving the accuracy of peripheral retinal refraction, laying the foundation for this study18–20. However, there are still certain differences from the physical morphology of the real eyeball, and they have not sufficiently captured the specific morphological features of the retina. Our model, based on high resolution, improves accuracy. By integrating OCT scan data into the ocular modeling process, the model’s precision in geometric reconstruction and optical simulation can be significantly enhanced. We aim to provide an alternative tool to MRI examination for use in myopia patients and leverage current big data and artificial intelligence technologies to contribute to global myopia prevention and control efforts.

Discussion

The application of DTs in the medical field will be groundbreaking21. Virtual models established in the metaverse can be used for clinical trials, real-time monitoring of physical entities, and predicting future state transitions through embodied AI agents22–24. Recently, DTs have been successfully generated for multiple diseases25,26. In ophthalmology. DTs might has a very promising application prospect in various fields of ophthalmic diseases, including glaucoma, orbital diseases, macular degeneration, especially in myopia research. On one hand, myopia is a global issue affecting over one-third of the global population, making it essential to improve prediction and prevention of visual impairment caused by related complications. On the other hand, advancements in digital technology and equipment have enabled large-scale collection of eye data. It is believed that DT technology will provide a significant opportunity for myopia research.

To date, due to limitations in examination equipment, the patterns of change in the anterior segment, equatorial region, and posterior segment of myopia eye were remain poorly defined. Although previous studies have investigated myopic eyeballs using indirect ophthalmoscopy, MRI, and OCT5,15,27–29, there is still a lack of large-sample analyzes that can comprehensively evaluate the association of ocular deformation with myopia progression, myopic complications, and visual acuity across all age groups. Identifying a convenient method to establish a DT whole eye model that integrates high-throughput genetic and molecular approaches, single-cell and whole-genome sequencing, big data, cloud-based electronic medical records, and AI could provide revolutionary insights into myopia research. Theoretically, a DT eye model based on MRI is considered the ideal approach; however, its limited accessibility remains a major constraint. Our study addresses these shortcomings by establishing a virtual whole eye model, CET-1, which enables rapid collection of data from the anterior and posterior segments of myopic eyeballs and facilitates the monitoring of the entire myopia lifecycle. We believe that large-scale data collection with CET-1 can offer valuable insights into myopia research.

The accuracy of the CET-1 model is a key focus of this study. Previous attempts to perform 3D reconstruction of the retina using SS-OCT had several drawbacks14–17. First, the scan time was longer (10–15 s) and easily affected by eye movement. Additionally, these studies only implemented AL correction without refractive correction, which may have impacted the model’s accuracy, especially in cases of high myopia. Furthermore, none of the previous studies quantitatively confirmed comparability with MRI morphology. To address these issues, we developed a new radial scanning model that completes anterior and posterior segment scans within 3–5 s each, facilitating implementation in children. More importantly, we conducted quantitative cross-modal comparisons of the CET-1 and MRI models within the same 3D space. The results demonstrated high accuracy regardless of the eye size, particularly within the posterior clinically crucial area, containing the macula and optic disc (Supplementary Fig. 4B), with errors were mainly concentrated at the peripheral edges of the model. Moreover, the current MRI resolution ranges from 0.5 to 1 mm, while the OCT model resolution reaches 1 μm, representing a 1000-fold improvement. This high resolution allows the CET-1 model to simultaneously display retinal layer information, providing better replaceability and advantages compared to MRI.

Previous studies have reported increases in ACD and ACV in myopic eyes30,31, our results also confirmed this. Moreover, we found that the ACA, SSA, AOD, and TISA all showed significant increases with myopia progression both in 500 μm and 700 μm (700 μm data not shown). Morphological changes of the lens in myopic eyes have been reported to be related to age. In adults, HM eyes present thinner lens thickness (LT), tilt, and power32. In children and adolescents with HM eyes, the lens exhibits reduced power but adopts a more curved shape without a decrease in LT33. In our study, the average age of the participants was over 30, and the decrease in LV may be related to the thinner LT and lower accommodation ability34. High myopia is a known risk factor for primary open-angle glaucoma and cataracts35,36, although direct and convincing evidence is still lacking. Analyzing the anterior segment of myopic eyes using the CET-1 model aims to provide more useful information in this regard.

In the analysis of the posterior segment, this model demonstrated excellent accuracy, especially in 16 eyes from the EM group (Supplementary Fig. 3 and Movies). In a preliminary analysis, Gaussian curvature showed significant differences as myopia progressed, particularly in the HM group, consistent with the findings of Luo et al.15. Surface Gaussian curvature can serve as an assessment indicator for high myopia. We believe that our new model, combined with data collected from larger sample sizes and more detailed analyzes of the fundus region, will aid in more thorough classification and provide additional data for monitoring myopia.

This model has certain limitations. First, due to current restrictions in OCT technology, the scan cannot cover the entire eye. The CET-1 model was reconstructed using a virtual equatorial region, which may omit important structural information about the equatorial area. Second, this study focused on comparing the accuracy of the CET-1 and MRI models. Due to ethical constraints, we did not recruit children and were unable to provide comparative results for pediatric eye data. Furthermore, the sample size in this study was relatively small; the preliminary analysis of anterior and posterior segment data from 70 eyes with varying degrees of myopia may contain some bias, necessitating further research to confirm the findings.

Methods

This study was approved by the institutional committee on medical ethics of Shenzhen eye hospital and adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki (2024KYPJ012). Written informed consent was obtained from all cases.

Study participants

This study was conducted at the Shenzhen Eye Hospital. All participants underwent comprehensive ophthalmic assessments, including best corrected visual acuity evaluation, diopter measurement, fundus examination, intraocular pressure measurement, and SS-OCT 3D radial scans, and a written informed consent was obtained from all cases. Participants with ocular diseases that could interfere with fundus imaging were excluded. Refractive errors were measured as spherical equivalent refraction (SER) in diopters. The IOL Master 500 (Carl Zeiss Meditec, Jena, Germany) was used to measure AL. Eyes were categorized based on SER as follows: normal control (NC) (−0.5D < SER), low myopia (LM) (−3D < SER ≤ −0.5D), mild myopia (MM) (−6D < SER ≤ −3D), high myopia (HM) (−9D < SER ≤ −6D), and extreme myopia (EM) (SER ≤ −9D) groups. Eligible participants then underwent an MRI and an SS-OCT scan for paired analysis.

Ultra-widefield SS-OCT scan

To obtain ocular structural data more accurately and quickly, we developed a new radial scanning mode (Supplementary Fig. 2) based on SS-OCT equipment (BM-400K BMizar; TowardPi Medical Technology Co., Ltd, Beijing, China). Compared to traditional 3D scanning modes, this radial scanning method has a shorter imaging time (3–5 s), generates fewer overall data, and is less affected by eye movements. Moreover, it requires less data correction and computation, making it more convenient, efficient, and accurate for reconstructing the actual physical morphology of the anterior and posterior segments of the eye. The participants were instructed not to blink and to keep their eyes focused on the bright dot of the OCT during scanning. The scans were assessed by an experienced clinician and repeated if necessary. The anterior segment scan was set to a width of 16 mm and a depth of 6 mm, covering the entire cornea and part of the sclera (Fig. 6A). Virtual whole eye model (CET-1) reconstruction

To enhance the accuracy of the data, the CET-1 model software integrates an image correction function for both anterior and posterior segments. Following refractive calibration, the anterior OCT images are processed using a deep learning model nnU-Net to segment tissues and automatically extract layered contours, including those of the cornea, sclera, iris, and lens. The nnU-Net segmentation algorithm was developed jointly by our team and the company (TowardPi Medical Technology Ltd, Beijing, China). Training and validation were conducted using a private dataset. The training set for the anterior segment segmentation AI algorithm comprises 1576 images, with 328 images in the validation set, while the posterior segment segmentation AI algorithm training set comprises 3418 images, with 560 images in the validation set. The optical surfaces, such as the cornea and lens, are fitted and reconstructed into a 3D model using the Zernike method37, while the iris surface is reconstructed using the actual collected data (Fig. 6A).

Variations in the AL of the eye leads to differences in the fundus data obtained from OCT scanning. The software incorporates a correction function for axial difference in retinal magnification15,38,39. In the corrected OCT images, the Bruch’s membrane layered contour is selected to represent the ocular contour parameters (Fig. 6B). By combining the AL with the spatial position of the corneal vertex, we achieve 3D reconstruction of the posterior segment (Fig. 6B, C). For the middle portions of the sclera that are not visible, we perform constrained fitting and reconstruction by integrating the layering lines of both the anterior and posterior segments. This approach allows us to create a comprehensive 3D model of the entire eye by merging the reconstructed tissues of the anterior and posterior segments (Fig. 6B, and Supplementary Movie 1).

Orbital MRI and comparison with CET-1

To determine the accuracy of the CET-1 model, all patients underwent imaging with a 3 Tesla clinical MRI system (MAGNETOM Spectra; Siemens, Munich, Germany), covering the region from the supraorbital margin to the inferior border of the maxillary sinus. The participants lay supine in a comfortable position, with their heads fixed in a 20-channel head coil (Siemens, Germany). Additionally, they were asked not to wear eye makeup on their eyelids to prevent metallic susceptibility artifacts. During the scan, they were instructed to keep their eyes closed, remain awake, and refrain from engaging in any mental activity. The imaging plane was aligned to intersect with the orientation of the optic nerve. A 3D T1-weighted image was acquired using the volumetric interpolated breath-hold examination sequence with the following parameters: repetition time (TR), 6.41 ms; echo time (TE), 2.46 ms; flip angle, 9°; parallel acceleration factor, 2; total acquisition time, 2 min and 50 s. The in-plane resolution was 0.7 mm × 0.7 mm with a slice thickness of 1 mm. A total of 56 slices were acquired to cover the entire eyeball. T1-weighted imaging was chosen for its ability to clearly delineate the outer contour of the eyeball, including the posterior sclera—features not visible in OCT or previous T2-weighted MRI-based models6,9.

The 3D structural reconstruction of ocular MRI included the eyeball and optic nerve; the reconstruction methods are detailed in Supplementary Fig. 1. Briefly, we used contour segmentation methods to quickly extract the eyeball shape by delineating it in the transverse, sagittal, and coronal planes (Supplementary Fig. 1A). For the reconstruction of the optic nerve used for localization, we employed a single-step segmentation algorithm where seed points were used to enable the computer to automatically determine the threshold for that location. By defining the extraction area, the optic nerve could be rapidly extracted (Supplementary Fig. 1B). The minimum units of the MRI model and the whole eye model are millimeters and micrometers, respectively.

Alignment of the MRI and CET-1 models

To facilitate quantitative comparison, the MRI model served as reference. A realignment process was introduced to ensure accurate and reproducible comparison between the two modalities (Supplementary Fig. 1C, D). The corneal, anterior lens, and posterior lens surfaces were fitted with fourth-order polynomial curves, and their vertices were defined as the points with the lowest anterior–posterior coordinates. A line connecting these vertices was used to estimate an optical axis, and its intersection with the retinal surface defined a virtual foveal position. The optic disc was defined as the extension of the optic nerve to the retinal plane. In the CET-1 model, the corresponding landmarks (corneal vertex, virtual fovea, and optic disc center) were determined using the anterior and posterior segment SS-OCT data, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 6A).

A rotational angle was then estimated from the two-dimensional MRI slice passing through the corneal vertex and lens center by calculating the angle between the optical-axis vector and the vertical axis of the slice, while the rotational axis is the out-of-plane axis. This angle was applied to the entire MRI volume using imrotate3 in MATLAB to standardize its anatomical orientation (Supplementary Fig. 6B). Rigid alignment between the MRI and CET-1 was achieved by translating both models so that their optic disc centers coincided at the origin. This was followed by matching the MRI model so that its corneal vertex/fovea vector aligned with the corresponding CET-1 vector. A rigid iterative closest point algorithm (pcregistericp, MATLAB) was then used to fine-tune the alignment.

To ensure the accuracy of this objective realignment, an experienced ophthalmologist visually inspected the overlaid CET-1 and MRI models, focusing on clinically critical regions such as the optic disc and the foveal region. The horizontal plane, defined by the fovea and the optic disc in the projected fundus view, was used solely for a qualitative check of the physiological orientation.

Quantitative comparison of MRI and CET-1 models

Two models were exported into the MATLAB platform for cross-modality comparison. Statistical analysis

The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess normality. The gender distribution in each group was expressed as the proportion of females to males. The mean and standard deviation of the main parameters were calculated. The consistency between the MRI- and OCT-derived models was evaluated using two quantitative metrics: point-to-surface distance and RMSD. The group comparisons between the different myopia groups were analyzed using generalized estimating equations to reduce the impact of the correlation between the two eyes of the same individual. Regression lines were plotted to illustrate the relationships between quantitative measurements and AL. Statistical significance was set at a p-value less than 0.05. All statistical analyzes were performed using Python or R Programming Language.
