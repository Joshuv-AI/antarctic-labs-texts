An evaluation of machine learning for soil analysis in internet of things-enabled smart farming
Authors: Preety Chaudhary, Preeti Gulia, Nasib Singh Gill, Noha Alduaiji, Piyush Kumar Shukla, Susheela Vishnoi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Soil plays a foundational role in sustaining agricultural productivity and ecological stability, yet traditional soil analysis methods remain labour-intensive, slow, and often inadequate for real-time decision-making in modern precision agriculture. With the rise of Agriculture 4.0, machine learning (ML) and Internet of Things (IoT) technologies offer transformative potential for accurate, scalable, and data-driven soil assessment. Current research in this domain remains relatively fragmented, with a lack of clarity in models, sensing methodologies, and algorithmic strategies that produce the highest accuracy and operational value across diverse agricultural contexts. To address this gap, this research used a PRISMA-guided systematic literature review to systematically examine contemporary machine learning (ML) and IoT-based soil analysis methodologies. The review utilized sophisticated search queries across databases: Scopus, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, for identifying studies published between 2019 and 2024. After rigorous screening that involved removing duplication and full-text assessment of the retrieved entries, 77 high-quality articles were identified that met the eligibility criteria from an initial set of 180 entries. Data extraction was performed under descriptive and thematic synthesis, thereby allowing the comparative evaluation of supervised and unsupervised learning models, IoT sensing frameworks, soil parameters, dataset characteristics, evaluation metrics, and deployment constraints. Comparative analysis revealed that the use of supervised models, such as Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Gradient Boosting Machine (GBM), Convolutional Neural Networks (CNN), and deep ensembles, produces higher accuracy in the classification of soil quality, fertility, pH, and nutrient levels, especially in structured datasets like the Soil Fertility Dataset. IoT-based sensing systems significantly improve the reliability of predictions by offering continuous and detailed measurements of soil moisture, nutrient status, and environmental conditions.

Introduction

One of the most important elements of the Earth is the soil, alongside the atmosphere and liquid water. According to environmental studies for nature conservation, the following definition of soil largely satisfies the goals: Soil is an intricate, three-phase, multifaceted open structure that emerges on the crust of Earth as a result of interactions between organisms and parent mineral components, occasionally aided by human activity1.

Soil is unique due to its temporal and geographical variability, multiplicity, and occasionally multidirectional effects from earth-forming forces. Because of the heterogeneity of the parent geology rocks, flora, relief, fauna, and activity by humans, soils vary spatially. The term “soil memory” refers to some characteristics that soils hold onto for a long time. “Soil moments” are the terms used to describe the fast changes in soil properties over hours or days. Natural soils are the most vital component of the Earth’s biodiversity, but because human activity is continually changing them, they might undergo the full transformation and long-term retention of their new characteristics, which aren’t necessarily good for the stability of ecosystems. Currently, soils are regarded as non-renewable natural resources (for practical durations)1. In agriculture, the soil is an essential non-renewable resource; therefore, its sustainability is crucial2.

The purpose of soil in agriculture contributes to its close relationship with people’s daily lives. For people and all living things, the soil is their natural source of nourishment3. About 95% of meals are created either directly or indirectly through the soil, according to the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations in 2015. According to the Natural Resources Conservation Service (USDA, n.d.), Soil is a typical substance found in the earth and a natural component that consists of solids (such as organic materials and minerals, gases, and liquids. This emphasizes the significance of soil since food is the primary source of nourishment and energy for humans. The world is paying increasing attention to food productivity efficiency because of concerns about food insecurity and supply; smart agriculture keeps soil health intact by providing crops with more precise nutrients4. Due to population expansion, the quantity of soil used for agriculture has decreased over the last few decades. The expanding population demands a rise in the production of food grains on viable land. To improve food production and maintain the condition of the soil for future generations to come, sustainable farming methods are becoming more and more important5.

Methodology for systematic literature review (SLR)

The components of a systematic literature review are the search parameter specification, review methodology, and research questions that are developed. It will be performed through the clear definition of research questions and objectives, the subsequent selection of the necessary sources and databases, and the systematic search of the chosen paper in accordance with the eligibility criteria that have been defined. The search phrase is a query that contains all the keywords required in a research query, and their synonyms.

Research design

The research design of the study is systematic and follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework that provides methodological transparency, reproducibility, and coverage of the existing literature on the topic of soil analysis using ML, DL, and IoT technologies. PRISMA was chosen due to the fact that it offers a systematic and rigorous guide to the identification, screening, assessment, and synthesis of peer-reviewed studies, which minimizes selection bias and is relevant to the state of knowledge of smart agriculture. The research design will start with a clear statement of the purpose and research questions of the review and then proceed to the formulation of a powerful search strategy. Several academic databases were searched in detail, such as Scopus, Web of Science, IEEE Xplore, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, and Google Scholar. It was done through the systematic implementation of controlled keywords and Boolean combinations applicable to soil analysis, ML, IoT-based smart agriculture, soil fertility prediction, and precision farming technologies. In the identification step, all the potentially relevant records that were published during the given time period were accessed. Automated and manual cross-checking were used to remove duplicate entries. During the screening stage, titles and abstracts were critically assessed in relation to the set inclusion and exclusion criteria to select the studies that specifically used ML, DL, the IoTs, or a combination of these methods to study soil, soil quality, nutrient monitoring, and soil type classification or data-driven crop recommendations. At this stage, articles that lacked adequate methodological description, non-agricultural uses, and articles that were not relevant to soil analytics were filtered out. The eligibility stage involved a critical review of the rest of the articles, with a focus on methodological rigor, experiment design, datasets, performance measures, and correspondence with the research questions. The papers that included empirical model development of ML and DL, IoT-based soil sensing architectures, or holistic smart agriculture systems were chosen to be reviewed, and the ones that failed to meet the minimum requirements of validation or made invalid claims were filtered out. The PRISMA flow diagram was used to capture all the eligible studies as a whole, with the number of records identified, screened, and excluded at each stage of the process being recorded. The data extraction was performed with the help of the standardized coding scheme to extract the required features, such as algorithmic approaches, data sources, sensor types, sensed soil properties, testing methodologies, and limitations expressed. The thematic and comparative analysis allowed extracting data to synthesize them and find recurring trends, strengths, and limitations within methodological approaches, and research gaps.

The PRISMA-based PRISMA-driven article selection workflow (illustrated in Fig. 2) is a systematic and comprehensive description of the methods applied in the process of identifying, screening, appraising, and incorporating the final corpus of studies into the qualitative synthesis. The identification phase started with retrieving 180 documents in key academic search engines, i.e., Google Scholar and Semantic Scholar. The earliest records were the broadest possible range of possibly useful literature. Following a thorough evaluation of the titles and abstracts, 45 articles were eliminated as they did not correspond to the inclusion criteria, in particular, they were not related to soil analysis, ML, or applications of IoT, or context in the context of smart agriculture. This initial filtering reduced the data to 135 records to be further filtered. At the Screening stage, the rest of the records were searched against duplicates, and 34 duplicate articles were identified and rejected so that only original studies would be forwarded to the next phase. The end of the screening process resulted in 101 unique records, after which those were moved to a full-text evaluation in the Eligibility phase. In turn, the Eligibility stage encompassed a comprehensive judgment about methodological rigor, alignment with the research questions, and the extent to which each study effectively addressed how soil analysis was applied through ML/DL, IoT, or advanced agricultural systems. Following this initial screening, an additional 24 articles were excluded based on the fact that their full texts either did not emphasize soil analysis or failed to present or discuss foundational principles of soil science. In total, 77 articles met all the pre-defined inclusion criteria in the last step and, therefore, formed part of the qualitative synthesis. These studies formed the basis for investigating the status of developing technologies for application in the assessment of soils and precision agriculture.

Search parameters and quality

The search strategy for this systematic literature review was designed to be comprehensive, transparent, and reproducible so that it includes all relevant investigations related to soil analysis using ML, IoTs, and smart agriculture technologies. This has been achieved by using structured search parameters and pre-set quality assessment standards. The search strings were progressively developed with the identification of core concepts from initial readings, and refinements were done through keyword mapping and validation against controlled vocabularies such as MeSH, IEEE taxonomy terms, and Scopus index keywords.

The final search query included a comprehensive list of related words and phrases in a bid to maximize coverage while minimizing omissions. To enhance retrieval precision, Boolean operators, proximity operators, and wildcard expressions were added to the core keywords that included “soil analysis,” “soil quality,” “soil fertility,” “ML,” “deep learning,” “IoT,” “sensor networks,” “precision agriculture,” “smart farming,” and “soil monitoring.” The Scopus advanced search query was built as follows: TITLE-ABS-KEY ((“soil analysis” OR “soil quality” OR “soil fertility” AND machine learning” OR deep learning” AND IoT” OR IoTs” AND smart agriculture) OR precision agriculture). This successfully included the papers published between 2019 and 2024, which can be considered the current trends in Agriculture 4.0, sensing technologies, and AI-driven soil analytics.

Similar search adaptations were conducted in a number of databases, including the ACM Digital Library, IEEE Xplore, and ScienceDirect. All the adaptations were based on the indexing structures and search facilities of the respective platforms. Google Scholar was used as a supplementary source to identify high-citation conference papers, items in early-access that are not fully indexed in the primary databases, and grey literature. A quality assessment was conducted systematically after the search phase to make sure that the papers included were rigorous and relevant. All retrieved articles were screened according to a set of inclusion and exclusion criteria on the type of publication, clarity of methodology, experimental validity, and topicality to the study topics. Only the peer-reviewed journal articles, conference proceedings, book chapters, and high-quality reviews that directly cover the topic of soil analysis, ML/DL modeling, IoT-based soil sensing, or integrated smart agriculture systems have been considered in the assessment.

Publications on non-agricultural data, publications that are not empirically validated, and agronomic theory without technological intervention were not included. Additional quality parameters were providing clear descriptions of the research design, providing detailed descriptions of the dataset, algorithmic transparency, the ability to reproduce the results, and high evaluation metrics such as accuracy, RMSE, F1-score, AUC, and confusion matrix analysis. Each study was evaluated in terms of the methodological quality using modified quality assessment checklists according to PRISMA, CASP (Critical Appraisal Skills Programme), and AMSTAR-2 guidelines. The selection strategy enabled the inclusion of the sources that could be deemed as valid, reliable, and transparent regarding the methodology.

Data analysis and synthesis

The data analysis and synthesis process followed a structured, multi-stage procedure aligned with the nature of the studies included through the PRISMA-based selection process. After the identification of the eligible articles, a systematic coding framework was created to retrieve, categorize, and analyze the information that was relevant. The most important data that were obtained with the help of the coding scheme were the nature of the ML or DL model used, the parameters of the soil that were investigated, the characteristics of the dataset, the settings of the IoT sensors, the indicators of evaluation, the benefits of the technology, and the limitations that were observed. All the studies were carefully reviewed, and the information obtained in each of them was properly organized into tables to allow drawing a clear comparison of the findings on the methodological, algorithmic, and application-specific levels. The analytical paradigm was combined with descriptive synthesis and thematic synthesis paradigms. The general synthesis was to measure the prevalence of various types of studies, determine existing algorithms, evaluate the source of datasets, and summarize the most common parameters of soil that have been studied in the literature. The research trends observed during this period were the dominance of supervised learning model applications in the classification of soil, the emerging interest in the DL model to analyze soil images, and the increasing use of IoT sensors to monitor soil in real-time. Thematic synthesis was performed to identify existing themes in the statements about the issues surrounding model performance, technological integration barriers, and opportunities of smart soil analysis. The analysis performed addressed sensor reliability and data quality concerns from the perspective of model performance, the significance of chosen features, and the complexity of the algorithm to soil prediction accuracy, and operational constraints to IoT-based soil sensing systems. A comparative synthesis was conducted to study in a systematic way differences between ML and DL approaches, respectively, and conventional methods of soil analysis in terms of their comparative advantages and trade-offs. The synthesis brought together the results about predictive accuracy, scalability, computational intensity, and practical feasibility in order to derive meaningful information. Special consideration was made to the research that used benchmark datasets, including the Soil Fertility Dataset, in order to evaluate the performance of models in controlled conditions. Finally, synthesized results were discussed within the framework of the research questions to allow explaining the current state of the art, defining the issues that have not been addressed yet, and suggesting future perspectives on how the further development of ML- and IoT-enabled soil analysis can be improved.

Soil characteristics and properties

Soil characteristics and properties entail physical, chemical, and biological factors that determine the ability of the soil to sustain the growth of plants, water retention, and ecosystem processes. The soil properties are presented in Fig. 3. The principal properties of soil are the following:

Physical properties: Density, texture, and structure are the physical properties of the soil. IoT sensors for soil analysis

The IoT sensors are becoming more and more crucial in agriculture, especially for soil analysis. Energy efficiency, storage, mobility, computing power, robustness, dependability, and affordability are the key characteristics of IoT devices for agriculture36. A difficult objective for academics and farmers is the era of innovative farming and precision agriculture (PA), with a focus on environmentally friendly farming. Several scientific studies provide advice for optimizing resource use by employing sensor systems to monitor all agricultural management operations effectively and precisely37.

Farmers may make more informed choices when it comes to fertilization, watering, and other elements of crop management with the use of these sensors, which can detect a wide range of parameters that affect plant growth and health. The intelligent sensor node consists of three parts: Sense, Analyse, and Communicate. Actual world characteristics, such as moisture, temperature, and so on, are captured by the sensor part. The component for communication makes sure that the gateway and its sensor nodes may share information with other gateway nodes and that the computational component can preprocess the collected parameter value. Several sensors can be used to measure and compute a farming field’s specs38. The EC (conductivity) value, PH value, soil water content, and many other parameters were monitored by the soil sensors39. Several sensors are mostly used in agricultural IoT to gather data40. The following are a few of the most popular kinds of IoT sensors used in soil analysis:

Soil moisture sensors

The quantity of moisture in the soil is measured by these sensors. To prevent overwatering or submerging their crops, farmers need to know this information for effective irrigation management. Soil moisture sensors come in a variety of forms, such as tensiometers, resistance-based sensors, and capacitance-based sensors.

Soil temperature (sensors)

Soil temperature, as measured with sensors, assumes an important role in modulating microbial activity, plant development, and nutrient uptake. By deploying IoT soil temperature sensors, farmers can observe the soil temperature and protect crops against extreme heat.

Soil pH (sensors)

A pH meter can be used to determine the acidity and alkalinity of the soil13. Due to the varied pH preference of various plants, soils with high acidity or high alkalinity can greatly limit the supply of some essential nutrients. IoT-enabled soil sensors of pH enable farmers to check the soil pH and make the required adjustments.

Soil electrical conductivity (sensors)

The EC of the soil is used to measure the level of dissolved salts in the soil. The salinity or compaction might be present due to high levels of EC. They both are capable of affecting root systems negatively and, hence, hindering plant growth. The IoT soil EC sensors enable farmers to be able to monitor the soil EC and make the right decisions to control the salinity or compaction.

Soil nitrate (sensors)

Nitrate is among the most significant nutrients that are vital in the growth of plants, and its determination using sensors is important in determining the health of the soil. Nitrate levels are dangerous to human health. IoT-based fertilizer nitrate sensors assist farmers in measuring the nitrate level in their soils and also monitoring that their fields do not take in too much nitrate. IoT soil sensors can be transmitted remotely to a central repository where the data can be further monitored and analyzed. This data can be used to develop time-series analyses and soil condition maps, as well as more accurate irrigation and fertilization schedules. Farmers who have to produce more crops without harming the environment can consider IoT soil sensors as one of their assets.

Nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P), and potassium (P) (NPK) sensors

NPK sensors in the soil enable the accurate establishment of the amount of nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium in the soil. The fact that these three macronutrients are present not only means that the soil is fertile, but that the presence of these macronutrients is also critical in realizing the full potential of plants. Such a system directs the farmers on the most appropriate time to apply their fertilizers, thus saving on the micronutrient value of the soil41.

Machine learning techniques

AI is a broad field that creates systems to carry out activities that typically need human intellect by simulating human intelligence processes in robots and computers. A subset of artificial intelligence called ML aims to provide computers the ability to autonomously learn from supplied data via the use of statistical models and algorithms47. ML has been transforming several areas of computer science48. Simultaneously, ML algorithms such as Random Forests, Decision Trees, and Neural Networks forecast the nutrient requirements of the soil, promoting a more sustainable method of farming49. To make predictions or judgments, ML entails creating mathematical models to analyze data patterns50. It’s common to categorize these machine-learning methods as supervised or unsupervised51.

In the context of the reviewed works, supervised learning clearly dominates soil, crop, and environmental analytics, while unsupervised learning appears more sporadically, mainly for feature extraction, clustering, or trend discovery52–54. The following discussion focuses on the references in which ML or DL plays a substantive role, and comments for each reference explicitly highlight whether the work is grounded in supervised learning, unsupervised learning, or a combination of both55–58.

Comparative analysis of ML/DL techniques for soil analysis

Table 8 synthesizes the state of the art in ML and DL approaches for soil analysis by contrasting the targeted tasks, data sources, and methodological benefits with their practical constraints. The body of work shows a clear predominance of classical ML methods for tabular, sensor-based, and spectral soil data, with DL emerging more strongly in image-based and multi-parameter prediction tasks. Works like Huang et al.4, Islam et al.11, Vibhute et al.12, and Pant et al.61 demonstrate the use of Random Forest (RF), Support Vector Machines (SVM), Gradient Boosting, and ensemble learning on the prediction of soil physicochemical properties for nutrient monitoring, spectral soil classification, and fertility assessment using field data, IoT measurement data, and analytical datasets. These models are further good at finding nonlinear relationships between features and soil characteristics, which improves classification and regression performances related to soil suitability, fertility, and pH prediction, along with automated decision-making in soil and crop management4,11,12,26,59,61,69. However, these methods suffer from limitations such as regional dependencies due to data, sensitivity to sensor noise and calibration issues, class imbalance in datasets, and decreased model interpretability in large ensembles, which may affect the generalization and transparency of real-world deployments as seen in4,8,13,26,59–61. Several research studies have highlighted cost-effective, IoT-enabled soil sensing environments integrated with ML models in a smart agriculture environment. The studies by Shahab et al.7, Reshma et al.13, Naresh and Munaswamy8, Senapaty et al.70, and Aarthi et al.68 integrate decision trees, regression models, Naïve Bayes, artificial neural networks, and SVM with IoT sensor networks. This aids in real-time monitoring of nutrients, moisture, pH, and fertility indices of the soil to enable improved precision agriculture and backyard gardening.

Such systems help to maintain constant soil health monitoring, automatic crop suggestions, and economical soil examination in resource-restricted environments by utilizing smart farm IoT records and case-study applications7,8,11,13,68. Their limitations are primarily rooted in infrastructure and data quality, including power consumption, network reliability, small-scale or localized deployments, dependency on accurate sensor calibration, and difficulties in scaling the same models across heterogeneous agro-climatic zones7,8,11,13,68. In addition, specialized models such as Extreme Learning Machine for soil fertility indices46 and GBRT–DNN surrogate hybrids for soil suitability classification35 report faster training and improved predictive performance, but incur sensitivity to hyperparameters and demand for larger, well-curated datasets for stable generalization. DL is predominantly adopted where high-dimensional images or complex multi-feature patterns are involved.

Lightweight and advanced convolutional architectures are applied for soil image classification, segmentation, and field phenotyping in studies5,52,62,66, where CNN, RCNN, SegNet, and deep ensemble models operate on soil image datasets or large agricultural image collections. The employed methodologies have significantly improved the accuracy of classification and segmentation tasks, and they have allowed for automated irrigation strategies informed by visual indicators of soil conditions and for detailed evaluation of soil nutrient levels and pH from complex feature representations5,52,62,66. However, they typically suffer from the common weaknesses of deep learning, such as requiring large labeled image datasets, being sensitive to changes in illumination and environmental conditions of field images, having high computational needs, and requiring complex tuning of parameters that may be challenging for smallholders or institutions with low resources5,52,62,66. Expanding these benefits to more general soil prediction and soil quality assessment tasks, more generic DL approaches using CNNs, LSTMs, autoencoders, and deep neural networks achieve superior representation learning and extraction of non-linear patterns from open or multi-source agricultural datasets53,62,63,67. Although the performance of these models is impressive, they also come with drawbacks: overfitting is a problem when data is limited. The black-box character of these models poses a problem in terms of explainability, which is becoming widely considered an extremely desirable feature of a successful agronomic decision-making process and policy translation53,63.

Hybrid and review-based works can serve as bridge studies, where ML and DL are considered in the context of precision agriculture. The hybrid ML and artificial intelligence operates in28,35,67, combining IoT systems, ANN, and surrogate deep models to improve the soil nutrient analysis, fertilizer recommendations, and precision analytics of agriculture, particularly in the special case of coconut farming. These articles demonstrate that AI implementation in combination with ground analysis can only work when it is accompanied by a robust sensing infrastructure and agronomic expertise. Yet, they also indicate multiple issues regarding hardware supply, climate change, and high volumes of training data demands28,35,67,71,72.

Challenges and future scope

The analysis of soil research as a part of the IoT-based smart agriculture demonstrates a wide range of technical and working issues, as well as significant prospects of development. In the article62, the authors argue that traditional analysis tools cannot be used to assess the soil in real-time and that recurrent neural network models struggle with the vanishing and exploding gradient issues, which hinder the stable training. According to reference98, limitations created by the scarcity of hardware resources, the size of the devices, and power consumption are all barriers to the large-scale implementation of the IoT. The weaknesses of laboratory-based measurement of soil fertility, inconsistent use of agrochemicals, increased soil pollution, and the inability to create inexpensive on-field soil analysis instruments are discussed in reference68. According to reference70, there are obstacles in predicting crop diseases, controlling IoT equipment, and handling the intricate flows of data that are inherent to the precision farming setting. Battery constraints of UAVs and IoT devices and the urbanization of agricultural lands are mentioned in99. The article100 highlights the challenges related to the use of electromagnetic field measurements in subsurface sensing and the necessity to adjust sensor nodes to environmental conditions, including temperature and moisture. The wider agricultural system issues, such as climate change and waste production, are described in reference101, which also covers the issues of the successful integration of modern IoT technologies. According to reference102, the farmers are still grappling with the problems of weather variability, soil fertility, and water-level problems, even though the IoT systems are trying to improve precision farming. Major technological challenges associated with the reliability, privacy, security, cost-effectiveness, interference, high volume of data processing, and regulatory compliance of IoT systems are addressed in reference103. The complications of irrigation control, greenhouse temperature control, and the effect of population growth on agricultural demand are discussed in reference104. The estimations expressed in these reports are indicative of a strong belief that smart agriculture, which is enabled by the IoT, will be revolutionary. The article62 presents the ISNpHC-WVE model, which is aimed at precision farming and real-time monitoring of nutrients. Reference98 predicts the development of IoT applications with the help of improved sensor networks that will allow the collection of agricultural data more efficiently. The improvements of the systems proposed in reference68 include monitoring more properties of soil and generating more complex IoT models to make correct predictions and monitor them in real time. As pointed out in reference70, the prospects of future research include the possibility of more accurate agricultural recommendations through the use of more advanced IoT sensors, ML algorithms, and satellite images. The importance of IoT and artificial intelligence in the context of improved food security and sustainable agriculture is echoed in reference99. Additional possibilities in further rural IoT monitoring, improved water management, and low-cost design of LPWAN sensor networks are listed in reference100. Reference101 predicts additional adoptions of the IoT to reduce waste and maximize the total production. The article102 supports the application of IoT innovations in the direction of enhanced monitoring and productivity, and it mentions that image processing techniques are used to analyze crop composition better. Reference103 concentrates on the consumer trust development based on the transparent monitoring and enhancement of agricultural productivity due to the broader use of the IoT. According to reference104, the opportunities in the future of energy-saving IoT systems, sensor self-diagnosis, and secure data exchange mechanisms are identified to enable a robust agricultural ecosystem105,106.

Result and discussion

Our detailed review of the literature has revealed important new directions and emerging themes that are essential for the advancement of ML and IoT technologies. In response to the original research questions, the research findings are summarized in this discussion, which also concludes a thorough examination of selected studies carried out between 2019 and 2024. Using smart farming techniques may have a good effect on several issues, such as reducing poverty, reducing cost, reducing analysis time107, increasing food production, promoting health through nutrient-dense crops, using water efficiently, implementing renewable energy, creating jobs, advancing technology, and being environmentally conscious29. Farmers may increase output and control waste using the IoT108.

Table 11 summarizes the reported accuracy of widely used ML and DL models applied in soil analysis across recent studies. Traditional ML models like Random Forest and Support Vector Machines are very accurate, generally between 80% and 92%. This performance can be explained by their strength in handling different soil datasets and capturing nonlinear relationships. In structured datasets applied for soil suitability evaluation, most traditional models are outperformed by gradient-boosting techniques such as XGBoost and GBRT, which reach an accuracy of up to 94%. KNNs and simple Decision Trees score from moderate to low due to the fact that they are prone to noisy IoT data and are prone to overfitting. Advanced DL models, able to capture both visual soil properties and temporal moisture or nutrient trends, such as CNNs and hybrid CNN-LSTM architectures, showed higher accuracies, often lying within the range of 87% to 96%. Deep ensembles, which combine CNN, DNN, and RF, have higher performances, reaching as high as 97% while consuming a large number of computational resources.

Table 12 lists the soil properties that are most frequently explored through ML and IoT-based methods. Soil pH and macro-nutrients like N-P-K are among the most important because of their strong impact on crop growth, fertilization needs, and soil health in general. For those parameters, ML models, and especially Random Forest and Support Vector Machines, are showing strong predictive skills, while IoT nutrient sensors support real-time monitoring. Soil moisture is a very critical parameter being explored in the literature. The integration of temporal IoT data with LSTM models allows for accurate forecasting and irrigation management. Organic matter and soil texture are considered of moderate importance, evaluated often by spectral analysis or through soil imagery, where CNN-based models have also shown quite good performances. Electrical conductivity, temperature, and soil color are the most commonly encountered parameters; however, they are more sensitive to sensor noise or changes in the environment. On the contrary, ML models continue to assist in early deficiency diagnosis, but research related to soil density and micronutrients such as zinc, iron, and manganese is less frequent due to smaller datasets and restricted sensor availability.

Table 13 shows key implications of IoT–ML integration in soil analysis. The reliability of IoT sensors is generally found to be moderately consistent across the studies due to calibration discrepancies, environmental interference, and device-specific variations that lead to compromised data integrity. However, continued collection of data via IoT systems significantly enhances the model accuracy over and above traditional manual sampling. Scalability is a major issue due to the requirement for reliable connectivity, regular maintenance, and resilient power solutions for extensive deployments of sensors. The performance results of real-time monitoring of soil have been very impressive, especially when LSTM or hybrid neural models are applied to IoT time-series data. However, battery-powered sensors limit long-term deployment in remote regions, and therefore, energy efficiency presents a pragmatic challenge for nodes. Network technologies such as LoRaWAN, WiFi, and contemporary 5G systems achieve remarkable improvements, of which 5G offers superlative throughput with minimum latency. Integration of soil databases, sensor APIs, and ML pipelines presents considerable complexity, especially in heterogeneous agricultural settings. While ML is cost-effective post-training, continuous integration and maintenance of IoT hardware devices require sustained expenses. User adoption is presently found to be low to moderate, primarily owing to limited technical literacy among farmers and infrastructural deficiencies inherent in rural areas.

Conclusions

The research methodically examines the latest developments in soil analysis made possible by ML, DL, and IoTs technologies in the overall framework of smart and precision agriculture. Through a PRISMA-based review and an organized data synthesis, this study finds specific trends in the algorithmic performance, soil parameters modeling, sensor integration, and issues of practical deployment. The findings indicate that ML and DL methods are much more accurate, efficient, and scalable in assessing the soil as compared to the traditional methods in the laboratory and manual methods in the field. In the case of soil fertility, nutrient levels, pH, moisture, and soil texture, supervised learning algorithms such as Random Forest, Support Vector Machines, and Gradient Boosting, combined with deep networks such as Convolutional Neural Networks and hybrid CNN-LSTM networks, make the predictive accuracy always high. Sensing systems with IoT capabilities also expand the analytic abilities by the continuous real-time data streams that facilitate dynamic tracking of soil conditions and enable accurate agricultural decisions. In the context of these developments, a number of limitations that do not allow the implementation to be practical are also quoted in this review. Problems with sensor reliability, noise in the environment, the heterogeneity of data, and high computing costs of highly sophisticated models and generalizability between regions of soil are still problems. Potentials of IoT infrastructures are high, yet they themselves are energy efficient, connected, and maintenance-related issues, particularly in rural and resource-poor settings. Additional important areas that emerge in this regard are associated with the assimilation of heterogeneous datasets and interpretable ML solutions. Such limitations may possibly indicate that, despite the high potential of technology, successful implementation will have to deal with infrastructural, technical, and socioeconomic variables.
