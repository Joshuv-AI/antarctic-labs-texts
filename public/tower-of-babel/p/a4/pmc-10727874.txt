Generalized Pustular Eruption in a Patient with Adult-Onset Immunodeficiency Due to Anti-Interferon-Gamma Autoantibodies
Authors: Hsuan-An Su, Hong-An Chen, Shu-Hui Wang, Yu-Chia Chen
Published: 2023

Dear Editor:

We report the case of a 67-year-old Taiwanese woman presenting with relapsing fever, arthralgia, left femoral purulent lymphadenopathy and extensive cutaneous erythematous pustular eruptions. The patient had type 2 diabetes mellitus for years. Her medical history in the past 4 months included Sweet’s syndrome, herpes simplex vulvitis, recurrent salmonellosis with bacteremia and non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infection with Mycobacterium intracellulare. The patient was hospitalized for recurrent NTM infection this time. She developed new-onset generalized erythematous patches with coalescing pustules and scales on the lower abdomen, lower back and proximal extremities (Fig. 1). A review of her medication history revealed polypharmacy, including cephalosporins, penicillins, antimycobacterial agents, antifungals, anti-inf lammatory drugs and so forth. Laboratory tests showed leukocytosis (37,820/µl) with neutrophil predominance (78%), minimal eosinophil count (<1%) and C-reactive protein elevation (5.037 mg/dl). Renal and hepatic panels were within normal limits. Potassium hydroxide (KOH) examination of the skin lesions revealed negative results. A pustule was biopsied. Histological specimens revealed focal parakeratosis, subcorneal pustules, Munro’s microabscess, acanthosis, exocytosis and minimal eosinophilic infiltration (Fig. 2). Differential diagnoses included pustular psoriasis and acute generalized exanthematous pustulosis (AGEP). Owing to the patient’s multiple opportunistic infections and reactive dermatologic presentation, anti-interferon-γ immunodeficiency syndrome (AIIS) was highly suspected, and anti-interferon gamma-1 (anti-IFN-γ) antibodies were detected in the serum. The patient was treated with systemic and topical antibiotics and corticosteroid treatments, and the pustules resolved gradually over 4 weeks without recurrence. Although diagnoses of AGEP with culprit drug history and pustular psoriasis with a Munro’s microabscess could not be excluded, the patient’s minimal eosinophilic profile and treatment response did not support these diagnoses. Therefore, a diagnosis of generalized pustular eruption with AIIS was made.

AIIS is a rare disease that is primarily reported in East and Southeast Asia1. The mean age of onset of AIIS is around 50 years and ranges from 20 to 78 years, without sex preponderance1,2. Generalized or localized lymphadenopathy (93.8%), prolonged fever (63.8%), and weight loss (48.8%) are typical manifestations of AIIS2. Patients are susceptible to opportunistic infections in the absence of human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) infection2. Up to 80% of cases involve cutaneous manifestations, including reactive dermatoses (e.g. generalized pustular eruption, Sweet’s syndrome) and infective dermatoses1,2. The diagnosis of AIIS has not been established in the literature. Based on the criteria adopted by Jutivorakool et al.1, we suggest AIIS be diagnosed if a patient meets the following criteria, (1) prescence of anti-IFN-γ antibody in the serum, (2) absence of HIV infection, and (3) at least two episodes of uncommon intracellular infections. NTM infection, reactive dermatoses and Asian ethnicity are of supportive diagnostic values.

Neutrophilic dermatoses may arise as a compensatory hyperactivation of innate immunity in response to mycobacterial and intracellular infections1,3. IFN-γ limits neutrophil recruitment and survival via inhibition of interleukin-17 (IL-17) production and other mechanisms4, and activates multiple important immunological functions against a wide variety of pathogens5. Therefore, the loss of inhibition of IL-17 production in the presence of anti-IFN-γ antibodies may contribute to abnormal accumulation of neutrophils without proper immunological functions in the skin.
