Why are rural hospitals closing in the U.S.? Predictors identified using explainable machine learning
Authors: Kiruthika Balakrishnan, Tesfamariam M. Abuhay, Hana E. Hinkle
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveRural hospital closures in the U.S. reduce access to essential healthcare services and worsen health and economic outcomes in rural communities. This study uses national longitudinal data and explainable machine learning (XML) to identify and interpret risk factors of rural hospital closures.Materials and methodsWe conducted a retrospective longitudinal study of U.S. rural hospitals using national datasets from 2011 to 2022, integrating hospital-level financial, operational, policy, population, and community data. Longitudinal feature engineering and statistical summarization were performed, class imbalance and multicollinearity were addressed using random undersampling and variance inflation factor (VIF) analysis, and an XGBoost classifier was trained using stratified three-fold cross-validation and Optuna for hyperparameter tuning. Model explainability was assessed using SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP).ResultsAmong 2,683 rural hospitals, 139 (5.2%) closed during the study period. The model achieved a recall of 86%, accuracy of 75%, and an AUC of 89%. The key variables linked to closure risk are sustained financial weakness and volatility (e.g., total margin, variability in return on equity, and current ratio), operational instability (low patient volume and variability in bed-days available), policy context (e.g., years since Medicaid expansion), and community context, including sociodemographic characteristics of the service area (e.g., proportion of Black or African American residents), which may reflect underlying structural and socioeconomic conditions rather than direct predictors or causal drivers of closure.DiscussionThese findings indicate that the risk of closure of rural hospitals reflects accumulated long-term financial and operational instability, as well as policy and community context, rather than short-term distress, highlighting the value of longitudinal and explainable modeling approaches.ConclusionLongitudinal data integrated into XML provides a practical approach for early warning systems that enable proactive monitoring and timely interventions, particularly for hospitals serving historically underserved communities.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14810-w.

Introduction

Rural hospitals are essential primary healthcare providers for more than 60 million rural Americans, accounting for approximately 18% of the total U.S. population [1]. They deliver a broad range of services, including emergency and inpatient care, laboratory and radiology services, primary and long-term care, maternity care, rehabilitation, health education, and chronic disease management [2]. Despite often serving populations with greater healthcare needs than their urban counterparts [3], rural hospitals face persistent challenges, including workforce shortages, lower patient volumes due to low population density, and difficulty covering fixed operating costs [3, 4]. Together, these structural pressures frequently lead to financial distress, increasing the risk of hospital closure [3]. Since 2005, 195 rural hospitals have closed or converted services (Supplementary Material 1 (SM1), Figure S1) [5]. Among these, 110 hospitals closed entirely, while 85 discontinued inpatient services but continued to offer limited outpatient or primary care [5]. Many additional rural hospitals remain at risk of closure [6].

Hospital closures have been linked to delayed emergency care, increased maternal complications, and reduced access to both inpatient and outpatient services [7]. For example, the average travel time to emergency services increases by 11–15.7 minutes following closures [8, 9], while the median distance to general inpatient care increased from 3.4 miles in 2012 to 23.9 miles in 2018 [10, 11]. In addition, rural hospital closures have significant economic impacts [12], including decreased income and economic growth [13–21]. Earlier studies conducted in 2006, 2017, and 2020 reported that unemployment rates increased by approximately 1.6%–3.1% following rural hospital closures [14, 19, 20]. These effects extend beyond healthcare employment to other sectors such as education, construction, real estate, information technology, research, and local services [19, 20]. Recent evidence published in 2022 also suggests that the economic effects of closures are more complex, with more consistent declines in labor force size and population, rather than uniform increases in unemployment rates [22].

Given these widespread impacts, preventing rural hospital closures is essential. Previous studies have identified multiple categories of factors associated with rural hospital closures, including financial, organizational, policy, and community-level characteristics [3, 23–30]. While prior studies have provided important insights into closure risk, most have relied on traditional statistical and causal inference approaches, which may be limited in capturing nonlinear relationships [23]. In this study, we build on prior work by applying an explainable machine learning (XML) approach to national longitudinal data to identify nonlinear relationships and provide an additional data-driven perspective on rural hospital closure risk. Specifically, we examine the following research question: Why are rural hospitals closing in the U.S., and which hospital operational, financial, policy, population, and community-level factors are linked with closure risk?. By integrating XML with national hospital and community data, this study aims to identify interpretable patterns and potential early warning indicators that may help inform future prevention efforts.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Section “Literature review” presents the literature review, Section “Materials and methods” describes the materials and methods, Section “Results” presents the results, Section “Discussion” discusses the findings, and Section “Conclusion” presents the conclusions and future directions.

Literature review

Previous works have extensively examined the effects of rural hospital closures [31] and factors contributing to closure risk [25]. Early predictive efforts, such as the Financial Distress Index (FDI), were developed using hospital and community characteristics to identify hospitals at risk of financial distress and closure [24]. Key predictors included lower profitability, limited reinvestment capacity, smaller hospital size, higher poverty levels, lower Medicaid reimbursement, reduced market share, and increased competitive pressure [24].

Beyond these early models, subsequent studies have consistently identified organizational, financial, and market-related factors as central drivers of rural hospital closures. Earlier research from the 1980s and 1990s found that hospitals with lower occupancy rates, fewer beds, limited service offerings, weaker organizational structures, and for-profit ownership were more likely to close [25]. Later studies expanded on these findings by highlighting the role of market competition and healthcare system changes, showing that hospitals located closer to competitors or with lower market share were at greater risk, while geographically isolated hospitals or those offering specialized services were more likely to remain open [25]. These findings are consistent with more recent analyses demonstrating that closures are strongly associated with poor financial performance, including lower profitability, weaker liquidity, higher debt burden, and reduced service utilization [32].

In addition to hospital-level characteristics, community, policy, and sociodemographic factors also play an important role in closure risk. Studies have shown that closures are more likely in areas with higher unemployment, greater population density, and higher proportions of underserved populations [30]. Similarly, studies using hospital-year panel data have identified higher unemployment rates and a greater proportion of uninsured populations as significant predictors of closure risk [27]. Policy and organizational factors further influence these outcomes. For example, hospitals in Medicaid expansion states are less likely to close due to reductions in uncompensated care and improved financial stability, while hospital system affiliation has been shown to have varying effects depending on financial condition [3, 26]. In addition, payer mix, including higher Medicare Advantage penetration, has been associated with improved financial stability and reduced closure risk [28]. More recent work has focused on developing predictive models using longitudinal hospital data. For example [22], used hospital-year observations to predict financial distress and closure risk, reinforcing the importance of financial, structural, and market-related factors [29].

Prior studies have commonly used established statistical and causal inference approaches, such as regression, survival models, and difference-in-differences designs [23]. While these approaches have played a central role in identifying key drivers of rural hospital closures, they typically rely on predefined functional forms and prespecified hypotheses, which may limit their ability to flexibly explore complex, nonlinear relationships and interactions among predictors [33, 34].

Machine learning (ML) offers a complementary analytic approach for addressing these limitations [35]. ML methods can analyze high-dimensional data, detect nonlinear relationships, and model complex interactions without requiring all relationships to be specified a priori. In addition, explainability techniques such as SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) enable the interpretation of model outputs and identification of influential predictors, supporting interpretable and practical insights.

However, to the best of our knowledge, no work has applied XML to national rural hospital data to systematically examine and interpret predictors of rural hospital closure [23]. This study, thus, aims to extend the existing literature by providing a data-driven perspective on risk factors of rural hospital closure using XML. Guided by evidence from a recent systematic review [23], which identified key predictor domains including financial distress, workforce shortages, policy influences, economic and demographic conditions, and market competition, we integrate multiple predictors derived from longitudinal data to support the identification of potential early warning signals for rural hospital closures. The final predictor set was further refined using data-driven screening. Specifically, multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF) to remove redundant variables [36], and SHAP [37] was used for feature importance analysis to identify and interpret the most influential predictors. This approach supports a comprehensive and interpretable assessment of factors linked with rural hospital closure risk.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study employed a retrospective observational design using longitudinal hospital data summarized into features to examine and extract patterns associated with rural hospital closure in the U.S. using XML. Annual hospital-level operational, financial, population, and community data from 2011 to 2022 were integrated from multiple national sources and linked to a binary closure outcome derived from publicly available rural hospital closure records. In addition, a policy-level variable capturing Medicaid expansion status was incorporated to account for the impact of state-level healthcare policy on hospital closure risk. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the analytic workflow progressed from data preprocessing and longitudinal feature engineering to exploratory analysis and multicollinearity assessment, followed by model development and post-hoc explanation using SHAP to characterize risk factors of rural hospital closure. Data sources

Hospital operational and financial data were obtained from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) Hospital Cost Report Information System (HCRIS) [38]. The analytic sample was restricted to rural hospitals, including Critical Access Hospitals (CAHs). Hospitals were classified as rural if they met any of the following criteria: (1) located in a non-metropolitan county per Office of Management and Budget (OMB) definitions [39]; (2) located in a ZIP code with Rural-Urban Commuting Area (RUCA) code ≥ 4 [40]; or (3) designated as a CAH. Hospital closures, defined as the discontinuation of inpatient services, were identified using the Cecil G. Sheps Center for Health Services Research rural hospital closures database [5], which provides the year and type of closure (full closure or conversion). These data were used to construct a binary outcome variable indicating hospital closure status (1 = open; 0 = closed). Population and community-level characteristics, including age distribution, racial and ethnic composition, median household income, insurance coverage, and employment status, were obtained from the U.S. Census Bureau American Community Survey (ACS) 5-yeasr estimate [41] at the ZIP Code Tabulation Area level. Because the ACS does not provide data for USPS ZIP codes, hospital ZIP codes were mapped to corresponding ZCTAs using a ZIP–ZCTA crosswalk [42] before linkage with ACS covariates. State-level Medicaid expansion data were obtained from the Kaiser Family Foundation (KFF), which provides state-and year-specific information on Medicaid expansion decisions across U.S. states beginning in 2014 [43]. All datasets were integrated to create a longitudinal dataset spanning 2011–2022, which was subsequently used for feature engineering and model development.

Data preprocessing

Annual CMS cost report data (2011–2022) were cleaned and combined into a hospital-year longitudinal dataset. For hospitals with multiple submissions within a fiscal year, records were aggregated to a single hospital-year observation using variable-specific aggregation rules. Missing numeric values were imputed using hospital-level (then overall) medians, and categorical variables were imputed using within-hospital (then overall) modes. Extreme outliers were capped using an interquartile range (IQR) rule. To preserve a balanced longitudinal structure, missing hospital-year observations were identified; only 22 of 2,683 hospitals (0.8%) had at least one missing year within their longitudinal records, and these missing years were imputed within each hospital’s expected observation window (2011 to the closure year or 2022) using the same imputation strategy (median imputation for numeric variables and mode imputation for categorical variables). ZCTA-level ACS covariates were linked to hospitals using the ZIP–ZCTA crosswalk and handled using the same imputation strategy. Detailed preprocessing procedures are provided in Section 1 of SM1.

Feature engineering was performed to construct predictors by deriving standard financial ratios, utilization measures, and payer-mix indicators from CMS data using published formulas [29, 44], and by incorporating community-level characteristics from the ACS. In addition, state-level Medicaid expansion information was merged with the hospital-level dataset using year-specific expansion data. Medicaid expansion status was coded as a binary policy variable (1 = adopted; 0 = not adopted). The expansion implementation date was used to derive the expansion year, and a “Years since Medicaid expansion” variable was calculated based on each hospital’s observation end year, defined as the closure year for closed hospitals and the final year (2022) of available data for open hospitals. For hospitals that closed before their state’s Medicaid expansion implementation year, negative values were assigned to indicate the number of years between closure and expansion. Hospitals in non-expansion states were assigned a value of 0 for years since Medicaid expansion.

Longitudinal feature summarization was then applied to generate hospital-level summary features from multi-year data, capturing both central tendency and variability. For continuous variables observed across multiple years, summary statistics including the mean, median, standard deviation (SD), and interquartile range (IQR) were calculated. For categorical variables, the most frequently observed category (mode) across years was used. The definitions of all the included variables and the construction of descriptive summary features are provided in Table 1. Detailed feature engineering and summarization procedures are provided in Section 2 of SM1, and the complete feature dictionary is provided in SM2. Exploratory analyses were performed to summarize trends by outcome group using descriptive statistics, and group differences were assessed using Mann–Whitney U and chi-square tests [45, 46]. Multicollinearity was assessed using VIF, and continuous predictors with VIF > 2 were excluded [36]. Model development and evaluation

The final analytical dataset consisted of hospital-level observations, where each row represents a hospital and columns correspond to aggregated predictor features derived from longitudinal data. All predictor variables were constructed using information available up to each hospital’s final observation year (closure year for closed hospitals and 2022 for open hospitals). Only predictors that satisfied the multicollinearity threshold (VIF ≤ 2) were included in the final model.

The final dataset was split into 80% training and 20% testing subsets using stratified sampling, ensuring balanced representation of open and closed hospitals during model development and evaluation. The training set was used exclusively for hyperparameter tuning and model training, while the testing set was reserved for independent assessment of model performance and explainability. The training dataset was highly imbalanced, with substantially fewer closed hospitals than open hospitals. To address this, random under-sampling [47] and over-sampling using SMOTE (Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique) [48] were applied to the training dataset. The resulting balanced dataset was then used for hyperparameter tuning and model development. In this study, we employed the XGBoost algorithm for binary classification to identify key predictors of rural hospital closures. XGBoost [49] was selected due to its strong predictive performance, ability to model nonlinear relationships, and robustness to heterogeneous feature distributions commonly observed in health services data. The model was trained using the training dataset and subsequently evaluated on the test dataset. The XGBoost model hyperparameters were optimized using the Optuna hyperparameter optimization framework [50, 51]. During hyperparameter tuning, each configuration was evaluated using stratified 3-fold cross-validation, with the mean ROC-AUC across folds used as the performance metric. The complete list of tuned parameters and their search ranges is provided in SM1 Table S2. A total of 100 Optuna trials were conducted, and the best-performing hyperparameter configuration was selected to train the final XGBoost model. The performance of the XGBoost model on the training and testing datasets was evaluated using standard classification metrics, including accuracy, precision, recall, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$F_{1}$$\end{document}-score, and ROC-AUC [52].

To interpret the XGBoost model’s predictions and identify the most influential predictors of rural hospital closure, we applied SHAP, a post-hoc interpretability framework that quantifies each feature’s contribution to model predictions [37]. Because XGBoost is a tree-based model, TreeSHAP was used for all analyses to ensure efficient and consistent estimation of feature attributions. SHAP summary, dependence, and waterfall plots were used to examine how key financial, operational, and community-level features contributed to closure risk prediction [53]. These analyses enabled a transparent explanation of the model’s predictions and provided insights into why a given hospital was predicted to have higher or lower closure risk and how each factor contributed to the prediction. This modeling approach is intended to complement prior statistical and causal inference methods by focusing on prediction, nonlinear pattern detection, feature interaction analysis, and interpretable risk-factor identification rather than causal effect estimation.

Results

Exploratory data analysis results

The CMS cost report dataset included 6,918 hospital-year observations from 2011 to 2022, representing 2,683 rural hospitals, across all 50 U.S. states, identified using RUCA codes, OMB rural classifications, and CAH status. Of these hospitals, 2,544 (94.8%) remained open during the study period, while 139 (5.2%) closed according to Sheps Center records. The mean fiscal year length differed significantly between groups: open hospitals reported longer fiscal years (mean = 360.67 days, SD = 24.97) than closed hospitals (mean = 342.63 days, SD = 39.86; p < 0.001), likely reflecting partial-year reporting during pre-closure transitions.

Figure 2 shows annual distributions of financial, operational, and community racial characteristics (White and Black or African American) among open and closed rural hospitals from 2011 to 2022. Closed hospitals exhibited shifts toward lower values, with more pronounced negative tails across several financial and operational indicators, suggesting greater variability and poorer performance compared to open hospitals. Yearly medians indicate consistently lower performance among closed hospitals across key indicators, including current ratio, equity financing, occupancy rate, operating margin, total margin, and return on equity. In contrast, closed hospitals were associated with higher Medicare discharge share, higher levels of uncompensated care, and greater variability in Medicaid payer mix. In terms of community characteristics, closed hospitals were more likely to serve populations with lower proportions of White residents and higher proportions of Black or African American residents, with greater variability observed across years. These differences are evident several years prior to closure, suggesting persistent structural and financial disparities and highlighting the value of longitudinal information for understanding rural hospital closure risk. In total, 148 longitudinally derived features were extracted from hospital-level financial, operational, population, and community characteristics. Differences between open and closed hospitals were assessed using the Mann–Whitney U test [45] for continuous variables and Pearson’s chi-square test [46] for categorical variables; descriptive statistics and p-values are reported in SM1 Table S3, given the large number of predictors. Among categorical variables, CAH status, ownership type, and negative equity category differed significantly between groups (all p < 0.001).

Significant differences were also observed across multiple continuous measures. Closed hospitals had fewer total hospital days and beds, lower occupancy rates, higher uncompensated care burdens, more negative operating and total margins, lower current ratios, lower return on equity, and reduced equity financing compared with open hospitals (all p < 0.001). Payer-mix characteristics also differed, with significant differences observed in Medicare and Medicaid discharge shares (p < 0.05). Policy-related variables showed strong differences between groups. A significantly lower proportion of closed hospitals were located in Medicaid expansion states, and closed hospitals had fewer years since Medicaid expansion compared with open hospitals (both p < 0.001).

Community characteristics further distinguished the two groups. Closed hospitals were located in areas with higher unemployment and lower median household income (all p < 0.001), as well as higher proportions of adults aged 25–64 and ≥ 65 years. Racial composition differed significantly, with closed hospitals serving communities with higher proportions of Black or African American residents and lower proportions of White residents (all p < 0.001, see Fig. 2). Closed hospitals were also associated with higher uninsured rates across multiple demographic subgroups (p < 0.001). RUCA classification did not differ significantly between groups (p = 0.376). Figure 3b shows that closures are geographically widespread but appear more frequently in the South and Midwest, while hospitals in the Northeast and West are more likely to remain open, highlighting regional variation in rural hospital closure risk. Overall, a substantial proportion of longitudinally derived features differed significantly between open and closed rural hospitals, underscoring the combined importance of financial, operational, population, community-level, and policy-related factors in understanding rural hospital closure risk.

Substantial multicollinearity was observed among continuous predictors. VIF analysis was conducted on 142 features, excluding six categorical variables, and predictors with VIF > 2.0 were iteratively removed (excluded variables and VIFs in SM1 Table S4). This yielded 34 predictors with acceptable multicollinearity (VIF ≤ 2.0). Spearman correlations among retained features (SM1 Figure S2) confirmed that no variable pairs exceeded acceptable thresholds, indicating the feature set was suitable for ML model development.

Model performance evaluation results

As shown in Table 2, the dataset was highly imbalanced, with substantially fewer closed than open hospitals; this was addressed using random undersampling applied to the training data only. XGBoost hyperparameters were optimized using Optuna with stratified 3-fold cross-validation, and the optimal hyperparameters are reported in SM1 Table S2. The final model was trained using these parameters and evaluated on an independent test set. For comparison, additional models were trained using the original (no sampling) and SMOTE-balanced training datasets, with hyperparameter tuning performed separately but using the same optimization procedure. The confusion matrices for all three models are shown in Table 3. As shown in Table 4, the no-sampling model achieved high test accuracy (95%) and AUC (92%) but demonstrated very low recall for the closed class (recall = 18%), correctly identifying only 5 of 28 closed hospitals (5 true positives and 23 false negatives). The SMOTE-balanced model achieved a test AUC of (90%) and identified 8 of 28 closed hospitals (recall = 29%), with 20 false negatives and 15 false positives. The undersampling-trained model achieved the highest recall for the closure class (86%), correctly identifying 24 of 28 closed hospitals, with 4 false negatives and 128 false positives, but at the cost of reduced overall accuracy (75%) and AUC (89%) (Fig. 3a). Why are rural hospitals closing in the US?

Figure 4 shows that rural hospital closure risk is primarily driven by hospital-level financial performance, operational capacity, policy-related factors, and community context. The most influential predictors include total margin (Median), years since Medicaid expansion, return on equity (Standard deviation), current ratio (Median), DSH (Interquartile range), Black or African American population percentage (Median), total hospital days (Median), and total hospital bed-days available (Standard deviation). Together, these factors highlight the critical role of sustained financial distress, operational capacity constraints, policy issues, and community characteristics in shaping rural hospital closure risk. Figures 5 and 6 show dependence plots for the top six predictors and their interactions. Lower median total margin is associated with higher predicted probability of closure, indicating that sustained low profitability shifts the model output toward the closure class. Hospitals with median total margins below approximately -2 exhibit the highest predicted risk, those between -2 and 0 show intermediate risk, and hospitals with positive margins are more likely to be classified as remaining open (Fig. 5A). Median total margin showed a largely monotonic relationship with closure risk and interacted with variability in return on equity. Figure 5B shows that, at comparable margin levels, hospitals with higher variability in return on equity tended to exhibit higher predicted closure risk, particularly when median total margin was low or negative. This suggests that financial instability amplified the adverse effect of poor profitability, while stronger profitability appeared to reduce closure risk even when return on equity varied. Liquidity further differentiated hospitals at risk of closure. Hospitals with very low median current ratios showed higher predicted closure risk, whereas hospitals with current ratios near or above 1.0 were more likely to be classified as remain open (Fig. 6A). This relationship was nonlinear, with the largest change in SHAP values occurring around current ratio values of approximately 0.8–1.2. The interaction with the median percentage of Black or African American residents was limited, as the overall liquidity-risk pattern remained similar across community compositions (Fig. 6B). However, among hospitals with low liquidity, higher community Black or African American population percentages appeared in some higher-risk regions, suggesting that community context may further contribute to closure vulnerability when financial liquidity is weak.

Financial stability also played a critical role, as the standard deviation of return on equity showed a nonlinear relationship with closure risk (Fig. 5E). Hospitals with very low variability exhibited the highest closure risk, whereas moderate variability (approximately 1–3) was associated with a greater likelihood of remaining open. At higher levels of variability, the protective effect diminished, and closure risk increased slightly, although not to the extent observed at very low variability. This pattern suggests that both persistently poor performance and excessive financial instability are associated with increased predicted closure risk. This relationship showed limited interaction with variability in DSH adjustment (Fig. 5F), as the effect of return on equity variability on closure risk appeared relatively consistent across different levels of safety-net reimbursement variability.

Similarly, variability in safety-net-related reimbursement contributed to closure risk. Greater variability in DSH adjustment was associated with higher predicted closure risk (Fig. 6C). Hospitals with low variability had values near zero or slightly positive, indicating a lower likelihood of closure, whereas increasing variability was associated with progressively more negative values, reflecting higher closure risk. This pattern suggests that instability in safety-net-related reimbursement may contribute to hospital vulnerability. This relationship showed some interaction with median total margin (Fig. 6D). At higher levels of DSH adjustment variability, hospitals generally exhibited increased closure risk across all margin levels. However, hospitals with lower median total margins tended to cluster in the most negative range, indicating that poor financial performance may intensify the adverse effects of unstable reimbursement. Taken together, while variability in DSH adjustment was an important predictor, its effect appeared stronger among hospitals already experiencing financial distress.

Policy context also influenced closure risk prediction. Years since Medicaid expansion showed a clear association with predicted closure risk. Hospitals in states with fewer years since Medicaid expansion (approximately 0–4 years) exhibited higher closure risk, whereas those with longer exposure (approximately ≥ 6 years) were more likely to be predicted as remain open (Fig. 5C). This pattern suggests that the protective effect of Medicaid expansion strengthens over time. This relationship interacted with CAH status (Fig. 5D). At comparable Medicaid expansion durations, CAHs and non-CAHs showed similar directional trends, although differences in magnitude indicate that hospital designation may modify the extent of the policy’s protective effect.

Finally, community context was also associated with closure risk. Community racial composition was associated with closure risk (Fig. 6E). Hospitals serving areas with lower Black or African American population percentages showed more positive values, indicating a lower likelihood of closure. As the proportion increased, values decreased sharply, becoming negative, indicating higher closure risk. At moderate to high population percentages, this relationship stabilized, with consistently elevated closure risk. This pattern suggests a nonlinear association, where increases in community Black or African American population percentage are associated with higher closure vulnerability, particularly at lower-to-moderate ranges. This relationship showed limited interaction with years since Medicaid expansion (Fig. 6F). Although hospitals serving communities with higher Black or African American population percentages showed higher closure risk, this pattern appeared relatively consistent across different Medicaid expansion durations. Therefore, the figure does not suggest that Medicaid expansion was limited in these communities, but rather that Medicaid expansion duration did not substantially change this association.

Additionally, local explainability (Fig. 7) further illustrates how these global patterns manifest at the individual hospital level prediction. Hospitals that remained open (Fig. 7a) were characterized by favorable contributions from sustained profitability, stronger liquidity, and longer exposure to Medicaid expansion, along with moderate financial variability and lower uncompensated care burden. In contrast, hospitals that closed (Fig. 7b) exhibited large negative contributions from poor financial performance (e.g., strongly negative total margin), limited Medicaid expansion exposure, low or unstable financial returns, and weaker liquidity, along with additional contributions from operational and ownership-related factors. Overall, these findings suggest that rural hospital closures in the U.S. are driven by the combined effects of sustained financial weakness, financial instability, policy environment, and operational factors, rather than isolated short-term distress. This underscores the importance of early identification of sustained risk signals to support timely intervention and policy response. Discussion

This study used national longitudinal data from 2011 to 2022 and XML to identify and explain interpretable factors and patterns associated with rural hospital closures in the U.S. By integrating hospital-level financial and operational indicators with population, community, and policy-related characteristics, and by leveraging longitudinally derived features, this work advances prior literature [23] that has primarily relied on traditional statistical and causal inference approaches by providing a complementary data-driven approach to identify nonlinear relationships and interpretable risk patterns, including interaction patterns observed through SHAP dependence analysis, associated with closure risk. Specifically, this approach captures long-term patterns through multi-year medians and variability measures and applies XML to examine how key factors are associated with closure risk through interpretable and nonlinear risk patterns. Given the observational design and XML approach, the findings should be interpreted as predictive associations and interpretable risk patterns rather than causal effects. The central finding is that closure risk reflects accumulated and sustained financial and operational weakness, as well as policy and community context, rather than poor performance in a single year. Notably, these signals were observable several years before closure, highlighting opportunities for proactive monitoring and targeted intervention before hospitals enter irreversible pre-closure pathways.

Persistently low profitability (median total margin) and limited liquidity (median current ratio) emerged as important factors associated with closure risk. SHAP dependence patterns indicate that hospitals with very low or negative profitability were associated with substantially higher closure risk, whereas hospitals with sustained positive margins exhibited consistently lower estimated closure risk. Importantly, the model reveals threshold behavior, with risk highest at strongly negative margins and declining as margins approach and exceed zero. Similarly, very low liquidity was associated with higher closure risk, whereas hospitals with current ratios near or above 1.0 were more likely to remain open, with the largest transition in risk occurring around 0.8–1.2. These findings are consistent with prior studies identifying profitability and liquidity as key determinants of rural hospital viability [24, 30, 32]. However, this study extends the existing literature by demonstrating how longitudinally derived features and XML uncover nonlinear risk patterns and complex relationships between factors that vary across financial and contextual conditions that are not captured by traditional approaches.

The relationship between profitability and closure risk varied with financial stability. At comparable levels of median total margin, hospitals with higher variability in return on equity exhibited greater closure risk, particularly when margins were low or negative. This finding suggests that financial instability may amplify the adverse effects of poor profitability, consistent with the SHAP dependence analysis. Similarly, liquidity effects showed modest interaction with community characteristics. While low current ratios were consistently associated with higher closure risk, hospitals with both low liquidity and higher proportions of Black or African American residents appeared more frequently in higher-risk regions. This suggests that community context may contribute to closure vulnerability under financial stress, although the overall liquidity–risk relationship remained consistent across populations, indicating that this interaction effect is present but relatively limited in magnitude. These patterns highlight that closure risk is not determined by individual factors in isolation but by the combined effects of financial performance, stability, and contextual factors.

Beyond profitability and liquidity, financial stability also emerged as an important factor associated with closure risk. Variability in return on equity (standard deviation) showed a nonlinear relationship with closure risk, where both very low variability (reflecting persistently poor performance) and higher variability (reflecting instability) were associated with increased closure risk, while moderate variability was associated with a lower likelihood of closure. This finding highlights that not only the level of financial performance but also its stability over time is critical for hospital survival. Similarly, variability in DSH adjustment was associated with higher closure risk, particularly among hospitals already experiencing financial distress, suggesting that instability in safety-net-related reimbursement may exacerbate vulnerability, with stronger effects observed among hospitals with lower median total margins.

Policy factors also emerged as important factor associated with closure risk. Years since Medicaid expansion showed a clear relationship with closure risk, where hospitals in states with fewer years since expansion exhibited higher risk, while those with longer exposure to Medicaid expansion were more likely to remain open. This pattern suggests that longer exposure to Medicaid expansion was associated with lower closure risk, potentially through reductions in uncompensated care and improvements in financial stability. These findings are consistent with prior literature indicating that Medicaid expansion is associated with improved hospital financial performance and reduced closure risk [26]. SHAP dependence analysis further suggests that the association between Medicaid expansion and closure risk may vary across hospital characteristics. In particular, the effect of years since Medicaid expansion showed modest interaction with hospital type (e.g., CAH status), indicating that while the overall trend was consistent, the magnitude of this association differed slightly across hospital groups.

In addition to hospital-level financial and policy factors, community characteristics associated with hospital closure risk include the proportion of Black or African American residents in the service area. Hospitals serving communities with higher proportions of Black or African American residents showed elevated model-estimated closure risk. This finding is consistent with existing literature, which has shown that hospitals serving communities with higher proportions of Black residents and other underserved populations face elevated risks of closure. This study extends prior evidence by using XML to identify a nonlinear relationship between racial composition and closure risk. Specifically, SHAP dependence patterns indicate that closure risk increases sharply as the proportion of Black or African American residents rises at lower-to-moderate levels and then stabilizes at higher levels, suggesting a threshold-like effect. The interaction with policy context (years since Medicaid expansion) appears limited, as the overall relationship between community racial composition and closure risk remains consistent across different expansion durations. This suggests that the observed association reflects broader structural and socioeconomic factors rather than strong modifying effects of policy exposure.

Prior research suggests that these communities may include rural areas located closer to metropolitan markets, where rural hospitals face competition from better-resourced urban hospitals and health systems [30]. More proximate factors affecting hospital financial viability include unemployment, uninsured rates, payer mix, Medicaid reliance, uncompensated care burden, and broader socioeconomic disadvantage [25, 30, 54]. Thus, area-level racial composition should be interpreted as a contextual marker of broader structural and market conditions rather than an independent causal or predictive factor. From an equity perspective, closure-prevention strategies should account for both hospital financial conditions and structural constraints, including payer mix, uncompensated care burden, market competition, and socioeconomic disadvantage, particularly given that hospitals serving more vulnerable communities in this study exhibited higher closure risk [54].

In general, this study extends previous research in three key contributions. First, it demonstrates the importance of longitudinal monitoring: hospitals that eventually closed exhibited persistently poor long-term financial and operational instability, as well as policy and community context, rather than short-term distress, indicating that year-to-year trends contain meaningful early warning signals. Second, the use of XML enabled the identification of practical risk thresholds and nonlinear patterns. For example, closure risk was higher among hospitals with very low liquidity, whereas hospitals with current ratios near or above 1.0 and consistently positive margins were more likely to remain open. Third, we identified interpretable interaction patterns that help explain how closure risk manifests in real-world settings. For instance, the relationship between profitability and closure risk varied with financial stability, particularly in the variability of return on equity, while the effect of liquidity showed a modest interaction with community context. Together, these findings clarify not only which factors matter but also when they become risky and under what conditions, supporting the development of explainable early warning tools that policymakers and hospital leaders can understand and trust.

Based on the longitudinally derived features analysis and XML results, prevention strategies for rural hospital closures should prioritize early identification and stabilization of sustained financial and operational risk rather than reacting to short-term declines. Policy-related findings suggest that Medicaid expansion duration may be an important contextual factor in closure risk monitoring, particularly because years since Medicaid expansion was among the most influential predictors and closed hospitals had fewer years since expansion compared with open hospitals. Policies that improve the stability of Medicaid, Medicare, and safety-net-related reimbursement may help reduce liquidity constraints, particularly among hospitals with persistently weak financial performance, unstable DSH adjustment patterns, and high uncompensated-care burdens. Because hospitals serving more vulnerable communities, including those with higher proportions of Black or African American residents and higher socioeconomic disadvantage, faced elevated closure risk, closure-prevention efforts should incorporate equity-focused, context-sensitive resource allocation. Finally, interventions should address operational instability by monitoring long-term patient volume and variability in bed availability, as low patient volume and bed-day variability emerged as important indicators of closure risk.

Conclusion

This study used longitudinal national data (2011–2022) and XML to investigate and understand why rural hospitals are closing in the U.S. By integrating hospital-level financial and operational measures with policy, population, and community characteristics, the findings suggest that closure risk reflects accumulated patterns of sustained financial weakness, volatility, operational instability, policy context, and community-level conditions rather than short-term fluctuations. Persistently low profitability and liquidity, as reflected in lower median total margins and current ratios, were identified as important risk factors. Financial volatility, particularly variability in return on equity, emerged as an early risk signal, while operational instability, including low long-term patient volume and fluctuating bed availability, was also associated with higher closure risk. Policy context, particularly years since Medicaid expansion, was also associated with closure risk, suggesting that policy exposure may be an important contextual factor in monitoring rural hospital vulnerability. Community context was also associated with closure risk: hospitals serving communities with higher proportions of Black or African American residents showed elevated model-estimated risk, which should be interpreted as reflecting broader structural, socioeconomic, and market-related conditions rather than race itself as a direct predictor or causal factor. Methodologically, this study highlights the potential value of combining longitudinally derived features with XML to identify nonlinear relationships, thresholds, and interactions while maintaining explainability. As an alternative and complementary approach to traditional methods, these findings should be interpreted as risk signals rather than causal effects and may support the development of explainable early warning systems to enable proactive monitoring and targeted interventions, thereby preserving access to care in vulnerable rural areas.

In the future, we plan to include more detailed analyses of workforce, service, ownership, and policy factors, including service line dynamics (e.g., openings and closures of key services such as surgery, cardiology, and obstetrics), spatial relationships between hospitals, market competition, mergers and acquisitions, and distinguish between full hospital closures and conversions to alternative care models to better understand different closure pathways. In addition, applying these longitudinally derived and explainable risk patterns within a periodically updated AI-based early warning system may help policymakers and hospital leaders identify at-risk hospitals earlier and support timely interventions to improve rural hospital sustainability.
