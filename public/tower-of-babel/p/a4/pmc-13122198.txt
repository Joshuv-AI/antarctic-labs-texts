Solitary renal metastasis from salivary epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma mimicking renal cell carcinoma: A case report of robot-assisted partial nephrectomy
Authors: Sayo Daidoji, Eri Fukagawa, Shinji Ito, Yu Yamaguchi, Kensuke Suzuki, Shu Nakasuka, Michinori Shimizu, Makoto Tanaka, Kazuki Honda, Suguru Oka, Kazushige Sakaguchi, Shinji Urakami
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRenal metastasis from salivary epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma (EMC) is exceptionally uncommon. A 74-year-old woman with parotid EMC had previously undergone two pulmonary metastasectomies. A 27-mm mass was detected in the upper pole of the left kidney 14 years after initial parotidectomy. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography demonstrated mild enhancement without washout, indicating non–clear cell renal cell carcinoma or metastatic disease. Robot-assisted partial nephrectomy was performed, and histopathology confirmed metastatic EMC with biphasic atypical tubules composed of CK7-positive luminal epithelial components and p63-positive myoepithelial components. This case highlights diagnostic challenges and the need to consider late metastasis after prolonged disease-free intervals.Keywords: Epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma, Nephrectomy, Parotid neoplasms, Secondary kidney neoplasms

Introduction

Epithelial–myoepithelial carcinoma (EMC) is a rare salivary gland malignancy that accounts for less than 1% of all salivary gland epithelial neoplasms.1 It arises predominantly in the parotid gland and is generally regarded as a low-grade carcinoma with indolent behavior, reflecting its relatively low rates of lymph node involvement and distant metastasis.2,3 Although its metastatic potential is limited, distant metastasis has been reported in approximately 5% of cases.2 Reported metastatic sites include the lungs and bones.2,4,5 Delayed recurrence and late metastasis have also been documented in the literature, underscoring the unpredictable clinical course of this tumor.4,6,7

Renal metastasis from salivary EMC is exceedingly uncommon, with only isolated cases described in the literature.7 To our knowledge, no such case has been reported in Japan. Here, we describe a case of solitary renal metastasis identified 14 years after initial parotidectomy for EMC, highlighting the diagnostic and therapeutic considerations associated with this rare presentation.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old woman with a history of hypertension was independently ambulatory (performance status 1). Her medical history included uterine fibroids and breast cancer treated 7 years earlier (estrogen receptor-positive, human epidermal growth factor receptor 2-positive, cT1N0M0), which was treated with multimodal therapy and showed a pathological complete response. Regular medications included amlodipine, anastrozole, and atorvastatin. Preoperative renal function was normal (serum creatinine 0.59 mg/dL), and all serum tumor markers were within reference ranges.

The patient previously underwent resection of a left parotid EMC at another hospital. According to available records, the tumor was staged as cT1 with involvement of one of the four regional lymph nodes (pN1), absence of lymphovascular invasion (LV0), and no distant metastasis (cM0). Eight years following initial parotidectomy, a solitary pulmonary metastasis was resected thoracoscopically and histologically confirmed as a metastatic EMC. Twelve years after the initial surgery, bilateral pulmonary metastases were managed with thoracoscopic metastasectomies.

Surveillance computed tomography (CT) performed 14 years after the initial parotidectomy revealed a 27-mm mass in the upper pole of the left kidney [Fig. 1]. Contrast-enhanced CT demonstrated mild heterogeneous enhancement in the corticomedullary phase and persistent mild enhancement in the nephrographic phase without obvious washout [Fig. 2]. The renal pelvis and ureter were unremarkable. Moreover, no involvement of the renal sinuses or veins was observed. Concurrent chest and abdominal CT revealed no evidence of additional metastatic lesions, confirming a solitary renal mass. The R.E.N.A.L. nephrometry score (Radius, Exophytic/endophytic, Nearness to the collecting system, Anterior/posterior, and Location relative to the polar lines) was 7a.

The tumor was located near the renal hilum, adjacent to the renal pelvis and vein. Given the proximity of the tumor to the renal pelvis, a preoperative ureteral stent was placed to facilitate intraoperative identification of the collecting system. Robot-assisted partial nephrectomy (RAPN) was performed using the transperitoneal approach. The operative time was 309 min with an estimated blood loss of 200 mL. The procedure was completed without complications, and the patient was discharged on postoperative day 6.

Gross examination revealed a well-circumscribed tan-white tumor with a pseudocapsule [Fig. 3]. Microscopically, the tumor exhibited characteristic biphasic architecture of EMC, with duct-forming atypical CK7-positive luminal epithelial components encircled by atypical p63-positive clear myoepithelial components [Fig. 4]. These histopathological features were morphologically concordant with those of previously resected parotid tumors, supporting the diagnosis of metastatic EMC. The tumor was completely excised with negative surgical margins.

Postoperative renal function remained stable, with serum creatinine levels ranging from 0.55 to 0.63 mg/dL during follow-up. Surveillance contrast-enhanced CT over 18 months demonstrated no evidence of local recurrence or new metastatic lesions. The patient continued to undergo routine follow-ups at our institution.

Discussion

Metastatic tumors to the kidney are occasionally encountered, most commonly originating from lung cancer, breast cancer, and gastrointestinal malignancies.8,9

In contrast, renal metastasis of salivary EMC is exceedingly rare, with only a limited number of cases described in the literature. In a clinicopathologic series of 16 cases, Corio et al. described one patient who developed renal metastasis after six local recurrences over a 28-year period; the patient subsequently died following nephrectomy.7

In the present case, a renal mass was identified 14 years after initial parotidectomy. Contrast-enhanced imaging demonstrated mild heterogeneous enhancement without obvious washout. Differentiating metastatic renal tumors from primary renal cell carcinoma (RCC) based solely on imaging remains challenging, particularly in atypical cases. Clear cell RCC typically demonstrates strong early enhancement with washout, whereas non–clear cell RCC tends to show more modest enhancement patterns.10 Metastatic renal tumors are generally hypovascular and often present as multiple lesions; however, solitary metastases have been reported and may mimic primary renal tumors.9 In this context, the imaging findings in this case were nonspecific and overlapped with those of both non–clear cell RCC and metastatic tumors. Therefore, the differential diagnoses included primary RCC, metastatic breast cancer, and metastatic EMC. When evaluating a solitary renal mass in patients with a history of salivary gland malignancy, metastatic disease should be considered even after a prolonged disease-free interval, as EMC has been reported to recur or metastasize even decades after initial treatment.4,7

From a therapeutic standpoint, RAPN was considered the most appropriate strategy, as it served both definitive diagnosis and local control regardless of the final pathology. For cT1 primary RCC, nephron-sparing surgery is the standard of care when negative margins are achievable.10 Clinically apparent solitary renal metastasis from breast cancer is uncommon; a systematic review identified only 15 cases of non-disseminated breast cancer presenting as an isolated renal lesion.11 Even if the lesion had represented solitary breast cancer metastasis, surgical resection would have allowed definitive histopathological diagnosis and local control. For EMC, disease-specific management strategies remain undefined because of its rarity; however, complete surgical excision with negative margins remains the principal treatment option in resectable disease.4,6 Therefore, surgical intervention was pursued to address all differential possibilities, prioritizing nephron-sparing and local disease control.

The clinical utility of renal mass biopsy (RMB) should be considered in the context of whether the result would alter management.10 In patients with a history of extrarenal malignancy, RMB may be particularly useful when metastatic disease is suspected in the setting of disseminated lesions or when systemic therapy would be prioritized over local treatment. However, RMB has limitations, including non-diagnostic results and sampling error, and may be less informative in tumors with potential histological heterogeneity. In the present case, surgical resection was expected to provide both definitive diagnosis and local disease control across all considered differential diagnoses. Therefore, RMB was unlikely to alter the overall management strategy and was not pursued.

Surgical resection enabled histopathological confirmation of metastatic EMC, characterized by a dual-cell population of duct-forming atypical CK7-positive luminal epithelial components and atypical p63-positive myoepithelial components, concordant with the patient's prior salivary EMC.

Although EMC is generally regarded as a low-grade malignancy, accumulating case reports indicate that EMC may recur or metastasize after long disease-free intervals.4,6,7 In the present case, renal metastasis was identified 14 years after the initial surgery. The American Society of Clinical Oncology recommends continued annual follow-up beyond 5 years for salivary gland malignancies.12 These observations collectively highlight the potential for late recurrence and support the need for long-term surveillance, even when the initial disease appears localized and adequately treated.

Conclusion

Renal metastasis from salivary EMC is rare and may occur after prolonged disease-free intervals. In patients with a history of EMC who present with a renal mass, metastatic disease should be considered even many years after initial treatment. Surgical resection can provide both definitive diagnosis and effective local control in resectable cases.
