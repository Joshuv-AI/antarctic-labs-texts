Urban–rural differences in sarcopenia, nutritional status, and health-related quality of life among older adults with dementia in Taiwan
Authors: Yu-Nu Wang, Wen-Chuin Hsu, Yu-Chen Ku, Sajad Yarahmadi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Objective: Compare sarcopenia, nutritional status, and health-related quality of life (HRQoL) between urban and rural community-dwelling older adults with dementia in Taiwan.Methods: Cross-sectional analysis of pooled data from two cohorts (N = 240; ≥ 65 years) from a neurology clinic in northern Taiwan (August 2022–September 2023) and a dementia care center in central Taiwan (September 2024–July 2025). Sarcopenia was defined using 2025 Asian Working Group criteria. Nutritional status and HRQoL were assessed. Logistic regression estimated associations with urban residence, adjusting for demographic and clinical covariates.Results: Urban participants had more prevalent sarcopenia (36.3% vs. 23.8%, p = 0.01) and low muscle mass (34.8% vs. 18.1%, p = 0.01) but less prevalent malnutrition or risk (2.2% vs. 30.5%, p < 0.01). After adjustment, urban residence was associated with higher odds of sarcopenia (OR 2.41, 95% CI 1.25, 4.62) and low muscle mass (OR 2.74, 95% CI 1.35, 5.57), and lower odds of malnutrition or risk (OR 0.33, 95% CI 0.18, 0.61). Associations with physical and mental HRQoL were not significant.Conclusions: Urban-rural differences in sarcopenia and nutritional status were observed; HRQoL was not independently associated with residence. Interpretation is limited by cross-sectional design, non-random sampling, and residual confounding.

Introduction

Dementia is a growing global public health concern, particularly in rapidly aging societies. The global dementia population exceeds 57 million, with nearly 10 million new cases each year, most occurring in Asia (World Health Organization, 2025). Taiwan is among the fastest-aging nations in the world. The proportion of older adults increased from 7% in 1993 to over 20% within three decades (National Development Council, 2024), reaching 20.06% of the population by the end of 2025 and marking Taiwan as a super-aged society (Department of Household Registration, Ministry of the Interior, 2026). According to national statistics, 12.3% of older adults in Taiwan are living with dementia, and this prevalence is expected to continue rising as population aging accelerates (Ministry of Health and Welfare, 2025). Dementia frequently coexists with other geriatric conditions that increase disability and care demands (Culberson et al., 2023; World Health Organization, 2025).

Among these comorbidities, sarcopenia—characterized by progressive loss of skeletal muscle mass, strength, and function—is common and debilitating (Chen et al., 2025). It increases the risks of falls, frailty, hospitalization, and mortality (Beaudart et al., 2017; Cruz-Jentoft and Sayer, 2019; Nakamura et al., 2021), and its prevalence may exceed 40% among older adults with dementia in Taiwan (Wang et al., 2025a). Sarcopenia often co-occurs with malnutrition, which further accelerates physical and cognitive decline and worsens health-related quality of life (HRQoL) (Norman et al., 2021; Perry et al., 2023). Although studies have examined associations between sarcopenia, nutritional indicators, and HRQoL in dementia populations (Wang et al., 2025a; Wang et al., 2025b), these relationships have rarely been examined as a multidimensional construct across geographic contexts.

HRQoL in dementia is influenced by physical limitations, emotional distress, and social isolation (Fábrega-Cuadros et al., 2021; Manrique-Espinoza et al., 2017) and may vary by geographic context. Geographic inequalities in dementia care are recognized globally (Giebel et al., 2025), and in Taiwan, disparities persist between urban and rural areas in healthcare access, rehabilitation services, and dementia-specific programs (Shih et al., 2023). Medical and rehabilitation resources are concentrated in urban centers, whereas rural residents often rely on primary care clinics or mobile services, which may delay access to specialized care (Shih et al., 2023). Rural areas may also face limitations in dietary diversity and service availability (Molani-Gol et al., 2025; Quandt et al., 2000), while urban environments may be associated with more sedentary lifestyles and psychosocial stressors (Goldberg et al., 2021; Park et al., 2020).

Despite increasing attention to dementia-related health disparities, evidence explicitly examining urban-rural differences in sarcopenia among older adults with dementia in Taiwan remains limited. Furthermore, the combined relationships between sarcopenia, nutritional status, and HRQoL have not been well characterized across geographic settings. It remains unclear how these interrelated factors vary by residential context and whether outcomes differ by classification.

Therefore, applying the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2025 criteria, the objectives of this study were to: (1) describe and compare sarcopenia status, nutritional status, and the physical and mental components of HRQoL between urban and rural community-dwelling older adults with dementia in Taiwan; and (2) examine the associations between residential setting and these outcomes after adjusting for relevant covariates. We expected that urban residents would have a higher prevalence of sarcopenia, rural residents would demonstrate poorer nutritional status, and HRQoL patterns would differ between settings.

Methods

Study design and population

This quantitative, non-experimental, cross-sectional study examined urban–rural differences in sarcopenia, nutritional status, and HRQoL among community-dwelling older adults with dementia in Taiwan. De-identified data were pooled from two cohorts: (1) an outpatient neurology clinic at a regional teaching hospital in northern Taiwan (August 2022–September 2023) and (2) a community dementia care center affiliated with a regional teaching hospital in central Taiwan (September 2024–July 2025). Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed.

A total of 240 participants (120 per cohort) were included in all analyses. Eligibility criteria were: age ≥ 65 years; neurologist-confirmed Alzheimer's disease diagnosis, vascular dementia, or mixed dementia based on standard clinical criteria; Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) 0.5–2; ability to ambulate; measurable handgrip strength; ability to communicate in Mandarin or Taiwanese; and written informed consent from the participant and a legally authorized representative. Exclusion criteria were residence in long-term care facilities, conditions that could compromise safe participation in physical assessments, and the presence of a pacemaker or metal implant; three individuals were excluded on this last basis. Participants assessed as having a high risk of falls or requiring continuous supervision for mobility were excluded to ensure safety during chair stand and handgrip strength testing.

Participants were identified by site personnel and research staff. In the outpatient clinic cohort, participants were selectively recruited based on clinic attendance and eligibility screening; in the dementia care center cohort, participants were recruited consecutively among eligible residents. The participation rate was high (> 95%) in the dementia care center cohort, whereas it was not available for the outpatient clinic cohort due to the nature of the recruitment process. Purposive sampling was used due to feasibility constraints.

Sample size was estimated using conventional medium effect sizes (w = 0.30; d = 0.50) (Cohen, 1988), requiring at least 143 participants for 80% power at α = 0.05. Reported sarcopenia prevalence in Asian older adults with dementia (32.7%–40.8%) supports the plausibility of detecting group differences of this magnitude (Wang et al., 2025a; Ye et al., 2025). For logistic regression, model adequacy was evaluated using the ≥10 events-per-variable rule (Peduzzi et al., 1996; Vittinghoff and McCulloch, 2007).

Residential status was determined from the address recorded on the consent form. Addresses were mapped to administrative districts and classified using official population and administrative data from Taiwan's Department of Household Registration, Ministry of the Interior (2026). Consistent with the Local Government Act (Ministry of the Interior, 2025), districts with populations >100,000 and developed infrastructure were classified as urban, and less densely populated areas as rural. Classification was performed by the primary investigator and independently verified. All participants were assigned exclusively to urban or rural categories (135 urban, 105 rural).

Measures

Data were collected face-to-face by trained personnel in Mandarin or Taiwanese. All assessments were completed in a single session. Questionnaire responses were clarified with assistance when needed; HRQoL responses were provided directly by participants.

Sarcopenia was defined according to the 2025 AWGS criteria (Chen et al., 2025). Because all participants were aged ≥65 years, all met the age-based case-finding criterion. Participants were classified into three categories based on muscle strength and muscle mass: (1) at risk for sarcopenia (neither low muscle strength nor low muscle mass), (2) possible sarcopenia (low muscle strength only); and (3) sarcopenia (low muscle strength and low muscle mass). For regression analyses, participants without sarcopenia (at-risk and possible sarcopenia groups) were combined as the reference category. This approach aligns the descriptive classification with the binary outcome used in regression analyses.

Handgrip strength was measured using a Jamar® Hydraulic Hand Dynamometer (Performance Health, Canada). Participants were seated with the elbow flexed at 90°. The highest of three trials was recorded; the unaffected hand was used if needed. Low muscle strength was defined as <28 kg (men) and < 18 kg (women) (Chen et al., 2025).

Appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) was measured using bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (InBody® S10; InBody Co., Ltd., Seoul, South Korea) according to manufacturer guidelines. Low muscle mass was defined using height-adjusted ASM (ASM/height2), with cutoffs of <7.0 kg/m2 (men) and < 5.7 kg/m2 (women) (Chen et al., 2025).

Although not required for the AWGS 2025 sarcopenia classification, physical performance was retained as a secondary functional outcome. Physical performance was assessed using the Five-Time Chair Stand Test. Those who required ≥12 s or were unable to complete the test indicated poor physical performance (Chen et al., 2025).

Nutritional status was assessed using the Chinese Mini Nutritional Assessment (MNA) (Vellas et al., 1999), categorized as well-nourished (≥ 24), at risk (17–23.5), or malnourished (< 17).

HRQoL was measured using the traditional Chinese 36-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-36) (Ware et al., 1994) with Taiwan-specific norm-based scoring (Tseng et al., 2003). Scores were standardized (mean = 50, SD = 10); ≥ 50 indicated above-average and < 50 below-average health. The Physical Component Summary (PCS) and Mental Component Summary (MCS) were analyzed. Responses were self-reported by participants with interviewer assistance. Internal consistency was high (Cronbach's α = 0.91). For regression analyses, PCS and MCS scores were dichotomized at 50, with scores <50 indicating poor HRQoL and ≥ 50 indicating average or above-average HRQoL.

Demographic and clinical variables were collected via interview and medical records. Covariates included age, sex, marital status, education, BMI (Health Promotion Administration, Ministry of Health and Welfare, Taiwan, 2025), presence of ≥1 chronic condition (hypertension, diabetes mellitus, or arthritis), dementia severity (Chinese CDR; Lin and Liu, 2003), functional status (Chinese Barthel Index; Dai and Lou, 1996), and cognitive function (Chinese Mini-Mental State Exam [MMSE]; Guo et al., 1988), estimated from Cognitive Abilities Screening Instrument (CASI) scores using a validated conversion method (Chiu et al., 2021). MMSE scores range from 0 to 30, with lower scores indicating greater cognitive impairment. Lower Barthel Index scores indicate greater functional dependence.

Statistical analysis

Data were screened for accuracy, missing values, and outliers. Continuous variables were assessed for approximate normality using skewness and kurtosis. Descriptive statistics (means, SDs, frequencies, and percentages) were used to summarize participant characteristics.

Group differences between urban and rural participants were examined using chi-square tests for categorical variables and independent-samples t-tests for continuous variables. Residential area (urban vs. rural) was specified as the exposure variable.

Binary logistic regression models were used to estimate associations between residential status and each outcome, including sarcopenia (confirmed sarcopenia vs. at risk or possible sarcopenia), nutritional status (malnutrition or at risk vs. well-nourished), and HRQoL, analyzed separately as poor physical HRQoL (PCS < 50) and poor mental HRQoL (MCS < 50). Both unadjusted (crude) and adjusted models were estimated. Adjusted models included age, marital status, education, MMSE, BMI, chronic conditions, and Barthel Index.

Covariates were selected primarily based on prior literature and clinical relevance, with bivariate analyses used as a secondary consideration to assess associations within the sample. Key variables (e.g., age, cognitive function, and functional status) were retained irrespective of bivariate statistical significance to ensure appropriate adjustment for potential confounding.

Cohort (region and recruitment period) was not included as a covariate because it was highly aligned with the primary exposure (urban vs. rural residence), with minimal overlap between cohorts by residential classification. Including both variables would introduce multicollinearity and compromise model stability. No major changes in healthcare policy or dementia services occurred during the study period. Multicollinearity among covariates was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF; maximum = 1.25). Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Statistical significance was set at a two-sided p < 0.05.

There were no missing data across variables. Complete-case analysis was used, and no imputation was performed. Sixteen participants (6.7%) unable to complete the Five-Time Chair Stand test were classified as having poor physical performance rather than treated as missing. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, given the exploratory nature of the analyses. Potential biases include non-random sampling, exclusion of non-ambulatory individuals, and interviewer-assisted responses. Residential classification was partially aligned with recruitment setting and period, which may introduce residual confounding.

This study analyzed de-identified data collected under two Institutional Review Board (IRB)-approved protocols (Chang Gung Medical Foundation; IRB Nos. 202200421B0 and 202401445B0). The analyses were conducted within the scope of the approved protocols, and additional ethical approval was not required. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants and their legally authorized representatives. All data were anonymized prior to analysis and stored securely to protect participant confidentiality. Analyses were conducted using SPSS Statistics® version 26.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA).

Associations between residential area and health outcomes

Participants unable to complete the Five-Time Chair Stand Test (n = 16, 6.7%) were classified as having poor physical performance. In unadjusted models (Table 3), urban residence (reference: rural) was associated with higher odds of sarcopenia (vs. at risk or possible sarcopenia) (OR = 1.82, p = 0.04) and low muscle mass (OR = 2.42, p = 0.01), but not low muscle strength or poor physical performance. Urban residence was associated with lower odds of nutritional risk (OR = 0.34, p < 0.01). Discussion

This cross-sectional study identified urban–rural differences in sarcopenia and nutritional status among community-dwelling older adults with dementia in Taiwan. After adjustment, urban residence was associated with higher odds of sarcopenia and low muscle mass, and lower odds of malnutrition or risk of malnutrition. Associations with HRQoL were not statistically significant after adjustment, and the estimate for poor mental HRQoL was imprecise.

Urban participants had higher odds of sarcopenia (defined as low muscle strength with low muscle mass) and low muscle mass. Prior studies suggest that physical inactivity may contribute to muscle decline in older adults (Beaudart et al., 2017; Perry et al., 2023) and that urban environments may be associated with more sedentary patterns (Park et al., 2020; Goldberg et al., 2021). However, physical activity and environmental exposures were not measured in this study, so underlying mechanisms cannot be determined. In addition, residential classification was partially aligned with recruitment site, geographic region, and recruitment period; therefore, the observed differences may partly reflect site- or cohort-related effects rather than residential context alone.

Malnutrition was more common among rural participants and remained associated with residential setting after adjustment, consistent with evidence indicating disparities in dietary diversity and access to nutrition-related services in rural settings (Molani-Gol et al., 2025; Quandt et al., 2000). However, dietary intake and food access were not measured, limiting causal interpretation. The contrasting patterns observed for sarcopenia and malnutrition suggest these conditions may be influenced by partially distinct determinants.

For HRQoL, crude differences in PCS and MCS scores were attenuated after adjustment for covariates, suggesting that observed differences in HRQoL may be largely explained by demographic and clinical factors rather than residential setting alone. Prior literature indicates that HRQoL in dementia is shaped by physical, emotional, and social factors (Fábrega-Cuadros et al., 2021; Manrique-Espinoza et al., 2017) and may vary by environmental context (Giebel et al., 2025; Shih et al., 2023). In adjusted models, residential setting was not independently associated with physical or mental HRQoL.

These findings should be interpreted considering several limitations. The most important is that residential classification was partially aligned with recruitment site, geographic region, and recruitment period. Recruitment strategies also differed between cohorts: Outpatient clinic participants were selected based on clinic attendance, whereas dementia care center participants were recruited consecutively. Together, these factors limit the ability to isolate urban-rural effects from site- or cohort-related influences and introduce potential selection bias that limits comparability between groups. The cross-sectional design and non-random sampling further preclude causal inference, and the unknown participation rate in the clinic-based cohort is an additional source of selection bias. At the measurement level, key explanatory variables—including physical activity, dietary intake, social support, and environmental characteristics—were not assessed, introducing potential residual confounding (Rothman et al., 2008). HRQoL and some covariates relied on assisted self-report, which may introduce measurement error in the context of the participants' cognitive impairment.

Regarding generalizability, exclusion of non-ambulatory individuals means findings are most applicable to ambulatory, community-dwelling older adults with mild to moderate dementia in Taiwan. Finally, because sarcopenia, nutritional status, and HRQoL were analyzed as separate outcomes, cumulative functional decline may not be fully captured; future studies might consider a frailty-based composite index for a more integrated assessment.

Despite these constraints, the study provides evidence of differential patterns in sarcopenia and nutritional status across residential contexts within a dementia population. From a clinical perspective, the findings support incorporating routine screening for sarcopenia and malnutrition into dementia care. Interventions may need to be tailored to context; however, these implications should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating, not causal evidence of urban–rural differences. Future longitudinal, multisite studies with standardized sampling and comprehensive measurement of behavioral and environmental factors are needed to clarify underlying mechanisms and better characterize multidimensional vulnerability in this population.

Conclusions

Urban-rural differences were observed in sarcopenia and nutritional status among community-dwelling older adults with dementia in Taiwan, whereas HRQoL was not significantly associated with residential area after adjustment. Given the cross-sectional design, pooled cohorts, and incomplete participation data in one setting, causal inference is not possible and residual confounding remains. These findings suggest context-sensitive assessment and care planning and highlight the need for longitudinal, multi-site studies to better isolate geographic effects.
