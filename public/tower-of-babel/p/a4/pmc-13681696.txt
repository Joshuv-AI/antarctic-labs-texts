New design for universal respiratory vaccines: A new paradigm on integrated immunity
Authors: Zhen Liu, Shuo Wang
Published: 2026

Respiratory infections and related diseases are among the leading contributors to the global burden of disease. Given the diversity (including viruses, bacteria, and fungi) and variability of respiratory pathogens, identifying broad-spectrum vaccine design strategies remains a key research priority [1]. Mucosal vaccines have garnered increasing attention due to their unique ability to induce both local secretory antibody responses and tissue-resident immune cell responses at the respiratory entry sites, thereby providing protection against infection and transmission. Developing broad-spectrum and effective mucosal vaccines has thus remained an important question worthy of exploration in the field.

A longstanding observation in vaccinology is that certain live-attenuated vaccines, such as Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG), confer nonspecific protection against unrelated infections. This phenomenon has been partly attributed to trained immunity—a memory-like state in innate immune cells driven by durable epigenetic and metabolic reprogramming, which enhances their responsiveness to secondary microbial challenges [2]. Building on this framework, recent studies have shown that intranasal mucosal vaccination can similarly reshape the immune landscape of the lungs, suggesting that this approach may also confer nonspecific protection.

Recently, a study published in Science by Zhang et al. reported an intranasal liposomal vaccine combining Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and TLR7/8 agonists (GLA-3M-052-LS) with the model antigen ovalbumin (OVA). This vaccine conferred durable, broadly protective immunity in mice against diverse respiratory viruses, Staphylococcus aureus, Acinetobacter baumannii, and house dust mite–induced allergic asthma [3]. This work advances the concept the authors term “integrated organ immunity”—a coordinated interplay between antigen-specific tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs), epigenetically reprogrammed alveolar macrophages (AMs), and lung structural cells [4]. Here, we summarize the key findings of this study, discuss the underlying mechanisms, and highlight unresolved questions and translational challenges.

BROAD VACCINE DESIGN AND THE MECHANISMS OF HETEROLOGOUS PROTECTION

In the study by Zhang et al., mice were intranasally vaccinated with the combination of the TLR4 agonist GLA and the TLR7/8 agonist 3M-052-LS together with ovalbumin (GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA) and were subsequently challenged with a panel of respiratory pathogens at various time points [3] (Figure 1). Vaccinated mice exhibited markedly reduced weight loss and lower viral loads following respiratory viral challenge, and cross protection was observed against related mouse-adapted respiratory coronaviruses. In addition, the vaccine conferred durable protection against intranasal bacterial challenge with S. aureus and A. baumannii, with reduced lung bacterial loads persisting for at least three months. This broad antibacterial protection is particularly noteworthy given the central role that pattern recognition receptors (PRRs)—especially TLRs, nucleotide oligomerization domain-like receptors, and C-type lectin receptors—play in detecting bacterial pathogens and initiating innate immune signaling cascades within the pulmonary immune system. These findings suggest that the dual TLR4/TLR7/8 agonist formulation may potently engage these conserved sensing pathways to prime the lung against diverse bacterial threats. Immunological characterization revealed that intranasal vaccination generated a high frequency of long-lived OVA-specific CD4+ and CD8+ T cells in the lungs. Importantly, these cells exhibited a TRM phenotype and persisted for at least three months post vaccination [3]. The adaptive immune response was essential for durable, broad protection. Depletion of both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells abolished vaccine-mediated protection against both respiratory viral challenge and S. aureus, confirming a requirement for adaptive immunity. Notably, although the same group previously demonstrated that BCG-mediated cross protection depends on interferon gamma (IFN-γ) [6], in the present model, pharmacological inhibition of IFN-γ, tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), or CD40L did not abrogate GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA–induced protection [3], indicating a mechanistically distinct protective pathway. Instead, the authors identified receptor activator of nuclear factor-κB ligand (RANKL) as a critical molecular mediator. The neutralization of RANKL during immunization abolished vaccine-induced protection against both respiratory viral challenge and S. aureus. This raises the intriguing possibility that GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA vaccination operated through a RANKL-dependent, IFN-γ–independent pathway. Notably, RANKL is produced by a broad range of cell types beyond T cells, including stromal cells, dendritic cells, and B cells. Therefore, the precise cellular sources responsible for the protective RANKL signal in this context remain to be defined. Furthermore, the downstream signaling events linking RANKL-RANK engagement on AMs to specific chromatin modifications (e.g., which histone marks are altered and which chromatin remodelers are recruited) need to be further explored.

ALVEOLAR MACROPHAGE REPROGRAMMING AND TERTIARY LYMPHOID STRUCTURE FORMATION

Another central finding of the study is that vaccination with GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA induces persistent epigenomic remodeling of AMs, establishing a heightened state of readiness that persists long after initial innate responses have subsided to baseline levels. Single-cell assay for transposase-accessible chromatin using sequencing (ATAC-seq) on lung cells revealed that chromatin loci associated with antigen-presentation genes, interferon-stimulated genes, and immunoregulatory genes, and membrane lipid transport remained accessible in AMs for at least three months post vaccination. Transcription factor motif analysis showed sustained enrichment of activator protein 1 (AP-1), signal transducers and activators of transcription (STATs), interferon regulatory factors (IRFs), and nuclear factor kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) family motifs in AMs and T cells, consistent with features of trained immunity [2,7]. Crucially, T-cell depletion during immunization suppressed AM activation and major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II) upregulation, demonstrating that the adaptive immune response is not merely a bystander but an essential driver of innate reprogramming [3].

Functionally, the reprogrammed AMs exhibited an enhanced phagocytic capacity as evidenced by the increased uptake of fluorescently labeled S. aureus as well as apoptotic and necrotic neutrophils. Depletion of AMs via clodronate liposomes before the bacterial challenge compromised vaccine-induced protection, confirming their role as critical early effectors [3]. Furthermore, spatial transcriptomic analysis showed that these trained AMs exhibited enhanced ligand‒receptor interactions with T cells and alveolar epithelial cells, suggesting the existence of a tissue-level communication network that supports integrated organ immunity.

Upon respiratory viral challenge, vaccinated mice rapidly formed organized tertiary lymphoid structures (TLSs), featuring distinct T-cell and B-cell zones in the lung tissues by day 3 post infection, while these features were absent in unvaccinated controls at the same time point. These organized structures are reminiscent of inducible bronchus-associated lymphoid tissue (iBALT), which is typically observed during respiratory viral infections and may facilitate the establishment of long-term immune memory within the lungs. The rapid emergence of such structures in vaccinated animals suggests that GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA imprints the lung microenvironment with the capacity for accelerated iBALT-like organogenesis upon pathogen encounter. Crucially, these rapid pathogen-specific responses were accompanied by reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines (IL-6, CXCL10, CCL2, and GM-CSF), suggesting that vaccination not only accelerates pathogen clearance but also limits immunopathology [3]. However, because the study did not analyze TLSs prior to viral challenge, it remains formally unclear whether such iBALT-like structures had already begun to form following GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA immunization or whether they were entirely induced and accelerated by the viral challenge itself—an important question that warrants direct verification in future work. Interestingly, the study also demonstrated that GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA conferred long-lasting protection against house dust mite–induced allergic asthma, characterized by reduced eosinophil infiltration, decreased Th2 cytokines, and lower serum IgE levels. This finding extends the scope of integrated organ immunity–based interventions beyond infectious diseases to allergic diseases [3].

LIMITATIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The Specificity–breadth Trade-off: What if OVA were Replaced?

This is a highly innovative study in the field of broad-spectrum vaccine design. However, an intellectually provocative question remains: what would happen if the model antigen OVA were replaced with a pathogen-derived antigen, such as the hemagglutinin protein of the influenza virus or other respiratory viral antigens? In principle, this could combine the broad innate protection conferred by TLR-mediated AM reprogramming with the specific adaptive immunity (neutralizing antibodies and pathogen-specific T cells) against the included antigen.

However, an important consideration is whether specificity and breadth are truly additive or might involve trade-offs. It is well established that immune responses are shaped by competition among T-cell clones and by the nature of the inflammatory milieu at the time of priming [2]. Specifically, a strongly immunodominant pathogen-derived antigen could skew the T-cell repertoire and the cytokine environment in ways that either enhance or diminish the nonspecific innate reprogramming. For instance, T cells specific for a dominant viral antigen might produce a different cytokine profile than OVA-specific T cells, potentially altering the RANKL-dependent AM training. Whether broad innate readiness and focused adaptive precision can coexist within the same vaccine platform remains an open question that merits systematic experimental investigation.

Translational Considerations

All experiments in this study were performed in specific pathogen-free (SPF) mice—immunologically naïve animals whose mucosal immune landscapes differ profoundly from those of adult humans, whose airways have been shaped by decades of microbial and environmental exposures. The authors acknowledge this limitation, noting that although protection was maintained in influenza-experienced mice, the more complex exposure history of humans could potentially alter vaccine responsiveness [3]. Future controlled human infection studies will therefore be essential to elucidate this. In addition, given the well-recognized limitations of animal models and human vaccine outcomes, the translational advancement of GLA-3M-052-LS + OVA should be approached with caution. Systematic studies comparing safety, immunogenicity, and protective efficacy would help define the extent to which the observed effects are conserved across species and would provide stronger support for subsequent clinical evaluation.

Additional translational questions include whether human AMs can be similarly epigenetically reprogrammed, whether human TRMs express sufficient RANKL to sustain the proposed feedback circuit, and whether repeated intranasal administration of potent TLR agonists is safe and tolerable in the human upper airway. While the favorable clinical safety records of GLA and 3M-052 in other contexts [3] provide some reassurance, nasal mucosal tolerance and the risk of chronic inflammation will require careful evaluation. The authors note that two to four intranasal doses conferred protection in mice, suggesting that future optimization to two doses combined with user-friendly devices such as nasal sprays may enable practical deployment in humans [3]. A further important consideration is the durability of the response. Although protection was maintained for at least three months in mice, this duration may be insufficient when judged against conventional vaccines. Consequently, the long-term durability of protection—particularly in humans—warrants more thorough investigation.

TOWARD A NEW GENERATION OF BROADLY PROTECTIVE VACCINES

Zhang et al. present a study showing that protection does not require antigen matching between the vaccine and the pathogen. Their finding—that a mucosal vaccine carrying an irrelevant antigen can confer broad, durable protection against viruses, bacteria, and allergens through integrated organ immunity [4]—represents a conceptual advance in vaccinology. Beyond infectious disease control, this paradigm may have implications for vaccine development against antimicrobial-resistant (AMR) pathogens, as well as for protecting immunocompromised populations and for individuals who respond suboptimally to conventional antigen-specific vaccines.

An important open question is how the qualitative features of immune memory and pathogen-specific responses elicited by such broad-spectrum designs differ from those produced by classical antigen-matched vaccines. It is also worth examining the effectiveness and mechanism of such broadly protective vaccines in real-world natural infection contexts, where coinfections, prior exposures, and host genetic variation may modulate outcomes. Future work dissecting how environmental and host factors shape integrated organ immunity will be valuable.

Overall, by reframing vaccine design around tissue-level immune coordination rather than antigen specificity alone, this study provides important theoretical support for the development of universal mucosal vaccines against diverse respiratory threats.
