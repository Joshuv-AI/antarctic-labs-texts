Do the habits and preferences of heated-tobacco-product users affect the emission characteristics of nicotine, PG, and VG?
Authors: Dong-Han Kim, Dae-Hyeon Kim, Youn-Suk Son
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, the delivery of three substances (nicotine, propylene glycol (PG), and vegetable glycerin (VG)) in vapor generated by HTPs was evaluated based on the habits and preferences of users. To achieve this, the following five key characteristics were considered: operation method, device temperature, impact of the cartridge, flavor, and capsule break. These key features, influenced by the habits and preferences of users, were shown to affect emission profiles. This study was conducted in compliance with Health Canada Intense and used a smoking machine to capture vapor from HTPs. Each device has different operation methods that can be chosen by users. The cartridge had the most significant impact on substance delivery depending on the operation mechanism. Additionally, device temperature was correlated with concentrations of emitted substances. Tobacco sticks also showed significant impact on substance delivery. Notably, capsule break increased relative standard deviation (RSD) of PG from 2.17% to 10.22% and RSD of VG from 3.57% to 28.07% in Glo Hyper X2 fruit-flavored tobacco sticks. These findings suggest that delivery of substances is influenced by conditions of HTPs and the conditions are influenced by the habits, behaviors, and preferences of users.

Introduction

Conventional cigarettes have been widely used for many decades. As a result, there are many research studies about their harmful effects on human body (Das, 2003; Doll and Hill, 1950; Hbejan, 2011). According to previous studies, conventional cigarette smoke contains over 6000 compounds (Rodgman and Perfetti, 2012). The World Health Organization (WHO) has gone beyond advocacy through the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control (WHO FCTC), a legally binding global treaty that commits Parties to implementing comprehensive tobacco control measures (WHO, 2003). Due to these WHO and social trends, some cigarette companies, such as Philip Morris International (PMI), British American Tobacco (BAT) and KT&G, have introduced new cigarette types such as Heated-Tobacco-Products (HTPs). These HTPs heat the tobacco stick to approximately 350 °C to create vapor without burning it (Paumgartten, 2018; Ratajczak et al., 2020).

HTPs were first introduced in 1988 by R.J.Reynold (RJR) under the name ‘Premier’. In 1994, RJR launched ‘Eclipse’ and PMI introduced ‘Accord’ to the market. However, these HTPs were inadequate to meet the needs of consumers and soon discontinued. In 2014, PMI relaunched an HTP named ‘IQOS’ in Italy and Japan (Proctor, 2018). Despite these advances, HTP did not deliver the throat hit desire by users (Caputi, 2017). To address this, cigarette companies developed various types of HTPs with different heating methods in order to increase the delivery of propylene glycol (PG) and vegetable glycerin (VG), which can enhance flavor, throat hit, and volume of vapor (Li et al., 2016; Spindle et al., 2018). PG is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) as generally recognized as safe (Fowles et al., 2013). It has also been reported that VG does not cause any issues when ingested into the human body. However, it has been reported through mice experiments that PG and VG were toxic when inhaled in the form of aerosol, and they indicated that PG and VG can also be harmful to the human body (Phillips et al., 2017; Werley et al., 2016). Furthermore, PG and VG produces various volatile organic compounds (VOCs) including acetaldehyde and formaldehyde by pyrolysis (AlGemayel et al., 2024; Goenka, 2023).

Given these toxicological concerns related to aerosolized PG and VG and their degradation products, various studies have investigated how different heating methods in HTPs influence substance emissions. However, these studies either focused on particulate matter rather than PG, nicotine (NC), and VG (Zervas et al., 2024), did not address newer heating methods (Jankowski et al., 2019), or, despite mentioning multiple heating configurations, compared HTPs only with conventional cigarettes rather than directly comparing emissions across difference heating methods (Upadhyay et al., 2023). Therefore, this study aims to compare emission profiles across three representative heating methods currently commercially available. Furthermore, we conducted an in-depth analysis of HTPs to clarify the role of cartridges in substance delivery.

Several studies have reported that HTPs generally deliver lower nicotine yields per stick than conventional cigarettes (Bitzer et al., 2020). As a result, users may engage in successive or compensatory puffing in order to achieve their desire over time. Previous studies have also shown that higher temperatures are associated with increased emissions of PG and VG (AlGemayel et al., 2024; Jiang et al., 2025; Kim and An, 2020). In this study, we implemented realistic consecutive vaping sessions to compare emission characteristics at elevated device temperature. In addition to device-related factors, the flavor of tobacco stick itself can also influence substance emissions. In particular, a wide variety of flavored tobacco sticks has been introduced in recent years, and several studies have therefore examined how emissions differ according to stick flavor. Yet, these studies have been limited to specific HTPs (Davigo et al., 2024). Therefore, in this study, we selected menthol-flavored and fruit-flavored tobacco sticks for three representative HTPs to compare their emission characteristics. Not only flavor, but also capsule break can also affect substance emissions. Several studies have also addressed this issues, however, they have either focused primarily on VOCs emissions (Lim et al., 2023), or have been limited to analyses of conventional cigarettes (Lim et al., 2022). In this study, we aimed to quantitatively characterize the emission features of selected substances according to capsule break in HTPs.

Collectively, previous studies have examined emissions from HTPs across a range of device-related and stick-related factors. However, most of these studies have focused on a limited set of specific products or primarily on VOCs emissions. VOCs can be emitted directly from the tobacco matrix, but they can also be formed via thermal degradation of PG and VG, indicating that both formulation and operating conditions play important roles. Moreover, emission levels can vary across manufacturers (Davigo et al., 2024), and they can be further modified by user habits and preferences (Mallock et al., 2018). However, from this perspective, comparing changes based on specific characteristics is relatively challenging due to the trace amount of VOCs produced. While VOCs and aldehydes are important emission constituents, PG and VG are present at substantially higher concentrations, allowing more robust and consistent comparison across devices and products characteristics. Consequently, we intend to verify these changes using PG and VG, which are relatively more abundant. For this end, we analyzed emission characteristics of HTPs as a function of both device and stick, focusing on five key factors: 1) heating method, 2) impact of cartridge, 3) device temperature, 4) flavor, and 5) capsule break.

Materials and methods

Materials

Target compounds

Three substances (PG, nicotine, and VG) generated from HTPs were assessed according to types of devices and stick. To accurately quantify levels of these substances, calibration standards were prepared using commercially available PG, nicotine, and VG, each with a purity of over 99.5%, 99%, and 99%, respectively. All of these substances were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (USA). Methanol as a diluent solvent was purchased from Samchun (South Korea) with a purity over 99.5%.

HTPs

To evaluate features of emitted substances depending on mechanical characteristics of HTPs, we purchased three different types of HTPs: Lil Solid 2.0 (KT&G, South Korea) known to directly heat tobacco leaves with a heating blade, Glo Hyper X2 (BAT, UK)known to heat tobacco stick with an induction method, and Lil Hybrid 3.0 (KT&G, South Korea)known to combine induction heating with a cartridge system. The cartridge was filled with liquid that consisted of PG and VG. Fig. 1 shows mechanical characteristics of each device. In addition, given that menthol flavor is prevalent in most flavored capsules, we chose to use one menthol-flavored cigarette and one fruit-flavored cigarette for each device (Dolka et al., 2013): Glo Hyper X2 (New Neo Boost (menthol-flavored) and New Neo Purple Boost (grape-flavored)), Lil Solid 2.0 (FIIT Change (menthol-flavored) and FIIT Sparky (apricot-flavored)), and Lil Hybrid 3.0 (MIIX Icean (menthol-flavored) and MIIX Blusome (pineapple-flavored)). Experimental methods

Vapor generation and collection

Numerous standardized methods exist for capturing smoke from conventional cigarettes, including CORESTA, Health Canada Intense (HCI), and ISO. Conversely, a standardized method for capturing vapor from HTP sticks is currently lacking. Consequently, we adopted the HCI method (puff volume: 55 mL; puff duration: two seconds; two puffs per minute; puff numbers: 14 times; ventilation hole was blocked). The HCI method has been widely employed in previous studies (Forster et al., 2018; Yu et al., 2022). Given that the HCI method requires almost seven minutes to complete and that HTPs typically have an operating time of less than 5 min, we adjusted the procedure to utilize two tobacco sticks and performed seven puffs per stick. To minimize variability related to product storage and handling, tobacco sticks were used within 24 h of opening, and capsules were broken immediately before heating when required. To capture the vapor from HTPs, a smoking machine (Modified TE-2e system, Teague Enterprises, CA, USA) was used. Fig. 2 shows the overall sampling system including the smoking machine. The flow rate of the smoking machine was 1.5 L/min. We checked the flow rate for every experiment using a flow meter (Series 4100, TSI, USA). To capture the vapor generated by HTPs, 44 mm Cambridge Whatman glass fiber filter pads (Whatman, UK) were used. To prevent contamination of a vaporing collection device, the cleaning of all paths for sampling was processed sufficiently with ambient air after each sampling session (over 10 min; ventilation flow rate: 2 L/min). Additionally, all Tygon tubing within the sampling path were replaced prior to collecting each sample to prevent interference from moisture and contaminants.Fig. 2A sampling system for collecting vapor generated from HTPs.Fig. 2

Analysis

After collecting vapor, filters were placed in a 10 mL amber vial filled with 10 mL of methanol (Samchun, Korea) and sonicated for 30 min. After sonication, the methanol solution was filtered with a 0.45 μm membrane syringe filter (0.45 μm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) filter media, Whatman, UK) and subsequently analyzed using Gas Chromatography (GC) combined with a Flame Ionized Detector (FID) (6890, Agilent, USA). The column was a fused-silica capillary column (DB-Wax, 30 m × 0.25 mm i.d.; 0.25 μm film thickness). Nitrogen was used as a carrier gas. The GC-FID system was programmed from 60 to 220 °C (2 min hold) at a rate of 10 °C/min. Each sample of 2 μL was injected using a splitless mode at an injector temperature of 250 °C. QA/QC

Liquid working standards (WSs) for three target substances (nicotine, PG, and VG) were prepared by diluting reagent-grade chemicals (RGCs) with methanol. To prepare the first WS, RGCs of the three target substances were combined with methanol. Concentrations of nicotine, PG, and VG were 0.8, 0.56, and 1.4 μg/μL, respectively. The first WS was then diluted with methanol to create the second WS, resulting in concentrations of 0.4 μg/μL for nicotine, 0.28 μg/μL for PG, and 0.7 μg/μL for VG. A total of five-point calibration data were created using this method. Concentration ranges for nicotine, PG, and VG were 0.05–0.8, 0.035–0.6, and 0.0875–1.4 μg/μL, respectively.

Quantitative calibration of the GC-FID response was performed using WS. Strong linear correlation (R2 > 0.999) was observed between injected mass and peak area for all analytes, as illustrated in Fig. S1. The method detection limit (MDL) and relative standard deviation (RSD) were used to assess the sensitivity and precision of the detection system. The MDL calculated as three times the standard deviation of the response at the lowest calibration point was below 16.43 ng for nicotine, PG, and VG (n = 7). The RSD determined from the variability of peak area response to repeated injections of samples with the same mass was less than 8.5% (n = 3) (Table S2).

Results and discussion

Device

Types of HTPs

We evaluated emission characteristics of substances (nicotine, PG, and VG) depending on the operation mechanism of HTPs. Glo Hyper X2 indirectly heats the tobacco stick through an induction process. Lil Solid 2.0 directly heats tobacco leaves using a heating blade. Lil Hybrid 3.0 utilizes a cartridge with an induction heating method, leading to lower heating temperature than others. Additionally, two different flavored cigarettes were used for each device. Fig. 4 shows mean concentrations of emitted substances based on the operating mechanism of HTPs. Mean concentrations (RSDs) of nicotine for menthol-flavored cigarette with broken capsules from Glo Hyper X2 (G-M-O), Lil Solid 2.0 (S-M—O), and Lil Hybrid 3.0 (H-M—O) were 1.18 ± 0.08, 1.56 ± 0.14, and 1.19 ± 0.12 mg/cig (6.66, 9.23, and 10.03%), respectively. Nicotine delivery was stable across all devices, as indicated by RSDs. However, PG and VG showed different patterns with nicotine. Mean concentrations (RSDs) of PG for G-M-O, S-M—O, and H-M-O were 0.52 ± 0.03, 0.89 ± 0.14, and 4.15 ± 0.20 mg/cig (6.13, 16.13, and 4.75%). VG values were 4.45 ± 1.56, 8.36 ± 0.46, and 15.78 ± 0.45 mg/cig (35.03, 5.55, and 2.86%), respectively. Glo Hyper X2, which utilizes the induction heating method showed unstable delivery for VG. Conversely, Lil Solid 2.0, which utilizes a heating blade, showed unstable delivery in PG. While these two devices showed unstable delivery at PG and VG respectively, the delivery of Lil Hybrid 3.0 for all substances was stable. Similar trends were observed in fruit-flavored cigarettes. Mean concentrations (RSDs) of nicotine in fruit-flavored cigarette with broken capsules (G-F-O, S-F-O, and H-F-O) were 1.28 ± 0.12, 1.57 ± 0.13, and 1.17 ± 0.07 mg/cig (9.14, 7.99, and 6.27%), respectively. However, mean concentrations (RSDs) of PG were 0.79 ± 0.08, 0.30 ± 0.10, and 3.94 ± 0.35 mg/cig (10.22, 34.01, and 8.85%), and VG values were 4.29 ± 1.20, 10.04 ± 1.41, and 13.40 ± 0.86 mg/cig (28.07, 14.01, and 6.45%), respectively. Not only in menthol-flavored cigarettes, but also in fruit-flavored cigarettes, Lil Hybrid 3.0 which utilizes the cartridge system demonstrated more consistent delivery compared to other devices. Both in menthol and fruit flavored cigarettes shows the stable delivery for nicotine. However, PG and VG deliveries were unstable across two different flavors. Different HTPs utilize distinct heating configurations, allowing users to choose products according to their preferences. At the same time, each commercially available device was evaluated with its own compatible tobacco sticks; therefore, the observed differences should be interpreted at the product-system level rather than as the isolated effect of heating mechanism alone. Within this framework, the comparatively stable emission profiles observed for Lil Hybrid 3.0, together with the cartridge-condition results, suggest that cartridge state may contribute to stabilizing aerosol constituent delivery. By contrast, the larger variability observed for Glo Hyper X2 and Lil Solid 2.0 indicates that emissions from these systems may be more sensitive to device- and stick-related conditions. Consequently, device choice may lead to substantial variation in the delivery of nicotine, PG, and VG, potentially beyond users' initial expectations.Fig. 4Concentrations of propylene glycol, nicotine, and vegetable glycerin emitted from different types of HTPs. Previous studies have indicated that PG and VG themselves exhibit toxicity and can be converted into toxic aldehydes through decomposition (Bekki et al., 2014; Dai et al., 2018). Depending on the heating method, the amounts of PG and VG emitted from the tobacco stick can be unstable or even exceed generally expected levels. This implies that there may be unforeseen health risks to the human body.

Cartridge

The delivery of nicotine is facilitated by vapor generated during vaping of HTPs (Sussman et al., 2023). Lil Hybrid 3.0 utilizes a cartridge which filled with a liquid consisting of PG and VG. PG and VG in HTPs could enhance throat hit, vapor production, and flavor. To evaluate the impact of cartridge on concentrations and emission stability of substances, two different flavored cigarettes with broken capsules were used. Additionally, we compared different cartridge conditions: fully charged cartridge with upright position (H-M—S, H-F-S) and cartridge charged below 50% with horizontal position (H-M—N, H-F-N). Fig. 5 shows concentrations and RSD values for generated substances, with statistically significant differences identified through t-tests and denoted by an asterisk (*) (p < 0.05). Mean concentrations (RSD values) of PG, nicotine, and VG for H-M-S were 4.15 ± 0.20, 1.19 ± 0.12, and 15.78 ± 0.45 mg/cig (4.75, 10.03, and 2.86%), respectively. Conversely, mean concentrations (RSD values) for H-M-N were 2.64 ± 0.60, 0.64 ± 0.15, and 16.90 ± 3.13 mg/cig (22.67, 24.13, and 18.55%), respectively. The mean concentration of PG and nicotine for H-M-S were higher than those for H-M—N, as confirmed by the results of a t-test (p < 0.05). VG concentration showed a different pattern compared to the other two substances. The mean concentration of VG of H-M-S was slightly lower than that of H-M—N, but the difference was not statistically significant (p > 0.05). The RSDs showed significantly high values in H-M—N. An analogous trend was found for fruit-flavored cigarettes. Mean concentrations (RSD values) of PG, nicotine, and VG substances for H-F-S were 3.94 ± 0.35, 1.17 ± 0.07, and 13.40 ± 0.86 mg/cig (8.85, 6.27, and 6.45%), respectively. Mean concentrations (RSDs) for H-F-N were 3.66 ± 0.54, 0.90 ± 0.38, and 15.64 ± 6.57 mg/cig (14.85, 41.76, and 42.03%), respectively. Mean concentrations of PG and nicotine was higher in H-F-S compared to H-F-N, VG values were lower in H-F-S. However, unlike menthol-flavored tobacco, the decrease in mean concentration was not statistically significant (p < 0.05). Since nicotine is delivered through vapor, and vapor is influenced by PG and VG (Li et al., 2020), unstable delivery of PG and VG can affect the delivery of nicotine. While nicotine concentration remains unaffected by the cartridge, nicotine delivery can be influenced by the properties of the generated vapor. Additionally, the cartridge solution can vary depending on an individual's usage habits (upright or horizontal storage when not in use), which in turn affects the volume of vapor generated. Consequently, the amount of substance absorbed into the human body may also vary due to differences in generated vapor volume. Moreover, these findings imply that not only vaping sessions but also the storage conditions of the HTP and individual vaping habits impact substance emissions. Taken together, these findings suggest that cartridge state, including fill condition and device orientation under the tested conditions, may play an important role in stabilizing aerosol constituent delivery in cartridge-assisted HTP systems.Fig. 5Levels of propylene glycol, nicotine, and vegetable glycerin according to cartridge conditions. Temperature

Fig. 6 presents emission characteristics of substances depending on device temperature. To compare emission concentrations according to device temperature, three products (G-M-X, S-M-X, and H-M-X) were used. Emission levels of PG, nicotine, and VG varied depending on device temperature regardless of the device type. Due to the differences in heating methods, the heating temperature varies depending on the type of device. For Glo Hyper X2, the peak temperature during the first session was 206 °C, whereas it increased to 234 °C during consecutive sessions. In the case of the Lil Solid 2.0, which utilizes a heating blade, the peak temperature during the first session was 202 °C, rising to 234 °C in the consecutive sessions. The Lil Hybrid 3.0 showed lower heating temperatures compared to the others, with a peak temperature of 136 °C during the first session and 168 °C during consecutive sessions. These increases in temperature across devices are caused by the residual heat from the first vaping session. Concentrations of these substances were generally higher during the consecutive vaping sessions than those during the first vaping session. PG levels for G-M-X, S-M-X, and H-M-X devices during the first vaping session were 0.40, 1.21, and 3.69 mg/cig, respectively. In the subsequent session, these values increased to 0.48, 1.40, and 4.32 mg/cig. Concentrations of nicotine and VG showed similar trends to PG. Conclusions

In this study, we quantitatively evaluated emissions of nicotine, PG, and VG from HTPs based on five critical factors: operation mechanisms, cartridge conditions, heating temperature, flavor, and capsule break. Nicotine emissions were consistently stable across all devices, regardless of operational differences. However, VG emissions from Glo Hyper X2 exhibited high variability, and PG emissions from the Lil Solid 2.0 were also highly variable, resulting in a high RSD. Lil Hybrid 3.0, combining cartridge and induction heating, demonstrated the most stable emissions for all substances. Cartridge conditions significantly impacted emission stability. Notably, in horizontal position, emission variability markedly increased, showing higher RSDs for nicotine, PG, and VG. Temperature of the device also affects emission of substances. Especially, because HTPs deliver less nicotine than conventional cigarettes, users tend to vape repeatedly. These consecutive sessions cause residual heating, which increases emission of PG, nicotine, and VG. Flavor difference also affects the emission of substances. While nicotine emissions remained consistent across flavors and devices, PG and VG concentrations and their variabilities depended significantly on flavor, with menthol variants generally showing higher PG levels and different VG stability profiles. To enhance or change flavor of tobacco sticks, users often break their capsules. However, capsule break reduced nicotine emissions while markedly increasing the variability of PG and VG delivery in all devices except for Lil Hybrid 3.0. Combined with consecutive vaping sessions prompted by lower nicotine delivery, this instability may elevate the exposures of users to emitted substances beyond initial expectations. These findings extend previous studies that have often focused on limited products or selected emission constituents by showing that constituent delivery can vary substantially across HTPs and use-related conditions. From a public health and regulatory perspective, such variability suggests that simplified assumptions of reduced harm for HTPs should be interpreted cautiously. However, the present study did not provide a quantitative health risk assessment or direct comparisons with broader toxicant panels, regulatory benchmarks, or conventional cigarettes, and the findings should therefore be interpreted as evidence of variability in nicotine, PG, and VG delivery rather than as direct estimates of comparative health risk.
