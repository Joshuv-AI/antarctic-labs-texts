Direct Synthesis of High‐Valence Protein@UiO‐66 Composites: Linking Crystallization Pathways to Protein Encapsulation
Authors: Jesús Cases Díaz, Jana Glatz, Elisa Merced Olivas, Ramon Pons, Joseph P Patterson, Mónica Giménez‐Marqués
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe direct synthesis of high‐valence metal–organic frameworks (HV‐MOFs) under biocompatible conditions remains an outstanding challenge due to the strong Lewis acidity and sluggish coordination kinetics of their metal centers. Here, we report a mild, aqueous synthesis of UiO‐66—a benchmark Zr4 +‐based framework—using the conventional benzene dicarboxylic acid (H2BDC) linker. The approach enables the formation of highly crystalline protein@UiO‐66 composites while preserving biomolecule integrity and activity. In situ SAXS and cryoEM reveal that protein incorporation modulates prenucleation and growth dynamics, providing mechanistic insight into how the Lewis acidity of Zr4 + governs framework assembly and biomolecule confinement. This work establishes a direct, biocompatible route to UiO‐66 and elucidates fundamental principles underlying MOF–biomolecule co‐assembly in highly Lewis‐acidic systems, advancing the design of robust biointegrated hybrid materials.Keywords: biocomposites, growth kinetics, high‐valence metal–organic frameworks, protein encapsulation

Introduction

The integration of proteins within metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) offers a powerful route to enhance the functional stability and reusability of biomolecules in catalysis, sensing, and biomedical applications [1, 2, 3, 4]. The structural tunability and porosity of MOFs provide ideal micro‐environments for immobilization and protection [5], enabling hybrid materials that merge biological activity with the robustness of inorganic frameworks.

Among available strategies, in situ encapsulation—where the MOF crystallizes around the biomolecule—has attracted particular attention [6, 7, 8]. This one‐step process promotes intimate coupling between the protein and framework precursors, which can modulate nucleation, crystal growth, and the spatial distribution of the protein within the resulting material [9]. Zeolitic imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs), and especially ZIF‐8, have been widely studied for protein encapsulation [10]. The Zn2 +–imidazolate coordination involves soft–soft Lewis acid–base interactions, resulting in labile bonds that are sensitive to biomolecules. Under such electrostatic chemical scenario, protein addition can strongly influence crystallization pathway, topology, and morphology on ZIF‐8 formation, for example through pI‐dependent effects. Essentially, proteins with pI < 7 favor the formation of the sod topology, whereas those with pI > 7 fail to promote crystallization [11]. This pronounced protein dependence stems from the moderate Lewis acidity of Zn2 + cations, which undergo dynamic coordination equilibria during framework assembly. Thus, the evident active role of the biomolecule in the formation kinetics of protein@ZIF‐8 raises the question of whether similar effects extend to other MOF families, especially those based on more strongly coordinating metals.

According to the hard–soft acid–base (HSAB) principle [12], protein encapsulation may be more effectively directed when using hard Lewis acid cations as MOF constituents. Based on this HSAB principle, we recently established a Lewis acid‐mediated strategy to successfully encapsulate elusive, high pI proteins by using MIL‐100(Fe) and MIL‐110(Al) [13, 14]. We demonstrated that protein‐dependent formation effects are negligible under this different electrostatic chemical scenario, suggesting a more passive role for proteins during MIL‐100(Fe) and MIL‐110(Al) crystallization.

Extending this rational, zirconium(IV)—a hard Lewis acid that forms exceptionally strong and stable bonds with carboxylate ligands (hard Lewis bases)—represents an ideal candidate to govern the electrostatic interactions at the protein‐MOF interface. However, the required biocompatible conditions–typically aqueous solvents, mild temperatures, and near‐neutral pH– which are essential to maintain the structure and function of biomolecules are environments fundamentally incompatible with those required for the crystallization of many HV‐MOFs. Specifically, UiO‐66, a prototypical Zr4 +‐based framework renowned for its outstanding thermal and chemical stability, generally requires organic solvents, acidic modulators, and elevated temperatures to achieve crystallinity. Although the exceptional stability of UiO‐66 originates from the strong hard–hard coordination between Zr4 + and carboxylate linkers, this same feature limits the framework's responsiveness to biomolecular templating. The rigid coordination environment of zirconium demands higher activation energies for ordered nucleation and growth, making crystallization difficult to initiate under mild, aqueous conditions. Consequently, achieving crystalline HV‐MOFs such as UiO‐66 in biocompatible media remains challenging due to slow kinetics and limited molecular adaptability during early assembly.

Recent efforts have sought to overcome these synthetic challenges through indirect or multistep strategies. Stoddart and Farha et al. [15] achieved the mild synthesis of UiO‐66 analogue using a fluorinated benzene dicarboxylic acid (H2BDC‐F) as linker that enhances solubility and modulates coordination kinetics. While effective, this approach relies on costly linkers and less translatable conditions. Other reports have pursued amorphous‐to‐crystalline transformations as an alternative route [16], enabling protein encapsulation within an amorphous phase formed in mild environments followed by controlled crystallization. However, these indirect routes often compromise reproducibility and structural homogeneity.

Here, we report a direct synthesis of UiO‐66, under fully biocompatible conditions, using the standard benzene dicarboxylic acid (H2BDC) as linker. The method proceeds without fluorinated derivatives, post‐synthetic transformations, or harsh reagents, yielding highly crystalline UiO‐66 in aqueous media. Our protocol enables the incorporation of proteins to form protein@UiO‐66 composites with high crystallinity and optimal yields, with retention of payload activity, and demonstrating viability for upscale. Through a combination of in situ and ex situ advanced characterization, we reveal the crystallization pathway of UiO‐66 under mild conditions and elucidate how protein presence modulates pre‐nucleation dynamics, nucleation kinetics, and intraparticle organization.

Beyond establishing a direct route to UiO‐66 under biocompatible conditions, this work provides new mechanistic insight into the early stages of high‐valence UiO‐66 formation. By correlating in situ crystallization dynamics with protein–framework interactions, we uncover how the Lewis acidity of Zr4 + governs nucleation and growth, and interface evolution at the molecular level. The results clarify the balance between coordination strength, kinetic accessibility, and biomolecule confinement that defines the formation of robust, protein@HV‐MOF architectures. More broadly, this study advances the fundamental understanding of how biomolecules engage with highly Lewis acidic metal nodes, offering design principles for next generation biointegrated MOFs with controllable structure and function.

Results

Biocompatible and Direct UiO‐66 Crystallization for In Situ Growth of Protein@UiO‐66 Composites

A direct, protein‐compatible UiO‐66 crystallization protocol has been developed. To achieve the production of the representative HV‐MOF balancing crystallinity and biocompatibility required optimization of the synthetic parameters. Key aspects involved adjustment of the reaction pH media, the use of aqueous‐soluble benzene dicarboxylate (BDC) salts added under rate‐controlled conditions, and the employment of Zr6O4(OH)4(Ac)12 oxo clusters as the zirconium source [17]. In a typical synthesis, the equimolar addition of benzene dicarboxylate aqueous solution onto the acetate zirconium oxo cluster aqueous solution at 20 mL/h rate enables the preparation of crystalline, monodispersed UiO‐66 nanoparticles at room temperature. The formation of crystalline material was facilitated by modulation with acetic acid (HAc) and triethylamine (TEA) (see experimental conditions). The protocol developed demonstrates that careful control over both precursor form and reaction kinetics is critical to obtaining high‐quality crystalline HV‐MOF nanoparticles under mild conditions.

Once established the mild synthetic strategy to obtain crystalline for UiO‐66, a direct protein encapsulation was carried out. In presence of Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA) selected as a model protein, the direct strategy enables the efficient formation of BSA@UiO‐66 nanoparticles. Characterization of BSA@UiO‐66 Biocomposite

Encapsulation of the reference protein BSA was carried out employing the same direct, biocompatible method established for UiO‐66 with the addition of certain amount of protein to the acetate zirconium oxo cluster aqueous solution (see experimental section for details). Under these conditions, crystalline BSA@UiO‐66 material was successfully obtained, as revealed from X‐ray powder diffraction (XRPD) pattern featuring highly intense diffraction peaks at 2θ values of approximately 7.3° and 8.5° corresponding to the (111) and (002) crystal planes, respectively, confirming the successful synthesis of its cubic framework (Figure 2B). This notable crystallinity contrasts with the poorly crystalline materials obtained in previous attempts where UiO‐66 aqueous‐based synthesis were targeted with similar synthetic conditions [18]. Encapsulation efficiency was first determined from the reaction supernatant by bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay, revealing close to 100% of efficiency. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT‐IR) further confirms the formation of BSA@UiO‐66, showing the characteristic carboxylate bands of UiO‐66 at 1600 and 1450 cm−1. The presence of BSA is confirmed by the identification of the Amide I band at 1650 cm− 1 (Figure 2C). However, the low intensity of the protein signal and partial overlap with the UiO‐66 carboxylate bands cm− 1 hinder the clear identification of BSA Amide II and III bands (1470–1570 and 1250–1350 cm− 1, respectively) [19]. Protein loading within the composite was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA). While ligand decomposition in UiO‐66 and BSA@UiO‐66 occurs above ∼500°C, free BSA gradually degrades between 250°C and 400°C (Figure 2A). BSA@UiO‐66 thermal profile exhibits an additional weight loss in this temperature range which can be attributed to BSA decomposition. Weight losses in the BSA@UiO‐66 profile result in a lower inorganic residue at 700°C compared to UiO‐66, which enables to estimate the BSA loading as ∼14% (Table S1). This value corresponds well with the stoichiometric BSA addition, corroborating the ca. 100% efficiency of the encapsulation. Morphological analysis of the materials was carried out by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Obtained UiO‐66 material forms nearly spherical particles with an average size of 44.5 ± 12.1 nm (Figure 2D‐i,ii). BSA@UiO‐66 nanoparticles exhibit a similar morphology, though with a rougher surface, likely reflecting structural defects induced by protein incorporation (Figure 2D‐iii). The average size of the composite particles is 50.7 ± 12.5 nm, close to that of the reference UiO‐66 material (Figure 2D‐iv).

Evaluation of Enzymatic Activity of HRP@UiO‐66

Following the same procedure, horseradish peroxidase (HRP) was encapsulated in UiO‐66, yielding the HRP@UiO‐66. HRP was selected as a model enzyme since can oxidize organic compounds in presence of hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). These reactants are relatively small molecules and are expected to enter through the pores of UiO‐66 scaffold. Prior catalytic tests, HRP@UiO‐66 material was characterized (Figure S1), showing similar trends to the observed in BSA@UiO‐66 composite. Crystalline nature of HRP@UiO‐66 material was clearly confirmed by XRPD (Figure S1A). FT‐IR established the compositional similarity of UiO‐66 and HRP@UiO‐66 (Figure S1B) although provided little insight about protein content. Protein loading was estimated by TGA analysis (Figure S1C), where a similar profile to the observed for BSA@UiO‐66 resulted in a 10.7% loading (STable 1). The retention of porous structure in HRP@UiO‐66 was confirmed by N2 sorption analysis (Figure S1D), with only a slight reduction compared to reference UiO‐66. This intrinsic porosity enables the diffusion of molecules inside the scaffold and opens the possibility to evaluate enzymatic activity with encapsulated enzymes.

Peroxidase assays were carried out with both free HRP and HRP@UiO‐66 employing 3,3',5,5'‐tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (see methods for further details). As shown in Figure 3A, both HRP and HRP@UiO‐66 exhibit an atypical kinetic profile attributed to pronounced substrate inhibition [20], a feature previously reported for this enzyme [21]. At low substrate concentrations, the typical Michaelis–Menten kinetic profile is observed (Figure 3A, inset). Both free and encapsulated HRP present comparable Michaelis constants (Km) (Table S2), consistent with previously reported values for HRP [22], suggesting that encapsulation does not significatively affects substrate affinity. However, HRP@UiO‐66 exhibits a markedly lower maximum rate (Vmax) compared to the free enzyme (Table S2). The negligible peroxidase activity of UiO‐66 (Figure 3A,B) indicates a passive role of the solid matrix in HRP@UiO‐66, likely restricting the diffusion of reactants through the scaffold. This interpretation is further supported by the complete activity recovery of enzymatic activity after release of HRP from HRP@UiO‐66 due to MOF degradation upon exposure to chelating ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) (Figure 3B), confirming the preserved integrity of the encapsulated enzyme. Note that activities slightly above 100% are attributed to experimental variability and normalization effects, rather than true enzymatic enhancement.

The recyclability of the enzyme catalyst was assessed to evaluate its operational stability and potential for reuse in catalytic applications (Figure 3C). The composite exhibited a good activity retention across consecutive peroxidase cycles, retaining 92% of the initial activity after eight runs. Notably, the structural integrity of the HRP@UiO‐66 composite remained unaltered after repeated use, as demonstrated by XRPD (Figure S2A) and the retained presence of HRP, as assessed by TGA (Figure S2B). These findings highlight that the UiO‐66 framework provides effective spatial confinement and protection of HRP [23], enabling catalytic recyclability.

Finally, the stability of the enzyme in extreme conditions was evaluated. Exposure to high temperatures (100°C) and organic solvent (DMF) resulted in complete deactivation of free HRP (Figure 3D). In contrast, the encapsulated enzyme exhibited remarkable resilience, retaining 28% of its initial activity after heat treatment and 49% after DMF exposure (Figure 3E). These results are consistent with the expected behavior of an enzyme confined within a MOF scaffold, where the porous framework provides partial protection against denaturing or inactivating environments while simultaneously enables recyclable catalytic performance. Overall, this enhanced stability highlights the protective role of UiO‐66 encapsulation, reinforcing the potential of MOF‐based systems for enzyme stabilization and reuse under harsh operational conditions.

Understanding Protein@UiO‐66 Growth

Once the conditions for protein@UiO‐66 synthesis were established, we aimed to elucidate how this coprecipitation mechanism operates in a highly Lewis‐acidic chemical scenario. The formation of UiO‐66 and BSA@UiO‐66 was first investigated by monitoring the reaction progress through dynamic light scattering (DLS), providing key insight into the underlying growth mechanism. For UiO‐66, colloid formation commenced approximately 5 min after ligand addition, with particles of around 50 nm appearing abruptly (Figure 4A). This behavior suggests an initial stage in which the ligand reacts with zirconium oxo‐clusters to form pre‐nucleation intermediates. During this induction phase, the ligand concentration remains insufficient to trigger framework assembly until a critical threshold is reached, after which rapid nucleation occurs followed by gradual particle growth. The reaction kinetics, monitored through the continuous increase in scattered light intensity (Figure 4A), display a linear trend after 5 min, consistent with steady material formation concurrent with ligand addition.

In the case of BSA@UiO‐66 formation (Figure 4B), a similar induction period is observed, however, additional features appear in early stages, corresponding to ∼8 nm particles attributed to BSA molecules. After approximately 5 min, particle size increases sharply, though this transition is more transient than in the pure UiO‐66 synthesis, and is followed by a comparable growth phase. The overall formation kinetics of BSA@UiO‐66 thus mirror those of UiO‐66, indicating that the presence of the protein does not significantly alter the MOF formation mechanism. Furthermore, BSA incorporation was tracked by quantifying the concentration of non‐encapsulated BSA in the supernatant after removal of solids (Figure 4B). Interestingly, the linear decrease in free BSA concentration throughout the synthesis suggests that protein encapsulation proceeds concomitantly with MOF nucleation and growth, supporting a coupled assembly mechanism between the protein and the forming UiO‐66 framework.

The crystallinity of the formed product was monitored by XRPD at different times of the synthesis using TiO2 (anatase) as internal standard (Figure S3). Both UiO‐66 and BSA@UiO‐66 present diffraction peaks from the onset of solid formation, with intensities increasing linearly after 5 min, mirroring the trends observed by DLS. These observations suggest that the formation of UiO‐66 and BSA@UiO‐66 occurs simultaneously with ligand addition to the modulated metal precursor solution, directly yielding a crystalline material.

Further insight into the materials formation was provided by time‐resolved small‐angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS) (Figure S4). Data were collected by continuously circulating the reaction media through a 1.5 mm capillary and recording profiles every 30 s. Guinier fits of the low‐q region show initially unstable radii of gyration (Rg ) that fluctuate between ∼25 and 45 nm up to ∼5 min after injection for UiO‐66 and up to ∼12 min for BSA@UiO‐66 (Figure S5A). Beyond these induction windows, Rg increases smoothly and reaches plateaus of 33.5 and 32.7 nm for UiO‐66 and BSA@UiO‐66, respectively corresponding to particle diameters of ∼80 and ∼70 nm—under a sphere approximation R=53Rg—. The overall Rg trajectories are similar, but the rise for BSA@UiO‐66 is delayed.

Complementary information is obtained from the partial Porod invariant (Equation (2)) which tracks densification. For UiO‐66, Q(t) increases sigmoidally and stabilizes after ∼30 min, consistent with completion of nanoparticle formation (Figure S5B). For BSA@UiO‐66, the increase is slower and the plateau lower, reflecting delayed and somewhat reduced densification. Framewise Porod slopes in the 2.5–3.5 nm− 1 range do not yield the ideal value of −4 because of low concentration and low signal to noise ratio; nevertheless, the time evolution of 𝐼(𝑞)𝑞4 confirms progressive interface sharpening (Figure S6A). The 𝑅g and Porod‐invariant trajectories define a staged mechanism of UiO‐66 formation in the absence and presence of BSA in accordance with DLS and XRPD, entailing an initial induction phase, followed by nucleation, particle growth and densification before reaching a steady state, marking the formation of final nanoparticles after ligand addition is completed (see Supporting Information for details).

To verify that this trajectory is consistent with a nucleation–growth pathway, we next fitted the forward scattering 𝐼0(𝑡) with Avrami kinetics (Figure 4D, Equation (4)). Both systems gave similar exponents (𝑛 ≈ 3, Table S3), indicative of 3D growth with finite nucleation. In contrast, the apparent rate constant k was reduced by more than a factor of two in case of BSA addition, confirming that protein slows particle growth but does not alter its dimensionality or mechanistic pathway. To isolate protein contributions, we examined a band limited Porod invariant in the mid‐q range (0.40–1.0 nm− 1, Equation (3)). Subtracting the UiO‐66 baseline considering a lag alignment of ∼7 min, yielded an excess scattering signal, ΔQ, specific to BSA (Figure S6B). The excess fraction rises steeply, peaks around 12 min, and then decays. This trajectory is consistent with progressive consumption of free BSA, initially present as dispersed protein, then maximally contributing during nucleation, and finally incorporated into the forming UiO‐66‐composite particles, where its independent mid‐q signature fades. Furthermore, neither system develops a low‐q upturn during growth, ruling out aggregation as a contributor to the SAXS signal. Together, Guinier, Porod‐invariant, Avrami, and ΔQ analyses establish a coherent picture of UiO‐66 formation and the kinetic influence of BSA.

To further validate this interpretation and obtain size parameters directly from scattering, we fitted the SAXS profiles with physically consistent particle models (Figure 4E,F). For UiO‐66, a single polydisperse sphere adequately reproduces the low‐ and mid‐q evolution (mean χ2 ≈ 3–17.1, Table S4A), yielding radii that grow smoothly in time and converge to 43 ± 5 nm, in agreement with Guinier‐derived sizes (Figure S5A). For BSA@UiO‐66, the same single‐sphere model also provides an acceptable description of the data (mean χ2 ≈ 42.7–61.1, Table S4B). On the other hand, a hierarchical model [24] in which each large particle contains a population of small inclusions is evaluated. For UiO‐66, this model provides a good fitting when radius with sizes close to the MOF's pore size (1.2 nm) is employed, an assumption that lacks physical meaning. This same model for BSA@UiO‐66, provides a significantly better fit quality (mean χ2 ≈ 1.8–9, Table S4C) in the low/mid‐q window used here (high‐q range was intentionally not fitted to excluded Bragg features). As for empty UiO‐66, the overall particle radius converges to 40 ± 10 nm, whereas small sphere radii are fitted between 3.6 and 4.9 ± 0.3 and 0.4 nm, in good agreement with the expected radius for BSA (∼9 nm). Both fits reproduce the kinetic trajectory already evident from Guinier radii and Porod invariants: an initial induction phase, followed by particle growth with a ∼7 min delay for BSA@UiO‐66 relative to UiO‐66. Overall, SAXS data align well with the determined growth observed by DLS and XRPD.

This pathway for HV‐MOF composite formation contrasts with the biomineralization strategy generally established for divalent cation‐based MOFs, particularly ZIFs, which is triggered by proteins and proceeds through amorphous intermediates to yield discontinuous crystalline domains.

Direct Visualization of Spatial Distribution of Protein in Fn@UiO‐66

Insight into the protein distribution within protein@UiO‐66 particles and its evolution during the synthesis was achieved using high resolution TEM (HR‐TEM). For this purpose, ferritin (Fn) was employed to obtain Fn@UiO‐66. Fn is a ubiquitous protein found in eukaryotes, bacteria and archaea and serves as a key component of cellular iron storage and transport [25]. It is a globular protein constructed by 24 subunits that assemble into a 12 nm oligomer containing an 8 nm inner cavity, where iron ions accumulate to form a ferrihydrite core [26]. This electron‐dense structure serves as a contrast agent in TEM. Additionally, its uniform size and stable structure facilitate its identification, making it an excellent model to evaluate protein distribution within MOF particles [27].

Crystalline Fn@UiO‐66 (Figure S8A) can be prepared in the same manner as BSA@UiO‐66 and HRP@UiO‐66, with an encapsulation efficiency close to 100%. The presence of Fn in the composite can be detected by FTIR (Figure S8B) and TGA (Figure S8C) with a protein loading of ca. 21% (Table S5). Initial morphology characterization of the particles was performed by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) imaging which reveals sphere‐like particles for both UiO‐66 and Fn@UiO‐66 (Figure 5A,B). Transmission microscopy provides detailed insight into the materials, as it provides information over the structure of the sample. As observed, UiO‐66 forms spherical particles with a uniform aspect and smooth borders (Figure 5C). In contrast, Fn@UiO‐66 preserves the spherical shape, but particles appear more polydisperse in sizes (Figure 5D), with the Fn cores clearly visible. Whereas a magnified view of UiO‐66 reference material shows a uniform internal structure with the absence of mesopores (Figure 5E), the iron oxide cores of Fn are clearly observed as dark spheres of ∼ 8 nm inside the Fn@UiO‐66 particles (Figure 5F). Moreover, mesopores of around 12 nm are present in some particles, which may be attributed either to partially enclosed Fn that is weakly bonded to the MOF scaffold's surface (and lost during washes), or to apoferritin present in the stock protein. In both UiO‐66 and Fn@UiO‐66 nanoparticles, lattice fringes are observed across the particles (Figure 5F, respectively), indicating that the nanoparticles are entirely constituted of crystalline MOF domains. Analysis of fast fourier transform (FFT) patterns of regions with lattice fringes enables identification of interplane distances of the material. Measured patterns correspond to interplane distances of UiO‐66 planes with Miller indexes (111) and (002), with d‐spacing of 1.2 and 1.0 nm, respectively (insets in Figure 5E,F).

Thanks to the enhanced contrast of Fn cores in TEM, they can be easily identified and analyzed (Figure 6A), therefore providing a means to evaluate the spatial distribution of protein within the MOF particles. The uniform size distribution (polydispersity index 0.03) of the darker circles with a mean measured size of 6.8 ± 1.2 nm (Figure 6B) support their assignment to Fn cores. Incorporation of Fn during the synthesis led to smaller MOF particles, with a mean size of 34.6 ± 9.2 nm (Figure 6C), compared to the 44.6 nm of the reference UiO‐66. Areas such as those highlighted in the inset of Figure 6A, where Fn@UiO‐66 particles are homogeneously deposited forming a dense, closely packed layer covering the surface, were employed to analyse the distribution of Fn in the composite. For this purpose, the Nearest Neighbour Distance (NND) method [28] was used to assess both spatial distribution and potential clustering of Fn particles. The values obtained show no significant deviation from a theoretical random particle distribution, with an average NND of 11.0 ± 0.8 nm across multiple images. This non‐clustered distribution of Fn within Fn@UiO‐66 particles suggests that the protein molecules are uniformly incorporated into the composite without forming preferential aggregation domains. These results are consistent with the BSA encapsulation profile observed in Figure 4B, where protein incorporation occurs steadily as MOF formation proceeds.

Another parameter that can be extracted from TEM images is the estimated number of Fn per Fn@UiO‐66 particle. Images in which particles form a uniform coverage of the surface and can be individually distinguished were analysed to determine the distribution of Fn per particle (Figure 5D), yielding a mean value of 2.3 ± 1.8 Fn/particle. The number of Fn per particle shows a relatively broad distribution, including some particles without any Fn.

Additional insight to support the location of the Fn within the MOF particles can be obtained by N2 sorption analysis if the internal cavity of apoferritin (ApoFn) is accessed. To this aim, iron from the Fn core can be removed from Fn@UiO‐66 by incubation in acetohydroxamic acid/urea solution [29], yielding a composite containing ApoFn referred to as ApoFn@UiO‐66. This transformation from Fn@UiO‐66 to ApoFn@UiO‐66 is accompanied by a clear color change (Figure S9A) without compromising the MOF scaffold (Figure S9B). ApoFn formation within the MOF is confirmed by FTIR and TGA (Figure S9C,D, respectively). N2 isotherms of ApoFn@UiO‐66 (Figure 7A) shows a new feature at ∼0.8 p/po as compared to Fn@UiO‐66, and analysis of the pore size distribution reveals a newly formed pore of ∼9.5 nm (Figure 7B), close to the size of the reported ApoFn cavity size [30]. This pore is also present in smaller amounts in the starting material Fn@UiO‐66, which may be attributed to a certain percentage of ApoFn in the protein stock. The newly formed pore in ApoFn@UiO‐66 represents 4.8% of the total pore volume of the material, as calculated from pore size distribution. This value compares to the observed from Fn@UiO‐66 TEM characterization. The expected pore volume percentage attributed to the Fn core is calculated by using the measured 2.3 Fn per particle and an average particle size of 34.6 nm, considering each ApoFn core as an empty sphere with its volume fully accessible. The theoretical pore volume of UiO‐66 (0.44 cm3/g) and the calculated density of bulk crystal (1.22 g/cm3) can be used to determine the accessible volume fraction for UiO‐66, calculated as 0.54. Employing Equation (5), these values give an expected 6.1% pore volume corresponding to the Fn core. This theoretical value is in close agreement with the experimental value obtained from the N2 sorption measurement, although a more realistic estimate would be slightly lower if the structural defects typically present in UiO‐66 are considered, as such defects increase the pore volume [31] and consequently the fraction accessible for N2. Even assuming an ideal UiO‐66 framework, the close agreement between theoretical and experimental values supports the estimation of the Fn per particle number obtained from TEM images. It is noted that a more accurate and detailed description of the distribution of proteins per particle could be obtained with techniques capable of 3D visualization of composite particles.

Visualization of the Formation of Fn@UiO‐66 With cryoTEM

Insight on the formation mechanism of Fn@UiO‐66 and the evolution of Fn distribution in the composite were also obtained from imaging with cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryoTEM). Imaging with cryoTEM provides snapshots of transient species that occur during the crystallization process, preserving the native state. For this purpose, samples of the synthesis at 1, 5, and 15 min were employed and prepared by vitrifying the samples in liquid propane. Within the first minute of synthesis, free Fn are observed in solution, within solvated regions (Figure 8A,B). As previously suggested from UiO‐66 and BSA@UiO‐66 characterizations with DLS and SAXS, soluble molecular species are formed at initial stages of synthesis. Size measurements of these particles support their attribution to Fn cores (Figure S10A). Hollow circles are also observed and are attributed to ApoFn present in the protein stock. At 5 min, early crystallization is observed, and colloid particles are captured (Figure 8C,D) showing both Fn@UiO‐66 particles and free Fn, indicating that only a fraction of Fn is encapsulated at this stage. The shape of Fn@UiO‐66 particles appears as rough spheres, with lattice fringes visible, which can be attributed to the UiO‐66 phase in the material (Figure 8D with inset). This indicates that crystalline Fn@UiO‐66 composites form from the beginning of synthesis without the intermediate formation of amorphous material and later crystallization, as observed in other protein@MOF composites [32]. After 15 min of reaction, approximately halfway to synthesis completion, Fn@UiO‐66 particles are more abundant and exhibit a more defined spherical shape (Figure 8E,F). Free Fn are still observed but are less pronounced than at earlier time points, consistent with their progressive incorporation into the forming composite. Lattice fringes (Figure 8F inset) with spacings corresponding to the (111) planes of UiO‐66 are observed across the entire surface without discontinuities. The absence of multiple crystalline domains in the particles suggest a continuous growth of the UiO‐66 phase on the previously formed nuclei. Furthermore, it indicates that Fn incorporation does not disrupt framework growth, resulting in Fn@UiO‐66 particles that form as single‐crystalline entities with the biomolecule enclosed within the structure.

Particle size was also determined from images at different synthesis times. Initially, only free Fn with a core size of 7.2 ± 0.8 were observed, and Fn@UiO‐66 formation was not triggered until 5 min after synthesis start. Subsequently, particle size increased continuously, from 27.9 ± 7.6 nm at 5 min (Figure S10B) to 33.4 ± 9.3 nm at 15 min (Figure S10C) and 34.6 ± 9.2 nm at the end of synthesis (30 min), as summarized in Figure 8G. This particle size evolution agrees with trends observed in DLS and SAXS.

The distribution of Fn in the particles was evaluated using nearest neighbor distance (NND) measurements providing quantitative insight into the uniformity of protein localization. After 1 min, only free Fn are observed with and NND consistent with a random distribution, as expected for protein in solution. Measurement of entire image fields after 5 min show a moderate reduction of NND due to the coexistence of free Fn and immobilized Fn, while a clustered distribution is observed only at 15 min and 30 min (Figure S11), due to minor aggregation of Fn in Fn@UiO‐66 particles. Analyses of images considering free Fn and Fn in composite as two different populations reveal that the measured NND of free Fn increases from 1 to 15 min, as expected due to the incorporation of protein into the composite and its removal from the reaction media (Figure 8H). In contrast, the NND of Fn within Fn@UiO‐66 particles remains almost constant at 5, 15 and 30 min (Figure 8H), indicating that the protein is incorporated uniformly within the particles throughout the synthesis.

Discussion

Knowledge of the formation mechanism of protein@MOF composites is crucial to understand their structural properties and optimize functionality. However, previous works have been mainly restricted to composites based on ZIFs, whose formation is strongly influenced by the presence of protein and its surface charge [11]. In this case, the pathway of composites formation involves an initial amorphous phase that later crystallizes into protein@ZIF‐8 [32, 33]. This process leads to the formation of protein@ZIF‐8 particles composed of discontinuous crystalline domains [34] and protein aggregates [27, 35]. Our hypothesis is that under different electrostatic chemical scenario dominated by strong Lewis acid cations that constitute the MOF composite, this formation mechanism may be different.

To this aim, we have established a direct, biocompatible protocol that enables protein encapsulation within the archetypal high‐valence UiO‐66. The intrinsic chemistry of UiO‐66 components—highly reactive Zr oxo clusters and carboxylate ligands capable of promoting strong coordination—dictates rapid nucleation and directs framework formation. To control the process and allow proper formation of the UiO‐66 phase, rate‐controlled ligand addition is employed, making the addition rate a key determinant in particle growth and crystallization.

Drawing on comprehensive analyses and insights gained from multiple advanced and complementary characterization techniques, we propose a distinct formation mechanism for UiO‐66. During the early stages of synthesis, the reaction medium contains a mixture of precursors existing as prenucleation clusters, with no detectable solid phase formation. This stage resembles the nucleation and growth behavior observed in zeolite synthesis, providing a valuable point of comparison. Upon continued ligand addition, these prenucleation clusters reach a critical supersaturation threshold, triggering UiO‐66 nucleation and particle formation. Subsequent material development proceeds through particle growth and progressive crystallization on pre‐existing nuclei, ultimately yielding nanoparticles composed of a single crystalline domain. As illustrated in Figure 9, the proposed formation mechanism follows the classical LaMer model [36], characterized by homogenous nucleation and growth.

Schematic representation of the proposed UiO‐66 and Fn@UiO‐66 formation steps. After precursor mixing, nucleation and growth stages are distinguished.

The evidence collected in this work indicates that protein@UiO‐66 formation essentially follows the same stages as pristine UiO‐66 (Figure 9). The presence of protein in the reaction medium slightly prolongs the initial pre‐nucleation phase, likely due to weak interactions that stabilize the highly reactive precursor species. Despite this initial delay, the subsequent nucleation and growth of protein@UiO‐66 proceed along trajectories comparable to those of UiO‐66 synthesized in the absence of protein. Moreover, protein incorporation into the MOF scaffold occurs linearly with particle growth, directly following precursor addition, indicating a coupled mechanism between framework assembly and protein encapsulation. Encapsulation occurs through the adhesion of protein molecules onto the surfaces of pre‐formed UiO‐66 particles, followed by framework overgrowth, which incorporates the protein within the lattice without disrupting the underlying crystal structure. The proteins appear to be incorporated as discrete entities randomly distributed within the solid matrix, with no evidence of aggregation. Notably, the cavities formed to accommodate the proteins do not give rise to separate crystalline domains, resulting in single‐crystalline protein@UiO‐66 particles.

In summary, the formation mechanism of protein@UiO‐66 closely mirrors that of pristine UiO‐66, with proteins serving primarily as passive stabilizers of prenucleation species rather than active modulators of crystallization. The intrinsic chemical reactivity and coordination preferences of the MOF building blocks—Zr oxo clusters and carboxylate ligands—dictate nucleation and growth, while simultaneously controlling the spatial distribution of proteins, ensuring their uniform, discrete incorporation within the single‐crystalline framework. Encapsulation proceeds in a stepwise manner, highlighting the direct coupling between UiO‐66 formation dynamics and protein localization. These findings underscore that precise control over MOF chemistry and kinetics is essential for the rational design of hybrid biomolecule@MOF materials with predictable and tunable spatial organization.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduce a direct and fully biocompatible route to crystallize high‐valent UiO‐66, overcoming the kinetic constraints typically imposed by the strong Lewis acidity of Zr(IV). A key outcome of this strategy is the efficient one‐step synthesis of protein@UiO‐66 nanoparticles, demonstrating that protein encapsulation can proceed without compromising crystallinity, yield and biomolecule integrity.

By combining DLS, XRPD, in situ SAXS and advanced TEM characterization, we map the fully crystallization pathway of UiO‐66 in both the absence and presence of protein. In all cases, the process begins with an induction period, followed by nucleation, growth, and subsequent particle densification. Final nanoparticles emerge once ligand addition is complete, marking the transition to a steady‐state regime. The presence of BSA influences these early stages but does not inhibit framework formation, highlighting how proteins engage with highly Lewis‐acidic systems under gentle conditions. These results are supported by direct visualization of biocomposite formation by means of HR‐TEM and imaging with cryogenic electron microscopy (cryoTEM).

Together, these findings establish a scalable route to protein@UiO‐66 materials and provide insight into how biomolecules interact with high‐valence MOF precursors during assembly. This advances both the synthesis of robust biocomposites for their practical use and the broader understanding of protein–MOF co‐formation in strongly coordinating chemical media.

Experimental Section and Methods

Materials

All reagents were used as received without further purification. Zirconium propoxide 70% in 1‐propanol, glacial acetic acid (HAc), benzene dicarboxylic acid 99% (BDC), triethylamine 99.5% (TEA), bovine serum albumin (BSA), ferritin (Fn) from equine spleen in saline solution (70 mg/mL), acetohydroxamic acid 98% and urea 99% were purchased from Sigma–Aldrich. Deionized water (18.2 MΩ·cm) was obtained from a Mili‐Q IQ 7005 purification system.

Instrumentation

XRPD powder diffraction patterns were measured with Bruker D8 Discover. FT‐IR spectra were measured with a spectrometer Bruker Alpha II equipped with an attenuated total reflectance (ATR) accessory. TGA profiles were measured with TA Instruments Waters Discovery TGA 550 employing high resolution mode (10°C/min) with nitrogen as purge gas. TEM images were taken with JEOL JEM 1010 equipped with an AMT camera (8 Mpx). SAXS measurements on capillary were performed at the BL11 NCD‐SWEET beamline at ALBA Synchrotron Radiation Facility (Spain). The scattering patterns were recorded using a Rayonix LX255‐HS area detector. SEM images were obtained using an FEI Magellan 400 XHR microscope. Samples were drop‐cast onto aluminum stubs with conductive carbon tape and sputter‐coated with ∼4 nm of iridium (Quorum Q150T) to minimize charging effects. HR‐TEM and ryoEM were imaged on a JEOL 2100F TEM equipped with a Schottky‐type field emission gun operating at 200 kV. For HR‐TEM, samples were prepared by drop‐casting a diluted sample solution followed by air drying. For cryoEM imaging, lacey carbon grids (Electron Microscopy Sciences) were glow‐discharged for 75 s to enhance hydrophilicity before use. Samples (3 µL) were applied under ambient humidity, blotted for 6 s, and vitrified in liquid propane using an Automatic Plunge Freezer ME GP2 (Leica Microsystems). Grids were loaded into a Gatan cryo‐TEM holder and images were acquired using Serial EM software and a Gatan OneView CMOS camera at 4k × 4k resolution. Nitrogen sorption isotherms were measured with Micromeritics Tristar II Plus. Samples were activated previously with VacPrep 061 Sample Degas System at 100°C for 1 h under vacuum. Sample centrifugation was carried out employing a SL Plus Series centrifuge (Thermo Scientific). Photographs were taken with a smartphone camera.

Synthesis of Materials

Synthesis of the Zirconium Oxocluster [Zr6O4(OH)4(Ac)12]2·(HAc)6

5 mL of zirconium propoxide (Zr(OPr)4, 11.70 mmol) 70% in 1‐propanol were added to 3.75 mL of glacial acetic acid (HAc, 65.25 mmol) in a closed container. The reaction was left at room temperature without stirring for 24 h. White crystalline powder is formed and can be collected by solvent evaporation under vacuum. The solid can be employed for MOF synthesis without further purification. The yield was quantitative (based on Zr).

Mild Synthesis of UiO‐66

In a typical synthesis, 16.6 mg of the ligand benzene dicarboxylic acid (H2BDC, 0.10 mmol) and 28 µL of triethylamine (TEA, 0.20 mmol) were dissolved in 10 mL of water to form the ligand BDC solution that was loaded in a syringe. In parallel, a 10 mL solution with 200 µL of HAc and 70 µL of TEA (2% HAc, 50 mm TEA) was prepared, and 26 mg (0.0167 mmol) of the acetate zirconium oxo cluster were dissolved, forming the HAc:TEA:Zr solution. This solution was put in a vial under stirring (1000 rpm) and at room temperature. The ligand BDC aqueous solution was added in a controlled rate at 20 mL/h, to the metallic aqueous HAc:TEA:Zr solution. A turbidity in the vial immediately forms and gradually increases, indicating that the UiO‐66 MOF is formed. After complete addition of the ligand BDC solution, the product was collected by centrifugation and washed with water three times. It could be stored in suspension or dried on air. Yield around 90%.

Mild Synthesis of BSA@UiO‐66, HRP@UiO‐66 and Fn@UiO‐66

Synthesis of protein@UiO‐66 composite proceeds similarly to the synthesis of UiO‐66, with the addition of 5 mg of bovine serum albumin (BSA), Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) or ferritin (Fn) from a stock concentrate (respectively 100 and 70 mg/mL) to the HAc:TEA:Zr solution. After complete addition of the ligand BDC solution, the composite was collected by centrifugation and washed with water three times. It could be stored in suspension or dried on air. Yield around 90%.

Mild Synthesis of ApoFn@UiO‐66

10 mL of pre‐synthesized Fn@UiO‐66 in suspension (2 mg/mL, 20 mg) were mixed with 10 mL of a solution of 200 mm acetohydroxamic acid and 200 mm urea (pH 6). The resulting mixture was incubated overnight at room temperature with orbital shaking. Then, solid was collected by centrifugation and washed with water. The treatment with Acetohydroxamic acid:urea was repeated to the solid, washed again and finally left drying on air.

Methods

Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP) Activity Measurement

HRP activity assay relies on the catalytic oxidation of 3,3′,5,5′‐Tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) with H2O2. The oxidation of TMBred to TMBox can be measured spectrophotometrically. In a microplate well, 10 µL of sample were added, followed by 250 µL of reagent solution: 50 mm sodium acetate pH 5.0, different concentrations of H2O2 and 535 µm of TMBred (H2O2 concentrations assayed were 98.0, 49.0, 24.5, 9.8, 4.9, 2.45, 0.98,0.49, 0.245, 0.098, 0.049 and 0.0245 mm). Enzyme concentration was measured in advance to prepare sample with the same concentration (5 µg/mL). The formation of TMBred product was monitored at 652 nm in 10 s intervals for 5 min. The initial activity slope was employed as measure of activity.

Comparative Peroxidase Assays

The activity of free HRP and encapsulated HRP was compared following same procedure employing a fixed concentration of H2O2 (250 µm).

To assess the effect of the framework on the enzyme activity, both free HRP and HRP@UiO‐66 were dissolved in EDTA 50 mm pH 8 to allow MOF dissolution and protein release. Enzyme concentration was measured in advance to prepare sample with the same concentration (5 µg/mL). Relative activity is referred to the activity of free HRP.

Similarly, both free HRP and HRP@UiO‐66 (500 µg/mL HRP in both cases) were suspended in DMF or boiling water for 1 h. After that time, treated samples were diluted to 5 µg/mL and employed as sample for activity assessment. Activity of the treated samples were compared to the activity of the respective untreated material.

Repeated activity assays were performed in 1.5 mL centrifugal tube with 1 mg of HRP@UiO‐66 material and 1 mL of reagent solution (50 mm sodium acetate pH 5.0, 50 µm H2O2, 535 µm of TMBred). After 1 min of activity, the solid was collected by centrifugation at 13 000 rpm 1 min. Supernatant was saved for later quantification, and the solid was employed as collected for further activity assays. In this case, activity was quantified as absorbance at 652 nm. After completion of the experiments, the solid was collected by centrifugation, washed twice with ddH2O and dried on air for further characterizations.
