HK2 promotes angiogenesis through H3K18 lactylation in peripheral arterial disease
Authors: Jing Chen, Yue Wang, Xuan Wang, Jin Li, Yu Liu, Jipeng Zhou, Chuanchang Li, Wanzhou Wu, Yongping Bai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripheral arterial disease (PAD) is characterized by impaired angiogenesis, yet the molecular mechanisms linking metabolic dysregulation to epigenetic reprogramming in endothelial cells (ECs) remain poorly understood. Here, we identify hexokinase 2 (HK2) as a critical regulator of angiogenesis through histone lactylation in PAD. In clinical specimens and murine hindlimb ischemia (HLI) models, HK2 expression was significantly downregulated. Functional assays in hypoxia-serum starved (HSS) human umbilical vein ECs (HUVECs) demonstrated that HK2 overexpression rescued angiogenesis by enhancing proliferation, migration, tube formation, and pro-angiogenic protein expression (VEGFA), while HK2 knockdown suppressed these phenotypes. Mechanistically, HK2 deficiency selectively reduced histone H3 lysine 18 lactylation (H3K18la) among eight tested histone lactylation sites, and HK2 restoration under HSS restored H3K18la level. Exogenous lactate reversed angiogenic defects in HK2-knockdown HUVECs by elevating H3K18la, which directly activated VEGFA transcription, as shown by Chromatin Immunoprecipitation coupled with quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction (ChIP-qPCR). Furthermore, Sirtuin 2 (SIRT2), as a de-lactylase, inhibition in HK2-knockdown HUVECs restored H3K18la, angiogenesis, and pro-angiogenic protein expression. In HLI mice, SIRT2 inhibitor treatment improved blood flow recovery, increased EC density, and upregulated H3K18la. Our findings establish HK2 as a metabolic-epigenetic nexus driving angiogenesis via lactate-dependent H3K18la modification and propose SIRT2 inhibition as a novel therapeutic strategy to bypass HK2 deficiency in PAD.

Introduction

More than 230 million people worldwide suffer from PAD1, which is characterized by stenosis or blockage of any portion of the aortoiliac segment from the pedal arteries2. Although more than half of individuals impacted are asymptomatic, it contributes to 3–4% of amputations and has a crude 5-year mortality rate of 82.4 fatalities per 1000 patient-years when adjusted for follow-up time3. To date, there is no effective drug treatment to inhibit arterial stenosis4, and existing medicines utilized in clinical trials for the treatment of PAD have not yet demonstrated satisfactory efficacy5. There is thus an urgent need to investigate the potential mechanisms underlying PAD and develop effective therapies.

Disease of the peripheral arteries, ischemia occurs when the lower limb tissues and muscles do not receive adequate oxygen and nutrients due to a lack of blood supply to the arteries6. Therapeutic angiogenesis emerged as a preferred strategy to treat symptomatic PAD, which can promote the development of new arterial vessels and improve perfusion of ischemic tissue7. Angiogenesis is defined as the growth and proliferation of blood vessels from existing vascular structures8. A key process of angiogenesis is the sprouting of ECs from pre-existing capillaries, regulated by angiogenic factors such as vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) produced in ischemic tissues9. Although clinical trials for angiogenesis therapy in PAD using approaches involving genes, cells, proteins, and small molecule drugs have been conducted for over two decades, no treatment strategy has demonstrated successful clinical benefits and effectiveness10–12. Therefore, finding new “therapeutic angiogenesis” drug targets is important for the treatment of PAD.

Endothelial angiogenesis is crucial for restoring blood flow and repairing tissues that have been damaged due to a lack of blood supply. ECs rely on glycolysis to provide more than 85% of their energy13–15, HK2 catalyzes the rate-limiting first stage of glycolysis, phosphorylating glucose to glucose 6-phosphate (G6P)16. Increasing evidence suggests that changes in HK2 activity are often associated with the progression of various pathological conditions, including osteoarthritis17, neuropathic pain18, and liver fibrosis19. Dysregulated HK2 expression disrupts lactate homeostasis and correlates with pathological angiogenesis in cancer20,21. Notably, lactate—a glycolysis byproduct—has recently been identified as a key epigenetic modulator via histone lysine lactylation (Kla)22, influencing gene transcription in processes ranging from tumor immune evasion to calcification23,24. However, whether HK2-mediated lactate flux regulates angiogenesis through epigenetic reprogramming in PAD remains unexplored.

Here, we revealed that restoring the impaired function of HK2, which is downregulated in PAD, promotes angiogenesis by augmenting H3K18la. HK2-derived lactate suppresses the de-lactylase SIRT2, stabilizing H3K18la to activate pro-angiogenic gene transcription. Our work uncovered the HK2/lactate/SIRT2/H3K18la axis as a metabolic-epigenetic switch driving angiogenesis, offering novel therapeutic targets for PAD.

Results

HK2 was downregulated in PAD

Recent advances in gene sequencing technologies and bioinformatics have empowered systematic identification of disease-associated genes with therapeutic potential. As outlined in Fig. 1a (Fig. S1), our analytical workflow began with the acquisition of the GSE120642 dataset from the GEO database. Initial quality control through sample cluster analysis led to the exclusion of outlier samples CLI.1 and CLI.13 in the critical limb ischemia (CLI) group (Fig. S2a, b). Principal component analysis (PCA) revealed that the CLI skeletal muscle sample cluster was markedly different from health (HA) and intermittent claudication (IC) muscle samples, whereas HA and IC muscle samples had similar clusters (Fig. S2c). Using weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we established a hierarchical clustering tree with a soft threshold power of 4 (Fig. S2d, e), ultimately identifying 12 distinct gene co-expression modules in PAD (Fig. S2f, g). A consensus approach through Venn diagram analysis identified HK2 as the most robust signature gene across both analytical comparisons (Figs. S3f, S4f and Fig. 1b). Expression profiling in the GSE120624 dataset revealed significantly reduced HK2 levels in CLI compared to IC (Fig. 1c), suggesting a potential correlation between HK2 downregulation and disease severity. Diagnostic evaluation using ROC analysis demonstrated HK2’s high discriminatory capacity (AUC = 0.855) for PAD detection (Fig. 1d). This diagnostic validity was further corroborated in the independent GSE113873 dataset, where HK2 expression was similarly decreased in PAD patients with comparable diagnostic efficacy (AUC = 0.744) (Fig. S5a, b). To validate these findings, we employed a murine hindlimb ischemia (HLI) model, a well-established preclinical system for studying PAD. Laser Doppler imaging confirmed significant blood flow reduction in HLI mice compared to sham controls (Fig. 1e). The expression of HK2 in the gastrocnemius muscle of HLI mice was reduced at both mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 1f–h). These multi-modal findings systematically demonstrate that HK2 was downregulated in PAD.

HK2-regulated ECs angiogenesis

To investigate the regulatory mechanisms of HK2 suppression in PAD, we performed gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) using the GSE120624 dataset. This revealed significant enrichment of endothelial cell sprouting angiogenesis pathways (NES = 1.41, FDR q < 0.1; Fig. 2a), suggesting compromised vascular remodeling in PAD. Immunofluorescence quantification confirmed reduced HK2 expression in ECs of gastrocnemius muscle vessels from HLI mice compared to controls (Fig. 2b, c). Using HSS as an established in vitro PAD model25,26, we found that HK2 expression was downregulated in HUVECs at both the mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 2d and Fig. S6b, respectively). Given HK2’s role in catalyzing the first step of glycolysis (Fig. S6a), we assessed glycolytic pathway integrity. HSS-treated HUVECs exhibited downregulation of key glycolytic regulators at both the mRNA and protein levels (PFKFB3, PKM2, and LDHA; Fig. S6b–d) and impaired angiogenic functions: diminished EdU-positive cell proliferation (Fig. S6e–f), reduced migration (Fig. S6g, h), and compromised tube formation (Fig. S6i, j). Mechanistically, HSS suppressed the VEGFA/VEGFR2 signaling axis, as evidenced by reduced levels of VEGFA, phosphorylated VEGFR2, and downstream effectors (p-FAK, p-PI3K, and p-ERK1/2; Fig. S6k, l). These findings collectively demonstrate HSS-induced suppression of glycolysis and angiogenesis in ECs. Lentivirus-mediated HK2 overexpression in HSS-HUVECs (Fig. 2e) reversed HSS-induced dysfunction, restoring glycolytic enzyme expression (Fig. S7), increasing EdU-positive cells (Fig. 2f, g), enhancing migration (Fig. 2h, i), improving tube formation (Fig. 2j), and enhancing the phosphorylation/activation of key angiogenesis-associated signaling proteins (Fig. 2k, l). CRISPR-Cas9-mediated HK2 knockdown in normoxia HUVECs (Fig. 3a) recapitulated the HSS phenotype: reduced glycolytic regulators (Fig. S8), decreased proliferation (Fig. 3b), impaired migration (Fig. 3c, d), diminished tube formation (Fig. 3e), and downregulated the phosphorylation/activation of key angiogenesis-associated signaling proteins (Fig. 3f, g). HK2-regulated H3K18 lactylation in ECs

Glycolysis serves as the primary source of lactate production in ECs. To establish the functional link between HK2-mediated glycolysis and lactate generation, we first quantified lactate levels in HK2-knockdown HUVECs. Both intracellular lactate content and extracellular lactate secretion were significantly reduced in HK2-knockdown cells compared to controls (Fig. 4a and Fig. S9a). We next investigated whether this lactate reduction impacts global histone lactylation. Immunoblotting revealed a marked decrease in pan-histone lactylation levels in HK2-knockdown HUVECs, while histone acetylation remained unchanged (Fig. 4b). To dissect site-specific lactylation dynamics, we systematically analyzed eight established lactylation sites: H3K9la, H3K14la, H3K18la, H3K27la, H4K5la, H4K8la, H4K12la, and H4K16la. Strikingly, HK2 knockdown significantly reduced lactylation at H3K18, H3K27, and H4K16, with the most pronounced decrease observed at H3K18 (P < 0.01; Fig. 4c, d and Fig. S9b). To validate these findings under pathological stress, we overexpressed HK2 in HSS-treated HUVECs. HK2 overexpression restored intracellular and extracellular lactate levels (Fig. 4e and Fig. S9c) and rescued pan-histone lactylation, which were suppressed under HSS, without altering histone acetylation (Fig. 4f). Importantly, while all eight lactylation sites exhibited reduced modification under HSS (Fig. S9d), HK2 overexpression in HSS-treated HUVECs selectively restored lactylation at H3K18 and H4K16. Notably, H3K18la returned to baseline levels (Fig. 4g, h). The predominant reduction of H3K18la in HK2-knockdown ECs, coupled with its full restoration under rescue conditions, identifies H3K18la as the critical lactylation site regulated by HK2. Genome-wide CUT& Tag analysis reveals HK2 regulates angiogenic genes via H3K18 lactylation

To identify biological processes and candidate genes regulated by HK2 through H3K18 lactylation, we performed genome-wide cleavage under targets and tagmentation (CUT&Tag) analysis in control and HK2-knockdown (HK2KD) endothelial cells. The analysis revealed that HK2 knockdown led to increased chromatin accessibility at 368 genes and decreased accessibility at 132 genes (Fig. 5a–d). H3K18la peaks were significantly enriched in endothelial cells (Fig. 5e, f). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis indicated that upregulated genes were primarily associated with neovascularization processes, including cell proliferation, migration, adhesion, and angiogenesis (Fig. 5g). Given the enrichment of H3K18la at genes related to angiogenic pathways, we further examined the effect of H3K18la on key angiogenesis-related promoters. In HK2KD HUVECs, H3K18la peak levels were markedly reduced at the VEGFA promoter (Fig. 5h). A slight but detectable decrease was also observed at the promoters of FGF2, EGF, HGF, and EPO, whereas no significant change was found at the promoters of ANGPT1 or PDGFB (Fig. S10). Lactate increased H3K18 lactylation to promote angiogenesis

HK2 knockdown in ECs significantly reduced intracellular lactate production, which correlated with diminished H3K18 lactylation and impaired angiogenesis. Given the established role of lactate in promoting angiogenesis27,28, we tested whether exogenous lactate rescues these defects via histone lactylation. In HK2-knockdown HUVECs, supplementation with 5 mM lactate restored physiological lactate levels (Fig. S11a, b) and reversed angiogenic dysfunction, as evidenced by increased EdU⁺ proliferation (Fig. 6a), enhanced migration (Fig. 6b, c), improved tube formation (Fig. 6d), and enhanced the phosphorylation/activation of key angiogenesis-associated signaling proteins (VEGFA, p-VEGFR2, p-FAK, p-PI3K, and p-ERK; Fig. 6e, f), and elevated H3K18la levels (Fig. 6g). ChIP-qPCR using an H3K18la-specific antibody revealed increased lactylation enrichment at the VEGFA promoter in lactate-treated HK2-knockdown HUVECs (Fig. 6h), directly linking H3K18la to transcriptional activation. Similarly, in HSS-treated HUVECs, 10 mM lactate normalized intracellular lactate concentrations (Fig. S11c, d) and rescued angiogenic capacity by restoring proliferation, migration, tube formation and H3K18la levels (Fig. S12a–g). Inhibition of SIRT2 promoted angiogenesis and alleviated PAD

Lactylation, a recently discovered post-translational modification, has emerged as a critical mechanism through which lactate regulates cellular processes22. The establishment and removal of lysine lactylation typically require specific “writers” (e.g., P300, CREBBP, and KAT8) and “erasers” (e.g., HDAC1-3, and SIRT1-3)29. Our qPCR analysis revealed that among these regulators, SIRT2 expression was significantly upregulated in HK2KD HUVECs compared to controls (Fig. S13a). Notably, supplementation with 5 mM lactate in HK2KD HUVECs substantially reduced SIRT2 expression at both the mRNA and protein level (Fig. S13a, c). A similar pattern of SIRT2 elevation was observed in HSS-treated HUVECs (Fig. S13b, d). To investigate SIRT2’s functional role in PAD, we performed SIRT2 knockdown using siRNA in HK2KD HUVECs. This intervention effectively rescued angiogenesis deficits by increasing EdU-positive cell proliferation (Fig. 7b, c), enhancing cell migration (Fig. 7d, e), improving tube formation capacity (Fig. 7f, g) and restoring H3K18 lactylation levels (Fig. 7a). To validate these findings in vivo, we administered AGK2 (a commonly used selective SIRT2 inhibitor30–32) in an HLI model. Compared to vehicle-treated controls, AGK2-treated mice exhibited accelerated blood flow recovery at days 7, 14, and 21 post-ischemia (Fig. 7h, i), enhanced endothelial cell density (Fig. 7j, k) and elevated H3K18 lactylation levels in ischemic gastrocnemius muscle (Fig. 7l, m). Discussion

PAD is characterized by impaired angiogenesis in ischemic tissues, a process critically dependent on endothelial cell functionality and metabolic adaptation33. Our study uncovers a novel mechanism by which HK2, a key glycolytic enzyme, regulates angiogenesis through H3K18la in PAD. By integrating bioinformatics, in vitro functional assays, and in vivo models, we demonstrate that HK2 downregulation in PAD disrupts lactate-driven histone lactylation, thereby impairing vascular repair. These findings bridge metabolic dysregulation and epigenetic reprogramming in PAD pathogenesis, offering therapeutic insights for ischemic vascular diseases.

In this study, we proposed that integrated bioinformatics and machine learning approaches could identify critical biomarker genes associated with PAD. Through systematic analysis, HK2 was ultimately identified as a key candidate. Functional enrichment analysis (Fig. 4g) between the HA and CLI groups demonstrated significant enrichment of pathways related to the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and exhibited pronounced associations with glucose metabolism dysregulation, supported by clinical evidence showing abnormal glucose metabolism in 65.3% of PAD patients34. Complementary findings by Terence E. Ryan further revealed specific deficiencies in glucose metabolism-related Reactome pathways through RNA sequencing analysis of CLI skeletal muscle35. Supporting these clinical findings, experimental validation showed significant HK2 downregulation in the gastrocnemius muscle of HLI mouse models. Collectively, our multi-modal investigation establishes HK2 as a functionally relevant biomarker exhibiting consistent downregulation patterns across clinical cohorts and experimental models of PAD, with particular significance in advanced disease stages characterized by metabolic dysregulation.

Peripheral arterial ischemic disease arises from inadequate arterial blood supply, leading to hypoxia and nutrient deprivation in lower limb tissues, which critically impairs angiogenesis—a process essential for restoring perfusion via neovascularization8. Angiogenesis requires tightly coordinated endothelial cell activation, proliferation, and migration36, with VEGFA acting as a central regulator. VEGFA binding to VEGFR2 triggers downstream signaling cascades, including ERK1/2-mediated proliferation, FAK-driven migration, and PI3K-dependent survival37. While prior studies established that HK2 knockdown suppresses FGF2-induced EC sprouting38 and HK2 overexpression enhances angiogenesis via p38-MAPK-lactate signaling20, our work elucidates the context-dependent role of HK2 in vascular remodeling. Our study observed downregulation of glycolytic enzymes (HK2, PFKFB3, PKM2, LDHA) and impaired angiogenesis in HUVECs under HSS, contrasting with a report showing PFKFB3 upregulation under similar HSS conditions25. This discrepancy may arise from differences in culture media: the cited study used endothelial starvation medium supplemented with 1 mM pyruvate25, whereas our experiments employed standard endothelial basal medium. Pyruvate, a mitochondrial substrate, could compensate for glycolytic suppression by fueling the TCA cycle. Our findings highlight the critical influence of metabolic microenvironment on glycolytic enzyme regulation, particularly when modeling ischemic stress. Under HSS, HK2 overexpression robustly enhanced EC proliferation, migration, and tube formation, paralleled by upregulation of VEGFA and other pro-angiogenic factors. Conversely, HK2 knockdown under normoxia significantly attenuated these phenotypes, highlighting HK2’s dual regulatory function contingent on metabolic stress. These findings establish HK2 as a critical rheostat for EC-driven angiogenesis, bridging glycolytic metabolism to vascular adaptation in ischemic injury.

We observed that HK2 knockdown in endothelial cells resulted in significantly enlarged morphology along with proliferation inhibition. This phenotype resembles features of cellular senescence, and recent studies have confirmed that HK2 inhibition can directly induce senescence in multiple cell type39. The underlying mechanisms may involve energy stress, reactive oxygen species accumulation, and cell cycle arrest triggered by metabolic dysregulation. Notably, endothelial-specific knockout of transient receptor potential melastatin 7 (TRPM7)—a key regulator of endothelial glycolysis—has been shown to suppress HK2 and downstream glycolytic activity. That study further demonstrated that inhibiting endothelial glycolysis via HK2 downregulation reduces endothelial H3K18 lactylation, thereby promoting the expression of senescence-associated genes and accelerating endothelial aging. This process is characterized by endothelial dysfunction, increased SA‑β‑gal staining, higher γ‑H2A.X positivity, and elevated expression of senescence markers such as p16 and p2140. In our model, however, these hallmarks remain to be validated through assays for SA‑β‑gal activity, p21/p16 expression, cytoskeletal markers (e.g., F‑actin), or junctional proteins (e.g., VE‑cadherin).

Although HK2 is implicated in EC angiogenic regulation, its precise molecular mechanisms remain elusive. ECs rely on glycolysis for over 85% of their energy production, a process that generates lactate—a metabolite recently shown to modulate angiogenesis. Notably, histone lactylation, an emerging epigenetic modification, has garnered attention for its role in bridging metabolic states to transcriptional regulation29,41. A critical unanswered question is whether HK2 governs EC function indirectly by orchestrating lactate-dependent epigenetic remodeling. Our findings reveal that HK2 knockdown in ECs significantly reduces H3K18 lactylation levels, while exogenous lactate supplementation or HK2 overexpression under HSS restores this modification. This suggests that HK2 modulates glycolytic flux to regulate lactate production, thereby adjusting histone lactylation dynamics. We performed H3K18la-targeted CUT&Tag sequencing to directly compare the enrichment of H3K18la modification at the promoter regions of multiple classic angiogenic factor genes in control versus HK2KD endothelial cells. Notably, the sequencing analysis revealed that under HK2 knockdown conditions, besides VEGFA (Fig. 5h), the promoter regions of other angiogenic factors (such as FGF2, EGF, HGF, and EPO, Fig. S10) also exhibited varying degrees of decreased H3K18la modification, while ANGPT1 and PDGFB showed no significant change (Fig. S10). This indicates that HK2/lactate axis-driven H3K18 lactylation exerts selective regulation on its target genes, with VEGFA being its most prominent and crucial effector target. This regulatory specificity likely collectively determines the preferential inhibition pattern of the angiogenic program under metabolic stress. Further mechanistic insights emerged from ChIP-qPCR analysis: Lactate supplementation in HK2-knockdown HUVECs increased VEGFA promoter-associated H3K18la, correlating with elevated VEGFA expression. This implies that lactylation enhances chromatin accessibility at angiogenic gene loci, directly enabling transcriptional activation. These results position HK2 as a metabolic gatekeeper that fine-tunes histone lactylation by controlling lactate availability. This framework aligns with studies demonstrating lactate’s dual role as both a metabolic byproduct and an epigenetic substrate42. Collectively, our work establishes that HK2 reprograms angiogenic transcription through lactate-mediated histone lactylation, offering a mechanistic bridge between glycolytic metabolism and vascular repair.

In our study, we observed that lactate supplementation in HK2-knockdown endothelial cells suppressed SIRT2 expression, initially suggesting SIRT2 may function as a downstream effector of lactate. To directly test whether lactate regulates SIRT2 at the transcriptional level via histone lactylation, we performed H3K18la CUT&Tag sequencing and a SIRT2 promoter luciferase reporter assay. Our experimental data provide clear evidence: first, H3K18la enrichment at the SIRT2 gene promoter showed no significant change upon HK2 knockdown. Second, neither HK2 knockdown nor subsequent lactate restoration altered the transcriptional activity of the SIRT2 promoter (Fig. S14a, b). These results demonstrate that, in our model, lactate does not regulate SIRT2 expression through direct transcriptional mechanisms involving H3K18la at its promoter. Therefore, the suppression of SIRT2 expression by lactate in HK2-deficient endothelial cells likely occurs through transcription-independent pathways. This finding distinguishes our endothelial cell model from other reported cellular contexts and underscores the cell-type-specific nature of metabolic-epigenetic crosstalk. Our revised model firmly establishes that in endothelial cells under metabolic stress, lactate operates upstream of SIRT2, but its inhibitory effect is mediated through a post-transcriptional or post-translational regulatory layer, the precise nature of which warrants future investigation.

Our findings highlight SIRT2 inhibition bypassed HK2 deficiency, suggesting that stabilizing lactylation—rather than solely augmenting lactate production—may be sufficient to reactivate angiogenesis. This dual therapeutic strategy, which combines enhanced lactate availability with blockade of de-lactylation, holds promise for overcoming metabolic constraints in PAD. While gene therapy targeting HK2 upregulation faces translational hurdles, our findings identify SIRT2, a histone deacetylase, as a viable upstream target to restore lactylation homeostasis43. In HK2KD HUVECs, pharmacological SIRT2 inhibition rescued angiogenic defects by elevating H3K18la levels, indicating that SIRT2 hyperactivity drives lactylation loss in PAD. To our knowledge, this is the first demonstration that SIRT2 inhibitors (e.g., AGK2 is a commonly used selective SIRT2 inhibitor30–32 restore perfusion in hindlimb ischemia models, concomitant with increased endothelial cell density and H3K18la. These observations align with emerging roles of lactylation in other pathologies: in cancer, SIRT2 inhibition enhances METTL16 lactylation at K229, and combining AGK2 with elesclomol synergistically reduces tumor progression44. Our work repurposes SIRT2-targeted approaches for vascular repair, underscoring the conserved role of lactylation in stress adaptation. Mechanistically, SIRT2 inhibition may enable “epigenetic reprogramming” of angiogenic pathways, circumventing HK2-dependent metabolic limitations. This paradigm shift highlights the therapeutic potential of modulating lactylation dynamics, rather than solely focusing on lactate production, to ameliorate ischemic vascular disease.

Clinical implications and limitations

Notably, exogenous lactate supplementation bypasses lactate insufficiency caused by HK2 deficiency, restoring H3K18la and angiogenesis. A critical unanswered question is whether combining SIRT2 inhibitors with lactate could yield additive or synergistic benefits. Such a dual approach—simultaneously boosting lactylation substrates (lactate) and blocking their removal (via SIRT2 inhibition)—may maximize epigenetic reprogramming of pro-angiogenic genes. Future studies should test this hypothesis in preclinical PAD models and evaluate long-term safety, particularly given the systemic roles of SIRT2 in aging and immunity.

Methods

Data collection

The Gene Expression Omnibus database (GEO database) was used to download the gene expression profiles for the GSE120642 dataset. Ryan TE submitted GSE120642, a dataset that included the skeletal muscle gene expression patterns of 15 older people in good health, 20 patients with intermittent claudication (IC), and 16 patients with critical limb ischemia (CLI) requiring limb amputation. Download the GSE113873 dataset from the GEO database, which was used to study the effects of exercise on gene expression in PAD patients. The dataset is divided into four groups: Healthy_Baseline, PAD_exercise_Baseline, PAD_exercise_Final, and PAD_no exercise_Final. We selected baseline data as the study object.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA)

Horvath & Zhang created the weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) in 200545,46, and a R package is accessible on the official R website. The WGCNA algorithm was used to generate modules while filtering out power values. The independence and average connection degree of numerous modules with varied power values (ranging from 1 to 20) were evaluated using the gradient method. When the degree of independence reached 0.9, a suitable power value was established. To acquire suitable modules, the minimal size of a gene module was set to 200, and the threshold for merging comparable modules was set to 0.25; all other parameters were left at their default values, unless otherwise stated.

Identification of significant modules and hub genes

We used WGCNA to analyze the connections between modules and clinical progression features. This enables us to find modules that are highly connected. Gene significance (GS) for each expression profile was determined as the absolute value of the correlation between the profile’s expression and each trait, and module membership (MM) as the correlation between the profile’s expression and each module eigengene. GS and MM were established for each significant gene after the modules of interest were chosen.

Differentially expressed gene (DEG) analysis

The microarray dataset GSE120642 was retrieved from the GEO database. Raw data were preprocessed using the limma package (v3.54.0) in R (v4.1.2), including log2 transformation and normalization. Probes were annotated to gene symbols using the platform-specific annotation file. For genes mapped to multiple probes, the probe with the highest expression value was retained. Differential expression analysis between disease and control groups was performed using limma’s empirical Bayes-moderated t-test47. DEGs were identified with the following thresholds: absolute fold change (FC) >2, false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction), and average log2 expression >1 across samples.

Functional enrichment analysis

Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis was performed to identify biologically relevant pathways associated with differentially expressed genes (DEGs). Gene identifiers were first converted to KEGG-compatible ENTREZ IDs using the org.Hs.eg.db Bioconductor annotation package (e.g., org.Hs.eg.db for Homo sapiens). Enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler R package, with statistical significance defined by a hypergeometric test adjusted for multiple comparisons via the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (adjusted p value <0.05). Pathways meeting both significance (FDR <0.05) and minimum gene count thresholds (≥5 genes per pathway) were retained. All graphical outputs were refined for publication quality using ggplot2.

Identification of candidate hub genes

Machine learning is a technical method for achieving artificial intelligence (AI). It can automatically evaluate current data to derive rules or models, then use the models to predict unknown data48. Then, to screen hub genes, two machine learning algorithms—least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and random forest—were used. The glmnet package was used to implement the LASSO analysis with penalty settings for tenfold cross-validation49. Utilizing the “randomForest” R package, the random forest (RF) technique was used to filter hub genes. We developed a random forest tree model after calculating the error rate of 500 trees. Then, we determined the feature important scores of each potential hub gene candidate and eliminated the genes with importance values higher than zero. The intersection genes of these two machine learning algorithms were identified as the candidate hub genes.

Gene set enrichment analysis

The probable role of the signature gene was determined using the GSEA50. The ordered gene expression matrix based on the Pearson correlation between the signature gene and other genes was used in the GSEA, which was carried out using the “clusterProfiler” R package51, to study the biological activities of the signature gene. The standard for determining if enrichment was significant was a P < 0.05.

Curve analysis of receiver operating characteristics (ROC)

The sensitivity and specificity of the diagnostic prediction model were analyzed by the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and quantified by the area under the ROC curve (AUC). ROC curves were plotted using the “pROC” package in R software52, and the area under the curve (AUC) >0.7 was considered valuable.

Serial blood flow monitoring in hindlimb ischemia

Hindlimb perfusion was longitudinally assessed using a laser Doppler imaging (LDI) system (MoorFLPI-2, Moor Instruments) at four timepoints: preoperatively (baseline), immediately post-ligation, and on days 3, 7, 14, and 21 post-surgery. Blood flow was quantified within a standardized region of interest (ROI) encompassing the plantar foot surface. Perfusion data were analyzed using Moor Instruments’ proprietary software (v5.0) and expressed as the ratio of ischemic to contralateral limb flux (mean ± SEM). Three consecutive measurements per limb were averaged to reduce motion artifacts.

Scratch wound healing assay

Confluent HUVEC monolayers cultured in 12-well plates were subjected to a standardized scratch wound using a sterile 200 μL pipette tip. After washing with PBS to remove detached cells, the wounded monolayers were maintained in complete endothelial growth medium under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). Wound closure was monitored at 0, 6, and 12 h post-scratch using a Leica DMi8 inverted phase-contrast microscope equipped with a 10× objective and a calibrated environmental chamber. The migration rate was calculated using the formula:

Migration rate (%)=(initial wound area−final wound area)/initial wound area × 100. Quantitative analysis of migration was performed using ImageJ software (v1.53, NIH) with the “MRI wound healing tool” plugin. The data represent six independent experiments, each conducted with triplicate technical replicates.

Cell proliferation assay

HUVEC proliferation was assessed using the EdU Cell Proliferation Kit (#C0071S, Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s guidelines. Briefly, cells were seeded in 96-well plates at a density of 4 × 10³ cells/well in complete endothelial growth medium and allowed to adhere for 24 h under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂). After pretreatment per experimental design, 50% of the medium was replaced with EdU labeling solution (20 μM in serum-free medium) for a 2 h pulse. Cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (15 min), permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (20 min), and incubated with Click reaction cocktail containing Alexa Fluor 488-azide (1:1000) for 30 min in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (1 μg/mL, 5 min). Fluorescence images were acquired using a Leica DMi8 fluorescence microscope (20× objective), with five random fields captured per well. Proliferating cells (EdU⁺) and total nuclei (DAPI⁺) were quantified using ImageJ with the “Cell Counter” plugin. Proliferation rates were calculated as the percentage of EdU⁺ cells relative to total nuclei. The data represent six independent experiments, each including triplicate technical replicates.

Tube formation assay

HUVECs were pretreated for 24 h under specified experimental conditions. Prior to seeding, 10 μL of growth factor-reduced Matrigel (#40183ES, Yeasen) was pipetted into each well of a μ-Slide Angiogenesis plate (#81506, ibidi) and polymerized at 37 °C for 30 min. Cells (1 × 10⁴/well) were resuspended in 50 μL of conditioned medium (pre-equilibrated to experimental treatments) and seeded onto the Matrigel-coated wells. After 3 h of incubation under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO₂), tubular networks were imaged using a Leica DMi8 inverted phase-contrast microscope (10× objective). Three random fields per well were captured to ensure spatial representation. Quantification was performed using ImageJ with the “Angiogenesis Analyzer” plugin, measuring total branch length (μm) and tubular structure counts per field. Data were normalized to untreated controls and represent six independent experiments, each with triplicate technical replicates.

Hypoxia-serum starvation (HSS)

Hypoxia and serum starvation (HSS) were induced by culturing cells in serum-free endothelial basal medium (ECM, #1001; ScienCell) under hypoxic conditions (2% O₂) for 24 h using a tri-gas incubator (5% CO₂, balanced N₂). This dual stress model simultaneously deprived cells of both growth factors and oxygen to mimic pathophysiological ischemic conditions. The hypoxic environment was maintained through continuous gas monitoring and automated regulation to ensure precise oxygen tension control throughout the experimental duration. All functional assays (proliferation, migration, and tube formation) following HSS treatment were performed with the cells maintained under continuous HSS conditions for the entirety of the 24-h experimental period.

Lentivirus production and transduction

Lentiviral particles for HK2 knockdown (HK2KD) and overexpression (HK2OE) were generated in HEK293T cells (ATCC CRL-3216). Briefly, 3.8 × 10⁶ cells were seeded in 10-cm culture dishes with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and incubated for 20 h at 37 °C under 5% CO₂. Two hours prior to transfection, the medium was replaced with Opti-MEM (#31985070; Gibco). Lentiviral vectors—plentiCRISPRv2-sgHK2 (for knockdown) or plvx-HK2 (for overexpression)—were co-transfected with packaging plasmids psPAX2 (Addgene #12260) and pMD2.G (Addgene #12259) at a 4:3:1 mass ratio using Lipofectamine 3000 (#L3000015; Thermo Fisher). After 12 h, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh DMEM/10% FBS. Viral supernatants were harvested at 48, 72, and 96 h post-transfection, pooled, and centrifuged at 1500×g for 5 min to remove cellular debris. Clarified supernatants were filtered through 0.45-μm PVDF membranes (Millipore) and concentrated using PEG8000 (#A100159; Sangon Biotech) at 4 °C overnight, followed by centrifugation at 7500×g for 2 h. For transduction, HUVECs were incubated with lentiviral particles (MOI = 10) in ECM containing 8 μg/mL Polybrene (#40804ES; Yeasen) for 48 h. Stable cell lines were selected with 1 μg/mL puromycin (#A424862; Sangon Biotech) for 7 days, with media refreshed every 48 h. Knockdown and overexpression efficiency were validated by Western blot using anti-HK2 and β-actin antibodies. Validated pools were utilized for subsequent experiments.

Luciferase reporter assay

For the luciferase reporter assay, HUVECs under three different conditions—Control, HK2KD, and HK2-knockdown supplemented with 10 mM sodium lactate (HK2KD + Lac)—were co-transfected in 12-well plates using Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Invitrogen). Each well received a mixture of either the pGL3-SIRT2 reporter plasmid or the empty pGL3-Basic control vector, together with the Renilla luciferase normalization plasmid pRL-TK (Promega). Cells were harvested 24 h after transfection and lysed. Firefly and Renilla luciferase activities were measured sequentially with the Dual-Luciferase® Reporter Assay System (Promega) following the manufacturer’s protocol. Relative promoter activity was calculated as the ratio of firefly to Renilla luciferase luminescence. Data from three independent experiments, each performed in triplicate, were normalized to the Control group (set as 1).

CUT& Tag

CUT& Tag was performed by Novoprotein Co., Ltd. (Suzhou, China) using the NovoNGS CUT& Tag High-Sensitivity Kit for Illumina (N259-YH01-01A, Novoprotein). Briefly, the HUVECs were bound to concanavalin A-coated beads. After permeabilization and incubation with primary antibody (anti-H3K18la, PTM BIO, PTM-1427RM), the DNA was precisely tagmented with pA-Tn5 transposase and enriched by PCR to create sequencing-ready libraries. The library preparations were sequenced using the Illumina Novaseq platform, and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated.

RNA interference

Gene silencing was performed using Lipofectamine 3000 transfection reagent (#L3000015; Thermo Fisher Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, HUVECs were seeded in six-well plates at a density of 2 × 10⁵ cells/well in antibiotic-free ECM medium and incubated for 24 h to reach 70–80% confluency. For each transfection, 100 nM siRNA targeting SIRT2 (siSIRT2: sense 5′-GCAAAGGTACGACGATCTA-3′) or scrambled negative control siRNA was diluted in Opti-MEM (#31985070; Gibco) and mixed with Lipofectamine 3000 reagent at a 1:1 ratio (v/v). The siRNA-lipid complexes were incubated for 15 min at room temperature and added dropwise to the cells. After 6 h, the transfection medium was replaced with fresh ECM, and cells were harvested 48 h post-transfection for downstream analyses. Knockdown efficiency was validated by Western blot using anti-SIRT2 and β-actin antibodies.

Quantification of lactate levels

Intracellular and extracellular lactate concentrations were measured using a Lactate Assay Kit (#S0208S, Beyotime Biotechnology) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For exogenous lactate treatment, HUVECs cultured under HSS conditions were incubated with 5, 10, or 25 mM sodium lactate (#71718; Sigma-Aldrich) in ECM medium for 24 h. Parallel experiments were conducted in HK2-knockdown HUVECs to assess lactate uptake and retention. Data were expressed as mM lactate/106 cells (intracellular) or mM lactate (extracellular). All measurements were performed in triplicate across three independent biological replicates.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation quantitative PCR (ChIP-qPCR)

Chromatin immunoprecipitation was performed using the ChIP Assay Kit (#P2078; Beyotime) following the manufacturer’s protocol with modifications. Briefly, HUVECs were crosslinked with 1% formaldehyde in culture medium for 10 min at 37 °C, followed by quenching with 1.25 M glycine (5 min, RT). Cells were washed twice with ice-cold PBS containing 1 mM PMSF and lysed in ChIP lysis buffer (1% SDS, 50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.1, 5 mM EDTA, protease inhibitor cocktail). Chromatin was sonicated to ~300 bp fragments using a Bioruptor Pico (Diagenode) under the following conditions: 40 W power, 10 s ON/30 s OFF cycles (16 repeats), 4 °C. Lysates were centrifuged at 10,000×g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet debris. For immunoprecipitation, 2 μg of rabbit anti-H3K18la antibody (#PTM-1427RM; PTM Biolabs) or rabbit IgG isotype control (#AC005; ABclonal) was added to 100 μg of chromatin and incubated overnight at 4 °C with rotation. Protein A + G Agarose were then added for 4 h at 4 °C. Immune complexes were washed sequentially with low-salt, high-salt, LiCl, and TE buffers. Crosslinks were reversed by incubating with 200 mM NaCl at 65 °C for 12 h, followed by proteinase K treatment. DNA was purified via phenol-chloroform extraction and analyzed by qPCR using VEGFA promoter-specific primers (see Quantitative Reverse Transcription Polymerase Chain Reaction) and SYBR Green Master Mix (#11185ES; Yeasen). Input DNA (1%) served as the normalization control.

Statistics and reproducibility

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.1.2) and GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software). Continuous data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM) for in vivo experiments (n ≥ 6 animals per group) and mean ± standard deviation (SD) for in vitro studies (n ≥ 3 biological replicates with triplicate technical repeats), unless otherwise specified. Normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests, respectively. For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-tests (parametric) or Mann–Whitney U-tests (non-parametric) were applied. Multiple group comparisons were analyzed by one-way or two-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test (parametric) or Kruskal–Wallis with Dunn’s correction (non-parametric). Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Conclusion

We elucidate the HK2-lactate-SIRT2-H3K18la axis as a metabolic-epigenetic switch essential for angiogenesis in PAD. By coupling glycolytic metabolism to epigenetic regulation, HK2 ensures transcriptional activation of pro-angiogenic programs. Restoring lactylation via SIRT2 inhibition emerges as a paradigm-shifting strategy to bypass metabolic deficits, offering new therapeutic avenues for ischemic vascular diseases.
