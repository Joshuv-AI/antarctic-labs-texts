Psychosocial stressors related to extreme weather events and multiple resource insecurities: qualitative insights from refugee youth in an Ugandan humanitarian setting
Authors: Carmen H Logie, Ofir Sivan, Rachel Leggett, Moses Okumu, Miranda Loutet, Frannie MacKenzie, Simon Odong Lukone, Nelson Kisubi, Lesley Gittings, Peter Kyambadde, Caetano Dorea, Manjulaa Narasimhan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractExtreme weather events (EWE) contribute to heightened psychosocial stressors through complex pathways, including by worsening resource insecurities. Refugee settlements globally are disproportionately exposed to EWE compared with host national populations, yet refugees' experiences of resource insecurity-related psychosocial stressors in low-income humanitarian settings are understudied. Our study focused on understanding the lived experiences of psychosocial stressors in the context of EWE and resource insecurity among refugee youth in Bidi Bidi Refugee Settlement, Uganda. This qualitative study involved 32 walk-along interviews with a purposive sample of refugee youth aged 16–24 (16 men, 16 women); youth led the interviewer to 1–3 places where they obtained food, water, and/or sanitation resources, discussed the place's meaning and impact on wellbeing, and took photos. We also conducted 12 in-depth interviews with key informants with expertise in refugee youth wellbeing, EWE, and/or resource security. We conducted framework thematic analysis informed by resource scarcity and water insecurity-related distress frameworks. Participant narratives reflected four key themes regarding linkages between EWE, resource insecurities, and psychosocial distress: 1) material deprivation and uncertainty (sub-themes: drought-related food and water insecurity; flooding-related infrastructure and agricultural damage); 2) shame of social failure (sub-themes: sanitation insecurity stressors; unemployment and food insecurity distress and related substance use); 3) interpersonal conflict, including multi-level violence (sub-themes: increased violence; concerns about crime and theft); and 4) coping and asset management strategies (sub-themes: social and economic infrastructure; social capital; household relations). Together findings suggest the need for integrating psychosocial support within social and economic opportunities and poverty reduction with refugee youth.Keywords: Extreme weather events, Uganda, Resource insecurity, Refugee settlement, Youth, Social capital, Psychosocial stressors

Introduction

Refugee and displaced people are at elevated risk for experiencing climate change-related extreme weather events (EWE) as refugee settlements are located in geographic regions disproportionately exposed to such events compared to national averages (Fransen et al., 2024). EWE are associated with increased food, water, and sanitation insecurity (Carr et al., 2024; Kompas et al., 2024; Mirzabaev et al., 2023; Stringer et al., 2021). EWE (Connerton and Wooton, 2019; Helldén et al., 2021), and resource insecurities such as food (Bateson et al., 2025; Trudell et al., 2021), water (Wutich et al., 2020), and sanitation (Kimutai et al., 2023) insecurity, are associated with psychosocial stressors. Refugees, who experience high and persistent rates of psychosocial challenges such as depression and post-traumatic stress disorder due to histories of conflict and trauma (Blackmore et al., 2020), are therefore at the nexus of EWE, resource insecurities, and psychosocial stressors.

Yet knowledge gaps persist regarding refugee adolescent and youths' lived experiences of the relationships between environmental stressors, resource insecurities, and psychosocial stressors, particularly in low and middle-income country (LMIC) humanitarian settings (Devonald et al., 2022; Rother et al., 2022a; Trudell et al., 2021). In fact, Rother et al., 2022a, Rother et al., 2022b systematic review of the impact of EWE on child and adolescent mental health in Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) summarized: “There is an alarming lack of research focusing specifically on the mental health of youth exposed to EWE in SSA, where EWE, especially extreme heat, flooding and droughts, continue to increase.“ (p. 1) Attention to EWE and related resource insecurity is urgent in light of ongoing cuts to international food aid (Mawdsley et al., 2025; Osendarp et al., 2025) that exacerbate persisting food insecurity in LMICs such as Uganda (Khan, 2020; Rosso, 2025; Simonsen Nilsen et al., 2024; UN News, 2020; WFP, 2020). Uganda hosts the highest number of refugees in Africa, with over 1.81 million refugees—nearly half between the ages of 12–35 (UNHCR, 2025a). The ongoing influx of refugees into Uganda has strained fertile land, causing deforestation in refugee settlements such as Bidi Bidi as well as surrounding areas (Berke and Larsen, 2022; Ssentongo et al., 2024). This has contributed to increased land degradation and bushland, woodland, and cropland loss, and decreased healthy vegetation areas, largely driven by demand for wood as cooking fuel, although biophysical characteristics vary across Bidi Bidi's five zones spanning 250 km2 (International Bank for Reconstruction and Development et al., 2019; United Nation Environmental Programme & Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), 2019). Such environmental stress may also intensify tensions regarding resources with host communities, creating additional stress within refugee-host community dynamics (Logie et al., 2021a; Nakalembe et al., 2022; Santschi, M., & Jacobsen, K, 2019), also noted in other Ugandan humanitarian settings (Bjørkhaug, 2020; Bjørkhaug et al., 2025). There is a need to address climate-related changes, such as a later and shorter rainy season, on host and refugee community agricultural practices in humanitarian settings such as Bidi Bidi (United Nations Environmental Programme & Office for the Coordination of Humanitarian Affairs (OCHA), 2019).

EWE exposure is associated with direct negative psychological health effects, such as increases in anxiety, depression, PTSD and suicide, as well as psychosocial stressors such as displacement, community violence, educational disruption, food insecurity, and economic deprivation (Connerton and Wooton, 2019; Helldén et al., 2021; Rother et al., 2022a). SSA children and youth may be more vulnerable to mental health impacts from EWE due to high rates of pre-existing child poverty which exceeded 40% in 2022 (Hague et al., 2023) and a disproportionate burden of climate change impacts such as flooding, drought, and increasing temperatures (WMO, 2024). In Northern Uganda, for example, 2022 and 2023 were characterized by prolonged drought and extreme floods (Global Climate Risks, 2023). Additionally, in a 2024 survey with refugee youth in Bidi Bidi, 61.5% reported experiencing two to four EWE and 30% reported over five EWE in the past-year (Logie et al., 2025b). While empirical literature examining the relationship between EWE and psychosocial stressors among youth in Africa is scarce (Mugeere et al., 2021), findings suggest that EWE-related stressors may be associated with increased psychosocial stress. For instance, a cross-sectional survey with youth (ages 18–30) in a climate-vulnerable region prone to EWE, including landslides, in Eastern Uganda found a 65% prevalence of mental health challenges, largely anxiety (44%) (Batte et al., 2024). In a mixed-methods study with adolescents and youth in Kenya, drought conditions were associated with food and water insecurity, depression, anxiety, and posttraumatic symptoms, while also being described by participants as scary and traumatic (Straight et al., 2025).

Limited studies have explored the lived experiences of youth, including refugee youth, in the context of EWE and resource insecurities in SSA (McMichael, 2020; McMichael et al., 2012; Schütte et al., 2018; Severoni et al., 2024). Yet the population of adolescents and youth (aged 15–24) in Africa is rapidly increasing and is expected to grow 26% between 2005 and 2035 (Mugeere et al., 2021). Refugee youth are important to include in EWE-related health and psychosocial research, as Ugandan refugee settlements are disproportionally affected by high temperatures and low rainfall (Fransen et al., 2024). Flooding in Uganda causes destruction to agriculture, roads, houses, and sanitation infrastructure (Nuwagira and Yasin, 2022), while droughts lead to water scarcity that harms rainfed local agriculture and amplifies food insecurity (Nuwagira and Yasin, 2022; Sunday et al., 2023). Uganda is therefore a particularly important context to examine the linkages between EWE, resource insecurities, and psychosocial stressors among refugee youth.

While research on the associations between EWE, resource insecurities, and psychosocial stressors among refugee youth remains scarce in SSA specifically, there is ample evidence that resource insecurities are linked with increased psychosocial stressors, including poorer mental health. Systematic reviews that included African countries found associations between water and sanitation insecurity and common psychological disorders such as depression and psychological distress (Kimutai et al., 2023) and between food insecurity and depressive symptoms (Smith et al., 2023). Cross-sectional studies with refugee youth in Uganda found associations between water insecurity and increased odds of depression in Bidi Bidi (Logie et al., 2023a, Logie et al., 2023b) and among urban refugee youth in Kampala (Logie et al., 2024a). Among a survey with non-refugee rural Ugandan households, water insecurity was associated with severe depression among adults (Mushavi et al., 2020). Cooper-Vince et al. (2018) found that women residing in water insecurity hotspots in Uganda had a 70% higher risk of probable depression compared with women not residing in water insecurity hotspots. Food insecurity was also associated with increased odds of depression in longitudinal studies with urban refugee youth in Kampala (Admassu et al., 2024; Logie et al., 2022). Research in SSA contexts highlights psychosocial factors as possible mechanisms for the relationships between EWE-related resource insecurity and mental health outcomes. For example, qualitative research in Bidi Bidi refugee settlement found that multiple resource insecurities (food, water, firewood) were associated with psychosocial stressors such as sexual and gender-based violence in families, intimate partnerships, and communities (Logie et al., 2021). Another qualitative study in Uganda's Kiryandongo and Adjumani refugee settlements found challenges faced meeting basic needs (e.g., food, education) affected adolescents' psychosocial wellbeing, including increasing irritability, stress, hunger, and caregiver violence (Meyer et al., 2019). Finally, Mushavi et al. (2020) describes the feeling of “choice-lessness” and distress, interpersonal relationship challenges, and community conflict as contributing to the connection between water insecurity and depression. Researchers also describe tension and conflict over natural resources between host and refugee communities as a key stressors among refugees (Betts et al., 2023; Habib, 2023; Habib and Roy Chowdhury, 2023). Yet when accompanied with sufficient humanitarian aid and planning, the presence of refugee communities can benefit the host community via social service improvements and resource “spillover” (Gengo et al., 2018; Zhou et al., 2023). Within this research on resource insecurities, refugee youths' experiences remain understudied, reflecting larger knowledge gaps on youths' experiences of multiple, co-occurring resource insecurities (Logie et al., 2025a; Maxfield, 2020).

Conceptual frameworks

Conceptual frameworks propose various pathways connecting resource insecurities to psychosocial stressors. Bisung and Elliott, 2017a, Bisung and Elliott, 2017b scoping review of the psychosocial impacts of water and sanitation insecurity discuss four interrelated stressors: physical stressors (e.g., unsafe infrastructure); financial stressors (e.g., costs of purchasing water); social stressors (e.g. community conflict); and perceived inequity-related stressors (e.g., feeling marginalized). There is also increased attention to the co-occurrence of food and water insecurity (Brewis et al., 2020; Workman et al., 2021) that can exacerbate psycho-emotional stress.

The resource insecurity framework assesses how food and water insecurity exacerbate psychosocial stressors via pathways of uncertainty and unpredictability, stigma and social failure, and social injustice (Wutich and Brewis, 2014). For instance, uncertainty and unpredictability regarding meeting food and water-related household consumption needs can lead to psychosocial distress. Being unable to meet water and food-related needs can also be a marker of social failure, in turn, leading to shame, self-blame, and stress (Wutich and Brewis, 2014). Finally, perceived inequity regarding one's inability to access sufficient food/water can lead to feelings of social injustice and subsequent anger, shame, and distress. Wutich et al. (2020) built on this framework to conceptualize additional mechanistic pathways from water insecurity to psychological and emotional distress. These include: expanding discussions of social failure to include resource insecurity-related unemployment and loss of livelihoods, which can result in shame and distress related to lower socio-economic status; frustration regarding lost opportunities and autonomy (e.g., educational disruption); and interpersonal conflict, including intimate partner violence (Wutich et al., 2020).

Research increasingly focuses on coping strategies for managing resource insecurities, spanning communities (e.g., water committees), infrastructure (e.g., repairing roads), and households (e.g., sharing resources) (Bisung and Elliott, 2017a; Connerton and Wooton, 2019). Households may also engage in additional means to access resources, including alternative income generation strategies and/or modifying consumption patterns (Wutich and Brewis, 2014). The assets vulnerability framework (Moser, 1998) may be valuable for understanding pathways from EWE to resource insecurities and psychosocial stressors. The ability to mobilize and manage assets among people in poverty faced with environmental and/or economic stressors may account for variability in experiences of vulnerability. Such assets include: household income and gendered labour distribution; productive assets (e.g. housing, land); access to vocational and educational programs; functioning infrastructure (e.g., sanitation); and social capital (e.g., social ties) (Moser, 1998).

Individual and collective coping approaches can also reduce EWE-related stressors, yet warrant additional focus (Bisung and Elliott, 2017b), particularly with refugee youth. Ugandan research has explored community resilience to cope with the detrimental effects of EWE such as drought and floods. For instance, qualitative research with leaders in Uganda's Bidi Bidi refugee settlement emphasized the importance of collaboration and dialogue with the host community to create land allocation and usage policies that strengthen self-reliant agriculture and refugee community climate resilience (Berke and Larsen, 2022). In non-humanitarian Ugandan settings, bottom-up, community-based agricultural education programs can build resilience and reduce food insecurity (Osumba et al., 2021). Others focus on knowledge sharing and bridging ties to advance climate resilience and adaptation (Cooper and Wheeler, 2015). A review of climate change and youth in Uganda recommended that climate resilient strategies include gender sensitive approaches, youth engagement in decision-making and programming, and awareness-raising (Mugeere et al., 2021). Research on the lived experiences of adolescents and youth regarding the linkages between EWE, multiple resource insecurities, and psychosocial stress remains limited (Helldén et al., 2021), particularly in Ugandan humanitarian settings (Mugeere et al., 2021). To address these knowledge gaps and respond to calls for additional qualitative research on EWE and youth mental wellbeing (Helldén et al., 2021), we explored how refugee youth (16–24) in Uganda's Bidi Bidi settlement experience the interconnections between EWEs, resource insecurities, psychosocial stressors, and coping mechanisms.

Methods

Study background

This article is based on qualitative data from the community-based mixed-methods Woli Na Kalen (Planetary Health) study that examined the relations between social, health, and environmental wellbeing among refugee adolescents and youth in Bidi Bidi refugee settlement, Uganda. We conducted individual interviews with refugee youth and key informants from September 2022 to August 2023. The analysis presented in this article focuses on linkages between EWE, resource insecurities, and psychosocial stressors and coping.

We received ethical approval from the Research Ethics Board at the University of Toronto (41103), Mildmay Uganda Research Ethics Committee (MUREC-2021-85), and the Uganda National Council for Science and Technology (SS1315ES). All participants received information on the study's purpose and activities before enrolling in the study and provided written informed consent. In line with guidelines for adolescent health research (Flicker and Guta, 2008; Santelli et al., 2025), and our past research with youth in Bidi Bidi (Logie et al., 2022; Logie et al., 2023a), we received ethics approval for 16 and 17 year olds to provide their own consent to participate.

Reflexivity

Positionality influences all aspects of the research process. The study co-authors include graduate and doctoral students; early-career and senior academic faculty; global scientists; medical and social work practitioners; clinicians; and community-based service providers. Co-authors identify as cisgender men and women, as well as heterosexual and sexually diverse. They represent a range of racial identities, including white, Black, and other racialized persons, and include individuals with relevant lived experiences, such as forcible displacement, (im)migration, economic and resource insecurity, violence, and exposure to multiple systems of oppression (e.g. sexism, racism). The co-authors are researchers and practitioners with experience living and working in Uganda, multiple African countries, Canada, Europe, and the United States, including Ugandan co-authors residing both inside and outside of Uganda. These diverse positionalities shaped study design and recruitment of participants; we aimed to include a range of perspectives and lived experiences and a balanced representation across gender and age.

Setting and participants

This study took place in Bidi Bidi refugee settlement in the West Nile sub-region, northern Uganda. As of October 2025, Bidi Bidi was the third largest refugee settlement in Uganda with a population of over 209,000 refugees—28% aged youth aged 15–24 (UNHCR, 2025b). This was a community-based research collaboration between an academic institute, the Ugandan Ministry of Health, and a community agency supporting refugee youth wellbeing and livelihoods in Bidi Bidi (Uganda Refugee and Disaster Management Council [URDMC]). We conducted one-on-one interviews with key informants (n = 12) and refugee youth (n = 32; 16 young women and 16 young men). Key informants (KI) were purposively selected by the research team due to their expertise working in Bidi Bidi in areas of refugee youth health, resource security (e.g., food, water), and/or climate-related issues. KIs, who included two refugees, offered diverse perspectives from international and local non-governmental organizations and local government officials. Youth participants learned about the study by word of mouth and flyer distribution/advertising in community/religious centres. Youth were recruited using purposive, venue-based sampling methods by peer navigators (PN). PN were refugees between age 20–30 employed by URDMC, Bidi Bidi residents, and fluent in English and Juba Arabic and/or Bari. Eligibility criteria for youth participants included being aged 16–24 years old, residents of Bidi Bidi Zone 3 or 4 (where URDMC works), able to speak English, Juba Arabic, or Bari, and reporting experiences of either an extreme weather event (e.g. drought, flooding) or resource insecurity (i.e., food, water) within the 14 days prior to the interview.

Data collection

The resource scarcity framework (Wutich and Brewis, 2014) guided the development of data collection tools. Its contextual relevance was established in past research to understand multiple co-occurring resource insecurities with refugee youth in Bidi Bidi (Logie et al., 2021) Our interview guides aimed to understand connections between structural drivers such as ecological factors (e.g., floods), socioeconomic factors (e.g. unemployment), social structures (e.g., gender norms), resource insecurities (e.g., sanitation, water, food), and refugee youth wellbeing, including psychosocial stressors (e.g., distress). The semi-structured KI and youth interview guides were developed collaboratively by a team of local community agencies, academics, the Ministry of Health, and URDMC. After pilot-testing the interview guides locally, feedback was applied to ensure contextual relevance and clarity. A paper-based survey was used to collect sociodemographic information for all interviewees (e.g., gender, education).

KI interviews included questions on how changes in season, EWE, and resource access (i.e., food, water) impacted the wellbeing of refugee youth in Bidi Bidi, as well as existing coping mechanisms. Interviews were approximately 1 h in duration and conducted by two-senior UDRMC research staff. Staff received extensive qualitative training prior to conducting the interviews by the PI, and her team at the University of Toronto.

Refugee youth walk-along interviews were one to two hours in duration and conducted by one of two trained URDMC refugee research assistants (one woman and one man) in English, Bari or Juba Arabic. They were later translated into English by trained translators for analysis. The research assistants received extensive qualitative interviewing training from academic team members. The interviews followed ‘walk-along’ and ‘go-along’ methodological approaches, designed to elicit rich understanding of lived experiences in a target environment, including contextual and micro-scale environmental factors (Carpiano, 2009; Kusenbach, 2003). During each one-on-one walk-along interview, the youth were invited to bring the research assistant to one to three places where they access food, water, or sanitation.

Participants received tablets to photograph each selected location, after which they conducted interviews with the research assistant in a private, quiet space. The interview guide contained questions about the reasons for their choice of place, the impact of weather and seasonal change on their experiences of each place, the corresponding impact of the place on their wellbeing, and suggestions for improving each place. Each interview was recorded and transcribed verbatim by URDMC staff.

Data analysis

The socio-demographic information obtained from paper-based surveys with youth and KIs were summarized, including calculating the proportion for categorical measures, and the mean and standard deviation for continuous measures. The transcribed interviews, photos, and photo descriptions from walk-along-interview were coded using Dedoose software (Dedoose, 2021) by two trained graduate-level research assistants with the lead author managing coding discrepancies.

We conducted thematic analysis applying a framework approach, building a structured codebook to guide coding and analysis (Braun and Clarke, 2006, 2023; Gale et al., 2013) developed using deductive codes adapted from the resource scarcity framework (e.g., water insecurity) (Wutich and Brewis, 2014) and inductive codes identified as emerging within the data (e.g. menstruation insecurity). We applied framework thematic analysis steps, including: familiarizing ourselves with the data through close reading and re-reading of transcripts; developing and applying a codebook; organizing codes into a framework; identifying themes and sub-themes, and their relationship with one another, to generate a coherent narrative; and adding quotations to support the description of themes and sub-themes (Braun and Clarke, 2023; Gale et al., 2013). Quotes from refugee youth are identified with the participant's age, gender, and participant identification number (ID); quotes from the KIs are identified only with the gender, employment sector, and participant ID to maintain participant confidentiality.

Themes were largely similar between KI and youth narratives for water, food, and EWE-related issues. KIs were more likely to raise issues related to the broader economic context (e.g. work history, vocational training, economic opportunities) and healthcare access, whereas refugee youth were more likely to discuss sanitation/toilet-related challenges, energy scarcity, and finding institutional support and/or practical, resource-intensive coping methods such as farming.

Theme 1: material deprivation and uncertainty

Participants shared that EWE exacerbated poverty and resource insecurity challenges, leading to uncertainty about meeting material needs and emotional distress. Subthemes include: drought driven water and food insecurity as sources of distress, and flood-related infrastructure and agricultural damage and its psychosocial impacts.

Drought-driven food and water insecurity as sources of distress

Participants described drought and the prolonged dry season as leading to water and food insecurity. Participants mentioned Bidi Bidi's existing environmental, structural, and ecological factors, including barren land, insufficient access to fields for food cultivation, and inadequate supplies. One youth participant showed the interviewer the dry land (Fig. 1) and described the current situation as reminding them of past experiences of hunger and distress in their home country:

Bidi Bidi makes me stressful because when people were brought here, they found out that the food is not enough and there are no fields where people can cultivate to get another food to supplement on the food we get from the FDP (food distribution point). The ground is very hard. In our country soils are not like this, rocky. Some refugees end up leaving. They say that it’s better to work in South Sudan so they can get enough food to sustain their lives. And at least if the size of food is being topped up so that people will stay comfortable, not remembering their hunger which they have already left. (man, age 19, ID 03–02–05)

caption: “In our country soils are not like this, rocky. Some refugees end up leaving. They say that its better to work in South Sudan so they can get enough food to sustain their lives.” (man, age 19, ID 03–02–05)

Participants highlighted how the reduced World Food Program (WFP) rations forced them to skip meals during the dry season. The interplay of water insecurity and limited food access led to significant mental and emotional distress:

In the dry season this place is too hot, mentally most people are not okay because they get stressed with scarcity of water, limited food––and then WFP reduced our food rations, so people get even more stressed. In the dry season, you don’t have food and sometimes you are eating once a day. (man, age 18, ID 03–02–06)

The high temperatures and water scarcity in the dry season also made it difficult for refugees in Bidi Bidi to become self-reliant by growing their food, leading to increased stress and desperation: “When all my crops die, I feel very sad because I plant those crops to help me in future. Now, they are drying off. What am I going to eat now?” (woman, age 24, ID 04–01–01). Agricultural failure prevented refugee youth from generating extra income for food and essentials and deepened poverty: “When these things [crop destruction] happen, you will experience poverty because we plant some of the crops with the intention of selling them, and others for food” (woman, age 22, ID 04–01–03).

Participants often brought up instances where feelings of desperation, hopelessness, and helplessness from coping with the harsh environmental conditions in Bidi Bidi could ultimately lead to suicide. For instance, a participant described a suicide following crop failure and related debt:

There was a man who hired land, he planted two acres of cassava and one acre of simsim (sesame), now too much sunshine came for two months, it struck the crops and they all dried. That man had used a loan to plant all those things. He was expecting to harvest, sell the crops and pay the loan. Things didn’t go as he had planned. This man had nowhere to pick the money to pay back, so he ended up committing suicide, which was a bad thing. (woman, age 22, ID 04-01-03)

Another youth explained challenges with accessing food in the settlement and struggles with food delays that led to feeling uncared for, migrating, or at times suicide:

Receiving food is hard because you must explain and follow long procedures … UNHCR explaining and explaining until they organize for you a day to get food. If you miss the food, the next month you don’t have what to eat so things become hard, I am telling you sir, life in the settlement is very bad. These people don’t care about us, that is why most of friends are going to South Sudan to hustle and some are killing themselves. (man, age 20, ID 03-02-02)

Flood-related infrastructure and agricultural damage and its psychosocial impacts

Flooding, heavy rains, and strong winds cause severe damage to the already precarious infrastructure in Bidi Bidi. Participants described loss of housing and property leading to emotional suffering:

Many houses were destroyed because of the heavy rain, cold, and the heavy wind. The rain came with heavy winds, and houses were broken down. People were suffering that time. There was nothing like carpets to cover our goods and property. By that time, people were suffering and crying. (man, age 21, ID 03-02-03)

Participants also described how local road infrastructure was vulnerable to flooding during periods of heavy rain (Fig. 2). Emotional suffering was intensified by the destruction of roads, which hampered food deliveries and in turn exacerbated food insecurity and hunger:

In the dry season, it is easy for (humanitarian agency) to bring the food from their stores to the FDP (food distribution point) here. But when it is rainy season, it will be difficult because the roads in the settlement become impassable and the lorries carrying food get stuck. When it rains almost a full day, people from far villages come here late and sometimes miss to get food so they suffer. (man, age 20, ID 03–02–02)

caption: “… when it is rainy season, it will be difficult because the roads in the settlement become impassable and the lorries carrying food get stuck.” (man, age 20, ID 03–02–02)

Flooding and storms also contributed to food scarcity through agricultural destruction. Crops were uprooted by strong winds and waterlogged by heavy rains. Combined with reduced food rations, this loss of crops worsened suffering:

Too much rainfall destroys our small crops that we have grown, all being blown down. The maize, the sorghum, the wind will break them, and the plants will end up dying. This will bring about little produce from the crops during harvesting time. Our [food] ration has been reduced. Our hope is in farming, and we always have hope in our plants but when they are destroyed then life becomes hard. (man, age 18, ID 03–02–06)

Additionally, youth described how flooding disrupted food distribution (Fig. 3), depriving families of sufficient food supplies. As a result, family-level psychosocial stress increased:

If there is flooding in the distribution point, it is now very difficult for me to come and receive my food during the ration time. It causes a lot of suffering in my home because there is no food. And we are surviving on WFP (World Food Programme) food because we don't have enough land for cultivation. (young man, age 19, ID 03–02–08)

caption: “We are surviving on WFP (World Food Programme) food because we don't have enough land for cultivation, so we suffer a lot.” (man, age 19, ID 03–02–08)

Theme 2: Shame of social failure

Participants described that the inability to acquire sufficient resources due to the convergence of EWE-related resource insecurities and poverty lead to psychosocial distress through shame of social failure. Subthemes include: 1) sanitation insecurity and related hygiene challenges as sources of distress, and 2) unemployment and food insecurity-related distress and substance use coping.

Sanitation insecurity and related hygiene challenges as sources of distress

Water scarcity during the dry season rendered it challenging for refugee youth to maintain hygiene and sanitation practices. Young women faced challenges with menstruation management, which led to shame and suffering:

In the dry season, the girls suffer a lot compared to the boys. If a girl is in her menstruation period, she needs to bathe three times a day but there is no water. Girls move long distances to look for water. Sometimes they fear going to school because they didn't bathe. (man, age 18, ID 03-02-06)

In many interviews, participants referred to menstruation management insecurity as the main reason for school absences and dropouts among young refugee women:

Interviewer: Why would you not go to school?

Interviewee: It will be a big shame, just imagine the blood appears on your dress, or your uniform and then people will start laughing at you. (woman, age 17, ID 04-01-08)

Key informants also described how resource scarcities at large reduced the ability of youth to focus and engage in school:

Because you are stressed you fail to catch up. For instance, I am here working but I have not left water at home, I am going to fail to concentrate in what I am doing. If I have taken two days without bathing, not even put some little water to drink, I can’t cook, I can’t bathe, I can’t wash. I may fail to concentrate and it’s going to have impact on my performance. It will [also] have impact on the performance of this person as an adolescent [aged] 16–24. (key informant [KI], woman, sexual and reproductive health, ID KI-8)

Flooding and heavy rains also constrained the daily maintenance of personal hygiene. In the rainy season, young women described being more vulnerable to the physical and emotional impacts of unmet sanitation insecurity needs related to menstruation management: “When it is raining, and you are in your menstruation, the rain washes you up to the inside and the blood in the pad will come and flow out. The smell will also be there, and you will not feel comfortable” (woman, age 22, ID 04–01–03). The heavy rains also lead to flooding of pit latrines (Figs. 4 and 5), adding another layer of difficulty in maintaining personal hygiene.

“I am not comfortable using this pit latrine in the rainy season, because the water doesn't only splash on your buttocks but also in the private parts.” (woman, age 23, ID 03–01–03)

Example of pit latrine shared by young woman refugee participant (woman, age 23, ID 03–01–03)

Unemployment and food insecurity-related distress and substance use coping

Key informants described the dry season as particularly challenging for refugee youth in Bidi Bidi. The environmental conditions, such as barren soil and extremely high temperatures, reduced the opportunity and motivation for youth to be involved in agricultural production, which participants identified as leading some youth to resort to substance use for coping:

I have realized when the soil is not loose enough, they [refugee youths] will never go into cultivation for production. Many youths became redundant, ending up going into drug abuse. Because of some of these effects of the climate, when you tell youths that they are supposed to be productive, they will tell you that this is the weather. (KI, man, community development, ID KI-06)

Flooding and drought-related food insecurity were perceived to contribute to increased alcohol consumption. To endure poverty-related suffering and stress, participants described how some men turned to drinking, which in turn increased intimate partner violence (IPV):

We have some people in the community that find getting things is very hard. When food is over, they just rely on drinking. Last month one came when he was drunk, shouting and wanting to beat a woman. I had to come out and talked to him. Most of the people turn now to drink just to avoid that stress. (KI, women, refugee sector, ID KI-7)

Sometimes, alcohol use exacerbated existing household food insecurity and destabilized the whole family system. Participants described how some men sold their family's food rations to buy alcohol, leading to increased hunger and psychological distress. A key informant highlighted how this is particularly harmful to women and girls who are traditionally responsible for feeding their families:

Most suicide victims are women and some of these issues come because of having a husband who is drunkard. You find that your husband takes 50% of the food you have brought home and sells it [for alcohol]. At the end of the day, the burden of managing the food is on the woman, so that thing will keep torturing her psychologically. It mostly affects the women, women and girls. (KI, man, refugee sector, ID KI-2)

Interpersonal conflict, including multi-level violence

Participants discussed that EWE and related resource insecurities can heighten interpersonal conflict which can result in multi-level violence. Sub-themes include: resource insecurity-related community and household-level violence and conflict, and resource insecurity-related increased community crime and theft.

Resource insecurity-related community and household-level conflict and violence

Participants described violence could be sparked over resource insecurity-related conflict within communities and families. Participants shared that food and water scarcity undermine community cohesion and solidarity, for instance, through leading to arguments and quarrels at line-ups for collecting water at boreholes. Community-level conflict and violence could have mental health effects, as a key informant described:

When you go to a borehole and you find the 100–200 jerricans, sitting for all these hours waiting for your turn to fetch, it will affect you mentally. People end up causing a lot of commotion at the borehole which makes them start fighting at the water point, so this kind of trauma is about mental health. (KI, man, food insecurity sector, ID KI-10)

Violence resulting from resource insecurity also affected household conflict. This disproportionately affected young women and girls. Girls and women were typically responsible for fetching water for their families, and many participants showed researchers the busy water taps in their walk-along interviews (Fig. 6). Prolonged droughts made this household task more difficult, as water sources became scarcer, forcing girls and women to walk further distances. This longer time/distance for water collection could lead to parental mistrust and subsequent household-level conflict and violence against adolescent girls and young women. An adolescent girl described an example of this:

If we come back home our parents will start shouting at us that, 'where have you been, have you really been fetching water or have you been doing wrong things' and they will start beating us, accusing us that we have been doing bad things. (woman, age 17, ID 03–01–08)

caption: “… our parents will start shouting at us that, 'where have you been, have you really been fetching water or you have been doing wrong things', and they will start beating us.” (woman, age 17, ID 03–01–08)

The constant stress pertaining to household resource scarcities could also lead to parents having less patience with children, in turn elevating violence against children. A key informant explained:

There was a period when we met some household during follow-ups, we got a woman who beat her child properly because the child is wasting water and that is the time where water is very scarce, why do you waste water? (KI, man, community development, ID KI-6)

EWE-related resource insecurities could also increase IPV. For instance, a youth shared an example of conflict pertaining to gendered role expectations and substance use in the dry season that could lead to IPV:

Sometimes during dry season, you may find that my mother is expecting my father to clear the land before we start digging but my father will refuse, he will go and drink alcohol. After drinking alcohol, reaching back home, you may find that violence will occur. (man, age 21, ID 03-02-03)

Resource insecurity-related increased community crime and theft

Participants discussed the ways in which crime and theft were harmful coping mechanisms within communities. Many described theft increasing during the dry season, at a time when economic and resource insecurity is particularly high. For instance, the difficulty of growing crops during the dry season could result in people stealing one another's crops:

During drought, due to strong sunshine some people cannot cultivate their crops. Then, people will go and steal the crops and in the time that you go to harvest your crops, you will find they are not there. (man, age 19, ID 03-02-05)

Thefts were not restricted only to food crops. Research participants also mentioned cases of stealing animals or infrastructure to sell for money to buy food. Another young man described how theft was brought on by food insecurity and survival challenges:

There was a man who was arrested. Due to lack of food, he went and stole someone’s goat during night hours. When the man was untying the goat to take it, a woman arrested him. Due to lack of food some people have become thieves, stealing things so that they can also survive like others. (man, age 19, ID 03-02-05)

While participants acknowledged that poverty and resource insecurities were drivers of community theft and crime, they also spoke to how such stealing destroyed community cohesion and contributed to general feelings of insecurity and a lack of safety. A key informant explained how crime and theft could worsen the resource insecurities within the community, even while being driven by resource insecurity:

We had cases in which our water system has been vandalised, solar panels and batteries being stolen, and not for use. They sell it to get money to meet some of their needs like food. (KI, man, water insecurity sector, ID KI-4)

Participants described feelings of anxiety and insecurity resulting from community theft and crime, especially during the dry season when people are forced to sleep with their doors open due to the high temperatures:

When it reaches dry season, inside the houses is hot, you will go and sleep outside. While you are sleeping, someone wake you up [asking you] 'give me whatever you have'. Sometimes, they will take all your property like the food. Now, if you are in a family of two, you get only 6 kilos, this can lead to hunger. (woman, age 18, ID 04–01–05)

As the above quotation again illustrated, the result of community theft and crime was increased resource insecurities for affected households.

Theme 4: Coping and asset management strategies

Participants discussed a range of coping and asset management strategies enacted by refugee youth and communities to mitigate the effects of EWE on related resource insecurities. These included: 1) social and economic infrastructure as climate adaptation resources; 2) social capital to respond to EWE and resource insecurity hardships; and 3) household cooperation as a strategy for shared survival.

Social and economic infrastructure as climate adaptation resources

Participants discussed that refugee communities built and strengthened food (e.g. gardens), water (e.g. water pumps), and sanitation (e.g. latrine) infrastructure and acquired additional material resources (e.g. fertilizer) to reduce vulnerability to EWE and related resource insecurities. These adaptation efforts were perceived as helpful for immediate survival needs as well as long-term sustainability and stability. For instance, key informants outlined community-engaged actions to improve water collection, storage, and pumping techniques to reduce climate-related water shortages. One participant enumerated several actions to adapt to the climate conditions and increase the water supply:

First, they need to do maintenances [on the] system, always making sure that they are functional. Second, trees must be planted, otherwise we are to leave this settlement to go into desertification. Third, we must have a proper storage for the waters that are produced during the rainy season to prevent water shortage. Fourth, we need proper running systems for the water for the food system [agriculture], so that will help us bridge the gap. (KI, man, refugee sector, KI-12)

Another KI who also reinforced the importance of planting trees to improve rainwater infiltration highlighted the importance of engaging refugee youth in such initiatives:

If you impact youth right now, it affects the next generation … we cannot talk [about] planting trees without talking about the youths because they are the energetic people, they are the community influencers now … they are the key drivers, the knowledge. For community interventions at local level … youth are at the forefront. (KI, man, food insecurity sector, ID KI-5)

Additionally, participants discussed the role of water user committees that are responsible for maintaining the settlement's water points and increasing the water supply using creative solutions:

We have water user committees. We have put in place to manage these water points in case there are break downs. They know what to do and in terms of the water points […] in dry season, you will find that the water cables are low, so it means the amount of water we are expecting is also reduced, mostly in the motorized system … so, we have close to 129 or 130 hands pumps that have been drilled across the settlement by partners so all this in order to increase the water supply” (KI, man, refugee sector, ID KI-2)

These examples highlight community initiatives to improve living conditions, including strengthening sanitation infrastructure. Another example of a community initiative included WASH (water, sanitation, and hygiene) education and training:

We have our WASH officers here who go to the community and train them on how they can maintain hygiene and sanitation in their communities. Also, we have community structures throughout who support us in following up with these households to ensure that they maintain hygiene and sanitation. (KI, man, food insecurity sector, KI-10)

Alongside improving water and sanitation infrastructures, participants mentioned the importance of providing household resources to increase agricultural production, such as fertilizers: “For our gardens to be better, we need to be provided with fertilizer. Because the land is not fertile, crops are not coming good” (man, age 21, ID 03-02-03).

Finally, key informants highlighted the importance of educational and vocational opportunities for refugee youth for human capital development:

Youth centres should be educative enough. They must get youth out of this dependency syndrome [to] be better people in the society. The ones who have dropped out of school, we encourage them to go back to school. Educated persons can get a job or employ themselves. Knowledge is better than power. (KI, woman, community development, KI-9)

Building social capital

Participants discussed how communities strengthened and leveraged relationships and collective action to respond to climate and poverty-related crises. Several components of social capital were discussed by participants as relevant to climate-related coping and adaptation. First, participants discussed community education and consciousness raising. For instance, key informants emphasized the importance of community engagement in identifying their challenges and addressing their problems:

As (non-government agency), we engage communities because … when you understand the problem, you can come up with the best solutions. We want to put communities at the forefront for fighting the challenges they are having. (KI, man, food insecurity sector, ID KI-5)

Others described the importance of community education and consciousness-raising about the environmental and climate factors driving EWE:

One thing we have been doing … has been sensitization, especially on environment, on climate change, for people to know why 'ABCD' is happening, why lately we have delayed rainfall, or we have too much wind that cannot easily be contained and it is blowing off some kinds of houses. (KI, man, community development, ID KI-6)

A youth participant also stressed the importance of community dialogue to create safer environments for refugee girls and women:

We also need to provide security for ourselves in a way that we don’t jump on women when they are accessing the [water] tap. We as the community, we need to have meetings, we need to alert the community with the rights and obligations of both male and female, you need to promote equal treatment here. (man, age 18, ID 04-02-08)

Another recommendation was to foster dialogue between the refugee and host (Ugandan) communities to encourage resource sharing:

We have our peace building program that targets basically host communities and the refugees so that we engage them in dialogue meetings, because the more we engage the host community in meetings and dialogues, the more they will be able to share resources among themselves, resources like firewood, resources like land and other things. (KI, man, food insecurity sector, KI-10)

Second, participants emphasized the need for organized collective action to address material conditions that lead to resource insecurity. For instance, a youth recommended collective action to clean water collection points:

Based on the sanitation, we as refugees, we can put our hands together and clear the bush around the borehole and leave it to be clean. We need also to fence our borehole to avoid the stray animals from just coming to drink water anyhow. (man, age 18, ID 03-02-06)

Another example was a recommendation for village savings and loans associations to mitigate the impact of drought and flooding on agricultural production.

Strengthening household relations

Participants highlighted family and household initiatives to address resource insecurity. One example provided was families working together to increase their household income to improve food security:

For the girls it depends according to the arrangement made in the family, especially in our home when we are going for these types of jobs which we call Lejaleja, we go together with my sisters, so we work there as a family, after getting the money we come and buy food. (man, age 18, ID 04-02-08)

Participants described using their own food resources, such as the goods provided through the food distribution points (Fig. 7) to sell and purchase resources for farming and gardening to help their families strengthen livelihoods and food insecurity:

When we receive food from the FDP, we sell part of that food, and then we use that money to buy the seeds that we plant, expecting to get some good output, and then sell it to get profits. (woman, age 21, ID 04-01-02)

caption: “When we receive food from the FDP [food distribution point], we sell part of that food, and then we use that money to buy the seeds that we plant.” (woman, age 21, ID 04-01-02)

Participants explained how mutual support was enacted between households as a response to resource shortages:

If you have three jerricans then when you miss to get water, you go to your friend then you say that give me at least a half jerrycan or one jerrycan so that I can go and use it. (KI, woman, refugee sector, KI-7)

Collectively, the themes underscore how EWE-related resource insecurities intensified psychosocial distress among refugee youth in Bidi Bidi. Environmental stressors, including drought and flooding, intersected with structural (e.g. poverty) and social (e.g. inequitable gender norms) vulnerabilities to harm mental wellbeing and erode community cohesion. Participants also identified multi-level (infrastructure, community, household) strategies for coping with these stressors.

Discussion

We explored lived experiences of extreme weather events (EWE), resource insecurities, and psychosocial stressors among refugee youth in Bidi Bidi. We identified four interconnected themes: material deprivation and uncertainty; shame of social failure; interpersonal conflict and multi-level violence; and coping and asset management strategies. Together, findings illustrate how environmental shocks converge with chronic food, water, and sanitation insecurities to produce psychosocial stress and community conflict in protracted displacement. Findings can inform youth-centred strategies for reducing psychosocial distress in resource-constrained humanitarian settings.

Our findings corroborate research showing that food and water insecurity exacerbate psychosocial distress in Ugandan humanitarian (Admassu et al., 2024; Logie et al., 2022; Logie et al., 2023b; Meyer et al., 2019) and non-humanitarian (Cooper-Vince et al., 2018; Mushavi et al., 2020) settings and other African settings (Kimutai et al., 2023; Smith et al., 2023). We extend this evidence by demonstrating how drought and flooding intensify existing resource insecurities amid declining humanitarian aid (Do et al., 2020; Simonsen Nilsen et al., 2024). Participants linked these insecurities to stress, hopelessness, helplessness, emotional suffering, and in some cases, suicide. Seasonal patterns of drought and flood-related crop failure amplified uncertainty and chronic precarity, aligning with the resource insecurity framework's pathways to psychosocial distress through material deprivation and uncertainty (Wutich et al., 2020; Wutich and Brewis, 2014), and Bisung and Elliott’s (2017) discussion of WASH-related financial stressors.

Sanitation insecurity generated distress through the inability to meet hygiene norms, particularly for adolescent girls and young women who missed school due to the inability to bathe and/or manage menstruation. This aligns with evidence that EWE disrupt sexual and reproductive self care, including menstruation management (Logie et al., 2025a, Logie et al., 2025c; Narasimhan et al., 2023), and supports research linking menstruation insecurity with school absenteeism (Belay et al., 2020; Miiro et al., 2018; Sommer et al., 2018). Our findings highlight mechanisms by which environmental stressors contribute to lower educational attainment, particularly among girls (Helldén et al., 2021) and underscore the need for peer-based support strategies in schools and communities to destigmatize menstruation (Narasimhan et al., 2025) and EWE-tailored strategies to meet menstruation management needs in LMIC humanitarian settings (Hennegan et al., 2019; Logie et al., 2024c; Robinson and Barrington, 2021; Winkler and Satterthwaite, 2017). Findings align with the resource insecurity framework's pathway to psychosocial stressors through shame of social failure, and Bisung and Elliot’s (2017) discussion of physical-related WASH stressors.

Participants also discussed intersections between unemployment, unmet material needs, and problematic substance use, which were often framed through the lens of shame and social failure. For instance, substance-use related coping was usually discussed among men, suggesting a gender difference in how social failure and shame may be experienced. This aligns with the resource insecurity framework's discussion of job loss and poverty as causing distress and social marginalization (Wutich et al., 2020). Prior research similarly linked poverty in humanitarian settings with internalized stigma, social isolation (Purgato et al., 2018), and harmful coping practices such as problematic alcohol use (Tol et al., 2013).

Resource insecurities contributed to community and household conflict and violence, which intensified during the dry season and during water scarcity. Quarrels and fighting at community water points, increased water collection burden on girls, and heightened IPV reflect pathways from resource insecurity to interpersonal conflict (Wutich et al., 2020) and Bisung and Elliot’s (2017) discussion of social stressors. These findings also build on evidence of increased household conflict and IPV during times of water and food insecurity among youth in Ugandan humanitarian settings (Logie et al., 2021; Logie et al., 2024b). Community-level conflict, including theft of crops, food reserves, livestock, and infrastructure, further demonstrate how resource insecurities can fuel social tensions and divisions. Our findings also highlight the need for gender-transformative and youth-tailored strategies (Mannell et al., 2019) to reduce community and household violence during periods of resource scarcity. For example, Narasimhan et al. (2025) highlight several self-care solutions for advancing sexual and reproductive health during EWE-related water shortages, including organizing large groups to fetch water, involving men in water collection efforts, and educating the community about the sexual and gender-based violence risks that water collection poses for young women and girls.

Despite these EWE and resource insecurity-related stressors, participants demonstrated resilience and shared examples of diverse coping and adaption strategies, including strengthening environmental infrastructure (e.g. gardens, latrines) and building social capital (e.g., community education, dialogue, collective action). These approaches reflect Connerton and Wooton's (2019) adaptive coping mechanisms of education and sensitization, institutional and community resilience, and building infrastructure; asset vulnerability framework domains (Moser, 1998); and Mugeere et al.'s (2021) discussion of climate change education and sensitization with Ugandan youth. These findings also align with calls for engaging adolescents and youth in Sub-Saharan Africa in climate education and leadership (Gaithuma et al., 2025). Household-level strategies such as generating additional income and resources, pooling family income, and mutual sharing with neighbours align with asset vulnerability (Moser, 1998) and resource scarcity framework (Wutich and Brewis, 2014) approaches to engaging households in alternative income, resource generation, and neighbourhood sharing to meet resource needs. These findings also corroborate Balakrishnan et al.'s (2024) discussion of informal support and care systems with neighbours to cope with the mental, physical, and economic outcomes of EWE. Future research can explore whether these strategies can mitigate refugee youths' psychosocial distress.

Our findings signal the utility of applying the resource insecurity framework (Wutich et al., 2020) to understand how multiple co-occurring resource insecurities shape refugee youths’ psychosocial stressors, and highlight the value of exploring asset-based approaches in humanitarian contexts (Moser, 1998). Disaster resilience perspectives offer insight into the role of communities in using local knowledge and adaptive capacities to pro-actively prepare to face crises and disasters (Manyena, 2006). Future research can meaningfully engage refugee youth in co-creating multi-level climate resilient and disaster risk reduction strategies to promote multi-systemic resilience and reduce psychosocial stressors (Gaithuma et al., 2025; Ungar, 2021).

Strengths and limitations

Limitations include non-random purposive sampling that may have led to overrepresentation of youth connected with community collaborator programs and resources, and perhaps less resource insecure youth. Walk-along interviews may have constrained discussion on psychosocial issues and other stigmatized topics in public spaces, yet interviewers attempted to mitigate these limitations by conducting interviews in places with privacy. As the focus was not on mental health, we did not explore all facets of mental health and substance use or diagnostic frameworks. However our findings align with Ugandan conceptualizations of mental health that focus on social mechanisms, psychosocial/emotional suffering, and community cohesion (Knizek et al., 2024; Lee et al., 2025). The convergence of EWE, conflict, and international aid cuts faced by LMIC humanitarian settings reflect a polycrisis that can amplify harmful impacts via complex feedback loops (Lawrence et al., 2024), making it difficult to isolate contributors to psychosocial stress. Future quantitative research could apply a syndemics lens to examine the interactions between social, environmental, and health stressors (Tsai, 2018). The walk-along interview focused on understanding how youth access water, food, and sanitation, and how this access shifted with weather events and/or season, so we did not inquire about specific EWE. Future work could apply life-course approaches to generate insight into youths’ experiences with particular weather events.

Strengths include reflecting a range of perspectives from gender and age diverse refugee youth and KIs across diverse domains of practice with localized expertise. In addition, our integration of multiple qualitative methods, including walk-along interviews with photos, provide rich and contextualized data (O'Reilly et al., 2021) and we respond to calls for qualitative research on impacts of climate change on adolescent mental health and wellbeing (Helldén et al., 2021).

Conclusion

This study shared experiences regarding psychosocial stressors among refugee youth in Bidi Bidi, Uganda. Findings suggest the salience of a resource insecurity approach to consider the ways in which resource insecurities cause material deprivation and uncertainty, shame of social failure, and interpersonal conflict—in turn causing psychosocial stress. Our findings also signal the importance of nurturing multi-level coping and asset management strategies in LMIC refugee settlements such as Bidi Bidi and exploring their impact on psychosocial stressors among refugee youth. Meaningfully engaging refugee adolescents and youth in disaster resilience and risk reduction (Gaithuma et al., 2025) holds the potential to reduce poverty and stress and to strengthen rights, wellbeing, and adaptive capacity.
