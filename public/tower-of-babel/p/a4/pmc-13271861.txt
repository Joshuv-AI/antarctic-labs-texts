Association between stress hyperglycemia ratio and cognitive function in older patients with diabetes or prediabetes: a cross-sectional study from the NHANES
Authors: Luwen Zou, Weiguang Ye, Chao Li, Shuai Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
To explore the association between stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) levels and cognitive function in older patients with diabetes or prediabetes. Cognitive function was assessed through a composite Z-score, the Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s Disease (CERAD) subtest, the Animal Fluency Test (AFT), and the Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST). SHR was calculated as the fasting blood glucose divided by the estimated average glucose from glycated hemoglobin. In subsequent analyses, SHR was divided into quartiles (quartiles 1 to 4), with the second quartile serving as the reference group. A weighted linear regression model was used to assess the association between SHR and cognitive function. After adjusting the corresponding covariates, the analysis revealed that compared to the second quartile, the first [β: –0.26; 95% confidence interval (CI): –0.39– –0.12], third (β: –0.13, 95% CI: –0.25– –0.02), and fourth (β: –0.21, 95% CI: –0.36– –0.06) quartiles were all associated with a decrease in Z-score. The first quartile was associated with a decline in AFT (β: –1.46, 95% CI: –2.57– –0.36). The first (β: –3.72, 95% CI: –6.06– –1.38) and fourth (β: –2.58, 95% CI: –4.98– –0.17) quartiles were associated with a decline in DSST. No statistically significant associations were observed between any of the quartile groups and CERAD (all p > 0.05). Both higher and lower SHR were associated with poorer overall cognitive function, with higher and lower SHR being related to worse memory dimensions, while lower SHR was associated with impaired executive function.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment (CI) refers to a broad loss or reduction in cognitive functions, including memory, language, logic, attention, problem-solving, executive functions, and visuospatial skills [1]. According to estimates, the number of individuals with CI is projected to increase from an estimated 18.3 million in 2020 to 35.4 million by 2060 in the United States [2]. As the global population ages, cognitive dysfunction is becoming a significant public health issue, as it severely threatens the quality of life and health of the old people and imposes a heavy burden on society [3]. Glucose metabolism is closely related to brain tissue activity [4]. Abnormal glucose metabolism is associated with a decline in cognitive function [5]. The brain is highly susceptible to fluctuations in blood glucose levels, with both hypoglycemia and hyperglycemia potentially leading to neurological damage [6]. The evidence indicates that the prevalence of CI and dementia is significantly higher in individuals with diabetes and prediabetes compared to those with normal glucose tolerance [7, 8].

Stress hyperglycemia (SH) is a common physiological state that typically occurs during acute stress events, characterized by a transient increase in blood glucose levels due to physiological or psychological stress [9, 10]. The occurrence of the aforementioned phenomenon is often inseparable from the regulation of hormone secretion, immune responses, and neural activity [11]. Mild to moderate SH serves a certain protective role in the functioning of the body. On one hand, mild to moderate SH provides essential fuel for the immune system and the brain during stress [12]. On the other hand, mild to moderate SH can prevent and avoid pathological damage by inhibiting apoptosis, accelerating angiogenesis, and increasing plasticity [11]. However, the generation of additional hyperglycemia or sustained hyperglycemia may lead to side effects. Excessively high blood glucose levels can induce oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, hypo-perfusion, promote the release of pro-inflammatory factors, and damage the blood-brain barrier, thereby inducing neuro-inflammation [13]. In addition, stress hypoglycemia or relative hypoglycemia may lead to adverse outcomes such as cerebrovascular events due to the accumulation of advanced glycation end products (AGEs) in the later stages [14]. Hypoglycemia can also lead to oxidative stress and the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines [15, 16].

To avoid misestimating the true prevalence of SH, a stress hyperglycemia ratio (SHR) calculated using fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) has been proposed [17]. This approach aims to minimize the impact of long-term chronic blood glucose factors on stress-induced hyperglycemic levels [17]. The prognostic significance of the SHR in acute and critical care has been frequently documented [18-20]. Recent research suggests a “U-shaped” relationship between SHR and the risks of all-cause mortality and renal dysfunction in patients with diabetes or prediabetes, where both low and high SHR levels are significantly associated with higher mortality and increased prevalence of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [21, 22]. In old hospitalized patients, SHR values that are either too low or too high are also linked to an increased risk of delirium [23]. Moreover, in patients with diabetes who have experienced an acute ischemic stroke, a high SHR has been found to correlate with a decline in cognitive function [24]. Nonetheless, the association between SHR and CI in the old population is still not well-established. Particularly in patients with diabetes or prediabetes, whether SHR can serve as a potential marker for the decline in cognitive function has not yet been sufficiently confirmed.

In the present study, we therefore contributed to utilizing the large National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) database to investigate the association between SHR levels and cognitive function in older adults with diabetes or prediabetes.

Materials and Methods

Study design

NHANES is an ongoing research program conducted under the guidance of the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) in the United States, designed to assess the health and nutritional status of the Unite States adult and child population. The uniqueness of the NHANES survey lies in its combination of household interviews and physical examinations, collecting a vast array of information from a nationally representative sample drawn annually from across the country [25]. This study was a cross-sectional analysis, with data sourced from the NHANES database spanning from 2011 to 2014. It was noteworthy that all data utilized in our study were derived from a public database, which can be freely accessed via the following link: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Nhanes/.

Study population

The present study extracted records from the NHANES database for patients aged 60 years or older with diabetes or prediabetes (n = 2,562). From the above, we excluded patient records missing information on FPG (n = 1,197), HbA1c (n = 3), Consortium to Establish a Registry for Alzheimer’s disease (CERAD) test data (n = 126), Animal Fluency Test (AFT) data (n = 15), and Digit Symbol Substitution Test (DSST) data (n = 58). Ultimately, we identified 1,163 patients aged 60 or older with diabetes or prediabetes who had both cognitive function test data and diabetes laboratory test data. The specific flow chart of the study population selection is shown in Fig. 1. Additionally, the determination of diabetes and prediabetes was based on the 2021 American Diabetes Association (ADA) guidelines [26, 27]. The diagnostic criteria for diabetes were FPG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L or 2-hour postprandial blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L or HbA1c ≥ 6.5% or self-reported diabetes or those receiving hypoglycemic treatment. The diagnostic criteria for prediabetes were FPG ranging from 5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L or 2-hour postprandial blood glucose ranging from 7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L or HbA1c ranging from 5.7% to 6.4% or self-reported prediabetes. Hypoglycemic treatment was defined as self-reported use of insulin (DIQ050) or hypoglycemic drugs (DIQ070 or hypoglycemic drug code 358-metabolic agents-99-antidiabetic agent).

Evaluation of SHR

The calculation of SHR was conducted based on the following formula [17].

SHR = [FPG (mmol/L)] ÷ [1.59 * HbA1c (%) – 2.59]

All patients were categorized into quartiles of SHR: First quartile (Q1) (SHR < 0.825), second quartile (Q2) (SHR: 0.825–0.903), third quartile (Q3) (SHR: 0.903–0.985), and fourth quartile (Q4) (SHR ≥ 0.985), with Q2, the median group, serving as the reference group. Since the participants in each NHANES survey cycle are different, the FPG and HbA1c measurements within each survey period are independent.

Definition of outcomes

The cognitive function evaluation across three dimensions—memory function (CERAD score), executive function (AFT score), and attention and psychomotor speed (DSST score)—along with the composite total cognitive function (Z-score) derived from these assessments, constitutes the four primary outcome variables. CERAD was a test designed to assess immediate and delayed recall of new verbal information in humans [28]. The CERAD consisted of three consecutive learning trials and a delayed recall trial. During the learning trials, participants were instructed to read aloud 10 unrelated words, one at a time. After the presentation of the words, participants were asked to immediately recall as many words as possible. In the three learning trials, the order of the 10 words was changed in each trial. The maximum score for each trial was 10. The total score for the CERAD was the sum of the three learning trials and the delayed recall trial. The AFT was a measure of categorical verbal fluency [29]. Participants were instructed to name as many animals as possible within a one-minute timeframe. The score of AFT was the total number of correct responses provided. The DSST assessed processing speed, sustained attention, and working memory [30]. The test was conducted using a paper form that featured a key with nine numbers paired with symbols. Participants were required to match the corresponding symbols in 133 boxes adjacent to the numbers within two minutes. The score was the total number of correct matches made. Global cognition score was calculated based on all test scores (CERAD, AFT, and DSST), represented by the Z-score, with higher values indicating better cognitive performance. The Z-score was calculated as [(individual test score – mean score) ÷ standard deviation (SD)] of these three individual tests (DSST, AFT, CERAD).

Study covariates

All demographic information, survey questionnaires, and laboratory examination data were obtained from the NHANES website. The following covariates were acquired: age (years), gender (male/female), race (non-Hispanic white/non-Hispanic black/Mexican American/other race), education level (high school and below/above high school), marital status (married/living with partner or single/separated/divorced/widowed), poverty income ratio (PIR) (<1.3 or 1.3–3.5 or >3.5), smoking (never/ever/now), heavy drinking (yes/no/unknown), physical activity (PA) [<600 metabolic equivalent (MET)·min/week or ≥600 MET·min/week or unknown], CKD (yes/no), hypertension (yes/no), dyslipidemia (yes/no), cardiovascular disease (CVD) (yes/no), depression (yes/no), body mass index (BMI) (<25 kg/m2 or ≥25 kg/m2), stroke (yes/no), anemia (yes/no), healthy eating index-2015 (HEI-2015) (score), anti-diabetic medication (yes/no), anti-Parkinson medication (yes/no), anti-anxiety medication (yes/no), sedatives and hypnotics use (yes/no), psychotherapy medication (yes/no), and total energy (kcal). Specific definitions or criteria for each covariate are provided in the supporting materials (Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Quantitative data were described as mean [±standard error (S.E)], and comparisons between two groups were made using weighted variance analysis. Count data were described as frequency and percentage [n (%)], and group comparisons were made using the Rao-Scott Chi-square test. Given the existence of missing data, the variables with missing information were imputed using the chained equations multiple imputation method from random forests. Supplementary Table 2 displays the outcomes of the sensitivity analysis comparing the data before and following imputation, assessing for any statistical discrepancies between the two datasets.

The NHANES database employed a diverse sampling methodology. Consequently, as per the guidelines, it was necessary to apply sample weighting when utilizing data from NHANES (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/tutorials/Weighting.aspx). The weighting variables included were SDMVPSU, SDMVSTRA, and WTSAF2YR. A weighted univariate linear regression model was initially employed to screen for potential confounding factors (Supplementary Tables 3–6). Subsequently, variables with statistically significant associations (p < 0.05) were included in a backward stepwise regression analysis, retaining those with p-values still less than 0.05 as the final covariates. The final covariates included in the Z-score outcome model were age, gender, race, PIR, education, antidiabetic drug use, depression, CKD, and total energy. For the CERAD outcome, the covariates included were age, gender, race, education, hypertension, and anemia. The AFT outcome model included age, race, education, PA, antidiabetic drug use, depression, and antiparkinsonian agents as covariates. The DSST outcome model comprised covariates including age, gender, race, PIR, education, antidiabetic drug use, depression, CKD, psychotherapeutic agents, and total energy. The specific process for the selection of confounding factors for each of the four outcome variables is detailed in the supplementary materials. A weighted multivariable linear regression analysis was conducted to explore the association between SHR and cognitive function. Additionally, subgroup analyses were performed according to age, gender, and diabetes/prediabetes status to further investigate whether the impact of SHR on cognitive function varies across different populations. The evaluation metric was β [95% confidence interval (CI)]. The non-linear relationships of SHR with different cognitive function scores (CERAD, AFT, DSST, Z-score) were evaluated by the restricted cubic spline (RCS) curves.

All analytical processes were completed using R version 4.3.3. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with a confidence level of alpha = 0.05, and a p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Association between SHR and various dimensions of cognitive function

Z-score

Table 2 presents the results of the association between SHR and overall cognitive function. Using SHR = 0.825–0.903 (Q2) as the reference group, without adjusting for any covariates, only the SHR < 0.825 group showed a statistically significant association with Z-score (β: –0.42, 95% CI: –0.65– –0.20). However, after adjusting for covariates including age, gender, race, PIR, education, antidiabetic drug use, depression, CKD, and total energy, the other groups were all associated with a decrease in Z-score. The β, 95% CI for Q1, Q3, and Q4 were –0.26 (–0.39– –0.12), –0.13 (–0.25– –0.02), and –0.21 (–0.36 – –0.06), respectively (all p < 0.05).

CERAD

The association between SHR and CERAD is delineated in Table 2. As can be seen from the table, regardless of whether covariates were adjusted or not, there were no statistically significant differences across all groups (all p > 0.05).

AFT

Using Q2 as the reference group, SHR < 0.825 (Q1) was associated with a decline in cognitive function as measured by AFT, without adjusting for any covariates (β: –2.15, 95% CI: –3.55– –0.75). The above association persisted after adjusting for covariates including age, race, education, PA, antidiabetic drug use, depression, and antiparkinsonian agents (β: –1.46, 95% CI: –2.57– –0.36) (Table 2).

DSST

Table 2 also displays the weighted linear regression model for the association between SHR and DSST. Discussion

This study investigated the association between SHR and cognitive function in older patients with diabetes or prediabetes using a range of cognitive function assessment tools across different dimensions. The results indicated that higher and lower SHR were associated with poorer total cognitive function (Z-score). Higher and lower SHR were linked to lower attention and psychomotor speed (DSST score), and lower SHR was associated with a decline in executive function (AFT). No statistically significant association was observed between SHR and memory function (CERAD score). Additionally, subgroup analyses showed that lower SHR was associated with a decrease in Z-score across various populations.

Among patients with diabetes or prediabetes, several studies have found that both elevated and reduced SHR indicate poor prognoses for patients [21, 22, 31]. Regarding cognitive function, numerous studies have confirmed a negative correlation between hyperglycemia and CI, accelerating the progression of cognitive dysfunction [32-34]. In a cohort study by Song et al., both lower and higher SHR were associated with an increased risk of delirium in elderly hospitalized patients [23]. However, few studies have investigated the relationship between SHR and cognitive function, particularly in elderly patients with diabetes or prediabetes. Our results provided the relationship between SHR and various dimensions of cognitive function. For different cognitive function dimensions, higher and lower SHR were related to poorer attention and psychomotor speed, while only lower SHR was associated with impaired executive function, and the relationship between SHR and memory was not statistically significant. The phenomenon suggested that the fluctuations in blood glucose levels reflected by SHR may impact different dimensions of cognitive function through certain specific pathways [35]. However, our study was merely a preliminary exploration, and the validity of the above inference would require more rigorous and high-quality research in the future to validate.

SHR reflects significant fluctuations in blood glucose levels during severe illness or acute stress events [10]. Furthermore, in the current study population, SHR may reflect glycemic disparity (i.e., inconsistency between FPG and HbA1c) or chronic glycemic variability. The association between SHR and CI is complex. Although there is no definitive biological mechanism, several potential pathways of influence have been reported. Chronic hyperglycemia may accelerate neurodegenerative pathology through oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, increased inflammatory responses, and disruption of the blood-brain barrier [36]. Hyperglycemia compromises the blood-brain barrier, increasing its permeability and facilitating the entry of damaging proteins, inflammatory mediators, and hematogenous amyloid-β peptides into the brain [37]. Elevated blood glucose levels indicate an over-activation of glucose metabolism, which is accompanied by a decrease in endogenous antioxidant levels and an increase in free radical production [38, 39]. The increased production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) exacerbates the antioxidant challenge in organ tissues, resulting in heightened oxidative stress in neuronal cells and subsequent brain functional impairment [40]. Secondly, the impact of SHR on cognitive function may also be attributed to inflammatory responses induced by blood glucose fluctuations [41]. Hyperglycemia is known to elicit neuro-inflammatory responses [42]. An increase in extracellular glucose levels enhances the formation of AGEs, which subsequently elicit inflammatory responses within neurons [43]. Additionally, hyperglycemia can lead to endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, thereby disrupting cellular protein homeostasis [44-46]. On one hand, ER stress can trigger neuronal apoptosis by activating caspases; on the other hand, mitochondrial dysfunction, while reducing adenosine triphosphate (ATP) production, also accelerates the generation of ROS, creating a vicious cycle that exacerbates oxidative damage to neurons [47]. Hyperglycemia can directly damage myelin sheaths, leading to neuro-signaling dysfunction; it can also indirectly participate in macrovascular and microvascular changes within neurons. In addition, glycemic variability is related to an increased risk of microvascular and macrovascular complications, as well as an increased all-cause mortality [48]. Vascular complications may increase the risk of cognitive impairment through mechanisms such as direct tissue damage (e.g., macroscopic infarction, microinfarcts, white matter lesions), neuronal atrophy, alterations in oligodendrocytes and astrocytes within white matter, and localized inflammation [49, 50].

A low SHR in the current population may reflect postprandial hyperglycemia or frailty/malnutrition. Postprandial hyperglycemia is an important influencing factor of HbA1c [51], while malnutrition is related to hypoglycemia [52]. Postprandial hyperglycemia has been shown to induce endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and increased oxidative stress [53]. Moreover, tissues in a hypoglycemic state can be subject to oxidative stress and inflammation damage [15]. As previously mentioned, abnormal inflammation and oxidative stress conditions can lead to damage to neural cells. Research conducted by Kahal among patients with type 2 diabetes indicates that hypoglycemia leads to a significant increase in inflammatory and oxidative stress markers among participants [15]. Some studies indicate that compared with those who have never experienced hypoglycemic events, individuals with hypoglycemia have a significantly increased risk of dementia [54, 55]. Severe hypoglycemic episodes are related to significant neuronal damage in the cortex and hippocampus [56, 57]. Hypoglycemia is also correlated with diffuse neuronal death, which may be caused by excitotoxicity, oxidative stress, polymerase-1 activation, and mitochondrial release of proapoptotic molecules [58]. Furthermore, there may be a bidirectional relationship between cognitive impairment and blood glucose levels. Individuals experiencing hypoglycemic episodes exhibit a significantly increased risk of dementia, while those with dementia also face a markedly higher risk of hypoglycemia [59]. Cognitive impairment may interfere with self-monitoring of blood glucose and achieving glycemic control [60, 61].

SHR, as a relatively accessible biological marker, may provide valuable early warning signals for the cognitive health of elderly patients with diabetes or prediabetes [21]. Identifying patients with low or high SHR in clinical practice may aid in the early detection of the risk of cognitive decline [23]. However, there is currently no specific medical reference range and threshold value for SHR [14]. Future research can further explore the causal relationship between SHR and cognitive function, investigate the clinical application potential of SHR in patients with different types of diabetes, and work towards determining the optimal reference range for SHR. Additionally, more clinical trials could be conducted to evaluate whether modulating SHR, such as through blood glucose management and stress response interventions, can effectively improve cognitive function in elderly diabetic patients, thereby delaying or reducing the occurrence of CI.

The limitations of our study should also be promptly acknowledged. Firstly, due to its cross-sectional design, the present study cannot infer causality from its conclusions. Secondly, although we included a variety of factors that could potentially impact the results, limitations of the database meant that certain confounders, such as detailed information on medication treatments, were not included in the analysis. Therefore, future large-scale prospective studies, randomized controlled trials, and toxicological research are needed to validate the relationship between SHR and cognitive dysfunction, as well as to explore its exact underlying mechanisms. Thirdly, during the screening process of the study population, many patients were excluded due to missing FPG data, which may introduce selection bias.

Higher and lower SHR were associated with a decline in overall cognitive function in elderly patients with diabetes or prediabetes, with higher and lower SHR linked to poorer attention and psychomotor speed, and lower SHR associated with impaired executive function.
