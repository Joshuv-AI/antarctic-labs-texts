A universal scaling framework for granular asteroid strength and its application to the surface of asteroid Bennu
Authors: Paul Sánchez, Emilien Azéma, Keanna Jardine, Christian G Hoover, Jens Biele, Andrew J Ryan, Ronald-L Ballouz, Robert J Macke, Maurizio Pajola, Harold C Connolly Jr, Dante S Lauretta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGranular asteroids exhibit surprising structural stability despite low gravity and high spin rates. Understanding the mechanisms behind their strength is essential for predicting their evolution and informing deflection strategies. Using 3D particle-dynamics simulations, we develop a scaling framework for the tensile strength of cohesive, self-gravitating regolith, examining how particle size and shape jointly control macroscopic behavior. We show that tensile strength can be scaled using either an equivalent particle diameter or particle shape, the latter through sphericity and its reference to an equivalent spherical packing. We apply this model to asteroid Bennu using data from samples returned by the OSIRIS-REx mission and derive a cohesive surface strength consistent with estimates below 1 Pa from remote sensing and sampling-event reconstructions. Our results indicate that Bennu’s extremely low strength arises from a scarcity of fine dust in its regolith. This study demonstrates how particle-level properties govern large-scale stability and heterogeneity in granular asteroids.Subject terms: Asteroids, comets and Kuiper belt; Planetary science

Introduction

Astronomical observations and recent space missions have revealed that at least some asteroids are not solid monoliths but rather loosely bound agglomerates1–3, primarily held together by their self-gravity, with additional contributions from cohesive and adhesive forces. Examples include the carbonaceous asteroids (101955) Bennu (e.g.,4) and (162173) Ryugu5, the stony asteroid (25143) Itokawa6, as well as asteroids Didymos and Dimorphos7, all of which are less than 1 km in diameter. These asteroids are best understood as self-gravitating granular systems, which are large assemblies of discrete solid particles—ranging from dust to boulders—whose overall structure and shape are determined by the interplay of gravity, interparticle forces, and the body’s spin state. This definition expands on the traditional understanding of granular materials, as outlined by ref. 8, and applies it to a planetary context.

Under this definition, every asteroid is a naturally occurring self-gravitating granular system9,10; however, for clarity in this work, we use the term “granular asteroid” to refer specifically to asteroids where both the interior and surface are composed of particles that follow cumulative size distributions that reflect a history of catastrophic disruption and subsequent gravitational reaccumulation, resulting in a body whose interior is thoroughly fragmented rather than dominated by a single large monolithic core11. This definition is closely related to, but somewhat stricter than, the commonly used term “rubble-pile asteroid”12. While all rubble-pile asteroids are granular by definition, our usage emphasizes bodies whose fragmental nature extends throughout, shaped by collisional disruption rather than geological processes.

Because the only direct information available about asteroids’ structure comes from their surface morphology, shape, and spin state, bulk properties have been inferred indirectly—either from external gravitational fields13,14 or, in some cases, from the disruption patterns of active asteroids15,16. This granular nature raises fundamental questions about their mechanical stability and strength.

The need to understand the strength of granular asteroids became evident when observations showed that some small asteroids (<150 m) exhibit spin rates exceeding the limit imposed by self-gravity alone17, suggesting the presence of additional forces contributing to their tensile and cohesive strengths. If these bodies are not monolithic, there is another source of this strength that must be identified. For more than a decade, researchers have investigated the hypothesis that van der Waals (VdW) forces play a key role in asteroid cohesion18,19. These forces, in a low-gravity environment (ranging from 10−3g to 10−6g), are comparable to the weight of millimeter- to centimeter-sized particles. Theoretical calculations have shown that the required cohesive strength to avoid rotational disruption can be up to 300 Pa for super-fast rotators (rotation period <1 min). Fast rotators (rotation period <2.3 h), such as asteroid (29075) 1950 DA, would require approximately 64 Pa20.

At the same time, the internal structure of these asteroids remains a subject of active debate16,21–23. Early models assumed granular asteroids (rubble-piles) to be homogeneous and strengthless, held together purely by self-gravity24. Later numerical studies challenged this view, suggesting the existence of some shear strength25 and layered structures16,21 without assigning them an origin. Later on, these structures were predicted to be driven by non-catastrophic impacts26 and supported by observational evidence from Ryugu23 and Bennu22. If (i) the strength of granular matter in microgravity conditions depends on the specific arrangement of particles (granular microstructure) and their interactions, and (ii) this microstructure changes, as observed, between the hypothesized subsurface layers, then factors, such as particle arrangement, particle shape, particle size distribution, and particle–particle VdW forces19 become crucial in determining the bulk mechanical behavior. A subset of these specific material properties can only be constrained by laboratory analysis of returned samples.

The OSIRIS-REx mission’s touch-and-go (TAG) sampling of regolith from Bennu took place on October 20, 202027. The boulders on the surface of the asteroid were observed to have two main morphologies: angular and rounded28. The sampling maneuver consisted of penetrating the regolith with an articulated, approximately 30-cm-wide cylindrical sampling device, the touch-and-go sample acquisition mechanism (TAGSAM)29. Thus, TAG also served as a microgravity penetration experiment into a granular medium. Reconstruction of the TAG event by ref. 30 enabled an estimation of <1 Pa for the cohesive strength of the sampling site, a crater called Hokioi located in Bennu’s northern hemisphere31. This estimate was consistent with other assessments of Bennu’s surface strength based on remote sensing analysis and modeling27,31,32. The Bennu samples were delivered to Earth on September 24, 2023, and analyses are underway, including physical and thermal characterization33. Similar to the rocks and boulders observed on the surface of Bennu, the particles of the returned sample were subdivided into groups, though based primarily on morphology. Three morphologies were identified: angular, hummocky, and mottled33. The physical and mineralogical characteristics of the samples, such as their total volume and the variation in particle sizes, shapes, chemical composition, and cohesive forces, enable, for the first time, the style of analysis presented here for an asteroid.

The relationship between macroscopic tensile strength (or, more generally, cohesive strength in the Mohr-Coulomb sense), local adhesion, and granular disorder has been a longstanding topic in granular physics34,35. The relationship between σT and d2, often referred to as the “Rumpf formula”38, has been revisited and validated across various works, emphasizing its robustness36,39–41. At the microscale, adhesion forces cause particles to agglomerate into clusters whose size depends on local adhesion and system dynamics42, generating a non-homogeneous, bipolar (parallel or perpendicular to the stress direction) network of self-stresses and interwoven chains of tensile and compressive forces43.

Most research on cohesive granular materials has traditionally focused on idealized systems composed of nearly monodisperse, spherical particles, primarily due to the simplicity of modeling and experimental control. Asteroid research is no exception. However, real granular materials—including those found in extraterrestrial environments—rarely conform to this idealization. Further studies increasingly highlighted the role of particle shape44,45 and surface roughness46, revealing nonlinear effects on macroscopic friction and underlining microstructure47–50. In particular, the inclusion of surface roughness in cohesive granular systems makes f0 a constant, independent of particle size51. Despite these advances, only a limited number of studies36,52,53 have directly addressed how particle shape influences cohesive strength in granular media.

This gap is particularly critical for asteroids, where cohesive forces, particle arrangement, and gravitational stresses are intrinsically coupled. At the grain scale, cohesive forces generate an intrinsic tensile strength, while at the scale of the entire aggregate, self-gravity imposes a confining stress that depends on size, shape, and bulk density. Each mechanism operates on characteristic stress and temporal scales, and their balance governs the stability, strength, and failure modes of the body. As highlighted in ref. 40, these effects can be combined within a unified, dimensionless framework that clarifies how cohesive forces, gravity, and dynamics compete or reinforce one another. This competition is particularly critical in low-gravity environments where fine particles may form a weakly cohesive matrix that binds larger boulders and enhances overall stability19. At the same time, realistic regolith particles—often angular or irregular—can exhibit multiple contact points, increasing particle–particle adhesion while restricting rotations and altering the interplay between cohesive forces and gravitational confinement.

In this paper, we test this hypothesis by exploring the role of regolith shape and size on the tensile behavior of a self-gravitating cohesive regolith matrix confined between two large boulders, by means of discrete element modeling. The regolith is modeled as polyhedral particles with varying aspect ratios, ranging from rounded to highly flattened or elongated shapes. The size of the regolith particles within the cohesive matrix is also varied. Our results present evidence that shows that the tensile strength of the resulting matrix can be predicted from the geometrical properties of the regolith. More specifically, tensile strength increases (following a power law) as the shape of the regolith deviates from spherical, and decreases (also following a power law) as the size of the regolith particles increases. If we suppose that these material characteristics are not very different from those of the particles in the interior of asteroid Bennu, that the segregation models are correct, and that the observations are indeed supporting the latter, it should be possible to estimate the tensile strength of the interior of the asteroid, based on the measurements made on particles extracted from the surface.

Once this model is developed and refined, we apply it to estimate the cohesive strength of Bennu’s surface using our current best knowledge of the Bennu samples’ physical properties. We compare these estimates anchored by ground-truth sample data to those from remote sensing observations and TAG reconstruction. In doing so, we are able to better understand Bennu’s near-surface physical properties and contextualize the outcome of our work with respect to other self-gravitating granular systems.

In this work, we offer a systematic, universal framework to study regolith in the space environment. By including the effects of realistic regolith particle shapes, we improve on granular-physics-based formulations of regolith strength for low-gravity and self-gravitating systems.

Results

Particle dynamics simulations of the failure of a granular bridge

We adopt the granular bridge protocol19, in which cohesive regolith particles form a self-sustained matrix between two large boulders. This setup provides a simplified yet physically relevant model to investigate the tensile strength of regolith and its role in maintaining structural integrity in asteroid environments. By employing extensive 3D discrete element method (DEM) simulations (“Methods”), we analyze the failure behavior of granular bridges composed of angular, cohesive particles of varying shapes and sizes, confined between two 1-m-diameter spherical boulders (Fig. 1a). These bridges, akin to liquid bridges in wet granular media, are generated following a rigorous procedure (“Methods”).

To evaluate the influence of particle shape, we construct bridges using either spherical or polyhedral particles with diameter d (Fig. 1). The polyhedral particles are dodecahedral, but their shapes are characterized by two aspect ratios, α1 = b/a and α2 = c/a, where a = d denotes the major axis of the encompassing ellipsoid (equivalent to the sphere diameter d used for spherical particles), and b and c are the minor axes. We systematically explore ten shape configurations with (α1, α2) ∈ {(1, 1); (1, 0.8); (1, 0.6); (1, 0.4); (0.8, 0.8); (0.8, 0.6); (0.8, 0.4); (0.6, 0.6); (0.6, 0.4); (0.4, 0.4)}. Six assemblies are generated: three monodisperse (d = 2, 3, 4 cm) and three polydisperse (d ∈ [2, 3], [3, 4], [4, 5] cm). For spherical particles, three independent configurations per case yield 18 simulations. For polyhedral particles, the combination of ten aspect ratios across six configurations results in 60 systems, leading to a total of 78 simulated granular bridges (Fig. 1d for an illustration of tested polyhedral shapes). The monodisperse samples contain Np = 5082, 2373, and 1287 particles for d = 2, 3, and 4 cm, respectively, while the polydisperse systems, generated with a random size distribution, contain Np = 2373, 1287, and 684 for d ∈ [2, 3], [3, 4], [4, 5] cm, respectively.

Cohesion is modeled as a constant tensile force f0 acting at contact points (“Methods”), capturing VdW-like interactions relevant in low-gravity environments. Additionally, gravitational forces acting on individual particles and boulders are explicitly incorporated to ensure a realistic representation of regolith behavior in asteroid-like conditions.

The failure test consists of applying a tensile force fp to separate the two spherical boulders. This force is incrementally increased by 0.02Fg every 5 s (corresponding to a force doubling time of approximately 250 s), where Fg represents the total gravitational force acting on a boulder. This loading rate is slow relative to the characteristic timescale for particle rearrangements (tc is approximately d2ρ/f0), which is on the order of 24–120 s for the particle sizes considered, allowing most interparticle contacts to reach their tensile strength f0 before macroscopic failure, thus enabling an accurate measurement of the failure threshold.

Basically, the peak stress increases with particle flattening: as α2 decreases from 1 to 0.4, the maximum stress rises from approximately 0.0008 to 0.0024 Pa. Conversely, the peak stress decreases with increasing particle size, dropping from about 0.005 to 0.001 Pa as the mean particle diameter d¯ increases from 2.5 to 4.5 cm. Based on these curves, we define the tensile strength at failure, σT, as the maximum value of σ¯yy before the abrupt drop to zero, marking the rupture of the granular bridge.

These results highlight the dual influence of particle size and shape on the tensile strength of regolith-like granular material. However, a deeper understanding requires a more fundamental perspective. Is there a unifying description that captures these effects across different particle geometries? Can the observed variations in σT be explained through a more microscopic approach, considering contact network properties and force transmission mechanisms?

Scaling and fitting functions

A fundamental challenge in describing the tensile strength of cohesive granular materials lies in linking particle-scale interactions and macroscopic stress transmission. For spherical particles, the well-known Rumpf equation provides a direct link between the macroscopic tensile strength σT and microscopic parameters as

where ϕ is the solid fraction and Z is the coordination number (i.e., the average number of contacts per particle). This equation suggests that σT decreases as the inverse square of the particle size, while also depending on the packing fraction and connectivity of the contact network.

However, this formulation is strictly valid only for spherical particles. In the case of irregularly shaped particles—such as those found in regolith—the absence of a well-defined diameter makes it non-trivial to extend this scaling directly. Moreover, unlike spheres, polyhedral particles have complex surface geometries that influence their packing, coordination number, and force transmission in ways that are not captured by a single length scale. In particular, the contact network structure and the available surface area for force transmission differ substantially from the spherical case, making a direct application of the Rumpf equation difficult.

To overcome this limitation, we define an equivalent diameter, deq, as the diameter of a sphere with the same volume as a polyhedral particle. This choice is motivated by the fact that volume is a conserved quantity across different particle shapes and plays a crucial role in defining the force per unit area in granular materials; note that an apparent link between σT and the particle shape is observed in Fig. 3. However, replacing d with deq alone is not sufficient to generalize the Rumpf equation to non-spherical particles. A key additional refinement involves replacing the coordination number Z with the connectivity number, Zc, which accounts for the complex contact topologies arising in polyhedral packings (see “Methods”). Indeed, unlike spheres, polyhedral particles can interact through point, edge, or surface contacts, leading to a more intricate contact network (“Methods”). To test this hypothesis, we normalize σT by its value for spherical particles and plot the rescaled tensile strength as a function of ψ¯, the mean value of ψ, revealing that it captures the general trend of the data onto a single curve (Fig. 4b). This confirms that shape effects can be effectively captured by sphericity. Furthermore, we observe that the tensile strength follows a (1 − ψ)−1 scaling trend, consistent with the expectation that increasing shape deviations from a sphere enhance mechanical interlocking and connectivity in the granular network. However, this approximation slightly underestimates our data, particularly at larger ψ, where more anisotropic particles exhibit greater resistance. A more accurate empirical fit is given by a (1 − ψ)−1.4 dependence, which better captures the observed trend.

As shown in Fig. 4b, the data collapse obtained when scaling the tensile strength with the shape parameter ψ is less satisfactory than that obtained with the equivalent diameter deq (Fig. 4a). Although the fit using the (1 − ψ)−1 dependence correctly captures the overall increasing trend of σT/σT0 with particle non-sphericity, it does not reproduce the data as closely as the simple scaling with deq. This indicates that while the sphericity parameter ψ captures a relevant aspect of particle geometry, additional shape-induced effects influence the tensile response. These effects—and the microscopic origin of the differences between the two scaling relations—are analyzed in detail in the following section.

Microscopic interpretations

To further investigate the discrepancy we observe between the predicted and measured σT for the most elongated shapes, we analyze the evolution of the microstructural parameters that appear in Eqs. 7 and 12. In particular, we examine how the connectivity number Zc and solid fraction ϕ vary with particle size and shape. These quantities are crucial to understanding the mechanical response of the granular bridge, as they govern the distribution and transmission of forces within the material. Note that the quantities reported in Fig. 5 are evaluated at the peak-stress instant. Although they evolve during loading, the stress-controlled protocol keeps the imposed deformation extremely small, so their variations prior to rupture are negligible, and their peak values remain very close to those measured in the initial state.

Figure 5a shows the evolution of Zc as a function of the average deq for all particle shapes, and the inset shows the corresponding variation of the solid fraction ϕ. Both parameters remain nearly constant across different particle sizes, indicating that the product Zcϕ does not vary meaningfully with deq. This observation justifies why the tensile strength follows the expected scaling with deq.

In contrast, Fig. 5b reveals a more complex dependence of ϕ on particle shape. While Z/Z0 remains close to unity across different ψ¯ values, ϕ/ϕ0 exhibits non-monotonic behavior, initially decreasing and then increasing as ψ¯ increases. This trend is consistent with previous work showing that the solid fraction varies non-monotonically as the particle shape deviates from sphericity, whether by elongation, increased angularity, or flattening. This variation in the solid fraction suggests that the packing efficiency plays a role in modifying the tensile strength scaling with shape. The deviation from the (1 + ψ)−1 approximation likely originates from this non-trivial dependence of ϕ on particle morphology, which is not fully captured by a simple shape parameter. This effect is likely responsible for the improved agreement obtained when using (1 + ψ)−1.4. This finding also suggests that additional shape effects, such as contact anisotropy or force chain localization, may play a role56.

If all particles were approximately 10 μm, then its d¯eq is approximately 10 μm, and its tensile strength could be up to 100 Pa. However, if that were the case, the surface would have been sufficiently strong to prevent the TAGSAM head from piercing it60, contrary to what was observed27. On the other hand, if d¯eq is approximately 1 mm, the sampling site’s tensile strength would go down to 0.001 Pa, which is much smaller than estimates from the reconstruction of TAG30, but fully agreeing with27. So, we can conclude that the true d¯eq must be somewhere in between these extremes, as we have a size distribution of particles.

Applying the model developed here and the material parameters obtained from the Bennu samples and remote sensing, and taking 1 mm as our d¯eq of the average particle size, which is close to the 1.2 mm average particle size of the sampled regolith, the tensile strength should be approximately 0.001–0.01 Pa; close to being effectively strengthless, even in a small asteroid. However, it can be argued that this d¯eq should be smaller as the measurements did not take into account smaller particles or that the arithmetic mean, used to calculate the average particle size of the sample, might only be appropriate for narrow size distributions like those we used for our simulations. However, there are other calculations that could help us estimate d¯eq and the strength of the surface of Hokioi crater.

Previous studies27,30 have calculated that the cohesive strength of this surface had to be <1 Pa. On the other hand61, showed that cohesive strength had to be < 0.6 Pa in the top few meters of Bennu’s surface and62 calculated a value of ≤2 Pa for a 70-m-diameter impact crater on Bennu. Experimental and theoretical studies on the strength of cohesive granular matter have found that the cohesive strength is between 2 and 4 times greater than the tensile strength63. This would mean that the surface tensile strength must be at most 0.5 Pa, and therefore d¯eq> 31.0 μm. In Fig. 6, the intersection of the shaded regions shows the allowed tensile strength and d¯eq of Bennu’s surface.

As naturally occurring regolith particles are not spherical, this would mean that the measured average size would be larger, but certainly of the same order of magnitude. A 1.2 mm particle with α1 = α2 = 0.7 has a deq = 0.96 mm. However, the slope of the particle size distribution is approximately −2.12, not as steep as the −2.5 required for a tridimensional Apollonian packing64, or −3.47 for a random Apollonian packing65, meaning that there are not enough small particles to fill the interstices optimally, which contributes to a lower packing fraction and fewer cohesive bonds. That is, even if there are many particles with equivalent diameters below this d¯eq, they do not exist in sufficient quantities to fill the interstitial space between the larger particles to strengthen the structure. Furthermore, analysis of the crater created by the TAG event27 found the cohesive strength of the sampled surface to be about 0.001 Pa; this implies that d¯eq is approximately 0.5 mm, supporting the idea that very fine particles in the top half-meter of the regolith are not adding much strength to it. Although the TAG event showed that fine dust exists in Bennu’s near-surface27, our simulations indicate that the Bennu regolith must be composed of a population of particles that are predominantly larger than hundreds of micrometers. This is consistent with thermophysical modeling of Bennu’s regolith and boulder population66. In the best-case scenario, even a d¯eq is approximately 154 μm would still result in a tensile strength <0.5 Pa (at the intersection between the dashed green and orange lines in Fig. 6).

Discussion

Our results reveal a strong interplay between the shape and size of regolith particles in determining the tensile strength of the cohesive regolith matrix in low-gravity environments, with direct implications for understanding the cohesive strength of granular asteroids. A key outcome of our study is the unexpected finding that if the f0 of an asteroidal material can be characterized by laboratory analysis, then the tensile strength of granular asteroids composed of that material can be effectively estimated using a single parameter—either the equivalent diameter or the sphericity—despite the inherent differences in particle size and shape.

One might expect that variations in both shape and size, involving multiple geometric parameters and the resulting differences in contact geometry and network structure, would produce complex, irreducible effects on tensile strength. However, our results show a certain form of universality: the tensile strength trends are dictated by the particles’ effective volume, with larger particles generally exhibiting weaker cohesive strength. However, this weakness is counterbalanced by particles with a higher level of deviation from the spherical shape, which reduces their effective volume and enhances stronger contact networks through geometric interlocking and a greater variety of contact types. As a result, a cohesive regolith matrix composed of larger, low-aspect-ratio particles can achieve tensile strengths comparable to, or even surpassing, those formed by smaller particles.

For Bennu, our analysis makes apparent that the scarcity of dust particles in the regolith is likely the main driver of the low surface strength at the OSIRIS-REx sampling site. If the f0 of other asteroid types is similar to that of the carbonaceous Bennu material, then we can expect that small asteroids that also have little dust should have relatively weak surfaces. However, given that f0 depends on the chemical composition and roughness of the particles, its value should change for different asteroid types. Furthermore, the strength of Bennu’s surface and interior is scale-dependent. At local scales, like the ones of concern for sampling or crater formation, we expect that the surface strength can be heterogenous because the particle size distributions on Bennu’s surface are also heterogeneous28. Moreover, if layers do indeed exist in Bennu’s subsurface22, then our analysis suggests that Bennu’s surface strength may change with depth (in agreement with62) as the relative population of finer particles appears to increase. This finding has implications for interpreting the cratering records on asteroids67,68 and the design of surface-interacting spacecraft operations, such as sampling.

The framework established in this study can now be applied to describe the mechanical behavior of granular asteroids more generally. Understanding how regolith shape and size influence cohesive strength allows us to predict how these bodies might resist rotational disruption or respond to external forces, such as impacts or gravitational tides. The shape- and size-dependent tensile strength suggests that regolith matrices could stabilize asteroids against rotational disruption. Particles with lower aspect ratios, through their greater interlocking, may increase cohesive strength, enabling small asteroids to withstand spin rates beyond the classical gravitational limit. Moreover, the combined effects of particle shape and size offer a plausible explanation for the non-uniform internal structures of asteroids, such as Bennu. As proposed previously19, finer, more angular regolith may act as a cohesive “cement,” reinforcing the surrounding material and contributing different strengths to the layered structures observed by space missions22,23.

The framework developed here highlights the pressing need for direct experimental measurements of other material parameters—namely Zc, ϕ, and especially f0—for as many asteroid samples as possible to enable more accurate predictions of asteroid strength. The results of the analysis of the Bennu sample will help us better determine which other, yet-to-be-discovered, parameters affect particle–particle interactions, including pressure at the contact points, as that would extend the area of contact. However, as other asteroids are formed by materials different from those found on Bennu, it would be logical to think that particle–particle interactions will be different. Differences in particle surface morphology, chemical composition, and crystallographic phases would result in different values of f0, coefficients of friction, and roughness since all of these parameters affect them. These differences will in turn influence how particles pack, the contact forces between them, and ultimately the asteroid’s bulk cohesive and tensile strength.

An important open question concerns the role of wide polydispersity. Our simulations consider modest size dispersions for which quantities, such as deq and Zc, remain well defined. However, Bennu’s regolith is highly polydisperse28, and the mechanical behavior of cohesive granular materials under broad size distributions is still poorly understood. Even in dry systems, surprising effects, such as the weak dependence of macroscopic friction on size dispersity, have been observed50. In cohesive materials, the only detailed study known to us69 shows that the effective tensile strength increases with size polydispersity, even if the mean particle diameter is unchanged. This suggests that simple averages of particle size and connectivity may be insufficient in highly polydisperse asteroid regolith, and future work should explore how force networks reorganize under these conditions.

Future work could expand our findings by examining more complex particle geometries, such as irregular polyhedra, and investigating the combined effects of shape, size, and a much wider polydispersity. Additionally, it is critical to understand the quantum mechanical origins of cohesive forces; only then will we be able to predict asteroid strength based on remote observations alone.

Contact dynamics simulation with polyhedral particles

We perform simulations using the contact dynamics (CD) method, a robust approach that is well suited for large assemblies of rigid, arbitrarily shaped particles70. Unlike conventional soft-sphere DEM (SSDEM), CD does not rely on elastic repulsive potentials or smoothed friction laws. Instead, unknown variables—particle velocities and contact forces—are directly computed via a nonlinear Gauss-Seidel scheme, enabling stable simulations with significantly larger time steps. Granular bridge: construction method

A geometrically dense assembly of Np spheres with diameters d uniformly distributed within the range [dmin, dmax] is first generated inside a cylindrical container of 1 m in diameter. Here, dmin and dmax correspond to the minimum and maximum particle sizes, respectively. The same spherical packing is then used as a template for angular particles, where each sphere serves as the circumscribing sphere of a polyhedral particle. To define the bridge structure, two large boulders, modeled as spheres of 1 m in diameter, are placed above and below the granular assembly. The second step consists of constructing a mechanically stable granular bridge by introducing gravitational forces. Given the relatively small number of particles in these simulations, gravity is computed directly rather than approximated through coarse-particle methods. At this stage, the universal gravitational constant G is made 105 times larger to ensure a densely packed structure, cohesive forces characterized by f0 are activated, and the friction coefficient μs set to 0.4. The particles are then provided with a random orientation and allowed to accrete and rearrange, with both spherical boulders constrained to vertical movement along the y-axis. As they aggregate, all particles feel a drag that is proportional to their speed and the radius of their enclosing sphere to accelerate energy dissipation.

The kinetic energy of the system is checked every 5 s; the total kinetic energy of the system is <1 × 10−5 J, and the variation of kinetic energy over consecutive energy checks is 0.001 of the total. Once stability is reached, G is set to its proper value, and the system is allowed to relax to accommodate the new interactions. The granular bridge is considered fully equilibrated when all contact forces have adjusted to the cohesion and friction law and to the new gravitational forces, and no further rearrangements occur. This final state serves as the initial configuration for subsequent analyses. Note that the two boundary boulders exert a weak, symmetric gravitational confinement on the regolith bridge of the order of 1.8 × 10−4 N. However, the imposed tensile loading applied to the boulders progressively counteracts this confinement, such that failure occurs solely when a significant fraction of interparticle contacts become fully mobilized and reach their tensile strength threshold f0 (see the following sub-section).
