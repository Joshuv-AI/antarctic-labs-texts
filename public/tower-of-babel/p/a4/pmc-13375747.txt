Endoanal ultrasound-measured internal anal sphincter changes after nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy for anal incontinence: a prospective single-arm pilot study
Authors: Shady M Elmosselhy, Ismail A Shafik, Mahmoud S El-Basiouny, A Abdalla
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFecal incontinence (FI) is a debilitating condition that may be associated with structural abnormalities of the anal sphincter complex. Nonablative anal laser therapy has been proposed as a minimally invasive treatment option; however, its anatomical correlates remain insufficiently defined.ObjectivesThe objectives of this study were to evaluate endoanal ultrasound (EAUS)-detected changes in internal anal sphincter (IAS) morphology after nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy and to explore their association with clinical outcomes.MethodsIn this prospective single-arm pilot study, 30 patients with mild-to-moderate FI and IAS defects or atrophy underwent up to six sessions of nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy. EAUS was performed before and after treatment to assess IAS thickness, IAS length, and defect length. Clinical response was assessed using the Wexner score, and anorectal manometry and Fecal Incontinence Quality of Life (FIQL) scores were evaluated.ResultsA total of 24 patients completed treatment and post-treatment reassessment. EAUS demonstrated small but statistically significant increases in IAS thickness and length, with a concomitant reduction in IAS defect length (p < 0.05). Symptomatic improvement was observed in 17/24 completing patients (70.8%) and was more frequent among patients with isolated IAS defects than among those with combined IAS and external anal sphincter defects. Greater reduction in IAS defect length was associated with clinical improvement (p = 0.015). Adverse events were generally transient; however, six patients discontinued treatment, including four owing to pain or burning sensation.ConclusionsIn this uncontrolled pilot study, nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy was associated with EAUS-detected IAS changes and symptomatic improvement in selected patients with FI. Because of the absence of a control group, small absolute sonographic changes, and limited follow-up, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary and require confirmation in controlled studies with standardized imaging and functional assessment.Keywords: Fecal incontinence, Internal anal sphincter, Endoanal ultrasound, Nonablative laser therapy, Diode laser, Anal sphincter defects, Minimally invasive therapy

Introduction

Fecal incontinence (FI) is a common, underreported, and debilitating condition associated with substantial social, psychological, and functional impairment. Its pathophysiology is multifactorial and may involve altered stool consistency and transit; impaired rectal compliance and sensation; disrupted anorectal reflexes; neuropathy; and structural or functional compromise of the anal sphincter complex. Among these contributors, impairment of the internal anal sphincter (IAS) is particularly relevant to continence at rest and is frequently implicated in passive leakage through reduced resting anal pressure and impaired mucosal sealing [1, 2].

Management of FI is typically stepwise. First-line strategies include dietary adjustment, pharmacologic modulation of stool consistency and frequency, perianal skin care, and pelvic floor rehabilitation (including biofeedback), aiming to optimize rectal filling, sensory discrimination, and sphincter–pelvic floor coordination. However, a clinically important subset of patients remains symptomatic despite optimal conservative measures. For these refractory patients, more invasive options such as sphincteroplasty (in repairable external anal sphincter injuries), injectable bulking agents, and sacral nerve stimulation may offer benefit, yet each approach is limited by invasiveness, cost, patient selection constraints, and variable long-term durability. This therapeutic “gap” has driven interest in minimally invasive adjuncts that may provide symptomatic improvement while preserving future treatment options [3–5].

Endoanal ultrasound (EAUS) is widely used for structural assessment of the anal sphincter complex and is particularly valuable for identifying IAS defects and atrophy. Beyond binary identification of sphincter disruption, EAUS enables quantitative evaluation of IAS thickness, length, and defect extent, offering a pragmatic method to link anatomical abnormalities to symptom severity and to monitor treatment-related change. This is especially relevant in patients with FI where structural compromise—rather than purely functional dyscoordination—may drive symptoms [6, 7].

Energy-based therapies have been explored as intermediate options between conservative care and invasive surgery. Temperature-controlled radiofrequency has demonstrated histologic evidence of collagen remodeling and fibroblast activation, but clinical outcomes have generally been modest and inconsistent across studies [8]. More recently, nonablative laser therapy has gained attention because of its established biostimulatory effects in adjacent pelvic floor conditions (notably stress urinary incontinence), where controlled photothermal stimulation can promote new collagen formation, angiogenesis, and connective tissue remodeling while preserving epithelial integrity [9, 10]. The rationale for applying nonablative laser transanally in FI is that targeted stimulation of the connective tissue-rich submucosa and adjacent sphincter complex may enhance tissue support and potentially contribute to sphincter remodeling or improved barrier function. The biological rationale for transanal nonablative laser therapy is therefore not limited to direct muscle repair, but may also involve photothermal stimulation of the connective tissue-rich submucosa and peri-sphincteric tissues, with potential effects on collagen organization, angiogenesis, tissue tightening, and mucosal sealing [11, 12].

To date, evidence for intra-anal nonablative laser therapy in FI has been limited largely to case-based reports, and the anatomical mechanism underlying symptomatic improvement remains insufficiently defined. In particular, it is unclear whether symptom improvement is accompanied by objective, measurable changes in the IAS or reduction in defect extent on imaging.

Accordingly, this prospective pilot study aimed to evaluate EAUS-detected changes in IAS morphology, including thickness, length, and defect length, after nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy in patients with mild-to-moderate FI, and to explore the association between these sonographic changes and clinical response (Figs. 1 and 2).

Methods

The objective of this study was to assess whether nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy was associated with measurable EAUS-detected changes in IAS thickness, IAS length, and IAS defect length in patients with FI, and to explore whether these changes were associated with clinical, manometric, and quality-of-life outcomes.

Study design and ethical approval

This prospective single-arm pilot study was conducted between May 2023 and December 2024 at a tertiary referral outpatient colorectal clinic. The study protocol was approved by the local medical Ethics Committee of Cairo University. All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study population

Adult patients of either sex presenting with mild-to-moderate FI of at least 3 months’ duration were screened for eligibility. All included patients had failed conservative management, including dietary modification, antidiarrheal medications, and lifestyle measures.

Inclusion required the presence of IAS defects or IAS atrophy, with or without concomitant external anal sphincter (EAS) defects, identified on baseline EAUS. The underlying etiology of FI was recorded for each patient. Traumatic causes included previous anal surgery for perianal fistula, internal sphincterotomy, or hemorrhoidectomy; obstetric history related to repeated vaginal delivery; and sexual trauma. Nontraumatic cases were attributed to degenerative sphincter changes or idiopathic atrophy.

Exclusion criteria comprised inflammatory bowel disease, active anal pathology (acute fissure or active perianal fistula), anorectal malignancy, rectal prolapse, chronic constipation with overflow incontinence, chronic diarrhea as the primary etiology of FI, prior pelvic irradiation, prior coloanal or ileoanal anastomosis, pregnancy, and uncontrolled psychiatric disorders.

Sample size justification

Because no prior controlled studies have established the expected effect size of nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy on EAUS-measured IAS thickness, IAS length, or defect length in patients with fecal incontinence, a formal disease-specific power calculation was not feasible. The target sample size of 30 patients was therefore selected pragmatically for this exploratory pilot study to assess feasibility, tolerability, preliminary clinical response, and the variability of sonographic outcomes. Existing case-based reports of anal laser therapy and ultrasound studies of vaginal laser treatment for stress urinary incontinence were considered only as supportive background for anatomical plausibility, not as a definitive basis for statistical powering [13, 14].

Baseline clinical assessment

At enrollment, all patients underwent comprehensive clinical evaluation, including medical, surgical, and obstetric history (when applicable). FI severity was quantified using the Wexner incontinence score (range 0–20) [15]. Quality of life was assessed using the Fecal Incontinence Quality of Life (FIQL) scale, covering lifestyle, coping/behavior, depression/self-perception, and embarrassment domains [16].

Clinical response was defined as a reduction in Wexner score accompanied by patient-reported symptomatic improvement. Because a universally accepted minimal clinically important decrease in the Wexner score in this specific laser-treated FI population has not been established, responder status was interpreted descriptively and in relation to changes in FIQL domains.

Digital rectal examination and anoscopy were performed to assess anal tone at rest and on squeeze, exclude luminal pathology, and identify clinically palpable sphincter defects.

Anorectal physiology and imaging

Baseline anorectal manometry was performed using a stationary system (THD® Anopress, THD SpA, Italy) to measure resting and squeeze pressures.

Baseline and post-treatment EAUS were performed using a 10-MHz linear endocavitary probe (65EC10EB, Mindray, China), with the patient in the left lateral position. The anal canal was systematically assessed along its longitudinal axis, with targeted measurements at the 3, 6, and 9 o’clock positions. IAS thickness was measured in the mid-anal canal, and IAS length was measured longitudinally from the anorectal junction/anorectal ring to the point of sphincter termination. IAS defects were identified as interruptions of the hypoechoic IAS layer and measured longitudinally. IAS atrophy was defined as thinning without fiber discontinuity and classified as mild (1.5–2.0 mm) or severe (< 1.5 mm). These thresholds were interpreted in relation to adjacent preserved IAS segments and published EAUS reference ranges, recognizing that normal IAS thickness varies according to age, sex, probe type, measurement level, and acquisition technique (Table 2).

All EAUS examinations and measurements were performed by a single experienced operator using the same standardized acquisition and measurement protocol at baseline and follow-up visits. This approach reduced interobserver variability and ensured consistency of repeated measurements within the same patient. However, the operator was not blinded to the treatment time point or clinical course, and formal intraobserver reproducibility testing was not performed.

IAS length was measured longitudinally from the anorectal ring/anorectal junction to the distal point of sphincter termination at the evaluated sector. These sectorial measurements were used for within-patient comparison between baseline and follow-up rather than as absolute anatomical reference values.

IAS atrophy was interpreted in relation to adjacent preserved IAS segments and published EAUS reference ranges, recognizing that normal IAS thickness varies according to age, sex, probe type, measurement level, and acquisition technique. Therefore, the mild and severe atrophy thresholds were used descriptively rather than as universally validated cutoffs (Figs. 1 and 2).

Laser treatment protocol

All patients underwent nonablative diode laser therapy using a 1470-nm diode laser system (Lasotronix Smart™, Poland). Procedures were performed on an outpatient basis under topical local anesthesia (prilocaine/lidocaine cream).

Laser energy was delivered in a noncontact manner using a 600-µm radial fiber mounted within a graduated metallic probe and guided by a self-illuminated transparent anoscope. Energy parameters were standardized: fluence 70 J/cm2, frequency 1 Hz (600 ms on, 400 ms off), and spot size of approximately 1 cm.

Laser application was performed sequentially from the anorectal ring to the anal verge at four quadrants (6, 9, 12, and 3 o’clock positions), with 1-cm withdrawal increments between applications. Sessions were repeated every 2 weeks until symptomatic improvement or completion of a maximum of six sessions (Fig. 3).

Post-treatment assessment and follow-up

Patients were clinically assessed during each treatment session for symptom change, tolerability, and adverse events. Wexner score and FIQL were recorded at baseline, during treatment follow-up, and at the final post-treatment assessment. For patients who completed the treatment course, final EAUS and anorectal manometry were performed 4 weeks after the sixth laser session using the same acquisition and measurement protocols as baseline.

At each treatment visit, patients were assessed clinically for changes in continence symptoms, burning sensation, pain, bleeding, obstructed defecation symptoms, and any adverse events. Treatment continuation or delay was determined according to symptom tolerance and local anorectal findings.

Outcome measures

The primary outcome was the change in IAS morphology detected by EAUS, including IAS thickness, IAS length, and IAS defect length, measured at baseline and 4 weeks after the final laser session. Secondary outcomes included changes in Wexner score, FIQL domains, resting anal pressure, treatment completion, adverse events recorded during treatment sessions, and exploratory associations between clinical response and sonographic changes.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 20.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages. Continuous variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test and summarized as range, mean ± standard deviation, and median with interquartile range. Friedman testing with Dunn’s post hoc test was used for non-normally distributed repeated measures, including Wexner score across baseline, third-session, and sixth-session assessments. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc testing was used for normally distributed repeated measures, including FIQL domain scores. Paired t-tests were used to compare baseline and post-treatment IAS thickness, IAS length, and defect length among treatment completers. Wilcoxon signed-rank testing was used to compare paired non-normally distributed resting anal pressure between baseline and the sixth session. Chi-squared testing was used for categorical comparisons, with Fisher’s exact or Monte Carlo correction where appropriate. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Safety assessment

All adverse events were recorded and graded according to severity and impact on treatment continuation. Particular attention was paid to pain, bleeding, mucosal injury, and obstructive symptoms. Patients were free to discontinue treatment at any time.

Results

Participant characteristics

A total of 30 patients with fecal incontinence were enrolled. The mean age was 45.37 ± 17.19 years, with a median age of 45 years (interquartile range [IQR], 29–58; range, 18–82). Overall, 18 patients were female (60.0%) and 12 were male (40.0%). Regarding symptom pattern, urge incontinence was reported in 6 patients (20.0%), passive incontinence in 11 patients (36.7%), and mixed urge/passive incontinence in 13 patients (43.3%). The etiology was traumatic in 28 patients (93.3%) and nontraumatic in 2 patients (6.7%). Traumatic etiologies included previous anal surgery for perianal fistula, internal sphincterotomy, or hemorrhoidectomy in 17 patients (56.7%), repeated vaginal delivery in 8 patients (26.6%), and anal intercourse in 3 patients (10.0%). The remaining two patients (6.7%) had degenerative sphincter changes. Isolated IAS defects were present in 13 patients (43.3%), combined IAS + EAS defects in 15 patients (50.0%), and IAS atrophy without a discrete defect in 2 patients (6.7%) (Table 1).

Treatment completion and follow-up

Of the 30 enrolled patients, 24 (80.0%) completed the treatment course and underwent post-treatment reassessment, while 6 patients (20.0%) discontinued treatment. In the overall enrolled cohort, 17 patients (56.7%) improved, 7 patients (23.3%) did not improve, and 6 patients (20.0%) dropped out. Among treatment completers, 17/24 patients (70.8%) improved, whereas 7/24 patients (29.2%) did not report satisfactory improvement. Because response was assessed among treatment completers, the 70.8% improvement rate should be interpreted alongside the 20.0% dropout rate.

Clinical response

Wexner score

Wexner score improved significantly across the three assessment points (p < 0.001). The mean Wexner score decreased from 7.70 ± 2.35 at baseline to 4.70 ± 2.00 after the third session and 3.67 ± 1.88 after the sixth session. Median Wexner score decreased from 8.0 (IQR, 6.0–9.0) at baseline to 4.0 (IQR, 3.0–6.0) after the third session and 3.0 (IQR, 2.0–5.0) after the sixth session. Pairwise comparisons showed significant improvement from baseline to the third session (p < 0.001), baseline to the sixth session (p < 0.001), and third to sixth session (p = 0.030) (Table 2).

Quality-of-life outcomes

FIQL scores improved significantly across all four domains. Lifestyle scores increased from 2.62 ± 0.53 at baseline to 3.00 ± 0.41 after the third session and 3.21 ± 0.41 after the sixth session (p < 0.001). Coping/behavior scores increased from 2.33 ± 0.60 to 2.68 ± 0.55 and 2.91 ± 0.55, respectively (p < 0.001). Depression/self-perception scores increased from 2.78 ± 0.55 to 3.04 ± 0.50 and 3.19 ± 0.50, respectively (p < 0.001). Embarrassment scores increased from 2.52 ± 0.59 to 2.85 ± 0.59 and 3.05 ± 0.60, respectively (p < 0.001). Pairwise comparisons showed significant improvement from baseline to the third session, baseline to the sixth session, and third to sixth session in all FIQL domains (Table 3).

IAS thickness

Among the 24 patients who completed treatment, IAS thickness showed small but statistically significant increases at all measured positions after laser therapy. Mean thickness increased from 1.97 ± 0.29 to 2.03 ± 0.30 mm at the 3 o’clock position (p < 0.001), from 1.87 ± 0.45 to 2.00 ± 0.39 mm at the 6 o’clock position (p = 0.002), and from 2.01 ± 0.33 to 2.09 ± 0.37 mm at the 9 o’clock position (p = 0.013). Given the small absolute magnitude of these changes, the findings should be interpreted cautiously in light of measurement variability, lack of blinded assessment, and absence of formal intraobserver reproducibility testing (Table 5).

IAS length and IAS defect length

IAS length increased significantly at all measured positions after treatment. Mean IAS length increased from 2.25 ± 0.57 to 2.38 ± 0.53 cm at the 3 o’clock position (p < 0.001), from 2.09 ± 0.52 to 2.26 ± 0.48 cm at the 6 o’clock position (p < 0.001), and from 2.50 ± 0.35 to 2.57 ± 0.36 cm at the 9 o’clock position (p < 0.001). Mean IAS defect length decreased from 0.92 ± 0.29 to 0.68 ± 0.31 cm (p < 0.001). IAS defects were most commonly located at the 6 o’clock and 3 o’clock positions; the remaining defects were at 2, 7, 8, and 10 o’clock in one patient each (Table 6).

Outcome according to defect type

Among treatment completers, improvement was numerically more frequent in patients with isolated IAS defects than in those with combined IAS + EAS defects. Improvement occurred in 9/11 patients with isolated IAS defects (81.8%) compared with 8/13 patients with combined IAS + EAS defects (61.5%), although this difference was not statistically significant (Fisher’s exact p = 0.386). Baseline IAS defect length was longer among non-improving patients than improving patients, but this difference did not reach statistical significance (1.10 ± 0.39 versus 0.85 ± 0.22 cm; p = 0.062). After treatment, residual IAS defect length was significantly longer among non-improving patients than improving patients (0.98 ± 0.34 versus 0.56 ± 0.20 cm; p = 0.015). These findings suggest that residual post-treatment defect length, rather than baseline defect length alone, was associated with clinical response.

Anorectal manometry outcomes

Resting anal pressure increased numerically but not significantly after treatment. Mean resting anal pressure increased from 45.20 ± 9.69 mmHg at baseline to 49.45 ± 12.34 mmHg after the sixth session. Median resting pressure increased from 41.50 mmHg (IQR, 39.0–50.0) to 50.0 mmHg (IQR, 38.5–59.0), but this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.314). These findings indicate that the EAUS-detected anatomical changes did not clearly translate into measurable short-term improvement in resting anal pressure (Table 7).

Safety and tolerability

Treatment-related adverse events were mostly transient and managed conservatively; however, tolerability was not universal. Burning sensation during or after treatment was the most common complaint and led to discontinuation in four patients. Minimal bleeding occurred in six patients and was attributed to superficial mucosal ulcerations that resolved with topical treatment before the subsequent session. Four patients developed transient heaviness or obstructed defecation symptoms related to congested prolapsed hemorrhoids; sessions were delayed and symptoms improved with medical therapy, although one patient discontinued treatment. These findings indicate that although the procedure was feasible in most patients, pain and burning sensation represent clinically relevant tolerability limitations.

Discussion

Fecal incontinence (FI) remains a highly burdensome condition with multifactorial pathophysiology and a management pathway that is frequently constrained by imperfect durability, invasiveness, and patient acceptance. Contemporary guidelines emphasize a stepwise approach beginning with stool optimization and pelvic floor rehabilitation, reserving invasive options such as sphincter repair, injectable agents, and neuromodulation for selected refractory cases [3, 17]. In this context, minimally invasive, tissue-remodeling interventions may occupy a clinically relevant “bridge” position—particularly for patients with mild-to-moderate symptoms who have failed conservative management but are not ideal candidates for, or prefer to defer, more invasive therapies.

Principal findings

The present prospective pilot study suggests that nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy is associated with symptomatic improvement, improved FIQL scores, and measurable EAUS-detected IAS changes. These included small but statistically significant increases in IAS thickness and length and a significant reduction in IAS defect length. Because this was a single-arm uncontrolled study, these changes should not be interpreted as definitive proof that laser therapy induced sphincter regeneration or remodeling. Rather, they represent preliminary anatomical correlates that require confirmation in controlled studies with standardized imaging, blinded image assessment, functional manometry, and longer follow-up.

Clinical response and quality-of-life interpretation

The clinical signal was supported by both symptom severity and quality-of-life measures. Wexner score decreased significantly across treatment sessions, and all FIQL domains improved significantly. Nevertheless, responder classification should be interpreted cautiously because no prospectively defined minimum Wexner-score reduction was specified. Therefore, the improvement rate should be viewed as a descriptive clinical signal rather than a validated responder endpoint. Reporting both the overall cohort response rate and the completer response rate is important, because the apparent 70.8% response among completers should be interpreted alongside the 20% dropout rate [18, 19].

Possible mechanisms and rationale for the 1470-nm diode laser

The observed clinical and EAUS changes are biologically plausible but remain mechanistically unproven. Nonablative laser energy may produce controlled photothermal stimulation of the anal canal submucosa and peri-sphincteric tissues, potentially contributing to collagen contraction, connective-tissue remodeling, tissue tightening, and improved mucosal coaptation. A related ultrasound-based study of Er:YAG vaginal laser therapy for stress urinary incontinence reported post-treatment reductions in vaginal dimensions, supporting the broader concept that nonablative pelvic-floor laser therapy may produce measurable soft-tissue changes; however, this evidence is anatomically indirect and cannot be assumed to predict IAS response in fecal incontinence. The 1470-nm wavelength was selected because of its affinity for water-containing soft tissues and its suitability for radial-fiber delivery, allowing localized circumferential photothermal exposure. Nevertheless, evidence from adjacent anorectal laser applications such as fistula closure or hemorrhoidoplasty supports procedural feasibility rather than efficacy for fecal incontinence, and the present study cannot establish histological regeneration or durable sphincter remodeling [12, 20–28].

Functional interpretation of manometry

The manometric findings are central to interpretation. Resting anal pressure increased numerically but not significantly after treatment, indicating that EAUS-detected changes should not be interpreted as functional IAS recovery. However, the absence of a significant resting-pressure change does not exclude symptomatic benefit, because continence depends on multiple interacting factors, including IAS tone, EAS recruitment, anal canal length, mucosal coaptation, rectal sensation, stool consistency, and reflex responses. Conventional manometry may not fully capture localized sectoral changes or altered mucosal sealing. Future studies should therefore incorporate high-resolution or three-dimensional anorectal manometry, sensory testing, and longer follow-up to determine whether sonographic changes translate into reproducible functional improvement [29–32].

Relationship to existing evidence and positioning among local therapies

Evidence for intra-anal nonablative laser therapy in FI remains limited, largely consisting of case-based reports. The present study advances this literature by providing prospective evaluation in a larger cohort than prior case reports, systematic pre/post EAUS measurement, and exploratory clinical–anatomical correlation centered on IAS defect dynamics. However, because the study lacked a control or sham arm, the findings should be viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

The broader field of local FI interventions has been challenged by heterogeneous mechanisms and variable durability. Temperature-controlled radiofrequency has been proposed to promote tissue remodeling, but clinical benefit has been modest or inconsistent, and sham-controlled evidence has raised concerns about the clinical magnitude of effect. Injectable bulking agents and biologic approaches may be attractive because they are local treatments, but they can be limited by durability, foreign-body or local tissue reactions, procedural cost, and uncertainty regarding responder selection. In this context, nonablative diode laser therapy may be positioned as a local, mucosa-preserving investigational adjunct rather than a replacement for established FI treatments. Its potential advantages include outpatient delivery, absence of implanted foreign material, repeatability, and preservation of future surgical or neuromodulatory options. Nevertheless, comparative effectiveness and durability remain unknown [3, 8, 33, 34].

Interpreting heterogeneity of response

Heterogeneity of response is expected in FI, where symptoms arise from interacting structural, sensory, neuromuscular, and stool-related factors. The exploratory subgroup findings suggest that response may be more likely when FI is driven predominantly by IAS pathology rather than combined sphincter disruption. Improvement was numerically higher among patients with isolated IAS defects than among those with combined IAS + EAS defects, although this difference was not statistically significant. Baseline IAS defect length was longer among non-improving patients but did not reach statistical significance, whereas residual defect length after treatment was significantly shorter among improving patients. This suggests that post-treatment defect behavior may be more informative than baseline defect size alone. However, because the analysis was limited by small subgroup numbers, lack of blinding, and absence of a control group, this should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than predictive.

The presence of combined IAS + EAS defects is particularly important when interpreting the results. In these patients, FI is unlikely to be driven solely by impaired IAS structure or resting tone; EAS disruption, impaired squeeze function, pudendal neuropathy, altered rectal sensation, and stool consistency may all contribute. This may explain why response appeared numerically higher among patients with isolated IAS defects than among those with combined IAS + EAS defects. Therefore, the present findings should not be generalized to all patients with complex sphincter injury, and future studies should stratify patients according to isolated IAS pathology, combined sphincter defects, and functional manometric profiles.

Imaging considerations and what EAUS can—and cannot—prove

EAUS is a pragmatic and widely accepted tool for structural assessment of the anal sphincter complex, particularly for IAS defects. In this study, all measurements were performed by a single experienced operator using a consistent protocol, which reduced interobserver variability. However, the operator was not blinded to treatment time point or clinical course, and formal intraobserver reproducibility testing was not performed. Therefore, the observed EAUS changes should be interpreted as exploratory imaging correlates only. Increases in measured IAS thickness or length and reductions in apparent defect length may reflect tissue contraction, transient edema, inflammatory change, altered echogenicity, improved mucosal coaptation, or short-term post-treatment tissue effects rather than definitive sphincter regeneration or durable remodeling. Future studies should incorporate blinded image assessment, repeated measurements, three-dimensional (3D) EAUS, and/or pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) to improve reproducibility and better characterize structural change [35, 36].

Clinical implications and positioning of laser therapy

These findings suggest that nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy may be considered an investigational or selectively applied adjunct for carefully selected patients with mild-to-moderate FI associated with IAS defects or atrophy who have failed conservative management. Its potential advantages include outpatient delivery, radial energy distribution, mucosa preservation, repeatability, and preservation of future treatment options. However, current evidence remains insufficient to recommend routine clinical adoption, and comparative effectiveness, durability, optimal patient selection, and the relationship between sonographic and functional change require further controlled evaluation [3, 17, 33].

Strengths and limitations

Strengths

This study has several strengths. First, it addresses an important evidence gap by moving beyond symptom-based reporting and providing objective imaging-based assessment of treatment-associated change. The use of EAUS to quantify IAS thickness, length, and defect extent provides anatomical data that can be explored alongside symptom and QoL outcomes. Second, the prospective design with standardized pre- and post-treatment assessments strengthens internal consistency. Third, the use of a single EAUS operator ensured consistency across visits, although this must be balanced against the absence of blinding and formal intraobserver reproducibility testing. Finally, the study explores clinical–anatomical correlations and therefore provides preliminary data for designing future controlled studies.

Limitations

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single-arm, nonrandomized design precludes causal inference and leaves the findings vulnerable to placebo effects, regression to the mean, natural symptom variability, and unmeasured co-interventions. Therefore, the observed clinical and sonographic improvements should be interpreted as exploratory associations rather than definitive treatment effects.

Second, the sample size was modest and pragmatically selected for a pilot study rather than based on a robust disease-specific effect size. Because no prior controlled studies have established the expected effect of nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy on EAUS-measured IAS outcomes in fecal incontinence, the study was not powered to provide confirmatory efficacy estimates or reliable subgroup comparisons.

Third, the absolute changes in IAS thickness and length were small and may overlap with expected EAUS measurement variability. Although all EAUS assessments were performed by a single experienced operator using a standardized protocol, the operator was not blinded to treatment time point or clinical course, formal intraobserver reproducibility testing was not performed, and no validated minimal clinically important difference exists for EAUS-measured IAS thickness, IAS length, or defect length in this setting.

Fourth, the EAUS findings should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of sphincter regeneration or durable remodeling. Increases in measured IAS thickness or length and reductions in apparent defect length may reflect tissue contraction, transient edema, inflammatory change, altered echogenicity, improved mucosal coaptation, measurement variability, or other short-term post-treatment tissue effects rather than true structural restoration of the sphincter complex.

Fifth, objective anorectal manometry did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in resting anal pressure at 4 weeks after the final laser session, despite symptomatic improvement and EAUS-detected changes. This limits the ability to infer functional IAS recovery from imaging findings alone.

Sixth, the responder classification was exploratory because no prospectively defined minimum clinically important Wexner-score reduction was specified. Although Wexner score and FIQL domains improved significantly, the improvement rate should not be interpreted as a validated responder endpoint.

Seventh, 6 of 30 patients did not complete treatment, including 4 who discontinued because of pain or burning sensation. This attrition may have introduced selection bias and may have inflated the apparent response rate among treatment completers.

Eighth, a substantial proportion of patients had combined IAS + EAS defects, making it difficult to attribute symptom change to IAS-related effects alone. Fecal incontinence is multifactorial, and nonstructural contributors such as stool consistency, rectal compliance, sensory dysfunction, neuropathy, and behavioral or pharmacologic factors may have influenced outcomes.

Ninth, imaging assessment relied on two-dimensional EAUS. Although EAUS is well established for IAS assessment, 3D EAUS or pelvic MRI may better characterize complex defect geometry, EAS atrophy, and longitudinal structural changes.

Finally, follow-up EAUS and manometry were performed only 4 weeks after the final laser session. This early assessment does not establish durability, and some sonographic changes may represent transient post-treatment tissue effects rather than stable long-term remodeling. Future sham-controlled or comparative studies with standardized EAUS acquisition, blinded image assessment, complete functional manometry, validated responder definitions, QoL outcomes, and longer follow-up are required.

Conclusions

In this prospective single-arm pilot study, nonablative 1470-nm diode laser therapy was associated with improved Wexner score, improved FIQL domains, and early EAUS-detected changes in IAS morphology, including small increases in IAS thickness and length and reduction in apparent IAS defect length 4 weeks after the final laser session. These findings suggest possible anatomical correlates of clinical response, particularly among patients with isolated IAS defects, but they should not be interpreted as proof of IAS regeneration, durable remodeling, or functional recovery. The observed EAUS changes may reflect tissue contraction, edema, altered echogenicity, mucosal coaptation, or other short-term post-treatment tissue effects. Because of the uncontrolled design, small absolute sonographic changes, lack of blinded EAUS assessment, exploratory responder definition, absence of significant resting-pressure improvement, attrition, and short follow-up, the results should be interpreted as preliminary. Controlled studies using standardized and blinded EAUS assessment, complete functional manometry, validated QoL outcomes, predefined responder criteria, and longer follow-up are needed before routine clinical adoption can be recommended.
