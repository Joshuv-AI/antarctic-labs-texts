Technical feasibility of combined uniportal unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression and interlaminar lumbar discectomy surgery for cauda equina syndrome due to lower lumbar disc herniation
Authors: Wu Pang Hung, Rohit Akshay Kavishwar, Hyeun Sung Kim, Brian Zhao Jie Chin
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTraditionally, open wide laminectomy and discectomy have been advocated for the treatment of cauda equina syndrome caused by lumbar disc herniation. We aimed to evaluate the technical feasibility of uniportal interlaminar endoscopy in treating cauda equina syndrome.MethodsNine patients with cauda equina syndrome underwent uniportal endoscopic decompression and discectomy from December 2020 to December 2022. Data were collected retrospectively. Patients diagnosed with cauda equina syndrome were operated on within 6 hours of presentation to the hospital. The visual analogue score (VAS), Oswestry disability index (ODI), and bladder/bowel score were used to measure the outcome.ResultsAnalysis showed that VAS scores for leg pain and back pain significantly decreased from preoperative scores of 8.22±0.79 and 4.67±1.76 to postoperative day 1 scores of 0.67±0.67 and 2.56±1.42 (p<.05). The ODI scores improved from preoperative 52.33±11.93 to postoperative (day 1) 14±6.80. Eight patients had early recovery (1 week) of bladder and bowel functions, and one had delayed recovery at 8 months. None of the patients had a residual bowel/bladder deficit. Macnab's criteria outcomes were excellent in all patients at the final follow-up.ConclusionsUniportal endoscopic lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression and subsequent interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy is a safe and effective minimally invasive course of treatment for cauda equina syndrome as an alternative to open laminectomy in our cohort of patients.Keywords: Cauda equina syndrome, Uniportal endoscopy, Endoscopic spine surgery, Interlaminar endoscopy, Spinal stenosis

Introduction

Cauda equina syndrome is a known surgical emergency with an extreme presentation of lumbar disc herniation (LDH) in 1% to 3% of patients [[1], [2], [3]]. It is a rare condition resulting from severe compression of nerve roots below the conus medullaris. Fraser et al.[3] define cauda equina syndrome as requiring the presence of one or more of the following symptoms: (1) bladder or rectal dysfunction, (2) sensory dysfunction in the saddle area, (3) sexual dysfunction, and/or (4) lower extremity pain and motor weakness. Good wide decompression laminectomy has been the standard of surgical treatment of patients with cauda equina syndrome [4,5]. The timing of surgery is highly crucial, and early surgical intervention leads to favorable outcomes [3,6,7]. Huge lumbar disc herniation removed by the open posterior approach is associated with complications such as damage to paraspinal muscles, bleeding, infection, wide epidural scar, and intraoperative neural injury [8]. With recent advances in minimally invasive spine surgery, endoscopic techniques have become common for degenerative spine diseases [9]. Early case reports by Kim et al. [10] and Subash et al. [11] showed encouraging results with transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy in cauda equina syndrome. As surgeons become familiar with endoscopy know-how, promising results are being published in patients with cauda equina syndrome [12]. Initially, most of the investigators did not recommend percutaneous endoscopic discectomy in cauda equina syndrome, regarding it as a high-risk procedure with complications [13].

Fujita et al. recommended full-endoscopic discectomy as a first option for treating lumbar disc herniation since it has a better safety profile and is minimally invasive compared with microendoscopic discectomy [14]. Their study also showed a shorter operation time, minimal invasiveness, reduced operative complications such as dural tears and epidural hematomas with endoscopic discectomy, and no difference in recurrence at a 2-year follow-up. Spinal endoscopy is expanding from its archetype management of disc herniations to spinal stenosis [15]. Chen et al. [16] and Li et al. [13] reported the successful use of percutaneous endoscopic lumbar discectomy(PELD) in treating cauda equina syndrome. Still, the technicalities of the procedure were not distinctly explained. Endoscopic surgery is further challenging in a highly compromised spinal canal patient with cauda equina syndrome with the added risk of injuring already compromised neural elements. After gaining experience in spine endoscopic surgeries, authors have hypothesized that uniportal interlaminar endoscopy could effectively treat patients with cauda equina syndrome. Hence, in this study, we investigated the technical feasibility of combined uniportal lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression (ULBD) followed by interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy(IELD) in patients with cauda equina syndrome.

Material and methods

This study was conducted as a cohort study with retrospective evaluation from December 2020 to December 2022. Patients diagnosed with cauda equina syndrome, as per Fraser's criteria, were operated on within 6 hours of presentation to the hospital.

Inclusion criteria covered all patients with a large lumbar disc herniation on MRI (one or two levels at L3/4, L4/5, L5/S1) with >50% canal compromise [17] and clinically diagnosed with acute cauda equina syndrome according to criteria given by Fraser et al. [3]. We retrospectively collected data from nine patients with cauda equina syndrome who underwent combined uniportal lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression (ULBD) followed by interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy(IELD). Patient demographics were noted, including the age, gender, time to surgery, back pain, leg pain, bladder symptoms, and neurological status. Patients with large lumbar disc herniation at upper lumbar levels (L1–L2, L2–L3), bleeding disorders, fractures, and tumors were excluded from this study.

Based on etiopathogenesis and clinical features, Shi et al. [18] divided cauda equina syndrome into a pre-clinical stage, early stage, intermediate stage, and late stage (Table 1). Out of nine patients, seven were in the intermediate stage, and two were in the late stage.

Surgical technique

Position, anesthesia, and approach

The procedure was performed under general anesthesia and with neuromonitoring. The patient was positioned prone on the radiolucent operating table with the spine in slight flexion using a Wilson frame. A single dose prophylactic antibiotic (Inj. Cefazolin 30 mg/kg) was given 20 to 30 minutes prior to the skin incision. Irrigation fluid pressure was kept around 25 mm Hg using a pump. Care was taken not to increase the water pressure beyond 25 mm Hg as the nerve roots are very sensitive and highly compromised in cauda equina syndrome due to ongoing pressure from compression. Skin marking was performed in the midline and the medial pedicle line with a marker pen under image intensifier guidance. We injected indigo carmine dye into the disc space through the transforaminal route so the herniated disc looked blue and became easy to identify (Fig. 1). A 1 cm skin incision was made vertically at the facet “V” point as described by Kim et al. [19]. The fascia incision was also made 1 cm to facilitate mobility of the working channel. The case described is a combined uniportal lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression (ULBD) followed by interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy(IELD) for a huge L5–S1 disc herniation causing cauda equina syndrome (Fig. 6- Case 1).

Phase 1: lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression

Insertion and docking of the endoscope

Guidewire was placed at the “V” point (Fig. 2), which is the laminofacet junction between the caudal lamina and cephalad inferior articular process, and subsequent serial dilatations were made with obturators through which a working cannula was inserted. Fluoroscopy was used to check the correct position of the scope and to reconfirm the level of decompression. We used an endoscope with an 11° viewing angle and an outer diameter of 9 mm, a working channel diameter of 5.5 mm, and a working length of 90 mm.

Docking point for endoscope (A) yellow circle, (B) AP view C-arm, (C) Lateral view C-arm. Endoscopic images showing soft tissue clearance and drilling of inferior articular process (D, E, F).

Soft tissue clearance and identification of the inferior border of the cranial lamina

After the insertion of the endoscope, a radiofrequency ablator (Bonss MedicalⓇ, Jiangsu, China) was used to achieve adequate hemostasis. In a cauda equina patient, we avoid using high irrigation fluid pressures to decrease bleeding and instead prefer radiofrequency ablation and hemostatic agents such as FlosealⓇ (Baxter International, Inc., Deerfield, IL, USA). Soft tissue was then dissected off the caudal lamina to identify the inferomedial border of the inferior articular process and inferior margin of the cranial lamina on the ipsilateral side (Fig. 2). The inferior articular process was drilled on its medial border in the caudo-cranial direction to expose the underlying superior articular process. Drilling beyond the medial pedicle was performed meticulously to preserve as much of the medial facet as possible while removing sufficient amounts of the medial ligamentum flavum.

Exposure of ligamentum flavum

Cranial and caudal laminae were drilled on the ipsilateral side to expose the free margins of the deep layer of the ligamentum flavum (Fig. 3). A superficial layer of the ligamentum flavum was removed while its deep layer was left intact to protect the underlying dura. This was followed by drilling of the base of the spinous process and the under surface of contralateral cranial and caudal laminae to achieve over-the-top decompression (Fig. 4 and Fig. 5C). The sublaminar approach aims to preserve the contralateral facet. We tend to remove the ligamentum flavum en bloc late in the procedure as it helps to avoid the pressure effects of irrigation fluid on already compromised neural elements. Laterally, the ligamentum flavum was released from the tip of the superior articular facet on both sides with the help of a Penfield dissector or endoscopic curette (Fig. 5A). We recommend not to use a Kerrison punch for detaching the ligamentum flavum to avoid inadvertent blunt trauma by the keel of the Kerrison punch. En bloc removal of the ligamentum flavum facilitates proper visualization of the herniated disc material and exact compression site of neural elements. This reduces the chances of iatrogenic injury to the nerve roots. The authors do not recommend splitting or cutting the ligamentum flavum in patients with cauda equina syndrome or blunt insertion of a small-diameter endoscope in a compressed spinal canal. Disc removal before retraction of the nerve

We try to avoid any inadvertent retraction of the nerve root. After the removal of the ligamentum flavum, in all of our cases, we saw the edges of the disc fragments compressing the nerves. The larger fragments were carefully and gently removed without vigorous movements under endoscopic vision. While removing a huge disc fragment, nerve roots can become further compromised if the ligamentum flavum is not removed completely; hence, the authors advocate phase 1 completion of lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression.

Phase 2: interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy

Larger fragments of the herniated disc compressing the thecal sac were removed first, following which we inserted a small-diameter endoscope through the same skin incision. The working retractor tube was used to gently mobilize the traversing nerve root to expose the disc space. Phase 2 interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy was performed to remove residual smaller fragments of the disc to prevent recurrence. Pulsating nerve roots were identified upon removal of the disc. Thermal radiofrequency was used for disc bulge shrinkage. The water pressure was reduced by reducing the height of saline bags or water and adequate hemostasis was achieved with the help of a radiofrequency ablator and Floseal. The skin was closed in layers.

Postoperative MRI

Postoperative day 1 MRI is highly recommended (Fig. 6) regardless of the patient's symptoms in view of medicolegal obligation. Any residual disc on the MRI must be discussed with the patient and attenders if the patient does not improve symptomatically.

Patient follow-up and outcome measures

The extent of surgery, time from the presentation of symptoms to surgery, estimated blood loss (EBL), and intraoperative complications were noted for all patients. The EBL was calculated based on output from suction minus the total saline input. We recorded if there was any case of incomplete decompression on post-operative day 1 MRI. All of the patients were followed up after the surgery. We devised a score to evaluate the recovery of bladder and bowel functions (Table 2). The visual analogue score (VAS), Oswestry disability index (ODI), and bladder/bowel score (Table 4, 5) were used to measure the outcome.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed with SPSS version 18 statistical analysis software (IBM corporation, NY), and the continuous variables were expressed as the mean and standard deviation (SD). A paired t test was used for the analysis of the clinical visual analogue scale (VAS) for back pain and leg pain and Oswestry disability index (ODI), and measurements at preoperative, 2 weeks postoperative, 3 months postoperative, 6 months postoperative, and final follow-up reported by patients were analyzed. A p-value of <.05 was considered statistically significant.

One patient had two-level lumbar disc herniation, while eight patients had single-level pathology. The percentage of canal compromise was >50% on axial MRI imaging for all the patients [17]. All patients underwent uniportal interlaminar endoscopic decompression and discectomy from the more symptomatic side. The average EBL was 10.2 (5–20) mL and the duration of surgery was 55 minutes (50–70 minutes), respectively. One patient had a dural tear, which was treated with a patch-blocking repair technique without any further complications [20]. The VAS scores and ODI scores for all the patients' back pain and leg pain significantly improved on post-operative day 1 (p<.05), and all the patients were self-ambulant. There was no case with a residual disc on post-operative day 1 MRI.

Eight patients showed early recovery of bladder/bowel symptoms (within 1 week). One patient had delayed recovery of bladder/bowel functions, which was around 8 months from surgery (Table 4). All nine patients had excellent outcomes based on Macnab's criteria at the final follow-up. None of the patients had any residual neurological deficits at the final follow-up.

There was a statistically significant improvement in the postoperative VAS score for back pain when compared with the preoperative score with a mean ± standard deviation of 2.56±1.42 at the immediate post-operative period (POD 1), 2.11±1.10 at 2 weeks, 1.78±0.63 at 3 months, and 1.44±0.50 at 6 months, and 1.56±0.68 at final follow-up, p<.05 (Table 4). Additionally, when we compared the preoperative VAS scores for leg pain with the postoperative scores, there was a statistically significant improvement in the VAS score with a mean ± standard deviation of 0.67±0.67 at the immediate postoperative period (POD 1), 0.56±0.68 at 2 weeks, 0.22±0.63 at 3 months, 0.22±0.63 at 6 months, and 0 at final follow-up. The ODI scores at immediate post-operative (POD1), 2 weeks, 3 months, 6 months, and final follow-up were 14±6.80, 7.34±5.16, 3.11±3.66, 1.78±1.99, and 0±0 with p-values<.01.

Discussion

Early intervention in cauda equina syndrome is essential for favorable outcomes [6,21,22]. Bladder recovery depends on the severity of preoperative urinary dysfunction, according to Qureshi and Sell et al. [23]. Gleave and Macfarlane et al. [21] reported 79% recovery of bladder bowel function at final follow-up amongst 33 cases of acute urinary retention in patients with cauda equina syndrome who underwent laminectomy. However, only 22% of their patients had complete resolution of sensory defects in the limbs or saddle area. Kanemastsu et al. reported that posterior decompression surgery improved defecation and urinary symptoms in 30% to 50% out of 93 patients, but they also included patients with chronic symptoms [24]. Shapiro et al. [22] had a 72% recovery rate of bladder functions with laminectomy on 14 patients with cauda equina syndrome; 28% had persistent urinary incontinence and fatigue at final follow-up. Dinning and Schaeffer [25] reported a 77.3% (17/22) recovery rate with laminectomy. Olivero et al. [4] reported unilateral hemilaminotomy and wider laminectomies had similar results. Microdiscectomy has reliable results and is currently the gold standard for cauda equina syndrome. With the development of endoscopic spine surgery and its promising results such as reduced infection rates, shorter hospital stays, less postoperative pain, and lwer potential for ambulatory surgery [26], the authors feel that there is potential for the application of uniportal endoscopy in patients with cauda equina syndrome.

Comparable results of PELD (percutaneous lumbar endoscopic discectomy) with conventional open surgery with 81.2% favorable results were reported by Li et al. [13]. However, one patient suffered post-operative transient motor weakness in their study. Kim et al. [10] in 2007 reported use of endoscopic discectomy in a pregnant patient with cauda equina syndrome and complete relief from pain in postoperative period but with a residual motor deficit. In our study, we had early recovery of bladder and bowel functions in 88.8% of patients, and all of the patients recovered fully within 8 months with no residual neurological deficits. We postulated that one of the reasons is that we performed surgery as a high-priority emergency surgery within 6 hours of surgery for all our patients. We posit that phase 1 lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression and the removal of the flavum helps to identify the edge of large prolapsed discs, which can be removed directly without any nerve root retraction; this may have contributed to our good clinical and neurological results.

One patient whose bladder and bowel functions did not return in the early post-operative period presented to us at Shi's late stage and was put on a comprehensive rehabilitation protocol. This included strength training for lower limbs, core stabilization exercise, active range-of-motion exercises, and gait training, which helps the patient perform daily living activities with ease [27]. For bladder and bowel recovery, the patient was prescribed pelvic floor strengthening exercises and sacral stimulation [28]. At the final follow-up, the patient had no numbness or motor weakness and could pass urine and stools with conscious effort. The results of our study corroborate with the reported literature. Due to our small sample size and the early presentation of patients, all of our patients recovered fully without any residual neurological deficits despite one dura tear complication.

Surgical removal of a giant disc in cauda equina syndrome is challenging because of its huge size and risk of additional neurological compromise due to iatrogenic manipulation [29,30]. There are high chances of dural tear and cerebrospinal fluid leakage due to pressure from huge disc herniation and reduced mobility of neural elements for retraction [31,32]. We can grasp a large fragment of disc from most of our cases without any nerve root manipulation after lumbar endoscopic unilateral laminotomy with bilateral decompression. After removing a large disc fragment, we inserted a small-diameter interlaminar discectomy endoscope to complete the discectomy. With this two-phase approach to performing the discectomy, there is less need for nerve root retraction in completing the discectomy. Transforaminal PELD, introduced via Kambin's triangle, always carries the risk of exiting nerve root injury [33,34]. Kim et al. [8] reported endoscopy-assisted OLIF (oblique lumbar interbody fusion) as an alternative option for patients with cauda equina syndrome. However, they reported the potential for iatrogenic dural tears and the possibility of retained disc fragments ventral to the dura.

We maintain a hydrostatic pressure of 25 mm Hg for all patients to mitigate damage to the neural elements, based on the literature of Kang et al., who described no changes in cervical epidural pressure with a flow set at and below 30 mm Hg [35]. We completed all bony endoscopic drilling and exposure of the edges of the ligamentum flavum. Subsequently, we removed the ligamentum flava en-bloc to limit the time of the direct exposure of neural elements to normal saline pressure from the endoscope.

Li et al. [13] performed PELD under local anesthesia and, based on the level and location of herniation, an appropriate approach was chosen. Their study preferred the transforaminal approach for the upper lumbar disc herniations and the interlaminar approach for lumbar disc herniations at L5–S1 and L4–L5 with high iliac crests. Since cauda equina syndrome occurs most commonly at L4–L5 and L5–S1 [3], the interlaminar approach, having the added advantage of familiar anatomy for spine surgeons, would be a better approach with no hindrance of the iliac crest. The transforaminal approach has a longer surgical time and increased radiation exposure due to multiple approach attempts [36,37]. Sharma et al. [38] successfully reported the use of transforaminal endoscopy for large central disc herniations under local anesthesia [38]. However, in cases of large central/paracentral lumbar disc herniations, the “Kambin's triangle” is not completely safe as the dural sac becomes flat and laterally expands due to disc compression [39]. However, in our opinion, transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy for upper lumbar segments, that is, L1/2 and L2/3, is preferred over interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy due to the narrow interlaminar window in upper lumbar segments.

Performing PTELD (percutaneous transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy) under local anesthesia can have medicolegal implications [40]. Under local anesthesia, the patient can become nervous, anxious, and even move intraoperatively, increasing the operative time [41], and pain experienced by the patient poses various challenges to successfully completing the operation [42]. A meta-analysis by Mooney et al. [42] demonstrated that a higher percentage of PELD patients achieved an excellent McNab score using GA as compared with LA at the final follow-up. Chen et al. [43], in their meta-analysis, reported better results with interlaminar endoscopic lumbar discectomy than transforaminal endoscopic lumbar discectomy in L5–S1 disc herniation due to the wide interlaminar space. PELD in the L5–S1 inter-laminar space can remove a ventrally migrated L5–S1 disc and a down-migrated disc in L4–L5 disc herniation [44].

Several studies have shown that the results of endoscopic decompression are comparable or achieve better results than microscopic decompression [15,45,46]. Lee et al. [15], in their study of 213 patients (232 levels) with lumbar canal stenosis, showed the efficacy of uniportal unilateral endoscopic decompression as there was no revision case due to incomplete decompression. The above study mentions zero incomplete decompression in lumbar canal stenosis; likewise, with the same technique, we also report no incomplete decompression in patients with cauda equina syndrome. Uniportal interlaminar endoscopy for cauda equina syndrome is an advanced technique with a steep learning curve and unsuitable for beginners. We did postoperative MRI to check adequacy of decompression and thus avoid medicolegal complications. In their study, Krishnan et al. [40] also performed postoperative MRI to confirm decompression in all patients undergoing transforaminal discectomy for cauda equina syndrome. We also understand the financial constraints of doing post-operative MRI in every patient. So, the surgeon has to follow local guidelines of their institute.

There are still challenges to overcome with endoscopic decompression such as iatrogenic instability due to the inevitable violation of the medial facet joint [47]. Thus, uniportal interlaminar endoscopic decompression and discectomy is an effective alternative to open laminectomy for patients with cauda equina syndrome and has the following benefits: less trauma, less post-operative pain, shorter hospitalization, and lower postoperative infection rates [48].

Limitations

In the study period, the authors did not perform any open surgery in the form of microdiscectomy or microdecompression for all of their cases; hence, this is a retrospective study with no control groups. We have evaluated this study by reviewing previous studies' data on decompression techniques in patients with cauda equina syndrome. Our sample size was small, and a single surgeon performed all the cases in our study; thus, there is a limitation in generalizing his experience to all other spine surgeons. A prospective randomized control study comparing uniportal interlaminar endoscopic discectomy and decompression with other decompression methods would be ideal. The bladder bowel recovery score used in patient follow-up is non-validated.

Conclusion

Uniportal interlaminar endoscopic discectomy and decompression surgery provides satisfactory clinical results in patients with cauda equina syndrome and can be considered an alternative to open laminectomy. In experienced hands, this technique yields excellent clinical outcomes in patients with cauda equina syndrome. There is a paucity of literature on this technique in patients with cauda equina syndrome; therefore, more surgeons need to implement this technique in their practice and report their results in future studies.
