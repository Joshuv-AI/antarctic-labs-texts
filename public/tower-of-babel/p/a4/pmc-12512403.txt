Tailored brain metastatic tumor cells-derived apoptotic bodies ameliorate Alzheimer’s disease by promoting microglia efferocytosis and neuroinflammation mitigation
Authors: Zhenlong Mu, Yang Chen, Yunfeng Hu, Hui Wang, Jin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Neuroinflammation, characterized by microglial overactivation and oxidative stress, plays a critical role in the initiation and progression of Alzheimer’s disease (AD). In this study, we focus on simulating the natural efferocytosis process to reprogram microglial and mitigate chronic neuroinflammation for combinational AD therapy. To achieve this goal, engineered apoptotic bodies derived from brain metastatic tumor cells (LAbs) are successfully developed. Specifically, LAbs-based nanocomposites were fabricated by hybridizing LAbs with liposomes co‐loaded with manganese dioxide nanoenzyme (BMn) and autophagy‐activating rapamycin (Rapa), referred to as LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa exhibits efficient BBB penetration via LAbs‐associated brain metastasis propensity of apoptotic bodies. Within the AD microenvironment, oxygen produced through BMn catalyzation in response to H2O2 triggers the structural disintegration of LAbs‐camouflaged liposomes and their reassembly into ultra‐small vesicles, thereby significantly enhancing intracranial delivery efficiency. In vitro and in vivo experiments confirm that this multi‐target strategy effectively normalizes microglia toward anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, scavenges reactive oxide species (ROS) accumulation, promotes β‐amyloid and phosphorylated tau clearance through synergistic intervention, restores the pathological microenvironment in the brain, and enhances cognitive functions in AD model mice. This study demonstrates a novel LAbs‐based biomimetic construction strategy that effectively penetrates the BBB and regulates microglia functions, offering a promising approach for AD treatment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03750-y.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a progressive, age-related, and multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder that leads to memory impairment and cognitive decline [1, 2]. According to the 2024 report from Alzheimer’s Disease International, more than 50 million individuals worldwide were affected by AD, and this number was projected to triple by 2050 [3]. This escalating prevalence causes substantial suffering for both patients and caregivers, and imposes a significant economic burden on societies worldwide.

Despite extensive research on AD treatment in recent years, the blood–brain barrier (BBB) restricts the delivery of therapeutic agents into the brain, limiting their therapeutic effects. As apoptotic bodies (Abs) are specialized membrane-enclosed extracellular vesicles (EVs) released during programmed cell death [4–6], they retain a significant proportion of membrane proteins inherited from their parent cells [7–9]. This retention enables Abs to preserve the functional characteristics of their parent cells, which is especially advantageous for brain-targeted delivery mediated by specific membrane proteins. Certain cancer cells, particularly those prone to brain metastasis, can penetrate the BBB, and this ability can potentially be leveraged to enhance drug accumulation in the brain. Lung cancer is the most frequent origin of metastatic brain tumors, accounting for an estimated 25%–50% of all cases [10, 11]. Studies indicate that CD44 proteins are crucial in lung cancer cell migration and brain metastasis [12]. Mouse Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells, known for their high CD44 protein expression levels, may use these proteins to cross the BBB by interacting with brain endothelial cells [13, 14]. Considering the well-preserved membrane proteins, these findings suggest that Abs derived from LLC cells can traverse the BBB through the transcytosis pathway and, hence, are promising candidates for novel brain-targeted delivery systems.

AD is characterized by two pathological hallmarks: extracellular amyloid beta-protein (Aβ) plaques and intracellular neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) composed of hyperphosphorylated tau protein [15]. These are closely associated with chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, and the progressive loss of neurons and synapses [16, 17]. The exact mechanisms of AD are still unclear, and whether these neuropathological changes are primary causes or secondary consequences of the disease remains a point of contention. Among various hypotheses, neuroinflammation, triggered by immune stimuli in the brain and mediated by activated microglia, is recognized as a significant process in the initiation and progression of AD [18, 19].

Microglia serve as the resident innate immune cells in the brain. Under physiological conditions, resting microglia perform immune surveillance and maintenance homeostasis in the central nervous system (CNS) [20]. The neurodegenerative process in AD induces microglial activation. Overactivated microglia, predominantly adopting a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, release excessive neuroimmune inflammatory cytokine, leading to amplified neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity. Moreover, these M1‐like microglia exhibit impaired phagocytic capacity, struggling to effectively clear pathological protein deposits. Conversely, the anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype demonstrates neuroprotective abilities by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines, promoting the phagocytosis of pathological protein aggregates and neuronal debris, and releasing various brain‐derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF). Therefore, restoring microglial homeostasis and facilitating the transition of M1‐type microglia to the neuroprotective M2 phenotype may be a promising strategy for ameliorating neuroinflammation throughout the pathological progression of AD.

The externalization of phosphatidylserine (PS) on the cell surface of apoptotic cells acts as a critical “eat me” signal, triggering phagocytosis and clearance of these dying cells by phagocytes, a process known as efferocytosis [21]. Extensive studies confirm that engulfing apoptotic cells modulate the immune responses by activating anti-inflammatory pathways [22]. Abs inherit surface signaling molecules and exhibit PS exposure, indicating their potential role in initiating macrophage polarization and inflammation resolution. Therefore, LAbs released from apoptotic LLC cells can target inflammatory regions through efferocytosis, transforming M1-like microglia into anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes. This indicates the ability of LAbs to serve as optimal EVs for alleviating neuroinflammation in AD.

Oxidative stress is another key factor in the neuropathological change in early-stage AD, and is closely associated with the formation of Aβ plaque and NFTs [23]. This pathological process disrupts the homeostasis of reactive oxygen species (ROS), leading to their excessive accumulation and impaired clearance within neurons. In addition, ROS, acting as pro‐inflammatory signaling molecules, can further activate microglia and progressively exacerbate neuroinflammation [24]. Based on these findings, targeting oxidative stress to restore intracellular oxidation–antioxidant balance by eliminating ROS is a promising strategy for alleviating AD symptoms. Nanoenzymes are artificial enzymes mimicking natural enzyme catalysis, exhibiting remarkable enzyme‐like activity and high stability, particularly in regulating redox homeostasis. Therefore, nanozymes are extensively applied in the treatment of various diseases such as inflammation [25], cancer [26], and neurodegenerative disease [27]. Accumulating evidence demonstrates that manganese dioxide nanozymes (BMn) possess both superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) activities. BMn, with this dual enzymatic activity, effectively scavenges the ROS accumulated in the CNS, significantly protecting neurons from oxidative damage. Furthermore, the oxygen (O2) burst released during CAT‐mediated ROS degradation disrupts and reorganizes BMn‐encapsulated liposome structures, achieving cascade amplification of therapeutic effects.

This study aimed to develop a novel nanotherapeutic system camouflaged with LAbs for multitarget intervention in AD. Specifically, BMn was successfully encapsulated within the inner aqueous cavity of liposomes using the reverse–phase evaporation technique, while the hydrophobic autophagy activator rapamycin (Rapa) was incorporated into the lipid bilayer. Subsequently, the resulting liposomes co-loaded with BMn and Rapa (Lip@BMn/Rapa) were hybridized with LAbs to obtain LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa (Scheme 1). After intravenous injection of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, the camouflaging with LAbs may enhance BBB permeability and drug accumulation in the brain. The excess hydrogen peroxide (H2O2) readily diffuses into the internal aqueous cavity, where it is catalyzed by the enclosed BMn to produce substantial O2 bubbles. The rapid release of O2 disrupts the liposomal membrane, releasing reconstituted ultra‐small Rapa‐loaded LAbs vesicles, potentially enhancing intracerebral drug delivery. Subsequently, the exposure of PS enables the LAbs based nanocomposites to be specifically recognized and engulfed by M1‐like microglial to relieve neuroinflammation.

LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa promotes microglia efferocytosis and scavenges ROS, demonstrating a synergistic effect in alleviating microglial cell activation and restoring the disturbed oxidation-antioxidant homeostasis. Therefore, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa offers a promising multi‐target strategy for AD therapy. We conducted both in vitro and in vivo experiments to comprehensively validate these amplified neuroprotective effects and impaired cognitive recovery. The findings of this study may provide novel evidence for realizing multitargeted therapy for AD based on early‐stage neuropathological microenvironment intervention, and may have better potential for clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Materials

DOTAP and cholesterol were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Shanghai, China). Aβ1–42 peptides were from Top–Peptide Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Mouse anti–human Aβ1–42 antibody was purchased from China Peptides Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from Tianhang Biotechnology Co. (Hangzhou, China). Bovine serum albumin was purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). Coumarin–6 and Thioflavin–T was purchased from Aladdin (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals and reagents were commercially qualified reagents.

Cell lines and animals

Mouse brain microvascular endothelial cells (bEnd.3), mouse microglial cells (BV2), mouse hippocampal neuronal cells (HT22), and mouse Lewis lung carcinoma cells (LLC) were purchased from the Cell Bank of Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). bEnd.3 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 Medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). BV2, HT22 and LLC cells were maintained in DMEM containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Each cell line was incubated with a humidity of 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Female Kunming mice were provided by the Experimental Animal Center of Xuzhou Medical University (Jiangsu, China). Animals were housed in a standard laboratory conditions with a 12/12 h light/dark cycle and with ad libitum access to food and water.

Isolation and characterization of Abs

The mouse Lewis lung carcinoma (LLC) cells were exposed to ultraviolet (UV) radiation for 30 min at an intensity of 150 mJ/cm2, followed by H2O2 treatment (800 nM) for 24 h to induce apoptosis. The culture medium was collected following apoptosis induction, and Abs were isolated through gradient centrifugation, as outlined in Fig. 1A. Briefly, after centrifugation at 300g for 10 min at 4℃, the dead cells and debris were removed. A further separation was conducted at 3000g for 20 min at 4℃. Subsequently, the supernatant was concentrated by further centrifugation at 12,000g for 30 min at 4℃ to enrich the LAbs. The sediments were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and finally resuspended in 500 µL of PBS. The isolated LAbs were stored at − 80 °C for further use.

The morphology of LAbs was observed using transmission electron microscopy (TEM; FEI Company, OR, USA). Size distribution was analyzed using dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Zetasizer Nano ZSE (Malvern Instruments, UK). Flow cytometry analysis was performed using an Annexin V Fluorescein Isothiocyanate (Annexin V–FITC)/propidium iodide (PI) apoptosis detection kit (A005–2; 7Sea Biotech, China) to evaluate the Annexin V+ ratios of Abs compared with non–H2O2/UV-treated LLC cells. Annexin V–FITC staining was also captured using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM; Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) to detect PS as a specific Abs marker. Sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) was conducted to characterize the protein profile. LAbs were labeled with coumarin–6 (C6) to investigate the internalization by microglia in vitro. Free C6 was used as a negative control. Specifically, mouse microglial (BV2) cells were seeded into 24–well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with either C6-labeled LAbs (C6-LAbs, 20 µg/mL) or free C6 at an equivalent dye concentration for 4 h. After incubation, the cells were washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min. Nuclei were stained with DAPI (1:1000). Fluorescence images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan), and intracellular fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA). Additionally, the distribution of deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) fragments in LLC cells and LAbs was further investigated via agarose gel electrophoresis (Biowest, France).

Synthesis and characterization of BMn

Bovine serum albumin (BSA)–templated BMn were synthesized according to a previously reported method [28]. Specifically, 250 mg of BSA and 31.6 mg of KMnO4 were separately dissolved in 7 mL and 3 mL of deionized water, respectively. The KMnO4 solution was then added dropwise to the BSA solution and further stirred at 37 °C for 1.5 h until the solution turned dark brown. The obtained BMn were purified by dialysis (MWCO = 8000–14,000) in distilled water for 24 h. The dialyzed product was lyophilized and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential were measured using a Nicomp 380/ZLS zeta potential/particle size analyzer (PSS; NICOMP Particle Systems, CA, USA). Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FT–IR) was performed using a Shimadzu IRAffinity–1 S Fourier transform infrared spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan), and ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) absorption spectra were recorded using a Shimadzu UV–2600i spectrophotometer (Shimadzu, Japan). The morphological examination of BMn was observed via TEM. Additionally, x–ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) and x–ray diffraction (XRD) were also conducted to further characterize the chemical composition and crystallographic structure of BMn.

Preparation of Lip@BMn/Rapa

Lip@BMn/Rapa was prepared via the reverse–phase evaporation method [29]. In brief, 1,2–Dioleoyl–3–trimethylammonium–propane (DOTAP), cholesterol, and Rapa with masses of 3.6 mg, 2.4 mg, and 0.38 mg, respectively, were individually dissolved in 4.2 mL of a chloroform and ether (1.2:3, v/v) mixture to form the organic phase. Subsequently, the aqueous phase containing 1 mg of BMn in 0.7 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) was introduced, followed by sonication in an ice water bath for 15 min at a frequency of 40 kHz and a power of 480 W. The aqueous phase was then added dropwise into the organic phase to form a stable water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion. The resulting W/O emulsion was then transferred to a round-bottom flask, and the organic solvents were evaporated at 30℃ under high vacuum conditions using a rotary evaporator (RE2000B; Yarong Biochemical Instrument Factory, Shanghai, China) until the gel-like phase disappeared. The resulting lipid film was hydrated with 5 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) and subsequently sonicated using a SCIENTZ-IID probe sonicator (SCIENTZ, China) at an output power of 200 W for 6 min. The final Lip@BMn/Rapa nanoparticles were purified by sequential filtration through 0.45– µm and 0.22– µm polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane filters (Millipore, USA).

Preparation and characterization of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa

The extracted LAbs were mixed with Lip@BMn/Rapa at a 1:1 mass ratio to prepare LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. Subsequently, the mixture was subjected to five freeze–thaw cycles between approximately 4 °C and 45 °C to facilitate the fusion of liposomal membranes with LAbs. The resulting suspension was then extruded through 400–nm and 200–nm polycarbonate membranes (10 times each) using an Avanti Mini Extruder (Avanti Polar Lipids, AL, USA). The final LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa was collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min. The hydrodynamic diameter and zeta potential of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa were measured by DLS, and their morphology was examined using TEM. Furthermore, the FT–IR spectra of Lip@BMn/Rapa, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa and LAbs were also analyzed. Protein retention on the LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa surface was evaluated via SDS–PAGE followed by Coomassie Brilliant Blue staining.

Catalase-like activity of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa

The catalase-like activity of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa was evaluated by monitoring the decomposition of H2O2 and the associated generation of O2. H2O2 levels were detected using an Amplex red fluorescent assay kit (Aladdin, Shanghai, China), which relies the reaction between H2O2 and Amplex red in the presence of horseradish peroxidase (HRP) as a catalyst, yielding a red fluorescent oxidation product. In brief, 3 mL of BMn or LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa was individually mixed with 3 mL of H2O2 solution (200 µM) and incubated at 37 °C. At predetermined intervals, 500 µL of the sample was withdrawn and mixed with Amplex Red (100 µM) and HRP(0.1 U/mL). The absorbance at 570 nm was subsequently measured using UV–vis analysis to accurately quantify the residual H2O2 concentration and a time-dependent degradation curve of H2O2 was plotted. An equal volume of PBS (pH 7.4) was mixed with H2O2 to establish a control group.

A portable, dissolved oxygen meter (JPBJ–608; Shanghai Yidian Scientific Instrument Co., Shanghai, China) was used to investigate the dissolved O2 concentrations. In this assay, 3 mL of BMn or LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa was mixed with 3 mL of H2O2 (200 µM). Representative images capturing O2 production were recorded, and the O2 concentration was measured at designated intervals to plot an O2 generation–time curve.

H2O2-triggered allosteric recombination and drug release

H2O2-induced allosteric recombination of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa was evaluated in terms of morphological changes and drug release behavior pre- and post-reaction with H2O2. Specifically, TEM was used to visualize the morphological alterations of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa following treatment with H2O2. Additionally, the in vitro release profiles of Rapa and Mn ions from LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa were assayed to compare the impact of H2O2 on drug release. In brief, dialysis bags (molecular weight: 8–14 kDa) containing 2 mL of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa were suspended in 40 mL of PBS (pH 7.4) containing 100 µM H2O2. The release of Rapa was quantified at different time intervals using high–performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), and the concentrations of released Mn ions were measured using inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP–OES, iCAP6300, Thermo Scientific, USA). Parallel control experiments were conducted under the same conditions but without the addition of H2O2.

•OH and O2− scavenging

3,3’,5,5’–tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) was selected as a substrate, which could be oxidized by •OH radicals to generate chromogenic products with a characteristic absorption peak at 652 nm. In detail, a solution containing 400 µL of TMB (2 mM) was mixed with 400 µL of H2O2 (100 mM) and 20 µL of FeSO4·7H2O (5 mg/mL) in the presence of either BMn or Lip@BMn/Rapa at room temperature. A control group containing only H2O2 and FeSO4·7H2O (without BMn and Lip@BMn/Rapa) was used to generate •OH radicals. The absorbance at 652 nm was measured using a UV–vis spectrophotometer, and the reduction in absorbance was used to quantify the •OH radicals scavenging capacity.

The O2− radical scavenging activity was analyzed using the superoxide anion assay kit (Solarbio, China) based on the hydroxylamine hydrochloride oxidation method. The reaction between O2− radicals and hydroxylamine hydrochloride resulted in the generation of NO2−, which subsequently formed a red azo compound with p–aminobenzenesulfonic acid and α–naphthylamine, exhibiting a characteristic absorbance at 530 nm. O2− radicals were generated using the ammonium persulphate–N, N,N′,N′-tetramethylethylenediamine (AP–TEMED) system. BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa were separately introduced into the reaction system. The absorbance at 530 nm was measured to determine the extent of O2− scavenging.

Electron spin resonance spectroscopy for ROS detection

The ability of BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa to eliminate O2− and •OH was further evaluated using an electron spin resonance (ESR) spectroscope (Bruker MS5000, Germany). A mixture of xanthine (10 mM) and xanthine oxidase (1 U/mL) was used to generate O2− ESR signals, which were subsequently trapped by the spin-trapping agent 5,5–dimethyl–1–pyrroline N–oxide (DMPO). ESR spectra were recorded both in the presence and absence of BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. Similarly, •OH radicals were generated by combining FeSO4·7H2O (10 mM) with H2O2 (40 mM), and then BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa were added to the mixture. DMPO was used to trap the •OH signals and form the spin adduct (DMPO/•OH).

Transportation across the BBB in vitro

The penetration capability of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa across the BBB was evaluated using an in vitro transwell BBB model. Specifically, the mouse brain microvascular endothelial (bEnd.3) cells were seeded at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well in the upper chamber of the transwell plate and incubated for several days to form a confluent monolayer. The integrity of the cell monolayer was verified by measuring transendothelial electrical resistance (TEER), with values exceeding 250 Ω/cm².

In the BBB model, C6-labeled Lip@BMn (named C6-Lip@BMn) and LAbs@Lip@BMn (named C6-LAbs@Lip@BMn) were incubated with tight junctions formed by b.End3 cells to investigate the crossing ability. At designated time points, 200 µL aliquots were withdrawn from the lower chamber for detecting C6 fluorescence intensity using a multifunctional microplate reader (Varioskan LUX; Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at excitation/emission wavelengths of 480/590 nm. We knocked down CD44 protein expression in LLC cells and isolated the derived Abs to prepare KD-LAbs@Lip@BMn as controls to investigate whether CD44 was a key mediator for enhanced BBB crossing. Western blot (WB) analysis was also employed to evaluate the expression levels of CD44 protein in LLC cells, LAbs, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. The CD44-knockdown LLC cells (KD) served as a control. In brief, the samples were incubated with primary antibodies (anti–CD44, AB2080, Merck, Germany; anti–GAPDH, BM0627, Boster, China) overnight at 4℃. Subsequently, the secondary antibody was added and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. The CD44 protein expression levels of different groups were detected under a V37 scanner (Epson, Japan).

Furthermore, we conducted an investigation into the neuronal uptake of C6-LAbs@Lip@BMn after BBB penetration. Specifically, b.End3 cells at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well were cultured on the apical chamber of the Transwell insert, while mouse hippocampal neuronal (HT22) cells were seeded in the basal side of the lower chamber at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well. TEER values were measured every 24 h to confirm ensure the integrity and tightness of the BBB model. Subsequently, the medium in the upper chamber was replaced with the medium containing C6-Lip@BMn or C6-LAbs@Lip@BMn. After incubated for 4 h, HT22 cells in the lower chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI. Cellular uptake was visualized using an inverted fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

Intracellular ROS scavenging and O2 generation

Intracellular ROS levels were assessed using the peroxide-sensitive fluorescent probe 2’,7’-Dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH–DA). Specifically, BV2 and HT22 cells were incubated in 24–well plates (1 × 105 cells/well) for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with a Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) medium containing H2O2 (250 µM) to induce oxidative stress and cultured for another 24 h. Afterward, cells were incubated with 10 µM of DCFH–DA for 30 min, washed twice with PBS, and subsequently treated with LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for additional 2 h. Finally, the intracellular green fluorescence was imaged under an inverted fluorescence microscope. Cells without H2O2 incubation served as negative controls, while those treated with H2O2 alone were used as positive controls.

The intracellular generation of O2 was measured using the [(Ru(dpp)3)]Cl2 probe (Ruixi, China). In particular, the medium containing 10 µM [(Ru(dpp)3)]Cl2 was added to H2O2-pretreated BV2 and HT22 cell models, followed by further incubation for 30 min. Subsequently, the cells were treated with LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for another 2 h. Following PBS washing, the cells were stained with DAPI and imaged using an inverted fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ software.

Investigation of microglia polarization

We further investigated the potential effects of LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa on microglia polarization in vitro through Immunofluorescence staining, flow cytometry assays, and enzyme–linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) detection. BV2 cells were polarized into M1-like or M2-like phenotypes using a conditioned medium containing lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (100 ng/mL) for M1 polarization or IL–4 (20 ng/mL) for M2 polarization, serving as positive controls. Non-stimulated BV2 cells were considered M0 phenotype. For comparison, BV2 cells representing M0, M1, and M2 phenotypes were included as controls.

Immunofluorescence staining was conducted to evaluate the expression of CD86 (M1 marker) and CD206 (M2 marker). Specifically, M1-like phenotype BV2 cells were seeded in confocal culture dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells per dish and incubated with LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for 12 h. The cells were then stained with anti–CD206–FITC (1:200) and anti–CD86–PE (1:200) overnight at 4℃. Finally, the cells were stained with DAPI and imaged using CLSM. Quantitative analysis of CD86 and CD206 fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software. For flow cytometry analysis, M1-like phenotype BV2 cells were stained with anti–CD86–PE (1:200) and anti–CD206–FITC (1:200) for 30 min at 4℃, and analyzed using a flow cytometer (BD FACSCantoⅡ, NJ, USA). Additionally, cell supernatants from different treatment groups were collected for quantification of interleukin–6 (IL–6) and IL–10 levels using respective ELISA kits.

Microglia proliferation and migration after polarization

In vitro scratch tests and transwell migration experiments were conducted to further evaluate the effects of microglia polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype on the proliferation and migration behaviors of BV2 cells. BV2 cells pre-polarized into M0, M1, and M2 phenotypes were used as controls.

For the microglia scratch test, the BV2 cells were seeded into 24–well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and cultured for 24 h. A sterile pipette tip (200 µL) was then used to create a uniform scratch. Subsequently, the M1-like BV2 cells were incubated with complete medium containing LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for 12 h. The width of the scratches was observed using an inverted microscope (DMI4000D; Leica, Germany) and quantified by ImageJ software.

An in vitro transwell microglia model was established to evaluate the migration of polarized microglia. In brief, M1 phenotype BV2 cells were seeded in the upper chambers at a density of 1 × 105 cells/well and incubated for 24 h. After treatment with a complete medium containing LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for 12 h, nonmigrated BV2 cells on the upper side of the membrane were gently removed using a cotton swab. In contrast, the remaining migrated cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet for 10 min. Images of the migrated BV2 cells were then captured under an inverted microscope (DMI4000D, Leica, Germany). The number of migrated cells was quantified in three randomly selected fields per well using ImageJ. The relative migration rate was calculated by normalizing the migrated cell counts from each treatment group to those of the M0 control group.

WB analysis of BDNF following microglia polarization

WB analysis was performed to assess the expression levels of BDNF secreted by M2-like microglia. Briefly, protein extracts from M1-like BV2 cells treated with various formulations (LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa) were separated using SDS–PAGE gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes. The membranes were incubated overnight at 4℃ with primary antibodies, including the anti–BDNF antibody (1:2000; HuaBio, China) and anti–GAPDH antibody (1:10,000; HuaBio, China). HRP–conjugated secondary antibodies were used to detect the primary antibodies. The BDNF protein expression levels of different groups were detected under a V37 scanner. Densitometric analysis of the bands was performed using ImageJ software. The M0, M1, and M2 phenotypes of BV2 cells served as controls.

Investigation of microglia immunophagocytosis normalization

The M1-like BV2 cells were seeded in confocal culture dishes at a density of 2 × 104 cells/dish and incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for 1 h. All samples were subsequently incubated with 10 µg/mL of FITC-labeled Aβ1–42 for an additional 15 min. DAPI and LysoTracker Red were used to stain the cell nucleus and lysosomes, respectively. After three washes with PBS, cellular co-localization were assessed observed using CLSM.

Thioflavin T fluorescence assay

The amelioration of tau-induced neuronal damage in HT22 cells was characterized by the thioflavin T (ThT) fluorescence assay. Specifically, HT22 cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were cultured in 24–well plates for 24 h, followed by treatment with 80 nM okadaic acid (OA) for an additional 24 h to induce intracellular phosphorylated tau (p–tau) aggregation. Subsequently, the cells were treated with Rapa, LAbs@Lip, LAbs@Lip@BMn, or LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for 12 h. Untreated cells (without OA) served as the control group. These cells were further incubated with ThT (20 µM) for 20 min. Finally, the cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with DAPI to label the nuclei. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope. Quantitative analysis of ThT fluorescence intensity was performed using ImageJ software.

Investigation of neuroprotective effects in vitro

JC–1 staining was performed to measure the mitochondrial membrane potential in neurons. HT22 cells were seeded in confocal culture dishes at a density of 3 × 105 cells/dish and incubated for 24 h. Neurotoxic models were established by treating the cells with Aβ1–42 (25 µM) for 24 h. Subsequently, LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa were administered and incubated for another 2 h. The cells without treatment with the aforementioned formulations and H2O2 exposure served as the control group. The medium was then removed, and JC–1 staining working solution (1 mL) was added and incubated at 37℃ for 30 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using CLSM. The red-to-green fluorescence intensity ratio was measured from fluorescence images using ImageJ software.

The viability of HT22 cells following Aβ1–42 stimulation was further assessed using live/dead cell staining and Cell Counting Kit–8 (CCK–8) assays (Beyotime, China). Specifically, HT22 cells were cultured in 96–well plates (1.0 × 104 cells/well) for 24 h and then pretreated with Aβ1–42 (25 µM) for an additional 24 h to construct neurotoxic models. Subsequently, the cells were exposed to LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for 2 h. Following incubation, the cells were stained with Calcein Acetoxymethyl Ester/Propidium Iodide (Calcein–AM/PI, Beyotime, China) for 30 min. Fluorescence images were acquired using an inverted fluorescence microscope. The green-to-red fluorescence intensity ratio was measured from fluorescence images using ImageJ software.

Briefly, HT22 cells pre-stimulated with Aβ1–42 were co–incubated with different formulations (LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa) at concentrations of 10, 50, 100, and 200 µg/mL for 4 h. Subsequently, 100 µL of CCK–8 working solution (10 µg/mL) was added and incubated for an additional 1 h, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan LUX). In vivo and ex vivo imaging of brain-targeted properties

The in vivo BBB penetration was evaluated using the in vivo imaging system (IVIS, PerkinElmer, China). Both Lip@BMn, KD-LAbs@Lip@BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn were labeled with near-infrared dye DiR. Four experimental groups were designed, in which female Kunming mice (n = 48) were intravenously injected via the tail vein with free DiR, DiR-Lip@BMn, DiR-LAbs@Lip@BMn, or DiR-KD-LAbs@Lip@BMn at a DiR dose of 0.4 mg/kg. The mice were anesthetized with 2% isoflurane, and fluorescence images were captured at predetermined time points. After completing in vivo imaging at 48 h, the brains and major organs were dissected for further analysis using ex vivo imaging. Fluorescence signals were analyzed and quantified using Living Image software.

Establishment of an AD mouse model

An AD mouse model was established through bilateral intrahippocampal co-injection of Aβ1–42 and OA [30, 31]. This procedure induced the accumulation of neurotoxic Aβ plaques and tau NFTs in the brain, leading to neuronal damage and deficits in learning and memory. Specifically, Aβ1–42 (5 µL, 100 µM) was mixed with OA (1 µL, 2 µM) and then slowly injected into the bilateral hippocampus (anteroposterior −2.5 mm, mediolateral ± 1.5 mm, and dorsoventral −2.0 mm) at a rate of 2 µL/min. In contrast, sham control mice were administered an equivalent volume of saline using the same protocol. All mice were allowed a 1–week recovery period prior to the administration.

Treatment of AD model mice

As illustrated in the experimental timeline (Fig. 7 C), AD model mice were randomly assigned into six groups (n = 10 per group). Mice in the treatment groups were administered intravenous injections of 200 µL of LAbs@Lip (LAbs protein dosage of 0.5 mg/kg), LAbs@Lip@BMn (LAbs protein dosage of 0.5 mg/kg; BMn dosage of 0.136mmol/kg), and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa (LAbs protein dosage of 0.5 mg/kg; BMn dosage of 0.136mmol/kg; and Rapa dosage of 0.23 mg/kg) every alternate day for 4 weeks. The mice in the Rapa-only group were administered intravenous injections of Rapa at a dosage of 2 mg/kg. Mice in the sham and AD control groups were administered intravenous injections of saline.

Morris water maze test

The Morris water maze (MWM) test was performed to evaluate the learning ability and spatial memory of mice [32]. The experimental process involved training for 5 days and a probe trial on the sixth day. The mice were trained in a circular pool measuring 160 cm in diameter and 50 cm in height. The water depth was set at 30 cm, and the temperature was maintained at 25 °C ± 1 °C. The MWM was divided into four quadrants, and one designated quadrant was equipped with an invisible submerged platform (9 cm in diameter), 1 cm below the water surface. The first 5 days involved training each mouse for four trials in different quadrants. Mice were given 60 s to find the hidden platform. If the mice failed to find the platform within the 60–s time limit, they were gently guided to the platform and allowed to stay for 15 s. The probe trials were performed on the sixth day with the platform removed, and the mice were allowed to swim in the pool for 60 s. The escape latency, swimming paths, time spent in the target quadrant, and number of crossings over the previous platform location were recorded and analyzed using the software provided with the MWM system.

Nest construction task

The nest construction experiment was conducted to assess the cognitive abilities of mice [33]. After treatment, mice from different groups were individually placed into separate cages, each containing 20 square towels (5 cm × 5 cm) for nesting behavior assessment. The mice were allowed to move freely, and their nesting behavior was recorded daily by capturing photographs and calculating the nesting scores.

Immunofluorescence and immunohistochemical analyses and Nissl staining

After completing the behavioral tests, mice were sacrificed and their brains were harvested for immunostaining assays. Specifically, mice were anesthetized and then underwent intracardiac perfusion with saline and 4% paraformaldehyde. Their brains were immediately excised and further fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and then dehydrated using a graded ethanol series before being embedded in paraffin for sectioning. The collected brain sections were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃, followed by incubation with fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. The following primary antibodies were used: 8–hydroxyguanosine (8–OHG) antibody (1:1000; Bioss, China), β–amyloid antibody (1:1000; CST, MA, USA), and ionized calcium–binding adapter molecule–1 (IBA–1) antibody (1:1000; Servicebio, China). The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min at room temperature. The images were captured using CLSM and mean fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ software.

For immunohistochemical analysis, the pre-prepared brain sections were subjected to antigen retrieval and blocking of nonspecific antibody binding. The brain sections were incubated with a primary antibody, specifically anti–p–tau Ser396 (1:500; Abcam, MA, USA), in a blocking buffer overnight at 4℃. This was followed by incubation with the secondary antibody for 20 min at room temperature. For chromogenic staining, 3,3′–diaminobenzidine (DAB) was used as the substrate, followed by hematoxylin counterstaining. The images were subsequently captured using a optical microscope and mean DAB staining intensity was measured using ImageJ software.

Furthermore, brain slices were stained with Nissl staining solutions, and the neuronal injury indicated by the Nissl staining was obtained under a pathological section scanner (3DHistech, Hungary) and analyzed using CaseViewer software (Biossci, Hubei, China).

Evaluation of safety profiles

Hemolysis assays, hematoxylin–eosin (H&E) staining, and ICP–OES measurements were investigated to evaluate biosafety. For hemolysis analysis, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa was diluted with saline at different concentrations and then mixed with freshly prepared 2% rabbit red blood cell (RBC) suspensions. Distilled water and saline mixed with 2% RBC suspensions served as positive (A100%) and negative controls (A0%), respectively. All samples were incubated at 37℃ for 1 h and subsequently centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, followed by measuring the absorbance at 541 nm using a UV–vis spectrometer (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The hemolysis rate (HR) was calculated as follows: HR (%) = (Asample − A0%)/(A100% − A0%) × 100%.

After completing behavior studies, the mice from each group were sacrificed for H&E analysis. The harvested brains and major organs were fixed with 4% formalin, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 5–µm–thick slices. The slices were stained with a 2% hematoxylin solution, followed by counterstaining with a 0.5% eosin solution, and were then examined under an optical microscope. The organs from the saline-treated mice and AD model mice served as controls.

Mice (n = 3) were euthanized on days 1, 7, and 14 following a single intravenous administration of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, and on days 1 and 7 after 4 weeks of repeated dosing, to assess the in vivo manganese (Mn) elemental content. The brains and other major organs were harvested, weighed, and solubilized using aqua regia. The Mn concentrations in these organs were quantified using the ICP–OES method. Mice injected with an equal volume of saline served as the control group.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was evaluated using a two–tailed Student t test or one–way analysis of variance (ANOVA) (SPSS, version 16.0). Differences were considered statistically significant as follows: *P < 0.05; **P < 0.01; ***P < 0.001; and ****P < 0.0001.

Results and discussion

Isolation and characterization of LAbs

The mouse LLC cells with a well-established high brain metastasis capacity were selected to fabricate engineered LAbs, which were capable of mimicking the apoptotic characteristic of LLC cells and exhibiting subsequent in vivo biological function. As depicted in Fig. 1 A, the LAbs were obtained by inducing apoptosis of LLC cells through co-intervention of UV radiation and H2O2 stimulation, followed by sequential purification procedures [34]. Flow cytometry analysis in Fig. 1B demonstrated that the apoptosis rate of LLC cells exposed to H2O2/UV treatment exceeded 75% compared with the untreated group (6.48%), indicating the successful induction of apoptosis in LLC cells. The morphology of LAbs was characterized under TEM observation. As shown in Fig. 1 C, LAbs exhibited typical spherical and cell-derived vesicles with membrane structures, with an average diameter of approximately 100 nm.

The hydrodynamic diameter of LAbs was 116.01 ± 2.41 nm (Fig. 1D), with a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.19 ± 0.03, measured by DLS analysis. The zeta potential value of LAbs was determined to be − 16.66 ± 2.95 mV (Fig. S2). The exposure of PS on the outer surface of apoptotic cells represents its distinctive structural feature, serving as an important “eat me” signal for their phagocytosis by macrophages [35]. Therefore, FITC-labeled Annexin V, an endogenous human protein with a high affinity with PS, was employed in vitro to detect apoptosis associated with programmed cell death and LAbs. As depicted in Fig. 1E, LAbs exhibited strong green fluorescence with no detectable blue fluorescence from DAPI (nucleus). In contrast, the LLC cells showed an opposite staining result, and exosomes derived from LLC cells displayed neither green nor blue fluorescence. These results confirmed the positive and distinctive presence of PS on the collected LAbs. The externalization of PS served as an essential “eat me” signal, facilitating specific recognition and efficient phagocytosis by microglia [36]. As shown in Fig. 1 F, SDS–PAGE analysis revealed that proteins from LLC cells were more abundant in LAbs compared with those in LLC and apoptotic cells. This enhanced protein retention likely facilitated the crossing of the BBB, thereby promoting more effective drug accumulation within the brain. According to previous studies, Abs has immunomodulatory effects on macrophages following phagocytosis [37]. The BV2 cells were cultured with coumarin–6-labeled LAbs to investigate whether the extracted LAbs could be internalized by microglia, which serve as the resident macrophages inside the CNS. Fluorescence microscopy revealed a time-dependent internalization of LAbs by BV2 cells within 2 h of incubation (Fig. 1G). This observation was further supported by quantitative analysis, which confirmed the efficient cellular uptake of LAbs (Fig. S3). As shown in Fig. S4, the purified LAbs exhibited no detectable DNA compared to their parent LLC cells. This result suggests that these LAbs may not induce genomic instability or potential tumorigenicity. Overall, these characterization results demonstrated that the isolated LAbs possessed a well–defined structure, exhibited typical characteristics, displayed excellent biological performance and reliable biosafety.

Characterization of BMn

BMn was synthesized through a one-step redox reaction between BSA and KMnO4 [38]. The synthesized BMn was characterized using TEM, DLS analysis, UV–vis, FT–IR, XRD and XPS. The TEM image (Fig. 2 A) revealed that BMn exhibited a spherical morphology and uniform dispersion with an ultra-small size of approximately 10 nm. As shown in Fig. 2B, the hydrodynamic diameter of BMn was measured to be 15.05 ± 1.14 nm. The UV–vis absorption spectrum of BMn displayed a distinct shoulder peak at approximately 280 nm, attributable to the characteristic absorption of BSA, and a broad absorption between 300 and 400 nm, corresponding to the plasma resonance absorption of MnO2 encapsulated within BSA (Fig. 2 C). Notably, the characteristic peaks of KMnO4 at 310, 524, and 544 nm were absent in the UV–vis spectrum of BMn, which confirms the complete reaction. As illustrated in Fig. 2D, the FT–IR spectrum of BMn exhibited characteristic stretching vibration peaks for –OH at 3400 cm−1, –NH at 1500 cm−1, and –C = O at 1650 cm−1, further verifying the presence of BSA in BMn. The XRD profile of the BMn (Fig. 2E) demonstrated distinct peaks at 2θ = 21.06°, which were consistent with characteristic peaks of MnO2 [39]. Moreover, the XPS spectrum (Fig. 2 F) showed two remarkable peaks at 641.8 and 653.5 eV, attributed to the Mn (IV)2p2/3 and the Mn (IV)2p1/2 spin–orbit peaks of MnO2 with the + 4 valence state of Mn, respectively [40]. The aforementioned results provided strong evidence for the successful synthesis of BMn.

Characterization of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa

The reverse-phase evaporation method was used for BMn encapsulation, achieving high internal aqueous loading, particularly for water-soluble components [41]. Hydrophobic Rapa was incorporated into the organic phase for co-encapsulation. The resulting Lip@BMn/Rapa was subsequently fused with LAbs through co-extrusion using a miniextruder to obtain LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa exhibited a spherical morphology and was well-dispersed with approximately 120 nm in size as observed in TEM images. The particles appeared black due to the strong electron absorption from the encapsulated BMn (Fig. 2G). As illustrated in Fig. S1, the hydrodynamic diameter of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa increased to 133.0 ± 1.43 nm. The fusion of LAbs resulted in a decreased surface charge of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, decreasing from 31.05 ± 0.31 mV to 14.39 ± 1.77 mV, as depicted in Fig. S2.

The fusion of Lip@BMn/Rapa and LAbs was verified through FT–IR characterization (Fig. 2H). Compared with the FT–IR spectrum of Lip@BMn/Rapa, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa presented five remarkable peaks at 997 cm−1, 1051 cm−1, 1533 cm−1, 1654 cm−1, 2941 cm−1, and 3296 cm−1, corresponding to the characteristic peaks of LAbs. This evidence confirmed the effective conjugation of LAbs with Lip@BMn/Rapa. SDS–PAGE was used to identify the protein expression. LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa well retained the majority of protein profiles from LAbs, indicating the successful fusion of Lip@BMn/Rapa and LAbs (Fig. 2I).

Catalase-like activity of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa

Excessive accumulation of ROS within the brain, including hydroxyl radical (•OH), superoxide anion (O2−), and H2O2, can disrupt redox homeostasis, induce oxidative stress, and trigger neuronal apoptosis. This process is widely recognized as a primary pathological mechanism in AD [42]. The catalase-like activities of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa are shown schematically in Fig. 3A. The H2O2 decomposition capabilities of BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa were examined, as illustrated in Fig. 3B. After 1 h of incubation, the H2O2 degradation efficiency reached 81.50% for BMn and 95.53% for LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, which was significantly higher than that of the control group (26.32%). The further enhanced H2O2 degradation by LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa could be ascribed to the redox interaction between LAbs and H2O2 based on the presence of BMn [43].

Furthermore, the O2 production ability of BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa through H2O2 decomposition was assessed by observing the generation of O2 bubbles (Fig. 3D) and quantitatively measuring the dissolved oxygen levels using a portable dissolved oxygen meter (Fig. 3 C). Notably, up to 10 mg/L of O2 was generated within 20 s from the reaction between LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa and H2O2. The O2 concentration continued to increase steadily and remained stable with increasing time. BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa had similar O2 production efficiency in the presence of H2O2. The observed H2O2 scavenging and O2 generation confirmed the catalase-like activity of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa.

H2O2-triggered structural remodeling and drug release

The brain experiences chronic oxidative stress throughout the entire progression of AD [44]. During this process, high levels of H2O2 readily permeate into LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa and are catalyzed by the encapsulated BMn to generate substantial O2 bubbles. This reaction triggers the structural collapse of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, which are reassembled into smaller vesicles to achieve cascade amplification for enhanced drug delivery. Figure 3E illustrates a schematic illustration of H2O2-triggered structural remodeling and drug release. The structural remodeling behavior was further investigated through TEM observation and in vitro drug release studies. TEM image (Fig. 3 F) revealed that most LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa transformed into smaller-scale vesicle structures after treatment with H2O2, and the structural rearrangement was further characterized by evaluating the release profiles of Mn ions and Rapa. As shown in Fig. 3G, the release of Mn ions and Rapa from LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa remained at a low level in the absence of H2O2. However, in the presence of H2O2, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa showed an initial 41.06% ± 5.10% release of Mn ions within the first 1 h, with cumulative release reaching approximately 101.96% ± 5.00% after 4 h. This rapid release can be attributed to the structural disintegration of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa induced by H2O2 treatment. In contrast, the release of Rapa was negligible, as it remained encapsulated within the fragmented vesicles, which subsequently reassembled into smaller vesicles.

These results demonstrated the successful preparation of an H2O2-sensitive LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa for AD therapy. The BMn-catalyzed O2 generation encapsulated within LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa not only disrupted the structural integrity for precise drug release but also facilitated the recombination into a larger number of smaller vesicles, thereby enhancing carrier delivery efficiency.

•OH and O2− scavenging

Iron ions catalyze H2O2 to produce •OH through the Fenton reaction [45]. In this study, we selected TMB as a probe for detecting •OH radicals, which was oxidized to undergo a noticeable color change from colorless to blue, with a prominent absorbance peak at 652 nm [46].

As shown in Fig. 3H, in the presence of FeSO4·7H2O and H2O2, a significant color change from colorless TMB to its blue product oxTMB was observed in control groups, and an absorbance at 652 nm was also measured. Following treatment with BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, this absorption peak markedly diminished and the corresponding color intensity also faded. The O2− scavenging capability was also evaluated using a superoxide anion assay kit. As shown in Fig. 3 J, the control group exhibited prominent absorption peaks at 530 nm due to substantial O2− accumulation. As expected, both BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa demonstrated a significant reduction in characteristic peak absorption at 530 nm compared with the control group. BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa exhibited high efficiency in scavenging •OH and O2−.

ESR analysis

ESR was used to detect whether LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa possessed antioxidant activity to eliminate •OH and O2− with the spin trap agent DMPO. Notably, compared with the ESR signal of the control group (1:2:2:1), ESR signal of BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa decreased significantly (Fig. 3I). Similarly, the ESR signal (1:1:1:1) decreased significantly in the presence of BMn or LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa (Fig. 3 K), indicating excellent •OH and O2− scavenging capabilities.

In vitro transcellular transport across the BBB

An in vitro BBB model was established using a transwell system to investigate the BBB-penetrating ability of LAbs@Lip@BMn. As shown in Fig. 4 A, bEnd.3 cells were cultured on the apical side of the transwell insert to form an intact monolayer with a TEER value of 250 Ω·cm−2. The permeability across the bEnd.3 monolayer was evaluated by measuring the coumarin–6 fluorescence in the reception pool and calculating the Papp. The CD44 protein is reported to be highly expressed in brain metastasis-prone tumor cells, such as LLC cells. It is crucial in facilitating their filtration and metastasis into the brain [47].

As illustrated in Fig. 4B, C6-KD-LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa displayed a significantly decreased delivery efficiency across the BBB compared with C6-LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. This observation, together with the abundance of CD44 expression in LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa as evidenced by WB images (Fig. 4 C), suggested that the recognition and binding of CD44 in LAbs to bEnd.3 cells are essential for efficient BBB transcytosis. Additionally, no significant changes in TEER values were found before and after the experiments (Fig. 4D), suggesting that the LAbs@Lip@BMn treatment did not disrupt the integrity of the BBB.

To assess neuronal uptake post-BBB crossing, we tested whether C6-LAbs@Lip@BMn can cross BBB and subsequently be internalized by HT22 cells. As demonstrated in Fig. S5, a BBB Transwell model was established by co-culturing bEnd.3 cells in the upper chamber and HT22 cells in the lower chamber, respectively (Fig. S5A). As shown in Fig. S5B, HT22 cells treated with C6-LAbs@Lip@BMn exhibited significantly higher fluorescence intensity compared to those treated with C6-Lip@BMn. indicating that BBB permeability and neuronal cells endocytosis of LAbs@Lip@BMn could be improved with the assistance of LAbs.

These findings suggest that LAbs derived from LLC cells possess an enhanced ability to cross the BBB and be internalized by neuronal cells through CD44-mediated transcytosis, highlighting their potential as innovative brain-targeted delivery vehicles.

Intracellular ROS scavenging and O2 generation

Excessive ROS accumulation could disturb intracellular redox homeostasis, leading to oxidative stress and severe neuronal damage. This process is widely recognized as a crucial factor in AD progression [48]. DCFH–DA was used to detect the ROS level in the H2O2-treated BV2 and HT22 cells. As shown in Fig. 4E, BV2 and HT22 cells exhibited significantly enhanced green fluorescence signals following H2O2 treatment compared with the control group. Nevertheless, the green ROS signals notably decreased after incubation with LAbs@Lip@Rapa or Lip@BMn/Rapa and almost disappeared after treatment with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. This demonstrated that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa possesses strong ROS scavenging abilities that are possibly attributed to the catalytic activity of BMn and the reaction between LAbs and intracellular H2O2 [49]. These synergistic effects ultimately lead to a significant reduction in ROS levels.

We used [Ru(dpp)₃]Cl₂ as a fluorescent probe to detect O₂ production in BV2 and HT22 cells. This probe typically exhibits red fluorescence under hypoxic conditions, which is effectively quenched upon exposure to O₂. As depicted in Fig. 4F–G and Fig. S6–Fig. S7, BV2 and HT22 cells treated with LAbs@Lip@Rapa and Lip@BMn/Rapa, respectively, showed significantly weaker red fluorescence compared with the H2O2-only-treated group, indicating elevated O₂ levels inside the cells. Notably, the red fluorescence intensity was nearly undetectable when BV2 and HT22 cells were exposed to LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, probably due to the cooperative effects of BMn and LAbs.

Investigation of microglia polarization

Microglia are the primary immune cells of the CNS, which are crucial in defending against inflammation and injury [50]. However, these cells are highly sensitive to pathological conditions, which can lead to their activation and polarization into the pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype. Increasing evidence indicates that pro-inflammatory M1-type microglia produce elevated levels of inflammatory cytokines, resulting in neuronal dysfunction, damage, and degeneration, thereby increasing the severity of neurodegenerative diseases [51]. Therefore, regulating the phenotypic polarization of microglia, particularly promoting the transformation from pro-inflammatory M1-type microglia to anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, represents a promising therapeutic strategy for normalizing the brain neuroinflammatory environment [52].

We evaluated the expression levels of M1 (CD86+) and M2 (CD206+) markers in vitro through immunofluorescence staining and flow cytometry assays to investigate whether LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatment can convert LPS-stimulated BV2 cells from pro-inflammatory M1 phenotypes into anti-inflammatory M2 phenotypes. As exhibited in Fig. 5A–B, co-incubation with LPS increased the expression of the M1 marker and decreased the expression of the M2 marker in BV2 cells, indicating the pro-inflammatory state of microglia. LAbs treatment not only reduced the level of CD86 but also significantly increased the CD206 expression. This indicated that LAbs can facilitate microglia transformation from a pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype through phagocyte-specific internalization, a process known as efferocytosis [53]. Moreover, the intracellular ROS scavenging effect of Lip@BMn also contributed to this M2-like microglia polarization, resulting in a significant increase in M2 (CD206+) markers and a decrease in M1 (CD86+) markers. Benefiting from the synergistic anti-inflammatory effects of LAbs and BMn, compared with the M1 group, treatment with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa achieved the most significant reduction (5.0-fold) in CD86-positive fluorescence and a notable increase (20.4-fold) in CD206-positive fluorescence (Fig. S8 and Fig. S9). Furthermore, a similar phenotype modulation effect was also demonstrated in flow cytometry analysis (Fig. 5C–D). Therefore, the synergistic effect of LAbs-induced efferocytosis and BMn-mediated ROS scavenging may offer a promising strategy to polarize microglia toward an anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype for AD treatment.

Studies have demonstrated that the overactivation of microglia into the M1 phenotype induces increased secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and their excessive accumulation in the brain may impair microglial immunologic function and exacerbate AD progression [54]. We further investigated the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL–6, and anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL–10, in LPS-induced BV2 cells through ELISA. As displayed in Fig. 5E–F, treatment with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa significantly inhibited the secretion of IL–6 and promoted the production of IL–10. This effect was more remarkable in the LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa group compared with the other treatment groups and similar to the M2 control group.

BDNF is a critical protein for neuronal survival and synaptic plasticity. Dysregulation of BDNF levels has been associated with the symptoms of AD, such as impaired memory and learning capabilities [55]. We quantified the cellular secretion of BDNF using Western blotting analysis to investigate the effect of microglia transformation on BDNF expression levels (Fig. S10). As illustrated in Fig. S11, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatment significantly increased BDNF expression to levels comparable with those in the M2 control group, surpassing other treatment groups. This elevation in BDNF levels could reverse the inflammatory environment induced by LPS exposure in microglia and be transferred to neurons through microglia–neuron interactions, thereby promoting the recovery of impaired synapses and alleviating neuronal damage [56].

Enhanced migration and phagocytic capacity after microglia polarization

AD is clinically characterized by the extracellular deposition of Aβ [57]. As resident macrophages in the CNS, microglia play a crucial role in the internalization and degradation of Aβ [58]. However, inflammatory responses can cause excessive activation of microglia, impairing their ability to effectively uptake and degrade Aβ [59].

We initially performed scratch and migration assays on LPS-stimulated BV2 cells to evaluate their proliferation and migration abilities after microglia phenotype transition to investigate the effect of microglia polarization toward M2 phenotype on Aβ clearance. Images from the scratch assay and transwell migration analysis are shown in Fig. 5G and Fig. S13, respectively. These images, together with corresponding statistical analysises (Fig. S12 and Fig. S14), revealed that the proliferation and migration rates of BV2 cells were significantly enhanced in both LAbs@Lip@Rapa and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa groups compared with the other treatment groups and the LPS-induced M1 control group. The levels were comparable with those observed in the IL–4-induced M2 group. These results indicated that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa intervention in inflammatory microglia led to a significant transformation toward an M2-type phenotype, significantly enhancing microglial proliferation and migration capabilities. This enhancement facilitated rapid microglia aggregation around damaged neurons, promoting efficient clearance of abnormal protein deposits and necrotic neurons.

CLSM was further performed to examine the effect of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa on microglia-mediated Aβ clearance. BV2 cells, pre-incubated with LPS to mimic an M1-like microglial neuroinflammatory microenvironment, were subsequently co-cultured with FITC-labeled Aβ1–42 after exposure to various formulations. As shown in Fig. 5H, LPS-treated BV2 cells without any formulation treatment displayed minimal intracellular green signal from FITC-labeled Aβ1–42, with green fluorescence primarily localized around the cell membranes. This observation confirmed that neurotoxic Aβ aggregates significantly inhibited microglial phagocytosis and prevented Aβ clearance. Following co-incubation with LAbs or Lip@BMn, the red fluorescence from lysosomes was observed to colocalize with green fluorescence, producing a yellow signal. This indicated that both LAbs@Lip@Rapa-induced and Lip@BMn/Rapa-induced microglia polarization can facilitate the intracellular clearance of Aβ. The colocalization of green Aβ fluorescence and red lysosome fluorescence clearly demonstrated that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa facilitated Aβ clearance through the microglial lysosomal pathway. These effects were further strengthened by LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatment due to the restoration of the cellular neuroinflammatory microenvironment through the synergistic action of LAbs and Lip@BMn. The recovery of microglial immunologic function in patients with AD shows significant potential for alleviating Aβ-mediated neurotoxicity.

ThT fluorescence assay

p–Tau proteins can aggregate within neurons to form NFTs and subsequent neuronal damage [60]. OA is a potent and selective inhibitor of protein phosphatase 2 A. It has been extensively used to induce p–tau aggregation to mimic intracellular tauopathy-like microenvironments [61]. As a tracer agent binding specifically to β–sheet structures, ThT could selectively bind to tau aggregates with a significant increase in green fluorescence intensity [62]. OA-induced HT22 cells were used to further investigate the inhibitory effect of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa on tau aggregation. The intensity of ThT green fluorescence served as an indicator of the degree of tau aggregation.

As shown in Fig. 6 A and Fig. S15, the green fluorescence signals were significantly elevated compared with negative control, indicating the successful induction of tau hyperphosphorylation cell models. Then, OA-induced HT22 cells were treated with different formulations. The fluorescence intensity diminished in the presence of Rapa, suggesting that Rapa could promote autophagy and degradation of p–tau [63]. Weak fluorescence was observed after LAbs@Lip treatment, which could be ascribed to the amelioration of the intracellular inflammatory environment verified by LAbs@Lip. LAbs@Lip@BMn treatment further decreased the fluorescence intensity, suggesting that the ROS scavenging ability of BMn significantly contributed to the removal of p–tau. Almost no fluorescence was observed in OA-induced cells treated with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, compared with Rapa or LAbs@Lip@BMn treatment alone. These results demonstrated that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, which integrates autophagy induction by Rapa and the inflammatory and oxidative stress microenvironment modulation by LAbs@Lip@BMn, exhibits synergistic therapeutic effects in reducing p–tau levels. This synergy could inhibit tau aggregation and prevent NFT formation, thereby exhibiting a protective effect against OA-induced cell damage.

Investigation of neuroprotective effects

To investigate the neuroprotective effects of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, the HT22 cells were exposed to aggregated Aβ1–42 to induce apoptosis, mimicking the neuronal damage observed in patients with AD. We performed JC–1 staining, Annexin V–FITC/PI double staining, CCK–8 assay, and Western blotting analysis on BDNF to further investigate the neuroprotective effects of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa.

Excessive ROS accumulation could induce neuronal apoptosis by disrupting mitochondrial membrane integrity [64], ultimately resulting in the loss of mitochondrial membrane potential [65]. We evaluated the changes in mitochondrial membrane potential in HT22 cells using JC–1 dye as an indicator. Typically, the accumulation of JC–1 within mitochondria leads to JC–1 aggregate formation with specific red fluorescence emission. In contrast, a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential reduces JC–1 aggregate formation and increases JC–1 monomer distribution with specific green fluorescence [66]. Therefore, the ratio of red to green fluorescence in JC–1-loaded cells is often used to monitor mitochondrial membrane potential. As shown in Fig. 6B and Fig. S16, HT22 cells treated with Aβ1–42 exhibited a significantly higher proportion of JC–1 monomers (green fluorescence) and a markedly lower proportion of JC–1 aggregates (red fluorescence) than the negative control, indicating a decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential due to mitochondrial dysfunction. After treating Aβ1–42-pretreated HT22 cells with LAbs@Lip@Rapa, Lip@BMn/Rapa, and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa, the mitochondrial membrane potential was restored to varying extents, as indicated by increased red fluorescence and decreased green fluorescence. Notably, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatment resulted in almost negligible green fluorescence compared with LAbs@Lip@Rapa or Lip@BMn/Rapa alone treatment. These findings suggested that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa not only effectively recovered the impaired mitochondrial membranes of neurons but also achieved neuroprotection against mitochondrial damage-induced apoptosis through mitochondrial dysfunction amelioration.

The CCK–8 assay was used to evaluate cell viability in the presence of Aβ1–42 aggregates. Judging from the results in Fig. S17, severe neuronal death (HT22 cell viability was merely 47.47% ± 1.00%) was found after Aβ1–42 aggregate treatment. However, LAbs@Lip@BMn@Rapa significantly rescued cell death with HT22 survival rates of 84.83% ± 4.67%, which was close to the normal HT22 cell group. This could be ascribed to their neuroinflammation resolution and quick ROS-scavenging synergistic effects. We also performed Annexin V–FITC/PI double staining assay to further confirm this phenomenon and found a better anti–apoptosis effect of LAbs@Lip@BMn@Rapa against Aβ1–42 aggregates (Fig. 6 C and Fig. S18).

In the CNS, decreased or depleted levels of BDNF are closely associated with p–tau, Aβ accumulation, neuroinflammation, and neuronal apoptosis [67]. This study used WB analysis to monitor BDNF levels in Aβ1–42-induced HT22 cells after treatment with different formulations. As illustrated in Fig. S19–20, the BDNF levels in Aβ1–42-induced HT22 cells were significantly decreased compared with the normal HT22 cells. After treatment, all tested groups exhibited a progressive increase in BDNF expression. Notably, the LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa-treated group showed the highest BDNF levels, nearly restoring them to those observed in normal HT22 cells. This significant elevation in BDNF levels effectively promotes neuronal development and synaptic repair, thus facilitating the recovery of impaired neuronal function.

In conclusion, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa exhibited remarkable neuroprotective effects by effectively alleviating the pathogenic neuroinflammation and oxidative stress associated with AD. These effects were accomplished through multiple mechanisms, including the inhibition of microglial activation, scavenging of ROS, reduction of Aβ deposition, and decrease in p–tau levels.

In vivo and ex vivo imaging of brain-targeted properties

Although the BBB is essential for maintaining the proper function of the CNS, it also poses significant challenges for treating neurological disorders, such as AD [68]. Successfully crossing the BBB is still a significant challenge in drug delivery research. Recent studies have demonstrated that CD44 plays a crucial role in cancer brain metastasis by selectively binding to polysaccharides on the BBB [69]. Based on this finding, we devised a strategy to isolate apoptotic bodies derived from LLC cells, termed LAbs, in which CD44 was integrated into their membrane. Their potential was then evaluated to mediate brain delivery and alleviate AD symptoms.

DiR was selected as the fluorescence probe to label Lip@BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn formulations to investigate the brain delivery efficiency following their intravenous administration. Figure 7 A–B displays the localization of fluorescent signals in the brains of mice and in the excised organs, and the quantitative analysis was illustrated in Fig. S21 and Fig. S22. Free DiR exhibited minimal accumulation in the brain. At various time points, the DiR signals in the brains of mice treated with DiR-Lip@BMn was consistently weaker, indicating reduced brain-targeting efficiency due to the presence of BBB. In contrast, the administration of DiR-LAbs@Lip@BMn resulted in significantly higher fluorescence signals in the brain compared with the DiR-Lip@BMn group at all time points. The fluorescent signals peaked 8 h after administration and persisted for up to 48 h, indicating superior brain targeting and prolonged brain residence. Furthermore, DiR-KD-LAbs@Lip@BMn exhibited decreased efficiency in brain delivery compared to DiR-LAbs@Lip@BMn group over a 48-h observation. Mice were euthanized after 4–h dosing, and their brains were collected and imaged. The fluorescence biodistribution revealed similar results. These observations suggest that LAbs@Lip@BMn has a superior ability to traverse the BBB and selectively accumulate in the brain. In this process, CD44 expressed on LAbs serves as the key mediator of cross-BBB delivery.

Amelioration of learning and memory impairments in AD model mice

Aβ1–42 and OA were co-injected into the bilateral hippocampus to induce an AD model using mice to investigate whether LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa could alleviate cognitive and memory impairments. The treatment schedule is shown in Fig. 7C. These AD model mice were administered different formulations through intravenous injections every alternate day for 4 weeks. The MWM and nest behavior tests were performed to evaluate improvements in memory and cognition following treatment.

In the MWM test, mice in the sham group displayed spatially oriented swimming behavior. The AD model mice treated with saline exhibited significantly impaired platform-searching performance and apparent deficits in spatial learning. This was confirmed by the longest escape latency time (Fig. 7 F), the shortest time spent in the target quadrant (Fig. 7G), and the fewest hidden platform crossings (Fig. 7H). These findings indicated that the Aβ/p–tau dual-pathology AD model could effectively trigger learning and spatial memory degeneration in mice. After 4 days of training, AD model mice treated with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa (P < 0.001) and LAbs@Lip@BMn (P < 0.05) exhibited remarkably shorter escape latencies than those in the AD group, while limited improvements were observed in other treatment groups (Fig. 7 F). Moreover, mice treated with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa spent more time in the targeted quadrant and crossed the hidden platform more frequently compared with the other groups (Fig. 7G–H).

Furthermore, a nesting behavior test was performed to further investigate hippocampal function in AD model mice after treatment, specifically focusing on executive function and daily activities. As illustrated in Fig. 7E and I, AD model mice exhibited significant deficits in constructing complete and complex nests due to cognitive decline. In contrast, AD model mice treated with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa exhibited markedly improved nest-building abilities, achieving scores comparable with those of the sham group. These results suggested that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatment can effectively restore spatial memory impairments and enhance cognitive performance in AD model mice.

Histology and Immunofluorescence in vivo

After behavioral evaluation, the brain tissues from the mice were harvested for a comprehensive series of pathological tests. These assessments included immunostaining for 8–OHG, IBA–1, and Aβ plaques, as well as immunohistochemistry for p–tau and Nissl staining for the evaluation of neuropathological changes.

Inspired by the findings that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa could polarize microglia from an M1-like phenotype to an M2-like phenotype and enhance microglia phagocytosis and clearance of Aβ at the cellular level, we further investigated whether these effects could be achieved in AD model mice. After 4 weeks of treatment, the levels of the microglia biomarker IBA–1 and the extent of Aβ plaque deposition were monitored using immunostaining. As shown in Fig. 8C–B, significant upregulation of both IBA–1 green fluorescence and Aβ red fluorescence was observed in the hippocampus of AD model mice, indicating substantial microglial activation and Aβ burden. Following treatment, all tested groups exhibited progressively decreasing fluorescence intensity of IBA–1 and Aβ plaques, with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa-treated AD model mice showing almost no detectable fluorescence. These observations were further supported by quantitative fluorescence intensity analysis (Fig. S24 and Fig. S25), which confirmed the imaging-based findings. In conclusion, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa could effectively alleviate the pathogenic neuroinflammation associated with AD by inhibiting microglial activation and reducing Aβ plaque deposition.

We further conducted immunohistochemical analysis on brain slices to investigate whether p–tau inhibition could prevent the formation of NFTs in the brains of AD model mice. As shown in Fig. 8D and Fig. S26, AD model mice exhibited markedly increased p–tau aggregations within NFTs compared with the sham group. In contrast, mice treated with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa showed significantly fewer or almost no detectable p–tau aggregations. This result indicated that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa not only enhanced brain-targeted delivery but also modulated the inflammatory and oxidative stress microenvironment associated with AD, thereby facilitating the autophagy-mediated elimination of intracellular p–tau by Rapa more effectively.

Usually, oxidative stress plays a crucial pathogenic role in the onset and progression of AD. Excessive accumulation of ROS in AD microenvironment can induce neuronal damage and trigger microglial activation. We monitored ROS levels by tracing 8–OHG fluorescence after LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatment to evaluate its ROS scavenging efficacy in the brain. As an oxidative damage product, a significant increase in the green fluorescence signal of 8–OHG was observed in the hippocampus of AD model mice compared with sham mice. As shown in Fig. 8 A and Fig. S23, the levels of oxidative stress were relieved to varying degrees following different treatments, especially with LAbs@Lip@BMn and LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatments. Notably, mice treated with LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa displayed markedly lower levels of 8–OHG compared with other groups. Based on these results, it was concluded that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa not only alleviated neuroinflammation but also demonstrated multi-enzyme-like activities that could effectively eliminate excessive ROS around the neurons.

Massive neuron loss and death are hallmark pathological features of AD, particularly prominent in brain regions associated with learning and memory [70]. We conducted Nissl staining to visualize nerve cells for evaluating the activity and density of neurons in the hippocampus. As illustrated in Fig. 8E, AD model mice exhibited a significant decrease in both the number and density of Nissl bodies compared with sham mice, indicating characteristic neuronal damage in AD. After 4 weeks of treatment, the LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa-treated AD model mice demonstrated a notable recovery in neuronal density, approaching levels observed in the sham mice. These results suggested that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa treatment effectively alleviated neuronal damage and provided excellent neuroprotection in the Aβ–Tau copathogenic AD mouse model. Future work will focus on developing advanced animal models that accurately recapitulate the chronic and complicated progression of human AD, allowing for a more comprehensive evaluation of the neuroprotective efficacy of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa.

Biosafety evaluation

Hemolytic analysis (Fig. S27) showed that LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa did not induce hemolysis at concentrations ranging from 50 to 500 µg/mL. The HR was measured at 2.44% ± 0.25%, even at the highest concentration of 500 µg/mL, which was far below the internationally accepted threshold of 5%. This result confirmed the excellent blood compatibility of LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa. Moreover, the mice were sacrificed to harvest the major organs for H&E staining to assess the biosafety of multiple formulations. As illustrated in Fig. S28, no obvious pathological changes were observed in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney after 9 days of treatment. The in vivo Mn element content in major organs was determined by ICP–OES. As shown in Fig. S29 and Fig. S30, the Mn levels in mice returned to normal within 7 days after single or repeated administration, suggesting efficient metabolic clearance of Mn in vivo. These findings further support that the carrier does not induce manganese accumulation or related toxicity.

Although no significant adverse effects were observed in our study, including hemolysis, histopathological changes, and Mn ion accumulation, the potential risk of LAbs inducing tumorigenicity or undesired immunological responses following repeated administration should be systematically investigated. Such evaluation is essential to ensure the safe translation of this technology into clinical applications for neurodegenerative disease treatment.

Conclusions

This study was novel in successfully developing a multitarget therapeutic system (LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa) specifically designed to modulate the AD neuropathological microenvironment and recover the downstream pathological abnormalities. This system leveraged the unique properties of LAbs for efficient BBB penetration and brain accumulation. More importantly, the efferocytosis process mediated by LAbs facilitated the transformation of microglia, effectively alleviating neuroinflammation. The encapsulated BMn effectively scavenged ROS and catalyzed O2 generation, facilitating the breakdown of liposome membrane and recombination into ultra-small LAbs-based vesicles, thereby significantly enhancing the intracerebral drug delivery efficiency. The multi-target synergistic effect normalized microglial overactivation, oxidative stress, Aβ plaque accumulation, and p–tau burden, further restoring memory and cognitive dysfunction in AD model mice. Therefore, LAbs@Lip@BMn/Rapa offers promising potential for early AD intervention by focusing on restoring microglial immunological function and redox homeostasis in the CNS and provides innovative multi-target therapeutic strategies for neurodegenerative diseases.
