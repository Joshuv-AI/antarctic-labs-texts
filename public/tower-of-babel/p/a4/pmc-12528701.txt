Evaluation of the antimicrobial and antioxidants potentialities of two Lebanese cultivars of Quinoa (Chenopodium Quinoa Willd.)
Authors: Mohamad Omeiri, Walid Meouche, Zainab Alawady, Hoda Yusef, Salwa Abdel Rahman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Quinoa is a historical food crop that has provided vital sustenance to communities for thousands of years, and its consumption has lately expanded due to its potent antioxidant properties and noteworthy antibacterial activities. The present research investigation was carried out to assess the antibacterial properties of two varieties of quinoa as well as their antioxidant activities. The ethanolic extract of both cultivars (Q12 and Taticaca) displayed moderate inhibition potentials that fall within the range of recognized broad-spectrum antibiotics against S. epidermidis, S. aureus, and E. coli. The quinoa extracts were found to have minimum inhibitory (MIC) and bactericidal (MBC) doses ranging from 375 to 750 µg/mL. The synergistic effects of quinoa extracts and antibiotics were also explored, revealing moderate synergistic interactions. The strains’ ultrastructure revealed abnormalities related to the protoplasm and cell wall following treatment with quinoa extracts. These antibacterial and antioxidant activities, as documented in this study, can provide insight into how the two Lebanese cultivars of Quinoa might be used for further hygiene and food product preservation applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-19992-7.

Introduction

Antibiotic-resistant microbes have become an increasingly pressing problem and a silent health threat in recent years, and this has been associated in part with the widespread prevalence of improper and indiscriminate antibiotic usage. Antibiotic resistance can be described as the propensity of pathogens to thwart the effect of antimicrobial substances, and it emerges when an antibiotic’s efficacy to ward off the proliferation of bacteria declines1. This sort of resistance might result in an epidemic of bacterial infections, which could become intense and result in extended sickness, vulnerability to infection among individuals undergoing surgical procedures, a spike in fatalities, and increased expenditures2. This emerged as one of the most serious public health problems of the present era, with the World Health Organization estimating that antibiotic resistance will lead to ten million fatalities by 20503.

Antibiotics are among the most significant innovations that revolutionized the well-being of humans by averting potentially fatal bacterial infections. Unfortunately, acquired resistance to such innovations throughout the years hampered their advancement, which worsened since the 1990’s when the antibiotic pipeline dropped, resulting in a fall in the progress and development of novel antibiotics4. Furthermore, the adverse consequences associated with certain antibiotics, notably their mechanism of action centered around reactive oxygen species (ROS), have prompted researchers to concentrate their efforts on plants in order to discover novel antibacterial substances that will safeguard the body from oxidative stress and free radical damage, which have been connected with diseases such as cancer, neurodegenerative, respiratory, and digestive diseases5,6.

The use of natural products as therapeutic agents in the treatment of illnesses caused by bacteria can be highly favorable when compared to manufactured medications. In comparison with synthetic antibiotics, the use of phytotherapy in traditional medicine may have fewer adverse consequences in terms of toxicological and pharmacological activity. In this regard, medicinal herbs are promising sources for the creation of novel medications, including antibacterial agents, due to their diverse biological activities7. This has led them to be the backbone of both cotemporary and conventional drugs and are now frequently utilized in the manufacture of commercially produced pharmaceuticals. According to statistics, reports declared that herbs are used in around 25% of prescription medications globally8. In this context, adequate scientific data as well as knowledge of the chemical elements of medicinal plants are essential for their ideal applications6.

Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is increasingly recognized as an important medicinal plant within the Amaranthaceae family that was initially cultivated around 7000 years ago in South American Andean nations for its nutritional benefits, drought endurance, and high salt tolerance9. Since the discovery of its importance 50 years ago, 100 countries have turned to growing quinoa in 2021 after being 8 in 19809. Around 250 Chenopodium species are found globally, and the grains of these species are regarded as edible and high in proteins, vital minerals, essential amino acids, and vitamins, making them superfood items10. The bioactive compounds in quinoa have been considered as potential molecules in different sectors due to their antioxidant activity11–18. Therefore, we can find carotenoids, tocopherols, polyphenols, and betalains. The antioxidant activity of quinoa seeds has been mainly attributed to hydrophilic polyphenols. The most abundant polyphenols in quinoa are phenolic acids and flavonoids (Fig. 1). Phenolic acids (mainly rosmarinic and chlorogenic acids) and flavonoids (mainly quercetin and isoquercetin) are water-soluble present a promising bioactive potential16,18. Moreover, quinoa greens have been demonstrated to be beneficial against bacteria, diabetes, cancer, obesity, and heart disorders19. The latter can be attributed to the fact that the leaves of quinoa are a good source of proteins, amino acids, potassium, copper, phosphorous, manganese, zinc, and sodium20,21. All quinoa plant parts have gallic acid, one of the main phenolic acids22. Several studies have found that quinoa extracts can eliminate viruses as well as gram-positive and gram-negative bacteria23,24. Among the published bacterial isolates that showed sensitivity towards quinoa extract, reports pointed out Escheichia coli ATCC 11,229, E. coli ATCC 25,922, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 25,619, P. aeruginosa ATCC 27,853, Salmonella enterica ATCC 10,708, Enterococcus faecalis ATCC 43,062, Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC 33,501, and S. aureus25,26. Furthermore, saponins derived from quinoa seeds and crude saponins have demonstrated antifungal activity against Candida albicans, preventing its proliferation22.

In light of the ongoing challenge posed by bacterial resistance and the need for safer, natural antimicrobial agents, this study aimed to evaluate, for the first time to our knowledge, the antibacterial efficacy of two Lebanese quinoa cultivars, Q12 and Titicaca, against a range of foodborne and non-foodborne bacterial pathogens. The antimicrobial activity was assessed using agar well diffusion assay, MIC and MBC determination, time-kill assay etc.], targeting bacterial strains including Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, Salmonella, and others including fungi species. Additionally, the antioxidant properties of these quinoa cultivars were examined to explore their potential as multifunctional natural therapeutics.

Materials and methods

Quinoa

Two varieties (cultivars) of Lebanese quinoa, named Q12 and Titicaca, were provided by the Lebanese Agricultural Research Institute’s (LARI) ‘Lebaa Station’.

Microorganisms

Five different bacterial strains were used for testing the antimicrobial activity of quinoa, such as E. coli ATCC 35,218, Salmonella sp. ATCC 14,028, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 29,213, Staphylococcus epidermidis ATCC 33,501, and Bacillus licheniformis ATCC 14,580, and four strains of fungi, Aspergillus sp. 1, Aspergillus sp. 2, Fusarium sp., and Penicillum sp. Some of them are foodborne pathogens (E. coli, Salmonella sp., Staphylococcus aureus, Aspergillus sp. 1, Aspergillus sp. 2, and Penicillum sp.).

Bacterial and fungal inoculum

Aerobic bacteria were cultivated and maintained on nutrient agar slants. The cultures were incubated at the appropriate temperature for each microorganism (35–37 °C for bacterial cultures; 28–30 °C for fungal cultures). Slants were held at 4 °C, with monthly transfers. Inocula were generated immediately by suspending colonies grown for 24 h on agar slopes in sterile saline solution (0.85%). The density of bacterial inocula was adjusted to meet the 0.5 McFarland standard (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL). Growing the isolates on glucose-peptone agar (GPA) slants yielded fungal inocula. For obtaining fungal inocula, 10 mL of sterile distilled water containing 1% Tween 20 was poured into each slant. The suspensions were spectrophotometrically modified to have optical densities ranging from 0.09 to 0.3 at 530 nm. Quantitative colony counts on GPA revealed that each stock solution contained primarily conidia and varied from 1 × 10⁶ to 5 × 10⁶ CFU/mL.

Phytochemicals analysis of Quinoa extracts

The ethanolic extracts of two quinoa cultivars (seeds and leaves) were submitted to several chemical analyses following the standard procedures described in Tiwari et al.27.

Antimicrobial efficacy of crude Quinoa extracts against the tested pathogens

The disk diffusion technique was used to examine the impact of the produced quinoa extracts on the replication of specified pathogens. An inoculum of 25 µl from each microbial suspension was spread onto the surface of either Müller-Hinton agar (MHA; for bacterial cultures) or Glucose Potato Agar (GPA; for fungal cultures) using a sterile swab by streaking back and forth from edge to edge. To allow for good diffusion, sterile filter paper disks (6 mm) were soaked in 25 µL of each quinoa extract, which had been sterilized by filtration using a 0.22 μm pore-size membrane filter, and left to dry for 15 min before incubation at 5–8 °C for 2–3 h. The plates were incubated for 24 h at 37 °C. Each experiment was performed in triplicate, and the mean diameters of inhibitory zones were determined in millimeters28. The investigation included positive as well as negative controls. The quinoa extraction solvents (ethanol and water) served as negative controls, while the antibiotics Vancomycin HCl (500 mg/mL) for Staphylococcus spp. and Gentamycin (40 mg/mL) for E. coli served as positive controls. The sizes of inhibition zones reflected antimicrobial activity; 9–12 mm measurements showed a moderately active drug, 13–18 mm measurements indicated an active substance, and > 18 mm measurements indicated the influence of a very active antimicrobial agent. The inactive substance’s impact was evident to an inhibition zone diameter of 9 mm.

Determination of minimal inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC)

The MICs for two tested antibiotics and the produced quinoa extracts were determined using the bacterial microdilution technique on rounded 96-well plates. Three to five colonies were transferred from overnight bacterial growth into 5 mL of MHB for each bacterial strain to achieve a density of 0.5 McFarland standard. All antibacterial compounds were serially diluted twice with the following concentrations: MHB was used to create extracts at 39–5000 g/mL for Gentamycin (40 mg/mL), 31.25–5000 g/mL for Vancomycin, and 187.5–3000 g/mL for each quinoa extract, and 50 µl of each was transferred to 96-well sterile microtiter plates. Each well had a bacterial inoculum of 50 µl. The lowest concentration restricting noticeable growth after 20–24 h of incubation at 37 °C was recorded as the MIC. The MBCs of quinoa extracts were determined by inoculating 10 µl of media from each of the MIC test wells, which were clear with no turbidity, onto Müller-Hinton agar plates and incubating at 37 °C for 24 h. MBCs were identified when there was no observable growth following an overnight incubation. According to NCCLS, the minimum bactericidal concentration was defined as the lowest concentration of quinoa extract that enabled less than 0.1% of the bacterial cells in the original inoculum to survive. For the quinoa extracts, the MIC index (MBC/MIC) was computed. If the MBC/MIC ratio was 1 or 2, the effect was termed bactericidal; if the MBC/MIC ratio was 4 or 16, the effect was called bacteriostatic.

Determination of fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC)

The checkerboard test was used to assess the fractional inhibitory concentrations (FIC) of a combination of commercially available chemical antibacterial agents and plant extract. Antibiotic stock solutions were generated (5000 g/mL Gentamycin and 3000 g/mL Vancomycin in nutritional broth, respectively). Using the two-fold broth dilution procedure, the MICs of the antibiotics and the promising quinoa extract were determined. Each antibiotic/quinoa combination was serially diluted twice before being inoculated with 100 µl of bacterial suspension (equal to 0.5 McFarland) and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. For each antibiotic-quinoa combination, the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was computed. The following is how the findings were interpreted: FICI ≤ 0.5 indicates synergy; 0.5–0.75 indicates partial synergy; 0.76-1 indicates an additive impact; 1–4 indicates indifference; and > 4 indicates antagonism29. Time-kill curve assay

The death rate of bacterial strains by a tested antibacterial agent was measured at various time periods. The time-kill curve test was carried out in accordance with the approach given in CLSI’s M26-A document (CLSI, M26-A, 1999). The experiment was carried out in flasks containing 20 mL of MHB inoculated with 1.5 × 108 CFU/mL and supplemented with the necessary concentration of quinoa extracts (seeds and leaves) (MIC X 1, MIC X 2, and MIC X 4), which were then incubated at 37 °C for 24 h in a rotary shaker (150 rpm). At 0, 2, 4, 6, 8, and 24 h, 1 mL aliquots of the media were collected. At 600 nm, the optical density (OD) was determined spectrophotometrically. A control growth was carried out in quinoa extract-free culture.

Antioxidant activity of Quinoa using DPPH radical scavenging assay

Quinoa radical scavenging activity was determined using the modified 1,1-diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging test published by Brand-Williams et al.31 and Loganayaki et al.32. Using ethanol, various concentrations of solutions were produced from the crude extracts of Q12 and Titicaca leaves and seeds. The extract solution was combined with DPPH, which was allowed to react at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was then measured at 520 nm in comparison to absolute ethanol. 1 mL of ethanol was mixed with 1 mL of DPPH to make the control solution. Positive control was ascorbic acid, a well-known natural antioxidant. The extract’s antioxidant activity was estimated using the following formula: RSA (radical scavenging activity (%) = [(A0 –A1)/A0] ×100) Where A0 represents pure DPPH absorbance and A1 represents pure DPPH absorbance in the presence of quinoa extract. By plotting the extract concentration with the corresponding scavenging action, the IC50 (concentration giving 50% inhibition) value for the dosage inhibition curve in linear range was also calculated from a standard curve33. Each experiment was conducted in three independent trials to ensure reproducibility.

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as means ± standard deviation, and the analysis of variance (one-way ANOVA) was performed using IBM SPSS v.27.0 software. To compare between means, the Least Significant Difference (LSD) test was utilized as proposed by proposed by Calinski et al.34. P-values less than 0.05 were deemed statistically significant.

Results and discussion

Screening for antimicrobial activity of crude Quinoa extracts against test microorganisms

Plant-derived medications are regarded as the first line of defense in the fight against illnesses and infections. Extracts of two quinoa cultivars were evaluated against the gram-positive bacteria Staphylococcus aureus, Staphylococcus epidermidis, and Bacillus licheniformis, as well as the gram-negative pathogens Escherichia coli and Salmonella sp. Quinoa extracts were also evaluated against four fungal strains: Aspergillus sp. 1, Aspergillus sp. 2, Fusarium sp., and Penicillium sp. The results showed that ethanoic extracts of two quinoa cultivars, Q12 and Titicaca (seeds and leaves), exhibited potential antibacterial activity against only S. aureus, S. epidermidis, and E. coli but had no impact on other bacteria or fungi. The quinoa aqueous extracts (made with hot and cold water) demonstrated no antibacterial activity against all bacterial and fungal pathogens tested. The extraction solvents were employed as a control, and no inhibition was found with any of the pathogens tested. Figure 2 reveal the bactericidal properties of quinoa against E. coli, with inhibitory zone widths for ethanoic extracts equaling 12.5 mm, 10.5 mm, 11 mm, and 7.5 mm for Q12 seeds, Q12 leaves, Titicaca seeds, and Titicaca leaves, respectively. The growth pattern of S. aureus was studied in the presence of quinoa extracts. The data in Table 1 showed that only an ethanoic extract of Q12 seeds prevented growth, with an inhibition zone of 13 mm. While the other extracts had no significant impact, the zones for Q12 leaves, Titicaca seeds, and Titicaca leaves were equivalent to 6.5 mm. As with E. coli, the aqueous extracts had no impact on S. aureus. Quinoa extracts were found to have significant antibacterial activity against S. epidermidis. The ethanoic extracts of the two varieties of quinoa, Q12 and Titicaca, inhibited S. epidermidis the most, with inhibition zones of 15 mm for Q12 seeds, 12 mm for Q12 leaves, 11 mm for Titicaca seeds, and 13.5 mm for Titicaca leaves (Table 1) (Figs. 1S and 2S, and 3S). The differential antimicrobial activity observed between bacterial strains and fungal strains may be attributed to fundamental structural and biochemical differences in their cell organization. Bacterial cells, particularly Gram-positive species, possess relatively simpler cell walls that are more susceptible to disruption by common phytochemicals found in quinoa extracts. However, fungal cells have more complex cell membranes containing ergosterol and rigid cell walls composed of chitin and glucans, which provide an additional barrier to many plant-derived antimicrobials. The current study’s findings differ slightly from those of Miranda et al.35, who found antimicrobial activity against E. coli (mean diameters of inhibition zones ranged from 8.29 to 14.79 mm) and S. aureus (mean diameters of inhibition zones ranged from 8.53 to 15.03 mm) in six different sources of quinoa seeds. Park et al.33 investigated the effect of three different cultivars of quinoa collected from three different countries (Korea, the United States, and Peru) on six pathogens, three of which were gram-positive bacteria: Staphylococcus aureus, Listeria monocytogenes, and Bacillus cereus, and three of which were gram-negative bacteria: Escherichia coli, Salmonella typhimurium, and Campylobacter jejuni. All of these extracts had a very mild impact on all of the bacteria examined; the inhibition zones obtained were less than 8 mm, with the exception of Listeria monocytogenes, which had no effect. Woldemichael and Wink36 discovered that certain saponins in quinoa had antifungal activity, inhibiting the development of Candida albicans. Furthermore, Mahdavi-Yekta et al.37 discovered that quinoa seeds, in addition to their high protein content and strong antioxidant characteristics, had a good antibacterial impact on S. pyogenes and E. coli, making them a suitable food preservative. According to the findings of Khan and Javaid38, quinoa leaf extract is a rich source of bioactive compounds and may be utilized to treat a variety of diseases.

Based on the previous findings, the extract with inhibitory action against gram-positive and gram-negative pathogenic bacteria was chosen for MIC and MBC assays. Table 1 displays the MIC values. The MIC values for all ethanolic extracts were the same and equivalent to 375 µg/mL, indicating that the extracts have an inhibitory effect on S. epidermidis at the same concentration. Ethanolic extracts of Q12 (seed and leaf extracts) and Titicaca (seed extract only) had a similar MIC value of 375 µg/mL against E. coli. While Q12 S was the sole extract that demonstrated action against S. aureus, its MIC value was likewise 375 µg/mL. The minimal bactericidal concentration (MBC) of quinoa extracts is their capacity to kill the cells of tested pathogens. The MBC of Q12 S against all test microorganisms was 750 µg/mL (Table 2). MBCs of Q12 L were 750 µg/mL against E. coli and 375 µg/mL against S. epidermidis, whereas MBCs of Titicaca S were 375 µg/mL against the same bacterium. Titicaca L MBC was evaluated solely against S. epidermidis and was 375 µg/mL. In several situations, the MICs and MBCs of Q12 L, Titicaca S, and Titicaca L had identical values, demonstrating the bactericidal effects of these extracts. Vancomycin was chosen for gram-positive bacteria and Gentamycin for gram-negative bacteria. Table 1 displays antibiotic MIC values. Gentamycin has a MIC and MBC of 78 µg/mL when tested against E. coli. When tested against S. aureus, the MIC and MBC values for Vancomycin were 125 µg/mL; however, when tested against S. epidermidis, the MIC was equivalent to 125 µg/mL and the MBC was equal to 250 µg/mL. In a study performed by Sun et al.39 on the antimicrobial potential of quinoa saponins on Fusobacterium nucleatum, it was revealed that the MIC was equivalent to 31.3 µg/mL and the MBC was 125 µg/mL. Similarly, Pereira et al.40 conducted research employing Chenopodium quinoa grains and tested the antibacterial activity of phenolic chemicals found in the grains, particularly quercetin and kaempferol derivatives. The mean MBC for these compounds on diverse bacterial strains was between 153 µg/mL and 916 µg/mL, according to one investigation. Moreover, Dong et al.41 discovered that quinoa husk extract has high antibacterial activity against S. aureus and S. epidermidis, with MIC values in the range of 62.5 µg/mL and MBC levels of 125 µg/mL.

Determination of MBC/ MIC ratio for some Quinoa extracts

The MBC/MIC ratio was determined for the quinoa extracts tested, and the results are shown in Table 1. According to Konaté et al.42, some extracts demonstrated bactericidal action against some bacterial infections (MBC/MIC ratio = 1 or 2), and the result was classified as bactericidal. The extracts of quinoa spp. seeds and leaves were shown to be bactericidal rather than bacteriostatic against the pathogens examined.

Phytochemical analysis of Quinoa extracts

The phytochemical analysis of quinoa extracts (Q12 and Titicaca) is presented in Table 2, revealing the existence of alkaloids, flavonoids, glycosides, and saponins in the seeds of the two cultivars Q12 and Titicaca, along with alkaloids, glycosides, phenols, saponins, tannins, and terpenoids in the leafy parts of both Q12 and Titicaca. The finding was nearly identical to that published by Miranda et al.35, who discovered that the six various sources of quinoa seeds embrace different types of phytochemicals that involve polyphenols (Flavanoids, phenols, and tannins) and saponins. Flavonoids are bioactive chemicals that inhabit plant components, such as fruits and flowers, as pigments. Flavonoids, which are believed to have antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial effects, are synthesized by plants in response to environmental stress and microbial diseases. They may disrupt cell membrane formation in both Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, expand into microbe membranes, and hinder nucleic acid synthesis; for example, the overall synthesis of DNA and RNA in Vibrio harveyi was drastically inhibited after the incorporation of genistein for 15 min43. Quinoa seeds possess a high concentration of saponins. Saponins are triterpenoids and sapogenin glycosides13. Woldemichael and Wink36 observed that quinoa saponins had antifungal action against Candida albicans. According to Kuljanabhagavad and Wink44, quinoa seeds contain 3-O–D-Glucopyranosyl oleanolic acid, a sapogenin, and so have strong anti-inflammatory effect. Yao et al.45 discovered that quinoa saponin fractions diminished the generation of inflammatory mediators and blocked the release of inflammatory cytokines. Polyphenols exhibit antibacterial action against a wide range of microorganisms. Among the polyphenols, flavanols, flavonols, and phenolic acids have the greatest efficacy against bacteria due to their ability to (1) block bacterial virulence agents such as enzymes and toxins, (2) interact with the cytoplasmic membrane, (3) inhibit bacterial biofilm development, and (4) provide a synergistic effect when combined with antibiotics. As a result, they are regarded as effective, inexpensive, and cost-effective antibacterial agents46. Tannins are big molecular weight compounds formed by the polymerization process of smaller phenolic compounds. Phenolic compounds are any chemicals produced from the phenol group. Terpenoids were previously documented by Bouyahya et al.47 to interfere with QS signaling and minimize biofilm formation by altering membrane fatty acids, coagulating proteins, perturbing oxidative phosphorylation, modulating efflux pumps, reducing membrane integrity, boosting membrane permeability, and inhibiting biofilm formation. Alkaloids are secondary metabolites found in the seeds, roots, and leaves of various plants that have been shown to be effective as antibacterial agents against certain microbes. The method of action of these alkaloids varies depending on the kind (metronidazole, quinolones, or others) and may include, among other things, suppressing bacterial cell division, decreasing respiration and enzyme activity, and damaging bacterial membranes48. Finally, the actual Phytochemical analysis correlate clearly with the antimicrobial results related to phenolic acids and flavonoids16–19.

Determination of fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC) of antibiotics and Quinoa extracts combinations

The combined impacts of quinoa and regularly used antibiotics to inhibit gram-negative and gram-positive bacterial pathogens have been investigated using fractional inhibitory concentration (FIC). All quinoa extracts evaluated against pathogenic bacteria had equivalent MICs of µ375 g/mL (Table 1). The integration of antibiotics with quinoa extracts significantly reduced the MIC of Vancomycin tested against S. aureus and S. epidermidis, and Gentamycin tested against E. coli, with one exception: Titicaca L in combination with Vancomycin was tested against S. epidermidis, and the MIC value did not change (375 µg/mL). Quinoa and antibiotics combined efficacy against tested microorganisms resulted in fractional inhibitory concentration indices (FICIs). For each antibiotic-quinoa combination, the fractional inhibitory concentration index (FICI) was computed. FICI = FIC of an antibiotic + FIC of a quinoa extract. The obtained values were interpreted as follows: FICI ≤ 0.5 indicates synergy; 0.5–0.75 indicates partial synergy; 0.76-1 indicates an additive impact; 1–4 indicates indifference; and > 4 indicates antagonism. Gentamicin’s FICI in conjunction with Q12 S or Titicaca S was 0.75 ± 0.06 (Table 3). These results suggested that combining CN with these extracts would result in partial synergy, but the FICI of CN with Q12 L was 1 ± 0.06, indicating that it had an additive impact against E. coli. Furthermore, the combination of VA and Q12 S extract displayed a synergistic impact against S. aureus, with FICI equal to 0.5 ± 0.04. The FICs of VA with various quinoa extracts (Q12 S, Q12 L, Titicaca S, and Titicaca L) against S. epidermidis were 0.5 ± 0.04, 0.75 ± 0.06, 1 ± 0.06, and 2 ± 0.05, respectively. The FICI index revealed that the VA/Q12 S combination had a synergistic impact against S. epidermidis, whereas the VA/Q12 L combination had a partly synergistic effect, an additive effect in the case of VA/Titicaca S, and an indifferent effect in the case of VA/Titicaca L (Table 3). Many studies have focused on the effect of combining antibiotics with plant extracts (which include antibacterial phytochemicals like quinoa) to boost antibiotic action. Yu et al.49 demonstrated the impact of combining Berberine, Oxacillin, and Ampicillin against S. aureus, with the FICI of Berberine and Ambicillin being 0.625, indicating partial synergy. Quinoa, on the other hand, is high in phytochemicals such as saponins, which have antibacterial properties against several infections. Tagousop et al.50 revealed that extracted saponins from Melanthera elliptica methanolic extract had a synergistic impact against tested pathogens (S. aureus ATCC 25923, E. coli S2, and Shigella flexneri SDINT) when combined with vancomycin and fluconazole. These findings are consistent with the current investigation, which found that combining Q12 S with VA against S. aureus and S. epidermidis had a synergistic effect.

Time-kill curve assay

A time-kill curve test was used to evaluate the bactericidal efficacy of various bioactive quinoa extracts over a 24-hour period. Based on the preceding findings in Table 1, all of the extracts with antibacterial properties were chosen to be tested against E. coli, S. aureus, and S. epidermidis. MIC x 4 had the most potent bactericidal effect of the three doses utilized in the time-kill experiment, followed by MIC x 2 and MIC x 1. Bacterial growth in MHB sans quinoa extracts served as a control. The time kill curves of E. coli, S. aureus, and S. epidermidis treated with three doses of Q12 S (MICx1, MICx2, and MICx4) are shown in Fig. 3. The results demonstrate that the growth of bacteria treated with Q12 S extract decreased after 4 h, and there was a significant drop in growth after 8 h when compared to the growth in MHB without quinoa extracts. Figure 3 shows the same behavior for E. coli and S. epidermidis, respectively. Similarly, Fig. 4 shows similar antibacterial activity of Q12 L against the tested bacteria. As a result, these findings demonstrated that the antibacterial actions of Q12 S and Q12 L were dose-dependent and mostly active during the exponential growth phase of bacterial development, when cells must divide. The exponential death is visible in the time-kill curve figures.

Transmission electron micrographs

The ethanolic extract of Q12 seeds was the most powerfully evaluated antibacterial bioactive agent based on MIC, MBC, and time-kill curve data. Bactericidal activity was observed against E. coli, S. aureus, and S. epidermidis. In the current investigation, S. epidermidis was impacted by all of the produced extracts. The micrographs (Fig. 5) clearly show that Q12 S extract had a negative impact on the cell structure of S. epidermidis. The cell structure may be deformed. When treated cells were compared to untreated cells, the cell wall and cytoplasmic membrane were perforated, and the protoplasm was destroyed. Figure 5A depicts the usual form of an untreated cell, whereas Fig. 5B,C depict injured S. epidermidis cells treated with Q12 seed extract. These findings imply that quinoa extract can cause alterations in bacterial cell structure, which can result in cell death. The impact of Aquilaria crassna leaf extract on S. epidermidis was studied by Kamonwannasit et al.51. They evaluated TEM pictures, which revealed that the extract induced bacterial cell wall breaches and altered bacterial morphology. The antibacterial mechanism of quinoa extract is yet unknown; one possible explanation for membrane damage is that quinoa contains saponins, which may react with bacterial cell membranes, causing rupture and release of protoplasm, resulting in cell shrinkage and death39.

Antioxidant effect of Quinoa

The extracts’ antioxidant activity was initially determined using the DPPH scavenging activity test and the results were demonstrated in Table 1S, 2S, 3S, 4S, and 5S in the Supplementary Materials. The DPPH scavenging assay has been frequently employed for evaluating primary antioxidant activity, which at first was measured by a spectrophotometer as the paramagnetic DPPH radical received an electron or hydrogen radical from a donor antioxidant substance to become a stable diamagnetic molecule31,32.The ethanolic extracts of various quinoa extracts revealed that Titicaca seeds’ extract had highest antioxidant activity, based on its IC50 value (0.0072 mg/mL), followed by Q12 seed extract ( IC50 = 0.0093 mg/mL), Q12 leaves extract (IC50 = 0.0096 mg/mL), and Titicaca leaves extract ( IC50 = 0.0138 mg/mL (Table 4). Ascorbic acid (vitamin C), on the other hand, is an antioxidant chemical renowned for its capacity to safeguard our systems from free radical damage and illness, with an IC50 value of 0.0321 mg/mL (Table 4) The statistical analysis revealed a substantial increase in the antioxidant activity of tested quinoa extracts with increasing concentrations (P ≤ 0.01). The outstanding antioxidant potential of quinoa extracts noticed in the present investigation has previously been reported in many studies. Nsimba et al.52 indicated that quinoa has antioxidant activity, that this activity belongs to non-phenolic compounds, and that it may play a greater role in free radical scavenging than the phenolic contents. The author, Bhaduri26, extracted two distinct quinoa seeds using six different solvents (hexane, acetone, methanol, ethanol, ethyl acetate, and water) and found that all extracts had considerable antioxidant activity. As previously proven by Yu et al.53, Alvarez-Jubete et al.54, Brend et al.55, and Carciochi et al.56, the antioxidant capacity of quinoa can be related to its phenolic components. It is worth noting that the IC50 values in this investigation were lower than those reported in earlier studies incorporating the DPPH scavenging test. Nsimba et al.52 found that the IC50 of quinoa seeds gathered from two separate nations, Bolivia and Japan, ranged between 0.1 and 22.4 mg/mL, which was greater than the values observed in the current study. Bhaduri26 said that the lowest IC50 of quinoa seed extract was 0.014 mg/mL, which was quite near the IC50 of 0.0138 reported for Titicaca leaf extract in the current investigation, although the IC50 of the other extracts was lower. According to Park et al.33, the IC50 of quinoa seed extracts grown in Korea was 0.25 mg/mL, which was the lowest antioxidant activity, followed by quinoa grown in the United States (0.26 mg/mL) and Peru (0.47 mg/mL). These variations might be attributed to the influence of soil and meteorological conditions on quinoa phytochemical components.

Conclusion

The majority of medications on the market today are derived from natural or organic compounds. Plants have been a valuable source of natural products containing active chemicals that have been exploited in the discovery and development of novel medications. Quinoa (Chenopodium quinoa Willd.) is rich in phytochemicals such as phenols, saponins, flavonoids, and other substances, which are responsible for quinoa’s antibacterial, antioxidant, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory properties, in addition to its nutritional benefits. In our recent investigation, we discovered that the two tested quinoa types (Q12 and Titicaca) had moderate anti-bacterial activity against several foodborne and non-foodborne pathogenic bacteria, as well as strong antioxidant activity, in addition to other quinoa activities. Furthermore, moderate synergistic effects were observed when combined with certain antibiotics, suggesting potential for enhanced antimicrobial strategies. While these findings highlight the value of quinoa as a natural source of bioactive compounds, their antimicrobial efficacy was not uniformly strong across all tested strains. Future research will concentrate on employing quinoa as a preservative for canned food instead of chemicals, which have negative impacts on consumer health and may affect food quality. The phytochemical analysis of quinoa (seeds and leaves) revealed high content of saponins, suggesting potential applications in the industrial sector in the future, such as in the production of soap, shampoo, and cosmetics.
