Unruptured congenital sinus of Valsalva aneurysm causing right ventricular outflow tract obstruction associated with hidden ventricular septal defect: a case report
Authors: Sajed Majadla, Saleem Majadleh, Moath Nairat, Issa Alkhdour, Yunis Daralammouri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSinus of Valsalva aneurysms are rare congenital lesions. Unruptured aneurysms are often silent, but when they are large, they can cause symptoms by compressing nearby structures. This case is notable because an unruptured aneurysm from the right sinus produced severe right ventricular outflow tract obstruction and concealed a ventricular septal defect that was not detected on preoperative imaging.Case presentationA 45-year-old man with a remote history of congenital heart disease and no follow-up presented with three weeks of progressive shortness of breath, which was consistent with New York Heart Association classes II to III. He was stable but had signs of right-sided heart failure, including elevated jugular venous pressure and bilateral leg edema. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed a large saccular aneurysm arising from the right sinus of Valsalva and protruding into the right ventricle with severe right ventricular outflow tract obstruction and a peak systolic gradient of 80 mmHg. Transesophageal echocardiography confirmed the aneurysm and obstruction; an agitated saline study did not demonstrate intracardiac shunting. Cardiac computed tomography confirmed the aneurysm anatomy and vascular dilation, and coronary angiography showed normal coronary arteries. The patient underwent surgical repair with excision of the large aneurysmal sac, closure of an intraoperatively discovered ventricular septal defect using a pericardial patch, and valve-sparing aortic root reimplantation with a tube graft and aortic valve repair. He was discharged five days after surgery without complications. At one month, echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular function, relief of outflow obstruction, no residual shunt, decreased pulmonary artery dilation, and mild residual aortic regurgitation.ConclusionsA large unruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysm can cause severe right ventricular outflow tract obstruction and can mask an associated ventricular septal defect despite negative shunt assessment. Surgeons and imagers should anticipate occult associated defects when planning repair.Keywords: Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm, Right ventricular outflow tract obstruction, Ventricular septal defect, Congenital heart disease.

Introduction

Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SOVA) is a rare cardiac defect characterized by abnormal dilation of the aortic root between the aortic valve annulus and the sinotubular junction [1]. SOVA may be congenital or acquired and occurs in less than 0.09% of the general population, accounting for approximately 3.5% of all congenital heart diseases [1, 2]. Given the anatomical location and physiological role of the sinuses of Valsalva, SOVA may lead to significant clinical consequences in both ruptured and unruptured states [2]. Small unruptured aneurysms are often detected incidentally, whereas larger lesions may produce symptoms because of mass effects on adjacent structures(2). Unruptured sinus of Valsalva aneurysms (SOVAs) can be asymptomatic or cause various clinical manifestations as a consequence of their mass effect on the coronary arteries, heart valves, and other adjacent structures [3]. Most sinus of Valsalva aneurysms are diagnosed by echocardiography, whereas computed tomography (CT) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) provide superior anatomical detail when further assessment is needed [1]. The aim of this case report is to highlight an unusual presentation of an unruptured congenital sinus of Valsalva aneurysm causing severe right ventricular outflow tract obstruction while an associated ventricular septal defect is concealed, emphasizing the risk of missed associated defects and the implications for preoperative assessment and surgical planning.

Case presentation

A 45-year-old man with a 20-year history of congenital heart disease was previously reassured and was lost to follow-up, with no available medical reports. He presented to the outpatient clinic with progressive shortness of breath over the preceding three weeks, corresponding to New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional classes II–III.

On presentation, the patient was hemodynamically stable but exhibited signs of right-sided heart failure, including elevated jugular venous pressure and bilateral lower limb edema. Electrocardiography demonstrated sinus tachycardia without significant ischemic or conduction abnormalities. Chest radiography revealed cardiomegaly.

Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) revealed a dilated left ventricle (LV end-diastolic diameter of 61 mm and LV end-systolic diameter of 42 mm) with preserved systolic function. Retraction, shortening and mild thickening of the right coronary cusp causing moderate aortic regurgitation were noted (pressure half-time 230 ms, vena contracta 5 mm), along with dilation of the ascending aorta measuring 43 mm. A large saccular aneurysm of the right sinus of Valsalva was identified, protruding into the right ventricle and causing turbulence (Fig. 1) and severe obstruction of the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT), with a peak systolic gradient of 80 mmHg (Fig. 2). Poststenotic dilation of the pulmonary artery was observed, measuring 49 mm without pulmonary regurgitation or stenosis.

Cardiac echocardiography—long-axis view—demonstrating a saccular aneurysm arising from the right sinus of Valsalva and protruding into the right ventricle outflow tract

Transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) confirmed the presence of a saccular aneurysm arising from the right sinus of Valsalva, resulting in severe RVOT obstruction and more significant aortic regurgitation than previously appreciated. The pulmonary artery was markedly dilated; there was no regurgitation of the pulmonary valve. An agitated saline study revealed no evidence of intracardiac shunting through the aneurysm or the interventricular septum (Fig. 3).

Transesophageal echocardiography, mid-esophageal view, and right ventricular inflow–outflow showing the saccular aneurysm of the sinus of Valsalva bulging (arrow) in the right ventricle (RV) after the agitation saline test

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) further confirmed a sinus of Valsalva aneurysm with mild dilation of the ascending aorta (43 mm) and significant dilation of the pulmonary artery (49 mm) (Fig. 4). The coronary arteries demonstrated a normal origin and course, without evidence of calcification or stenosis. Coronary angiography was subsequently performed to exclude concomitant coronary artery disease and revealed normal coronary arteries without stenotic lesions.

Cardiac CT angiography at the level of the Valsalva sinus showing the saccular aneurysm bulging in the RVOT (arrow)

The patient was referred for surgical management. Intraoperatively, the total area of the aortic root was extremely frail, especially after extensive surgical dissection; thus, we thought that it was better to address the whole area through a David procedure. The ascending aorta was transected above the sinotubular junction. The aortic sinuses were excised, and the left and right coronary buttons were created. The native aortic valve cusps and commissures were preserved, and valve-sparing aortic root reimplantation (David procedure) was performed. The SOVA sac was very large and masked a very large VSD that was immediately beneath the basal ring of the aortic valve. The saccular aneurysm originating from the right coronary sinus protruded into the RVOT. A ventricular septal defect (VSD) was detected in the upper interventricular septum beneath the aneurysm, which had not been visualized on preoperative echocardiography because of masking by the aneurysmal sac and the aortic regurgitant jet. There was dilatation of the pulmonary root and main pulmonary artery, which impinged significantly on the aortic root. The aneurysmal sac was excised; however, during the surgical dissection of the aortic root, an iatrogenic defect occurred between the left and right ventricular outflow tracts, which was repaired using a pericardial patch, and another pericardial patch was also used to close the VSD. The aortic root was replaced with a 28-mm tube graft, and the native aortic valve was repaired, including the right coronary cusp (Fig. 5).

A: Intraoperative image: The arrow indicates the aneurysmal sac in the right coronary sinus, held by forceps. B: Schematic illustration of the patch of the VSD and the resected aneurysmal sac (red segment)

The patient was discharged five days after surgery without complications. At the one-month follow-up, echocardiography revealed preserved left ventricular (LV) function, normalization of the left ventricle size, and decreased pulmonary artery dilatation after the right ventricular outflow tract (RVOT) obstruction was relieved. The David procedure yielded good results, with persistent mild aortic regurgitation (AR) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SOVA) is an uncommon congenital cardiac anomaly, with an estimated prevalence of only 0.09% in the general population and 3.5% of all congenital heart diseases [1, 4]. It predominantly involves the right coronary sinus (≈ 77% of cases) and occurs more often in middle-aged males [5]. Notably, congenital SOVAs frequently coexist with other cardiac defects—most commonly ventricular septal defects (VSDs)—reported in approximately 30–60% of cases as well as aortic valve abnormalities (e.g., bicuspid valve) or aortic regurgitation in nearly half of patients [4, 5]. This strong association highlights the need to evaluate for concomitant lesions whenever a SOVA is diagnosed.

Clinical presentations range widely: many cases of unruptured SOVA remain asymptomatic and are found incidentally, whereas rupture typically causes acute chest pain, heart failure, or hemodynamic collapse [6]. In our patient, the aneurysm had not yet ruptured but produced significant symptoms, indicating an uncommon presentation of unruptured SOVA. Mechanically, a large unruptured aneurysm can cause symptoms because of mass effects and flow obstruction [7]. In the right coronary sinus, an expanding aneurysm may bulge into the right ventricular outflow tract RVOT, effectively creating subpulmonic obstruction [8]. Such cases, while rare, have been documented to present with exertional dyspnea, fatigue, and signs of right-sided heart failure due to RVOT obstruction [9, 10]. In our case, the aneurysmal sac protruded into the RVOT and produced a severe outflow gradient, explaining the patient’s NYHA class II–III dyspnoea. In this patient, the marked obstruction from the aneurysm was associated with a prominent RVOT pressure gradient and compensatory hypertrophy/dilation of the right ventricle, as well as dilation of the downstream pulmonary artery as a physiologic consequence of RVOT obstruction.

Another key challenge in this case was the “hidden” VSD, which highlights the risk of missed associated defects. Congenital SOVA often coexists with VSD, yet the aneurysm itself may mask the defect, as was shown in published cases [5, 11]. In our patient, the longstanding membranous VSD remained undiagnosed preoperatively, likely because the aneurysmal sac effectively covered or functionally sealed the ventricular septal opening, thereby limiting visualization of shunting during preoperative imaging. This phenomenon has been noted in surgical reports; for example, in a series of SOVA repairs, a subset of patients had no interventricular communication because the aneurysm effectively sealed the VSD [12]. In our case, despite thorough preoperative imaging, the subaortic VSD remained undetected until surgical exploration. Our case is unusual because an unruptured congenital sinus of Valsalva aneurysm in an adult caused severe RVOT obstruction and at the same time masked an associated ventricular septal defect. This highlights how combined congenital lesions can distort the clinical picture and lead to false reassurance in preoperative studies.

Although excision of the sinus of Valsalva aneurysm (SOVA), closure of the ventricular septal defect (VSD), and repair of the aortic valve would have been technically feasible in this case, several factors led us to favour a more radical approach with a David procedure. The SOVA sac was notably large and obscured an equally significant VSD located immediately beneath the basal ring of the aortic valve, complicating localized repair. In addition, there was dilation of the pulmonary root and main pulmonary artery, which exerted significant mass effect on the aortic root. The aortic root tissue itself appeared markedly friable, particularly after extensive surgical dissection, raising concerns about the durability of more conservative repair. Furthermore, the aortic root was borderline dilated at 43 mm, further supporting a more definitive intervention.

Our report reinforces a practical point: when a large sinus of Valsalva aneurysm is present, a hidden VSD should remain on the differential even if routine imaging does not show a shunt. In this setting, all the regions of the aortic valve, sinus of Valsalva and septum should be more investigated before surgery: targeted multimodal imaging, including transesophageal echocardiography and, when needed, cardiac CT or cardiac MRI, can be crucial for identifying an underlying VSD that would change operative planning.
