The joint association of sleep duration and physical activity with frailty among older adults: the first evidence from CHARLS
Authors: Fu Yuan, Shiqiang Wang, Huatao Zheng, Xingcan Zhou, Xuan Tang, Dan Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThis study aimed to investigate the independent and joint associations of sleep duration and physical activity (PA) with longitudinal frailty developmental trajectories among older adults in the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS).MethodsWe utilized five waves of longitudinal data (2011–2020) from the CHARLS cohort. Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was employed to identify distinct dynamic frailty trajectories. Sleep duration was categorized as short (< 6 h/day), normal (6–8 h/day), and long (> 8 h/day). PA levels were classified into light-level PA (LPA, < 600 MET-minutes/week), moderate-level PA (MPA, 600–3000 MET-minutes/week), and vigorous-level PA (VPA, > 3000 MET-minutes/week) based on the International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ) recommendations. Multinomial logistic regression models were conducted to examine the independent and joint effects of sleep and PA on the identified frailty trajectories.ResultsFrom 2011 to 2020, the overall pooled prevalence of frailty was the lowest in the VPA group (20.42%) and the normal sleep duration group (19.85%) compared with all other subgroups (P < 0.001). Three distinct frailty trajectories were identified: low-level stable, medium-level increase, and high-level increase. In the fully adjusted model, MVPA, normal and long sleep durations significantly reduced the risk of transitioning into the medium-level and high-level increase trajectories compared to LPA and short sleep. In joint analyses, compared to the LPA with short sleep reference group, significantly lower odds of entering the high-level increase trajectory were observed in the LPA with normal sleep subgroup (OR = 0.203, 95% CI: 0.049–0.841) and MPA with normal or long sleep subgroups (normal: OR = 0.081, 95% CI: 0.021–0.312; long: OR = 0.074, 95% CI: 0.014–0.383). Notably, VPA consistently conferred substantial protection across all sleep categories (short: OR = 0.224, 95% CI: 0.064–0.788; normal: OR = 0.063, 95% CI: 0.018–0.221; long: OR = 0.131, 95% CI: 0.033–0.521).ConclusionsThe findings of this study demonstrate that adequate sleep duration and PA are crucial independent and synergistic protective factors against rapid frailty progression. These findings highlight that concurrently targeting both behaviors is a vital clinical strategy to mitigate severe frailty and promote healthy aging.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07841-9.

Introduction

The global population is undergoing a profound demographic transition marked by rapid population aging, a trend projected to accelerate significantly over the coming decades. According to the latest projections from the World Health Organization (WHO), the number of people aged 60 years and older will double to an estimated 2 billion by 2050 [1]. This demographic shift has brought the health of older adults to the forefront of global public health attention. Frailty is a comprehensive clinical indicator that reflects the overall health status of older adults, characterized by progressive decline in multiple physiological systems and functional capacities, and represents one of the most pressing public health challenges worldwide [2]. Frailty develops through the accumulation of health deficits arising from the complex interplay of physical, physiological, psychological, and environmental factors [3]. The profound clinical and public health significance of frailty is underscored by its robust associations with a wide spectrum of adverse health outcomes. Older adults with frailty are at significantly elevated risk of cognitive decline, cardiovascular diseases, other chronic conditions, and functional deterioration. Furthermore, frailty substantially increases vulnerability to falls, disability, hospitalization, and premature mortality [4]. Without early intervention, frailty contributes to excessive healthcare resource utilization and imposes substantial economic burdens, placing significant strain on both family caregivers and broader social healthcare systems [5]. Consequently, addressing frailty among older adults is critical. A key opportunity for frailty management lies in recognizing it as a dynamic and potentially reversible process [2]. Therefore, identifying modifiable risk factors and implementing targeted interventions to reduce frailty incidence is of paramount importance.

Sleep, as an important component of daily life, is closely associated with the development of frailty. A large-scale longitudinal study using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) demonstrated that short sleep duration was associated with a significantly increased risk of incident frailty [6]. A systematic review and meta-analysis has demonstrated a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and frailty risk, with both short and long sleep durations identified as significant risk factors for elevated frailty risk [7]. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining optimal sleep duration as a key preventive strategy against frailty. Physical activity (PA) is another well-established modifiable lifestyle factor closely associated with frailty development. Epidemiological evidence indicates that insufficient PA levels among adults aged 60 years and older have continued to rise globally, a trend that significantly increases the risk of adverse health outcomes [8]. PA has been consistently shown to exert a protective effect against frailty in older adults, with higher PA levels associated with reduced frailty risk [9–11]. Using data from the 2018 wave of CHARLS, Li et al. demonstrated that participants in the highest PA quartile had a 45% lower risk of frailty compared with those in the lowest quartile [12]. A systematic review and meta-analysis that pooled data from multiple cohort studies concluded that moderate-level physical activity (MPA) and vigorous-level physical activity (VPA) exert beneficial effects on frailty prevention and management via multiple mechanisms, including enhancing physical function, improving metabolic status, and slowing chronic disease progression [13]. Sleep and PA share a dynamic, bidirectional relationship, with each influencing the other through multiple biological and behavioral pathways [14–17]. However, prior research is constrained by two major limitations. Not only have previous studies largely neglected the combined effects of sleep duration and PA on frailty, but they have also failed to conceptualize frailty as a dynamic continuum when examining these associations. By primarily utilizing frailty status at a single time point as the outcome, most existing literature rarely accounts for longitudinal changes in frailty severity, thereby overlooking its inherent variability and potential reversibility. Therefore, we employed a longitudinal analytical approach utilizing data from the CHARLS (2011–2020). By identifying the dynamic developmental trajectories of frailty, we aimed to comprehensively investigate the independent and joint associations of sleep duration and PA with these distinct frailty trajectories. Our findings will inform the development of targeted public health policies and interventions, provide an evidence base for frailty prevention, and support healthy aging initiatives.

Methods

Study design and population

This study used data from the CHARLS, a nationally representative longitudinal survey of community-dwelling Chinese adults aged 45 years and older and their spouses. CHARLS collects comprehensive data on the social, economic, and health status of participants. The study protocol was approved by the Ethical Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015). All participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment [18]. CHARLS conducted five waves of surveys between 2011 and 2020: baseline survey data in 2011 (Wave 1), and follow-up waves in 2013 (Wave 2), 2015 (Wave 3), 2018 (Wave 4), and 2020 (Wave 5). Detailed information on the CHARLS study design, objectives, sampling framework, and questionnaires is available elsewhere [18].

We used data from all five survey waves to examine temporal trends in frailty prevalence. To ensure analytical rigor, we applied specific exclusion criteria. We excluded 21,811 participants aged below 60 years, 147 with missing sleep duration data, 6,637 lacking PA measurements, and 53,686 with incomplete frailty information. Missing data for frailty assessment encompassed incomplete records regarding physical function, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms. Physical function consisted of three components: 5,967 participants with missing data on chronic diseases, 26,515 with missing data on activities of daily living, 3,372 with missing self-rated health information, and 952 with missing audio-visual function data. In terms of mental and cognitive assessments, 14,341 participants had missing cognitive function data, and 2,584 had incomplete information on depressive symptoms. This process resulted in a final analytical sample of 14,335 participants: 1,495 in 2011, 1,385 in 2013, 1,799 in 2015, 3,801 in 2018, and 5,855 in 2020. Additionally, a final cohort of 974 participants with at least three waves of follow-up data was specifically selected to examine the independent and joint effects of sleep duration and PA on different frailty trajectories. Assessments

Frailty Index(FI)

The FI was used to assess frailty status among older adults. This is a well-validated and reliable tool for evaluating and predicting frailty in older adult populations. The index integrates multiple age-related health deficits according to established methodological standards [19]. According to the construction of the FI standard, the selected variables must be related to health and cover a series of functional systems in the older people body. The selected variables must be at least 30 or more. Under the above conditions, the variables can be freely selected for FI construction. The selection process is based on the established standard procedures and previous literature [20–22]. A total of 31 health-related variables were included to construct the FI, covering three domains: physical function, cognitive function, and depressive symptoms. (1) Physical function domain: Included 29 variables: 14 chronic diseases, 1 self-rated health item, 2 audiovisual function items, and 12 activities of daily living (ADL) items. (2) Cognitive function: Assessed using the CHARLS cognitive test battery. Cognitive impairment was defined based on educational attainment: score < 17 for illiterate participants, < 20 for primary school graduates, and < 24 for secondary school graduates or above [23]. (3) Depressive symptoms: Measured using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D 10) [24, 25]. Based on previous studies, the FI was calculated by summing the scores of all defects and dividing by the total number of defects, resulting in a score representing the frailty index for each participant, ranging from 0 to1 [26]. A frailty index of ≤ 0.10 was considered non-frailty, that of > 0.10 but < 0.25 was considered pre-frail, and that of ≥ 0.25 was considered frail [27].

Sleep duration

In the CHARLS, self-reported nighttime sleep duration was obtained through the question: “During the past month, how many hours of actual sleep did you get at night?” Nap duration was assessed through the following question: “During the past month, how long did you usually nap?” Total sleep duration was calculated as the sum of nighttime sleep and daytime nap duration. Consistent with prior studies [28, 29], participants were divided into three groups based on sleep duration: short (< 6 h/day), normal (6–8 h/day), and long (> 8 h/day).

Physical Activity(PA)

PA levels were assessed using the short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire (IPAQ), which measures light, moderate, and vigorous PA based on weekly frequency and duration [30]. CHARLS uses interval response options for PA duration rather than open-ended numerical responses. Consistent with previous studies [31, 32], we categorized the PA time, “≥10 min and < 30 min” was recorded as 30 min, “≥30 min and < 2 h” was recorded as 60 min, “≥2 h and < 4 h” was recorded as 180 min, and “≥4 h” was recorded as 240 min. Meanwhile, to avoid grouping errors, we re-encoded individuals who engaged in a certain intensity of PA for more than 3 h a day as 180 min based on the data truncation principle. This principle allows a maximum of 21 h (1 260 min) of PA per week to be reported for each intensity. According to the guidelines of IPAQ, the metabolic equivalent (MET) unit can be used to more accurately assess the intensity of physical exertion. The MET value is calculated using the following formula: MET = (8.0 × weekly vigorous PA frequency × daily time) + (4.0 × weekly moderate PA frequency × daily time) + (3.3 × weekly low PA frequency × daily time) [33]. According to IPAQ standards, weekly PA levels were categorized into LPA (< 600 MET-minutes/week), MPA (600–3000 MET-minutes/week), and VPA (> 3000 MET-minutes/week).

Covariates

We considered the following variables as potential confounding factors: age (60–69 years, 70–79 years, and ≥ 80 years), gender (male/female), educational level (illiterate, primary school or below, middle school and above), marital status (married, unmarried), residence (urban, rural), number of chronic diseases (0, 1, 2, ≥ 3), work employment status, and current smoking and drinking status (yes/no).

Statistical analysis

Group-based trajectory modeling (GBTM) was employed to identify distinct developmental trajectories of the frailty index over the follow-up period. GBTM is a latent class finite mixture model specifically designed for analyzing longitudinal data [34]. This approach classifies individuals based on their longitudinal trajectories, identifying subgroups with similar developmental trends over a specific period, thereby revealing the underlying heterogeneity within the population [35]. The frailty change trajectories of participants with at least three study visits were plotted based on their observed data. To determine the optimal number of frailty trajectories, a series of models ranging from two to four trajectory groups was fitted. The optimal model was selected based on clinical interpretability and stringent statistical criteria: a Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) closer to zero, Average Posterior Probability (AVEPP) > 0.7, Odds of Correct Classification (OCC) > 5.0, relative entropy (Ek) > 0.7, and a minimum group size of 5%. Finally, the correspondence between estimated and assigned group proportions was verified to ensure classification reliability [36]. Based on these criteria, a three-group trajectory model was selected to best fit the data (Supplementary Table S1).

All variables in this study were categorical. Baseline characteristics were summarized for the overall study population and across the distinct frailty trajectories. Data were presented as frequencies and percentages, with between-group comparisons performed using chi-square tests. Multinomial logistic regression models were employed to evaluate the independent associations of sleep duration and PA levels with frailty trajectories. To further investigate their joint effects, participants were cross-classified into nine subcategories based on the combination of their sleep duration and PA levels, and these joint associations were similarly evaluated using multinomial logistic regression. Frailty trajectories were estimated using the “traj” plugin in Stata version 18.0. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 27.0. Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value < 0.05.

In terms of baseline health and lifestyle factors, more than half of the study population reported moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA), and 52.7% maintained a normal sleep duration. Additionally, 43.2% and 31.2% of the participants reported smoking and drinking habits, respectively.

Changes in the prevalence of frailty in different sleep duration and PA between 2011 and 2020

The prevalence of frailty among older adults with LPA decreased by 18.68% from 2011 to 2020, declining from 45.70% to 27.02% (p < 0.001 for trend; Fig. 2), representing a relative reduction of 40.88%. Similarly, frailty prevalence among those with VPA decreased by 8.61%, from 24.68 to 16.07% (p < 0.001 for trend), equating to a 34.63% relative decline. Similarly, the prevalence of frailty among older adults in the normal sleep duration group decreased by 9.42%, from 24.26% in 2011 to 14.84% in 2020 (p < 0.001 for trend; Fig. 3). The long sleep duration group experienced a decrease of 13.88%, from 28.24% to 14.36% (p < 0.001 for trend).

Estimated frailty trajectories

A trajectory model consisting of three distinct trajectories showed the best fit using BIC, OCC, AvePP and interpretability in trajectory analysis (Supplementary Table S2). The plot of frailty trajectories was depicted in Fig. 4. According to the severity of frailty and its change patterns over time, we named these three groups the low-level stable group (n = 534, 54.83%), the medium-level increase group (n = 376, 38.60%), and the high-level increase group (n = 64, 6.57%). In the low-level stable group, the FI was generally low and stable, showing only a slight increase. In the medium-level increase group, participants exhibited a moderate FI level with a moderate increase. The high-level increase group presented with a high FI level and a rapid upward trend.

Independent association of sleep duration and PA with frailty trajectories

Tables 3 and 4 present the results from the multinomial logistic regression models. Using the low-level stable group as the reference category, VPA and adequate sleep demonstrated significant protective effects against frailty deterioration. These associations remained highly robust even after progressively adjusting for multiple confounding variables. In Model 3, older adults engaging in MPA and VPA exhibited a respective reduction of 42.4% (OR = 0.576, 95% CI: 0.344–0.964) and 40.5% (OR = 0.595, 95% CI: 0.366–0.967) in the odds of entering the medium-level increase group, compared to those maintaining LPA. Moreover, maintaining MPA and VPA respectively decreased the odds of entering the high-level increase group by 62.6% (OR = 0.374, 95% CI: 0.163–0.859) and 74.0% (OR = 0.260, 95% CI: 0.116–0.581). Similarly, compared to short sleep duration, older adults with normal and long sleep durations experienced a respective decrease of 57.0% (OR = 0.430, 95% CI: 0.293–0.630) and 53.4% (OR = 0.466, 95% CI: 0.287–0.755) in the odds of entering the medium-level increase group. Furthermore, maintaining normal and long sleep durations respectively lowered the odds of falling into the high-level increase group by 79.1% (OR = 0.209, 95% CI: 0.107–0.409) and 71.1% (OR = 0.289, 95% CI: 0.123–0.678).

Discussion

This study revealed that between 2011 and 2020, the prevalence of frailty among older Chinese adults decreased by 18.68% within the LPA group, 8.61% in the VPA group, 9.42% in the normal sleep duration group, and 13.88% in the long sleep duration group. Overall, the VPA (20.42%) and normal sleep duration (19.85%) groups consistently demonstrated the lowest frailty prevalence. Beyond these epidemiological trends, our longitudinal analysis identified three distinct frailty trajectories: low-level stable, medium-level increase, and high-level increase. Our findings indicate that MVPA, normal sleep duration, and long sleep duration are independently associated with a reduced risk of transitioning toward unfavorable frailty trajectories. Crucially, our joint analysis demonstrated that irrespective of sleep duration, individuals engaging in VPA consistently exhibited the lowest odds of entering an adverse frailty trajectory. Distinct from prior research that predominantly focused on static frailty status or incidence, our analysis captures the dynamic progression of frailty, thereby providing a longitudinal perspective on how modifiable lifestyles influence the development of frailty over time.

In the present study, we identified three distinct frailty trajectories among older adults, aligning with previous longitudinal research utilizing the CHARLS cohort [37, 38]. While our findings corroborate the significant association between sleep duration and frailty progression, the specific impacts of varying sleep durations remain heavily debated. For instance, a prior CHARLS-based analysis demonstrated that consistently short or increasing sleep durations were linked to an elevated risk of entering the medium-level increase trajectory [39]. Contrary to those findings, our study indicates that both normal and long sleep durations are associated with a reduced risk of transitioning into the medium-level increase group. Several factors may elucidate this discrepancy. First, we adopted short sleep duration as the reference group, whereas most previous studies used normal sleep duration as the reference, which may reverse the interpretation of the association between long sleep and frailty. Second, we defined total sleep duration as nighttime sleep plus daytime napping, while most prior studies only considered nighttime sleep duration. Napping is a crucial predictor of frailty, it may mitigate frailty risk by alleviating fatigue and restoring cognitive function [40]. Third, participants with long sleep duration in our sample generally maintained adequate PA levels, which may offset the potential adverse effects of prolonged sleep. Finally, differences in study population characteristics and frailty assessment indicators may also contribute to inconsistencies across research findings. Some studies have also focused on the association between sleep quality and frailty. Poor sleep quality is closely related to an increased risk of frailty [41]. Furthermore, a study based on the second wave data of the Global Aging and Adult Health Study (WHO-SAGE) in India indicates that compared to the recommended sleep duration (7–8 h), sleep duration ≥ 9 h was associated with a lower risk of pre-frailty and frailty, whereas short sleep duration corresponded to a relatively higher risk [42]. The results were similar to those in this study. From a biological perspective, evidence indicates that insufficient sleep can promote the release of inflammatory factors such as IL-6, which may reduce muscle mass and muscle strength and thereby exert direct or indirect effects on frailty development [43]. Longer sleep duration may be correlated with PA [44]. Individuals with long sleep duration were less physically active during the day [45], and may also maintain a state of low energy expenditure [46]. Excessively long sleep may lead to substantial declines in skeletal muscle energy metabolism and mitochondrial function, including reduced mitochondrial respiratory capacity and maximal mitochondrial volume, ultimately contributing to the development of physical weakness [47, 48]. The concurrent maintenance of PA among long sleepers likely offsets these detrimental mitochondrial effects, preserving muscle function. While a majority of existing studies have investigated the impact of sleep duration or sleep quality trajectories on frailty, few studies have analyzed frailty as a dynamic and modifiable process [49, 50]. Our study substantiates the impact of sleep duration on frailty, thereby extending current knowledge through a specific focus on frailty developmental trajectories. We emphasize the beneficial effects of sleep duration across the life course, confirming its pivotal role in mitigating the progression of frailty.

Beyond the effects of sleep duration, our findings corroborated earlier research demonstrating a robust association between MVPA and a reduced risk of frailty [9, 51, 52]. Crucially, we extend this existing knowledge by elucidating the longitudinal impact of PA on frailty trajectories, thereby addressing the frequent neglect of its dynamic evolution in prior research. Our results revealed that engaging in MVPA effectively protected older adults from transitioning into the medium-level and high-level increase frailty trajectories. In addition, prior literature highlights a non-linear dose-response relationship between PA and frailty. The risk of frailty remains elevated at lower PA levels, declines precipitously upon reaching a critical threshold, and eventually plateaus at higher volumes, indicating a diminishing marginal benefit as PA continues to increase [12]. These findings provide insights into the effects of specific PA doses on frailty occurrence. Mechanistically, these profound benefits can be explained through a multisystem physiological lens. Frailty is not merely a single-organ lesion, but rather the comprehensive manifestation of synchronous physiological reserve depletion across the nervous, endocrine, immune, and musculoskeletal systems, ultimately compromising whole-body homeostasis. PA acts as a potent multisystem regulator. Regular exercise enhances muscle strength and mitochondrial biogenesis, and optimizes autonomic balance via vagal tone modulation. Importantly, PA profoundly attenuates systemic inflammation, thereby simultaneously facilitating brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) signaling and restoring the function of the neuroendocrine-immune network [53]. Thus, individualized exercise interventions are among the most promising non-pharmacological strategies for reversing or delaying frailty progression, providing a robust theoretical foundation for targeted exercise intervention trials.

Compared to PA interventions, directly modifying sleep duration to reduce frailty risk may not always be a clinically feasible goal for older adults. Therefore, it is necessary to explore alternative strategies to directly or indirectly attenuate the detrimental effects of suboptimal sleep on frailty progression. The Spanish Senior-ENRICA cohort study demonstrated that meeting the World Health Organization’s (WHO) recommended MVPA significantly offset or even eliminated the increased mortality risk associated with poor sleep patterns in older adults. Therefore, maintaining regular and adequate MVPA may be a highly effective compensatory strategy to counteract the health risks of insufficient sleep [54]. Similarly, Martins et al. found that replacing 60 min of sedentary or sleep time with 60 min of MPA per day was significantly associated with a 48% reduction in frailty prevalence among older adults [55]. Furthermore, numerous studies have shown that increasing PA can improve multiple aspects of sleep and exert beneficial effects on physical health [56–58].

Furthermore, our study explored the joint effects of sleep duration and PA on frailty trajectories. The results indicate that, regardless of sleep duration, individuals in the VPA group generally exhibited the lowest odds of entering the high-level increase frailty trajectory. Nevertheless, LPA with normal sleep, and MPA with normal or long sleep also conferred a significantly reduced likelihood of entering this high-risk trajectory. Notably, these findings suggest that higher levels of PA can partially compensate for the elevated risk of frailty induced by inadequate sleep. Indeed, previous studies have shown that the protective joint effects of sleep duration and PA on frailty [59, 60]. A large-scale analysis of National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data demonstrated that combining MVPA with normal sleep reduced frailty risk by 39% to 61% [61]. These protective effects are likely achieved through a combination of several interacting pathways. A prospective CHARLS study indicated that maintaining adequate PA alongside healthy nighttime sleep is independently associated with a lower risk of functional disability [62]. Similarly, increasing PA and maintaining regular sleep patterns significantly attenuate bone mineral density loss and preserve skeletal health [63]. When aiming to prevent early bone mass decline or osteoporosis, comprehensive combined interventions are vastly more effective than evaluating single lifestyle behaviors in isolation. PA also optimizes sleep duration, which in turn enhances cognitive function, relieves depressive symptoms, and confers long-term mental health benefits [64]. Moreover, a lifestyle characterized by appropriate PA levels and healthy sleep duration is closely linked to a lower incidence of diabetes [65], cardiovascular disease [66], hypertension [67] and other chronic diseases [68]. Collectively, these favorable health outcomes contribute to reduced frailty risk among older adults. Mechanistically, the synergistic protective effects likely manifest through several biological pathways. On the one hand, insufficient sleep elevates circulating levels of pro-inflammatory markers, including IL-6, TNF-α, and C-reactive protein (CRP) [69, 70]. Elevated IL-6 may promote muscle proteolysis by activating the JAK/STAT signaling pathway or interfering with anabolic processes, thereby accelerating muscle loss and increasing sarcopenia risk [71]. The increase in inflammatory factor levels will have a direct or indirect impact on frailty. On the other hand, PA exerts systemic anti-inflammatory effects and promotes the phenotypic shift of immune cells from a pro-inflammatory to an anti-inflammatory profile [72]. In addition, abnormal sleep may disrupt cerebral metabolism, leading to impaired glucose homeostasis and insufficient brain energy supply. This can compromise judgment and executive function, ultimately impairing overall cognitive performance [73]. In contrast, PA exerts beneficial regulatory effects on brain metabolism. By enhancing cerebral blood perfusion, PA facilitates nutrient and oxygen delivery, maintains normal cerebral energy metabolism, and protects against cognitive decline [74]. These comprehensive physiological effects are closely implicated in the onset and progression of frailty.

These complex synergistic associations between sleep duration and PA likely reflect shared mechanistic pathways that mitigate frailty progression, underscoring the critical need to evaluate these interconnected lifestyle behaviors simultaneously. By capturing the dynamic evolution of frailty over time, our findings deliver robust empirical evidence that adequate PA levels and healthy sleep can profoundly reduce the risk of older adults progressing toward adverse frailty trajectories.

Limitations and prospects

This study has several limitations. First, PA levels were assessed using the IPAQ, which relies on retrospective self-report and may introduce recall bias. In addition, PA time was recorded via categorical intervals rather than continuous measurement, and the homogenization of these categorical data further reduces the precision of PA duration estimation. And sleep duration was measured by retrospective self-report, which is prone to recall bias and cannot fully reflect actual sleep conditions. Second, although we adjusted for known confounders, unmeasured or residual confounding may still exist, including sleep disorders, regular hypnotic medication use, and other medications potentially associated with frailty. Third, this study did not include sleep quality and sedentary behavior, which are critical parameters intimately linked to frailty. The original database did not collect specific information on sleep quality and sedentary behavior. Fourth, although our trajectory analysis captures the longitudinal progression of frailty severity, the 2-year intermittent follow-up intervals of the CHARLS database limit the precise identification of first-onset frailty incidence. Fifth, due to the inherent data constraints of the CHARLS database, we were unable to evaluate frailty across specific domains. While the FI effectively captures overall vulnerability, the lack of detailed variables on advanced cognitive domains and social support networks prevented us from examining physical, psychological, and social frailty separately.

Therefore, future research investigating the relationships between sleep duration, PA and frailty should incorporate both subjective and objective measures to assess sleep duration and PA. Objective assessment tools such as accelerometry are recommended to to enhance data precision and identify the optimal dose-response combinations. Subsequent research must also account for the nuanced influences of sedentary time, sleep quality, sleep disorders, and medication use. In addition, future research utilizing specialized cohorts or continuous monitoring is warranted to employ Cox model survival analyses, precisely evaluating frailty incidence and its time-to-event dynamics to establish definitive etiological evidence. Moreover, exploring the joint effects of PA and sleep duration on physical, psychological, and social frailty also holds profound clinical significance.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this longitudinal study identified three dynamic frailty trajectories, demonstrating that MVPA and adequate sleep act as critical independent protectors against adverse frailty trajectories. Crucially, our joint analyses revealed a powerful synergistic effect: combining MVPA with normal sleep maximizes protective benefits, while higher levels of PA can partially offset the frailty risks associated with short sleep. Consequently, integrated interventions addressing both behaviors are essential for mitigating severe frailty progression.
