Determinants of healthy birth intervals among working women in Western Province, Sri Lanka
Authors: D. S. Kodithuwakku, Manjula Gunarathna
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Healthy birth intervals are crucial for maternal and child health, yet their determinants among working women remain understudied in South Asian contexts. This study examined factors predicting healthy birth spacing (33–72 months between first and second births) among working women in Sri Lanka, with attention to occupational context. Using an exploratory-sequential mixed-methods design, we conducted 15 in-depth interviews with working women to inform questionnaire development, followed by a cross-sectional survey of 351 working women from four occupation sectors across 36 workplaces in Western Province. Binary logistic regression with cluster adjustment using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) identified predictors while accounting for workplace clustering. Overall, 61.3% of participants maintained healthy birth intervals. After cluster adjustment, significant predictors included younger age at first birth (ages 20–24: OR = 74.61, p < 0.001), current marriage (OR = 5.46, p = 0.038), and conscious career prioritization (OR = 4.50, p < 0.001). The model demonstrated excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.854) and good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow p = 0.386). Women’s occupation sector showed a bivariate association (p = 0.034) but not an independent association in multivariable models. Qualitative findings revealed four key themes: temporal negotiation, family as both resources and pressure, workplace as context rather than determinant, and healthcare as baseline enabler, which contextualize these quantitative patterns. Healthy birth spacing among working women is strongly associated with age at first birth, marital stability, and strategic career-reproductive planning. These findings suggest that individual-level resources and planning capacity may be more influential than sectoral differences in contexts with universal healthcare access, supporting policy focus on empowering women’s reproductive decision-making alongside workplace protections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12905-026-04401-6.

Introduction

Birth intervals, the time between consecutive live births, represent a critical yet modifiable determinant of maternal and child health outcomes. The World Health Organization [1] recommends a minimum interval of 24 months after a live birth before attempting the subsequent pregnancy, equivalent to a 33-month birth-to-birth interval. Mayo Clinic (2022) [2] further specifies an optimal range of 18–60 months between pregnancies (or 27–69 months birth-to-birth) to minimize adverse outcomes. Intervals shorter than recommended increase risks of maternal anemia, uterine rupture, preterm birth, and low birth weight [3], while excessively long intervals (> 5 years) may also carry elevated risks, including preeclampsia and macrosomia [4]. For this study, we define healthy birth intervals as 33–72 months between births, representing the WHO-recommended minimum plus an upper bound based on evidence of increased obstetric risks beyond 60 months.

For working women, maintaining healthy birth intervals assumes particular importance due to the dual demands of professional and familial responsibilities. Adequate spacing may enable physical and mental recovery from childbirth, facilitate return to work and career continuity, allow time for educational advancement, and support better work-family balance [5, 6]. Yet despite increasing female labor force participation globally, and in Sri Lanka, where it reached 34.8% in 2022 [7], research examining birth spacing determinants among working women remains limited.

International research has identified several consistent determinants of birth intervals across diverse settings. Sociodemographic factors, including maternal education, age at first birth, and household wealth, show robust associations with spacing outcomes in studies from sub-Saharan Africa [8, 9], South Asia [10], and Latin America [11]. Women with higher education and those initiating childbearing at younger ages tend to achieve longer intervals, likely due to greater reproductive knowledge, family planning access, and longer reproductive time horizons, respectively.

Access to family planning services emerges as a critical determinant across contexts. Studies in Ethiopia [12], Bangladesh [13], and India [10] demonstrate that women with access to modern contraceptive methods achieve substantially longer and more consistent birth spacing. Healthcare system characteristics, including quality of postnatal counseling and community health worker presence, also predict spacing outcomes [14].

Family and social factors influence spacing through both support and pressure mechanisms. In patrilocal South Asian societies, co-residence with in-laws and their reproductive preferences significantly shape the timing of subsequent births [15]. Conversely, spousal communication about family planning and shared reproductive decision-making is associated with optimal spacing [16].

For working women specifically, limited research suggests occupation-related factors may matter. Studies from urban Bangladesh [17] and Indonesia [18] found that women in formal sector employment with job security and maternity benefits achieved longer intervals than those in informal or agricultural work. However, this literature focuses predominantly on whether women work, not on how the type of work shapes spacing opportunities.

Theoretical framework

Understanding birth spacing among working women requires examining both early life course factors (such as age at first birth, itself shaped by educational and career timing) and contemporary socio-environmental influences (including education, marital context, workplace support, and family dynamics). While age-related biological constraints may limit reproductive timelines, socio-environmental factors represent modifiable targets for intervention.

This study integrates three theoretical perspectives. Life course theory [19] emphasizes that the timing of life transitions, including age at first birth, shapes subsequent trajectories, with earlier transitions potentially providing longer time horizons for optimal spacing. However, age at first birth is not purely biological; it reflects prior educational opportunities, cultural marriage norms, and career entry timing, themselves products of socio-environmental contexts.

Work-family interface theory [20] suggests that working women must balance competing role demands, with workplace support and conscious planning potentially facilitating this balance. This framework predicts that women who intentionally integrate career and reproductive planning will achieve better spacing outcomes, and that supportive workplace policies (maternity leave, flexibility) will enable optimal intervals.

Bronfenbrenner’s (1979) [21] ecological systems theory posits that individual outcomes emerge from nested systems operating at multiple levels: microsystem (family support, spousal relationships), exosystem (workplace policies, healthcare access), and macrosystem (cultural norms, labor regulations). This framework suggests that individual characteristics interact with contextual factors, and that universal healthcare or progressive labor policies might reduce occupation-sector disparities by equalizing access to enabling resources.

Research gaps

The literature reveals three critical gaps. First, existing research overwhelmingly focuses on short or long birth intervals as problems requiring prevention, with minimal attention to determinants of optimal or healthy intervals as a positive outcome warranting promotion. Second, despite increasing female labor force participation, birth spacing research rarely focuses on working women as a distinct population with unique determinants. Most critically, while studies identify participants as “working women,” they rarely analyze how the type of work (professional vs. industrial vs. informal) influences spacing outcomes through differential access to maternity leave, job security, workplace flexibility, and income stability.

Third, birth spacing research in South Asia, and Sri Lanka in particular, remains limited compared to extensive African literature. No published Sri Lankan studies examine birth spacing determinants among working women or compare outcomes across occupation sectors. Additionally, few studies systematically compare the relative importance of early life course factors versus contemporary socio-environmental factors, limiting understanding of which domains offer the most significant potential for intervention.

The Sri Lankan context

Sri Lanka presents a unique setting for examining these questions. The country has achieved impressive maternal and child health outcomes despite its middle-income status, with maternal mortality at 29 per 100,000 live births [22]. Universal healthcare provides free antenatal, delivery, and postnatal services, including family planning counseling, potentially reducing socioeconomic disparities in access.

Female labor force participation stands at 34.8% [7], with significant concentration in Western Province, which houses 28.7% of the national population [23] and exhibits diverse employment sectors, including service (banking, retail), industrial (garment manufacturing), informal (beauty salons, street vendors), and professional (information technology, corporate). Sri Lankan labor law mandates 84 days (12 weeks) of paid maternity leave for women in formal employment, though enforcement varies and informal sector workers often lack coverage.

Sri Lankan working women navigate distinctive cultural expectations shaped by South Asian family systems, including strong familial involvement in reproductive decisions, cultural preference for closely-spaced children to complete childbearing, and multi-generational household structures that provide childcare support but may also constrain reproductive autonomy. Understanding how working women successfully navigate healthy birth spacing in this context offers insights for similar settings.

Methods

Study design and setting

This exploratory-sequential mixed-methods cross-sectional study was conducted in Western Province, Sri Lanka, between July and September 2025. Western Province was selected for its highest female labor force participation rate (41.2%) nationally and representation of 28.7% of the national population, encompassing urban, suburban, and rural areas with diverse employment sectors. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Review Committee, University of Kelaniya (REF No: ERC/SS/2025/007). All participants provided written informed consent in their preferred language (Sinhala or English).

Mixed-methods design rationale

This study employed an exploratory-sequential mixed-methods design grounded in a pragmatic paradigm, which prioritizes practical problem-solving and recognizes that different methods can be combined to address research questions comprehensively [28]. The qualitative phase served dual purposes: (1) instrument development, generating contextually relevant items for the quantitative survey based on participants’ lived experiences; and (2) contextual understanding, providing rich narratives to interpret quantitative patterns, particularly unexpected null findings (e.g., non-significance of family support and workplace support variables). This sequential design aligns with Creswell and Plano Clark’s (2018) framework for exploratory-sequential mixed methods, where qualitative findings directly inform quantitative instrument construction and subsequent interpretation [29].

Formative qualitative phase

Between July 5–20, 2025, fifteen in-depth interviews were conducted with working women to inform questionnaire development. Participants were purposively selected to represent diversity in age (range: 22–45 years), employment sectors, parity (2–4 children), and socioeconomic backgrounds.

Purposive sampling for the qualitative phase was explicitly stratified by occupation sector to ensure representation across the four employment contexts examined quantitatively. Of the 15 interviews, 4 participants were from service sector workplaces (banking/finance), 4 from the industrial sector (garment manufacturing), 3 from the informal sector (beauty salons, street vendors), and 4 from the professional sector (IT companies). This distribution enabled cross-sector comparison of themes while acknowledging the smaller informal sector representation, consistent with population proportions in Western Province.

Sample size for the qualitative phase was determined by information power rather than strict saturation, following Malterud et al. (2016) guidelines [24]. Five dimensions contributed to adequate information power: (1) focused study aim (identifying factors for questionnaire development rather than generating comprehensive theory); (2) sample specificity (all participants were working mothers with multiple children, directly experiencing the phenomenon); (3) established theoretical framework (Life Course, Work-Family Interface, Ecological Systems) providing analytical scaffolding; (4) quality dialogue (mean interview duration of 58 min with substantial depth); and (5) cross-case analysis strategy across predefined diversity dimensions. Thematic sufficiency was assessed iteratively; no new themes emerged after interview 12, and subsequent interviews primarily confirmed and elaborated existing patterns. The 15-interview sample exceeded this threshold while maintaining analytical manageability.

Semi-structured interviews lasting 45–75 min (mean = 58 min) were conducted by trained female interviewers in participants’ preferred language in private settings. Interviews were conducted in private rooms arranged at participants’ workplaces (n = 8), nearby cafes with private seating (n = 4), or community centers (n = 3), all locations chosen by participants to ensure comfort and privacy. Audio recordings were made using encrypted digital recorders. Recordings were transcribed verbatim by a professional transcription service with confidentiality agreements, then verified against recordings by research assistants. Transcripts were imported into NVivo 14 (QSR International) for systematic coding and theme development. The interview guide developed for this study has been uploaded under the supplementary material section on the submission system (see Supplementary File 1).

Interview guide development

The semi-structured interview guide was systematically developed to operationalize our integrated theoretical framework. Life Course Theory informed questions about reproductive timing, age at first birth decisions, and how early transitions shaped subsequent spacing (Sects. 1–2 of the guide). Work-Family Interface Theory guided questions about career planning, work-life integration strategies, and how women navigated competing role demands (Sects. 3–4). Ecological Systems Theory structured questions across levels: microsystem influences (spousal relationships, family support—Sects. 6–7), exosystem factors (workplace policies, healthcare access—Sects. 5, 8), and macrosystem contexts (cultural norms, social expectations—Sect. 9). The guide was piloted with three working women (not included in the final sample) to assess question clarity, cultural appropriateness, and flow. Pilot feedback led to refinements in question wording (avoiding clinical terminology), reordering sections to build rapport before sensitive topics, and adding probes for occupation-specific experiences.

Qualitative data analysis

Qualitative data were analyzed using a coding reliability approach to thematic analysis, following guidelines for applied health research [25, 26]. This approach, distinct from Braun and Clarke’s (2006) reflexive thematic analysis, emphasizes structured codebook development and inter-coder agreement as markers of analytical rigor, appropriate for our instrument-development purpose, where reliable identification of themes across coders supported valid questionnaire construction [27].

The analysis proceeded through five phases: (1) Familiarization: Both coders independently read all transcripts twice, noting initial observations. (2) Codebook development: The first author developed an initial codebook based on theoretical frameworks and emergent patterns; the second author reviewed and refined codes. (3) Independent coding: Both coders independently applied the codebook to all transcripts using NVivo 14 software. (4) Reliability assessment: Inter-coder agreement was assessed on 20% of transcripts (3 interviews), yielding Cohen’s kappa of κ = 0.78, indicating substantial agreement. Discrepancies were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. (5) Theme development: Codes were grouped into broader themes through collaborative discussion, with themes refined iteratively against the data. These themes informed questionnaire development for the quantitative phase.

The interview team comprised two female research assistants with Master’s degrees in public health, both Sri Lankan nationals familiar with local cultural contexts. Their demographic similarity to participants (working women, similar age range) facilitated rapport while potentially introducing shared assumptions. The principal investigators (both senior academics) maintained analytical distance through independent review of themes. Power dynamics in workplace-based recruitment were addressed through explicit confidentiality assurances, private interview settings away from supervisors, and interviewer training on non-judgmental approaches. Interviewers debriefed after each interview, documenting emotional responses and potential biases that might influence interpretation.

Mixed-methods integration strategy

Integration occurred at two points following Fetters et al.’s (2013) framework [29]: Qualitative themes directly informed questionnaire construction. The ‘Temporal Negotiation’ theme generated the career prioritization item; ‘Family as Resource and Pressure’ informed family support and familial pressure items; ‘Workplace as Context’ shaped occupation sector classification and institutional support items; ‘Healthcare as Enabler’ confirmed inclusion of healthcare accessibility. Following quantitative analysis, qualitative findings were systematically compared against statistical patterns using a joint display matrix examining convergence, complementarity, and divergence. This integration directly informs the Discussion section’s interpretation of unexpected null findings.

Sample size was calculated using Yamane’s (1967) [30] formula: n = N / [1 + N(e²)], where N = estimated population of working women with ≥ 2 children in Western Province (8,245 based on labor force survey extrapolation), e = margin of error (0.05), and confidence level = 95%. Ultimately, 370 women across 36 workplaces were invited, and 351 provided complete data (response rate: 94.9%). No workplace refused participation at the organizational level, and individual-level refusals were minimal (n = 19, 5.1%), primarily due to time constraints.

Occupation sector classification

Women’s occupation sector was classified based on the workplace category from which they were recruited (service, industrial, informal, or professional). We acknowledge this represents a workplace-level proxy rather than an individual job role, as women recruited from an IT company might hold professional programming positions or administrative support roles. However, the workplace sector captures key environmental characteristics, including typical job security, benefit packages, workplace culture, and scheduling flexibility, that may influence birth spacing opportunities. Of the 351 participants, 84 (23.9%) were recruited from service sector workplaces, 103 (29.3%) from industrial, 39 (11.1%) from informal, and 125 (35.6%) from professional sectors.

Ethical safeguards

Given the sensitive nature of reproductive health topics and workplace-based recruitment, several ethical safeguards were implemented. First, all interviews were conducted in private rooms (not workspaces) to ensure confidentiality and reduce concerns about employer oversight. Second, participants received explicit assurance that individual responses would not be shared with employers and that data would be reported only in aggregate form. Third, participants were informed of their right to decline any questions or withdraw without consequence. Fourth, interviewers were trained in trauma-informed approaches to sensitively handle potential disclosures of reproductive coercion or intimate partner violence, with referral protocols to support services established. Fifth, all data were de-identified immediately after collection, with master lists linking codes to identities stored separately under password protection.

Qualitative-specific ethical considerations

Given the sensitive nature of reproductive health discussions, additional safeguards were implemented for the qualitative phase. Interviewers were trained in trauma-informed interviewing techniques. If participants showed signs of distress (tearfulness, emotional difficulty), interviewers paused, offered breaks, and explicitly stated participants could skip questions or terminate without consequence. To protect voluntary participation in employment contexts, (1) recruitment occurred through general announcements without supervisor involvement in identifying participants; (2) interviews were scheduled during breaks or after work hours at participant preference; (3) interview locations were private spaces away from supervisory oversight; (4) participants were explicitly informed that their employer had no knowledge of individual participation and no access to responses. Audio recordings were transferred to encrypted storage immediately after interviews and deleted from recording devices. Transcripts used pseudonyms, and any identifying workplace details were removed during transcription.

Data collection

A structured questionnaire was developed, integrating variables from the literature review, qualitative phase themes, and validated scales where available. The questionnaire developed for this study has been uploaded under the supplementary material section on the submission system (see Supplementary File 2). Content validity was assessed by an expert panel (n = 5, including reproductive health researchers, occupational health specialists, and clinicians); Content Validity Index was 0.92. Pilot testing with 30 working women from workplaces not included in the final sample assessed clarity, cultural appropriateness, and questionnaire length. Test-retest reliability (n = 20, 2-week interval) demonstrated strong agreement for categorical items (Cohen’s kappa: 0.82–0.91) and continuous items (intraclass correlation: 0.85–0.93).

Data collection occurred between August 10 and September 15, 2025. Six trained female research assistants administered questionnaires face-to-face in participants’ preferred language at or near their workplaces in private settings. Average administration time was 35–45 min. Participants received no monetary compensation but were provided with printed family planning educational materials.

Variables and measurement

Dependent variable

Healthy Birth Interval was defined as a birth-to-birth interval of 33–72 months between first and second live births. The lower bound (33 months) represents the WHO recommendation of 24 months between birth and next conception, plus 9 months of gestation [1]. The upper bound (72 months, or 6 years) reflects evidence that intervals beyond 5–6 years carry increased obstetric risks, including preeclampsia and pregnancy complications [4, 31], balanced with recognition that some women may intentionally space for career or educational reasons. This was coded as 1 (healthy) if the interval was 33–72 months, or 0 (non-healthy) if < 33 months (short) or > 72 months (long). Birth dates were calculated from self-reported month and year of each child’s birth, verified against available birth certificates or health records when provided.

Independent variables

We conceptualized two categories of predictors for analytical purposes, while recognizing that the distinction is analytical rather than ontological:

Early Life Course Factors: Age at first birth was categorized based on observed data distribution: 15–19, 20–24, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, or 45–49 years. The 25–29 age category, while included in the original data collection instrument, yielded zero observations upon data verification. This finding was unexpected and requires explanation. Careful review of birth dates confirmed no data entry errors. The absence of first births in the 25–29 age range in this sample appears to reflect the specific workplace-based sampling frame, which may have underrepresented women who followed normative educational-marriage-childbearing sequences (completing university at 22–23, marrying at 24–26, first birth at 26–28) if those women had not yet had a second child or were not yet returned to formal employment by the time of sampling. This represents a limitation in generalizability and is discussed fully in the limitations section. For logistic regression, 45–49 was used as the reference category in the main analyses, with sensitivity analyses using alternative reference categories presented.

Contemporary Socio-Environmental Factors: These comprised influences external to the individual that shape opportunities and constraints for birth spacing: maternal education, the highest degree attained, categorized as ≤Advanced Level (A-Level, Sri Lankan equivalent of high school completion), Bachelor’s degree, Master’s degree, or Doctoral degree. Advanced Level or below served as the reference category, current marital status: never married (reference category), currently married (married and cohabitating), separated or divorced, widowed, or married but never lived together (married but non-cohabitating, relevant in the Sri Lankan context where marriages may be arranged but couples delay cohabitation for work or other reasons), women’s occupation sector: service, industrial (reference), informal, or professional, based on workplace classification as described above, educational/career prioritization: single dichotomous item: “Did educational or career advancement considerations influence the timing of your second child?” (Yes/No). This captures conscious integration of professional and reproductive planning, family support: “Do you have family members (parents, in-laws, siblings) who help with childcare or household responsibilities?” (Yes/No). This assesses availability of instrumental social support, institutional/workplace support: “Does your workplace provide maternity leave, flexible scheduling, or other support for working mothers?” (Yes/No). This captures employer-provided accommodations, healthcare accessibility: “Do you have easy access to family planning services and reproductive healthcare?” (Yes/No). This assesses the perceived accessibility of health services relevant to birth spacing.

Missing data

Missing data were minimal, affecting 6 cases (1.7% of the sample). Little’s Missing Completely At Random (MCAR) test indicated data were MCAR (χ²=15.23, df = 18, p = 0.644), supporting the validity of complete case analysis. The 6 cases with missing data were excluded, yielding the final analytic sample of 351.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using Python 3.11 (pandas, statsmodels, scikit-learn libraries) with statistical significance set at α = 0.05 (two-tailed). Descriptive statistics characterized the sample overall and by birth interval status (healthy vs. non-healthy).

Accounting for cluster sampling

A critical methodological requirement was accounting for clustering of participants within 36 workplace clusters, as observations within the same workplace are likely correlated (e.g., shared workplace policies, peer influences, common socioeconomic characteristics). Failure to account for clustering violates the independence assumption of standard regression, producing artificially narrow confidence intervals and inflated Type I error rates. We employed Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) for all multivariable analyses. GEE extends logistic regression to account for within-cluster correlation by using robust (sandwich) variance estimators. We specified a binary logit link function, an exchangeable (compound symmetry) correlation structure, assuming equal correlation between any two observations within the same cluster, and robust standard errors.

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was calculated as the exchangeable correlation parameter, quantifying the proportion of variance in outcomes attributable to workplace clustering. For bivariate analyses in Table 1, we used GEE-based Wald tests rather than standard chi-square tests to ensure cluster adjustment throughout.

Model building and comparison

To address Research Question 3 regarding the relative importance of early life course versus socio-environmental factors, we fitted and compared three logistic regression models using GEE; model 1 (early life course): age at first birth only (6 categories), model 2 (socio-environmental): maternal education (3 dummy variables), marital status (4 dummy variables), occupation sector (3 dummy variables), career prioritization, family support, institutional support, healthcare accessibility (11 predictors total) and model 3 (combined): all predictors from models 1 and 2 (17 predictors total).

Models were compared using Nagelkerke pseudo-R² (variance explained), Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUC, discrimination), Akaike Information Criterion (AIC), and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), with lower values indicating better fit.

Model diagnostics

Multiple diagnostic checks assessed model validity; Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) were calculated for all predictors, with VIF > 10 suggesting problematic multicollinearity requiring remediation. Cook’s distance identified observations with disproportionate influence on regression coefficients, with Cook’s D > 4/n flagged for sensitivity analysis. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test assessed whether observed and expected probabilities matched across deciles of predicted risk, with p > 0.05 indicating adequate calibration. Calibration plots visualized this relationship. AUC quantified the ability to distinguish between healthy and non-healthy spacing, with values > 0.7 considered acceptable, > 0.8 excellent. Overall accuracy, sensitivity (correctly classified healthy intervals), and specificity (correctly classified non-healthy intervals) were calculated using the optimal threshold from ROC curve.

Sensitivity analyses

Several sensitivity analyses assessed robustness of findings; alternative reference category for age: using age 30–34 (more central category) instead of 45–49 to avoid extreme odds ratios, excluding doctoral degree holders: given small sample (n = 38) and potential influence, standard logistic regression with cluster-robust standard errors: confirming GEE results, excluding influential cases: assessing impact of high Cook’s d observations.

Power analysis

Post-hoc power analysis calculated achieved statistical power for the full multivariable model, accounting for design effect from clustering, to detect odds ratios of clinical interest (OR = 2.0) with α = 0.05.

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 351 working women participated, with current ages ranging from 22 to 48 years (mean = 33.5, SD = 6.2). Among the total sample, 215 participants (61.3%) maintained healthy birth intervals (33–72 months) while 136 (38.7%) experienced non-optimal spacing. Among the 136 with non-healthy intervals, 55 (40.4%) had short intervals (< 33 months), and 81 (59.6%) had long intervals (> 72 months).

The median age at first birth was 24 years (interquartile range: 20–30). Participants were highly educated: 52.1% held Bachelor’s degrees, 26.8% Master’s degrees, 10.8% doctoral degrees, and 10.3% had completed secondary education (≤ Advanced Level) as their highest qualification. Half (50.7%) were currently married and cohabiting, while 27.1% were separated or divorced, 10.3% widowed, 8.0% married but never cohabited, and 4.0% never married. The majority (63.8%) lived in urban areas. Over one-third (36.5%) reported that educational or career advancement considerations influenced the timing of their second child, indicating conscious career-reproductive planning.

Table 1 presents comprehensive descriptive statistics comparing participants who achieved healthy versus non-healthy birth intervals. Significant differences (using cluster-adjusted GEE Wald tests) were observed for age at first birth (p < 0.0001), maternal education (p < 0.0001), marital status (p < 0.0001), and career prioritization (p = 0.001). Among those with healthy intervals, 51.6% initiated childbearing at age 20–24, compared to only 12.5% of those with non-healthy intervals. Women with doctoral degrees were substantially underrepresented among those with healthy intervals (3.3% vs. 22.8% in the non-healthy group), while Bachelor’s holders were overrepresented (32.1% vs. 18.4%). Currently married women comprised 60.9% of the healthy interval group compared to 34.6% of the non-healthy group.

Qualitative phase findings

Thematic analysis of 15 in-depth interviews identified four major themes that informed questionnaire development and provided interpretive context for quantitative findings.

Theme 1: Temporal negotiation—balancing biological and career clocks

Participants described navigating competing temporal pressures between perceived fertility windows and career establishment needs. A 34-year-old IT professional articulated: “I knew I wanted two children, but I also knew I needed to establish myself first. There’s this constant calculation, if I wait too long, will it be harder? But if I don’t wait, will I lose the promotion I’ve worked for?’

Sub-themes included (a) age-related fertility concerns, (b) career milestone timing, and (c) strategic planning versus circumstantial timing. Women who reported conscious career-reproductive planning described deliberate timing decisions, while others described circumstances (unplanned pregnancies, relationship changes) that shaped their intervals.

Theme 2: Family as both resource and pressure

The extended family emerged as paradoxically supportive and constraining. Grandparental childcare enabled workforce participation, yet family members also exerted pressure regarding childbearing timing. A 29-year-old bank employee shared: “My mother-in-law watches my daughter every day, I couldn’t work without her. But she also keeps asking when we’ll have the next one. She thinks children should be close in age.”

Sub-themes included (a) instrumental support (childcare, household help), (b) normative pressure (timing expectations), and (c) navigating competing family expectations. Participants described varied strategies for managing family pressure while maintaining reproductive autonomy.

Theme 3: Workplace as context rather than determinant

Participants across sectors described varying workplace conditions but emphasized personal agency over structural constraints. A 31-year-old garment factory worker noted: “The factory gives us the legal leave, nothing extra. But I planned my second child for when I knew my mother could help. The factory rules don’t really change what I decide.”

This theme helps interpret the quantitative null finding for the occupation sector, suggesting that individual planning capacity may transcend workplace differences in contexts with universal healthcare.

Sub-themes included (a) maternity leave adequacy, (b) informal flexibility negotiations, and (c) prioritizing personal resources over workplace support. While participants acknowledged workplace constraints, they described adapting through personal and family resources rather than being determined by workplace policies.

Theme 4: Healthcare accessibility as baseline enabler

Near-universal positive experiences with Sri Lanka’s free maternal healthcare system were reported. A 36-year-old salon owner explained: “The government clinic is free, the midwife comes to check on you, and the hospital delivery costs nothing. I never worried about affording healthcare; that’s not a problem here.”

This theme contextualizes why healthcare accessibility, while qualitatively important, did not emerge as a significant quantitative predictor; its near-universal availability creates insufficient variation for statistical detection. Participants across all occupation sectors reported similar access to reproductive healthcare services.

Women’s occupation sector and birth interval outcomes

Birth interval proportion varied across occupation sectors: the industrial sector showed the highest proportion of healthy birth intervals (69.9%, n = 72/103), followed by the informal sector (64.1%, n = 25/39), the professional sector (58.4%, n = 73/125), and the service sector (53.6%, n = 45/84).

Using the standard chi-square test without cluster adjustment, this difference was not statistically significant (χ²=5.898, df = 3, p = 0.117). However, when accounting for workplace clustering using a GEE-based Wald test, the occupation sector association reached statistical significance (p = 0.034). This discrepancy illustrates the importance of cluster adjustment; the standard test underestimated the association by failing to account for within-workplace correlation.

However, as presented in the multivariable analysis in Table 2, the occupation sector did not remain a significant independent predictor after adjusting for individual-level characteristics (education, age, marital status, career planning). This suggests that the bivariate association reflects compositional differences in who works in each sector rather than independent effects of workplace characteristics per se.

Cluster adjustment and intraclass correlation

The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from the GEE model was 0.0011, indicating that 0.11% of the variance in birth interval outcomes is attributable to workplace clustering. While this represents a very low degree of within-cluster correlation, even low ICC values can meaningfully affect standard errors and statistical inference when cluster sizes are moderate to large (as in our study, with an average of 9.75 women per workplace cluster). Cluster-adjusted standard errors were 1.08 to 1.24 times larger than naive standard errors, affecting inference for several borderline findings.

Multivariable predictors of healthy birth intervals

Table 4 presents the complete multivariable GEE logistic regression model including all tested predictors. We present all predictors regardless of statistical significance to provide transparent reporting and allow assessment of effect sizes for non-significant associations.

The final combined model achieved excellent discrimination (AUC = 0.854, 95% CI [0.815, 0.893]), indicating a strong ability to distinguish between women who did and did not achieve healthy spacing. The model explained 46.5% of variance in birth interval status (Nagelkerke R²=0.465) with classification accuracy of 78.1%, sensitivity of 87.0% (correctly identifying healthy intervals), and specificity of 64.0% (correctly identifying non-healthy intervals). Notably, the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test indicated good model calibration (χ²=11.85, df = 8, p = 0.386), meaning observed and expected probabilities matched well across risk strata. This represents a substantial improvement from preliminary analyses that showed calibration failure, achieved through careful specification of the GEE correlation structure and functional forms.

Compared to women aged 45–49 at first birth (the oldest category in our sample), those who initiated childbearing at younger ages demonstrated substantially higher odds of maintaining healthy birth intervals. Women aged 20–24 at first birth had 74.61 times higher odds (OR = 74.61, 95% CI [6.76, 823.42], p < 0.001), representing the strongest predictor in the model. Those aged 15–19 similarly showed very high odds (OR = 34.92, 95% CI [2.60, 469.65], p = 0.007). Women aged 35–39 at first birth also had elevated odds relative to the 45–49 reference (OR = 10.11, 95% CI [1.04, 97.83], p = 0.046). Those aged 30–34 (OR = 5.93, 95% CI [0.48, 72.90], p = 0.164) and 40–44 (OR = 6.03, 95% CI [0.40, 91.07], p = 0.195) showed elevated point estimates but did not reach statistical significance.

The very wide confidence intervals reflect the small sample size in the reference category (n = 6 women aged 45–49 at first birth, with only 1 achieving a healthy interval). While this creates statistical uncertainty, the substantive finding is clear: younger age at first birth, particularly in the early twenties, is associated with substantially higher likelihood of achieving optimal birth spacing. This likely reflects a longer reproductive time horizon, reducing biological pressure to compress subsequent births to complete the desired family size before perceived age-related fertility decline.

Education showed a complex pattern. After adjusting for age at first birth and other covariates, women with Bachelor’s degrees (OR = 2.59, 95% CI [0.75, 8.95], p = 0.134) and Master’s degrees (OR = 2.57, 95% CI [0.71, 9.35], p = 0.151) showed non-significantly elevated odds compared to those with secondary education (≤ A-Level).

Notably, women with doctoral degrees showed lower odds (OR = 0.32, 95% CI [0.07, 1.44], p = 0.138), though this did not reach statistical significance after cluster adjustment. Only 18.4% of doctoral holders (7/38) achieved healthy intervals compared to 44.4% of those with ≤ A-Level education. This finding changed substantially from preliminary unclustered analyses, where it appeared significant (p = 0.018). The association likely reflects confounding by age: doctoral holders in this sample concentrated first births in older age ranges (30–39 years: 71% of doctoral holders vs. 38–40% for other education levels), and after adjusting for age at first birth, which has massive effects, the education effect is attenuated and no longer significant. This pattern suggests that educational attainment influences birth spacing primarily through its effect on the timing of the first birth rather than through independent mechanisms.

Currently married women demonstrated significantly higher odds of healthy birth intervals compared to never-married women (OR = 5.46, 95% CI [1.10, 27.18], p = 0.038). This association suggests that stable marital relationships may be associated with optimal spacing, potentially through socioeconomic security, shared reproductive decision-making, joint childcare, and social support systems associated with marriage.

Other marital categories showed elevated point estimates but did not reach significance: separated or divorced (OR = 2.96, 95% CI [0.54, 16.15], p = 0.210), widowed (OR = 0.93, 95% CI [0.20, 4.28], p = 0.930), and married but never cohabited (OR = 1.02, 95% CI [0.14, 7.59], p = 0.983). The wide confidence intervals reflect small samples in these categories and limit definitive conclusions.

Despite showing a significant bivariate association (p = 0.034 with cluster adjustment), the occupation sector did not show independent associations in the multivariable model. Compared to the service sector, women in the industrial sector workplaces had non-significantly elevated odds (OR = 1.90, 95% CI [0.88, 4.11], p = 0.102), as did those in informal sector (OR = 1.63, 95% CI [0.53, 4.95], p = 0.391) and the professional sector (OR = 1.20, 95% CI [0.63, 2.30], p = 0.575).

This pattern, a significant bivariate association but a non-significant multivariable association, suggests that apparent sectoral differences in birth spacing outcomes reflect compositional differences in who works in each sector (age, education, marital status) rather than independent effects of workplace characteristics. Women in industrial sectors may be younger or have different educational profiles, and once these individual characteristics are controlled, the workplace environment, per se, shows no additional independent association.

Women who reported that educational or career advancement considerations influenced the timing of their second child had substantially higher odds of healthy birth intervals (OR = 4.50, 95% CI [1.92, 10.55], p < 0.001), representing the second-strongest predictor after age. This demonstrates that conscious integration of professional and reproductive planning is strongly associated with achieving optimal birth spacing outcomes. This finding supports theoretical predictions from work-family interface theory and suggests that reproductive autonomy and intentional planning are key enablers.

In contrast to qualitative findings where family support emerged as a prominent theme, family support availability was not significantly associated with birth interval outcomes in multivariable analysis (OR = 0.51, 95% CI [0.20, 1.29], p = 0.155). Similarly, institutional/workplace support (OR = 1.44, 95% CI [0.71, 2.93], p = 0.317) and healthcare accessibility (OR = 1.91, 95% CI [0.87, 4.18], p = 0.106) were not significant independent predictors.

The divergence between qualitative and quantitative findings requires careful interpretation. These factors may function as enabling conditions that are necessary but not sufficient for optimal spacing. The near-universal availability of family support (87.2% of participants) and healthcare access (77.8%) in our sample creates insufficient statistical variation for detection as predictors, yet qualitative accounts reveal their practical importance in daily management of work-family demands. Alternatively, these factors may operate primarily by removing barriers rather than actively determining spacing, with individual characteristics (age, planning capacity) being the active drivers within a generally supportive context.

Healthcare accessibility approached but did not reach conventional significance (p = 0.106), with an OR of 1.91 suggesting a potentially meaningful association. This borderline finding may reflect Type II error, given the modest sample size, and future research with larger samples may detect this effect.

Model diagnostics

Multicollinearity assessment revealed that two predictors had VIF slightly above the conventional threshold of 10: currently married (VIF = 11.02) and age 20–24 (VIF = 10.66). These elevated values result from dummy coding of categorical variables with multiple categories and do not indicate problematic multicollinearity that would distort coefficient interpretation. All other predictors had VIF < 8.

Influential case analysis using Cook’s distance identified 25 observations (7.1% of sample) exceeding the conventional threshold of 4/n = 0.011. Sensitivity analysis excluding these cases was attempted but resulted in numerical instability due to quasi-complete separation in the age variable, reinforcing the appropriateness of our reference category selection and the challenge of extreme odds ratios with sparse cells.

Power analysis

Post-hoc power analysis for the full multivariable model, accounting for clustering (design effect = 1.2, ICC = 0.0011) and the number of predictors (k = 17), indicated achieved power of 84.5% to detect an odds ratio of 2.0 with α = 0.05. This exceeds conventional adequacy thresholds (80%) and suggests the study was adequately powered to detect moderate effect sizes. However, power for smaller effect sizes (OR < 1.5) or for specific subgroup comparisons (e.g., between occupation sectors) may have been insufficient, potentially explaining some null findings.

Figure 1 displays the observed proportion versus the expected probability of a healthy birth interval across deciles of predicted risk. Points cluster closely around the diagonal line of perfect calibration, and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test (χ²=11.85, df = 8, p = 0.386) confirms good calibration. This indicates the model’s predicted probabilities accurately reflect true probabilities in the population.

Discussion

This study of 351 working women across 36 workplaces in Western Province, Sri Lanka, found that 61.3% maintained healthy birth intervals (33–72 months between first and second births). After carefully accounting for workplace clustering using GEE, we identified younger age at first birth, current marriage, and conscious career-reproductive planning as significant independent predictors. Women’s occupation sector showed a significant bivariate association but not an independent association after adjusting for individual characteristics. Significantly, rigorous cluster adjustment changed several preliminary findings, most notably rendering the doctoral education association non-significant and revealing the occupation sector bivariate association.

Age at first birth, particularly childbearing initiation in the early twenties, emerged as the strongest predictor of healthy birth spacing (OR = 74.61 for age 20–24 vs. 45–49). This aligns with life course theory’s emphasis on timing of transitions [19] and replicates findings from Ethiopia [9], Bangladesh [13], and India [10] showing that early childbearing onset facilitates longer intervals. Age at first birth, while treated as an “early life course factor” for analytical purposes, is itself shaped by prior socio-environmental factors, including educational opportunities, marriage timing, cultural norms about appropriate ages for childbearing, and career entry. Women who initiate childbearing at age 20–24 typically complete education by their early twenties, marry soon after, and begin families without long career establishment periods. In contrast, women pursuing doctoral education or extended career development delay first births into their thirties, then face compressed timelines for subsequent childbearing if they desire multiple children.

Thus, the distinction between “early life course” and “socio-environmental” factors is analytical rather than ontological. Policies that shape educational pathways, career structures, and cultural norms about marriage and childbearing timing ultimately influence age at first birth, which then constrains or enables optimal spacing. This life course perspective has important policy implications: interventions earlier in women’s lives (supporting education while enabling earlier family formation for those who desire it, workplace policies for student mothers) may have downstream effects on birth spacing patterns.

The second-strongest predictor was conscious career-reproductive planning (OR = 4.50), with women who reported that career or educational considerations influenced their second birth timing showing dramatically higher odds of healthy spacing. This finding provides empirical support for work-family interface theory [20]. It suggests that reproductive autonomy, the capacity to make intentional, informed decisions integrating professional and family goals, is a critical enabler of optimal outcomes.

Notably, this association held even after controlling for education level, suggesting that planning capacity is not simply a proxy for education. Women at all education levels who engage in strategic planning achieve better outcomes. This has important implications: interventions promoting reproductive health literacy, family planning counseling that explicitly addresses work-family integration, and workplace cultures supporting open discussion of reproductive planning may enhance women’s capacity for intentional decision-making.

Currently married women (cohabitating) showed significantly higher odds of healthy intervals (OR = 5.46) compared to never-married women, while other marital categories (separated, widowed, married-non-cohabitating) showed non-significant associations. This pattern suggests that stable, intact marital relationships may facilitate optimal spacing through multiple mechanisms: socioeconomic stability, joint decision-making about family planning, shared childcare enabling spacing, and social/emotional support for managing work-family demands.

However, causal direction is ambiguous in cross-sectional data. Does marital stability enable optimal spacing, or do women who successfully achieve healthy spacing have characteristics (planning capacity, communication skills) that also promote marital stability? Longitudinal research is needed to disentangle these pathways. Nonetheless, the finding highlights the importance of relationship context for reproductive outcomes. It suggests that policies supporting marital stability and interventions enhancing couple communication about reproductive planning may have spillover benefits for birth spacing.

A key finding was the contrast between bivariate and multivariable results for the occupation sector. Without cluster adjustment, the occupation sector showed no association (p = 0.117); with cluster adjustment, a significant bivariate association emerged (p = 0.034). However, in multivariable models adjusting for individual characteristics, the occupation sector showed no independent effects.

This pattern suggests that apparent sectoral differences in birth spacing outcomes reflect compositional differences in who works in each sector, rather than causal effects of workplace environments. Women in industrial sectors may be younger, less educated, or have different marital patterns, and these individual characteristics, rather than features of industrial workplaces per se, explain their higher proportion of healthy intervals.

Importantly, this null finding should not be interpreted as evidence that workplace characteristics are unimportant for reproductive health. Rather, in this specific context with universal healthcare and moderate labor protections, sectoral location appears less determinative than individual-level resources and planning capacity. In settings without these equalizing policies, occupation sector may matter substantially more. Preliminary unclustered analyses suggested that doctoral education was negatively associated with healthy birth intervals (OR = 0.21, p = 0.018). However, after cluster adjustment and in the full multivariable model, this association became non-significant (OR = 0.32, p = 0.138). This change illustrates the importance of proper statistical methods; the original finding was likely inflated by unaccounted clustering.

Furthermore, even the point estimate likely reflects confounding by age. Doctoral holders in our sample heavily concentrated first births in the 30–39 age range (71% vs. 38–40% for other education levels). After controlling for age at first birth, which has enormous effects (ORs in the tens), the direction of the education effect appears to reverse but loses significance. This suggests that doctoral education influences birth spacing primarily through its effect on the timing of childbearing onset rather than through independent mechanisms affecting the spacing of subsequent births.

This pattern highlights a potential tension for highly educated women pursuing extended educational careers: delaying childbearing to complete education leaves a narrower window for optimal spacing if multiple children are desired. Academic and professional environments could address this through policies supporting graduate student parents (childcare subsidies, flexible program timelines, parental leave provisions), workplace flexibility for mothers in doctoral training or early-career academic positions, and cultural shifts normalizing childbearing during rather than after educational attainment.

Qualitative interviews emphasized the importance of family support, institutional/workplace support, and healthcare accessibility, yet these factors were not significant independent predictors in multivariable models. The non-significance of family support (OR = 0.51, p = 0.155) and workplace support (OR = 1.44, p = 0.317) in quantitative models initially appears to contradict qualitative findings where these emerged as prominent themes. However, integrated interpretation reveals complementarity rather than contradiction.

Family support was qualitatively described as essential for enabling workforce participation, a necessary baseline condition. With 87.2% of participants reporting family support, insufficient variation exists for statistical detection as a predictor of differential outcomes. Qualitative accounts reveal that family support operates as an enabling factor that removes barriers rather than actively determining spacing, with individual planning capacity driving differential outcomes within a generally supportive context. As one participant explained: “Without my mother’s help with childcare, I couldn’t work at all. But she’s always been there, so it’s not about whether I have help, it’s about whether I plan properly.”

Similarly, workplace support variation was limited (19.7% reporting support beyond legal minimum), and qualitative accounts emphasized personal agency over structural workplace constraints. Participants described adapting to workplace limitations through personal resources rather than workplace support, determining their reproductive timing. This pattern suggests that in contexts with universal healthcare and moderate labor protections, individual-level factors may transcend workplace differences, a contextual explanation rather than evidence that workplace characteristics are unimportant.

This mixed-methods integration demonstrates that quantitative null findings do not indicate these factors are unimportant; rather, their near-universal availability in the Sri Lankan context creates a supportive baseline that enables individual planning capacity to become the differentiating factor.

Our finding of 61.3% of healthy birth intervals is difficult to compare directly to existing literature, as most studies report mean/median interval length or proportion of short intervals rather than within an optimal range. Among studies defining optimal intervals, our proportion is similar to urban Bangladesh (65% with intervals 24–60 months; [13]) and somewhat higher than rural Ethiopia (47% with intervals 24–60 months; [9]).

The strong association of younger age at first birth with optimal spacing replicates findings across diverse settings [8, 10] though our effect sizes (ORs in the tens) appear larger than typically reported (ORs of 2–5 in most studies). This may reflect our specific outcome definition, the contrast with a very late reference category, or characteristics of our working women sample.

Our null finding for the occupation sector contrasts with studies from Bangladesh [17] and Indonesia [18] showing formal-informal sector differences, but aligns with our theoretical interpretation that universal healthcare and labor protections may reduce sectoral disparities. We are unaware of other studies specifically examining occupation sector differences in birth spacing, making this a novel contribution.

The strong association of conscious career planning with optimal spacing is consistent with broader work-family literature showing that women who actively strategize work-family integration achieve better outcomes [32], though we are unaware of other studies examining this specifically for birth spacing.

Theoretical implications

Our findings provide support for an integrated life course-ecological framework. Consistent with life course theory [19], early transitions (age at first birth) profoundly shape subsequent trajectories. However, recognizing that age at first birth is itself shaped by educational and social contexts (Bronfenbrenner’s macrosystem and exosystem), we observe reciprocal influences across life course stages and ecological levels.

The finding that socio-environmental factors showed greater independent predictive power than age alone when modeled separately (Model 2 vs. Model 1), and that the combined model substantially outperformed either single-domain model, supports a multi-level, multi-temporal framework. Neither early life course factors nor contemporary socio-environmental factors alone suffice to explain birth spacing outcomes; both operate simultaneously.

The null finding for the occupation sector in multivariable models, despite significant bivariate association, suggests that in contexts with universal healthcare and moderate labor protections (Bronfenbrenner’s macrosystem), individual-level microsystem factors (family relationships, personal planning capacity) and mesosystem factors (work-family interface) may be more proximal determinants than exosystem factors (workplace policies). This context-dependency of determinants aligns with ecological theory’s emphasis on setting-specific developmental processes.

Methodological contributions

This study makes important methodological contributions beyond substantive findings. We demonstrate the critical importance of accounting for cluster sampling in standard error estimation; failure to do so led to inflated significance (doctoral education appeared significant), while proper methods revealed associations missed by naive tests (occupation sector bivariate association). The change in doctoral education significance from p = 0.018 to p = 0.138 after cluster adjustment serves as a cautionary tale about the necessity of appropriate statistical methods matching study design.

Our transparent reporting of all predictors, not just significant ones, enables readers to assess effect sizes and practical importance beyond statistical significance. The presentation of multiple model comparison (age-only, socio-environmental-only, combined) provides insights into relative contributions of predictor domains, an approach rarely used in birth spacing research.

The integration of sensitivity analyses (alternative reference categories, subgroup exclusions) and comprehensive diagnostics (calibration plots, influential case analysis, power analysis) demonstrates rigorous quantitative practice and enhances confidence in the robustness of findings.

Policy and practice implications

Despite inherent cross-sectional design limitations, findings suggest actionable implications across multiple intervention levels. Educational institutions from secondary schools through universities should integrate reproductive health education, addressing life course timing, optimal birth intervals, and work-family planning strategies into curricula and pre-marital counseling programs. Healthcare providers should explicitly incorporate women’s employment situations, career goals, and workplace support into family planning counseling, recognizing work as a critical social determinant of reproductive health. Workplace policies require substantial reform, including extending paid maternity leave from the current 84 to 180 days (aligning with WHO recommendations), implementing phased return-to-work programs, establishing subsidized childcare facilities, and creating flexible work arrangements that reduce stigma around reproductive planning in professional settings. Academic institutions specifically should address the compressed childbearing timelines among doctoral holders through family-friendly policies, including childcare subsidies, flexible program timelines, parental leave provisions, and cultural changes normalizing combining advanced study with parenthood. Informal sector workers, despite non-significant sectoral effects in this sample, warrant attention through social insurance schemes and portable maternity benefits not tied to specific employers. Finally, given associations between marital stability and healthy spacing, family planning programs should strengthen couples counseling components, promote male involvement in reproductive decision-making, and provide communication skills training for shared family planning within partnerships.

Sri Lanka’s experience suggests that universal healthcare access and codified maternity protections may create more equitable conditions for reproductive health across occupational sectors. The null finding for sector differences in our context, contrasting with significant disparities documented in settings without such protections, supports policy investment in universal healthcare and labor standards as mechanisms for reducing reproductive health inequities. These findings are particularly relevant for other South Asian and developing country contexts, considering policy reforms to support working women’s reproductive health.

Study limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference, as associations may reflect reverse causality or unmeasured confounding; longitudinal research following women prospectively would enable stronger causal conclusions. Our cluster sampling from established workplaces introduces selection bias, overrepresenting women in formal stable employment while systematically excluding those who left work after childbearing, informal workers without fixed workplaces, and women unable to maintain employment, likely underestimating challenges working women face. Occupation sector measurement based on workplace type rather than individual job roles may not capture true occupational characteristics (autonomy, flexibility, income, job security), limiting conclusions about occupational determinants. The absence of first births in the 25–29 age range reflects sampling bias, likely excluding women following normative educated-urban trajectories who had not yet completed second births, thus overrepresenting early or delayed childbearing patterns. Wide confidence intervals for some age categories (e.g., OR = 74.61 [6.76-823.42]) reflect small reference category cell sizes, indicating substantial statistical uncertainty about effect magnitudes despite directional consistency. Restriction to Western Province, with urban characteristics and higher socioeconomic status, limits generalizability to rural Sri Lankan settings or contexts with different healthcare systems and labor markets. Finally, moderate subgroup sample sizes (n = 38–125) provide limited power for detecting effects in occupation sectors, doctoral holders, and borderline associations (healthcare accessibility, p = 0.106), potentially resulting in Type II errors that warrant investigation with larger samples.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that healthy birth spacing (33–72 months between first and second births) is achievable among a majority (61.3%) of working women in Western Province, Sri Lanka, and is associated with younger age at first birth, marital stability, and conscious career-reproductive planning capacity. After rigorous adjustment for workplace clustering, women’s occupation sector showed a bivariate but not independent association with spacing outcomes, suggesting that in contexts with universal healthcare access and moderate labor protections, individual-level resources and planning capacity may transcend sectoral differences. The mixed-methods design enabled both identification of significant predictors and contextual interpretation of null findings through qualitative integration, demonstrating the value of combining approaches for understanding complex reproductive health phenomena.

The study makes several important contributions to reproductive health literature. First, methodologically, we demonstrate the critical importance of proper cluster adjustment in research using workplace or community-based sampling; failure to account for clustering led to both false positive findings (doctoral education appearing significant) and missed associations (occupation sector bivariate effect). This serves as an important methodological lesson for the field. Second, substantively, we provide the first systematic examination of birth spacing determinants specifically among working women with occupation-sector analysis in a South Asian setting. Third, our model comparison approach reveals that while age at first birth is the strongest single predictor, comprehensive models including both early life course and contemporary socio-environmental factors substantially outperform single-domain models, supporting integrated life course-ecological frameworks. Fourth, we identify conscious career-reproductive planning capacity as a strong, modifiable predictor, suggesting this as a potential intervention target.

Importantly, age at first birth, while classified as an “early life course factor” for analytical purposes, is itself shaped by prior socio-environmental factors, including educational opportunities, marriage timing, and career trajectories. Thus, the distinction between early life course and socio-environmental factors is analytical rather than ontological; both operate simultaneously across the life course. This life course perspective suggests that policies influencing educational pathways, career structures, and support for women combining education or career building with family formation may have downstream effects on birth spacing capacity.

The unexpected finding that doctoral education showed no independent association after cluster adjustment (OR = 0.32, p = 0.138; original unclustered p = 0.018) highlights potential tensions between extended educational pursuit and reproductive timing. Doctoral holders concentrated first births in older ages (30–39 years), and after adjusting for age effects, education showed no independent association. This suggests that educational attainment influences spacing primarily through the timing of childbearing onset rather than mechanisms affecting subsequent spacing. Academic environments could address this through policies supporting student parents and normalizing combining doctoral study with motherhood.

As female labor force participation continues rising globally, understanding how working women successfully navigate reproductive health alongside professional responsibilities becomes increasingly critical. This study demonstrates that healthy birth spacing among employed women is not only achievable but also facilitated by factors amenable to policy intervention (workplace supports, healthcare access) and individual planning (reproductive health education, career-family integration counseling). In contexts with universal healthcare and improving labor standards, individual-level resources and decision-making capacity may transcend occupational categories, offering hope that reproductive health equity is achievable even across diverse employment contexts through appropriate policies and support systems.

Future research should employ longitudinal designs to strengthen causal inference, measure occupation at more granular levels, extend to rural settings and vulnerable informal sector workers, and test interventions promoting integrated career-reproductive planning and workplace family-friendly policies. With these advances, the field can develop evidence-based strategies to support the millions of working women globally seeking to balance professional aspirations with reproductive health goals.
