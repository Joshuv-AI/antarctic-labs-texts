Varied Presentations of Arterial Events in Vascular Ehlers-Danlos Syndrome
Authors: Milan Jaiswal, Sara Mansoorshahi, Sharon Zachariah, Thanila Macedo, Alana C. Cecchi, Bihong Zhao, L. Maximilian Buja, Dianna M. Milewicz, Gustavo S. Oderich, Rana O. Afifi, Akiko Tanaka, Anthony L. Estrera, Salman A. Arain, Siddharth K. Prakash
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Spontaneous arterial aneurysms and dissections are relatively common in patients with vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, and arterial rupture accounts for most deaths. Members of the Aortic and Vascular Disease Clinic at UTHealth Houston hold a monthly conference that includes cardiothoracic and vascular surgeons, cardiologists, geneticists, radiologists, and pathologists to formulate multidisciplinary approaches for the management of complex cases. In this case series, we highlight the variable presentations and clinical evolution of complex aortic and vascular disease due to vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome.

Vascular Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (VEDS) is a rare autosomal dominant arteriopathy caused by pathogenic variants in the COL3A1 gene encoding the alpha1 chains of type III collagen.1 Characteristic features include thin skin, facial dysmorphism, easy bruising, and gastrointestinal perforation.1,2 Spontaneous arterial aneurysms and dissections are relatively common in patients with VEDS, and arterial rupture accounts for most deaths.2,3 It is estimated that 25% of VEDS patients develop vascular complications before age 20, with the rate increasing to 80% by age 40.2 Although there are no established guidelines for the medical management or surgical therapy of VEDS, lifelong surveillance allows for prompt treatment of potential complications. At our institution, the personalized surveillance protocol includes clinical evaluations with titration of beta-blocker medications, echocardiography, and arterial imaging with computed tomography angiography (CTA) or magnetic resonance angiography every 6 months to 3 years, depending on the status of the affected arterial segment.

A multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and management that draws on the unique perspectives of specialist clinicians and researchers has the potential to enhance patient care and improve clinical outcomes. We present 3 patients with VEDS who were discussed at the UTHealth Houston Multidisciplinary Aortic and Vascular Conference to highlight the clinical and genetic features of this condition, review state-of-the-art medical and interventional treatments, and describe care plans formulated through collaboration with our experienced panel of specialists, invited colleagues, and expert consultants. The discussants for these cases included surgeons Akiko Tanaka, MD, PhD, Anthony Estrera, MD, Rana Afifi, MD, and Gustavo Oderich, MD; clinical geneticist Dianna Milewicz, MD, PhD; genetic counselor Alana Cecchi, MS, CGC; cardiologist Siddharth Prakash, MD, PhD; and cardiovascular pathologists Bihong Zhao, MD, and L. Maximilian Buja, MD.

Informed consent was obtained from all 3 of the patients.

Patient 1

A 48-year-old woman was seen in clinic for routine cardiovascular surveillance of VEDS caused by a pathogenic missense variant in COL3A1 (c.970G>A, p.Gly324Ser). She denied chest pain, palpitations, or dyspnea. Her past medical history was significant for 3 vascular events: an acute celiac artery dissection, open ligation of celiac and hepatic artery aneurysms, and bilateral external iliac artery dissections (Figure 1). Her mother was also affected and died from an acute aortic dissection at age 62. Other maternal relatives were reportedly affected, and her 18-year-old daughter carries the same pathogenic variant but had not yet experienced any vascular events. The patient was a former smoker and did not consume alcohol or recreational drugs. Significant physical examination findings included fragile skin and varicose veins. She was normotensive with equal pulses in all extremities, and no murmurs or gallops were auscultated. Transthoracic echocardiogram showed a mildly dilated aortic root (40 mm) and ascending aorta (34 mm) that had enlarged by 1 mm over the previous year. Medications included clopidogrel, propranolol for migraine prophylaxis, and carvedilol. Seven years after the initial encounter, the patient presented to the emergency department with sharp substernal chest pain. She was hemodynamically stable, but her electrocardiogram showed evidence of an acute inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. On emergent coronary angiography, there was an extensive dissection of the right coronary artery (RCA), with near-occlusion of distal flow (Figure 2A, Video 1). A single drug-eluting stent was implanted into the proximal RCA, with restoration of complete perfusion and temporary relief of angina (Figure 2B, Video 2). Shortly after the procedure, she developed new chest pain with hypotension and diaphoresis. The CTA demonstrated a large pericardial effusion. Pericardiocentesis with implantation of a pericardial drain withdrew 1,000 mL of bloody fluid. Repeat right coronary angiography detected no coronary fistula and normal proximal coronary flow with residual dissection (Figure 2C, Video 3). Pericardial drainage ceased by hospital day 3, and the patient was discharged on hospital day 6 with prescriptions for aspirin, clopidogrel, atorvastatin, propranolol, and colchicine. Patient 2

A 23-year-old woman with an unremarkable past medical history presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset, right-sided abdominal and flank pain. Her family history was significant for a maternal aunt who survived a mesenteric artery dissection and was diagnosed with a left subclavian artery aneurysm. The CTA showed acute thrombosis of the right renal artery, an extensive right renal infarct, splenic artery thrombosis with focal aneurysmal dilatation (1.1 cm), and evidence of focal dissections of the celiac, right renal, and right external iliac arteries (Figure 3). One month after discharge, the patient was seen in clinic. She noted multifocal arthralgias but denied chest pain, abdominal pain, dyspnea, or palpitations. Her vital signs were within normal limits at presentation. Physical examination revealed pes planus, wide atrophic scars, thin translucent skin with multiple bruises, blue sclerae, and hyperextensible elbows. The CTA identified a new type B aortic dissection with aneurysmal dilation of the juxtarenal aorta involving the superior mesenteric artery and the ostium of the right renal artery. There was a separate, new dissection in the infrarenal aorta that extended to the aortic bifurcation. The echocardiogram showed an aortic root aneurysm with mild aortic regurgitation. Genetic testing revealed a COL3A1 pathogenic variant (c.2302G>C, p.Gly768Arg), confirming the diagnosis of VEDS. The management plan included medical therapy with carvedilol as well as serial surveillance with head-to-pelvis images.

After 4 months, the patient experienced recurrent abdominal pain. A repeat angiogram revealed that the splenic artery aneurysm had increased in size from 1.1 to 2.3 cm, and she was admitted for elective splenic artery embolization. The postprocedure CTA revealed a new dissection at the bifurcation of the common hepatic artery (CHA) and gastroduodenal artery (GDA), with occlusion of the GDA by the thrombosed false lumen (Figure 4). During serial observations, the dissection remained unchanged, and the abdominal pain abated. Patient 3

A 35-year-old man with hypertension presented to an outside hospital owing to chest pain. He reported a family history of unspecified Ehlers-Danlos syndrome that had not been genetically confirmed. Imaging revealed an enlarged thyroid gland and a Kommerell diverticulum with an aberrant right subclavian artery (ARSA) that originated distal to the left subclavian takeoff and coursed posteriorly to the trachea and esophagus. There was also a type B aortic dissection that extended from the ARSA to the aortic bifurcation, with a mildly dilated proximal descending aorta (3.1 cm). He was admitted for antihypertensive therapies and observation. His hospital course was complicated by severe sepsis, pneumothorax, and a secretory thyroid goiter that caused tracheal obstruction and required thyroidectomy with tracheostomy. During his prolonged recovery, he was discharged on hospital day 38 to a long-term acute care hospital with prescriptions for lisinopril and carvedilol. He did not follow up for routine surveillance imaging or clinical care after discharge, and his chronic systemic hypertension remained uncontrolled. After 6 years without arterial or vascular events, he presented to the emergency department at our institution with acute abdominal pain, and CTA revealed a new 4.7-cm infrarenal abdominal aortic aneurysm. The distal arch and proximal descending aorta had progressively dilated to 5.4 cm (Figure 6A). Medical management of hypertension resolved his symptoms, and he was discharged on hospital day 4. At the follow-up clinic visit, he was asymptomatic and normotensive. Significant physical examination findings included striae, obesity, and facial features consistent with VEDS. Genetic testing identified a COL3A1 pathogenic splice acceptor variant (c.4255-1G>T) that was predicted to result in haploinsufficiency. This variant was previously reported in another patient with a history of visceral artery dissections before age 40. Six months later, the patient underwent successful right subclavian bypass that was complicated by right phrenic nerve palsy. Plans for completion thoracoabdominal repair were delayed by recurrent hospitalizations for respiratory complications and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction. Four years later, he presented to the emergency department with recurrent pain, and the CTA demonstrated progressive dilation of the distal thoracic and abdominal aorta to 6.5 cm (Figure 6B). He was admitted for open replacement of the distal transverse arch and descending thoracic aortic aneurysm (zones 2-5). The patient tolerated this procedure without complications and was discharged on hospital day 20 with plans for staged repair of his infrarenal abdominal aneurysm.

Discussion

VEDS predisposes to spontaneous arterial aneurysms and dissections in all medium and large elastic arteries. The median survival in VEDS is significantly shorter than population norms, and deaths are most frequently caused by arterial ruptures.2 Dissections and aneurysms can develop spontaneously without preceding symptoms or risk factors, as demonstrated in these vignettes. Patient 1 had survived multiple arterial aneurysms and dissections before developing a spontaneous dissection of the RCA. We hypothesize that the percutaneous intervention caused RCA perforation into the pericardial space and led to cardiac tamponade. The patient's symptoms rapidly resolved after the effusion was drained. Patient 2 initially presented with multiple dissections in the celiac, renal, and external iliac arteries, which subsequently progressed to a type B aortic dissection involving the juxtarenal and infrarenal aorta. A splenic embolization procedure was complicated by new dissections and pseudoaneurysms of the CHA and GDA. These 2 cases highlight the characteristic arterial fragility of VEDS, which can increase the risk of routine vascular interventions.4 The second case also highlights the unpredictable occurrence of thrombotic events in VEDS patients, which can require nuanced decision-making about antithrombotic or antiplatelet therapies. Patient 3 exemplifies the synergistic effects of VEDS and congenital aortic anomalies. Haploinsufficiency of COL3A1, due to splice site variants, typically results in milder clinical manifestations of VEDS, with delayed onset of arterial dissections.2,5 In this case, ARSA and Kommerell diverticulum, along with hypertension, likely accelerated the presentation and severity of thoracic aortic disease. All 3 cases demonstrate the unpredictable and multifocal manifestations of VEDS, which require a high index of suspicion for rapid diagnosis of arterial events and timely interventions. Multidisciplinary discussion regarding the management of VEDS patients is also crucial, as managing bleeding risk factors with arterial interventions can become complex.

The prevalence of ARSA with a left aortic arch is 0.7% to 2.0% in the general population. Aberrant subclavian arteries are associated with Kommerell diverticuli, which can progressively dilate and rupture.6 Thoracic aortic aneurysms are also increased in patients who have other types of aortic arch malformations, with a reported incidence of up to 10%. The risk of dissections or ruptures related to these abnormalities may be further increased in patients with heritable aortopathies.7 Careful anatomic evaluation of the aorta can be helpful for risk stratification and clinical decisions about the timing of surveillance imaging or interventions.

Characteristic findings in VEDS include easy bruising, thin skin, distinctive facial features, arterial dissections, and spontaneous ruptures of the intestines or uterus.1 Each patient in this series exhibited at least 2 of these criteria. Genetic testing is necessary to confirm the diagnosis where there is clinical suspicion and/or an unconfirmed history of Ehlers-Danlos syndrome, as in patient 3.8 There are no specific evidence-based guidelines for medical or surgical management of arterial disease in VEDS given sparse clinical trial data. The BBEST open-label trial showed a potential benefit of medical therapy with celiprolol, a beta-blocker, at doses of up to 400 mg/d, but the result was questioned because the study included a relatively high proportion of participants who did not have genetically confirmed COL3A1 pathogenic variants.9 However, the positive result of 1 randomized clinical trial of irbesartan (NCT02597361), an angiotensin II receptor blocker, was recently published,10 and another phase III trial of celiprolol (NCT05432466) may be published soon. In observational studies, both beta-blockers and angiotensin II receptor blockers appear to reduce clinical events and mortality by preventing arterial ruptures due to the characteristic vascular fragility in VEDS.11 Avoiding elective interventions may also prevent deaths because excessive bleeding may be more likely after invasive procedures or vascular surgery.5 Regular clinical follow-up with frequent surveillance imaging may also improve clinical outcomes.5,11,12 Therefore, VEDS patients require lifelong follow-up and expectant management to lower the risk of cardiovascular complications.
