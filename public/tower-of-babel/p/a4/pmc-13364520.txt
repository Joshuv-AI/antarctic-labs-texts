Subtle Neurological Manifestations With Unexpected Lesion Extension After High-Dose Methylprednisolone in Neuromyelitis Optica Spectrum Disorder
Authors: Gohei Yamada, Kenji Okita, Tomoyuki Kuno, Eiichi Katada
Published: 2026

Dear Editor,

We report a case of area postrema syndrome (APS) due to neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), in which intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) alleviated the neurological symptoms, but this was followed by radiological lesion extension.

A 40-year-old female with a 4-year history of rheumatoid arthritis (RA) presented with nausea and hiccups lasting for 3 months. Her RA was well controlled by oral prednisolone at 5 mg/day. Abdominal CT and gastric endoscopy did not identify a cause of her gastrointestinal complaints. Although a neurological examination was unremarkable, her symptoms were suggestive of APS. T2-weighted MRI of the cervical spine revealed a dorsal medullary lesion (Fig. 1A). Brain MRI showed no additional abnormalities. Since NMOSD-associated APS was suspected, high-dose IVMP (1,000 mg/day) was administered for 3 consecutive days, followed by oral prednisolone (20 mg/day). Her nausea and hiccups resolved within 12 hours after the first IVMP dose. A live-cell-based assay subsequently confirmed aquaporin-4 (AQP4) antibody positivity, establishing the diagnosis of NMOSD. Although the clinical improvement suggested a reduction in the dorsal medullary lesion, MRI performed 1 day after completing the IVMP course demonstrated extension of the T2-hyperintense lesion into the upper cervical cord, with increased signal on the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map consistent with vasogenic edema as well as slight hyperintensity on diffusion-weighted imaging consistent with T2 shine-through (Fig. 1B). The patient began to complain of postural tremor in her fingers 15 days after completing the IVMP course, despite follow-up MRI showing a slight reduction in the medullary lesion signal intensity without gadolinium enhancement (Fig. 1C). A neurological examination revealed subtle bilateral postural tremor of the fingers that was observable only during finger extension (Supplementary Video 1 in the online-only Data Supplement), slight bilateral weakness of the abductor pollicis brevis and abductor digiti minimi (MRC grade 4), and hyperreflexia in both lower extremities. Because neurological deterioration due to extension of the dorsal medullary lesion into the cervical cord–possibly accompanied by vasogenic edema and mild inflammation—could not be excluded, a second 3-day course of high-dose IVMP was administered. After completing this course, oral prednisolone was started at 15 mg/day with tapering to 5 mg/day over 5 weeks, and subcutaneous satralizumab was initiated. MRI performed 23 days after completing the second IVMP course showed further signal reduction of the medullary lesion (Fig. 1D), and the finger weakness and hyperreflexia in both lower extremities had resolved along with marked but incomplete improvement of the postural finger tremor. Three weeks later the postural tremor had completely resolved (Supplementary Video 1 in the online-only Data Supplement). The patient experienced no recurrence of NMOSD during the following 8 months.

Radiological extension of an NMOSD lesion despite clinical remission shortly after IVMP administration is uncommon in clinical practice. Although based on clinical observations, the subsequent subtle neurological manifestations in the present case may have reflected clinically relevant but nondestructive involvement of the cervicomedullary lesion. A prospective study of NMOSD patients with spinal cord lesions observed tremor in 33% (4/12) of AQP4-antibody-positive cases.1 Spinal tremor typically presents as a postural or kinetic hand tremor. Given the temporal relationship between steroid exposure and tremor onset in the present case, steroid-induced tremor represents a plausible alternative explanation.2

The clinical course of this case prompted consideration of a possible association between IVMP and lesion extension. Alternatively, the natural evolution of a dorsal medullary lesion during an active disease phase may also account for the lesion extension. Since lesions that are clinically silent between attacks are known to occur in NMOSD, clinicoradiological dissociation is not uncommon.3 Two possible explanations for the post-IVMP lesion extension were considered. The first is that a rebound surge of inflammatory cytokines after IVMP withdrawal contributed to the lesion extension; however, this was hypothetical since serum cytokine levels were not measured. The second possible explanation is that vasogenic edema in the dorsal medulla progressed either as part of its natural course or following IVMP administration. Disruption of the blood–brain barrier by AQP4 antibodies may contribute to the development of vasogenic edema.4 Moreover, vasogenic edema associated with acute inflammation is a characteristic MRI finding in NMOSD.5 In the present case, the areas of T2 hyperintensity and increased ADC signal in the dorsal medulla and cervical cord did not coincide, suggesting a combination of vasogenic edema and inflammatory processes. While these changes occur as part of the natural disease course, IVMP is an uncommon cause of posterior reversible encephalopathy syndrome (PRES). The possibility of overlapping NMOSD and PRES was also considered.4,6

In conclusion, this case illustrates a clinicoradiological course mismatch in NMOSD after IVMP. However, the mechanisms underlying the lesion changes and subsequent subtle neurological findings remain uncertain.
