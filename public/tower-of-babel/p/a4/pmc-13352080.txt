Clinical expressions, disease course, quality of life, and resilience in subgroups of patients with angioedema☆
Authors: Iris Leibovich-Nassi, Avner Reshef, Michal Itzhaki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePatients with angioedema caused by C1INH deficiency (HAE-C1INH) experience recurrent episodes of unpredictable swelling. Recently, new genetic variants have been identified, whereas patients with unknown causes and no identifiable genetic mutations constitute a distinct subgroup. As a lifelong condition, angioedema significantly impacts the patient's quality of life and ability to cope. With the emergence of new angioedema variants, comparing the characteristics and coping experiences across different designated subgroups presents an important unmet need.Materials and methodsA cross-sectional, prospective study was performed to analyze 3 angioedema subgroups: A: Hereditary Angioedema Types 1,2 (HAE-C1INH), B: Hereditary Angioedema with factor 12 mutation (HAE-FXII), and C: Angioedema with unknown cause (AE-UNK). Two validated questionnaires were used: The Evaluation of Prodromes and Attacks Questionnaire (HAE-EPA, part Q1) and the 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC).ResultsThe study involved 111 patients with a mean age of 44.51 years. The mean age of onset was 16.1 years, and the mean age at diagnosis was 19.58 years. Subgroups A, B, and C consisted of 72 (64%), 16 (14%), and 23 (20%) patients, respectively. The mean age of onset was earlier in the A subgroup (10.31 years) than in the B (19.70 years) and C (30.34 years) subgroups (p < 0.001). Similarly, the mean age at diagnosis was earlier in the A subgroup (13.85 years) compared to the B (24.93 years) and C (34.73 years) subgroups (p < 0.001). Significant differences were found in the triggers of attacks, but not in the body location of the attacks or in the experience of pre-attack prodromes. Subgroup A reported the highest mean coping ability (7.88 ± 2.44) compared to those in Subgroup B (5.13 ± 3.07) and Subgroup C (5.48 ± 2.81) (F = 41.6, p < .001). The mean quality of life was significantly higher in Subgroup A (8.47 ± 2.06) than in Subgroup C (7.74 ± 1.76) (F = 13.0, p < .001). The mean total Connor-Davidson Resilience score was highest in Subgroup A (31.25 ± 4.86), followed by Subgroup B (30.87 ± 5.21), and lowest in Subgroup C (27.21 ± 6.43) (F = 5.19, p < 0.001).ConclusionThe designated subgroups differ in both clinical course and coping strategies. Recognizing the unique characteristics of each AE subgroup can improve the management and quality of life of patients with this lifelong and rare disease.

Introduction

Angioedema (AE) is a diverse clinical syndrome characterized by recurrent episodes of tissue swelling resulting from increased vascular endothelial hyperpermeability.1,2 The classic pathogenesis of most Hereditary Angioedema (HAE) cases involves unregulated activation of the Kallikrein-Kinin (Contact) pathway (KKS) and elevated bradykinin (BK) production due to inadequate or faulty plasma C1-Inhibitor (C1INH).2 Patients experience sudden, unpredictable, and potentially dangerous swelling episodes affecting the face, extremities, genitals, tongue, and larynx, along with severe abdominal pain attacks. Swelling episodes can be triggered by various factors such as trauma, medical or dental procedures, menstruation, estrogen contraceptives, infections, medications, and emotional stress.1,2

An international consensus on clinical guidelines for diagnosing and treating HAE, along with a new nomenclature featuring updated definitions, was recently established.3,4 The scope of angioedema has recently expanded due to new findings of new genetic variants in individuals and families experiencing recurrent angioedema attacks, thus labeled: 'Hereditary Angioedema with Normal C1INH' (HAE-nC1INH).5 To date, 10 new genotypes have been described in individuals and families co-segregating with the typical AE phenotype.5 It was reported that these patients exhibit clinical features almost identical to HAE-C1INH, although the underlying mechanisms may differ.5 Unfortunately, many patients with recurrent angioedema attacks have not yet been genotyped or do not carry any known mutations and are therefore classified as 'HAE of unknown origin' (HAE-UNK).4

As a chronic and recurrent disease with an undulating course, HAE significantly impacts the patient's quality of life and ability to cope. 6, 7, 8 HAE patients experience a reduced health-related quality of life (HRQoL) between attacks despite full physical recovery. 9,10 It has been shown that the average HAE Quality of Life scores are generally lower in patients with high attack frequency.7,8

The behavioral patterns related to HAE management in daily life and during attacks are not fully understood and require further research.11 In a lifelong disease like HAE, adopting effective coping strategies are crucial for managing daily life. Lazarus and Folkman describe coping as the cognitive and behavioral efforts to master, reduce, or tolerate the internal and/or external demands created by stressful situations.12 In this context, developing personal resilience is one of the key positive qualities that predict better coping with chronic disease.13 Factors associated with promoting resilience in physically ill patients encompass a wide range of variables, including social support, psychological factors, and coping strategies. 14, 15, 16.

The prevalence of HAE-C1INH types 1 and 2 (C1INH deficiency) patients in our country (population: 10 million) is estimated at 1:30,000 of the population. However, there is limited data on other types of angioedema, such as HAE-nC1INH and HAE-UNK. In 2017, we genotyped 38 patients with HAE-nC1INH, of whom 16 (44.7%) were found with the factor 12 mutation (FXII-HAE), all were females carrying the missense mutation Thr328Lys.17 More recently, we studied an additional group of 61 patients by performing targeted sequencing of 6 HAE-nC1INH mutations that were known at the time. This study yielded 5 new female patients with HAE-FXII mutations, but none with other mutations. 18, 19, 20.

To date, limited information is available on the clinical characteristics and disease progression of HAE-nC1INH.5 Therefore, it is important to examine the differences and similarities in the disease course ("illness trajectory") among the extremely rare group of patients with confirmed genetic variants (ie, HAE-FXII) and compare them with diagnosed HAE-C1INH types 1,2 patients, as well as with a subgroup of patients with recurrent angioedema attacks showing no detectable underlying cause (AE-UNK).

This study aims to characterize a cohort comprising 3 subgroups of patients with angioedema, compare their clinical features, triggering factors, and disease progression, and, specifically, examine their personal coping strategies, including resilience.

Materials and methods

Study overview

The study was designed as an observational cross-sectional study, examining 3 subgroups of patients diagnosed with angioedema, analyzing and comparing their demographic, clinical, and coping competencies.

Study design and patients

We examined a cohort of AE patients divided into 3 subgroups: A: HAE (C1–INH deficiency, N = 72), B: HAE-FXII (Factor XII mutation, N = 16), and C: AE-UNK (Unknown cause, N = 23). We compared demographics, including age, gender, and ethnic origin, as well as the prevalence of the disease among patients' relatives, clinical features (such as age of onset, age at diagnosis, and disease progression), triggering factors, patients' health-related quality of life (HRQoL), and coping skills.

Key eligibility criteria included individuals aged 18–75 years of either sex with a clinical history and diagnosis of HAE, who have low antigenic or functional C1INH and low C4 levels, or patients with normal levels of these parameters who have undergone genetic analysis involving targeted sequencing of 6 mutations [F12 (2 variants), PLG, ANGPT1, MYOF, HS3ST6] using the Sanger method 17,18 for known HAE-nC1INH mutations.

Patients with clinical features compatible with AE (recurrent swelling attacks, abdominal attacks, no wheals, etc.), with normal C1INH/C4 levels, who have undergone genetic analysis and found negative to all known mutations, were designated a separate subgroup: "Angioedema with unknown origin" (AE-UNK). None of the patients in this group were using medications with a potential for triggering recurrent angioedema attacks, such as ACE-Inhibitors, NSAIDs, or Renin-Angiotensin System (RAS) inhibitors. All of these patients were treated with high-dose antihistamines for prolonged periods to exclude histamine-mediated AE, and none received anti-IgE (Omalizumab) treatment.

The study received approval from the Ethics Committee of the Medical Center (No. 0079-23) and the Ethics Committee of the University (No. 0007911-2). All participants gave their consent to participate by signing an electronic informed consent form.

Procedures

Qualified patients completed a 32-item questionnaire covering their disease course, performed via in-person interviews or phone calls. Study questionnaire items were adapted from a validated questionnaire (The Hereditary Angioedema Evaluation of Prodromes and Attacks, HAE-EPA part Q1), which demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach's alpha for attacks: 0.88–0.98, for prodromes: 0.78–0.98).19 In addition, we used the 10-item Connor-Davidson Resilience Scale (CD-RISC) for the first time in HAE studies. This scale was translated and validated into Hebrew (Cronbach's alpha: 0.89) after obtaining permission from the authors.21,22 The CD-RISC-10 consists of 10 statements describing different aspects of resilience, with items related to flexibility (1 and 5), sense of self-efficacy (2, 4, and 9), ability to regulate emotions (10), optimism (3, 6, and 8), and cognitive focus/maintaining attention under stress (7). Each item is scored on a five-point scale from 0 to 4. The total score is calculated by summing all 10 items, with a total resilience score ranging from 0 to 40. Higher scores indicate greater resilience, while lower scores suggest less resilience or more difficulty in bouncing back from adversity. Examples of these items include: "I can adapt when changes occur" and "I can deal with whatever comes my way."21,22 All questionnaires were anonymous and did not contain identifying details of the participants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using a commercially available software package (SPSS software, version 26), employing descriptive statistics based on the data. Comparisons of categorical variables were performed using Fisher's exact test or the χ2 test, as appropriate, while continuous variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA.

All values are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Two-tailed P-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. The estimated sample size for the calculation was, as is customary in studies of rare diseases, at least 66 participants.

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the entire cohort

The entire cohort included 111 patients. The average age of the participants was 44.51 ± 17.42 years, with an average of 2.8 ± 1.26 children. Subgroup A (HAE-C1INH) consisted of 72 patients, representing 64.9%, followed by Subgroup C (HAE-UNK) with 23 patients (20.7%), and Subgroup B (HAE-nC1INH) with 16 patients (14.4%). Most participants (84.5%) were born in Israel, while 38.7% of fathers and 43.2% of mothers were of Israeli origin. The mean age at first HAE attack was 16.01 ± 13.55 years, and the mean age at diagnosis was 19.58 ± 15.23 years. Previous hospitalizations for attacks were reported by 81.1% of patients, with an average of 5.27 ± 5.50 hospitalizations over their lifetime. Frequency and severity of attacks

The reported frequency of angioedema attacks varied. While 18.9% (21 patients) experienced attacks more than once a week, 23.4% (26) had 1-2 attacks per month, 22.5% (25) reported 3–4 attacks annually, 12.6% (14) experienced 1–2 attacks a year, and 22.5% (25) reported no attacks. The maximum attack frequency was once a week in 46.8% of cases, indicating a high disease burden among a significant portion of the cohort. A small percentage of women (15.3%) reported fewer attacks after pregnancy, and 16.2% after menopause, suggesting potential hormonal influences on disease progression (see Table 1).

Prodromes and predictability of attacks

The majority of patients (63.96%) reported experiencing premonitory pre-attack symptoms (prodromes) occurring within 12 h before an attack, and experiencing a prodrome in about a fifth (21.62%) of their attacks. However, only 53.15% of participants said they could predict an attack based on a prodrome. Notably, 78.4% of patients never experienced a prodrome without an attack ensuing, indicating that prodromal symptoms are a fairly consistent sign of oncoming attacks. Regression analysis could not disclose strong associations between certain clinical variables and prodromes as predictors of attacks (Data not shown)

Impact on daily life and psychological burden of disease

The patients reported missing an average of 12.06 ± 17.59 work or school days per year due to attacks, highlighting the disease's significant impact on their daily lives. On a 5-point scale, the disease's interference with daily life averaged 2.61 ± 1.38. On a 0–10 scale, it was rated as interfering with daily life at 5.23 ± 3.42. Mental stress caused by the illness was rated at 4.11 ± 3.36 on a 0–10 scale. Concerns about the disease affecting future generations were moderate, with a mean score of 6.86 ± 4.00 on a 0–10 scale (Data not shown).

Coping and resilience

Patients rated their ability to cope with the illness at a total of 30.36 ± 5.46 on a 0–40 scale (The Connor-Davidson scale), indicating a moderate level of adaptation. Mean resilience, measured using relevant questionnaire items, was 3.04 ± 0.55 on a 0–4 scale. Regression analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of patients' characteristics and disease course parameters on resilience. (Data not shown).

Treatment

Most patients (58.55%) reported using medication for their condition. Medications were classified into long-term prophylaxis (LTP), short-term prophylaxis (STP), and on-demand treatment (ODT). LTP was prescribed to 18.9% of patients, with 22.5% of those receiving research-based medications. 16.2% received STP, and 9% of these were used for oral procedures. Within the STP subgroup, a larger percentage, 42.3%, did not receive any preventive treatment. In the on-demand subgroup, 36.9% received C1INH concentrate, 22.5% received Icatibant, and 6.3% received research-based treatment. (Data not shown)

Disease course

Mean number of hospitalizations was highest in Subgroup B (7.13 ± 7.79) and lowest in Subgroup C (2.74 ± 3.60) (F = 3.69, p = 0.028). The highest mean maximal attack frequency was reported in Subgroup A (54.2%) and Subgroup C (52.2%), while only 6.3% of Subgroup B experienced frequent attacks (χ2 = 25.00, p = 0.002). Tongue/throat involvement was significantly higher in Subgroup C (1.00 ± 2.33) compared to Subgroup A (0.12 ± 0.55) and Subgroup B (0.57 ± 0.93) (F = 4.77, p = 0.010). Attack frequency (no. of attacks in certain time periods) and attack duration were not statistically different between the 3 subgroups, except in oropharyngeal involvement of subgroup C patients (p = 0.01) (see Table 2)

Treatment by medications

Results of the one-way ANOVA revealed significant differences across the 3 subgroups for the recommended categories. LTP was used by 86.1% of participants in Subgroup A, 12.5% in Subgroup B, and 4.3% in Subgroup C (p < 0.001). STP (pre-procedural treatment) was administered to 70.8% of subgroup A, 37.5% of subgroup B, and 3.4% of subgroup C (p < 0.001). ODT for acute attacks was given to 90.3% of subgroup A, 37.5% of subgroup B, and 8.7% of subgroup C (p < 0.001). A minority of patients reported receiving only ODT: 8.3% of subgroup A, 25.0% in subgroup B, and 0% in subgroup C (p = .026). (data not shown)

Burden of disease and coping

Concerns about future generations developing the disease were highest in Subgroup A (7.50 ± 3.80) (F = 19.68, p < .001), while mental stress was most pronounced in Subgroup C (5.35, SD = 3.56) (F = 13.13, p < .001). Patients in Subgroup A reported the highest mean coping ability (7.88 ± 2.44) compared to those in Subgroup B (5.13 ± 3.07) and Subgroup C (5.48 ± 2.81) (F = 41.6, p < .001). Discussion

Angioedema is a lifelong condition characterized by unpredictable attacks and fluctuating periods of remission, which vary among individuals and are triggered by multiple factors 1, 2, 3, 4, 5. It greatly affects patients' quality of life and their ability to manage the disease 6, 7, 8. Patients with HAE often have lower-than-average health-related quality of life (HRQoL) and must develop specific coping strategies to maintain their personal and social well-being.9,10 Recent research suggests that experienced patients tend to manage the disease course more effectively and use their resources more efficiently.23 Similar to other chronic illnesses with an irregular course, patients often see their illness as a limitation and develop personalized coping strategies accordingly 12,24,25. Additionally, more experienced patients tend to recognize early signs of attacks more accurately and use these skills to manage attacks, including timing the self-administration of treatments.26

Our study reveals differences in clinical presentation among the angioedema subgroups that go beyond basic demographic characteristics. The age at first attack and the medical diagnosis occur earlier for Subgroup A patients (HAE-C1INH), likely due to increased awareness and family history. However, the age at diagnosis is significantly higher in Subgroup C (HAE-UNK), which is less well defined and has fewer diagnosed family members. It is important to note that, unlike some HAE-nC1INH studies 27, 28, 29, we included in this subgroup all cases with normal C1INH/C4 levels, regardless of genetic mutations or family history. This approach aligns with other studies of normal C1INH in the absence of affected relatives.30

The current literature suggests that clinical features of HAE-C1INH and HAE-nC1INH may be difficult to differentiate. Our findings support some of these observations and provide additional insights into the clinical course, coping skills, and resilience. Besides previously noted differences, such as later onset in HAE-nC1INH and a higher occurrence of HAE-FXII in females, there are other notable distinctions between the subgroups. Interestingly, although a recent review indicates that prodromes are uncommon in HAE-nC1INH,5 both Subgroups B and C in our study report prodromes preceding their attacks.

Clearly, personal health assets include various internal and external strengths that support healthy behaviors and enhance overall health and well-being, which are essential for managing chronic diseases. 23, 24, 25 Patients with chronic medical conditions views their illness as a limitation and develops their own personal coping strategies.24,25

The discovery of higher mental stress levels in subgroup C (HAE-UNK) is concerning and calls for further investigation. This subgroup is highly diverse, including patients who present at a later age and are facing difficulties accessing modern HAE medications, therefore requiring closer clinical and psychological attention. Conversely, better coping skills and higher HRQoL scores in Subgroup A (HAE-C1INH) are probably due to their longer disease experience, such as more frequent attacks and a family history of HAE. Moreover, better access to new treatments for both acute episodes and prevention is likely to enhance this subgroup's confidence and increase their perception of control, and promote more effective coping strategies.31

It has been shown that experienced patients are more effective at managing chronic diseases and using their resources efficiently. Our findings agree with other studies, which suggest that experienced HAE patients manage chronic conditions more successfully and utilize their resources by developing personalized coping strategies. 9, 10, 11 We have demonstrated that personal coping strengths, gained through age and influenced by the duration of illness, may help explain some of the links between prodromes and attacks.19

In addition to the reported coping abilities, we introduced a new construct, resilience, as a parameter for coping in this study. Resilience is defined as the ability to maintain healthy functioning over time despite adversity or to return to normal functioning after facing adversity.13,14 It reflects the capacity to overcome difficulties in various life areas through perseverance, which acts as a protective factor against psychological distress. Resilience is viewed as a dynamic concept rather than a fixed personality trait and is an essential trait for coping with chronic disease.16 This psychological asset significantly impacts the quality of life for patients with chronic diseases and serves as a reliable indicator of their mental state and ability to cope with the hardships associated with their illness.31 A recent study of patients with chronic diseases suggests that psychological resiliency relates to the number of children, exercise habits, subjective health status, both objective and subjective support, utilization of support, and ADL.31 Similar to the HAE disease trajectory, recent studies on chronic diseases with a relapsing-remitting course, such as Inflammatory Bowel Diseases (IBD), support our decision to include resilience within the assessment tools for coping with HAE. 32,33 Both studies examined the protective effect of resilience, emphasizing its role in reducing the frequency and severity of depressive symptoms and enhancing self-care and disease management. These studies recommend incorporating resilience-building interventions into disease care programs. 32,33 In HAE, self-management of the disease is increasingly central to patient autonomy; therefore, patients need to recognize their strengths and utilize personal health assets, including a range of internal and external resources.23 We found no other studies on HAE that utilized resilience as a coping strategy. However, we believe that this clinical parameter more accurately reflects the dynamic nature of HAE and warrants further research.

The study employs a newly introduced categorization system that includes endotypes and subtypes of angioedema, based on clinical experience and genotypic analysis of individuals and families with the AE phenotype.4 HAE terminology now includes several new genetic variants belonging to the HAE-nC1INH category.5 So far, very few studies have examined and compared different subgroups of AE patients. Although this study is relatively small, it adds to the limited data on clinical signs, disease progression, and, most importantly, the patient's ability to manage the personal burden of illness across various angioedema subgroups.

The main limitation of this study is that it was conducted in 1 country and involved patients from a single angioedema expert center. It includes a relatively small group of patients, most of whom are classified as the HAE-C1INH endotype, resulting in a cohort that is not perfectly balanced. Additionally, the patients labeled as HAE-UNK (subgroup C) are a diverse group that exhibits the angioedema phenotype without clear genetic co-segregation. Also, the role of spirituality was not examined in this study, although it can influence both patients' perceptions of illness and resilience, and should be investigated in future AE studies.

We conclude that clinical manifestations, disease progression, quality of life, coping skills, and resilience, all reflecting the patient's personal perception of their illness trajectory, vary among the 3 designated AE subgroups. Recognizing the unique characteristics of each AE subgroup can improve management and quality of life for patients with this lifelong, rare disease.
