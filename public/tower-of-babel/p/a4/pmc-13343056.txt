qREDS score compared with the REDS and NEWS2 scores to risk-stratify emergency department suspected sepsis patients for in-hospital mortality and 168-hour survival probability: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Narani Sivayoham, Oliver Mason, Adil N Hussain, Ashvni Sivanandan, Cameron Fleming
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe Risk-stratification of Emergency Department suspected Sepsis (REDS) score requires external validation. The six dichotomous variables and lactate can be accurately extracted electronically unlike refractory hypotension (RH), where the recording of the fluid-bolus and blood pressure measurement may be inaccurate. The impact of excluding RH is not known.ObjectivesTo develop the quick-REDS (qREDS) score, by removing RH from the REDS score and to validate its prognostic performance in comparison to the original score and the National Early Warning Score (NEWS)2 score, for all-cause in-hospital mortality and 168-hour survival probability (SP).MethodsVital signs, blood results, outcome at 168 hours and at discharge, were extracted from the electronic records of emergency department (ED) adults admitted after intravenous antibiotics for an infection. The REDS, qREDS and NEWS2 scores were calculated, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves constructed for in-hospital all-cause mortality, the area under the ROC (AUROC) curves compared and cut-off points noted. Test-characteristics at cut-off points ≥1, ≥3, ≥5 and ≥7 were studied. Kaplan-Meier (KM) curves were constructed for all the scores for SP at 168 hours for score bands 0–2, 3–4 and ≥5.ResultsOf 3202 patients, 433 died in hospital and 209 died at 168 hours. AUROC curve: REDS 0.74 (95% CI 0.72 to 0.75) and qREDS 0.73 (95% CI 0.71 to 0.75); both greater than NEWS2 0.66 (95% CI 0.65 to 0.68); p<0.0001. Cut-off points: REDS ≥3, qREDS ≥3 and NEWS2 ≥6. Specificity for in-hospital mortality of score ≥5; REDS 89.2% (95% CI 88.0% to 90.3%), qREDS 90.6% (95% CI 89.4% to 91.6%) and NEWS2 43.9% (95% CI 42.1% to 45.8%). All KM curves were significant, log-rank test p<0.0001. SP at 168 hours: whole population 93.4% (SE 0.4); SP at ≥5points: REDS 78.3% (SE 1.2), qREDS 78.0% (SE 2.0) and NEWS2 90.6% (SE 0.7). SP trends were similar at 24, 48 and 72 hours.ConclusionqREDS is a valid surrogate for the REDS score and had significantly better prognostic performance than the NEWS2 score for in-hospital mortality and 168-hour survival probability, in ED patients with suspected sepsis.Keywords: Hospital Mortality, Sepsis, Risk management, Emergency department, Critical care

Introduction

Sepsis1 is a prevalent,2 3 life-threatening condition. A patient with an infection may develop sepsis as a result of a dysregulated immune system response, causing organ dysfunction and death. Most patients in hospital with sepsis are admitted as an emergency; the vast majority of whom are admitted with community acquired sepsis4 and through the emergency department (ED). It is, therefore, incumbent on the ED to risk-stratify patients, as early recognition and treatment are crucial to improving patient survival. Without appropriate stratification there is a greater risk of under-treating, leading to missed cases and higher mortality. Commencement of antibiotics is the most common recommended intervention and the Surviving Sepsis Campaign guidelines5 recommend initiating antibiotics within an hour of recognition in patients with shock and within 3 hours for all patients with suspected sepsis.

The ED is responsible for immediate treatment and arranging onward care, the level of which will be determined in light of each individual’s comorbidities, in discussion with the patient and/or their next of kin. This must be done early in the patient’s journey as treatment priorities may differ; in some patients, sepsis may be the terminal event. The urgency with which the decision is made will depend on the risk of deterioration and death. Scoring systems are often used to risk-stratify patients for deterioration and mortality. For the purposes of implementing an intervention which is potentially aggressive or resource intensive, the score threshold should have a high specificity, thus minimising false positives.

The Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) score6 is a scoring system used in the intensive care unit (ICU) setting. It scores each of six organ systems from 0 to 4 and ranges from 0 to 24. An increase of two points from baseline in the context of an infection is used to define sepsis.1 However, this requires the calculation of the score two times, which is labour intensive in a time-pressured environment such as the ED. The SOFA-2 score7 is yet to be validated in the ED. The qSOFA score8 is an abbreviated 3-point screening tool used to identify patients at risk of deterioration and mortality but is known to have a low sensitivity and is not recommended for screening.5

The National Early Warning Score (NEWS) was developed in 20129 and updated to the NEWS2 score in 2017. It uses six physiological variables and the use of oxygen and ranges from 0 to 20 points. The Royal College of Physicians recommends the use of the NEWS2 score to monitor adult patients for deterioration10 and is widely used across the National Health Service in the UK. The National Institute for Health and Care Excellence guidelines for the management of sepsis (2025)11 recommend the use of the NEWS2 score to stratify patients with suspected sepsis and suggest a score of 5 indicates moderate risk. But recent evidence suggests a NEWS2 score of ≥5 may identify only half of the patients with sepsis.12

The Risk-stratification of Emergency Department Suspected Sepsis (REDS) score13 is a scoring system composed of six dichotomous and two stratified variables. The full breakdown of the score is presented in supplemental file online supplemental figure S1. The REDS score ranges from 0 to 12 points and a score of ≥3 is deemed high-risk for in-hospital mortality.

In ED patients admitted with an infection or suspected sepsis, one or more of the ‘specified comorbidities’ (SC) have been shown to be present in 80% of patients who die without an admission to the ICU.13 The SCs are present in 45–50% of those admitted14 15 with suspected sepsis but 70% of in-hospital deaths14 in the same population. The SCs are: (1) dementia, (2) active malignancy, (3) care home residency or a minimum three times a day care package which would reflect a degree of dependency for activities of daily living and align with a clinical frailty scale of 6 or more,16 (4) use of long-term oxygen therapy and (5) previous community or in-hospital do-not-attempt-cardio-pulmonary-resuscitation order. Patients with significant comorbidities and at high risk of death will need decisions to be made regarding the level of treatment that is appropriate, after discussion with the patient and/or the next of kin. These conversations can be difficult and time-consuming in the ED. It is, therefore, important to know the urgency with which the conversation needs to be had and requires a valid scoring system.

The REDS score was developed and validated in a single centre13,1517 and externally validated in small studies18,20; it requires external validation in a large multicentre study to confirm generalisability. Such a study is best conducted by electronic data extraction. The extraction of the six dichotomous variables and lactate can be reliably automated, making the validation study scalable. However, electronic capture of refractory hypotension (RH) may be unreliable, as it will not be known when the fluid bolus was completed, or if the blood pressure after the fluid bolus was recorded electronically in a timely manner. Excluding the RH scores from the REDS score will lead to a score that is quicker to calculate, earning the name ‘quick-REDS’ score, abbreviated to qREDS; it will range from 0 to 9 points.

The prognostic performance of the REDS score is comparable to that of the SOFA score14 15 17 and better than the NEWS214 15 17 and qSOFA13 scores. The prognostic performance of the qREDS score is not known, nor is its performance in comparison to the REDS and NEWS2 scores.

The primary aim of this study is to develop qREDS—a modified scoring system derived from the REDS score—by removing refractory hypotension, a difficult-to-assess variable and to validate its prognostic performance and practical applicability in comparison to the original score and the NEWS2 score, for all-cause in-hospital mortality.

Methodology

Setting and study population

This study was carried out in the ED of a large inner-city tertiary referral hospital. The ED has an annual attendance of approximately 120 000 adults. Adult patients admitted after receiving intravenous antibiotics for the treatment of an infection are routinely audited. An electronic report of adult patients admitted after receiving antibiotics is retrieved a few days after admission. Patients whose primary diagnosis is identified as one that is not an infection, or received only oral antibiotics, are excluded. Those with a suspected infection who received intravenous antibiotics are entered into the audit registry. The number of days that were audited each month depended on the availability of resources. This is a retrospective study of audit registry.

Data collection

Medically qualified researchers were trained to abstract the required information from the electronic patient records and enter the extracted data directly into a pre-formatted password-protected Excel spreadsheet. Age, sex, the date and time of admission to the ED, the initial vital signs; respiratory rate, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, fraction of inspired oxygen, temperature, baseline and presenting Glasgow Coma Scale, the presence of altered mental state were noted. Vital signs missing in the initial set of observations were substituted with the value in the next set of observations. Results of blood tests taken on admission to the ED were also noted; namely, white cell count, neutrophil count, international normalised ratio (INR), the use of oral anticoagulants (warfarin and direct oral anticoagulants), C-reactive protein, serum albumin and point-of-care lactate. SCs were noted. The presumed source of infection, admission to the ICU, discharge outcome, the development of RH and stratification by lactate level after the fluid bolus, the use of vasopressors in the ED and the ceiling of treatment for patients with RH who did not receive vasopressors were recorded.

The date and time of death certification was collected for those who died in hospital. Patients who were discharged on or before the seventh day after admission were studied further for their outcome on day 7. The electronic patient record is connected to the National Health Service’s national Personal Demographic Service which records date of death and evidence of survival beyond day 7 of admission via the ED. Patients whose outcome was unknown beyond the date of discharge were censored on the date of discharge. Patients who died more than 168 hours after arrival were counted as survivors. For patients who were discharged and died by day 7, the time to death was counted in full days and converted to hours.

Data collected between 1 January 2021 and 31 October 2025 were collated. Implausible results for the variables used to calculate the REDS and NEWS2 scores were checked against the source data and any errors were corrected. The scores attributed to the variables that make up the NEWS2 score were also checked and any errors corrected, as was the calculation of the NEWS2 score. Patients on oral anticoagulants were given a score of 0 for INR when calculating the REDS and qREDS scores.

Patients missing a lactate, serum albumin and or an INR were excluded to facilitate complete case analysis. Patients who remained inpatients were also excluded. Patients who had opted out of data use were identified by the hospital’s information department and excluded. The data were anonymised prior to analysis.

Data analysis

The calculation of the qREDS score was similar to the calculation of the REDS score but excluding the RH scores. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed for the REDS, qREDS and NEWS2 scores for in-hospital mortality. The area under the ROC (AUROC) curves were calculated and compared. The comparison of the AUROC curves were repeated after dividing the study population into those with and without the SCs. The test-characteristics for all three scoring systems were studied for the population as a whole, for those with and without the SCs, at the commonly used score thresholds; ≥1, ≥3, ≥5 and ≥7. In patients with RH, the mortality rates associated with the different score bands in the different scoring systems were also studied. For those who died, the time to death from the time of arrival was calculated. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were constructed for the whole population for survival at 168 hours from arrival in the ED for the three scoring systems and the log-rank test was performed. Survival rates at 24, 48, 72 and 168 hours were noted for the population as a whole and the different scores at a score of ≥5. The mean time to death together with the SE were noted for the different score bands of the different scores.

Sample size

10 outcomes per variable, is the required minimum sample size.21 With eight variables, the REDS score had the largest number of variables. Thus, the study population or any subpopulation studied should consist of a minimum of 80 deaths.

Statistical analysis

MedCalc Statistical Software V.23.3.7 (MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium; https://www.medcalc.org; 2025) was used for statistical analysis. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test was used to test continuous data for normality. Normally distributed data are presented as mean and SD. Data that was not normally distributed is presented as median and IQR. Unpaired t-tests were used to analyse normally distributed data and non-normally distributed data were analysed using Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical data were compared using the χ2 test or the Fisher’s exact test. The AUROC curves were compared using the DeLong method.22 Statistical significance was accepted when p<0.05.

All variables were available for calculating the REDS, qREDS and NEWS2 scores, enabling complete-case analysis. Normality was rejected in all continuous variables.

Among patients with RH, significantly fewer patients with the SCs (35.1%) were admitted to the ICU compared with those without the SCs (87.3%; p<0.0001). Among those with RH, the mortality rates in those with the SCs was 51.1% compared with 32.9% in those without the SC, p=0.02. Of the 173 patients with RH, 94 had vasopressors commenced in the ED and were transferred to the ICU, together with 13 who did not have vasopressors commenced in the ED. Of the remaining 66 patients, 60 had a ‘ward-based ceiling of care’ decision at the point of admission to the ward. The remaining six patients had resolution of hypotension after further fluids or time.

iii. Survival probability at 168 hours:

Of the 212 in-hospital deaths noted by day 7, 204 were noted to have occurred within 168 hours of arrival in ED. Eight deaths occurred between 171 hours and 183 hours after arrival. A further five deaths were noted to have occurred within 7 days of ED attendance; one had been transferred to another hospital and four had been commenced on an end-of-life pathway in hospital. 19 patients were censored at discharge (within 7 days).

Figure 3 illustrates the Kaplan-Meier survival curves at 168 hours for the three scoring systems, all log-rank test p<0.0001. However, the survival probability at 24, 48, 72 and 168 hours (online supplemental material table S2) of the NEWS2 score ≥5 was similar to that of the overall population, unlike the REDS ≥5 and qREDS ≥5 scores which were associated with substantially lower survival probability. Similar findings were noted in the mean survival time (online supplemental table S3).

iv. In-hospital mortality rates and time to death:

Online supplemental table S4 presents mortality rates and median time to death from arrival in the ED for the different score bands in the three scores in all three populations. Patients with a REDS or qREDS score of ≥5 had a median time to death of around 132 hours after arrival, with 25% dying within 48 hours of arrival. Unlike the NEWS2 score, the REDS and qREDS scores were associated with progressively shorter time to death as scores increased.

Patients without the SCs had an increase in the time to death when the REDS (or qREDS) scores were ≥7 compared with those with a score of 5–6. This reverse in trend was not apparent in those with the SCs. Of note, in the population of patients with a REDS score of ≥7, 20.5% of those with the SCs and 84.8% of those without the SCs were admitted to the ICU; p<0.0001; the mortality rate in those admitted to ICU with a REDS score of ≥7 was 50% in those with the SCs and 41.1% in those without the SCs, p=0.6.

Online supplemental material table S5 reveals the mortality rates associated with the different scores in those who developed refractory hypotension. With increasing REDS and qREDS scores, mortality increased. However, this was not seen in the initial NEWS2 score. Of note, two patients with an initial NEWS2 score of 0 had REDS scores of 5 and 7 and qREDS of 3 and 4. Both died despite being commenced on vasopressors and transferred to the ICU.

Discussion

In this study, we have found that the qREDS score has a prognostic performance for in-hospital mortality that is similar to the REDS score, particularly in those without the SCs. Both the qREDS score and the REDS score were significantly better than the NEWS2 score for stratifying ED patients with suspected sepsis for in-hospital mortality. The prognostic performance of the REDS and qREDS scores was significantly better in patients without the specified comorbidities than in those with them, whereas the performance of the NEWS2 score did not differ between the two groups. In the overall study population, the cut-off point for the REDS and qREDS scores was ≥3 and that for the NEWS2 score was ≥6. Patients with RH made up only 5.4% of the study population but accounted for 17.1% of the deaths.

The REDS and qREDS scores had the same cut-off point in the study population. This may be explained by the fact that the population of patients with RH was small; despite having a disproportionately high representation in the deceased population, the majority of deaths occurred in those without RH. In addition, in patients with RH, the other constituents of the qREDS score continued to risk-stratify for mortality, as seen in online supplemental table S5. Such stratification for mortality was not found in the initial NEWS2 score. The lack of difference in the AUROC curve between the REDS and qREDS scores in those without the SCs was probably because those with RH were more likely to be admitted to the ICU for ongoing treatment with better outcomes. Therefore, the mortality rate in this very small percentage of the population did not impact the AUROC curve of the qREDS score.

We found that a NEWS2 score ≥5 (the recommended threshold for sepsis screening) had a sensitivity of 78%, comparable to that of a REDS/qREDS score ≥3 (76%). However, specificity at these respective thresholds was higher for the REDS/qREDS score (57%) than for the NEWS2 score (44%). The recommended threshold for mandatory referral to the CCOT is a NEWS2 score of ≥7. At this threshold, we found the NEWS2 score to have a sensitivity of 55% and a specificity of 66%; these test characteristics are not helpful for functioning as a screening tool or for implementing a resource intensive intervention. The latter is best implemented when the specificity is high. A qREDS/REDS score of ≥5 had a sensitivity of 35–40%, a specificity of 90% and a mortality rate of 37%. The NEWS2 score did not achieve comparable mortality rates or specificities across any of the score thresholds studied. Furthermore, the qREDS score of ≥5 was associated with a median time to death of 131.6 hours, with 25% of the deaths occurring within 48 hours of arrival in the ED. These statistics make a qREDS score of ≥5 an ideal threshold for implementing resource-intensive processes, such as a mandatory CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis when the source of infection/sepsis is ill-defined, and for ICU referral. Those with a REDS/qREDS score of 3–4 should be monitored closely for deterioration.

In 2017, Churpeck et al23 studied over 30 000 patients and found the NEWS score had the highest AUROC curve for in-hospital mortality compared with the MEWS, qSOFA and the SIRS criteria. Similarly, Brink et al24 and Lui et al25 have found that the NEWS2 score performed the best compared with the qSOFA score and the Systemic Inflammatory Response Syndrome (SIRS) criteria.

We have previously reported that the REDS score consistently demonstrated significantly greater prognostic performance compared with the NEWS2 score14 15 17 for in-hospital mortality. This finding extends to the qREDS score given its retention of the core discriminative components of the REDS score. Across both short-term and medium-term outcomes, the REDS score outperformed the NEWS2 score for mortality prediction, reinforcing the limitations of relying on physiological derangement alone for sepsis risk stratification. An important additional finding in this study was the clear association between increasing REDS and qREDS scores and progressively shorter time to death. This potentially suggests that these scores may reflect underlying disease momentum or trajectory rather than physiological abnormalities present at the time of assessment alone. In contrast, this relationship was not consistently observed with the NEWS2 score, where the 25th centile for time to death in those with a NEWS2 score of 3–4 was significantly shorter than those with a score of 5–6.

The NEWS score was developed as a standardised physiological early warning escalation tool rather than a sepsis specific prognostic instrument, as outlined by the Royal College of Physicians.10 Systematic review and meta-analysis by Wei et al26 found that the NEWS2 score good discrimination for very short-term mortality but its ability to predict in-hospital and 30-day mortality was more modest. Its reliance on physiological parameters may mean that the NEWS2 score may underestimate risk in patients with early sepsis, where significant underlying pathology may be present despite relatively preserved vital signs. By contrast, use of REDS and qREDS scores suggests that sepsis-derived scores capture relevant information in relation to prognosis, beyond acute physiological abnormality. In the context of sepsis, the consistent superior performance of the REDS and qREDS scores over the NEWS2 score supports their preferential use for prognostic risk-stratification once sepsis is suspected, while NEWS2 remains best suited to the early identification of physiological deterioration.

Practical implementation and future considerations

The REDS and qREDS scores require external validation to confirm generalisability. The qREDS score lends itself to electronic extraction and calculation as it is made up of six dichotomous variables and lactate. Electronic calculation of the score can not only be used to externally validate the qREDS score on a large scale but also to alert the clinician in the contemporaneous clinical setting. Once externally validated, the qREDS score can be readily implemented in any hospital, as it uses routinely measured variables. Therefore, there will be no added cost to implementation, with the exception of a minimal cost of training.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. First, it is a retrospective study of data and as such may be subject to bias and unknown confounders. Second, a large number of patients had to be excluded as they did not have certain blood tests taken. It is likely these patients were less severely ill and led us to study the more severely ill end of the spectrum, resulting in selection bias, but this is a reflection of real-world data. However, it is our view that this would have had a minimum impact on the results and conclusion of the study as the purpose of the study was to compare the prognostic performance of the different scores in the same population. Third, we did not note the final diagnosis to determine if the patient had an infection on admission. However, we have previously noted that the REDS score risk-stratifies all patients, irrespective of the aetiology of their illness.15

Conclusion

The REDS and qREDS scores had significantly better prognostic performance than the NEWS2 score for in-hospital mortality and survival at 168 hours, in patients admitted with suspected sepsis. The qREDS score is a valid surrogate for the REDS score.
