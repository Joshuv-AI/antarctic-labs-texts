Comparative response to pharmacological management of asthma in overweight/obese children versus their counterparts: a systematic review and call for data
Authors: Jose A. Castro-Rodriguez, Samantha H. Averill, M. Isabel Castro-Guevara, Francine M. Ducharme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundObese children with asthma experience higher morbidity than their counterparts; whether different treatments should be considered remains to be clarified.ObjectiveTo explore the potential group differences in the treatment response to any asthma medications between overweight/obese and normal/underweight children.MethodsWe systematically reviewed case–control, cohort studies and randomised controlled trials (RCTs) examining treatment effects by weight status, identified in four bibliographic databases from inception until January 2025. A narrative synthesis was presented because a meta-analysis could not be conducted due to methodological issues.ResultsNine studies (one RCT, five post hoc analyses of RCTs, two cohorts, and one post hoc analysis of a case–control study), including 7089 children (33.9% overweight/obese), met the inclusion criteria. Pharmacological treatment pertained to step 1–2 (n=1), step 2 (n=3), step 3 (n=2), step 5 (n=2), and bronchodilator-response (n=1). Compared with normal weight, step 1–2 showed a trend toward reduced inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) efficiency in minimising symptom days in overweight/obese preschoolers. Regarding step 2 monotherapy, overweight/obese children may benefit as much (or more) from nedocromil or leukotriene receptor antagonist (LTRA) than from ICS. In step 3, adding LTRA (versus placebo) to ICS monotherapy was more effective, whereas higher-dose ICS monotherapy was less effective than LTRA or long-acting β2-agonist (LABA) combination therapy in obese than normal-weight children; adding LABA appears equally effective across weight groups. A similar or better response to omalizumab was observed in obese compared to nonobese children.ConclusionWhile no firm recommendations could be made regarding potential differential treatment responses, the superiority of ICS monotherapy or step-up therapy compared to other controllers appears lost in overweight/obese children.

Introduction

Asthma and obesity are the most common chronic conditions in the paediatric population worldwide. The global prevalence of current asthma was 11.0% in adolescents aged 13–14 years and 9.1% in children aged 6–7 years [1]; in the United States of America (USA), the asthma prevalence in children (0–18 years) was 7.7% in 2023 [2]. The worldwide prevalence of obesity and overweight among children and adolescents aged 5–19 years has risen dramatically, from 8% in 1990 to 20% in 2022 [3]; among children and adolescents in the USA, the increase was 19.7% from 2017 to 2020 [4].

Many studies, including systematic reviews [5, 6], have established an association between obesity and asthma. Being overweight or obese increases the risk of subsequently developing asthma in children, with sex acting as a modifying factor [5, 7, 8]. Approximately 23% to 27% of new asthma diagnoses in children may be related to pre-existing obesity [8]. Indeed, several epidemiological studies suggest that increased body mass index (BMI) or adiposity precedes the development of asthma in children [7, 9]. Asthmatic obese children have more asthma-related symptoms, more frequent exacerbations, and lower quality of life compared with normal-weight asthmatics [10–12]. Conversely, other studies showed that children with poorly controlled asthma have an increased risk of developing obesity [13, 14], suggesting a bidirectional association between asthma and obesity.

Forno et al. [15] were the first to report that, compared to nonoverweight children, overweight/obese children showed a decreased response to inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) in terms of lung function and exacerbations requiring emergency department (ED) visits or hospitalisations in the Childhood Asthma Management Program (CAMP) trial. Suggested explanations for differential treatment responses in obese children include their type 2-low inflammation phenotype, systemic inflammatory profile, different lung mechanics, and comorbidities [10]. Recent guidelines, e.g. Global Initiative for Asthma (GINA) 2024 [16], National Asthma Education and Prevention Program Coordinating Committee 2020 [17], British Thoracic Society/Scottish Intercollegiate Guidelines Network 2024 [18], and Canadian Thoracic Society 2021 [19] have remarked on this apparent lower response to ICS in obese individuals with asthma; however, no specific pharmacological recommendation for this population was mentioned, presumably due to lack of solid evidence, other than standard nonpharmacological management (e.g. weight loss and exercise).

The purpose of this systematic review was to explore and quantify any differential treatment response to any asthma medications between overweight/obese and normal/underweight children.

Methods

Search strategy

Four bibliographic databases (PubMed, Embase, Latin American and Caribbean Literature on Health Sciences and Cochrane Library) were searched from inception to 14 January 2025, using a detailed, comprehensive search strategy (supplementary material S1). The reference lists of relevant studies were reviewed. After removing duplicates, three reviewers (J.A. Castro-Rodriguez, M.I. Castro-Guevara, S.H. Averill) independently screened the retrieved titles and abstracts. All potentially relevant publications were assessed in full text. At each stage, uncertainty or conflict about the eligibility of studies was resolved through discussion or input from another reviewer (F.M. Ducharme). The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (identifier number CRD42024559238), and reporting followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines [20].

The eligibility inclusion criteria were: 1) randomised controlled trials (RCTs), prospective or retrospective cohorts, and case–control studies; 2) participants aged <18 years; and in case the study included children and adults, we consider only those studies where children were analysed separately; 3) children with asthma defined according to international guidelines; 4) use of pharmacological drugs for asthma including ICS, leukotriene receptor antagonist (LTRA), long-acting β-agonist (LABA), long-acting muscarinic antagonist, and/or biological drugs; and 5) reported outcomes comparing overweight/obese versus normal/underweight status by subgroup or multivariate analyses. Exclusion criteria included duplicate or multiple publications of the same study, publication language other than English or Spanish, weight-loss drugs or surgery, lifestyle changes, exercise, nutrients or dietary interventions, and nonstandard pharmacological asthma treatments (e.g. macrolides, statins). Attempts were made to contact corresponding authors for additional clarification or data when needed.

Our two co-primary outcomes included asthma exacerbation of any severity (number or time to the first exacerbation) and asthma symptoms (controlled days or clinical scores), according to reported metrics. Secondary outcomes included 1) exacerbations requiring rescue oral corticosteroids (OCS); 2) exacerbations requiring an acute care visit (including ED visits and hospital admissions); 3) lung function or bronchodilator response (BDR); and 4) adverse effects.

Study extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (F.M. Ducharme, S.H. Averill) assessed each study's risk of bias, using the revised Cochrane risk-of-bias tools for nonrandomised intervention studies (RoB-I) or RCTs (RoB-2), as appropriate [21, 22]. Any discrepancy was resolved through discussion or input from a third author (J.A. Castro-Rodriguez). Rating for each domain was “critical”, “serious”, “moderate” and “low” risk of bias in RoB-I [21], and “low”, “some concerns”, or “high” risk of bias in RoB-2 [22]. The overall risk of bias for each study was driven by the lowest rating affecting two or more domains.

Data analysis and synthesis

Data collection and curation were performed using Microsoft Excel. The intervention and comparator were classified according to the 2024 GINA step therapy [16]. A meta-analysis was planned if at least two studies reported the same outcome metric in similar-aged populations and treatments. Without at least two studies testing the same intervention presenting the same outcomes or metrics, performing a meta-analysis was impossible; a narrative review thus summarised the study findings, focusing on interaction analyses between weight status and intervention, when reported.

Results

Out of 1028 citations, 814 titles and abstracts were reviewed after removing duplicate entries; 96 potentially eligible papers were reviewed as full-text, with 87 citations excluded, primarily due to ineligible design or population (figure 1). Nine studies (n=7089 children, 33.9% overweight/obese) conducted in nonacute ambulatory settings were included [15, 23–29]: five post hoc analyses of RCTs [15, 23, 28–30], two retrospective cohort studies [24, 26], one RCT [25], and one post hoc analysis of a case–control study [27] (table 1). BMI status was classified using Centers for Disease Control and Prevention values in five studies [15, 23, 25, 29, 30], World Health Organization values in two [24, 26] studies, and unspecified reference values in two [27, 28] studies. Tested interventions corresponded to GINA step 1–2 in one [30], step 2 in three [15, 23, 24], step 3 in two [25, 26], step 5 in two [27, 28], and one study for BDR [29]. The risk of bias was “low” in six studies, “moderate” in two, and “high” in one (figure 2).

Step 1–2: episodic versus daily ICS versus placebo

One post hoc study [30] combined data from three large US multicentre RCTs: two 12-month parallel-group trials (Prevention of Early Asthma in Kids (PEAK) and Maintenance and Intermittent Inhaled Corticosteroids in Wheezing Toddlers (MIST)) and one 4-month crossover trial (Individualized Therapy for Asthma in Toddlers (INFANT)). These trials compared various interventions in 736 preschoolers (aged 24–59 months) with mild-to-moderate persistent asthma or recurrent wheezing; 32.5% of participants were overweight/obese (BMI ≥85th percentile) and had significantly lower blood eosinophils than normal-weight children. The post hoc study, at low risk of bias, contributed data to one of our co-primary outcomes (annualised asthma symptom days) and one secondary outcome (exacerbations requiring OCS).

Within each intervention arm, the annualised symptom days and OCS bursts experienced by children allocated to daily ICS (fluticasone propionate (FP) in the PEAK and INFANT trials) or intermittent ICS (FP in the INFANT and nebulised budesonide (BUD) in the MIST trial) showed no significant difference by weight status. In the MIST trial, overweight/obese participants allocated to daily nebulised BUD experienced significantly more OCS bursts than normal-weight participants (p=0.01); no interaction analysis was reported. In the placebo-controlled PEAK trial, among children receiving placebo, overweight/obese children experienced 70% more annualised asthma symptoms days (mean 90.7, 95% CI 61.8–133.2 versus 53.2, 40.1–70.5 days; p=0.020) and 75% more exacerbations requiring OCS (1.4, 1.0–2.1 versus 0.8, 0.6–1.1 exacerbations; p=0.009), compared with normal-weight individuals, after adjusting for race/ethnicity; no such group difference occurred in the normal-weight group. The treatment-by-overweight/obese status interaction p-values approached, but did not reach statistical significance, for annualised asthma symptom days (p=0.065) or rescue OCS (p=0.13).

In the absence of reported or significant interaction, no firm conclusion could be made about the effect of weight status on the relative effectiveness of daily versus intermittent ICS and of daily ICS versus placebo. Evidence points to higher morbidity in non-ICS untreated overweight/obese than normal-weight preschoolers, with a trend towards reduced ICS efficiency for minimising asthma symptoms days in overweight children.

Step 2: ICS versus LTRA or nedocromil monotherapy

Three studies contributed to this comparison: two post hoc analyses of RCTs and a retrospective cohort study. A post hoc study [15] of the CAMP trial, a large American three-arm 4-year RCT comparing inhaled BUD, inhaled nedocromil, and placebo in 1027 children aged 5–12 years with mild to moderate asthma; overweight/obese (BMI ≥85th percentile) children represented 31.4% of the cohort. Due to a lack of efficacy in the primary trial, nedocromil was combined with the placebo arm for this post hoc analysis. This post hoc analysis contributes data only to secondary outcomes (i.e. OCS bursts, ED visits/hospitalisations, and lung function/BDR). No significant baseline differences in total IgE levels or eosinophil counts were observed between the BMI groups or treatment arms. This study had a low risk of bias. Compared to placebo/nedocromil, both nonoverweight and overweight/obese children receiving BUD experienced a significant reduction (within weight-status) in the number of OCS bursts reported at each visit (p<0.0001 and p=0.001, respectively); however, BUD reduced the risk of requiring an ED visit/hospital admission by ∼44% (95% CI 20.7–60.9%; p=0.001) among nonoverweight children, but not in overweight/obese children (p=0.97); interaction was not examined. Significant interaction between BMI and treatment was observed in lung function in the adjusted longitudinal analysis; each 1% increase in BMI diminished the response to BUD by ∼0.04% (95% CI 0.02–0.02) in forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) and by 0.025% (95% CI 0.0001–0.005) in BDR; no significant interaction was observed for FEV1 % predicted.

The second post hoc study [23] combined two American RCTs (CAMP and Asthma Clinical Research Network (ACRN)/Childhood Asthma Research and Education (CARE)) involving children aged 5–12 years. In this study, the CAMP trial (described earlier) was analysed as per the three original allocation groups (BUD, nedocromil, or placebo); in the ACRN/CARE trial, 669 children with mild-to-severe asthma were randomised to ICS or montelukast. Obesity, defined as a BMI ≥95th percentile, was observed in 17.8% and 14.3% of children in the CAMP and ACRN/CARE trials, respectively. The study contributed data to a co-primary outcome (asthma control based on symptoms, short-acting β-agonist (SABA) use, and exacerbations; although defined slightly differently across trials) and to several secondary outcomes (table 1). This study was at low risk of bias. After ∼1 year of treatment in the CAMP study, a greater proportion of obese children were well-controlled with nedocromil than with BUD (52.6% versus 16.0%, p=0.008) or with placebo (52.6% versus 20.9%, p=0.01); in contrast, more nonobese children with asthma were well-controlled with BUD than with nedocromil (48.6% versus 33.8%, p=0.015) or placebo (48.6% versus 34.2%, p=0.016). No interaction analysis was reported. In the ACRN/CARE dataset, there were no significant differences in the proportion of obese children who became well-controlled asthma with ICS or montelukast (14.3% versus 7.9%, p=0.3); in contrast, in the nonobese group, significantly more children became well-controlled with ICS than montelukast (33.6% versus 18.9%, p=0.01); no interaction analysis was reported [23].

In the retrospective Canadian cohort [24] using comprehensive clinical and administrative provincial databases, 518 children aged 2–18 years with specialist-diagnosed asthma initiating with, or continued, on low dose-ICS or LTRA (montelukast or zafirlukast) monotherapy, were assessed for “time to management failure” (i.e. any step-up in therapy, acute care visit, hospitalisation, or OCS for asthma, whichever occurred first), an outcome linked to our secondary outcomes. The study adjusted for multiple demographic characteristics. Children were classified into a normal-weight (BMI ≤80th percentile) and an excess-weight (BMI >80th percentile) group, with the latter comprising 49.4% of the population. The effect of increasing BMI percentile was also examined. The study had a moderate risk of bias. A statistically significant interaction between weight status and time to management failure was observed overall and in the LTRA group. Overall, irrespective of treatment, the risk of management failure increased by 5% (HR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01–1.10) for every 10-unit increase in the BMI percentile. A 17% reduction in time-to-failure (e.g. benefit) was observed for every 10-unit increase in BMI percentile among LTRA users (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.70–0.99), with a significant interaction; no relationship was observed in low-dose ICS users (HR 0.95, 95% CI 0.86–1.04). When weight status was dichotomised, excess-weight children displayed trends towards lower odds of management failure on LTRA compared to normal-weight individuals (HR 1.14, 95% CI 0.61–2.13 versus 1.72, 1.01–2.93); the opposite was observed for low-dose ICS monotherapy, with a trend towards higher odds of treatment failure in excess-weight versus normal-weight children (HR 1.21, 95% CI 0.84–1.74 versus 0.96, 0.62–1.48). The authors concluded that, whereas low-dose ICS was more effective than LTRA at prolonging time-to-management failure in normal-weight children, ICS and LTRA monotherapies appeared equally effective (despite a trend in disfavour of ICS in children with higher BMI), suggesting that LTRA achieved the best response at higher BMI percentile.

Although ICS was superior to alternative controllers in combined weight groups and in nonobese children, collectively these findings support a loss of ICS superiority in their counterparts' children. Consequently, overweight/obese children could benefit more or as much from nedocromil (particularly for lung function) or LTRA, than from ICS monotherapy. The conclusion is supported by several significant interaction terms, derived from two low-risk (RCT) studies and a moderate-risk (cohort) study.

Step 3

LTRA+medium/high-dose ICS versus medium/high-dose ICS

In a small double-blind RCT [25], 26 school children and adolescents with mild-to-moderate persistent early-onset/atopic asthma received montelukast or placebo for 24 weeks, in addition to medium- to high-dose ICS. The trial contributed to one coprimary outcome (asthma control score) and one secondary outcome (lung function). This trial was at high risk of bias, in part due to its small sample size and high attrition (38.5%). The Asthma Control Test score at end-point (24 weeks) was significantly higher among overweight/obese children (BMI ≥85th percentile) receiving montelukast versus placebo adjunct therapy (score 25.0 (n=4) versus 15.7 (n=4), p<0.01), but showed no significant group benefit among normal-weight children (24.0 (n=4) versus 20.5 (n=4), p=0.30). The percentage peak expiratory flow (PEF) was also significantly higher at the end-point in the montelukast group compared to the placebo group among normal-weight children (108.1% versus 74.4%, p<0.02), but not among overweight/obese children (100.2% versus 92.8%, p=0.6). No weight-based group differences in other spirometry parameters (e.g. FEV1, FEV1/FVC, forced expiratory flow at 25–75% of FVC), or biomarkers (i.e. exhaled nitric oxide fraction (FENO), urinary leukotriene E4) were observed at the end-point. Although the authors concluded that adding LTRA, compared with placebo, to medium/high-dose ICS may be more effective in overweight/obese than in normal-weight children, interactions between treatment and weight subgroup were not statistically significant for any outcomes. As no firm conclusion could be drawn, the findings suggest the superiority of LTRA over placebo as adjunct therapy to ICS in obese children only.

LTRA or LABA + low/medium-dose ICS versus medium/high-dose ICS

A retrospective cohort study [26] was conducted using provincial clinical and administrative databases in Canada on 328 children (aged 2–18 years) with specialist-confirmed persistent asthma, initiated on medium/high-dose ICS monotherapy (n=231) or low/medium-dose ICS with LTRA (montelukast or zafirlukast) or LABA (n=97). This study had a moderate risk of bias. This study reported time-to-first exacerbation (i.e. ED visit, hospitalisation, or OCS, whichever occurred first), one of our secondary outcomes. Cox models, adjusted for multiple demographic characteristics, were used to estimate the effect of obesity and treatment on time-to-exacerbation. Obesity was defined as BMI >97th percentile (30.2% of the population). Compared with nonobese children/adolescents, the HR of exacerbation was significantly higher in obese children (1.67, 95% CI 1.41–1.98), irrespective of therapy and adherence, than their counterparts. Of interest, compared with nonobese nonadherers, the HR for obese nonadherers was 1.54 (95% CI 0.97–2.45), suggesting higher morbidity in obese than nonobese children not taking adjunct therapy to ICS. The HR of exacerbation for higher dose ICS monotherapy among nonobese and obese children was 0.58 (95% CI 0.37–0.91) and 0.85 (95% CI 0.47–1.52), respectively, whereas the HR for combination therapy (ICS+(LRTA or LABA)) among nonobese and obese children was 0.46 (95% CI 0.23–0.92) and 0.50 (95% CI 0.13–1.89), respectively. As the interaction was not statistically significant, these findings supported (without proving) a greater protective effect of higher-dose ICS in nonobese than in obese children, with a similar protective effect from adjunct therapy in both weight groups. The authors concluded that obesity might be a determinant of shorter exacerbation-free time and that combination therapy of ICS with LABAs or LTRAs could confer a greater benefit, than higher-dose ICS monotherapy, in obese children/adolescents; combination therapy appeared equally effective across weight groups, although a differential response to step 3 therapies by obesity status could not be ruled out, due to lack of precision [26]. Collectively, the findings suggest that ICS combination therapy with LTRA/LABA is at least as effective as (or more) effective than higher dose ICS, in obese versus nonobese individuals.

Step 5: biological drugs (omalizumab)

Two post hoc studies of RCTs at a low risk of bias contribute data to this step.

The post hoc analysis [27] of the Inner-City Anti-IgE Therapy for Asthma (ICATA) trial included 318 children and adolescents (aged 6–20 years) with persistent allergic asthma, treated with monthly subcutaneous omalizumab versus placebo, as an adjunct to guidelines-based therapy for 60 weeks. This trial evaluated both co-primary outcomes at each 4-week injection visit: number of symptomatic days in the previous 2 weeks and exacerbation rate. Obesity was defined as a BMI ≥25 kg·m−2 (27.9% of the population). The interaction between BMI and treatment groups was reported. Although reductions in the number of symptomatic days per week and in ICS dose between omalizumab and placebo were statistically significant only in nonobese individuals, the interaction was not significant. In contrast, compared to placebo, obese children receiving omalizumab experienced a significantly greater reduction in monthly exacerbations (OR 3.17, 95% CI 1.73–2.6), with a significant interaction (p=0.03) compared to nonobese individuals (OR 1.46, 95% CI 1.00–2.12). Of note, BMI was defined by an absolute value rather than a z-score or percentiles.

The second post hoc analysis [28] involved three placebo-controlled, multicentre trials (IA05, ICATA, and Preventative Omalizumab or Step-up Therapy for Fall Exacerbations (PROSE)) in children aged ≥6 years, assessing omalizumab response stratified by BMI, eosinophil count, FENO, and baseline severity indicators, using Poisson regression. Only the PROSE trial (n=342 inner-city children aged 6–11 years with moderate to severe asthma) provided information on BMI stratification and could be considered in this review. The PROSE trial was a three-arm, randomised, double-blind, double-placebo-controlled, multicentre trial conducted among participants receiving ongoing guideline-based asthma care. Obese children (BMI ≥85th percentile) were 48.2% of the population. This post hoc study included two of our secondary outcomes: exacerbations requiring OCS and hospitalisations within 90 days following the first day of the school year. Relative exacerbation rate (omalizumab rate-placebo rate/placebo rate) showed a numerically, but not statistically significant, greater reduction with omalizumab compared with placebo (−48%, 95% CI −74.2–3.2% in the obese, versus −12%, 95% CI −58.8–80.3% in the nonobese, groups); although not statistically different, there was also a trend towards a greater reduction in asthma exacerbations with omalizumab in obese children. No interaction analysis was presented.

In conclusion, in one post hoc study, obese children experienced a greater reduction in exacerbation with omalizumab than nonobese children, with a clear interaction between intervention and weight group; a similar favourable trend, not reaching statistical significance, was observed in the second RCT. Collectively, the data suggest a similar or superior impact of omalizumab in obese children compared to their counterparts.

BDR in children on ICS versus LTRA versus ICS+LABA

To examine if obesity influences the response to albuterol and contributes to worse asthma control or the need for more asthma controller medications, a post hoc analysis of two identical, parallel, case–control studies (Study of African Americans, Asthma, Genes, and Environment II and Genes-environments and Admixture in Latino Americans II) among Black and Latino children and adolescents with moderate persistent asthma (n=2963, aged 8–21 years) was performed [29]. This study was at low risk of bias.

A positive BDR was defined as a ≥12% increase and a ≥200 mL increase in FEV1. Children received one of three controller therapies: ICS or LTRA monotherapy, or ICS+LABA combination therapy. Obese children (BMI ≥95th percentile) comprised 36% of the population. After adjusting for age, race/ethnicity, sex, recruitment site, baseline lung function (FEV1/FVC), and controller medication, the odds of being bronchodilator-unresponsive were 24% higher (OR 1.24, 95% CI 1.03–1.49; p=0.025) among obese compared with nonobese counterparts. Of note, while asthma controller medication use differed by BMI, there were no significant differences in ICS use across weight categories in the bronchodilator-unresponsive group, and no significant association was observed between controller medication and obesity status in the bronchodilator-responsive group. The authors concluded that, among Black and Latino asthmatic children and adolescents, obesity was associated with more bronchodilator-unresponsiveness than in nonobese children [29]; however, no interaction analysis was presented.

Discussion

This systematic review of nine trials summarises the evidence regarding a potential differential response to standard pharmacological treatment between overweight/obese and normal-weight children, primarily with regard to asthma control and exacerbations. Although most trials were at low risk of bias, the multiple interventions and control therapies, as well as the variability in the definition of overweight and/or obesity, reported outcomes and metrics, precluded a quantitative meta-analysis; the inconsistent reporting of interaction terms between treatment response and BMI further limits firm conclusions. Yet, in most studies, overweight/obese children allocated to placebo displayed higher morbidity; they also generally displayed a greater response to LTRA (or nedocromil) as mono- or adjunct therapy compared to ICS than the normal-weight children, in whom ICS superiority is consistent.

In terms of steps 1–2 therapy, no significant difference in responsiveness between daily and intermittent ICS compared to placebo was observed for overweight/obese and normal-weight preschoolers, regarding annualised asthma symptom days or OCS bursts [30]. Compared to other monotherapies, daily ICS was consistently superior in normal-weight children, but this superiority decreased with increasing weight. In contrast, overweight/obese children displayed at least a similar response to nedocromil or LTRA than to ICS monotherapy, displaying significant weight×controller interaction. In other words, children with excess weight experienced less acute care resource utilisation (ED visits/hospitalisation) and greater improvement in lung function with nedocromil than BUD and placebo, compared to normal/overweight children [15, 23] Compared to placebo, children with excess weight showed a greater response to LTRA monotherapy compared to their counterparts, with every 10-unit increase in BMI prolonging by 17% the time-to-treatment failure; no such effect was observed for ICS [24]. Collectively, the data confirm the long-known superiority of ICS over LTRA and nedocromil, overall and in normal-weight children; however, this ICS advantage appears lost in overweight/obese children, with accumulating evidence suggesting similar or perhaps greater efficacy of LTRA (or nedocromil) compared to ICS, as was recently reported [31]. Consideration of favouring montelukast use should include the potential for mood and sleep problems declared by the US Food and Drug Administration [32]. Those neuropsychiatric effects were not specifically reported in the included studies. Therefore, the use of montelukast needs to be balanced on an individual basis.

In adults, conflicting findings were reported for step-2 therapies. Similar to our findings, a post hoc analysis [33] of four RCTs in the USA enrolling 3073 patients aged >15 years with moderate asthma, randomised to placebo, montelukast, or beclomethasone monotherapy, showed a BMI-by-treatment group interaction. Compared to placebo or montelukast, ICS response for all end-points (i.e. asthma control days, FEV1, β2-agonist use, and nocturnal awakening) was lower with increasing BMI points, with a significant interaction only for asthma control days and night awakening; in contrast, the response to montelukast remained stable across BMI categories. The interaction for asthma control was not statistically significant in subgroup analyses (normal-weight, overweight, and obese children), but it was statistically significant when the latter two weight categories were combined. In contrast, another post hoc analysis [34] of two RCTs in the USA of 1052 patients aged >15 years with mild-to-moderate persistent asthma, randomised to fluticasone or montelukast for 12 weeks, reported that patients on fluticasone displayed significantly greater improvement in FEV1, morning PEF, symptoms score, and less albuterol use than montelukast, irrespective of BMI categories [34]. In conclusion, the loss of superiority of ICS over other monotherapies observed in children is supported by some, but not all, adult studies.

As for adjunct therapy to ICS in children and adolescents, we identified a small RCT at a high risk of bias [25] and a large retrospective study [26]. Those studies suggest that adding LTRA to medium/high-dose ICS monotherapy is more effective in obese than nonobese children; whereas a medium/higher ICS dose may be more effective in normal-weight than obese individuals, with combination therapy (LABA or LTRA with low/medium-dose ICS) appearing equally effective across all weight classes. A post hoc analysis on adults (n=1510) of four trials examining responses to asthma therapy by BMI [35], showed that fluticasone propionate/salmeterol produced greater improvements compared to montelukast in all asthma outcomes (FEV1, asthma symptom score, and albuterol use) at 12 weeks, over the entire BMI range, with statistically significant differences noted among normal-weight, overweight, obese-1, and obese-3 subjects. Because a higher ICS dose was not used in a comparison group in adults, the paediatric findings cannot be directly compared to those in adults.

There are physiological as well as behavioural hypotheses that could explain why the superiority of ICS over alternative non-ICS non-LABA controllers, used as mono- or adjunct therapy, appears lost in obese children. First, a recent study [36] assessing peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) in obese children with asthma reported that dexamethasone-induced CD4+ FKBP51 expression is uniquely associated with worse asthma control, suggesting corticosteroid resistance in this population. Similarly, obese asthmatic adults show a reduced ability of dexamethasone to induce mitogen-activated protein kinase phosphatase-1 (a glucocorticoid-responsive gene) in PBMC and bronchoalveolar lavage cells [37]. A recent study [38], using single-cell RNA sequencing, reported on CD4+ T-cell programming that underlies nonatopic T-cell responses, including type 1 T-helper cell (Th1) polarisation with CDC42 upregulation, in paediatric obesity-related asthma; also, the NR3C1, coding for the glucocorticoid receptor (GR), was downregulated, while genes coding for NLRP3 inflammasome were upregulated, in clusters with CDC42 upregulation and Th1 responses. Previous studies showed that interleukin (IL)-17 contributes to steroid resistance in obese asthma by upregulating GR-β, a dominant-negative inhibitor of GR-α, the classical active receptor for steroids [39], in adipocytes and serum from obese adults with asthma [40]. No systematic review on the biological phenomena was found. Second, a behaviour hypothesis could also be at play, with a higher unintentional or intentional nonadherence to ICS in overweight than in normal-weight asthmatic children [26]. As shown in a large real-life retrospective paediatric Canadian cohort [41], in which asthmatic children with excess weight (BMI >85th percentile) displayed a trend toward a 53% increased risk of primary nonadherence observed in children newly prescribed low-dose ICS and towards a 24% increased risk of secondary nonadherence in children initiating medium/high-dose ICS. Consistently, another post hoc analysis of a cross-sectional study [42] conducted in Dutch asthmatic children reported that compared with normal-weight children with BMI ≥85th percentile showed a trend toward 54% increased odds of parent-reported general nonadherence (intentional and unintentional) and 71% increased odds of objectively documented general nonadherence in those with moderate-to-severe asthma; odds of intentional nonadherence showed a trend toward higher values in children with excess weight than in those with normal weight [42]. Collectively, three studies [26, 41, 42] suggest that nonadherence may be a potential contributor, not only to higher morbidity, but also to lower ICS response in obese children with asthma. Unfortunately, adherence reported in the remaining studies included in this systematic review was not considered.

Other plausible contributing factors to the reduced effectiveness of ICS and other therapies in children with overweight/obesity, apart from adherence, that were not consistently measured across the included studies, are psychosocial barriers, inhaler techniques, comorbidity burden, and complex treatment regimens. Also, in the context of obesity, treating asthma is made more difficult by the common comorbidities that are linked to obesity, such as deconditioning, gastro-oesophageal reflux and obstructive sleep apnoea, as well as the higher prevalence of mental health disorders in obese individuals [43]. Children with asthma and obesity are often thought to be difficult to treat and may respond less well to preventative treatment with controller drugs such as ICS. The documented alterations in airway mechanics associated with obesity, such as dysanapsis and peripheral airway dysfunction, which may be developmental or anatomical rather than necessarily connected to airway inflammation, may at least partially account for this. Other possible pathways include systemic inflammation associated with obesity, non-type 2 airway inflammation, and so on. Additionally, children with obesity who are hospitalised for asthma are more likely to require mechanical breathing and typically stay longer [43].

As for step 5, two post hoc paediatric analyses showed a similar or superior impact of omalizumab in obese children compared with nonobese children [27, 28]. A recent review [44] of retrospective or post hoc analysis of RCTs demonstrated that most monoclonal antibodies, particularly omalizumab, are generally effective in overweight or obese asthmatic adults; however, notable limitations and heterogeneity in findings preclude definite conclusions.

Finally, regarding response to SABA, among Black and Latino children with moderate persistent asthma treated with ICS or LTRA monotherapy, or ICS+LABA combination therapy, obese participants had significantly lower BDR than nonobese participants [29]. This aligns with an earlier study reporting decreased BDR with increased BMI, in primarily non-Hispanic white children, especially girls [45]. In adults with poorly controlled asthma, a trend towards a lower prevalence of BDR in obese than nonobese individuals was observed [46]. Similarly, in adults with severe asthma, those with insulin resistance had lower FEV1 responses to β-adrenergic agonists and to systemic corticosteroids than those without insulin resistance [47]. Finally, a study in obese and nonobese adults with and without asthma concluded that a high-fat diet increased neutrophilic inflammation and decreased the BDR response in obese asthmatics, thus suggesting a mechanistic explanation for these observations [48].

Of note, the observed lower response to bronchodilators (and ICS) among overweight/obese than normal-weight children raises concerns about a potential differential response to an anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) (ICS+SABA or formoterol). As this is the preferred reliever strategy for adolescents and adults recommended by the GINA report [16], a post hoc analysis of the effectiveness of airway AIR by weight status is thus urgently needed, and if replicated, understanding the mechanisms of poorer response to bronchodilators or AIR in obese individuals would be crucial.

We acknowledge the following strengths and limitations. We conducted an extensive literature review, strictly adhering to best practice regarding methodology and risk-of-bias assessment and data extraction, yet, due to the small number of studies with various designs, outcome selection and metrics, and definitions of overweight/obesity, a meta-analysis could not be performed; the inconsistent reporting of weight×treatment interaction further limited the ability to draw firm conclusions (particularly with LABA). Of note, we conservatively interpreted interaction terms using p<0.05, acknowledging that thresholds of ≥0.10 are increasingly used [49]. We purposely did not cover other important aspects of obesity management, including comorbidities, lifestyle changes, exercise, surgical weight loss, nutrients and dietary supplementation, and drugs for insulin resistance or metabolic syndrome, including metformin and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, nor new therapeutic targets under investigation (e.g. IL-6, arginine metabolites, nitro-fatty acids, and mitochondrial antioxidants) [43, 44].

Implications for practice

As the relative advantage of inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) is reduced in obese/overweight compared to normal-weight children, we suggest considering: LTRA as monotherapy instead of ICS in overweight/obese children (grade C), particularly if suboptimal response to ICS.LTRA or LABA as adjunct therapy to ICS instead of higher dose ICS in overweight/obese children (grade C), particularly if suboptimal response to, or concerns about side-effects of higher-dose ICS.

Future directions

Since substantial data confirmed the higher asthma morbidity of overweight/obese children compared to their counterparts, and considering that prevalence of excess weight is likely to increase in the future, it is imperative that future trials consider stratified randomisation and/or analysis, as well as documentation of adherence, by weight status (e.g. approved overweight/obesity threshold or by BMI-point increase), and adhere to standardised reporting of results, using common outcomes and metrics, ideally with accessible data for individual patient data meta-analysis.

Conclusion

Accumulating evidence supports, but does not firmly prove, the loss of ICS superiority over LTRA and nedocromil monotherapy and the greater benefit of adjunct therapy compared to increasing ICS dose, in asthmatic children with increasing BMI. A similar or better response to omalizumab was observed in obese compared to nonobese asthmatic children. The apparent poorer response to short-acting bronchodilator reported in obese asthmatic children merits further investigation.
