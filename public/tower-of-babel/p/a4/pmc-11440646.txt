Differentially Induced Autophagy by Engineered Nanomaterial Treatment Has an Impact on Cellular Homeostasis and Cytotoxicity
Authors: Victor Alcolea-Rodriguez, Verónica
I. Dumit, Rico Ledwith, Raquel Portela, Miguel A. Bañares, Andrea Haase
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Considering the increasing production of engineered nanomaterials (ENMs), new approach methodologies (NAMs) are essential for safe-by-design approaches and risk assessment. Our aim was to enhance screening strategies with a focus on reactivity-triggered toxicities. We applied in vitro tests to 10 selected benchmark ENMs in two cell models, lung epithelial A549 and differentiated THP-1 macrophage-like cells. Previously, we categorized ENMs based on surface reactivity. Here we elucidated their reactivity-triggered cytotoxicity and mode of action using the WST-1 assay (metabolic activity), LDH assay (cell membrane integrity), autophagosome detection, and proteomics. Nonreactive SiO2 NM-200 showed no significant impact on cell viability. Conversely, highly reactive CuO and ZnO (NM-110 and NM-111) disrupted cell homeostasis. Interestingly, moderately reactive TiO2 (NM-101 and NM-105) and CeO2 (NM-211 and NM-212), apparently without an adverse effect, induced autophagosome formation, evidencing autophagy as a defensive mechanism. Our improved in vitro testing strategy, combined with state-of-the-art reactivity information, screens ENMs for potential reactivity-triggered toxicity.

The steadily increasing number of engineered nanomaterial (ENM) types, which, moreover, can be produced in multiple variants by altering parameters such as size or surface treatment, presents a formidable challenge to the conventional paradigm of hazard and risk assessment.1 The main aspect of this challenge lies in the reliance on animal experimentation for evaluating substance safety, as each ENM variant requires individual evaluation, consuming significant resources, experimental animal assays, and time.2 Conducting animal studies for every new material becomes impractical and resource-intensive. Moreover, the European Union is committed to the 3R principle of animal testing (replacement, reduction, and refinement). Therefore, there is a pressing need for new approach methodologies (NAMs), in particular for highly innovative fields such as nano- and advanced materials.3−5 NAMs are integrated through specific testing strategies, facilitating the elucidation of the mode of action of ENMs (MoA). Understanding this MoA enables the development of adverse outcome pathways (AOPs), upon which integrated approaches to testing and assessment (IATAs) are constructed.6 Key aspects in these nanospecific AOPs are “ROS generation”, “oxidative stress”, and “persistent inflammation”.7−10

Assays to recognize reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation,11−13 like 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin (DCFH2)14−18 oxidation or thiol consumption,19,20 or to evaluate the number and nature of surface active sites, such as methanol chemisorption and its temperature-programmed surface reaction (MeOH-TPSR),21−25 offer insights into this aspect, and their results may be used to categorize ENMs based on their chemical properties and, eventually, predict their reactivity-triggered toxicity. However, although reactivity is an extrinsic property considered a key parameter to understanding ENMs’ behavior,26,27,20,28 many possible mechanisms are reported to trigger events leading to toxicity.29−31 Consequently, in vitro test strategies are required to predict the effects of ENMs in contact with living organisms by unraveling their mode of action. Moreover, certain ENMs with reactivity different than redox, such as TiO2,32 generate ROS,19,20 but do not present cytotoxicity according to cell viability assays.33−35 Contrary to TiO2, CeO2 presents redox reactivity,36,37 but not cytotoxicity, as reflected in cell viability and permeability assays.38 Hence, it is also crucial to understand the mechanisms that cells employ to protect themselves from ENM reactivity. Along this line, autophagy is a nonapoptotic process aiding in cell survival under nutrient shortage or acting as a death pathway, when apoptosis is blocked, by degrading cellular components. Triggered by external insults or defective proteins and organelles, autophagy responds to oxidative stress, inflammation, hypoxia, infections, and endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress,39−42 conditions that can be well triggered by ENMs.43−45

Given the complexity in risk evaluation of ENMs, this study assessed autophagy to determine the effects of eight representative test nanomaterials from JRC (Joint Research Center) repository, on human lung epithelial A549 and dTHP-1 (human leukemia monocytic cell line THP-1, differentiated into macrophage-like) cells, emphasizing inhalation as the main exposure route.46−49 The ENMs selected from the JRC repository comprised four substances, each in two variants, that have been extensively characterized and represent four commercially important material categories: titanium dioxide (TiO2), cerium dioxide (CeO2), zinc oxide (ZnO), and silicon dioxide (SiO2), with copper oxide (CuO) included as a positive control.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to directly correlate reactivity-triggered toxicity with measured reactivity in benchmark JRC ENMs and investigate why moderately reactive ENMs show no apparent toxicity, on the hypothesis that autophagy acts as a cytoprotective mechanism against ENMs’ detrimental effects. To achieve this goal, we employed a comprehensive in vitro approach. Cell metabolic activity tests, including water-soluble tetrazolium (WST-1) and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) assays, were used to assess the cytotoxicity. These tests were supplemented with proteomics to provide information about the MoA at subcytotoxic concentrations. Finally, the quantification of autophagosome production confirmed our hypothesis that autophagy acts as a cytoprotective mechanism in A549 and dTHP-1 when exposed to moderately reactive ENMs.

Cytotoxicity in A549 and dTHP-1 Cells

Viability of A549 and dTHP-1 cells, indirectly assessed through LDH release and the WST-1 metabolic activity assay, is summarized in Figure 1. After 24 h of exposure, concentrations up to 100 μg/mL did not induce significant adverse effects (p < 0.05) on any of the cell lines for TiO2 NM-101, TiO2 NM-105, CeO2 NM-211, CeO2 NM-212, SiO2 NM-200, SiO2 NM-201, and Fe2O3 ENMs. However, ZnO NM-110, ZnO NM-111, and CuO exhibited varying levels of cytotoxicity relative to the control across the two cell lines and tests. ZnO NM-110 did not alter LDH release but affected the metabolic activity of A549 cells, from concentrations of 25 μg/mL, and of dTHP-1 cells, from 3 μg/mL, as indicated by the WST-1 assay. For ZnO NM-111, a significant uptick in LDH release was noted at 100 μg/mL in A549 cells (58%), whereas dTHP-1 cells showed no discernible change up to this concentration, and the WST-1 assay revealed a negative impact on the cells at concentrations equal to or higher than 25 μg/mL for A549 (23%) and 3 μg/mL for dTHP-1 (75%). CuO exposure resulted in a notable increase in LDH release at concentrations of 6–25 μg/mL for A549 and 6–12 μg/mL for dTHP-1, decreasing at higher concentrations. The fact that LDH release goes through a maximum value for this EMN is attributable to the interference caused by Cu+ ions on the LDH enzyme, a phenomenon previously documented in the literature.51 Regarding WST-1, metabolic activity decreased significantly and continuously for CuO concentrations from 6 μg/mL (52%) for A549 and from 12 μg/mL (67%) for dTHP-1, being the most toxic of the evaluated ENMs in the series.

Proteomics to Unravel Homeostasis Disruption

Proteomics was used to get further insight into the effects of the 7 ENMs with medium to high reactivity, for which some level of toxicity was observed (see Table S3). Exposure to TiO2 NM-101, TiO2 NM-105, CeO2 NM-211, and CeO2 NM-212 at a concentration of 50 μg/mL did not result in significant protein alterations in A549 or dTHP-1. A comprehensive assessment of exposure to CuO, ZnO NM-110, and ZnO NM-111 ENMs at 3, 6, and 12 μg/mL, respectively (the highest concentration at which cell viability was not significantly affected), showed different results depending on the cell line: in A549 the changes to the proteome were scarce; from the 2885 detected proteins, copper oxide was found to alter just 2, ZnO NM-110 disrupted the equilibrium of 6, whereas ZnO NM-111 had no effect (see details in Table S4). In a contrasting scenario, the proteomic examination of dTHP-1 cells subjected to these ENMs exhibited several alterations (Table 1). From a total of 2712 detected proteins, ZnO NM-110 altered the level of 118, mainly exosomal proteins, whereas ZnO NM-111 affected 409 proteins, mostly related to cadherin binding and ribosome pathways, marking the most significant disruption in the series. CuO exposure caused a cellular stress response and affected ribosomal proteins, influencing a total of 113 protein levels.

Autophagosome Detection

The fluorescence-based test for quantification of autophagosome generation highlighted the pronounced impact of certain ENMs at concentrations for which harmful effects were not detected with LDH and WST-1 assays in any of the two assessed cell lines. The study specifically examined TiO2 NM-101, TiO2 NM-105, CeO2 NM-211, CeO2 NM-212, SiO2 NM-200, SiO2 NM-201, and Fe2O3, medium- to low-reactivity ENMs in the series (see Table S3), at concentrations of 25 and 50 μg/mL (Figure 2). For CuO, ZnO NM-110, and ZnO NM-111, highly reactive materials, dosages were adjusted in line with cytotoxicity data to discern a dose-dependent response while ensuring intact cell viability.

In the A549 cell line, fluorescence readings indicated a notable surge in the autophagosome presence at 25–50 μg/mL for TiO2 NM-101, TiO2 NM-105, CeO2 NM-211, and CeO2 NM-212. Similarly, differentiated THP-1 cells displayed evident changes in autophagosome concentrations with TiO2 NM-101, TiO2 NM-105, and CeO2 NM-212 at 25–50 μg/mL, but only at 50 μg/mL with CeO2 NM-211. The other assessed ENMs did not show any substantial shifts in autophagosome generation when juxtaposed with the control group for both cell lines.

The Reactivity of ENMS Correlates with Cytotoxicity in A549 and dTHP-1 Cells

Existing toxicity data for titanium are in line with our findings: A549 cell viability results for TiO2 NM-101 show no cytotoxic effects.33,52 To the best of our knowledge, there are no reports for this ENM in dTHP-1. Similarly aligned with our results, dTHP-1 and A549 were tested after TiO2 NM-105 exposure by MTT and LDH, without a significant decrease of cell viability at 24, 48, and 80 h exposure.53,54 Regarding ceria and silica nanomaterials, to our knowledge, CeO2 NM-211, and SiO2 NM-201 have not been tested in A549 and dTHP-1 cells previously, but cytotoxicity experiments (LDH, Alamar Blue, and cytokines release) for CeO2 NM-212 in these cell lines after 24 and 80 h of exposure showed no cytokine release and absence of cytotoxicity up to 22 μg/cm2 and 100 μg/mL, respectively.38,54 For SiO2 NM-200, our results—no adverse effects on THP-1 or A549 using both LDH and WST-1 tests—differ from the significant decrease in dTHP-1 cell viability reported after 24 h of exposure to this ENM tested by MTS,55 but are aligned with the literature, whereSiO2 NM-200 is categorized as unreactive.19 Fe2O3 did not present cytotoxicity in our experiments, in line with Lai et al., who observed Fe2O3 internalization after 24 h of exposure at concentrations of 5 μg/mL, but up to 200 μg/mL, no adverse effects, as assessed by the MTT test, or formation of intracellular ROS, tested by using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (DCFH2), were observed.56

CuO from Sigma-Aldrich and ZnO NM-110 and ZnO NM-111 altered the cell viability of A549 and dTHP-1. In the literature, this CuO ENM is described as highly cytotoxic for A549, with cell viability decreasing after 3 and 24 h exposure at concentrations of 10 and 5 μg/mL, respectively, as tested by MTT. In addition, for the same exposure time, CuO produced IL-8 release at 10 μg/mL.57,58 Regarding ZnO ENMs, which are highly soluble (half-life <48 h), concerns regarding particle accumulation are minimal in the literature, and toxicity is primarily associated with ion release, a mechanism that leads to different biological effects compared to exposure to zinc salts.59,60 NM-110 caused a decrease in dTHP-1 cell viability at 50 μg/mL assessed by MTS,55 while other markers such as WST-1, LDH, and resazurin unraveled adverse effects at even lower concentrations (EC50 between 6.4 and 9.5 μg/mL).61 A549 cell viability loss was apparent at 48 μg/mL (EC50 = 76) by resazurin62,63 or 75 μg/cm2 (EC50 = 53 μg/mL) via Alamar blue,64 which is consistent with our data. In the case of NM-111, a significant decrease in A549 cell viability was observed at 30 μg/cm2 (EC50 = 37 μg/mL).64 The high cytotoxicity is consistent with our LDH and WST-1 data, which revealed cytotoxicity at 25 μg/mL.

In summary, the WST-1 assay shows a higher sensitivity than that of LDH for cytotoxicity effects in A549 and dTHP-1 cells by the tested ENMs. This is an expected trend, since the WST-1 assay is an earlier response assay, which measures the metabolic activity of the cell, providing insights into cellular health.65 In contrast, the LDH assay reports the death of the cell. Additionally, the literature suggests that LDH assay results may be subject to interferences arising from interactions between the LDH enzyme and metal ions released by soluble oxides.51,66,67

Highly Reactive ENMs Produce Homeostasis Disruption

Other works19,20 suggest classifying ZnO NM-110, ZnO NM-111, and CuO as the most reactive ENMs in the series (Table S3).20 Moreover, these materials exhibit some degree of solubility,68,69 which may lead to a higher incidence of adverse effects in in vitro and in vivo assays. Therefore, and in light of this information and the observed decrease in cell viability, ZnO NM-110, ZnO NM-111, and CuO proteomic evaluation results substantiate the expected disruption of cellular homeostasis following exposure to these three ENMs. Previous findings on their toxicity mechanisms support our hypothesis. Proteomic studies conducted on a CuO nanomaterial similar to the one employed in our research revealed alterations of proteins associated with oxidative stress in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) and lungs of rats.70 Other reports on CuO have documented inflammation and oxidative stress in cell lines such as A549, BEAS-2B, and dTHP-1.71 Regarding the proteomics results of TiO2 NM-101, TiO2 NM-105, CeO2 NM-211, and CeO2 NM-212, no protein was deregulated in any cell line, so their moderately oxidative capacity (NM-211 and NM-212) or the presence of acid surface sites (NM-101 and NM-105) was not enough to disrupt cellular homeostasis.

Autophagy as a Cytoprotection Mechanism from Reactive ENMs

Profound proteomic shifts are observed with highly reactive ENMs such as CuO and both ZnO (NM-110 and NM-111). After exposure to these oxidant ENMs, cells may not effectively counteract toxicity through mechanisms such as autophagy, leading to a disruption in homeostasis. However, for moderately reactive ENMs, autophagy activation appears to protect them from ENM toxicity. The connection between exposure to ENMs and autophagy has been established in prior studies across various target organs, including the kidney.72 Moreover, it has been identified as a mechanism of cytoprotection, based on the inhibition of autophagosome–lysosome fusion, resulting in the accumulation of autophagosomes and their associated substrates.43 However, our hypothesis posits that autophagy acts as a defensive mechanism vs reactive ENMs to avoid cytotoxic effects, not only in terms of oxidative potential but also for acidic reactivity. This theory provides a plausible explanation for the observed accumulation of autophagosomes in both cell lines following exposure to TiO2 (NM-101 and NM-105) and CeO2 (NM-211 and NM-212). We would anticipate alterations to the proteome, but the proteins corresponding to the autophagy pathway were not detected by our measurements.

Conclusions and Outlook

We present an improved in vitro testing strategy that combines state-of-the-art reactivity information with a set of suitable in vitro assays and screen 10 benchmark JRC ENMs for their reactivity-triggered toxicity. Our work leads to the conclusion that (1) CuO, ZnO NM-110, and ZnO NM-111 induce loss of homeostasis in A549 and dTHP-1 cell lines, in line with the high reactivity reported in the literature; (2) unreactive nanomaterials, specifically SiO2 NM-200 and SiO2 NM-201, do not produce significant effects in both cell lines; and (3) the exposure of A549 and dTHP-1 cell lines to CeO2 NM-211, CeO2 NM-212, TiO2 NM-101, and TiO2 NM-105 leads to a significant accumulation of autophagosomes, compared to other treatments. This suggests that autophagy acts as a protective mechanism, preventing the loss of homeostasis in response to moderately reactive nanomaterials.

The relationship between in chemico reactivity and in vitro toxicity in A549 and dTHP-1 highlights varied modes of action across different engineered nanomaterials. CuO and ZnO (NM-110 and NM-111), characterized by their partial dissolution, are highly reactive in methanol-TPSR and liquid-phase probe reactions for the oxidative potential. This suggests a multifaceted interaction among solubility, surface reactivity, and cytotoxic potential. These highly reactive materials overpower cellular defense mechanisms, leading to a significant disruption in cell homeostasis. This is evidenced by the broad range of proteins impacted in proteomics studies in dTHP-1, with notably affected pathways, including stress response (CuO), ribosomal proteins (CuO and NM-111), exosomal proteins (NM-110), and cadherin binding (NM-111). Contrastingly, other ENMs with moderate reactivity neither exhibit significant reductions in cell viability nor alter cellular homeostasis. Autophagosome quantification conducted to elucidate the cytoprotective strategies employed by cells revealed that, in response to exposure to reactive but noncytotoxic ENMs, cells maintain homeostasis via autophagy.

In conclusion, the combination of assays to evaluate the cytotoxic potential (LDH release and cell viability by WST-1) and autophagosome detection appears to be a useful screening strategy, as confirmed by proteomics, to comprehensively assess the potential impacts of ENMs.
