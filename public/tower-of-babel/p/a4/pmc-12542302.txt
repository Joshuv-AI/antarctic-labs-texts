Calcium dissociation with carbonate ions from Pf‐SCP, sarcoplasmic calcium‐binding protein in Pinctada fucata, contributes to calcium mineralization for shell formation
Authors: Yuto Namikawa, Lingxiao Zhu, Peng Lu, Koji Nagata, Michio Suzuki
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPf‐SCP is an EF‐hand protein identified in Pinctada fucata that is responsible for calcium transport and concentration in the mantle for shell formation. Previous studies have reported the calcium‐binding properties of the EF‐hand domains and the localization of Pf‐SCP. To understand the calcification from Pf‐SCP as a source of calcium, the dissociation of calcium from Pf‐SCP must be investigated. However, calcium dissociation from EF‐hand proteins, particularly in the presence of carbonate ions, remains poorly understood. In this study, we demonstrated that calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP was induced by carbonate ions using the fluorescence spectra of Pf‐SCP, and this was followed by the synthesis of calcium carbonate that was characterized using scanning electron microscope‐energy dispersive X‐ray spectrometry (SEM‐EDS). To gain insight into the calcium dissociation of Pf‐SCP at the atomic level, we conducted molecular dynamics simulations using a multi‐state ion model for calcium ions. The proposed mechanism of calcium dissociation in Pf‐SCP is as follows: Water molecules first replace the amino acids in the EF‐hand domain to coordinate calcium ions. Next, the carbonate ions bind to the calcium ions, decreasing the binding affinity of the EF‐hand domains for the calcium ions. Finally, the calcium ions detach from the EF‐hand, forming a complex with water molecules and carbonate ions. These findings provide a detailed understanding of the structural dynamics of calcium dissociation and the biomineralization mechanism in P. fucata, particularly in relation to the mantle calcification process.Keywords: calcium carbonate, EF‐hand, Pf‐SCP, Pinctada fucata, X‐ray crystallography

INTRODUCTION

Biomineralization is the bioprocess of mineral production that enhances the mechanical properties of invertebrates. Biomineralization involves the formation of biominerals whose structure is precisely controlled by organic matrices. Pinctada fucata, also known as the Japanese pearl oyster, is one of the most extensively studied organisms for biomineralization in terms of its optical properties, physical structure, and biological function. The shells of P. fucata are primarily composed of calcium carbonate and a few organic matrices. Calcium ions, bicarbonate/carbonate ions, and organic matrices are secreted from the mantle epithelial cells (Marie et al. 2012). Certain organic matrices initially form insoluble organic frameworks on which calcium carbonate crystals grow with soluble matrices (Suzuki et al. 2009, 2011).

Pf‐SCP (sarcoplasmic calcium‐binding protein from P. fucata) was identified in the mantle tissue cultured in artificial seawater with 10 times higher concentration of calcium ions than natural seawater (10ASW) (Matsuura et al. 2018). The protein expression level of Pf‐SCP cultured in 10ASW was increased. Pf‐SCP is a 21 kDa protein with two EF‐hand motifs that bind to calcium ions. The immunostaining experiment indicated that Pf‐SCP was localized within the entire mantle epithelial cell, suggesting that Pf‐SCP was responsible for the transport of calcium ions during shell formation.

Sarcoplasmic calcium‐binding proteins (SCPs) belong to the EF‐hand protein family and are only present in invertebrates (Hermann and Cox 1995). The function of SCP is similar to that of vertebrate parvalbumin which accelerates muscle relaxation by binding to calcium ions in the skeletal muscles (White et al. 2011). The EF‐hand motif is characterized by a helix–loop–helix structure (Grabarek 2006; Yap et al. 1999). The calcium‐binding loop of the EF‐hand motif typically consists of 12 amino acids that form a pentagonal bipyramidal geometry around the calcium ion (Babu et al. 1988; Halling et al. 2016; Kumar et al. 2016). This loop coordinates a calcium ion through six ligands, designated X, Y, Z, −X, −Y, and −Z (Figure 1) (Chattopadhyaya et al. 1992). The ligands at positions X, Y, and Z are oxygen atoms of the side chains of Asp or Asn. Position −X is typically occupied by a water molecule, while position −Y is occupied by an oxygen atom of the main chain. Position −Z is a conserved bidentate ligand formed by the side chain of Glu or Asp. The structural dynamics and calcium‐binding properties of EF‐hand proteins have been investigated in previous studies (Ababou and Zaleska 2015; Hu et al. 2020; Immadisetty et al. 2021; Keeler et al. 2013; Zhang et al. 2012). Despite extensive research investigating the calcium‐binding mechanisms of EF‐hand proteins, calcium dissociation from the EF‐hand domains, particularly in the presence of carbonate ions, remains poorly understood. The crystallization of biominerals typically begins in the amorphous phase (Addadi et al. 2003; Grünewald et al. 2022). Acidic organic matrices such as polysaccharides and proteins bind to these amorphous precursors, facilitating the formation of crystalline minerals (De Yoreo et al. 2015; Gal et al. 2016; Suzuki et al. 2009). Consequently, calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP and the subsequent transport of calcium to other organic matrices are critical steps in the crystallization of calcium carbonate. Elucidating the calcium dissociation mechanism from Pf‐SCP is necessary to deepen our understanding of calcium transport and provide new insights into the shell formation of P. fucata.

RESULTS AND DISCUSSION

Overall structure of Pf‐SCP

The final crystal structure of Pf‐SCP was refined at 2.0 Å resolution by the molecular replacement method using the AlphaFold model (PDB ID 9L5M). The data processing and refinement statistics are summarized in Table S1, Supporting Information. The Pf‐SCP crystals contained three monomeric Pf‐SCPs, 290 water molecules, and six calcium ions. The Ramachandran plot of the Pf‐SCP structure indicated that 97.19 and 2.81% of the residues were in favored and allowed regions, respectively. Figure 2 presents a graphical representation of the crystal structure of monomeric Pf‐SCP. Each protein crystal structure of Pf‐SCP contained nine α‐helix domains, four of which form two EF‐hands binding calcium ions. In EF1, the calcium ion was coordinated to the oxygen in the side chains of D16, N18, D20, and D27, the oxygen in the backbone of K22, and a water molecule that formed hydrogen bonds with the oxygen atoms in the side chain of S24. D27 was a bidentate ligand. In EF2, the calcium ion was coordinated to the oxygen atoms in the side chains of D106, N108, D110, E114, and E117 and to the oxygen in the backbone of Q112. E117 was a bidentate ligand. Electrostatic surface representation indicated that two EF‐hands with negative charges serve as calcium‐binding domains.

Calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP revealed with fluorescence spectroscopy

Measurement of intrinsic fluorescence is a typical technique for detecting the overall conformational changes in the EF‐hands induced by calcium ions (Busch et al. 2000; Kuznicki et al. 1995; Lin et al. 2021). A fluorescence study examining centrin‐1, a calcium sensor protein containing four EF‐hand motifs, reported a Trp fluorescence peak shift depending on the calcium concentration (Phanindranath et al. 2021). Pf‐SCP contained sufficient aromatic amino acids for intrinsic fluorescence, including 4 Trp, 7 Tyr, and 12 Phe in 178 full‐length amino acids as represented in Figure S1. Although the EF‐hand domains of Pf‐SCP do not contain aromatic amino acids, the α‐helix regions near the EF‐hand domains contain aromatic amino acids. Calcium dissociation from recombinant Pf‐SCP (rPf‐SCP) was demonstrated using changes in the fluorescence spectra derived from conformational changes within the EF‐hand. Calcium ions coordinated to the EF‐hand in rPf‐SCP were dissociated using carbonate ions and EDTA, a metal chelator often used to remove calcium ions from EF‐hand proteins (Beccia et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 1995). The fluorescence spectra of holo‐ and apo‐rPf‐SCP were measured under excitation at a wavelength of 280 nm (Figure 3). The inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS) measurement revealed the calcium binding ratio of the holo‐ and apo‐rPf‐SCP to be 2.0 ± 0.1 and 0.6 ± 0.2 (mean ± SD, n = 3). Although the binding ratio of apo‐rPf‐SCP was lower than that of the holo‐form, a non‐zero value was observed, likely due to contamination. Compared to holo‐rPf‐SCP, a peak shift was observed in apo‐rPf‐SCP, 50 mM EDTA, and 500 mM Na2CO3, indicating that carbonate ions induced a conformational change in rPf‐SCP by metal chelation in the same manner as that of EDTA. No peak shift was observed for 50 mM Na2CO3, indicating that the chelation capacity of carbonate ions was lower than that of EDTA. Specifically, carbonate ions can cause calcium dissociation at high concentrations in the same manner as EDTA.

Black, red, blue, orange, and green lines indicate holo‐rPfSCP, apo‐rPf‐SCP, rPf‐SCP in 50 mM EDTA, rPf‐SCP in 50 mM Na2CO3, and rPf‐SCP in 500 mM Na2CO3, respectively. 500 mM Na2CO3 induced calcium dissociation in the same manner as apo and EDTA. Three individual measurements were averaged for spectrum calculation.

Since the availability of carbonate ions is highly pH‐dependent, we next investigated the effect of pH. While the peak shift was observed at pH 9, it did not occur when repeated at pH 8, even in the presence of 500 mM NaHCO3 (Figure S2). This finding indicates a sharp pH threshold for calcium dissociation that is specifically triggered by carbonate ions, not bicarbonate ions. The strategy of elevating local pH to modulate carbonate availability is utilized in the biomineralization processes of marine organisms. The foraminifera and coral, for instance, elevate local pH to approximately nine in vivo to increase carbonate ion concentration for calcium carbonate deposition (François et al. 2025; Kubota et al. 2024; Toyofuku et al. 2017; Ujiié et al. 2023).

The canonical EF‐hand motif uses a specific set of acidic residues (D at positions 1, 3, 5, and E at 12) to bind Ca2+ with high affinity (Chernorudskiy et al. 2020). However, Pf‐SCP uses N instead of D at position 3 and, in EF1, D instead of E at position 12. Previous studies have shown that both of these substitutions decrease Ca2+ affinity (Blumenschein and Reinach 2000; Cates et al. 1999; Schreiber and Salkoff 1997; Wu and Reid 1997). Therefore, Pf‐SCP likely possesses a relatively weak affinity for Ca2+, which may trigger calcium dissociation with carbonate ions.

Characterization of calcium carbonate synthesized with rPf‐SCP

Calcium carbonate synthesis was performed to demonstrate that carbonate ions can bind to calcium ions on the EF‐hand of Pf‐SCP and become calcium carbonate crystals. To ensure that Pf‐SCP was a calcium source, free calcium ions in the solution were removed by ultrafiltration. Carbonate ions were supplied to the solution using the ammonium carbonate diffusion method, which is a typical method for supplying carbonate ions to a solution without changing its volume (Montagnani et al. 2011; Okumura et al. 2013; Song et al. 2022). Figure 4a presents an SEM image of the synthesized particles that exhibited a rhombohedral shape with several holes on the surface, which is typically observed in experiments on calcium carbonate synthesis using organic matrices related to biomineralization (Kintsu et al. 2017; Kong et al. 2019). Figure 4b shows the EDS spectra of the elemental composition of synthesized particles in Figure S3, which shows an SEM image of carbon‐coated particles. The C, O, and Ca peaks in the EDS spectra indicate that the precipitate was calcium carbonate. SEM‐EDS characterization revealed that calcium carbonate was synthesized using a calcium source from Pf‐SCP with carbonate ions.

(a) SEM image of synthesized particles with rPf‐SCP. The surface was coated with Au to obtain a high resolution image. Aggregates of rhombohedral crystals were observed. The inset shows the enlarged region circled in red. (b) EDS spectra of synthesized particles shown in Figure S3. The peaks of C, O, and Ca were observed, which proved the synthesized materials were calcium carbonate.

Conformational stability of Pf‐SCP without carbonate ions

First, we assessed the conformational stability of Pf‐SCP simulated from the crystal structure as an initial state. As the conventional force field for calcium ions is inaccurate for protein–calcium interactions due to the complex coordination geometry (Lepšík and Field 2007; Li et al. 2015; Zhang et al. 2021), a multi‐state ion model consisting of a central atom and six dummy atoms was applied for the molecular dynamics simulation (Zhang et al. 2020). Figure S4a presents the root mean square deviation (RMSD) without carbonate ions as a function of time for three repeated simulations. All the crystal structures reached equilibrium with an RMSD value of approximately 0.15 nm during 1 μs, and this represented that the crystal structure of Pf‐SCP was stable over the course of the simulations. Radius of gyration (R g) remained constant at 1.59 nm throughout the simulations as presented in Figure S4b. To provide insight into the flexibility of the individual amino acids in Pf‐SCP, the root mean square fluctuation (RMSF) value was calculated and projected onto the crystal structure for better visualization (Figure S5). α‐helix regions were most stable in Pf‐SCP, with an RMSF value of approximately 0.05. Loop regions between α‐helices represented high fluctuations with RMSF values of over 0.1. In contrast, the two loop regions in the EF‐hand region exhibited relatively low RMSF values (Kucharski et al. 2016; Lindert et al. 2012, 2015). Calcium ions stabilized the loop structure by coordination geometry, and this is consistent with previous results indicating that the absence of calcium ions increased the structural flexibility of the EF‐hand (Henzl and Tanner 2007; Mäler et al. 2002; Zhang et al. 1995).

The distances between calcium ions and each amino acid in the EF‐hand were calculated to analyze the binding mode. Figure 5 presents the distances between the oxygen atoms of the amino acids coordinating the calcium ions in the EF‐hand and the calcium ions as a function of time. The average distances are presented in Figure S6 with standard deviations. In EF1 and EF2, the oxygen atoms in the side chains of Asn, Ser, and Gln showed low coordination occupancy for the calcium ion, whereas those of Asp and Glu as well as the oxygen atoms of the backbone were stable in proximity to the calcium ion. The coordination of oxygen atoms to calcium ions was stabilized by electrostatic interactions. Though fluctuations in the calcium ions were observed at 700 ns in Repeat 1 of EF2, no calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP was observed. Overall, EF2 exhibited a higher mobility of oxygen atoms than did EF1, suggesting lower stability for calcium binding in EF2.

The distances between the oxygen atoms in EF‐hand and the calcium ion as a function of time in the three replicates of 0 M carbonate condition.

To estimate the average contribution of individual amino acids in the EF‐hand to calcium binding, we calculated the binding energy of the Pf‐SCP–calcium ion complex using the MMPBSA method with decomposition analysis. Despite the limitations in regard to accurately calculating absolute binding energies, particularly in calcium‐binding proteins, the MMPBSA method helps to compare binding energies (Basit et al. 2021, 2022; Greenidge et al. 2013; Hou et al. 2011). Figure S7 presents the average binding energies of the individual amino acids. The binding energies were similar among the three replicates in EF1, whereas those in EF2 varied among repetitions. The oxygen atoms in the side chains of acidic amino acids exhibited higher binding energies than those in the backbone such as K22 and Q112. However, N18 and N108 did not contribute to binding due to their positive binding energy values. Asn possesses a positive binding energy value and, therefore, does not contribute to calcium binding. The EF‐hand motif contains Asp and Asn residues that share the same side chain volume and geometry (Zhou et al. 2013). However, Asn lacks a non‐compensated negative charge, and this decreases its metal ion affinity (Avaeva et al. 1996; Grodsky et al. 2000; Permyakov et al. 2001; Taylor and Markham 1999).

Molecular dynamics of calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP with carbonate ions

Molecular dynamics simulations of Pf‐SCP with carbonate ions were performed to obtain insight into the mode of calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP. To assess the structural stability of Pf‐SCP with carbonate ions, RMSD and R g were analyzed over three simulations as presented in Figure S8. In all three simulations, the RMSD values increased from 0.15 to 0.2 nm, indicating that carbonate ions induced structural changes in Pf‐SCP (De Nicola et al. 2010; Shimoyama and Takeda‐Shitaka 2017; Yaduvanshi et al. 2021). In contrast to the RMSD, R g remained constant at 1.59 nm, and this was the same as that in the simulation without carbonate ions (Figure S4). The molecular dynamics simulations of Parvalbumin and STIM1 (both EF‐hand proteins) revealed increased R g in their apo‐forms (without calcium) compared to that of their holo‐forms (with calcium) (Immadisetty et al. 2021; Neamtu et al. 2023). Additionally, while minor structural changes in the loop regions occurred, Pf‐SCP maintained its overall globular structure during the simulations due to the fixed α‐helices.

The distances between the calcium ions and individual amino acids in the EF‐hand were calculated as a function of time as presented in Figure 6. In EF1 and EF2, D27 and E117, respectively, exhibited the most stable binding to calcium ions due to bidentate coordination in terms of the lowest distances (Enomoto et al. 2020; Sauge‐Merle et al. 2017). The distance between the calcium ions and either D27 or E117 was used as an indicator of the extent of calcium dissociation. Figure 7 indicates the smallest distance between the calcium ion and carbonate ions and the distance between the calcium ion and D27 or E117. Calcium dissociation occurred at 631, 715, 327, and 577 ns in EF1 of Repeat 2 and EF2 of Repeats 1, 2, and 3, respectively. In all simulations, as carbonate ions first approached calcium ions in the EF‐hand, a three‐layered structure of negative charge (carbonate ion), positive charge (calcium ion), and negative charge (amino acids) was formed, and this structure was previously observed in simulations of lysozyme‐mediated calcium carbonate nucleation (Sandya Rani and Saharay 2019). Subsequently, calcium ions detached from the amino acids in the EF‐hand, and calcium dissociation finally occurred to form calcium carbonate. N18 and S24 in EF1 and N108 and E114 in EF2 exhibited high fluctuation regardless of the presence of carbonate ions (Figures 5 and 6). Given that Pf‐SCP exists in a fully hydrated environment, surrounding water molecules are sufficient to compete with these intrinsically weak ligands. In contrast, the key acidic residues, such as Asp and Glu, remained stably bound in the absence of carbonate ions, indicating that water molecules cannot overcome their strong electrostatic interactions with the calcium ions. It was only in the presence of carbonate ions that the fluctuation of intrinsically strong ligands increased, resulting in the transition to a weakly bound state. This finding demonstrates that the divalent negative charge of carbonate ions enables them to be a superior competitor, displacing the key acidic residues, such as Asp and Glu, to complete calcium dissociation.

To provide additional insight into the coordination of calcium ions during calcium dissociation, snapshots of the simulations for EF1 and EF2 of Repeat 2 are presented in Figures 8 and 9, respectively. Regardless of EF1 and EF2, the mode of calcium dissociation was similar for all repeats. In the initial phase, the six oxygen atoms of the amino acids in the EF‐hand were coordinated to calcium ions. Subsequently, water molecules formed coordination bonds with calcium ions instead of amino acids. Water molecules were stabilized by the oxygen atoms of Asp or Glu via hydrogen bonds. Finally, the carbonate ions approached and bound the calcium ions that subsequently detached from the EF‐hand. The distances between the calcium ion and water molecules are crucial for forming hydrogen bonds (Fast et al. 2001), and even small changes in these distances can affect the stability of the calcium ion. Snapshots of calcium dissociation in EF2 from Repeats 1 and 3 are presented in Figures S9 and S10, respectively. Although the mode of replacement of amino acids with water molecules and the binding of carbonate ions were the same as those in Repeat 2, the direction in which calcium ions were released from the EF‐hand was different. The simulation results for Pf‐SCP differed from those of a previous study examining the C‐terminal lobe of calmodulin that demonstrated that calcium dissociation from the two EF‐hands occurred simultaneously with a single optimal direction for calcium dissociation (Zhang et al. 2008). A multi‐state ion model was applied to calcium ions, enabling us to sample the fluctuations in calcium ion positions and diverse conformations of amino acids within the EF‐hand.

Considering the results of this study, a mechanism of calcium dissociation and the contribution of Pf‐SCP to shell formation are proposed, as illustrated in Figure 10. Pf‐SCP binds calcium ions, preventing both the homogeneous and inhomogeneous nucleation of calcium carbonate, and it is then transported to the appropriate calcification site. An increased concentration of carbonate ions induces calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP by releasing free calcium ions. Finally, calcium carbonate formation and crystallization are induced during shell formation.

Proposed mechanism of calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP in the shell formation process of P. fucata. Pf‐SCP binds calcium ions and prevents inhomogeneous nucleation. Pf‐SCP is transported to the appropriate calcification site. Carbonate ions induce calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP by releasing free calcium ions. Finally, calcium carbonate is formed, and crystal growth continues for shell formation.

CONCLUSION

These results demonstrate that calcium dissociation from Pf‐SCP is induced by carbonate ions in the same manner as EDTA, and this is followed by the formation of calcium carbonate. Molecular dynamics simulations support the experimental results and provide insights into calcium dissociation at atomic resolution. Stabilization of the EF‐hand with calcium ions is crucial for calcium binding in EF‐hand proteins. During calcium dissociation, water molecules form coordination bonds with calcium ions instead of the amino acids in the EF‐hand. The binding of carbonate ions to calcium ions causes instability, ultimately leading to calcium dissociation. These results provide fundamental knowledge regarding the calcium dissociation process of EF‐hand proteins and the calcium transport mechanism for shell formation. However, many other proteins are involved in the biomineralization process of P. fucata. The focus of this study, calcium transport with Pf‐SCP, explains only the initial stages of calcium carbonate formation. Therefore, further research is required to understand the contribution of Pf‐SCP to the subsequent crystal growth process of calcium carbonate. Furthermore, how carbonate ions are involved in the biomineralization process and interact with proteins to promote calcium carbonate formation in vivo remains unknown, which requires future investigation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protein purification, crystallization, and structural determination

The procedure of expression, purification, crystallization of recombinant Pf‐SCP (rPf‐SCP) and X‐ray data collection and processing was previously reported (Zhu et al. 2021). Briefly, the cDNA of Pf‐SCP was cloned into the pET‐28a vector, transformed into Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) cells. rPf‐SCP, fused with an N‐terminal His‐tag, was overexpressed and purified using a Ni Sepharose column. The His‐tag was subsequently cleaved by thrombin, and the protein was further purified by size‐exclusion chromatography. Concentrated protein solutions (30 mg/mL) were used for crystallization via the sitting‐drop vapor‐diffusion method, yielding crystals in optimized conditions. X‐ray diffraction data were collected at SPring‐8 BL44XU and processed with XDS program package (Kabsch 2010), followed by molecular replacement using AlphaFold model and refinement using CCP4 and Phenix (Jumper et al. 2021; Liebschner et al. 2019; Winn et al. 2011).

The crystal structure of Pf‐SCP was visualized with UCSF Chimera (Pettersen et al. 2004). Electrostatic potentials of the crystal structure of Pf‐SCP were calculated using the Adaptive Poisson‐Boltzmann Solver (APBS) software (Jurrus et al. 2018).

Preparation of apo‐ and holo‐rPf‐SCP

The calcium‐bound form (holo‐form) of rPf‐SCP was prepared by adding CaCl2 to a final concentration of 1 mM. To remove excess calcium ions, three cycles of buffer exchange into 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 9.0) were conducted using ultrafiltration. To prepare the calcium‐free form (apo‐form) of the rPf‐SCP, EDTA (pH 8.0) was added to the protein solution to a final concentration of 50 mM. EDTA was subsequently removed from the solution by performing three cycles of buffer exchange into 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 9.0) by ultrafiltration.

The calcium concentration of holo‐ and apo‐rPf‐SCP was measured using ICP‐MS. The protein concentration was determined by measuring A 280. Then, the binding ratio of rPf‐SCP to calcium was determined.

Intrinsic amino acids fluorescence

Calcium dissociation from rPf‐SCP was analyzed by intrinsic fluorescence spectra derived from aromatic amino acids (Trp, Tyr, and Phe). Holo‐rPf‐SCP was diluted to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL by using the buffer containing 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 9) alone, with 50 mM ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA), with 50 mM Na2CO3, and with 500 mM Na2CO3. Apo‐rPf‐SCP (0.5 mg/mL) was also prepared using 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 9). For experiments at a lower pH, holo‐rPf‐SCP was concentrated to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/mL and buffer‐exchanged into 1 M Tris–HCl (pH 8) alone, with 500 mM NaHCO3 using ultrafiltration. The fluorescence analysis was performed using a FP‐6500 spectrometer (JASCO, Japan) with an exciting wavelength of 280 nm. Three individual measurements for each condition were performed.

Calcium carbonate synthesis from rPf‐SCP

Holo‐rPf‐SCP was ultrafiltrated using VIVASPIN 500 (SARTORIUS, Germany) with 20 mM Tris–HCl (pH 8) three times and concentrated to a final concentration of 10 mg/mL. Twenty microliters of rPf‐SCP were dropped onto a borosilicate glass plate (Matsunami Glass, Japan). Calcium carbonate crystallization was conducted by the ammonium carbonate diffusion method. The borosilicate glass plate, ammonium carbonate powder, and water were placed in a depressurized desiccator at room temperature for 24 h. The surface of the borosilicate glass plate was washed with Milli‐Q water three times.

SEM‐EDS analysis of synthesized calcium carbonate

The borosilicate glass plate with synthesized calcium carbonate was placed on an aluminum stage and coated with Au for high‐resolution secondary electron image recording and with carbon for elemental analysis. The morphology of calcium carbonate was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM) S‐4800 (Hitachi, Japan) with an acceleration voltage of 5 kV. The elemental point analysis of calcium carbonate was conducted using an energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectrometer (EDS) EMAX‐7000 (HORIBA, Japan) with an acceleration voltage of 20 kV for 15 min.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics simulations of Pf‐SCP were conducted using GROMACS 2024.2 (Abraham et al. 2015) with CHARMM36m force field (Huang et al. 2017). The input files were generated using CHARMM‐gui solution builder (Jo et al. 2008). Pf‐SCP was solvated with TIP3P water in an orthorhombic box where the distance between the protein and the edge of the solvation box was 10 Å. The simulation box was neutralized with chloride ions (“0 M carbonate” condition), and 1 M sodium carbonate was further added (“1 M carbonate” condition). A multi‐state ion model was applied for calcium ion in the EF‐hand of Pf‐SCP (Zhang et al. 2020). The system was energy‐minimized by the steepest descent method for 5000 steps and equilibrated for 125 ps at 298.15 K, followed by a further production run for 1 μs. The simulations were performed in triplicate. UCSF Chimera was used to create snapshots of the simulation trajectories. The tools gmx rmsd, gmx rmsf, gmx gyrate, and gmx distance in GROMACS were used for the analysis of RMSD (root mean square deviation), RMSF (root mean square fluctuation), R g (radius of gyration), and distance between the center of the calcium ion and oxygen atoms in the EF‐hand of Pf‐SCP. The binding energy of the Pf‐SCP–calcium ion complex was calculated using gmx_MMPBSA v1.6.3 by molecular mechanics/Poisson–Boltzmann surface area solvation (MM‐PBSA) (Valdés‐Tresanco et al. 2021). Decomposition of the binding energy was performed to extract the contribution of individual amino acids for Pf‐SCP–calcium ion binding.
