A nomogram for predicting adjacent segment disease after anterior cervical surgery
Authors: Burak Bahadir, Fatma Betül Saylak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Adjacent segment disease (ASD) is a recognized complication following anterior cervical surgery and may adversely affect long-term clinical outcomes. This study aimed to develop and internally validate a prognostic nomogram incorporating clinical and radiological variables to predict the risk of ASD after anterior cervical surgery. In this single-center retrospective cohort study, 234 patients who underwent anterior cervical surgery were included. The primary endpoint was the development of ASD during follow-up. Eleven candidate predictors were evaluated. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was applied for variable selection, followed by multivariable logistic regression to identify independent predictors. A nomogram was constructed, and its performance was assessed using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), calibration analysis, and decision curve analysis. Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling. ASD developed in 26 patients (11.1%). LASSO regression identified age, treatment modality (cage), mJOA score, disc height, and Cobb angle as the most predictive variables. In multivariable analysis, cage use was independently associated with an increased risk of ASD (odds ratio = 3.56; 95% CI: 1.46–8.68; P = .005). Increased disc height and Cobb angle were also significantly associated with ASD. The nomogram demonstrated good discriminative performance (AUROC = 0.78) with satisfactory calibration and no evidence of overfitting after internal validation. This nomogram provides an individualized risk prediction model for ASD following anterior cervical surgery. By integrating key clinical and radiological parameters, it may assist clinicians in identifying high-risk patients and optimizing postoperative management strategies.

1. Introduction

Cervical spondylosis is a progressive degenerative disorder characterized by intervertebral disc degeneration, osteophyte formation, hypertrophy of the ligamentum flavum, and congenital or acquired structural alterations. It presents with a wide clinical spectrum, and both conservative and surgical treatment modalities are utilized in its management. Among surgical options, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) has been one of the most commonly employed procedures for the treatment of cervical degenerative diseases for several decades.[1] ACDF is widely regarded as a reliable procedure for relieving radiculopathy and myelopathy, with well-documented short- and mid-term clinical efficacy. The historical evolution of anterior cervical surgery began with the anterior decompression and fusion techniques described by Smith and Robinson, followed by Cloward’s interbody fusion approach introduced in 1958, which involved placement of a cylindrical bone graft into the intervertebral disc space. In subsequent years, concerns were raised that such fusion techniques might accelerate degeneration at adjacent segments. These concerns are primarily based on the hypothesis that elimination of motion at the fused segment may increase biomechanical loading at adjacent levels. As an alternative to anterior fusion, motion-preserving surgical approaches emerged in the 1960s, and in 1966, Fernström described the first clinical applications of cervical disc arthroplasty. Although early implants demonstrated limited clinical success due to various complications, advances in implant design led to the widespread clinical use of artificial cervical discs. Nevertheless, the long-term effectiveness of cervical disc arthroplasty and its impact on adjacent segment degeneration remain subjects of ongoing debate.[1] In parallel with these developments, the terms “adjacent segment degeneration” (ASDeg), “adjacent segment disease” (ASDis), and ASD have been used in the literature to describe disc degeneration, osteophyte formation, facet arthrosis, spinal stenosis, and segmental instability following spinal fusion. The “adjacent segment” refers to the levels immediately superior or inferior to the index surgical segment.[2] To address terminological inconsistency, AO Spine proposed the term “adjacent segment pathology” (ASP), defining radiological changes alone as ASDeg and radiological changes accompanied by clinical symptoms as ASDis or clinical ASP.[2] This distinction is clinically important, as radiographic progression does not necessarily translate into symptomatic disease requiring intervention. Based on current evidence, ASD is considered a multifactorial and complex clinical entity that may adversely affect surgical outcomes after spinal stabilization procedures. The etiopathogenesis of ASD has not yet been fully elucidated, and existing literature emphasizes its multifactorial nature. In addition to genetic predisposition and the natural course of degenerative processes, increased biomechanical stress due to cervical malalignment and restricted segmental motion, postoperative anatomical alterations, changes in load distribution following fusion, increased intradiscal pressure, and insufficient restoration of sagittal alignment play critical roles in the development of ASD.[3,4] Both patient-related factors (such as age and baseline degenerative burden) and surgery-related factors (including implant selection and alignment restoration) are believed to contribute to this process. Reported incidences of ASD vary considerably across studies. A meta-analysis reported prevalences of 28.3% for radiographic ASD, 13.3% for symptomatic ASD, and 5.8% for reoperation-requiring ASD after cervical surgery.[5] Other studies have reported ASDeg and ASDis incidences ranging from 7% to 92% and 0% to 25%, respectively, with pooled prevalences of 32.8% and 6.3%.[6] These findings indicate that adjacent segment degeneration is a common postoperative phenomenon, whereas symptomatic ASD represents only a subset of radiographic changes. Therefore, identifying patients at risk of clinically meaningful ASD, rather than isolated imaging changes, is of particular importance in contemporary cervical spine surgery. In contemporary anterior cervical surgery, polyetheretherketone (PEEK) cages are frequently used to maintain disc height and vertebral alignment. However, immobilization of fused segments may lead to increased biomechanical stress at adjacent levels, resulting in ASD and pseudoarthrosis.[6] Nevertheless, whether the observed association between cage-based fusion and ASD reflects a direct biomechanical effect or is influenced by patient selection and surgical indication remains to be fully clarified. As a motion-preserving alternative, cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) aims to maintain the kinematics of the functional spinal unit and preserve the natural biomechanics of the cervical spine.[7]

Clinical studies, particularly in single-level disease, have demonstrated that CDA can yield comparable or superior clinical outcomes and lower rates of symptomatic ASD compared with ACDF using PEEK cages.[8-11] Despite the growing body of literature comparing ACDF and CDA, most studies have focused on isolated risk factors or group-level comparisons, whereas individualized risk prediction models for ASD remain limited. In particular, integrated models that simultaneously incorporate clinical severity, radiological alignment parameters, and treatment modality are scarce. The objective of this study was to develop a nomogram to predict the risk of ASD by incorporating clinical and radiological variables, including age, modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association (mJOA) score, intervertebral disc height, Cobb angle, sex, comorbidities, smoking history, treatment modality (cage or prosthesis), surgical level, Pfirrmann classification, and Modic changes. In the present study, ASD was defined based on both radiological degenerative changes at adjacent levels and corresponding new or progressive clinical symptoms during follow-up. The ultimate goal was to enhance patient-specific risk assessment, integrate preventive strategies and personalized treatment approaches into clinical decision-making, and reduce unnecessary complications and revision surgeries. Additionally, this study aims to fill an existing knowledge gap in the literature by providing an original contribution to ASD risk prediction after anterior cervical surgery.[12]

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Study design

This study was conducted as a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent anterior cervical surgery at a tertiary care center between April 2019 and January 2025. A total of 234 patients were included, of whom 26 developed adjacent segment degeneration (ASD). The study was approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (Approval No. TABED 1-25-1180, April 9, 2025) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Reporting adhered to the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines. To minimize selection bias, consecutive eligible patients were included. Standardized radiological criteria were applied to reduce information bias. Patients aged ≥18 years who underwent anterior cervical surgery were retrospectively included. ASD was defined based on radiological findings combined with new or worsening clinical symptoms, including neck pain, radiculopathy, or neurological deficits during follow-up. Four continuous variables (age, preoperative mJOA score, intervertebral disc height, and Cobb angle) and 7 categorical variables (sex, presence of systemic comorbidities, smoking history, treatment modality [cage vs prosthesis], surgical level, Pfirrmann grade, and Modic changes) were evaluated as potential predictors. All patients were assessed at a standardized 6-month postoperative follow-up time point, and ASD status was determined based on clinical and radiological findings recorded during this evaluation. Patients with prior anterior cervical surgery, cervical trauma, infection, or tumors, as well as those with incomplete data or insufficient follow-up, were excluded. As a retrospective study, sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients available during the study period.

2.2. Outcome definition

The primary outcome was the development of adjacent segment degeneration (ASD) following anterior cervical surgery. The cohort included patients diagnosed with cervical spondylosis who underwent either cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) or anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) between 2019 and 2025. ASD was defined as the presence of degenerative changes – such as osteophyte formation, facet arthrosis, or spinal stenosis – at segments adjacent to the operated level on MRI (magnetic resonance imaging) or CT (computed tomography), accompanied by new or worsening clinical symptoms. ASD development was evaluated at the standardized 6-month postoperative follow-up visit. Potential clinical, demographic, and radiological predictors were statistically analyzed, and a nomogram model was developed to estimate the individual risk of ASD.

2.3. Statistical analysis

A total of 234 patients who underwent anterior cervical surgery were included in the analysis. The primary outcome was adjacent segment degeneration (ASD), which occurred in 26 patients (11.1%). Eleven potential predictors were evaluated, including 4 continuous variables (age, modified Japanese Orthopaedic Association [mJOA] score, intervertebral disc height, and Cobb angle) and 7 categorical variables (sex, presence of additional disease [X disease = Comorbidity], smoking history, treatment type [cage vs prosthesis], surgery level [upper/mid/lower cervical], Pfirrmann classification, and Modic classification).

Descriptive statistics were calculated and compared between ASD and non-ASD groups using appropriate statistical tests. The chi-square or Fisher exact test was used for categorical variables, and the Mann–Whitney U test was used for continuous variables. Given the limited number of events (n = 26) relative to the number of potential predictors, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression was employed for variable selection to prevent overfitting. The optimal lambda coefficient (λ) was determined through 10-fold cross-validation, with λ.min (the λ with the lowest mean cross-validated deviance) selected as the tuning parameter. The LASSO-selected predictors were subsequently included in a multivariable logistic regression model, and a nomogram was constructed for clinical use. Patients with incomplete clinical or radiological data were excluded from the analysis; therefore, no imputation method was applied.

Due to sample size constraints, internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling (B = 1000) following TRIPOD (Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis) guidelines rather than creating a separate validation cohort. Model performance was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC), calibration plots, and decision curve analysis (DCA). The optimal cutoff threshold was determined using the Youden index. Optimism-corrected performance metrics were reported to account for potential overfitting. Given the limited number of ASD events, LASSO-based penalized regression, bootstrap resampling, optimism correction, and Brier score assessment were specifically employed to minimize the risk of overfitting and improve model stability. All statistical analyses were performed using R software version 4.5.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a significance level of α = 0.05.

3. Results

3.1. Demographic and clinical characteristics

The study cohort comprised 234 patients who underwent anterior cervical surgery, with a mean age of 51.6 years (standard deviation [SD], 11.6). Adjacent segment degeneration developed in 26 patients (11.1%) during follow-up. The ASD group showed a higher mean age (55.5 vs 51.1 years) and greater male predominance (61.5% vs 39.9%) compared to the non-ASD group. Treatment modality differed significantly between groups, with cage placement more frequent in the ASD group (50.0% vs 21.6%, Table 1). The median mJOA score was lower in the ASD group (16.00 vs 17.00), while mean disc height (4.45 vs 3.94 mm) and median Cobb angle (17.00° vs 14.00°) were greater in patients who developed ASD (Table 1).

3.2. Variable selection

All eleven candidate variables underwent LASSO regression analysis for feature selection (Fig. 1). The cross-validation curve demonstrated that at λ.min, 5 coefficients remained non-zero, identifying age, treatment type (cage), mJOA score, disc height, and Cobb angle as the most predictive variables. The coefficient paths illustrated progressive variable shrinkage as lambda increased, with these 5 predictors showing the strongest associations with ASD development (Fig. 1).

Variable selection using LASSO regression analysis. LASSO = least absolute shrinkage and selection operator.

3.3. Multivariable analysis and nomogram construction

The 5 LASSO-selected variables were entered into a multivariable logistic regression model, which demonstrated modest overall fit (Akaike information criterion [AIC] = 150.0, Bayesian information criterion [BIC] = 170.7, Nagelkerke R-squared [Nagelkerke R2 ] = 0.204).

After adjustment for other factors, cage treatment showed the strongest association with ASD development (odds ratio [OR] = 3.56, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.46–8.68, P = .005). Both disc height (OR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.11–2.60, P = .015) and Cobb angle (OR = 1.60, 95% CI: 1.08–2.37, P = .019) were significantly associated with increased ASD risk. Although age (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 0.88–2.11, P = .159) and mJOA score (OR = 0.72, 95% CI: 0.50–1.05, P = .092) did not reach statistical significance, they were retained in the final model based on LASSO selection results and their clinical relevance (Table 2). The odds ratios for continuous variables reflect a 1 standard deviation increase, as predictors were standardized during the LASSO procedure.

3.4. Model performance and validation

The nomogram demonstrated moderate discriminative ability with an AUROC of 0.782 (95% CI: 0.693–0.871, figure_auroc). Using the Youden index-determined optimal cutoff (predicted probability ≥ 0.093), the model achieved a sensitivity of 76.9% (20/26 ASD cases correctly identified) and specificity of 67.3% (140/208 non-ASD cases correctly identified). The positive predictive value was 22.7%, reflecting the low ASD prevalence in the cohort, while the negative predictive value was 95.9%, indicating that patients classified as low-risk had <5% probability of developing ASD. Overall classification accuracy was 68.4% (160/234 correctly classified; Fig. 3).

Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of the nomogram for predicting adjacent segment disease (ASD). ASD = adjacent segment disease, AUC = area under the curve, CI = confidence interval, ROC = receiver operating characteristic.

Internal validation using 1000 bootstrap resamples revealed an optimism-corrected C-index of 0.743 (apparent C-index: 0.782, optimism: 0.039), suggesting minimal overfitting. The Brier score was 0.087, indicating good overall accuracy. Calibration analysis showed a bootstrap-corrected slope of 0.827 and an intercept of − 0.274, with the calibration plot demonstrating good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities at low to moderate risk levels, though with some overprediction at higher risk estimates (mean absolute error = 0.016, figure_calibration, Fig. 4).

Calibration plot of the nomogram for predicting adjacent segment disease (ASD). ASD = adjacent segment disease.

Decision curve analysis demonstrated that the nomogram provided net benefit over both “treat all” and “treat none” strategies across threshold probabilities from 0.01 to 0.63 (Fig. 5). The maximum net benefit was observed at a threshold probability of 0.11 (net benefit = 0.044). At commonly used clinical thresholds, the model showed consistent utility: at a 10% threshold, net benefit was 0.051 (vs 0.012 for treat all); at a 20% threshold, net benefit was 0.029 (vs −0.111 for treat all); and at a 30% threshold, net benefit was 0.006 (vs −0.270 for treat all). These findings indicate that the nomogram provides the greatest clinical value for risk thresholds around 11%, supporting its use for identifying patients who may benefit from closer surveillance or preventive interventions (Fig. 5).

4. Discussion

In this study, a nomogram model developed to predict the development of adjacent segment disease (ASD) following anterior cervical surgery was evaluated, and its potential clinical applicability was statistically analyzed. Significant associations were identified between the predictive factors included in the model and the risk of ASD. These factors comprised treatment modality (cage-based fusion), radiological parameters such as disc height and Cobb angle, as well as age and mJOA score. These findings support the multifactorial nature of degenerative changes occurring at adjacent segments following cervical spine surgery. In our study, ASD was defined as radiological degenerative changes at adjacent segments accompanied by new or progressive clinical symptoms, thereby targeting clinically meaningful ASD rather than isolated radiographic progression.

Pathologies developing at adjacent segments after anterior cervical fusion have long been recognized as an important complication in spinal surgery literature. In a study reported by Iampreechakul et al, the estimated prevalence of symptomatic adjacent segment changes after anterior cervical fusion was 13.6% at 5 years and 25.6% at 10 years, with a proportion of these cases progressing to symptomatic ASD. These findings demonstrate that ASD is not merely a radiological phenomenon but may also present with clinically significant symptoms.[13]

Although the exact pathogenesis of adjacent segment pathology remains incompletely understood, existing literature emphasizes increased biomechanical stress, alterations in sagittal alignment, loss of segmental motion, and disruption of lordotic balance at the surgical level as major risk factors. Radiological parameters such as reduced cervical lordosis and sagittal imbalance have been identified as important predictors of ASD development. Meta-analyses have demonstrated a significant association between postoperative loss of cervical lordosis and increased ASD risk.[14]

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is widely regarded as the gold standard surgical approach for degenerative cervical disc disease and has demonstrated favorable neurological outcomes. However, long-term follow-up studies have reported that ACDF may predispose adjacent segments to new degenerative changes and increase ASD incidence.[15] The increased risk of ASD following ACDF is generally attributed to the redistribution of mechanical loads from the fused segment to adjacent levels. Once the operated segment becomes immobilized, adjacent segments are subjected to increased stress and motion demands, potentially triggering degenerative processes at a microstructural level. Nevertheless, whether this relationship is directly causal should be interpreted cautiously and considered in the context of patient selection, surgical indications, and preoperative degenerative burden. Because treatment allocation was not randomized and was based on clinical and surgical considerations, residual selection bias cannot be completely excluded. Therefore, the observed association between cage-based fusion and ASD should be interpreted as an association rather than definitive evidence of causality.

In response to this clinical concern, motion-preserving surgical techniques such as cervical disc arthroplasty (CDA) have been developed in recent years. CDA aims to preserve segmental motion and sagittal balance through artificial disc implantation, theoretically reducing biomechanical stress at adjacent segments. Meta-analyses of multicenter randomized controlled trials have reported that CDA significantly reduces ASD incidence and reoperation rates compared to ACDF.[9,16] These analyses have demonstrated lower rates of both radiographic and symptomatic ASD, as well as revision surgery, in patients undergoing CDA.[16] These findings provide clinical context for the identification of treatment modality (cage vs prosthesis) as a significant predictor in our model. However, the associations observed in our study should not be interpreted as evidence of direct causality due to the observational study design.

Nevertheless, some meta-analyses have reported no significant difference in symptomatic ASD incidence between ACDF and CDA.[17,18] These conflicting findings may be explained by heterogeneity in patient selection criteria, differences in surgical levels (single-level vs multilevel procedures), variations in prosthesis design, and inconsistencies in follow-up duration. Given the limited availability of long-term (>5–10 years) outcome data, it remains difficult to determine whether CDA completely prevents the clinical manifestations of ASD. Nonetheless, the overall trend in the literature supports a potentially protective effect of CDA against ASD development.

The nomogram developed in this study integrates age, surgical treatment modality, mJOA score, disc height, and Cobb angle to provide individualized ASD risk prediction. This approach enables personalized risk estimation within a multivariable framework rather than relying on isolated predictors. Internal validation using bootstrap resampling demonstrated a low risk of overfitting and consistent model performance. Furthermore, LASSO regression was applied for variable selection and coefficient shrinkage, while optimism-corrected performance measures and the Brier score were reported to further enhance model robustness despite the limited number of ASD events.

In this context, the model may be particularly useful for identifying high-risk patients at an early stage and tailoring surgical planning accordingly. Furthermore, decision curve analysis demonstrated a favorable net clinical benefit across a broad range of threshold probabilities, supporting the practical utility of the nomogram for individualized risk stratification and postoperative surveillance planning. For example, in patients predicted to have a high risk of ASD, consideration may be given to motion-preserving surgical techniques, optimization of sagittal alignment, or closer postoperative surveillance strategies.

Numerous retrospective and prospective studies have demonstrated the importance of radiological parameters in ASD development. Intervertebral disc height, sagittal alignment parameters (Cobb angle and loss of lordosis), T1 slope, and sagittal vertical axis measurements have all been associated with ASD.[14] In our study, the strong predictive value of disc height and Cobb angle further underscores the critical role of maintaining postoperative biomechanical balance.

From a broader perspective, sagittal alignment abnormalities are known to disrupt load distribution across the cervical spine and contribute to stress accumulation at adjacent segments. Therefore, strategies aimed at preserving or restoring sagittal balance in the preoperative period may represent a fundamental component in reducing ASD risk.

Age is a well-established risk factor for ASD, as in many degenerative spinal disorders. Several studies have demonstrated a positive association between advanced age and ASD development.[15] This relationship may be explained by the cumulative effect of age-related degenerative changes and the gradual decline in spinal load-bearing capacity.

Recent literature reports significant improvements in mJOA scores following anterior cervical procedures such as CDA and ACDF.[19] In the present study, the impact of preoperative mJOA score on ASD development was systematically evaluated. Inclusion of clinical scores such as mJOA in the model suggests a potential relationship between symptom severity, functional impairment, and ASD risk. Lower preoperative mJOA scores may reflect more advanced disc degeneration and segmental instability, potentially contributing to increased mechanical loading at adjacent levels.

The strengths of this study include the development of a multivariable nomogram specifically targeting ASD risk, the use of LASSO regression to reduce overfitting, and internal validation using bootstrap resampling. Moreover, the model provides a clinically applicable, patient-specific risk assessment tool that may assist surgeons in treatment decision-making.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. Due to the retrospective design, definitive causal inferences cannot be established. All patients were evaluated at a standardized 6-month follow-up time point, which reduced potential bias related to unequal follow-up durations; however, the relatively short follow-up period may limit the assessment of long-term ASD development. Furthermore, although penalized regression and internal validation methods were employed, the limited number of outcome events necessitates cautious interpretation of external generalizability. Long-term follow-up exceeding 10 years is particularly important in evaluating ASD pathogenesis. External validation of the model in prospective studies is warranted.

Future research should focus on multicenter, long-term prospective studies to evaluate the external validity of nomogram-based prediction models. Additionally, the quantitative impact of prosthesis design, surgical technique modifications, and biomechanical optimization strategies on ASD development should be further investigated.

Finally, integration of large datasets with artificial intelligence and machine learning–based modeling approaches may facilitate the development of more sophisticated predictive tools. Such approaches hold promise for enabling patient-specific surgical planning and reducing ASD risk.

5. Conclusion

The nomogram developed in this study enables individualized prediction of adjacent segment degeneration (ASD) risk following anterior cervical surgery. Multivariable analyses identified treatment modality as the strongest predictor of ASD, while age, mJOA score, intervertebral disc height, and Cobb angle also contributed significantly to risk estimation. This nomogram offers a practical and clinically applicable tool for early identification of high-risk patients, supports preventive strategies, and facilitates informed surgical decision-making. Nevertheless, external validation and testing in larger and more diverse patient populations are required to confirm the generalizability of this model.
