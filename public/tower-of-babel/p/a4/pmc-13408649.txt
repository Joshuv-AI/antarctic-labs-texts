Dentoalveolar outcomes and the long-term stability of tooth-tissue-borne versus bone-tissue-borne rapid maxillary expansion in growing patients:
Authors: Mehmet Akin, Esra Ulusoy Mutluol, Tugce Esra Gunes, Mucahid Yildirim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis retrospective study evaluated the short- and long-term dentoalveolar effects and the long-term stability of tooth-tissue-borne (TTB) and bone-tissue-borne (BTB) rapid maxillary expansion (RME) appliances in growing patients.MethodsIndividuals treated with TTB-RME (n = 20; 12 males, 8 females; mean age at T1 12.44 ± 0.75 years) and those treated with BTB-RME (n = 20; 10 males, 10 females; mean age at T1 12.86 ± 0.80 years), for a total sample of 40 patients, were retrospectively evaluated. Digital models were analyzed at four different time points before treatment (T1), 3 months after expansion (T2), at the end of fixed orthodontic treatment (T3), and at long-term follow-up (T4). Maxillary arch width, palatal area, and palatal volume were measured. Longitudinal changes were analyzed using a mixed-design analysis of variance (ANOVA).ResultsRME led to statistically significant increases in all evaluated parameters for both groups. A mixed-design ANOVA revealed significant main effects of time for all parameters (all P < 0.001) and significant main effects of Group for intercanine width, second interpremolar width, intermolar width, palatal area, and palatal volume (P < 0.05), whereas no significant Group × Time interaction was detected for any variable.ConclusionsBoth TTB-RME and BTB-RME produced significant increases in maxillary transverse dimensions in growing patients, with no significant difference between the two appliances in the pattern of longitudinal changes.Keywords: Rapid maxillary expansion, Skeletal anchorage, Digital model

INTRODUCTION

Rapid maxillary expansion (RME) is a widely validated approach for correcting transverse maxillary deficiency.1 However, its outcomes depend on the site of force application and anchorage design.2 Tooth-borne (TB) RME may lead to complications such as root resorption, fenestration, tipping, and gingival recession.3-7

To reduce undesirable dentoalveolar effects, a tooth-tissue-borne (TTB) RME appliance, known as the bonded acrylic splint expander, was designed.8,9 Recent advancements in implant technology have enabled the use of different RME appliances that incorporate support from temporary skeletal anchorage units.10-17 The first clinical report of this approach was published by Lee et al.16 in 2010. In bone-supported expansion appliances, it was argued that the application of expansion forces closer to the center of resistance and their direct transmission to the palatal bone facilitated a more parallel separation of the maxillary shelves, resulting in greater skeletal effects and potentially preventing undesirable dental movements.18

In RME applications, the midpalatal suture separates and subsequent bone regeneration occurs. However, the permanence of changes obtained in arches is still controversial.13,19,20 Intermaxillary transverse discrepancy should be corrected before the peak of skeletal growth to facilitate greater skeletal adaptation.1 To assess effects of RME, cephalograms, occlusal radiographs, and model analyses are commonly utilized.21-24 At present, digital model scans represent one of the most effective and reliable approaches for assessing dentoalveolar effects.25-27

Various studies have assessed dentoalveolar effects of different RME designs.10,11,13,17,20,28-32 To the best of our knowledge, no study has investigated long-term dentoalveolar effects of TTB and bone-tissue-borne (BTB) RME appliances in growing patients. Thus, this study aimed to compare short- and long-term dentoalveolar effects of TTB and BTB-RME appliances in growing patients with transverse maxillary deficiencies. The null hypothesis was that there was no statistically significant difference between the two treatment approaches over time.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective study used archived clinical records of individuals treated at the Faculty of Dentistry, Alanya Alaaddin Keykubat University. Ethical approval was obtained from the Alanya Alaaddin Keykubat University Faculty of Dentistry, Non-Interventional Clinical and Observational Research Ethics Committee (Approval No: 5-2/2025). The requirement to obtain additional study-specific informed consent was waived by the ethics committee because of the retrospective design of the study. The archived patient records included consent for the use of anonymized clinical data for scientific research purposes.

Subjects

The sample size was determined using G*Power 3.1.9.4 (Heinrich Heine University, Düsseldorf, Germany) for repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) (2 groups × 4 time points). With a medium effect size (Cohen’s f = 0.25),33 α = 0.05, and 95% power, a sample of 36 participants (18 per group) was sufficient.

The detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in Table 1. The screening and selection processes are summarized in the study flowchart (Figure 1). Of 24 patients treated with BTB-RME, 4 were excluded because of documented miniscrew failure. The study timeline and mean age of participants in both groups are presented in Table 2.

Appliance designs

The TTB-RME appliance used in this study was a modified acrylic-bonded expander based on the design by McNamara and Brudon (Figure 2).9 In the BTB-RME group, two 1.6 mm × 10.0 mm self-drilling miniscrews were bilaterally placed between the maxillary second premolars and first molars (Figure 3)34 and inserted under local infiltration anesthesia without flap elevation using an electric torque driver (Orthonia, 111 ED-010, Jeil Medical Co., Seoul, Korea). Patients with documented miniscrew failure were excluded to ensure uniformity in the expansion and retention protocols.

Tooth–tissue-borne rapid maxillary expansion appliance used in the present study.

Bone–tissue-borne rapid maxillary expansion with bilateral palatal miniscrew anchorage.

A Hyrax screw was activated twice daily with weekly checkups until radiographic confirmation of the midpalatal suture opening, after which activation was reduced to once daily. Expansion continued until the palatal cusp of the maxillary first molar was occluded by the buccal cusp of the mandibular first molar. The screw was secured, and the appliance was retained passively for 3 months to support bone formation.35

All patients received non-extraction treatment with 0.022-inch slot McLaughlin, Bennett, and Trevisi (MBT) brackets (Discovery™ Smart, Dentaurum, Ispringen, Germany). A standardized archwire sequence was applied, progressing from round nickel-titanium (NiTi) to rectangular NiTi and subsequently to rectangular stainless-steel archwires. A long-arm transpalatal arch was used during fixed therapy in the TTB-RME group, whereas the expander remained in place throughout treatment in the BTB-RME group. The overall treatment timeline was comparable between groups (Table 2). The treatment data were obtained from contemporaneous clinical records.

After the completion of fixed orthodontic treatment, the same retention protocol was prescribed for both groups. Fixed lingual retainers were bonded from the lateral incisor to the lateral incisor in the maxilla and from canine to canine in the mandible. Maxillary removable Hawley retainers were also delivered to all patients, and their use was prescribed full-time (except while eating or drinking) for the first 8 months, night-only for the subsequent 8 months, and every other night for an additional 8 months.

Timing of records and measurements

Maxillary plaster models were collected at four time points: before treatment (T1), 3 months after expansion (T2), at the end of fixed orthodontic treatment (T3), and at long-term follow-up (T4). In the BTB group, palatal area (PA) and volume measurements were not obtained at T2 because the expander was still in place and was used for retention.

The models were digitized using a 3Shape R700 scanner (3Shape A/S, Copenhagen, Denmark) and ScanIt Orthodontics (3Shape A/S). The intercanine width (IC), interpremolar width (IP), intermolar width (IM), PA, and palatal volume (PV) (Figure 4) were measured in stereolithography models by the same researcher (TEG) using Rapidform XOS3 (3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). The method error was evaluated by remeasuring the linear parameters (IC, IP, and IM) of 10 randomly selected models at each time point (T1–T4), with five models from each group, by the same examiner. For PA and PV, the method error was calculated from models obtained at time points T1, T3, and T4 in both groups and at T2 only in the TTB group because the expander remained in place in the BTB group at T2.

Digital model measurements of maxillary arch width, palatal area, and palatal volume. A, Transverse dentoalveolar measurement points identified on stereolithography (STL) models: linear distances were measured between the cusp tips of the right and left canines (3–3) for intercanine width, buccal cusp tips of the first premolars (4–4) and second premolars (5–5) for first and second interpremolar widths, respectively, and mesiobuccal cusp tips of the maxillary first molars (6–6) for intermolar width. B, Palatal area delineated on an STL model: this area was calculated by marking and connecting the palatal gingival margins from the central incisors to the first molars on the STL models, with the enclosed surface area recorded as the palatal area. C, Palatal volume delineated on an STL model: this volume was defined as the three-dimensional space between the measured palatal area and a horizontal reference plane formed by the palatal gingival margins, posteriorly limited by a plane passing through the distal surfaces of the first molars.

Statistical analysis

Data distribution and variance homogeneity were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests, respectively. All variables met assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variance. Longitudinal differences between appliances were evaluated using a two-way mixed-design ANOVA to test the main effects of Group and Time and their interaction, with Group (TTB vs. BTB) as the between-subject factor and Time (T1–T4) as the within-subject factor. For PA and PV, Time included T1, T3, and T4 only.

Additional analyses were performed to further characterize the clinical relevance of changes during specific treatment phases, including active expansion and 3-month passive retention, fixed orthodontic treatment, and long-term follow-up. Within-group changes across time points were evaluated using repeated-measures ANOVA with Bonferroni-corrected post hoc tests; effect sizes were quantified using partial eta-squared (η²p). Between-group differences at each interval were assessed using independent-samples t tests. These analyses were supplementary to the primary mixed-design ANOVA and the findings should be interpreted accordingly. All analyses were performed using SPSS software 21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Intra-observer reliability was assessed using a two-way random-effects intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; single-measure, consistency definition), Bland–Altman analysis (mean bias and 95% limits of agreement), and mean absolute error (MAE), expressed in the corresponding measurement units (mm, mm2, and mm3).

RESULTS

Overall longitudinal analysis

No statistically significant Group × Time interactions were observed for IC, first interpremolar width (IP1), second interpremolar width (IP2), IM, PA, and PV. The main effect of Time was statistically significant for all parameters. A significant main effect of Group was detected for IC, IP2, IM, PA, and PV, whereas no significant Group effect was observed for IP1. For PA and PV, analyses were restricted to time points available in both groups (T1, T3, and T4). The results of the mixed-design ANOVA are presented in Table 3.

Phase-specific changes

To further characterize the magnitude and direction of changes at each clinically relevant treatment phase, supplementary within-group and between-group analyses were performed.

Within-group changes

Between T1 and T2, IC, IP1, IP2, and IM distances were significantly increased in both groups. In the TTB group, PA and PV showed numerical increases; however, these parameters could not be assessed in the BTB group because of the ongoing retention of the expansion appliance.

Between T1 and T3, both groups showed significant increases in all parameters. In the TTB group, IC, IP1, IP2, and IM significantly decreased from T2 to T3, whereas PA and PV showed numerical decreases during this interval. In the BTB group, a statistically significant decrease was observed only for IP1 during this interval (P = 0.02); no other parameters changed significantly, and PA and PV were not assessed because of the ongoing use of the expander as a retainer. Between T1 and T4, both groups showed significant increases. Between T3 and T4, significant decreases were observed in IC, IP2, PA, and PV in the TTB-RME group and in PA only in the BTB-RME group; no other parameters changed significantly (Table 4).

Between-group changes

Between T1 and T2, increases in IC and IP1 were greater in the TTB group. However, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for IP2 and IM. PA and PV could not be compared at T2, because the expander remained in place as a retainer in the BTB group.

Between T1 and T3, greater increases were observed in the BTB group for IP2, IM, PA, and PV. Between T2 and T3, larger reductions in IC, IP1, IP2, and IM were observed in the TTB group, whereas PA and PV were not evaluated in the BTB group during this interval. From T1 to T4, no statistically significant between-group differences were observed for IP1, whereas larger increases were observed for IC, IP2, IM, PA, and PV in the BTB group. Between T3 and T4, a larger reduction in IC and PA was observed in the TTB group; however, no other statistically significant between-group differences were observed, including for PV. Measurement reliability

The intra-observer reliability was high for all parameters (Table 7). ICC values for the linear and area measurements ranged from 0.968 to 0.978, indicating excellent repeatability, whereas the ICC for the PV (0.897) reflected good-to-excellent reliability. The Bland–Altman analysis showed a low mean bias, with relatively narrow 95% limits of agreement for all variables. The MAE was 0.640–0.810 mm for the linear measurements, 20.540 mm2 for PA, and 30.979 mm3 for PV; these values were clearly lower than the magnitude of the treatment-related changes observed in the study. However, the intergroup difference in IC during the retention period (T4–T3) was 0.59 mm, which was below the absolute measurement error (MAE = 0.750 mm). Therefore, this specific finding should be interpreted with caution.

DISCUSSION

Recently, skeletal anchorage-assisted RME devices have gained popularity as a means to minimize adverse effects on anchorage teeth and to enhance skeletal expansion.16 However, comparative evidence on the dentoalveolar and skeletal effects of different RME designs remains limited, and reported findings are heterogeneous.2,11,13,28-30,32,36 This study compared the short-term and long-term dentoalveolar effects of TTB-RME and BTB-RME appliances in growing patients using digital model analyses, which are considered reliable for evaluating RME outcomes,20,37 including three-dimensional PA and PV measurements.38

Patients with skeletal and dental Class I malocclusion and bilateral skeletal posterior crossbite were included to eliminate arch form variations typical of Class II and III malocclusions;39 cases with functional crossbite were excluded to avoid variability from mandibular shifts.40 Maxillary expansion was performed in both groups using the same activation protocol and an identical Hyrax screw. In the BTB group, skeletal anchorage was provided using a two-miniscrew approach, which has been reported to be suitable for growing patients in previous studies.2,20,28 Post-expansion, appliances remained in place for 3 months for retention, consistent with evidence that lip and cheek pressures normalized by the third month post-expansion.41 Subsequently, the BTB expander remained in situ as a passive retainer throughout fixed orthodontic treatment, whereas the TTB group was stabilized with a transpalatal arch. This difference in the retention approach should be considered when interpreting intergroup comparisons.

The primary mixed-design ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Time on all evaluated parameters, confirming that both appliances produced meaningful dentoalveolar changes over time. A significant main effect of Group was detected for IC, IP2, IM, PA, and PV, indicating overall magnitude differences between groups across time points; however, no significant group effect was found for IP1. Importantly, no significant Group × Time interaction was detected for any parameter, indicating that the pattern of change over time did not differ systematically between groups. Nevertheless, as each treatment phase has a distinct clinical relevance, supplementary interval-based analyses were additionally performed, and the findings from these analyses were presented as descriptive characterizations of phase-specific changes.

Between T1 and T2, in the supplementary interval-based analysis, the TTB-RME group exhibited significantly greater increases in IC and IP1 distances than the BTB-RME group. However, expansions in IP2 and IM distances did not differ significantly between groups. These results align with studies by Lagravère et al.,13 Gunyuz Toklu et al.,28 and Pasqua et al.36 who also reported similar findings, except Gunyuz Toklu et al.28 observed more expansion in the second premolar region in the TB group. Similarly, Lin et al.10 reported less crown-level expansion in the molar and premolar regions of adolescents with BTB. Mosleh et al.,18 however, reported greater intercoronal expansion in the molar region with TTB compared with BTB-RME in preadolescents, which contrasts with the present findings. Regarding molar expansion, Canan and Şenışık11 and Gökçe et al.29 similarly reported no superior appliance, which is consistent with the present findings. The absence of PA and PV measurements at T2 in the BTB group limited the strength and symmetry of the short-term intergroup comparisons of these parameters.

Between T1 and T3, the supplementary interval-based analysis indicated greater increases in IP2, IM, PA, and PV in the BTB-RME group. From T2 to T3, reductions in IP2 and IM were significantly greater in the TTB-RME group than in the BTB-RME group. These greater reductions may reflect the transition from passive retention with the expander to stabilization with a transpalatal arch and should be interpreted within this methodological context. The possibility that BTB-RME facilitates a more favorable force distribution closer to the center of resistance of the maxilla10,20,42,43 remains a plausible mechanistic explanation for this trend but cannot be confirmed based on the present statistical findings alone.

Lagravère et al.13 did not detect notable long-term differences between TTB and BTB-RME in adolescents, with a molar expansion of approximately 4 mm and a retention of approximately 70%. In our study (T4–T1), IM expansion was 4.36 mm in TTB and 6.47 mm in BTB, with retention rates of 53.4% and 87.2%, respectively. The supplementary interval-based analysis suggested that this between-group difference emerged primarily during the fixed orthodontic treatment phase (T3-T2), when the groups differed in their retention protocols (d = −2.27, P < 0.001). In contrast, after both groups received the same retention protocol, no significant between-group difference in IM was observed at T4–T3 (d = −0.19, P = 0.55). This pattern suggests that the long-term intergroup difference in the IM largely reflects the difference in the retention approach during fixed treatment rather than an inherent biomechanical advantage of either appliance design. The transverse changes observed in this study should be interpreted in the context of ongoing craniofacial growth. Longitudinal evidence indicates that maxillary IM increases by a mean of approximately 3.08 mm during growth, with all maxillary transverse measurements largely reaching adult dimensions by approximately 16.5 years of age.44 Given that baseline ages were comparable between groups, growth-related contributions are unlikely to have substantially affected intergroup comparisons; however, in the absence of an untreated control group, the absolute expansion values obtained in both groups may include a physiological growth component, and this should be considered when interpreting the true magnitude of treatment effects.

Bazargani et al.32 reported no significant differences in dental expansion or stability between TB and tooth-bone-borne (TBB)-RME appliances after a 1-year follow-up in a randomized controlled trial of growing patients. Similarly, Garib et al.20 reported no significant differences in the IM, IP, or IC expansion between TB and TBB-RME in growing patients. While no significant Group × Time interaction was detected in the primary analysis in the present study, supplementary interval-based analyses indicated between-group differences at specific treatment phases, particularly during the fixed orthodontic treatment period (T2–T3) and the long-term follow-up (T4–T1).

Mehta et al.17 evaluated long-term dentoalveolar effects of TB and bone-borne (BB) RME in comparison with a control group using cone beam computed tomography (CBCT). After a follow-up period of approximately 2.8 years, they reported no significant intergroup differences in the IM. In the present study, the trend toward relatively smaller long-term dimensional reductions observed in the BTB group in supplementary interval-based comparisons may similarly be related to more limited vertical buccal bone loss and better preservation of alveolar support, as suggested by Mehta et al.;17 however, this hypothesis could not be directly evaluated owing to the absence of CBCT records and the lack of periodontal measurements and should be investigated in future studies.

Although a statistically significant between-group difference in PA was observed at T4–T3 in the supplementary interval-based analysis, this finding warrants particular caution, as the absolute measurement error for PA (MAE = 20.540 mm²) represents approximately 52% of the observed between-group difference at this interval, limiting its clinical interpretability.

The heterogeneity of findings across studies may reflect methodological differences, including sample age, periodontal status, suture maturation, appliance design, activation protocol, and retention approach.10,45-47 In particular, suture maturation and skeletal age critically influence RME outcomes, as skeletal effects are more likely in younger patients, whereas adolescents tend to predominantly exhibit dentoalveolar changes.1 Furthermore, the follow-up duration and nature of subsequent fixed orthodontic treatment vary considerably across studies, both of which may influence transverse outcomes and limit the comparability of findings. These factors should be considered when comparing results across studies with differing sample characteristics.

This study has some limitations. First, its retrospective design precluded randomization and limited control over confounding factors. The groups differed in their retention protocols during fixed treatment, as the BTB expander remained in situ, whereas the TTB group was stabilized with a transpalatal arch. Fixed orthodontic treatment may also have acted as a centralizing factor in both groups. Therefore, the observed intergroup differences may partly reflect these methodological factors; conversely, true differences may have been partially masked. Because the power analysis relied on a medium effect size assumption without prior long-term comparative data, the study may have been underpowered to detect smaller but clinically relevant between-group differences.

Second, the inclusion of only patients without miniscrew- related complications in the BTB group should be considered when interpreting observed stability outcomes. The exclusion of failure cases may have overestimated BTB stability, limited generalizability to the broader BTB population, and resulted in the BTB group being evaluated under more ideal clinical conditions. The risk–benefit balance of miniscrew use should be carefully considered, particularly in expansion protocols involving high force levels. Miniscrews have been reported to tolerate moderate loads. However, forces exceeding 10,000 g may be generated during rapid palatal expansion.48 Such high forces may lead to stress accumulation in the surrounding bone, thereby increasing the risk of failure. The 12-month survival rate of palatal miniscrews used for expansion has been reported to be 81%.49 In the present study, 20 of 24 BTB-treated patients (83.3%) successfully completed treatment without miniscrew-related complications, consistent with this reported rate. Nevertheless, treatment decisions should consider additional surgical requirements, patient comfort, and economic burden.

Third, CBCT was not used in this study, although it can better differentiate skeletal from dentoalveolar changes. Additional imaging is not routinely performed in patients who have completed treatment and is not ethically justified owing to radiation exposure. Finally, the inability to obtain palatal measurements at T2 in the BTB group owing to the presence of the appliance may have introduced systematic measurement bias and weakened short-term intergroup comparisons.

Although supplementary interval-based analyses indicated between-group differences in certain treatment phases, no significant Group × Time interactions were identified in the primary analysis, suggesting that the overall pattern of change did not differ systematically between the two appliances. Notably, phase-specific differences emerged primarily during the fixed orthodontic treatment phase, representing a clinically relevant finding that warrants further investigation. Future studies investigating the effects of different retention protocols within BTB-RME, such as in situ appliance retention versus the transpalatal arch, would help clarify whether the phase-specific differences observed in the present study reflect appliance biomechanics or retention-related effects. To the best of our knowledge, this study has one of the longest follow-up periods reported for the comparison of TTB-RME and BTB-RME in growing patients. Therapeutic approaches should be tailored according to the patient's chronological age, skeletal developmental status, and clinical context.1

CONCLUSIONS

Although supplementary interval-based analyses revealed between-group differences at certain time points, the absence of a significant Group × Time interaction precludes any definitive conclusion regarding the superiority of either TTB-RME or BTB-RME. Both appliances produced significant increases in the maxillary transverse dimensions in growing patients, with no significant difference in the overall pattern of longitudinal changes between the two appliances.
