Complete Remission of Dural-Based Leptomeningeal Metastasis in Patient With Non-Small Cell Lung Cancer by Osimertinib
Authors: Jemin Hwang, Beung Chul Ahn, So Hyeon Ji, Ho-Shin Gwak
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
We report complete remission of dural-based leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) in an 80-year-old female patient with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) by osimertinib. She was diagnosed with NSCLC (adenocarcinoma, T4N3M1a) 8 years ago. Mutation analysis of biopsied tissue revealed exon 19 deletion positive, and gefitinib was prescribed. Follow-up chest CT showed a radiological response, and whole-body positron emission tomography 3 years later revealed the disappearance of the previous high-uptake lesions. The medication was continued for maintenance but stopped 4 years later due to intolerable dermatitis. Two years after discontinuing chemotherapy, the patient had a gait disturbance, and brain MRI revealed a right cerebellar mass (diameter [d]=3 cm) with peritumoral edema, compatible with solitary brain metastasis. Retromastoid suboccipital craniotomy and gross total removal of the dura-attached lesion were performed. As the systemic cancer status evaluation revealed no radiological cancer lesion, only tumor bed radiation therapy was given (4,000 cGy/10 fractions) without re-introducing gefitinib. She was followed with a brain MRI at 6-month intervals, and a brain MRI 2 years postoperatively revealed a dural-based extra-axial mass in the left prepontine cistern (d=2.2 cm). Serial cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) cytology was positive for cancer cells. Upon LM diagnosis, the third-generation receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor osimertinib was given. Two-month follow-up CSF cytology and five consecutive tests over 14 months demonstrated negative conversion. Five-month follow-up brain MRI revealed near complete remission of dural-based LM, and the response was maintained until the 13-month follow-up brain MRI.

INTRODUCTION

Leptomeningeal metastasis (LM) is a dismal cancer complication without a definite cure that usually spreads along the surface of the brain, especially along the brain sulci, cerebellar folia, or spinal cord surface [1]. Local radiation therapy could show a radiological response but failed to disturb the disease progression [23]. Systemic/intra-cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) chemotherapy could prolong patients’ overall survival but usually fails to palliate symptoms [4].

Recently, we experienced a dural-based LM occurred in a patient with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), who received the craniotomy and removal of parenchymal brain metastasis 2 years ago. The diagnosis of LM was also confirmed by CSF cytology. Based on the patient’s past medical history of epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) exon 19 deletion positive of primary cancer and complete remission with gefitinib (Iressa®), the third-generation receptor tyrosine kinase inhibitor (TKI), osimertinib (Tagrisso®) was given. The patient achieved both radiological and cytological remission in 3 months and 2 months follow-up examination, respectively. And the remission was maintained more than a year up to this time of this case report.

We reviewed the occult LM after surgical removal of brain metastasis, the characteristics of dural-based LM without leptomeningeal involvement, and both the radiological and cytological response of EGFR-mutated LM to osimertinib.

CASE REPORT

An 80-year-old female patient was diagnosed with NSCLC (adenocarcinoma, T4N3M1a) 8 years ago. Mutation analysis of the biopsied tissue revealed exon 19 deletion positive, and gefitinib was prescribed. Follow-up chest CT showed a radiological response, and after 3 years, whole-body positron emission tomography (PET) showed the disappearance of the previous high-uptake lesions (Fig. 1). The medication was continued for maintenance but stopped after 4 years due to intolerable dermatitis. The patient was lost to follow-up 1.5 years later by her own decision.

Two years after the discontinuation of chemotherapy, the patient visited the clinic with the chief complaint of gait disturbance. Brain MRI showed a right cerebellar mass with peritumoral edema (diameter [d]=3 cm), compatible with a single brain metastasis (Fig. 2). One week after the radiological diagnosis, the patient underwent retromastoid craniotomy and the dural attachment of the lesion was confirmed on the surgical field. Gross total resection of the lesion was achieved and confirmed by postoperative brain MRI. Postoperative whole-body PET showed no abnormal high-uptake lesion. Following the craniotomy, the patient received radiation therapy in the tumor bed (4,000 cGy/10 fractions) and was followed regularly at the outpatient clinic without further adjuvant treatment.

Two years after the craniotomy, follow-up brain MRI showed a well-enhancing dural-based extra-axial mass (d=2.2 cm) on the left of the prepontine cistern just medial and superior to Meckel’s cave, without evidence of cavernous sinus, parenchymal invasion, or sulci enhancement (Fig. 3A–C). As the recent systemic evaluation, including chest CT, showed no radiological evidence of recurrence, a primary dural-based tumor, such as meningioma, was one of the differential diagnoses. However, considering the patient’s age and previous brain MRI without any dural-based lesion at the site of interest, the radiologist reported dural metastasis to be the most possible diagnosis. She revealed no neurological abnormality. Serial CSF analysis through lumbar tapping was performed, and the presence of cancer cells was confirmed at the second attempt. Upon diagnosis of LM, the primary cancer was re-evaluated, but the examination showed no evidence of primary recurrence. The serum was negative for EGFR mutation. Whole-spine MRI showed no presence of LM. Osimertinib was prescribed with a daily dose of 80 mg as an arbitrary uninsured medical benefit. CSF analysis 2 months after the initiation of osimertinib showed negative conversion of cancer cells, followed by five consecutive negative results for 14 months. Follow-up brain MRI 3 months after osimertinib administration showed near complete remission of the LM (Fig. 3D–F), which was maintained at least until the patient discontinued the medication due to financial reasons after 13 months of chemotherapy.

DISCUSSION

LM is characterized as metastatic involvement of meningeal structures by primary neoplastic lesions. The prevalence of LM is reportedly as high as 8%, and the most common primary origins include breast and lung cancer [5]. LM itself is not a rare condition, especially in advanced cancer patients, though isolated intracranial dural metastasis (DM) is more uncommon compared with typical LM, which mainly involves arachnoid membrane, subarachnoid space, or pia matter. The prevalence of pure DM is difficult to estimate as most previous studies relied on autopsy reports and did not differentiate DM from LM [6]. Nayak et al. [7] analyzed 122 patients with pure DM and showed that the most common origins of DM were breast, prostate, and lung cancer. As direct extension from skull metastases was the most common mode of spread, 70% of their cases had metastases of the overlying skull and 34% had brain invasion. In the presented case, the DM did not present with associated parenchymal or skull lesions.

LM following craniotomy, as in our case, has been reported, with the interest of location and relation to the primary surgical site. van der Ree et al. [8] reported that LM risk is higher following surgery performed at the posterior fossa rather than at the supratentorial location, which suggests that LM occurred due to direct metastasis of cancer cells or “drop metastasis” with more chance of exposure to CSF. In our previous study, the location of parenchymal brain metastasis that underwent surgical resection was a matter of concern, and we found that LM tended to occur in contacted or involved CSF pathway (ventricle or pial surface) lesions rather than separated ones [9]. From an anatomical point of view, DM occurred in the CSF space. Thus, we may postulate that the cancer cells pass through the CSF to attach to the dura or that dural-origin metastatic lesions are exposed to CSF flow. LM occurrence in DM or CSF pathway–contacted brain metastasis is under ongoing evaluation by one of our authors (GHS), with an interim title of “Occult LM.” CSF cytology was positive for cancer cells in 29% of the CSF samples acquired from these occult LMs, and actual LM occurred in 23% of the patients at the median time to develop of 6.0 months (unpublished data).

Treatment options for DM are still controversial due to the limited number of cases. For symptomatic DM, it is common practice to perform resection if it is a resectable and single lesion [56]. However, Meng et al. [10] suggested that resection alone is not sufficient to provide enough survival benefit, and adjuvant radiation to the resection site was associated with prolonged survival time based on a retrospective single-center study of 46 surgically resected DM cases. In their study, active systemic disease and proximity to the venous sinuses were bad prognostic factors. Nayak et al. [7] pointed out that because DMs are extra-axial lesions, delivery of chemotherapy agents should be less challenging compared with parenchymal brain metastasis and should be considered. In the presented case, systemic chemotherapy with osimertinib proved to be effective. The chemotherapy agent must be carefully selected to achieve the maximal survival benefit for the patient. Recent studies report the efficacy of small–molecular weight target inhibitors for LM [11]. Masuda et al. [12] suggested that although the penetration rate of the target inhibitors to the blood–brain barrier or blood–CSF barrier was not superior to that of conventional cytotoxic chemotherapeutic agents, the target inhibitors could achieve effective concentrations at the nanomolar level, whereas the cytotoxic chemotherapeutics required micromolar concentrations. In the presented case, we used osimertinib, a third-generation TKI, which has been shown to be effective against LM of NSCLC with EGFR mutation in both radiological and cytological response. Ahn et al. [13] reported LM activity with osimertinib (80 mg daily) in patients with EGFR T790M-positive advanced NSCLC and progression after previous EGFR TKI therapy in a multi-center retrospective study, as in our case. They observed radiological response in 55% of patients and a median LM progression survival of 11.1 months. However, they did not evaluate the CSF cytology response. Nanjo et al. [14] also reported the effect of the standard dose of osimertinib (80 mg daily) on refractory LM cases of EGFR T790M-positive NSCLC with a previous history of classical EGFR TKI failure. In their study, the calculated CSF penetration rate of osimertinib was 2.5%±0.3% and the CSF concentration reached 14.4±2.8 nM.

In conclusion, our patient showed DM 2 years after undergoing a craniotomy for dura-attached brain metastasis from NSCLC. The patient was treated with osimertinib based on CSF cytology confirmation and previous EGFR mutation, and the disease was successfully controlled for the 14-month follow-up period.
