Air pollution exposure during pregnancy and low birth weight and macrosomia: the role of gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Zhanhong Fan, Mingjun Yuan, Jie Zhang, Yangzhe Qin, Akinyemi Lydia Idowu, Li Sun, Zhiqiu Cao, Ziyi Yang, Jie Zhong, Feng Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between environmental air pollution and birth weight has been established, However, there is limited research exploring the role of gestational diabetes in the relationship between air pollution exposure and birth weight. This study aimed to investigate whether gestational diabetes influences the association between air pollution exposure and birth weight.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of 33,060 primiparous pregnancies between 2017 and 2022. Satellite-based estimates were employed for the five air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, CO, and O3). Multilevel logistic regression analyses were conducted, adjusting for individual and conception season. The moderating effect analyses was used to assess the role of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) in the association of pollutant exposure with low birth weight (LBW) and macrosomia at different trimesters of pregnancy. Additionally, sensitivity analyses were performed.ResultsThe results showed that exposure to air pollution during pregnancy increased the risk of both LBW and macrosomia. Additionally, exposure to air pollution during specific pregnancy periods significantly increased the risk of macrosomia in GDM patients. The study also observed the moderating effect of GDM on the relationship between air pollution exposure and pregnancy outcomes. In T1, GDM moderated the relationship between exposure to PM2.5 (β = -0.018, P = 0.029) and NO2 (β = -0.024, P = 0.048) and the risk of LBW. Furthermore, GDM also moderated the relationship between exposure to PM2.5 (β = 0.015, P = 0.013), PM10 (β = 0.018, P < 0.001), NO2 (β = 0.027, P = 0.002), CO (β = 0.001, P = 0.011) in T1, exposure to O3 (β = 0.013, P = 0.015) in T2, and exposure to NO2 (β = 0.024, P = 0.016) in T3, and the risk of macrosomia.ConclusionGestational diabetes significantly moderated the relationship between air pollution and birth weight. This study underscored the hazards of air pollution on maternal and newborn populations, and underlined targeted protection in susceptible subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-025-02171-2.Keywords: Air pollution, Pregnancy, Low birth weight, Macrosomia, Gestational diabetes mellitus

Introduction

Environmental air pollution consists of particulate matter and gaseous pollutants [1]. Air pollution poses a significant threat to global health [2] as it is known to cause diseases and fatalities [3]. The 2019 Global Burden of Disease (GBD) report indicated that 1.848 million deaths in China were attributed to air pollution exposure, with pregnant women and newborns accounting for approximately 20.1% of the total number [4]. Vulnerable demographics, including minors, the aging population, pregnant women, and individuals with comorbidities, are disproportionately affected by the negative impacts of air pollution [5, 6]. In addition to causing cardiovascular and respiratory diseases, environmental air pollution has detrimental effects on the health of pregnant women, contributing to serious pregnancy complications [7]. Additionally, air pollutants negatively affect birth weight and length of newborns through prenatal exposure [8, 9]. Studies have shown a positive correlation between exposure to air pollution and health outcomes in pregnant women as well as pregnancy complications, pregnant women exposed to air pollution (such as PM2.5, PM10, SO2, etc.) had a significantly higher risk of developing hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes, and preeclampsia [10, 11]. Biological studies have shown that air pollutants can penetrate the bloodstream, cross the placental barrier, disrupt uterine environmental homeostasis, and lead to abnormal fetal development [8]. Studies suggest that air pollutants potentially cause damage to the reproductive system through lipid peroxidation and dysregulation of iron metabolism, further impacting the health of both expectant mothers and their unborn fetuses [12, 13].

Macrosomia, defined as a birth weight of >4000 g [14], and low birth weight(LBW), defined as a birth weight of < 2500 g [15], are two of the most prevalent adverse pregnancy outcomes. In 2020, the global prevalence of newborns with LBW was estimated to be 14.7% [16], the DEEP cohort estimated that the prevalence of LBW in China was to be 4.9% from 2013 to 2021 [17]. LBW is a significant risk factor for cardiovascular disease, leading to an increased risk of mortality [18, 19]. It is one of the primary risk factors contributing to the global disease burden [20]. The prevalence of macrosomia is approximately 9.6% [21], it is associated with complications such as cesarean section and hemorrhage [22, 23], and it elevates the risk of developing metabolic syndrome in adulthood, including conditions such as hypertension and diabetes [24]. Most previous studies have reported an association between air pollution and LBW [25, 26] or have only assessed the effect of air pollutants on the risk of macrosomia [27, 28].

Evidence suggests a connection between air pollution and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Fine particulate matter and harmful gases can induce oxidative stress and systemic inflammation, potentially leading to insulin resistance and a higher risk of GDM [29]. Poorly controlled blood glucose levels in GDM can expose the fetus to excess glucose, resulting in macrosomia. Conversely, inadequate diabetes management can impair placental function, leading to intrauterine growth restriction and LBW, air pollution may indirectly affect fetal weight by worsening insulin resistance and disrupting placental function [7, 30]. This study hypothesized that GDM may moderate the relationship between air pollution and newborn birth weight. Furthermore, pregnant women exhibit varying degrees of susceptibility throughout the entire gestational period [31]. Therefore, it is essential to identify the susceptible exposure windows.

Methods

Study population

This study included pregnant women (n = 33,060) who delivered in Nantong City between 2017 and 2022, with maternal and newborn data sourced from medical records. The inclusion and exclusion criteria were as follows. Inclusion criteria: (1) singleton pregnancies; (2) primiparous women; (3) long-term residency in Nantong City; Exclusion criteria: (1) history of related diseases before pregnancy was documented (diabetes or hypertension or severe respiratory diseases); (2) missing newborn gender data. This study included 33,053 primiparous women with singleton pregnancies, 5214 were excluded due to the lack of newborn birth weight, and ultimately 27,839 were included in the statistical analysis (Figure S1). This study was approved by the Nantong University Ethics Committee (Ethics number: (2022)25). Because all data were anonymized and de-identified prior to the analysis, informed consent was not required.

Exposure assessment

We collected daily mean concentrations of five air pollutants (PM2.5, PM10, NO2, CO, and O3 measured in µg/m3) from January 1, 2017, to December 31, 2022, from the China Meteorological Administration. Data were obtained from an online database based on monitoring data from eight environmental quality monitoring stations in Nantong City. These stations use methods such as solar radiation intensity, surface temperature, remote sensing products, and ground observation data to monitor air pollution concentrations, thereby demonstrating high performance. The number of days of air pollution exposure during pregnancy was first calculated based on the time between the last menstrual period and the delivery date. Subsequently, spatial-temporal matching was performed according to the long-term residential addresses of the pregnant women, refining the spatial granularity to a 10-kilometer radius and temporal resolution to a daily scale [32]. This allowed for the determination of daily air pollution exposure level. Subsequently, we calculated the average air pollution exposure during the first trimester (last menstrual period to 13 weeks of pregnancy, T1), second trimester (14–26 weeks of pregnancy, T2), third trimester (27 weeks of pregnancy to birth, T3), and entire pregnancy.

GDM was screened and diagnosed at 24–28 weeks of gestation using a 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). Based on the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) [33]. GDM cases were identified if any of the following criteria were met: fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1 h plasma glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2 h plasma glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L, clinical diagnoses of GDM (diet and insulin-controlled) were obtained from antenatal records of pregnant women in this study.

Outcome data

The main findings of this study were LBW, macrosomia. LBW was defined as a newborn weighing less than 2500 g [15], and macrosomia as a newborn weighing over 4000 g [14].

Covariates

We collected demographic characteristics at recruitment from medical records and referenced relevant literature to determine covariates based on previous original studies, including maternal age, gestational week, newborn gender, delivery year and conception season, maternal conditions including heart disease, hysteromyoma, anemia, obesity (obesity means that BMI ≥ 30 pre-pregnancy).

Statistical analysis

We first calculated the average exposure concentrations of air pollutants throughout pregnancy and analyzed their correlations using Pearson’s correlation coefficients. A logistic regression model was used to assess the relationship between air pollutants and LBW or macrosomia, presenting both crude and adjusted odds ratios (OR) with 95%CI. Three models were constructed: (1) univariable analysis; (2) Model 1 (adjusted for maternal age, gestational week, newborn gender, delivery year, maternal conditions including heart disease, hysteromyoma, anemia, obesity); and (3) Model 2 (Model 1 + conception season). Only complete cases with available birth weight data were analyzed.

We also evaluated the moderating role of GDM on the relationship between pollutant exposure and birth weight. Prior to conducting the moderation analysis, we ensured that the criteria for a moderating effect were met, which requires that the effect of air pollution exposure on the outcome, birth weight, is influenced by the moderating variable (GDM). Multicollinearity was addressed by standardizing the predictor and moderator variables, and bootstrapping was used to estimate confidence intervals for the moderation effect [34]. To pursue the testing for the moderation effect, the following model with (1) direct effect of predictor (air pollution) on birthweight, (2) direct effect of moderator (GDM) on birthweight, and (3) direct interactions effect (air pollution x GDM) on birthweight. The interaction effect is auto-calculated in R 4.3.3, which also generates the proportion of the variance explained by the moderating effect of GDM (R2 increase due to interaction). The effect of covariates (maternal age, gestational week, newborn gender, delivery year and conception season, maternal conditions including heart disease, hysteromyoma, anemia, obesity) was adjusted in the moderation model.

Stratified analyses were performed by conception season and delivery year. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of the results. All analyses were performed with SPSS26.0 and statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Association between air pollution exposure and LBW or macrosomia

Figure S2 shows the relationship between exposure to five pollutants and LBW across three models. After adjusting for demographics and conception season, exposure to air pollution during pregnancy significantly increased LBW risk. PM2.5 exposure in T1 (OR = 1.026, 95% CI: 1.014, 1.034) and throughout pregnancy (OR = 1.026, 95% CI: 1.008, 1.044) were associated with higher LBW risk. O3 exposure, except in T3 (OR = 1.008, 95% CI: 0.997, 1.019), also increased LBW risk, with the highest for exposure throughout pregnancy (OR = 1.069, 95% CI: 1.050, 1.088). PM10, NO2, and CO exposure during any trimester or throughout pregnancy also raised LBW risk, with NO2 exposure throughout pregnancy showing the strongest association (OR = 1.086, 95% CI: 1.063, 1.110). Figure S3 shows the results of a regression analysis stratified by birth weight into macrosomia and controls. Exposure to all five pollutants during the trimesters increased macrosomia risk, with the highest risk in T3 and throughout pregnancy. O3 exposure in T1 and throughout pregnancy posed the greatest risk. Notably, NO2 exposure throughout pregnancy significantly increased the risk of macrosomia (OR = 1.129, 95% CI: 1.116–1.143).

We conducted a sensitivity analysis of air pollution exposure and LBW risk. Exposure to PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and CO during pregnancy increased LBW risk by 0.9%−38.3% in women who conceived in spring and summer versus winter, while PM2.5 and NO2 exposure in autumn reduced LBW risk; O3 showed the opposite trend. Pregnant women exposed to PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and CO from 2017 to 2021 had higher LBW risk than those in 2022, with mixed results for O3. Stratification by neonatal sex and preterm status showed PM2.5 increased LBW risk overall, and O3 exposure raised LBW risk in preterm newborns by 2.2% compared to full-term ones (Table S1). We conducted a stratified analysis of air pollution exposure and macrosomia during pregnancy, using women who conceived in winter as the baseline. The results showed that women who conceived in spring and summer and were exposed to PM2.5, PM10, NO2, and CO had a 0.8%−42.4% increased risk of macrosomia, while those conceiving in autumn had a 0.5%−6.2% increased risk with exposure to PM2.5, PM10, CO, and O3. Women delivering in 2022 had a lower risk of macrosomia compared to those delivering in 2017–2021, except for O3 exposure, which showed protective effects during certain period (Table S2).

Air pollution exposure during pregnancy and birth weight: role of GDM

GDM as an effect modifier - LBW

To further investigate the impact of GDM on LBW, we conducted a stratified analysis. The results showed that T1 exposure to PM2.5 (OR = 1.025, 95% CI 1.013–1.037) and NO2 (OR = 1.038, 95% CI 1.023–1.054) significantly increased the risk of LBW in women without GDM (P for interaction < 0.05). However, during other specific pregnancy periods, GDM did not significantly alter the relationship between air pollution exposure and LBW (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 1).

GDM as an effect modifier - macrosomia

To further investigate the impact of GDM on macrosomia, we conducted a stratified analysis. The results indicated that pregnant women with GDM exposed to five air pollutants throughout pregnancy had an increased risk of macrosomia (P for interaction < 0.05). The susceptible window were as follows: exposure to PM2.5 (OR = 1.023, 95% CI 1.006–1.040), PM10 (OR = 1.034, 95% CI 1.020–1.047), NO2 (OR = 1.051, 95% CI 1.028–1.073), and CO (OR = 1.003, 95% CI 1.002–1.004) in T1; exposure to O3 (OR = 1.039, 95% CI 1.023–1.055) in T2; and exposure to NO2 (OR = 1.118, 95% CI 1.091–1.146) in T3 significantly increased the risk of macrosomia (P for interaction < 0.05). However, during other specific pregnancy periods, GDM did not significantly alter the relationship between air pollution exposure and macrosomia (P for interaction > 0.05) (Fig. 3).

Sensitivity analysis in GDM and non-GDM groups

When stratified by delivery year, the results showed similar associations between air pollution exposure and birth weight in pregnant women with and without GDM. Compared to women who gave birth in 2022, pregnant women who delivered in 2017 had a higher association between exposure to air pollution and the risk of LBW and macrosomia, followed by those who delivered in 2018 (Table S3 and Table S4). This may be related to differences in pollutant concentrations across years and changes in national policies.

When stratified by conception season, compared to women who conceived in winter, no association between air pollution and LBW was found in pregnant women with GDM. However, the risk of macrosomia was lower in those who conceived in other seasons. In pregnant women without GDM, those who conceived in spring had a higher risk of LBW, and those who conceived in other seasons had a reduced risk of macrosomia (Table S5 and Table S6).

Discussion

Exposure to air pollution during pregnancy significantly increased the risk of LBW and macrosomia, especially with PM2.5, NO2, and O3. GDM moderated these effects, with specific interactions observed. In the first trimester, GDM negatively moderated the relationship between PM2.5, NO2, and LBW, while positively moderating the relationship between PM2.5, PM10, NO2, CO, and macrosomia. In the second trimester, GDM positively moderated the relationship between O3 and macrosomia. In the third trimester, GDM positively moderated the relationship between NO2 and macrosomia.

Numerous prior studies have focused on examining the relationship between PM exposure and LBW, with few focusing on the link between NO2, CO, and O3 exposure and LBW. Recent discoveries have indicated that exposure to PM2.5 [25, 34]and PM10 [35, 36]heightened the risk of LBW, which was consistent with our study. For NO2 and CO exposure, a meta-analysis indicated that exposure during the entire pregnancy and the T1increased the risk of LBW [37], And our study also observed susceptible windows during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy. For exposure to O3, a study indicated that exposure throughout pregnancy increased the risk of LBW [37], and our study also observed susceptible exposure windows in T1 and T2 of pregnancy. Few studies have examined the relationship between air pollution exposure and macrosomia. Li et al. [38] found that exposure to PM2.5, PM10, or specific trimesters, and exposure to O3 in T1 increased the risk of macrosomia. This result is consistent with the findings of the present study. Xu et al. [28] found that exposure to PM2.5, PM10, and CO during T2 and T3 of pregnancy and NO2 exposure during T3 were positively correlated with the risk of macrosomia. However, Huang et al. [39] observed conflicting results regarding the association between air pollution exposure and macrosomia using a dual-pollutant model. Variability among studies may stem from variances in study design and settings, while significant effects may be linked to varying levels of pollutant exposure during the study duration. Our study included comprehensive stratified analyses by season, year, sex, and preterm status across four distinct gestational windows. While we observed several interesting findings, these were not formally adjusted for multiple comparisons, which increases the risk of Type I error. Future studies are needed to validate the key findings in independent cohorts.

The mechanisms of air pollution on birth weight are not fully understood, but research suggests that it affects pregnant women’s endocrine, respiratory, and cardiac systems, leading to changes in pathophysiological responses, such as oxidative stress, metabolism, cardiovascular function, and immune-inflammatory responses [40–43]. Activation of the Nrf2 pathway and inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and anti-angiogenic effects [44]. Dysregulation of these pathways by air pollutants may exacerbate placental dysfunction and fetal growth abnormalities. Air pollutants induce systemic inflammation and oxidative stress can damage fetal tissues, especially rapidly developing organs, disrupt cell proliferation and differentiation [45, 46], and potentially activate the HPA axis, releasing stress hormones like cortisol, which may suppress fetal growth and contribute to LBW. And it has been demonstrated that air pollution can lead to structural breakdown or dysfunction of the placenta [47], potentially obstructing the placental transfer of oxygen and nutrients, consequently impairing fetal growth and development [48]. Premature birth often results in LBW, however, this study found an association between air pollution and LBW, even after controlling for gestational age. In contrast, specific periods of exposure to air pollution have also been linked to an elevated risk of macrosomia, potentially correlated with heightened levels of leptin and adiponectin in umbilical cord blood [49, 50], and air pollution potentially increases maternal blood levels of these two indicators [51]. Air pollution may impact fetal growth and development by interfering with the metabolism and signaling pathways of steroid hormones (such as estrogen and androgen) in both the mother and fetus [52]. Overall, air pollution can affect fetal growth and development through multiple mechanisms, such as disrupting fetal nutrient and oxygen supplies, inducing inflammatory responses, and affecting maternal metabolism. Consequently, these complex interactions may increase the risk of both low birth weight and macrosomia.

Based on previous studies, we conducted a moderating effect analysis to explore the influence of GDM on the relationship between air pollution exposure and LBW and macrosomia. Our findings indicated that GDM plays a significant moderating role between exposure to air pollution and macrosomia. The increased risk of insulin resistance and GDM due to air pollution exposure contributes to a higher chance of macrosomia through different mechanisms [53, 54]. The PI3K/Akt pathway, a central regulator of insulin signaling, is likely involved in this process, suggesting that air pollutants might disrupt this pathway to exacerbate insulin resistance in GDM, thereby amplifying adverse birth outcomes [55]. Additionally, GDM played a negatively moderating role between exposure to air pollution and LBW within specific exposure windows. Studies have shown a linear relationship between maternal glucose and birth weight [56]. The impact of air pollution on birth weight may depend on maternal glucose levels [57]. Furthermore, for every 10 µg/m³ increase in ambient PM2.5, there was an additional 0.14 kg (95% CI: 0.12, 0.17) of gestational weight gain (GWG) across the entire pregnancy [58]. Higher residential greenness was associated with a lower likelihood of excessive gestational weight gain (EGWG) [59], Excessive or inadequate GWG is associated with increased risks of poor maternal and fetal outcomes. Therefore, standardized control of maternal blood glucose and weight management during pregnancy [60], which may limit maternal weight gain, could mitigate the adverse effects of air pollution on LBW. Current mechanisms by which GDM regulates the relationship between air pollution and birth weight remain unclear, and future research is needed to investigate how maternal glucose levels and metabolic capacity moderate the association between air pollution and birth weight.

Our research has the following strengths. First, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to investigate the connection between air pollution exposure and birth weight among primiparous women. Secondly, we conducted a thorough evaluation of the impacts of air pollution exposure during four distinct stages of pregnancy on the risk of LBW and macrosomia, stratified by season of conception, year of pregnancy, and newborn gender, and reached comprehensive and significant conclusion. Finally, we investigated whether GDM moderated the relationship between air pollution exposure and birth weight, and explored the interplay among these three factors.

Limitations of our study: First, adjustments were not made for factors such as BMI, dietary patterns, smoking and socioeconomic status due to the lack of individual characteristics of pregnant women, which may confound the association results. Second, the exposure assessment in this study relied on data from monitoring stations rather than direct measurements of individual exposure, Pollutants can vary within short distances, and exposure can differ by semester due to factors like traffic, seasonal emissions, and personal mobility. but we cannot track participants’ daily movements or changes in routine, which could affect the accuracy of exposure assessment. As a result, there is potential for misclassification of air pollution exposure, which could affect the accuracy of the findings. Third, the baseline data for metabolic indicators (such as blood glucose and lipid levels) and inflammatory markers were lacking, as these measurements were not taken during the early stages of pregnancy due to ethical constraints. This may have introduced potential bias in the results. Fourth, PM2.5 and PM10 are highly correlated, making it challenging to independently assess their individual effects. We hypothesize that the pathogenic impact of PM10 may be influenced by the presence of PM2.5, which would be consistent with previous research [61]. In future research, we aim to more precisely classify particulate matter and investigate the distinct effects of PM2.5, PM2.5−10, and PM10 to better identify which specific components of particulate matter have the greatest influence on neonatal health risks. Lastly, because our study population was limited to the city of Nantong, there may have been a potential selection bias.

Conclusions

Exposure to air pollution during pregnancy significantly increased the risk of LBW and macrosomia, especially with PM2.5, NO2, and O3. GDM significantly moderates the relationship between air pollution and birth weight. This study emphasizing the need to focus on GDM at specific stages of pregnancy, particularly in the first trimester.
