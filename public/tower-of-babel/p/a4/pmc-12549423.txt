Assessing new treatments for central sleep Apnoea in heart failure: sample size considerations for reliable detection of safety signals
Authors: Holger Woehrle, Karl Wegscheider, Anita K Simonds, Michael Arzt, Jean-Louis Pépin, Patrick Levy, Raphael Heinzer, Atul Malhotra, Peter Cistulli, Adam V Benjafield, Eik Vettorazzi, Martin R Cowie
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeCentral sleep apnoea (CSA) and Cheyne-Stokes respiration (CSR) are prevalent in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) and are associated with increased mortality. Previous trials evaluating adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) for CSA/CSR in HFrEF have yielded conflicting safety outcomes. This analysis aims to determine the sample size and number of events required to reliably detect safety signals, specifically all-cause mortality, in future trials assessing new treatments for CSA/CSR in HFrEF patients.MethodsUtilizing data from the SERVE-HF trial, we conducted event-driven, non-inferiority sample size calculations focusing on all-cause mortality as the primary endpoint. The analysis employed a one-sided alpha of 0.025 and 80% power, considering hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.15 to 1.35, in line with FDA guidelines for cardiovascular safety assessments. Calculations assumed a control group event rate of 0.093 events/year, a 5-year accrual period, and a minimum follow-up of 3 years.ResultsTo detect a non-inferiority margin with an HR of 1.35, a trial would require at least 851 patients and 349 events. More stringent margins necessitate larger sample sizes; for an HR of 1.15, 3,924 patients and 1,607 events are needed. These findings underscore the importance of adequately powered studies to detect potential safety concerns in this patient population.ConclusionFuture clinical trials investigating treatments for CSA/CSR in HFrEF patients must incorporate sufficient sample sizes and event counts to ensure reliable detection of safety signals, particularly concerning all-cause mortality. This approach is critical for accurately assessing the risk-benefit profile of new therapeutic interventions.

Introduction

Central sleep apnoea (CSA) and Cheyne-Stokes respiration (CSR) are common patterns of sleep-disordered breathing in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF) [1]. The overall prevalence of CSA/CSR in this population is approximately 21–37% [2]. Rates of CSA/CSR in patients with HFrEF increase in parallel with increasing heart failure severity [3] and worsening cardiac dysfunction [4]. Furthermore, for patients with heart failure, untreated CSA/CSR is an independent predictor of poor prognosis and mortality [5].

The above prevalence and risk data suggest that patients with HFrEF would benefit from the effective treatment of CSA/CSR. However, this was not the case in the large, randomised controlled Treatment of Sleep-Disordered Breathing with Predominant Central Sleep Apnoea by Adaptive Servo Ventilation in Patients with Heart Failure (SERVE-HF) trial [6]. Although treatment with adaptive servo-ventilation (ASV) was an effective treatment for CSA/CSR, this did not translate into benefit on the primary combined endpoint (time to the first event of all-cause death, unplanned hospitalisation for worsening heart failure, or a life-saving cardiovascular intervention), which did not differ significantly between the ASV and control groups. In fact, the risk of dying from any cause or cardiovascular events was actually higher in the ASV group. In contrast, the Effect of Adaptive Servo-Ventilation on Survival and Cardiovascular Hospital Admissions in Patients with Heart Failure and Sleep Apnoea (ADVENT-HF) trial could not detect a safety signal during the use of ASV in a smaller, mixed population of patients with HFrEF who had obstructive sleep apnoea and/or CSA/CSR rather than only HFrEF + CSA/CSR [7].

Reported differences in the safety-related findings from the two large randomised controlled trials of ASV in patients with HFrEF mean that there is an important need to understand how to reliably determine the safety of new treatments targeting CSA/CSR in patients with HFrEF. Therefore, this analysis determined the number of events and sample sizes required to detect a between-group difference in all-cause mortality. The goal is to help inform the design of future studies evaluating therapies for CSA/CSR in patients with HFrEF.

Methods

Data from the SERVE-HF study (NCT00733343) [6] were used to calculate the sample size required to detect a safety signal were based on an event-driven non-inferiority study with a survival (all-cause mortality) endpoint, using a one-sided alpha (significance level) of 0.025 and beta (1–power) of 0.2. The event rate in the control group was 0.093 events/year and the assumed true hazard ratio (HR) for all-cause death in the treatment versus control group was 1.28, with an accrual time of 5 years and a minimum follow-up time of 3 years. Calculations of the sample size and number of events required to detect a safety signal were performed for non-inferiority margin HR values from 1.15 to 1.35 based on US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) recommendations for the assessment of the cardiovascular safety of new treatments for type 2 diabetes [8]. All calculations were performed using the R-package rpact in R version 4.0.2 (R Core Team 2020). While originally developed for diabetes trials, this methodological framework ensures a rigorous safety evaluation in cardiovascular-risk populations and is applicable here due to the mortality signal observed with ASV therapy.

Results

When the non-inferiority margin HR value was 1.35, the analysis showed that 851 patients and 349 events would be needed to detect any potential safety signal of a new treatment for CSA/CSR in patients with HFrEF (Fig. 1). Using the strictest non-inferiority margin HR value of 1.15, the number of patients and events needed to show the safety of a new treatment increased substantially, to 3,924 and 1607, respectively (Fig. 1). Based on a HR value of > 1.3, which is often used to define non-inferiority in cardiovascular trials based on US FDA recommendations for assessment of the cardiovascular safety of new treatment agents, a trial would need to enroll 1,114 patients and detect 456 events during the course of a 5-year study with a minimum follow-up time of 3 years to have sufficient statistical power to detect any potential safety signal associated with the use of a new treatment for CSA/CSR in patients with HFrEF (Fig. 1).

Discussion

This analysis showed that the number of participants recruited and the number of events detected during follow-up both need to be sufficient to ensure that a trial has adequate statistical power to detect a significant difference in all-cause mortality between the intervention and control group in trials of new interventions for treating CSA/CSR in patients with HFrEF, and therefore detect any potential safety signal. Although prior trials such as SERVE-HF and ADVENT-HF used composite endpoints, the focus on all-cause mortality in this analysis reflects the most critical component for assessing potential harm observed in SERVE-HF. Challenges during the period over which the ADVENT-HF study [7] was conducted limited the sample size and compromised statistical power [9], and the proportion of trial participants who had CSA/CSR and HFrEF was particularly small (198 in total [n = 92 in the ASV group and n = 106 in the control group], representing only just over one-quarter of the total intention-to-treat population of the study). In addition, the total number of primary endpoint events that occurred in the CSA subgroup during the trial was only 109. In contrast, the SERVE-HF trial included 1,325 patients with HFrEF and predominant CSA and the number of participants who experienced a primary endpoint event was 695. Therefore, not only were the sample size and number of events too small (i.e. the study was under-powered to detect a safety signal in patients with HFrEF), the study included two distinct populations that may well show a different safety profile during ASV therapy. Pivotal trials of other new treatment modalities for HFrEF have enrolled substantial numbers of patients. For example, studies of sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors in patients with HFrEF had a primary endpoint of cardiovascular death and HF-related hospitalisation, and enrolled 4,744 patients followed for 18 months with documentation of 888 primary endpoint events in DAPA-HF [10] or 3,730 patients followed for a median of 16 months with 202 endpoint events in EMPEROR-Reduced [11]. The findings of these studies were seen to be sufficiently robust to inform Class I recommendations for the treatment of stable HFrEF with dapagliflozin or empagliflozin in the European Society of Cardiology and the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology/Heart Failure Society of America (AHA/ACC/HFSA) heart failure guidelines [12, 13]. Similarly, the Prospective comparison of ARNI with ACEI to Determine Impact on Global Mortality and Morbidity in Heart Failure (PARADIGM-HF) study of the angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor sacubitril-valsartan included a large number of participants (n = 8,442) and events (1,546 total deaths, including 1,251 cardiovascular deaths). The risk of death was reduced by 20% in the sacubitril-valsartan versus enalapril group. In the above pharmacotherapy trials, investigative treatments were added to standard medical therapy for heart failure. It is important to note that there have been significant advances in guideline-recommended optimal medical therapy for HFrEF (including the agents mentioned above) since patients were enrolled in both the SERVE-HF and ADVENT-HF trials of ASV therapy. Given the fact that CSA/CSR prevalence in patients with heart failure increases as disease severity increases [3] and cardiac dysfunction worsens [4], optimisation of pharmacotherapy for heart failure and effective decongestion are the most important first steps to ameliorate CSA/CSR in this population [14]. However, due to the timing of patient recruitment, only 10–12% of participants in the ADVENT-HF study were using SGLT2 inhibitors. Cardiac resynchronisation therapy has also long been known to reduce the severity of CSA in patients with heart failure [15]. Although CRT is primarily indicated for heart failure, it may indirectly alleviate CSA severity by improving cardiac synchrony and reducing left atrial pressure, reinforcing the importance of optimal background therapy in future CSA trial designs.

In conclusion, the results of this analysis show that future studies of new treatments for CSA/CSR in the HFrEF patient population need to include a safety analysis and therefore enroll a sample size that exceeds 851 participants and/or detect at least 349 events over follow-up. These components are essential to be able to confidently ensure safety with the use of any treatment in this setting.
