Fuel–maintenance coupling reprograms mitochondrial homeostasis to enable peripheral nerve regeneration
Authors: Jun-kuan Shan, Jia-qi Fang, Xue-Han Jin, Bo Xu, Li-ping Nan, Zhi-jie Weng, Shu-hao Liu, Xin-hui Wu, Zhong Wu, Jun-jian Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peripheral nerve regeneration is frequently stalled by a “metabolic bottleneck” characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction and bioenergetic exhaustion. Although nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) provide structural support, most remain metabolically inactive and fail to address this energetic deficit. Here, we developed a fuel–maintenance coupling metabolic reprogramming strategy to support peripheral nerve repair. A biomimetic NGC was engineered with an aligned electrospun polycaprolactone (PCL) sheath filled with an injectable, in situ photocrosslinkable Magnolol-loaded chitosan–lipoic acid hydrogel (LA-CS@Mag). In this synergistic system, α-lipoic acid serves as the metabolic fuel to restore ATP production, while Mag functions as a mitochondrial quality controller to promote mitophagy-associated mitochondrial clearance. In vitro, LA-CS@Mag protected rat Schwann cells (RSCs) from oxidative stress, restored mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced ROS accumulation, and improved ATP production, accompanied by activation of BNIP3/Parkin-related mitophagy. Moreover, conditioned medium from LA-CS@Mag-treated RSCs reduced M1-like macrophage polarization and promoted an M2-like reparative phenotype, suggesting Schwann cell-mediated immunomodulatory effects. In vivo, implantation of the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit modulated macrophage polarization, suppressed excessive early inflammatory responses, and promoted a reparative immune microenvironment. In a rat sciatic nerve defect model, the bioactive conduit significantly accelerated axonal regeneration and remyelination, prevented target muscle atrophy, and achieved functional recovery comparable to autografts. Collectively, this study identifies mitochondrial homeostasis as a therapeutic target and provides a metabolically instructive strategy for next-generation nerve guidance conduits.

Introduction

Peripheral nerve injury (PNI) remains a formidable clinical challenge, frequently resulting in persistent functional deficits despite advances in microsurgical techniques [1]. Although autologous nerve grafting is regarded as the clinical gold standard, its application is inherently limited by donor-site morbidity and tissue scarcity [2,3]. Consequently, nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) have emerged as promising alternatives [4]. However, most existing NGCs primarily focus on providing structural guidance or partially modulating the inflammatory and oxidative microenvironment, while failing to address the intrinsic metabolic requirements that ultimately govern effective peripheral nerve regeneration [5].

Accumulating evidence highlights the central importance of energy metabolism in peripheral nerve regeneration [6]. Following injury, the lesion site rapidly evolves into a hostile microenvironment characterized by excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, impaired nutrient and oxygen supply, and inflammatory mediator release, which together disrupt mitochondrial integrity and bioenergetic homeostasis [7]. Essential regenerative processes, such as axonal elongation and remyelination, require substantial ATP production [8]. As a result, impaired bioenergetics creates a critical metabolic bottleneck that restricts intrinsic regenerative capacity, even in the presence of favorable structural guidance [9]. After injury, Schwann cells (SCs) undergo extensive phenotypic reprogramming to clear myelin debris and form Bands of Büngner, placing substantial demands on mitochondrial dynamics and continuous ATP production [10]. Accordingly, mitochondrial integrity in SCs is a key determinant of peripheral nerve regeneration.

In addition to ATP production, mitochondrial quality control is important for maintaining Schwann cell viability and regenerative capacity. Among these quality-control processes, mitophagy selectively removes damaged mitochondria and helps limit oxidative stress [11]. Through receptor- and ubiquitin-dependent recognition of damaged mitochondria, mitophagy selectively eliminates dysfunctional organelles, thereby limiting ROS amplification and restraining the release of mitochondrial danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) that sustain chronic inflammatory signaling [12]. When this selective clearance pathway is impaired, damaged mitochondria accumulate and perpetuate a pro-inflammatory microenvironment, biasing macrophage polarization toward a sustained M1-like phenotype [7]. Although transient M1 activation is required for debris clearance and early repair after nerve injury, prolonged or unresolved inflammation disrupts regenerative immune dynamics and ultimately impairs nerve regeneration [13]. Despite this central biological role, mitophagy-centered therapeutic strategies have rarely been incorporated into the design of nerve guidance conduits.

From a biomaterials perspective, recent bioactive hydrogels have begun to regulate tissue repair through energy-related mechanisms [[14], [15], [16]]. For example, alanyl-glutamine-modified gelatin methacryloyl hydrogel (GelMA-AG) releases glutamine during degradation to support TCA-cycle activity and ATP production, while succinate-containing bioenergetic-active hydrogels (BAHs) promote endogenous ATP generation and mitochondrial function through succinate-related degradation products [14,16]. In peripheral nerve repair, ROS/Ca2+ dual-responsive hyaluronic acid/GelMA hydrogels have been reported to preserve mitochondrial homeostasis through responsive drug release, structural remodeling, and immunomodulation [15]. However, these systems mainly address either metabolic substrate supplementation or mitochondrial protection, whereas coupling metabolic fuel supply with mitochondrial quality maintenance in a nerve guidance conduit remains insufficiently explored. In rat models of peripheral nerve injury, including chronic constriction and sciatic nerve crush injury, α-lipoic acid (LA) has been reported to reduce neuropathic pain, improve functional recovery, and enhance antioxidant enzyme activity after injury [17]. Sustained oral administration of LA at doses ranging from 600 to 1800 mg/day has been shown in randomized clinical trials to improve neuropathic symptoms and functional deficits [18]. These effects suggest a therapeutic role beyond simple antioxidant action, potentially tied to improved cellular energy handling and metabolic support. Chitosan (CS), when functionalized with LA, offers a compelling approach to meet this demand. As an endogenous mitochondrial cofactor, LA functions as both a potent antioxidant and a metabolic regulator that supports oxidative phosphorylation and ATP synthesis, thereby directly alleviating injury-induced energy deficits [19]. Nevertheless, metabolic fueling alone is insufficient to sustain long-term mitochondrial health in the absence of effective quality control. Magnolol (Mag), a bioactive polyphenol with well-established neuroprotective properties, has been reported to activate autophagic pathways and preserve mitochondrial integrity in diverse pathological contexts [20,21]. However, its capacity to regulate mitophagy in SCs and thereby facilitate peripheral nerve regeneration remains largely unexplored.

Here, we developed a fuel–maintenance coupling strategy for peripheral nerve repair by integrating metabolic support with mitochondrial quality control. A biomimetic NGC was engineered, comprising an aligned PCL sheath and a luminal core filled with a Magnolol-loaded, α-lipoic acid-grafted chitosan hydrogel (LA-CS@Mag). In this synergistic design, LA functions as a metabolic fuel to restore mitochondrial ATP production, while Mag serves as a mitochondrial quality controller that promotes mitophagy-associated clearance of damaged organelles, suppresses inflammation, and stabilizes cellular bioenergetics. We hypothesize that the coordinated integration of mitochondrial fueling and quality maintenance is essential for re-establishing a pro-regenerative microenvironment. Experimental section

Materials

Chitosan (CS, molecular weight ∼250,000 Da, deacetylation degree ≥95%), Polycaprolactone (PCL) granules, 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (TFE), Magnolol (Mag, purity ≥98%), N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS), and 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide hydrochloride (EDC) were all purchased from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). (R)-alpha-Lipoic acid (LA) was obtained from TCI (Shanghai, China). Glacial acetic acid (CH3COOH) was obtained from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). All other reagents and solvents were of analytical grade and used as received. Gibco (USA) supplied the DMEM and Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). A suite of detection reagents, including CCK-8, Live/Dead assay kit, JC-1 assay kit, Actin-Tracker Red-555, DAPI, Mito-Tracker Green, and Lyso-Tracker Red, were procured from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). All materials were used directly as supplied.

Preparation of LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduits

Chitosan–lipoic acid (LA-CS) was synthesized according to a previously reported protocol using EDC/NHS-mediated amide coupling [22]. Briefly, 2.0 g of chitosan was dissolved in 100 mL of 1% (v/v) acetic acid. Separately, 0.42 g of LA was activated with EDC (1.68 g) and NHS (0.50 g) in 10 mL of DMSO for 1 h, then added dropwise to the CS solution in an ice bath. After reacting for 6 h, the mixture was dialyzed (MWCO: 12–14 kDa) against distilled water for 3 days and lyophilized. For hydrogel preparation, LA-CS was re-dissolved in PBS at different concentrations (1%, 2%, and 3%, w/v) for formulation screening. For subsequent experiments, the 2% LA-CS solution was mixed with Mag stock solution to a final Mag concentration of 50 μM, and the pH was adjusted to 7.4 using sodium bicarbonate. The Mag-free LA-CS hydrogel was prepared using the same procedure with an equivalent amount of DMSO.

To fabricate the conduit, a PCL solution (1 g PCL dissolved in 6 mL TFE) was electrospun into tubular structures using a voltage of 12 kV, a flow rate of 0.8 mL/h, a collection distance of 25 cm, and a rotation speed of 400 rpm. After vacuum drying to remove residual solvent, the LA-CS@Mag hydrogel precursor was injected into the lumen of the PCL conduit to form the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit. The construct was then exposed to 365 nm UV light for 1 min to induce in situ crosslinking and gelation. The LA-CS/PCL conduit was prepared using the same procedure, except that Mag-free LA-CS hydrogel was used, while the PCL conduit without luminal hydrogel filling served as the control.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) characterization

The microstructure of the lyophilized hydrogels was examined using a field-emission scanning electron microscope (GeminiSEM 300, ZEISS, Germany). Prior to observation, the hydrogel samples were pre-frozen at −20 °C overnight. Subsequently, the samples were subjected to sublimation drying for 48 h in a lyophilizer (LGJ-12H, Beijing Sihuan, China). To expose the internal structure, the freeze-dried hydrogels were brittle-fractured in liquid nitrogen. After being sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to ensure conductivity, the cross-sectional morphology of the hydrogels was observed at an accelerating voltage of 5 kV.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) characterization

The chemical structures of CS and LA-CS were characterized using a Fourier transform infrared spectrometer (Nicolet Summit X, Thermo Scientific, USA). The spectra were recorded over a wavenumber range of 4000 cm−1 to 400 cm−1 to identify specific functional groups and confirm the successful modification.

Swelling ratio and degradation assessment

To evaluate the swelling behavior, dried hydrogel samples with a known initial weight (W0) were immersed in 50 mL centrifuge tubes containing 20 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The samples were incubated at 37 °C under constant shaking at 70 rpm. At predetermined time intervals, the swollen hydrogels were retrieved, centrifuged at 5000 rpm to remove surface water, and weighed as Wsw. The swelling ratio (Esw) was calculated using Equation (1).(1)Esw=[(Wsw−W0)/W0]×100%

For the biodegradation assay, pre-weighed dried hydrogels (W0) were incubated in PBS supplemented with 2.5 U/mL collagenase type I (Sigma) at 37 °C. To maintain enzymatic activity, the solution was refreshed every two days. At specific time points, the residual hydrogels were harvested, rinsed thoroughly with deionized water, lyophilized, and weighed as W1. The degradation ratio (Ewl) was determined according to Equation (2).(2)Ewl=[(W0−W1)/W0]×100%

Contact angle measurement

The surface hydrophilicity of the conduit lumen was evaluated by measuring the water contact angle on the exposed inner surface of the samples. Briefly, the lyophilized PCL conduit and LA-CS/PCL conduit were longitudinally cut open to expose the luminal surface. For the LA-CS/PCL conduit, the measured surface corresponded to the hydrogel-containing inner surface after lyophilization. Measurements were performed on dry samples using a video contact angle meter (JY-82C, Chengde Dingsheng Testing Machine Co., Ltd., China). For each measurement, a 5 μL droplet of distilled water was deposited onto the exposed inner surface, and the contact angle was calculated with a precision of ±0.5°. Three independent samples were tested for each group.

Mechanical properties

The mechanical properties of the LA-CS@Mag/PCL nerve guidance conduits (NGCs) were measured using a universal testing machine (HY series, Shanghai Hengyu Instrument Co., Ltd., China) at room temperature. At least three specimens were tested for each group. For uniaxial tensile testing, conduits (length: 12.0 mm; outer diameter: 4.00 mm) were stretched at 50.0 mm/min until fracture to determine tensile strength and elongation at break. Radial compression tests were performed on specimens of identical dimensions at a rate of 1.0 mm/min. Cyclic loading–unloading tests were conducted for 10 cycles, during which samples were compressed to 40% strain and unloaded to 5% strain in each cycle.

Rheological test

Rheological properties of the three hydrogel groups were evaluated using an Anton Paar rheometer (MCR92). After solidification under identical ultraviolet irradiation conditions, frequency sweep measurements were performed at a fixed strain of 1%, with frequencies ranging from 0.1 to 100 Hz. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) of the hydrogels were recorded.

Cell culture

RSCs and PC12 cells were obtained from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). RSCs were cultured in Dulbecco's Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), whereas PC12 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium. Both culture media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (PS) (Gibco Invitrogen, USA). The cells were incubated in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2 at 37 °C, and the medium was replenished every 2 days.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 according to the manufacturer's protocol. For Mag dose screening, RSCs were treated with different concentrations of Mag ranging from 0 to 200 μM, and cell viability was assessed at 24 and 48 h. For hydrogel extract assays, sterilized LA-CS and LA-CS@Mag hydrogels were incubated in culture medium to prepare extracts, which were then used to culture RSCs and PC12 cells. Briefly, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well and cultured for 48 h under different experimental conditions. At each predetermined time point, the culture medium was replaced with fresh medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent. After incubation for 2 h at 37 °C, the optical density was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Biocompatibility evaluation of materials

To evaluate cellular viability and cytoskeletal organization, RSCs and PC12 cells were cultured in the control group or seeded onto the LA-CS/PCL and LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduits at a density of 8 × 103 cells per well. After 72 h of culture, cell viability was assessed using a Live/Dead staining kit. Samples were rinsed with PBS and incubated with calcein-AM and propidium iodide for 20 min in the dark to distinguish live and dead cells. For cytoskeletal staining, duplicate samples were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 1% BSA. Subsequently, F-actin filaments and nuclei were stained with phalloidin and DAPI, respectively. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI6000B, Germany) and analyzed using ImageJ software.

Migration assay

For the scratch wound healing assay, confluent RSC monolayers were scratched with a sterile pipette tip, washed with PBS, and cultured under different treatment conditions. Images were captured at 0 and 48 h, and wound closure was quantified using ImageJ.

For the Transwell assay, RSCs (2 × 104 cells) suspended in 200 μL of basal medium with 1% FBS were seeded into the upper chamber of Transwell inserts (8.0 μm pore size, Corning), while the lower chamber was filled with 500 μL of complete medium as a chemoattractant. After incubation for 48 h, cells that migrated to the lower surface were fixed with 4% PFA and stained with 0.1% crystal violet (Solarbio). The migrated cells were imaged and counted using an optical microscope (Nikon TE2000-E).

Immunofluorescence staining for nerve regeneration markers

To assess the expression of phenotypic markers, RSCs and PC12 cells cultured on different substrates were fixed with 4% PFA, permeabilized, and blocked with 5% BSA. RSCs were incubated with primary antibodies against Nestin and GFAP (1:200, Proteintech, China), while PC12 cells were labeled with anti-Tuj1 and anti-NeuN (1:200, Proteintech, China) overnight at 4 °C. After incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies and DAPI, samples were imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI6000B, Germany).

Autophagy and mitophagy assessment with inhibitor intervention

To elucidate the relationship between mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, and cytoprotection, the mitochondrial fission inhibitor Mdivi-1 (MedChemExpress, USA) was employed in a rescue experiment. RSCs were exposed to H2O2-induced oxidative stress and treated with Mag, LA-CS@Mag, or LA-CS@Mag plus Mdivi-1 according to the experimental design. The formation of mitolysosomes was visualized by live-cell co-staining with Mito-Tracker Green and Lyso-Tracker Red (Beyotime) for 30 min. Concurrently, the expression of autophagy-related markers LC3B, p62, and ATG5, as well as mitophagy-related proteins BNIP3 and Parkin, was assessed by immunofluorescence. Primary antibodies were applied overnight at 4 °C, followed by secondary antibody incubation and nuclear staining with DAPI. Fluorescence images were captured and analyzed using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI6000B, Germany).

Assessment of intracellular ROS and mitochondrial membrane potential

To investigate the mechanism of mitochondrial protection under oxidative stress, RSCs were exposed to H2O2 and treated with Mag, LA-CS@Mag, or LA-CS@Mag plus Mdivi-1 according to the experimental design. Intracellular ROS accumulation and mitochondrial membrane potential were assessed using DCFH-DA (Servicebio, China) and JC-1 (Beyotime, China) probes, respectively. Fluorescence signals reflecting oxidative status and mitochondrial polarization were captured using a fluorescence microscope (Leica DMI6000B, Germany) and quantified using ImageJ.

Mitochondrial function and ultrastructure analysis

To further evaluate mitochondrial energy metabolism and structural integrity, intracellular ATP levels in RSCs were quantified using an ATP Assay Kit (Beyotime, China) following the manufacturer's instructions. Concurrently, the mitochondrial ultrastructure, including morphology and cristae arrangement, was examined via Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) according to standard sample processing procedures.

Animal surgery

Details regarding the animal surgeries are available in the Supporting Information. All procedures were performed in compliance with ethical regulations and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the Tenth People's Hospital of Shanghai (No. SHDSYY-2025-2305-a-zkahss).

Functional assessment

To evaluate motor function recovery, walking track analysis was performed at 4, 8, and 12 weeks post-surgery. Rats walked on white paper with red-inked paws to record footprints, and parameters including print length (PL), toe spread (TS), and intermediate toe spread (IT) were measured. The Sciatic Functional Index (SFI) was calculated using the Bain formula:SFI=(−38.3∗(OPL−NPL)/NPL)+(109.5∗(OTS−NTS)/NTS)+(13.3∗(OIT−NIT)/NIT)−8.8where −100 indicates complete dysfunction and 0 indicates normal function. At 12 weeks, general locomotor activity was assessed via an Open Field Test by recording spontaneous movement in a 1 × 1 m arena for 6 min; total distance and average speed were quantified using Smart 3.0 software (Reward Life Technology, China). Finally, electrophysiological analysis was conducted under anesthesia to measure the distal compound muscle action potential (DCMAP) and nerve conduction velocity (NCV) of the sciatic nerve according to established protocols.

Histological and morphological assessment

To evaluate muscle atrophy, gastrocnemius muscles from both sides were harvested, weighed, and subjected to Masson's trichrome staining to quantify collagen content and fiber diameter. For nerve regeneration assessment, the middle sections of the nerves were processed for histological and ultrastructural analysis. Samples for H&E staining were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde and embedded in paraffin. Conversely, samples for Toluidine Blue (TB) staining and Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM) were fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C and embedded in epoxy resin. Ultrastructural images were acquired using a Hitachi TEM system. Morphometric parameters, including axon diameter, myelin thickness, and G-ratio, were measured using ImageJ software to comprehensively evaluate the quality of nerve regeneration.

Immunofluorescence analyses

The expression of regeneration-associated proteins and inflammatory markers in regenerated nerve tissues was assessed by immunofluorescence staining. Double staining for S100/MBP and NF200/Tuj1 (all 1:500, Proteintech, China) was performed to visualize myelinated axons. Inflammatory responses were evaluated using antibodies against IL-6 (1:200, Abclonal), iNOS (1:500, Abclonal), CD68 (1:500), CD206 (1:1000), CD163 (1:2000), and Arg1 (1:500) (all from Proteintech unless noted). After incubation with corresponding secondary antibodies and DAPI, samples were imaged using a Leica fluorescence microscope.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were independently performed at least three times, and data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9 software (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student's t-test, while multiple-group comparisons were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test. Differences were considered statistically significant at ∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, and ∗∗∗P < 0.001; ns indicates no significance.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization of LA-CS hydrogel

To engineer a dynamically responsive matrix capable of conformal filling and metabolic regulation, the LA-CS hydrogel was synthesized and systematically characterized (Fig. 1a). Successful grafting of α-lipoic acid—functioning simultaneously as a photo-crosslinkable moiety and a metabolic cofactor—onto the chitosan backbone was confirmed by Fourier transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy. Compared with pristine chitosan, the LA-CS spectrum exhibited the emergence of characteristic amide I and II bands at 1648 cm−1 and 1551 cm−1, respectively, indicating effective chemical conjugation. In addition, the appearance of a distinct disulfide vibration band at approximately 600 cm−1 (–S–S–) provided molecular evidence for the photo-responsive crosslinking motifs required for UV-triggered gelation (Fig. 1c). The UV-triggered gelation of LA-CS is attributed to the photo-induced cleavage and recombination of disulfide bonds in the grafted LA moieties. Under 365 nm UV irradiation, disulfide bonds generate reactive radicals and subsequently reform into a dynamic disulfide-crosslinked network, converting the injectable precursor into a stable hydrogel without an additional photoinitiator. Elemental mapping showed that sulfur was uniformly distributed throughout the hydrogel matrix, indicating homogeneous incorporation of LA-derived disulfide groups within the CS network (Fig. 1b). The microstructure of the solidified hydrogel was further examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The hydrogel exhibited an interconnected three-dimensional porous structure, which provides space for cell accommodation and facilitates the diffusion of oxygen, nutrients, and metabolic waste during nerve regeneration [23]. Moreover, this 3D porous network may facilitate RSC infiltration and intercellular paracrine communication, collectively establishing a permissive microenvironment for regenerative processes [24].

Overall, the acylation-mediated modification yielded LA-CS with improved water solubility and built-in photo-crosslinking functionality, enabling injectable, photoinitiator-free hydrogel formation suitable for biological applications.

Fabrication and characterization of the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduits

To obtain a soft and injectable luminal matrix for conduit filling, the mechanical properties of the LA-CS hydrogel matrix were tuned by varying polymer concentrations from 1% to 3% (w/v). Rheological analysis revealed a concentration-dependent increase in storage modulus. The 1% formulation showed relatively weak mechanical integrity, whereas the 3% hydrogel formed a denser network that may limit molecular diffusion. In comparison, the 2% LA-CS hydrogel exhibited a storage modulus of approximately 200 Pa, providing a soft and compliant luminal matrix favorable for neural cell accommodation and molecular transport within the conduit (Fig. 1e) [25].

Consistent with the rheological results, the swelling, degradation, and Mag release profiles were also dependent on hydrogel concentration. The 1% formulation swelled and degraded rapidly, accompanied by accelerated Mag release, indicating insufficient network stability. In contrast, the 3% formulation showed slower degradation and more restricted Mag release, suggesting reduced molecular transport within the denser matrix. The 2% formulation displayed a more balanced profile, with moderate degradation and sustained Mag release (Fig. 1f–h). Therefore, the 2% LA-CS formulation was selected for subsequent conduit fabrication, as it provided a suitable compromise between matrix stability, mechanical compliance, and bioactive molecule delivery.

To identify a suitable therapeutic dose of Mag, a concentration screening of Mag ranging from 0 to 200 μM was performed in RSCs. While minimal proliferative effects were observed after 24 h, prolonged incubation for 48 h revealed a time- and dose-dependent effect on cell viability, with cytotoxicity observed at concentrations of 100 μM and above (Fig. 2a). Accordingly, Mag was incorporated into the 2% LA-CS hydrogel at a final concentration of 50 μM. After uniform mixing under ultrasonic agitation, the LA-CS@Mag precursor solution was injected into the lumen of the aligned electrospun PCL conduit and then photocrosslinked in situ under UV irradiation. Macroscopic images showed that the LA-CS@Mag hydrogel could be smoothly introduced into the conduit lumen, forming an integrated LA-CS@Mag/PCL composite conduit (Fig. S2). To further characterize the PCL nanofiber membrane layer, SEM and EDS analyses were performed. The conduit wall exhibited an interconnected electrospun fibrous architecture, and elemental mapping showed homogeneous C and O distributions, indicating the structural integrity and compositional uniformity of the PCL sheath (Fig. S3).

Contact angle measurements were performed on the exposed luminal surface after longitudinally cutting the lyophilized conduits. Compared with the pristine PCL conduit, the LA-CS/PCL conduit showed a markedly reduced contact angle, indicating that the hydrogel-filled luminal surface provided a more hydrophilic microenvironment for cell–material interactions (Fig. 1d).

To further assess the impact of hydrogel incorporation on conduit performance, tensile and compressive tests were conducted on PCL and LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduits. The results showed that the LA-CS@Mag hydrogel-filled conduits exhibited improved mechanical performance compared with the pristine PCL conduit while maintaining sufficient flexibility for implantation (Fig. 1i and j). Quantitative analysis further confirmed that both the ultimate tensile strength and compressive modulus increased after hydrogel filling, with the 2% and 3% LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduits showing significantly higher values than the pristine PCL conduit (Fig. S4). As no significant difference was observed between the 2% and 3% groups, the 2% LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit was selected for subsequent experiments considering its balanced mechanical properties, degradation behavior, and release profile.

In vitro biocompatibility and proliferation assessment

RSCs play essential roles in peripheral nerve regeneration by regulating axonal guidance and myelination, while PC12 cells serve as a widely used neuronal model to evaluate neuronal differentiation and neurite outgrowth [26,27]. Both cell types are therefore commonly used to assess the biocompatibility and neuro-supportive properties of nerve repair materials [28]. To explore the combined effects of LA-CS and Mag on cell proliferation, CCK-8 assays were performed using extracts from LA-CS and LA-CS@Mag hydrogels in RSCs and PC12 cells. The results showed that, in RSCs, the LA-CS extract alone significantly enhanced cell viability after 48 h of incubation, whereas incorporation of Mag further increased RSC viability (Fig. S5a and b). In contrast, in PC12 cells, the LA-CS extract showed limited effects on cell viability at 48 h, while Mag-containing extracts markedly promoted PC12 cell proliferation (Fig. S5c). These results indicate a cell-type-dependent pro-proliferative effect of LA-CS and Mag on RSCs and neuronal cells.

To further validate cytocompatibility in a direct-contact microenvironment, Live/Dead and cytoskeletal staining were performed on cells co-cultured with LA-CS/PCL and LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduits (Fig. 2b and Fig. S5d and e). All groups exhibited a high density of viable cells with few dead cells, along with healthy and well-spread morphologies characterized by organized actin filaments. Collectively, these results confirm that the LA-CS/PCL and LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduits provide cytocompatible and structurally permissive microenvironments supportive of neural tissue regeneration.

Enhancement of neurogenic differentiation and Schwann cell migration

To assess cellular maturation and motility following the observed proliferative responses, immunofluorescence staining was performed to evaluate neurogenic differentiation and Schwann cell phenotype [29]. Compared with the control and Mag groups, the LA-CS@Mag hydrogel markedly enhanced neurite outgrowth in PC12 cells, accompanied by significantly elevated expression of neuronal markers Tuj1 and NeuN (Fig. 2c–e). Further quantification of marker-positive cells showed that the percentages of Tuj1-and NeuN-positive PC12 cells were also significantly increased in the LA-CS@Mag group compared with the control and Mag groups (Fig. S6). These findings indicate that the composite system promotes neuronal maturation beyond proliferative support alone. In parallel, RSCs cultured on the composite hydrogel exhibited upregulated expression of Nestin and GFAP, suggesting induction of a repair-associated, activated phenotype characterized by enhanced progenitor-like plasticity and cytoskeletal remodeling (Fig. 2f–h). Consistently, the proportions of Nestin- and GFAP-positive RSCs were significantly higher in the LA-CS@Mag group than in the control and Mag groups (Fig. S6). When considered together with the increased cell viability observed in CCK-8 assays, these phenotypic changes suggest that the hydrogel primes RSCs toward a functionally competent state for supporting axonal regeneration.

Given that such phenotypic reprogramming is functionally coupled to directed migration, a prerequisite for bridging nerve gaps, the migratory capacity of RSCs was systematically examined [30,31]. At 48 h, both scratch wound healing and Transwell assays demonstrated significantly enhanced cell motility, with the LA-CS@Mag group exhibiting accelerated wound closure and increased transmembrane migration compared with the Mag and control groups (Fig. 2i–l).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that the functionalized hydrogel not only supports cellular proliferation but also enhances neuronal differentiation and Schwann cell migration, two processes essential for axonal guidance and the formation of Bands of Büngner [32]. These pro-regenerative phenotypes provide a functional rationale for further examining the mitochondrial mechanisms underlying the effects of LA-CS@Mag.

Mitophagy-associated mitochondrial quality control drives metabolic reprogramming

Previous studies have demonstrated that Mag induces autophagy, particularly PINK1–Parkin–mediated mitophagy, as evidenced by mitochondrial depolarization, Parkin translocation, and degradation of mitochondrial proteins. Importantly, genetic knockdown of PINK1 or Parkin, as well as pharmacological inhibition of mitophagy, markedly attenuated Mag-induced mitochondrial clearance, thereby functionally validating the involvement of mitophagy [20,21,33,34].

Because mitochondrial fission helps isolate damaged mitochondrial segments for subsequent lysosomal clearance, we next examined whether interfering with mitochondrial dynamics would affect Mag-induced mitophagic responses. To evaluate mitochondria–lysosome association as an indicator of mitophagic activity, co-staining of mitochondria (MitoTracker Green) and lysosomes (LysoTracker Red) was performed under H2O2-induced oxidative stress. While oxidative stress disrupted mitochondrial–lysosomal association, Mag treatment induced pronounced spatial co-localization of mitochondria and lysosomes, manifested as prominent yellow fluorescence. Importantly, this pro-mitophagic response was significantly attenuated by Mdivi-1, a selective inhibitor of mitochondrial fission, indicating that Mag-induced mitophagy-associated responses are closely linked to intact mitochondrial dynamics (Fig. 3b and c). Consistent with these observations, immunofluorescence analysis revealed that Mag markedly upregulated key mitophagy- and autophagy-associated proteins, including BNIP3, Parkin, LC3B, and ATG5, together with dynamic modulation of the autophagic adaptor p62 (Fig. S7) [21]. To further validate these molecular changes at the protein level, Western blot analysis was performed for BNIP3, Parkin, p62, LC3B, and ATG5. The results showed that Mag treatment increased the expression of mitophagy/autophagy-related proteins under oxidative stress, while the LA-CS@Mag group exhibited the most pronounced changes, including increased BNIP3 and Parkin expression, an elevated LC3-II/LC3-I ratio, and increased ATG5 levels. In contrast, Mdivi-1 treatment attenuated these protein-level responses (Fig. S8). These results further support that LA-CS@Mag promotes mitophagy-associated mitochondrial quality control in RSCs.

Given the role of mitophagy in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis, the functional consequences on cellular bioenergetics were further examined under oxidative stress conditions. ATP assays demonstrated that LA, LA-CS hydrogel, and Mag treatment each partially restored ATP levels in RSCs exposed to H2O2, whereas the LA-CS@Mag hydrogel group exhibited the most pronounced recovery of ATP production, indicating a coordinated benefit of Mag and LA-CS hydrogel in supporting cellular energy metabolism (Fig. 3i). Consistently, JC-1 staining revealed that LA-CS@Mag treatment more effectively restored mitochondrial membrane potential than Mag alone, as evidenced by reduced JC-1 monomer signals and increased JC-1 aggregate formation (Fig. 3d–f). Similarly, intracellular ROS levels were lowest in the LA-CS@Mag group compared with the H2O2 and Mag-treated groups (Fig. 3g and h). Importantly, pharmacological inhibition of mitochondrial fission with Mdivi-1 significantly attenuated these protective effects, leading to impaired mitochondrial membrane potential and elevated ROS levels.

Finally, transmission electron microscopy (TEM) provided ultrastructural validation. Under oxidative stress conditions, cells treated with LA-CS@Mag exhibited predominantly oval-shaped mitochondria with relatively preserved ultrastructure, consistent with improved mitochondrial homeostasis rather than pathological swelling (Fig. 3j) [36]. Collectively, these findings indicate that LA-CS@Mag engages a mitophagy-associated mitochondrial quality control program, which is associated with improved mitochondrial homeostasis and bioenergetic support in RSCs (Fig. 3a).

LA-CS@Mag promotes immunomodulatory and regenerative microenvironment remodeling

Mitochondrial dysfunction in injured SCs is not only a cell-intrinsic metabolic defect, but may also contribute to neuroimmune activation by enhancing ROS production and mitochondrial danger signaling [37,38]. Since SCs actively communicate with macrophages during peripheral nerve repair, we next asked whether LA-CS@Mag-mediated mitochondrial quality control in RSCs could reshape macrophage polarization through paracrine regulation [39]. To test this possibility, conditioned medium was collected from RSCs exposed to H2O2 and treated with Mag, LA-CS@Mag, or LA-CS@Mag plus Mdivi-1, and was then applied to RAW264.7 macrophages (Fig. S9a).

Immunofluorescence staining showed that conditioned medium from H2O2-injured RSCs increased the percentages of CD86−and iNOS-positive macrophages, indicating an M1-like inflammatory response. In contrast, conditioned medium from LA-CS@Mag-treated RSCs reduced CD86/iNOS-positive cells and increased CD206/Arg1-positive cells, suggesting a shift toward an M2-like reparative phenotype (Fig. S9b and c). This effect was partially attenuated by Mdivi-1, supporting the contribution of mitochondrial quality control in RSCs to macrophage-regulatory paracrine signaling.

To further examine the broader molecular programs associated with this immunomodulatory effect, RNA sequencing was performed. PCA revealed clear separation between LA-CS@Mag and control groups, indicating distinct transcriptomic profiles and high intragroup consistency (Fig. 4a). The volcano plot demonstrated substantial numbers of significantly upregulated and downregulated transcripts (Fig. 4b), while heatmap analysis further confirmed treatment-specific expression patterns (Fig. 4c). Functional enrichment analysis showed that differentially expressed genes were primarily associated with immune response and synaptic vesicle regulation (Fig. 4d). Notably, pathways involved in cellular iron ion homeostasis and ferric iron binding were also positively enriched, suggesting coordinated regulation of iron-related metabolic processes, which are closely linked to mitochondrial function and redox balance (Fig. 4f and g). In parallel, enrichment of cytokine receptor activity–related gene sets was observed, consistent with transcriptional modulation of inflammatory signaling pathways (Fig. 4h). To validate the RNA-seq results, representative differentially expressed genes covering the major enriched biological processes, including immune regulation, redox/iron homeostasis, energy metabolism, and regenerative microenvironment remodeling, were selected for qRT-PCR analysis. Consistent with the sequencing data, LA-CS@Mag significantly upregulated Socs3, Cebpb, Fth1, Gstp1, Ldha, and Ccn1, while downregulating Mmp13, Spp1, and Egr1 (Fig. S10). These results support the reliability of the transcriptomic analysis. Collectively, these findings indicate that LA-CS@Mag treatment is associated with coordinated transcriptional reprogramming involving metabolic homeostasis, immune signaling regulation, and regenerative microenvironment support, which is consistent with the macrophage polarization results described above.

Macroscopic observation and functional recovery assessment

To comprehensively evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the bioactive NGC in vivo, a 10-mm sciatic nerve defect model was established in adult Sprague–Dawley rats, which were randomly assigned to the PCL, LA-CS/PCL, LA-CS@Mag/PCL, and autograft groups (Fig. 5a) [40]. Postoperative motor function recovery was longitudinally assessed at weeks 4, 8, and 12 using walking track analysis (Fig. 5b) [41]. Rats in the PCL group exhibited persistent dragging gait and narrow footprints, indicative of severe functional impairment, whereas animals treated with the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit displayed progressive functional improvement characterized by clear toe spreading and distinct heel impressions (Fig. 5c). Consistent with the walking track results, Open Field Test (OFT) analysis demonstrated that rats treated with the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit exhibited increased total travel distance, prolonged time spent in the central area, increased distance traveled in the central area, and reduced immobility time compared with the PCL and LA-CS/PCL groups, indicating improved locomotor performance and behavioral recovery (Fig. 5d–h). To further assess the physiological quality of nerve regeneration, electrophysiological evaluations were performed at week 12. The LA-CS@Mag/PCL group displayed significantly higher nerve conduction velocity (NCV) and distal compound muscle action potential (DCMAP) amplitudes than the PCL and LA-CS/PCL groups, confirming enhanced axonal conduction and effective muscle reinnervation (Fig. 5k and l) [42].

Given that denervation resulting from peripheral nerve injury leads to progressive gastrocnemius muscle atrophy, and that restoration of sciatic nerve innervation is closely associated with recovery of muscle function, the wet weight ratio and morphology of the gastrocnemius muscle were further examined [41,42]. Gastrocnemius muscle on the surgical side (right) showed atrophy with varying degrees compared with those on the normal side (left) in each group (Fig. 5i). Quantitative analysis revealed a marked reduction in the gastrocnemius muscle wet weight ratio in the PCL group (30.00 ± 4.00). In comparison, the LA-CS/PCL group exhibited a moderate increase in muscle mass (49.33 ± 4.04), whereas treatment with the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit resulted in a substantially higher wet weight ratio (67.33 ± 3.51), approaching that of the autograft group (80.33 ± 3.51) (Fig. 5j). These findings were consistent with the macroscopic observations, indicating effective preservation of target muscle following nerve regeneration. Although the autograft group still showed the best overall recovery, the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit achieved a therapeutic effect approaching that of autograft.

To quantitatively evaluate the maturity of remyelination, transverse sections were analyzed via TEM. Mitophagy-associated reprogramming of the inflammatory microenvironment in vivo

To determine whether the mitochondrial quality-control response observed in vitro was recapitulated within the regenerative microenvironment in vivo, the autophagy/mitophagy status of regenerated nerve tissues was first examined. Immunohistochemical analysis revealed that the LA-CS@Mag/PCL group exhibited enhanced activation of autophagy- and mitophagy-associated pathways. Specifically, the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit induced coordinated upregulation of autophagic markers LC3B and ATG5, together with selective mitophagy regulators Parkin and BNIP3. These changes were accompanied by modulation of the autophagic adaptor p62, consistent with enhanced autophagic activity (Fig. S11). Co-administration of Mdivi-1 markedly attenuated these molecular signatures, in agreement with the in vitro findings and supporting the involvement of mitochondrial fission-related dynamics in LA-CS@Mag-mediated mitochondrial quality control in vivo.

As a key mechanism for maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and limiting inflammatory danger signals, mitophagy is closely linked to immune regulation following PNI [44,45]. In this context, macrophages exhibit dynamic polarization during nerve repair, with a pro-inflammatory (M1-like) phenotype dominating the early phase (approximately 1 week post-injury) to facilitate debris clearance, and a pro-healing (M2-like) phenotype becoming predominant during the later regenerative stage (around 4 weeks post-injury) [46,47]. Although transient inflammation is required for effective repair, sustained inflammation is detrimental to axonal regeneration [48]. Accordingly, iNOS+ M1-like macrophages were evaluated at 1 week post-injury, whereas Arg1+, CD163+, and CD206+ M2-like macrophages were assessed at 4 weeks post-injury. At 1 week post-injury, the percentage of iNOS-positive macrophages in the LA-CS@Mag/PCL group was significantly lower than those in the PCL and LA-CS/PCL groups, but remained higher than that observed in the autograft group (Fig. 7b and c). In contrast, at 4 weeks post-injury, the LA-CS@Mag/PCL group exhibited a higher proportion of CD206-, CD163-, and Arg1-positive macrophages compared with the PCL and LA-CS/PCL groups, approaching levels comparable to those of the autograft group (Fig. 7d–g). Moreover, IL-6 immunostaining was performed on regenerated nerve tissues. In vivo biocompatibility and systemic Safety assessment

Histological examination of major organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, showed no obvious pathological changes among the PCL, LA-CS/PCL, LA-CS@Mag/PCL, and autograft groups (Fig. S12). These findings suggest that implantation of the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit did not cause detectable systemic toxicity under the present experimental conditions, supporting its favorable in vivo biocompatibility.

Conclusion

In this study, we developed a metabolically instructive nerve guidance conduit based on a fuel–maintenance coupling strategy to address the metabolic bottleneck limiting peripheral nerve regeneration. By integrating α-lipoic acid–mediated metabolic support with magnolol-associated mitochondrial quality control, the LA-CS@Mag/PCL conduit effectively restored mitochondrial bioenergetics, attenuated oxidative stress, and enhanced mitophagy-associated mitochondrial homeostasis in RSCs. Moreover, LA-CS@Mag-treated RSCs promoted macrophage polarization toward a reparative phenotype through paracrine regulation, indicating a link between Schwann cell mitochondrial quality control and immune microenvironment remodeling. In vivo, these coordinated metabolic and immunomodulatory effects translated into enhanced axonal regeneration, remyelination, target muscle preservation, and functional recovery approaching autograft performance. Collectively, this work identifies mitochondrial homeostasis as a therapeutic target and provides a metabolically instructive framework for the design of next-generation bioactive nerve guidance conduits.
