The healing process of vertebral body fracture in Wistar rats: creation of an animal model and demonstration of the impact of anterolateral vascularization disruption on bone healing
Authors: Diogo Lino Moura, Diogo Casal, Sara Alves, Rui Almeida, Diogo Pais, José Casanova, António Bernardes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAlthough recognition of avascular necrosis of the vertebral body in post-traumatic cases has increased, it remains underdiagnosed and is one of the most unpredictable and challenging complications in spinal trauma. Vertebral arterial supply may play a key role in fracture healing, yet this remains unproven and is not currently considered in treatment algorithms. Surgical decisions between preserving or replacing the vertebral body in burst fractures are difficult, mainly due to limited knowledge of the biological factors influencing bone repair. This study aims to demonstrate the impact of vertebral vascular disruption on vertebral body fracture healing. We developed an experimental model in Wistar rats to replicate an L1 vertebral body fracture and analyze the normal healing sequence. Additionally, we examined the effects of disrupting the anterolateral blood supply on bone regeneration.MethodsSeventy female Wistar rats were divided into two groups. Group 1 (n=35) underwent an L1 burst fracture induced by ultrasonic tools. Group 2 (n=35) received the same fracture, followed by electrocauterization of the anterolateral vertebral surfaces and placement of a synthetic barrier to prevent revascularization. Vertebral specimens were collected weekly for 6 weeks. Healing was assessed macroscopically and histologically using an image-processing algorithm trained to identify inflammatory, fibroblastic/cartilaginous, and bone tissue. The predominant tissue type was used to determine the healing stage.ResultsAt week 1, the vascular disruption group showed significantly more inflammatory tissue (56.34%) than controls (24.25%, P=0.03), while fibroblastic/cartilaginous tissue was more common in controls (58.82% vs. 19.18%, P=0.03). By week 6, this tissue remained more prevalent in the intervention group (37.4%), while bone tissue predominated in controls (66.71% vs. 45.54%, P=0.009). Among animals already in the bone phase, trabecular structures were significantly more developed in controls (80% vs. 20%, P=0.031). Notably, all control animals reached the soft callus stage by week 1, whereas intervention animals only transitioned out of the inflammatory phase after the first week. Across all phases, progression was consistently faster in the control group, with statistical significance in the soft callus stage (P=0.002).ConclusionsDisruption of the anterolateral vascular supply significantly delays healing in L1 vertebral body fractures in rats, evidenced by slower phase transitions and reduced bone maturation. These findings underscore the essential role of vascularization in successful vertebral repair and suggest it should be considered in future therapeutic strategies.Keywords: Vertebral body, fracture, healing, vascular disruption, regeneration delay

Highlight box

Key findings

• Disruption of the anterolateral vascular supply significantly impairs bone healing in an L1 vertebral body fracture model in Wistar rats. This was evidenced by statistically significant delays across key stages of repair—including inflammation, soft callus formation, hard callus development, and remodeling—compared to controls. These findings underscore the relevance of vascular integrity in the evaluation and treatment planning of vertebral fractures.

What is known and what is new?

• While avascular necrosis of the vertebral body after trauma is increasingly recognized, it remains underdiagnosed and represents one of the most complex challenges in spinal trauma care. Currently, the choice between vertebral body preservation or replacement in burst fractures lacks reliable biological predictors of healing. Although vascularization is known to influence general fracture healing, the specific impact of intraosseous vascular injury in vertebral fractures has not been clearly defined or integrated into decision-making processes.

• This study offers new evidence showing that injury to the anterolateral vascular supply delays bone regeneration in vertebral body fractures, highlighting its importance as a prognostic factor in spinal trauma.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• The delayed healing observed in the intervention group suggests that vascular injury may contribute to clinical complications such as delayed union, non-union, or pseudarthrosis. Recognizing vascular damage as a critical risk factor could improve patient outcomes. Future research should focus on quantifying vascular compromise after vertebral fractures and incorporating this data into early treatment algorithms, supporting more personalized and effective strategies to prevent post-traumatic vertebral necrosis.

Introduction

The incidence of post-traumatic avascular necrosis of the vertebral body is gaining greater recognition, likely due to the increasing age of the population. This condition is most often observed at the thoracolumbar junction (T12 and L1) and tends to occur more frequently in elderly individuals, especially those affected by osteoporosis. Despite its clinical relevance, avascular necrosis remains significantly underdiagnosed. Existing studies have reported its presence in approximately 7% to 37% of vertebral compression fractures. It is more frequently associated with highly fragmented fractures, cases exhibiting severe vertebral body collapse, and injuries occurring in the less vascularized zones of the vertebral structure. These characteristics contribute to a higher likelihood of impaired bone healing, turning them into risk factors for delayed union, non-union, and the development of pseudarthrosis. Avascular necrosis represents a breakdown in the normal vertebral bone healing process and continues to pose one of the most difficult and unpredictable challenges in the management of spinal trauma (1-8).

The variables that predict successful bone healing following a complete burst fracture, classified as A4 in the AOSpine system (9), remain poorly defined. As a result, current surgical decisions between vertebral body-preserving or replacement surgery are predominantly based on surgeon and institutional experience and preference. Some centers favor stabilization techniques, whether short- or long-segment fixations, whereas others routinely proceed with ad initium vertebral body replacement, in the absence of a universally accepted, evidence-based treatment algorithm (10-22). To enhance the clinical management of these complex fractures, it is crucial to develop standardized treatment pathways, beginning with the identification of reliable prognostic markers for vertebral body bone healing from the outset. Early recognition of such predictors would allow for more precise selection between preserving the fractured vertebral body or opting directly for replacement surgery. This approach aims to avoid preserving a fractured vertebral body when healing is unlikely, allowing for its removal and replacement from the beginning. This strategy helps prevent complications such as non-union, avascular necrosis, vertebral collapse, and the associated risks of functional impairment and neurological injury (1-8).

Several researchers have suggested that vertebral arterial vascularization and nutritional supply are key biological factors influencing whether a vertebral fracture proceeds towards successful bone healing or evolves into necrosis (1-7,13,23-29). Despite supportive evidence from numerous in vivo animal studies highlighting the importance of vascular supply in bone repair, this concept remains largely theoretical in the context of human clinical application. As a result, vascular integrity is not yet integrated into the current decision-making framework when evaluating whether to pursue early vertebral body replacement (either interior or total replacement), which continues to be guided predominantly by the mechanical features of the fracture (1-7,13,30-41). At present, no diagnostic tools are available to biologically assess whether a specific fracture pattern has disrupted the vertebral body’s intraosseous blood supply to an extent that would significantly impair bone healing and increase the risk of delayed union or non-union (7). In our previous research, we found that most cases of post-traumatic vertebral osteonecrosis originated from burst fractures, accounting for 82.9% of the observed instances (1,2,8,13,29).

Following this line of thought, it is reasonable to infer that vertebral complete burst fractures [those involving the entire vertebral body, including both endplates and the posterior wall, as classified by AOSpine type A4 (9), and often characterized by high degrees of comminution] are highly likely to compromise the intraosseous vascular integrity of the vertebral body. This injured vascularity may render the biological environment insufficient to support proper bone healing. Our group therefore considers that, in this specific type of vertebral fractures, opting for vertebral body replacement as the initial fracture treatment appears to be a rational and preventive strategy. This may include interior vertebral body replacement through kyphoplasty or application of expandable intravertebral implants, or total replacement via corpectomy and anterior cage reconstruction. Implementing early vertebral body replacement in such high-risk fractures could act as a safeguard against the development of non-union and pseudarthrosis, helping to prevent one of the most complex and unpredictable complications in spine trauma management (24,25,42,43). In order to support and reinforce this therapeutic strategy, it is essential to demonstrate the biological relevance of intraosseous microvascular injury within the vertebral body as a predictive factor for bone healing capacity. Establishing this link would be particularly impactful in guiding the management of the prevalent thoracolumbar burst fractures, most notably in determining whether the fractured vertebral body should be preserved or replaced during initial surgical planning.

To explore this hypothesis, our initial objective is to develop a reproducible model of a complete L1 burst fracture using Wistar rats, allowing for a detailed analysis of the natural progression of vertebral bone healing over time. This animal model serves as a foundational step to characterize, in depth, the sequential biological events involved in bone repair, such as inflammation, soft and hard callus formation, and bone remodeling, and well as to define the typical timeline associated with each healing stage. Wistar rats were selected due to their widespread use in biomedical research, their easy availability and, most importantly, the anatomical similarity of their lumbar vertebral blood supply to that of humans, as demonstrated by previous studies including our research (1-8,44).

The L1 vertebral body was chosen because it is the most frequently affected vertebra in human vertebral compression fractures and is also one of the most susceptible to post-traumatic avascular necrosis, as supported by earlier findings (1-8,45). Following the establishment of this baseline fracture model and understanding of its typical healing dynamics, our next step is to investigate whether and how a targeted disruption of the anterolateral vascular supply alters this repair process. Specifically, we will compare the timing and quantitative features of each stage of bone healing between the control group, undergoing a standard L1 burst fracture, and the intervention group, in which the same fracture is accompanied by an induced anterolateral vascular injury. This comparative analysis aims to determine whether vascular compromise significantly impacts the healing capacity of the vertebral body. We present this article in accordance with the ARRIVE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-25-55/rc).

Methods

This study involved a cohort of seventy female Wistar rats obtained from Charles River Laboratories, each 3 months old and weighing between 250 and 350 grams. All animals were housed under standard environmental conditions and were subjected to a fasting period of 6 hours prior to surgical procedures. All in vivo studies strictly adhered to the recommendations outlined in the Guide for Proper Conduct of Animal Experiments and Related Activities in Academic Research and Technology [2006]. All animal experiments were performed under project licenses granted by the institutional ethics committee of Nova Medical School, Lisbon, Portugal (135/2019/CEFCM) and Faculty of Medicine, University of Coimbra, Coimbra, Portugal (CE-141/2023/FMUC), in compliance with Directorate General of Food and Veterinary national guidelines for the care and use of animals. A protocol including the research question, key design features, and analysis plan was prepared before the study without registration.

Surgical description of the induced L1 vertebral body fracture

A total of 35 rats underwent an anterior retroperitoneal abdominal approach to access the L1 vertebral body. Identification of L1 was performed by its anatomical position directly below T12, the last thoracic vertebra bearing the 12th rib (Figure 1A). Subsequent dissection allowed for full exposure of the anterolateral surfaces of L1, as well as the anterior margins of the adjacent intervertebral discs (T12–L1 and L1–L2) (Figure 1B). A small incision was made in the anterior annulus fibrosus to better delineate the borders of the L1 vertebral body. To replicate a complete comminuted burst fracture [classified as A4 under the AOSpine system (9), encompassing both endplates and the posterior vertebral wall], a series of precise steps were followed (Figure 1). First, a central perforation was created using an ultrasonic vibratory hook shaver (1.8 mm × 1.3 mm), starting at the anterior midline ridge and extending to the posterior vertebral wall, with the final defect corresponding to the shaver’s dimensions (Figure 1C). The purpose of this central perforation was to replicate the comminution typically seen in burst fractures (9). Then, fracture lines were induced using an ultrasonic vibratory blade (10 mm × 5 mm × 0.5 mm), beginning with a vertical sagittal cut extending from the superior to the inferior endplate (Figure 1D, D1), followed by a transverse median cut spanning the vertebral width, thereby generating a cross-pattern fracture through the vertebral body (Figure 1D, D2). Throughout the osteotomy procedures, performed with ultrasonic vibratory tools to replicate the main fracture lines of a complete burst fracture, cold saline irrigation was continuously applied to minimize thermal injury to the spinal cord and reduce the risk of neurological damage.

Surgical procedure of L1 burst fracture creation: (A) identification of the L1 vertebral body, the vertebra below T12, after locating the 12th rib (large arrow); (B) anterior dissection and complete exposure of the anterolateral surfaces of the L1 vertebral body; (C) creation of a hole at the center of the vertebral body (simulating the comminution of burst fractures) using an ultrasonic vibratory 1.8 mm × 1.3 mm hook shaver, starting from the most prominent point of the anterior median ridge and extending to the posterior wall; (D) creation of fracture lines with ultrasonic vibratory 10 mm × 5 mm × 0.5 mm blade: (D1) sagittal median cut from the superior endplate to the inferior endplate, starting at the anterior median ridge and ending at the posterior wall; (D2) transverse median cut from one side to the other, completing a cross-shaped fracture at the center of the vertebral body.

Surgical description of the intervention on the anterolateral surfaces of the fractured L1 vertebral body

In a separate cohort of 35 Wistar rats, we followed the same procedure for inducing a vertebral burst fracture as previously described and shown in Figures 1-3. In addition to the fracture creation, we performed targeted electrocauterization along the entire anterolateral surfaces of the L1 vertebral body with the objective of eliminating any vessels penetrating from the anterolateral direction (Figure 2B). To prevent thermal injury to the spinal cord and minimize the risk of neurological damage, this electrocautery was carried out simultaneously with the application of chilled saline solution. Following electrocauterization, we sutured a plastic barrier over the full extent of the anterolateral vertebral surfaces (Figures 2C,3D,3E). This step was intended to physically block revascularization from the anterior side and to ensure that the contribution of anterolateral arterial inflow was entirely excluded from the vertebral bone healing process.

Experimental groups and bone healing assessment methods

Based on this design, two experimental groups were established: a control group, in which the L1 burst fracture was created and allowed to follow its natural bone healing trajectory; and an intervention group, where, after inducing the same fracture, we deliberately impaired the anterolateral vascular supply and mechanically isolated the vertebral body anterolateral surfaces using a plastic barrier, in order to study the role of anterolateral vascular input in bone regeneration. Subsequently, the animals were sequentially euthanized via anesthetic overdose at weekly intervals, spanning from 1 to 6 weeks post-intervention (i.e., at 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6 weeks). At each designated time point, the L1 vertebral body was carefully harvested for analysis of bone healing progression. Two complementary assessment methods were employed: macroscopic mechanical evaluation and histological examination. The macroscopic analysis involved manual inspection to assess structural rigidity and intrinsic stability. This included palpation and resistance testing using mosquito forceps to apply compressive pressure, aiming to detect any independent motion among the four fracture segments of the vertebral body. Based on this evaluation, each specimen was classified as either structurally stable or unstable, depending on the observed integrity under mechanical stress.

For histological assessment, the harvested L1 vertebral bodies were first fixed in a 10% paraformaldehyde solution and subsequently decalcified using an 8% hydrochloric acid and formic acid mixture. Axial sections were then obtained from the mid-portion of each vertebra. The tissue sections underwent standard staining procedures using hematoxylin-eosin and Masson’s trichrome to visualize the structural and cellular components of the healing tissue. The stained slides were scanned at high resolution and analyzed using QuPath version 0.5.1, an open-source software platform for digital pathology. This tool enabled comprehensive whole slide image analysis, including region segmentation, feature extraction, and classification through machine learning. A Random Trees Pixel Classifier was developed and trained on a dataset of 495 manually labeled annotations to distinguish between the different histological phases of fracture healing. The classifier differentiated tissue types based on both color and morphological patterns identified within the labeled regions. The fracture zone within each axial section was manually delineated prior to classification. The algorithm was then used to quantify the percentage of the fracture area occupied by tissues corresponding to specific stages of healing: inflammatory, soft callus, hard callus, and bone remodeling. Tissue annotations included inflammatory and granulation tissues (e.g., erythrocytes, leukocytes, macrophages, and endothelial cells), fibroblastic and cartilaginous tissues (e.g., fibroblasts, chondroblasts, chondrocytes), and bone tissues (e.g., osteoblasts, osteocytes, and osteoclasts). The bone remodeling stage was characterized by the presence of mature, organized trabecular bone. To enhance classification accuracy, hematopoietic bone marrow was specifically excluded from analysis to prevent misclassification, due to its histological resemblance to inflammatory tissue. The predominant tissue type within each fracture zone was used to define the corresponding healing phase for that sample. Because of the close pixel-based similarity between hard callus and bone remodeling phases, the algorithm often struggled to differentiate them. To address this, a morphological evaluation of trabecular bone content was conducted in samples where bone was already the predominant tissue. If ≥50% of the fracture area contained well-defined trabeculae, the sample was categorized as being in the remodeling phase. If trabeculae were present in <50%, with predominance of immature bone, the phase was classified as hard callus.

In addition to the quantitative analysis, a qualitative assessment for necrosis within the fracture bed was also performed, though restricted to animals sacrificed at or beyond the 4-week mark, to avoid confusion with early-stage healing features. Necrosis identification was based on the presence of loss of nuclear staining (karyolysis, pyknosis), increased eosinophilia of the cytoplasm, and disruption of normal tissue architecture. The final dataset included both the percent composition of each healing tissue within the defined fracture area and qualitative indicators such as the presence of trabeculae and evidence of necrosis, offering a comprehensive overview of the bone healing status in each case.

Median values were computed for each time point in the fracture healing timeline (from 1 to 6 weeks), and the results were organized into tables (Tables 1-3) and visualized through graphical representations (Figures 4,5). These illustrated the progression of individual histological parameters throughout the bone healing process in both study groups: the control group, reflecting the normal healing trajectory, and the intervention group, which underwent anterolateral vascular disruption. Subsequently, statistical comparisons were conducted to identify significant differences in the measured parameters across the various time points within each group, as well as between groups at corresponding time intervals. This approach was designed to evaluate whether the anterolateral vascular injury had a measurable impact on the typical healing process of vertebral body fractures.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality testing using the Shapiro-Wilk test revealed non-normal distributions across most variables, prompting the use of nonparametric statistical methods. A P value of less than 0.05 was considered the threshold for statistical significance.

Results

Of the 35 Wistar rats subjected to the L1 burst fracture model procedure, constituting the control group, two animals did not survive the surgical intervention, and three others died within the first 48 hours postoperatively. The likely causes of these deaths were diaphragmatic injury and intraoperative hemorrhage, particularly in the earlier surgeries; however, spinal cord trauma may also have contributed to the fatal outcomes. Among the surviving animals, neurological deficits were observed in 7 rats (23.33%), consisting of paraplegia in 4 rats (13.33%) and unilateral hindlimb monoplegia in 3 rats (10%). During histological analysis, four vertebral sections were excluded due to the absence of a clearly identifiable fracture area, which rendered them unsuitable for evaluation. As a result, a total of 26 vertebral sections with visible fracture regions were included in the final histological assessment of bone healing, thereby defining the final sample size for the control group. These 26 cases were divided into six fracture duration groups: four groups, each with four specimens, corresponding to weeks 1 through 4, and two groups (weeks 5 and 6), each with five specimens. The histological results of the L1 vertebral fracture model in Wistar rats are summarized in Table 1, and the progression of each bone healing parameter over the 6-week fracture period is graphically depicted in Figure 4. As detailed in the Methods section, the healing stage for each animal (Figures 6-10) was determined by the predominant tissue type within the fracture area, which is indicated by an asterisk in Table 1. It was observed that all 12 rats euthanized during the first 3 weeks were in the soft callus stage of bone healing (Figure 7). Beginning at week 4, all animals demonstrated a predominance of bone tissue. In the fourth week, 3 out of 4 rats were classified as being in the hard callus stage (Figure 8), while 1 had progressed to bone remodeling (Figures 9,10). By weeks 5 and 6, all 10 remaining rats were determined to be in the bone remodeling phase. In Figure 4, the initial dominance of fibroblastic and cartilaginous tissue is clearly visible during the first 3 weeks, reaching its peak at week 3 before beginning to decline. The inflammatory component follows a consistent downward trend, remaining the second most prevalent tissue type until shortly after the third week. From that point onward, the bone tissue component begins to increase markedly, surpassing the fibrocartilaginous tissue just before week 4 and becoming the most abundant element. This predominance remains stable between weeks 4 and 5, followed by another sharp increase beginning in week 5. The presence of histological signs of necrosis was assessed in all specimens corresponding to fractures of 4 weeks or longer (n=14 in this group). Necrosis was detected in only 1 case (7.14%), corresponding to a specimen from a 6-week fracture in the bone remodeling phase.

Of the 35 rats subjected to the experimental protocol involving L1 burst fracture induction followed by anterolateral vascular injury, three animals did not survive the surgery, and two died within the first 48 hours postoperatively. The most likely causes of death were intraoperative hemorrhage and spinal cord injury. Among the surviving animals, neurological impairment was observed in 8 rats (26.67%), including paraplegia in 4 rats (13.33%) and unilateral hindlimb monoplegia in 4 rats (13.33%). Three histological sections were excluded due to the absence of a clearly visible fracture area, rendering them unsuitable for evaluation. Consequently, a total of 27 sections with identifiable fracture zones were included in the final histological analysis of bone healing, which established the definitive sample size for the intervention group. These were distributed into six subgroups according to fracture duration: four subgroups with four specimens each (weeks 1, 2, 3, and 4), one subgroup with five specimens (week 5), and one subgroup with six specimens (week 6). The histological results of the intervention group are presented in Table 2, and the evolution of each bone healing parameter over the 6-week fracture period is illustrated in Figure 4. As in the control group, the bone healing stage for each specimen was determined by the predominant tissue type, marked with an asterisk in Table 2. All four rats euthanized at week 1 were classified as being in the inflammatory phase (Figure 6). The soft callus phase (Figure 7) was identified beginning in week 2 and was the only healing stage present among specimens from weeks 2 and 3. This phase progressively decreased in representation over time, being observed in 75% of cases at week 4, 60% at week 5, and 33.33% at week 6. The hard callus stage (Figure 8) was observed in only one case during week 4, in 40% of week 5 samples, and in 33.33% of week 6 specimens. Notably, the bone remodeling phase (Figures 9,10) was identified exclusively at week 6, in 33.33% of the rats in this group. In Figure 4, it can be observed that during week 1, the inflammatory component is predominant, reaching its highest value at this point. It then follows a steep declining trend, stabilizing by week 3. In contrast, the fibrocartilaginous component displays an upward trajectory starting in week 1, surpassing the inflammatory component shortly before week 2, and peaking at week 3. From week 3 onward, the fibroblastic component begins a gradual decline, although it remains the most prevalent tissue until midway between weeks 5 and 6, at which point it is overtaken by the bone tissue component, which then becomes the dominant tissue. The bone component itself remains relatively constant from week 1 through week 4, after which it undergoes a marked increase, continuing to rise up to week 6, where it is clearly the predominant component within the fracture area. The presence of necrosis was evaluated exclusively in animals with fracture durations of 4 weeks or more (n=15). Necrotic features were observed in 1 rat (20%) in the 5-week group and in two rats (33.33%) in the 6-week group (Figure 11). Histological analysis of the specimens from the intervention group identified a well-defined interface between the applied plastic barrier and the surrounding tissues, with no evidence of invasion by organic tissue (Figure 12).

Discussion

Fracture healing is a highly complex biological process, the progression and nature of which are influenced by numerous contributing factors. Experimental models employing animal subjects are commonly utilized to investigate and anticipate fracture repair mechanisms in humans. However, interspecies differences, such as variations in bone regeneration pathways, rates of healing, growth cycles, and skeletal biology, pose significant considerations. Smaller mammals, such as mice, rats, and rabbits, typically exhibit faster growth cycles, higher bone metabolism rates, and more rapid bone healing compared to larger species. Therefore, selecting an appropriate animal model is essential for accurately replicating fracture events, enabling detailed observation of both physiological and pathological bone healing trajectories. This strategic model selection supports the elucidation of disease processes and facilitates the refinement and development of targeted therapeutic interventions, ultimately informing and enhancing clinical decision-making (44-46).

Vascular damage frequently accompanies skeletal trauma and may lead to localized ischemia at the site of injury. From a biological standpoint, such impairment compromises oxygen and nutrient delivery to the affected tissues, while also impeding the clearance of carbon dioxide and metabolic waste. This disruption can negatively affect the bone healing process, a phenomenon that has been consistently demonstrated in various animal models across multiple anatomical fracture sites (29-39,44). Several studies have emphasized the role of arterial vascularization and vertebral body blood supply and nutrition in influencing whether a vertebral body fracture proceeds toward successful bone healing or evolves into non-union and bone necrosis (1-7,13,23-28). Although this concept is supported by solid pathophysiological rationale, this potentially pivotal biological factor is not currently incorporated into the clinical decision-making algorithms regarding the initial indication for vertebral body replacement. Presently, the therapeutic choice between spinal stabilization with preservation of the vertebral body vs. its replacement is predominantly guided by the mechanical characteristics of the fracture and by surgeon experience and personal preference (10-22). To improve the management of such fractures, there is a pressing need to develop standardized treatment protocols grounded in reliable, objective, and reproducible predictors of vertebral body bone healing. Early identification of these prognostic indicators would enable clinicians to make informed decisions at the outset, selecting the most appropriate surgical approach—be it vertebral body preservation and stabilization or vertebral body interior or total replacement. The overarching objective is to avoid opting for surgical stabilization alone in vertebral body fractures with limited healing potential, thereby reducing the incidence of non-union, vertebral necrosis, and posttraumatic vertebral collapse. Such complications are associated with severe adverse outcomes, including local kyphotic deformity, impaired function, and elevated risk of neurological compromise. The goal is to predict, prevent, and minimize cases of non-union and post-traumatic necrosis, which remain some of the biggest challenges in spine traumatology (1-8).

To contribute new insights into the role of intraosseous vascular injury of the vertebral body as a potential predictive factor in the progression of bone healing, we designed an experimental study using a Wistar rat model. Our aim was to investigate how an anterolateral vascular injury might influence the normal bone healing trajectory of a previously induced burst fracture in the L1 vertebral body. As an initial step, we sought to comprehensively characterize the typical histological evolution of the bone healing process in this established and accessible animal model. The Wistar rat has been previously validated—by our group and others—as having vertebral body morphology and vascular architecture at the L1 level that closely resemble those of humans, thereby making it an appropriate and reliable model for studying vertebral blood supply-related phenomena (1-8,42). In addition to its anatomical relevance, the Wistar rat offers several practical advantages over larger animal models. These include ease of handling and maintenance, greater tolerance to anesthesia, relatively low susceptibility to infection, as well as faster growth cycles, shorter bone metabolic processes, all of which contribute to a more rapid bone healing process, enhancing the feasibility and efficiency of experimental protocols (47,48). The selection of the L1 vertebral body was based on its status as the most frequently affected site in human vertebral body fractures. This susceptibility is primarily attributed to its anatomical position at the transition between the rigid thoracic spine and the more mobile lumbar region. In designing our experimental model, we aimed to replicate, as closely as possible, the biomechanical environment of an induced burst fracture at this site. Despite the inherent limitations associated with the quadrupedal posture of rats, this approach was intended to enhance the translational accuracy of our findings to human clinical scenarios (43). At the thoracolumbar junction, the first lumbar vertebral body acts as a structural support for the rigid thoracic column. It marks the transition from the relatively immobile thoracic spine to the flexible lumbar spine. As a result, following trauma, both L1 and, to a lesser extent, T12 vertebral bodies are subjected to increased axial loading forces compared to other thoracic and lumbar vertebrae. This biomechanical context explains why the L1 vertebra is the most common site of vertebral fracture, accounting for approximately 34% of cases. These fractures typically exhibit an axial compression mechanism, often resulting in characteristic burst-type morphology (43). Due to this high prevalence of burst-type fractures, frequently comminuted, the thoracolumbar junction (particularly T12 and L1) is also the spinal region most prone to developing post-traumatic vertebral body necrosis, as previously noted (1-8,43).

Accordingly, in the first experimental group, we surgically induced a fracture in the L1 vertebral body of the Wistar rat, involving both endplates and the posterior wall. This was achieved through sagittal and transverse fracture lines, which were selected for their ease of reproducibility. Additionally, we created a central bone defect within the vertebral body (Figure 1). The fracture lines (Figure 1D) were designed to replicate the principal features of a complete burst fracture as defined in humans, corresponding to type A4 in the AOSpine classification system (9). The central bone void (Figure 1C) aimed to mimic the comminution commonly observed in such burst fractures. The high incidence of neurological injury observed (23.33% in the control group and 26.67% in the intervention group) was anticipated and consistent with the severity of the induced trauma. Given the anatomical proximity of the spinal cord to the vertebral body in rats, being only a few millimeters from the induced fracture, such outcomes were within expected parameters. In this experimental model, neurological injury may have arisen from several potential causes. These include direct mechanical trauma from ultrasonic vibratory tools, thermal injury to the spinal cord resulting from excessive heat generated by these tools or by electrocauterization, as well as postoperative instability of the vertebral column during the animal’s initial movements. The higher incidence of neurological impairment observed in the intervention group is plausibly attributable to the increased manipulation inherent to the additional surgical steps, particularly the electrocauterization of the anterolateral surfaces of the vertebral body, which in cases where insufficient cold saline irrigation was present, may have contributed to localized spinal cord heating. To minimize heat-induced neural injury, as detailed in the Methods section, both ultrasonic tools and electrocautery were consistently used in conjunction with the simultaneous application of cold saline solution. Indeed, the neurological deficits observed, ranging from monoplegia to paraplegia, closely mimic the types of neural complications commonly associated with complete burst fractures in the human spine (9,13).

Numerous studies have investigated the bone healing process in animal models, particularly focusing on fractures of long bones such as the femur and tibia, as well as cranial bones. These investigations encompass various experimental designs, including fracture models, bone defect models, and non-union models (49-60). Some of these studies specifically address the role of vascularization and ischemia in bone repair, demonstrating that ischemic conditions can significantly delay the bone healing process. However, most of these findings have been derived from studies focusing on long bones and cranial structures (29-34). Similarly, research in animal models and tissue engineering has shown that enhancing local vascularization can stimulate bone regeneration (35-39). However, these models are not directly applicable to the vertebral body due to fundamental anatomical differences. Long bones are primarily composed of tubular cortical architecture, and cranial bones consist of lamellar structures, whereas the vertebral body contains a significantly higher proportion of cancellous bone. Furthermore, none of these previously employed models replicate the complex biomechanical environment of the spine, rendering them unsuitable for accurately studying the progression of vertebral body fractures, which exhibit distinct pathophysiological features (49,61). More recently, several studies have attempted to reproduce vertebral injuries in vivo through the creation of bone defects in animal models (62-85). However, the replication of true vertebral fractures, particularly burst-type fractures, is still predominantly represented in the literature through in vitro biomechanical studies (86-108). There remains a limited number of in vivo investigations replicating true vertebral body fractures with high fidelity (109-113). To the best of our knowledge, no prior study has sought to reproduce the defining features of complete vertebral burst fractures [classified as type A4 in the AOSpine system (9)] in Wistar rats with the degree of anatomical and mechanical precision presented in the current work. Specifically, our model combines distinct and reproducible full-thickness fracture lines with the introduction of a central anterior bone defect (Figures 1,3), thereby more accurately simulating the clinical reality of these injuries.

Subsequently, we analyzed the progression of the L1 vertebral fracture induced in Wistar rats over a period of 6 weeks. A group of rats was euthanized each week, and the corresponding L1 vertebra was collected. Manual assessment enabled a rapid and straightforward evaluation of bone fragment stability. This method, though inherently subjective, yielded comparable results across both groups. From the third week onward, the fractures in both groups exhibited features of the soft callus phase, with the vertebral body behaving as a single consolidated unit due to the presence of fibroblastic and cartilaginous tissue. This degree of structural integration was not observed during the first and second weeks in either group.

Histological evaluation, in turn, included standard parameters commonly used in the analysis of bone healing phases. This was achieved through the development and prior training of an algorithm based on pixel detection of distinct tissue types within a predefined fracture region. The bone healing phase assigned to each histological section was determined according to the predominant parameter present, whether corresponding to inflammatory phase, soft callus phase, or bone phase, as described in the “Methods” section.

In histological evaluations, pixel-level classification methods offer a powerful alternative to traditional approaches by enabling tissue analysis with exceptional resolution and objectivity. Rather than relying on observer interpretation, these techniques assess each pixel independently, allowing for a refined distinction between tissue types based on defined histomorphological features. Their utility lies not only in their ability to detect microscopic structural differences but also in their capacity to deliver consistent, automated, and reproducible quantitative measurements across samples. This computational approach minimizes interobserver variability and enhances diagnostic precision, particularly when subtle changes in tissue architecture are present and may suggest the onset of pathological conditions or early healing events. Overall, pixel classification systems contribute to a more standardized workflow in histological research, offering reliable tissue categorization once appropriately trained, and thereby reducing the potential for subjective error in tissue assessment (114,115). Despite this, following the initial assessments, we concluded that the pixel-based classification algorithm was not reliable in distinguishing between the hard callus and bone remodeling phases. This limitation arises from the fact that the transition between these two phases is primarily morphological, based on the presence and organization of bony trabeculae, rather than on differences in tissue type, since both phases are characterized by bone tissue. The distinction between these two bone phases is inherently gradual and is defined by a continuum involving both immature bone and remodeled bone. As such, we opted to quantify the percentage of trabecular bone in specimens identified as being in the bone phase of healing. If the trabecular content was below 50%, the specimen was classified as being in the hard callus phase; if 50% or higher, it was considered to represent the remodeling phase. We believe this method provided a more accurate and consistent distinction between these stages compared to the pixel-based algorithm, which struggled to differentiate the two due to its inability to discern architectural nuances within the same tissue type. This approach also allowed for a detailed characterization of the typical progression of vertebral body fracture healing at L1 in the Wistar rat model (Table 1, Figure 4). This baseline characterization provides a valuable control reference for future studies assessing the impact of various interventions on the bone healing process of this specific vertebral fracture, facilitating a more detailed histological understanding of their effects.

It is worth highlighting the notable progression of bone healing components observed histologically in this fracture model group (Tables 1,3, Figure 4). The distinct stages of bone healing in fact represent dynamic mixtures of various tissue types that gradually replace one another during the regenerative process. The predominance of each tissue type evolves over time, from the initial inflammatory phase, through the fibroblastic and cartilaginous stages, and ultimately to the bone phase. This reflects a continuous and progressive regeneration of bone tissue, rather than a sequence of sharply defined stages. The conventional classification of healing phases based on the dominant tissue at a given time serves primarily as a practical framework for descriptive and comparative purposes. However, in biological terms, the process follows a fluid continuum. Therefore, evaluating the temporal evolution of individual tissue parameters quantitatively provides deeper insight into fracture healing than focusing solely on distinct bone healing phases. This approach captures the gradual transitions inherent in the repair process and offers a more detailed understanding of tissue regeneration over time (Tables 1,3, Figure 4).

The progression of bone healing following the burst-type fracture induced in the L1 vertebral body of Wistar rats mirrored the typical healing pattern described in other vertebral fracture or bone defect models in rodents (61,62,64,77,78,80-83). In our study, the temporal dynamics of bone healing were documented with detailed quantitative histological data specifically tailored to vertebral body fractures (Tables 1,3, Figure 4). In this L1 fracture model, which served as the control group, a predominance of fibrocartilaginous tissue was observed during the initial weeks, accompanied by a gradual decline in inflammatory components. Bone tissue began to dominate slightly before the fourth week, transitioning from a hard callus phase, characterized by immature osteoid matrix, to the appearance of structured trabecular bone between the third and fourth, and fourth and fifth weeks. By the fifth and sixth weeks, all specimens exhibited features consistent with the bone remodeling phase (Tables 1,3, Figure 4). It is important to note that the induced fracture in this model was highly comminuted, consisting of four distinctly unstable fragments and a substantial central bone void relative to the overall size of the vertebral body. As previously noted, this design aimed to replicate the complete burst-type fracture described in humans. Nevertheless, all control group rats had progressed to at least the hard callus phase by the fourth week post-fracture, indicating that bone vertebral stability had been re-established by this time point, despite individual variability in the stages of subsequent bone remodeling. Notably, there were no instances of non-union observed within the control group. This healing trajectory, along with the presence of bone necrosis in only one animal, reinforces the Wistar rat’s robust and efficient capacity for bone regeneration, even in the context of a severe vertebral body fracture that simulates a human complete burst fracture (Tables 1,3).

As previously discussed, several researchers have suggested that the arterial vascular supply and nutritional status of the vertebral body may be key determinants in the progression of fractures either toward successful healing or toward non-union (1-8,23-27). Although conclusive scientific evidence remains limited, it is reasonable to hypothesize that one of the primary mechanisms underlying the development of non-union in vertebral fractures is the disruption of intraosseous blood vessels at the moment of injury. Such vascular compromise impairs blood supply to the vertebral body, thereby limiting the delivery of essential biological components, including growth factors, blood cells, nutrients, minerals, cytokines, inflammatory mediators, collagen, and hormones, which that are critical to the orchestration of bone repair. This insufficiency may not only delay the healing process but also jeopardize it entirely, thereby increasing the risk of delayed union, non-union, avascular necrosis, and the formation of pseudoarthrosis (33,34,56,57).

Currently, no available diagnostic modality allows for the reliable assessment of vertebral body intraosseous vascular integrity in a way that can predict whether a specific vertebral fracture pattern has caused significant disruption of the vertebral body’s internal blood supply—an event that could greatly increase the likelihood of bone healing complications, such as delayed union or non-union (7).

In previous investigations, our team identified that the vast majority of vertebral fractures eventually progressing to post-traumatic osteonecrosis were classified as burst-type injuries, representing 82.9% of such cases. This finding supports the hypothesis that mechanical trauma to the vertebral body may compromise critical intraosseous vascular structures, thereby interrupting the delivery of essential elements for bone regeneration and initiating a pathophysiological sequence that culminates in non-union and pseudarthrosis at a predictable anatomical site (1,2,8,13,28).

Building on this rationale, it is reasonable to infer that in complete burst fractures, defined as those involving the entire vertebral body architecture, including both endplates and the posterior vertebral wall [A4 classification according to the AOSpine system (9)], there is a substantial risk of disruption to the intraosseous vascular network. Such compromise may render the biological environment inadequate to sustain effective bone regeneration. Accordingly, in our clinical practice, when confronted with severely comminuted complete burst fractures (A4 type), we adopt a cautious and proactive stance and, based on empirical and pathophysiological considerations, we assume that the intraosseous arterial supply has been significantly damaged, thereby reducing the potential for spontaneous bone healing. For this reason, our initial therapeutic strategy in this specific type of vertebral body fracture prioritizes vertebral body replacement, either interior, through intravertebral techniques such as kyphoplasty or expandable implant-application procedures, or complete replacement, via corpectomy followed by anterior column reconstruction. This approach serves both as a treatment and a prophylactic measure against non-union and the development of pseudarthrosis, aiming to pre-empt one of the most complex complications in spine trauma management (1-8,24,40,42). Interestingly, the vertebral fractures treatment algorithm proposed by McCormack et al. implicitly integrates these vascular biological concerns. By incorporating fracture comminution and fragment alignment into surgical decision-making between vertebral body-preserving vs. vertebral body replacement surgical treatment, their model indirectly accounts for the extent of vascular disruption (13). This happens because a higher degree of bony fragmentation undoubtedly correlates with more significant intraosseous vascular injury. To strengthen the foundation of this therapeutic paradigm, we emphasize the need to formally elucidate the biological and vascular implications of intraosseous circulation in thoracolumbar burst fractures. This would provide critical support for guiding decisions on whether to preserve or replace the damaged vertebral body in such high-risk cases.

To explore whether disruption of the intraosseous arterial microvasculature of the vertebral body following fracture affects or predicts its capacity for bone healing, we implemented a targeted vascular injury protocol within the same L1 burst fracture model in Wistar rats. This protocol involved two consecutive procedures: first, electrocauterization of the anterolateral vertebral body surfaces (Figure 2B), intended to ablate arterial branches penetrating from this direction; second, the application of a plastic barrier suture (Figure 2C), designed to prevent revascularization and block the anterior migration of cells and growth factors. Through this combined approach (Figure 2A-2C), we aimed to eliminate the influence of what is likely the primary vascular source of the vertebral body, based on our group’s previous histological anatomical study of the intraosseous L1 vertebral body in Wistar rats: anterolateral penetrating arterial branches stemming from the segmental lumbar arteries, which arise directly from the abdominal aorta. The dominant vascular supply to the vertebral body, whether anterior or posterior, remains a debated topic in both Wistar rats and humans, with conflicting reports in the literature (42,116). Following the anterolateral vascular intervention, we conducted the same macroscopic and histological analyses that had previously been applied to the control group of the fracture model. These assessments were performed under identical conditions and over a 6-week period, with the objective of evaluating the biological impact of our intervention on the bone healing process previously described.

Regarding macroscopic evaluation of bone stability, the outcomes were consistent across both experimental and control groups (Tables 1,2). Fractures were classified as unstable during the first 2 weeks and as stable from the third week onwards. By this time point, vertebrae in both groups exhibited features characteristic of the soft callus phase (Tables 1,2). Histologically, a well-defined interface was identified between the plastic barrier and surrounding organic tissues in the intervention group. This interface showed no signs of tissue infiltration, vascular ingrowth, or cellular migration, thereby confirming the functional effectiveness of the anterolateral barrier. This outcome aligns with the intended purpose of the intervention and is illustrated by a representative histological example in Figure 12.

Subsequently, a detailed histological evaluation, analogous to that performed for the original fracture model, was conducted (Tables 2,3, Figure 4). The most notable differences observed were related to the timing and progression of healing stages. In the intervention group, all four animals sacrificed at week 1 remained in the inflammatory phase, with the transition to the soft callus phase only becoming evident from week 2. The soft callus phase was the exclusive finding at weeks 2 and 3, and although it declined thereafter, it persisted in 75% of specimens at week 4, 60% at week 5, and 33.33% at week 6. This extended presence contrasts with the control group, where the soft callus phase was no longer detected after week 4. Similarly, the hard callus phase was more frequently observed in the intervention group compared to controls. It first appeared in one specimen at week 4, was identified in 40% of cases at week 5, and in 33.33% at week 6. In contrast, the remodeling phase was markedly delayed: it was observed in only 33.33% of specimens at week 6, whereas in the control group, this phase appeared earlier and more consistently. These findings are detailed in Tables 2,3 and illustrated in Figure 4. Regarding the percentage of necrosis observed in fracture areas analyzed at or beyond the fourth week, the control group exhibited a mean value of 7.14%, whereas the intervention group presented a substantially higher percentage of 26.67% (Tables 1,2). Despite this apparent difference, the variation between groups did not reach statistical significance. Given the highly comminuted nature of the induced fracture and the intentional anterolateral arterial exclusion performed in the intervention group, a higher incidence of necrosis in this group is theoretically plausible. Nevertheless, based on the current data, it is not possible to determine whether the vascular injury directly accounts for the increased prevalence of necrotic areas observed in the intervention group compared to the control group.

A comparative statistical analysis was then conducted on the quantitative data obtained from the histological assessments. Initially, intra-group comparisons were made to evaluate the temporal evolution of the various components involved in bone healing over the 6-week fracture period. In the intervention group, statistically significant differences were identified in the inflammatory component when comparing week 1 to both weeks 3 and 6, with inflammation being markedly predominant in the first week. These differences were not observed in the control group (Tables 1-3, Figure 4), indicating a pronounced early inflammatory response in the intervention group. All specimens in this group were found to be in the inflammatory phase during the first week, with minimal evidence of fibroblastic activity. This finding suggests a delay in the transition to the soft callus phase when compared to the control group, in which all animals had already entered the soft callus phase by the end of the first week. It is likely that this transition occurred during the first week in the control group but went undetected in our study due to the absence of earlier sacrifice time points. In contrast, in the intervention group, the inflammatory-to-soft callus transition appears to have occurred between weeks 1 and 2 and was therefore captured in our analysis (Tables 1-3, Figure 4). Regarding the fibroblastic component, the control group showed statistically significant differences between the first 3 weeks and week 6, reflecting a progressive decrease in fibrocartilaginous tissue as bone formation became predominant (Table 1, Figure 4). In the intervention group, significant differences were observed only between weeks 1 and 3, indicating a less pronounced temporal shift. These findings highlight a marked presence of fibrocartilaginous tissue during the initial 3 weeks in the control group, followed by a clear reduction over the final 3 weeks as the bone component became dominant. The fact that the fibroblastic component in the intervention group reached statistical significance only between weeks 1 and 3 can be attributed to its minimal presence in the first week, during which the inflammatory component was dominant in all cases. Additionally, week 3 represented the peak of fibrocartilaginous tissue in this group, followed by a progressive decline that was less pronounced compared to the control group (Tables 1-3, Figure 4). Regarding the bone component, statistically significant differences were observed in the control group between weeks 2 and 3 when each was compared to week 6. These differences were not found in the intervention group. This pattern in the control group is likely due to the sharp increase in bone tissue, beginning in week 3 and reaching its highest levels by week 6. In contrast, in the intervention group, bone tissue formation began to rise only from week 4 onward, and the increase was more gradual. These findings indicate a slower rate of bone tissue replacement in the intervention group when compared to the control, reflecting a delayed progression of the bone healing process (Tables 1-3, Figure 4).

Subsequently, intergroup comparisons were conducted to identify differences in the various components of bone healing between the control and intervention groups at each weekly time point following fracture induction. Statistically significant differences were observed at weeks 1 and 6. At week 1, the inflammatory component was significantly more prominent in the intervention group compared to the control group (Tables 1,2, Figure 4). Conversely, during the same time point, fibroblastic and cartilaginous tissue components were significantly more prevalent in the control group than in the intervention group. These findings are consistent with previously described results, confirming a predominant inflammatory response in all animals from the intervention group during the first week, accompanied by a relative scarcity of fibrocartilaginous tissue. In contrast, all animals in the control group had already entered the soft callus phase by week 1. This intergroup difference reinforces the notion that the intervention group exhibited a delay in the transition from the inflammatory phase to soft callus formation, when compared with the more rapid progression observed in the control group (Tables 1-3, Figure 4). At the 6-week mark following fracture, the intervention group exhibited a significantly higher proportion of fibrocartilaginous tissue compared to the control group, whereas the bone tissue component was significantly more dominant in the control group (Tables 1-3, Figure 4). This finding aligns with previous observations and reinforces the conclusion that the bone healing process progressed more slowly in the intervention group. In this group, the fibrocartilaginous tissue had not yet been substantially replaced by bone tissue by week 6, suggesting that the healing process remained in an earlier transitional phase, from soft callus to bone formation, relative to the control group. This delayed progression was also evident in qualitative assessments: while all animals in the control group had reached the bone remodeling phase by week 6, the intervention group displayed a broader distribution of healing stages, with one-third of specimens in the soft callus phase, one-third in hard callus, and only one-third achieving bone remodeling (Tables 1-3, Figure 4). The greater efficiency of bone healing in the control group is also evident in the later stages of the process, as demonstrated by the earlier onset of trabecular bone differentiation. In this group, well-defined trabeculae began forming progressively from week 4 and were present in 100% of specimens by weeks 5 and 6 (Table 1). In contrast, in the intervention group, well-structured trabecular bone was only observed in one-third of the animals at week 6 (Table 2). Accordingly, in animals that had reached the bone healing phases (from week 4 onward), a significantly higher prevalence of trabecular bone was found in the control group compared to the intervention group. This difference was particularly pronounced at week 5, where the remodeling phase was significantly more prevalent in the control group (Tables 1,2). When comparing the time (in weeks) required to reach each stage of bone healing between the two groups, a statistically significant difference was observed, with the intervention group showing a higher median time across all healing phases (Table 3, Figure 5). However, the only phase in which this difference reached statistical significance was the soft callus phase, likely due to the larger number of cases included in this category (n=28 across both groups). This comparison further supports the conclusion that the bone healing process occurs more slowly in the intervention group, as these animals required a longer duration to reach each specific phase of healing compared to those in the control group (Table 3, Figure 5). When comparing the number of cases corresponding to each stage of bone healing between the two groups, a statistically significant difference in distribution was identified (Table 3). In the control group, the most represented stages were soft callus (46.15%) and bone remodeling (42.31%). This distribution is likely due to the fact that animals were only sacrificed starting from week 1, by which time the inflammatory phase had already progressed to soft callus, while bone remodeling was already evident by week 4 and became the predominant phase by weeks 5 and 6, with all animals in this phase. In contrast, the intervention group included four animals still in the inflammatory phase and showed a predominance of soft callus (59.26%), which can be explained by its persistent presence from week 2 through week 6. The hard callus phase was more prevalent in the intervention group (18.52%) compared to the control group (11.54%), likely because several animals in the control group had already advanced to the remodeling phase (42.31%), whereas in the intervention group only 7.41% of specimens had reached remodeling. When comparing healing stages directly, animals in the hard callus phase accounted for 62.5% of the intervention group and 37.5% of the control group. Conversely, animals in the bone remodeling phase made up 84.6% of the control group but only 15.4% of the intervention group. These differences further substantiate the delayed bone repair process observed in the intervention group, which was characterized by a greater proportion of animals remaining in earlier stages of bone healing and fewer progressing to more advanced phases.

Based on the detailed comparisons and statistical analyses presented and discussed, this study successfully demonstrated, in an animal model replicating a complete burst fracture of the L1 vertebral body in Wistar rats, a statistically significant difference in the bone healing process between the standard healing trajectory (control group) and the altered response following injury and exclusion of the anterolateral vascular supply (intervention group). These findings highlight the biological relevance and contributory role of vertebral body anteriorly derived intraosseous vessels and their blood supply in vertebral fracture repair. The data presented above support the inference that, in the intervention group (where the anterolateral vascular supply was intentionally excluded from the bone regeneration process) bone healing still proceeded through the same sequential stages observed in the standard fracture model. In no case was the typical orderly progression of bone healing phases interrupted, as no instances of non-union were observed. This preservation of healing dynamics is likely attributable to compensatory perfusion provided by the posterior vascular supply and the development of collateral circulation. Nevertheless, although the overall sequence and characteristics of bone healing were maintained, the process was significantly slower in the intervention group when compared to the control group. Thus, this experimental study, conducted in an animal model with vertebral vascular anatomy comparable to that of humans and using a thoracolumbar vertebral body fracture that mimics the biomechanical features of complete burst fractures, provides statistically significant evidence that anterolateral vascular injury has a detrimental impact on bone healing. The delayed bone healing observed in the intervention group, evidenced by the longer time required to reach specific stages of healing compared to the control group, may, when translated to clinical practice, predispose to delayed union, non-union, and pseudarthrosis. This reinforces the role of vascular injury as a clear risk factor for impaired bone repair.

We therefore propose that damage to the anterolateral vascular vertebral body structures (including their penetrating branches such as primary and secondary periosteal arterioles) should be recognized as a critical biological risk factor for delayed-union and non-union. This type of vascular disruption almost certainly occurs in human vertebral fractures, particularly in comminuted burst-type injuries. Given its potential to compromise bone regeneration, such vascular injury should be incorporated into therapeutic decision-making algorithms. Specifically, it should influence the choice between vertebral body-preserving stabilization procedure, which aim to support natural fracture healing, and vertebral body replacement surgeries, whether interior or complete. In the latter case, the biological capacity of the fractured vertebral body to heal is considered insufficient, justifying early replacement to prevent progression to non-union. Although no diagnostic method currently exists that enables the vascular and biological assessment of whether a specific vertebral fracture pattern has caused significant disruption to the intraosseous blood supply, thereby increasing the risk of non-union, research in this area appears essential for improving the management of severe vertebral body fractures. Advancing this understanding could help prevent non-union and post-traumatic vertebral necrosis, while promoting faster recovery and reducing the incidence of complications. The development of a standardized and clinically applicable method to evaluate the extent of vascular injury in vertebral fractures, and to determine whether it is sufficient to impair bone regenerative capacity, seems increasingly necessary. Such a tool would enable the early prediction and prevention of one of the most challenging complications in spinal trauma: non-union and progression to post-traumatic vertebral necrosis. Ultimately, this would facilitate the creation of a more effective and biologically informed therapeutic algorithm in vertebral body fracture care (7).

Such a method should integrate anatomical knowledge of the intraosseous arterial microvasculature specific to the affected vertebral body with high-resolution imaging capable of delineating the three-dimensional orientation of fracture lines. The presence of fracture lines in anatomical regions known to contain key arterioles, critical for sustaining the intraosseous blood supply, would suggest a high probability of vascular injury. This risk must be quantitatively assessed and systematically studied to determine its correlation with impaired bone healing outcomes. Once a threshold level of intraosseous arterial damage is established, based on future research, there should be a more clear and standardized indication to proceed, from the outset, with either interior or total vertebral body replacement. This would reflect the biological improbability of successful bone healing in such cases and ensure a more consistent and effective therapeutic response. Conversely, if the extent of vascular injury falls below the defined threshold, the fracture is more likely to progress toward successful bone healing. In such cases, preservation of the vertebral body may be considered appropriate, with stabilization surgery alone being sufficient, as the likelihood of spontaneous bone union is deemed high. Naturally, these remain theoretical considerations that require validation through extensive research, first in animal models, and subsequently in clinical human studies, in order to develop an effective method for assessing whether the degree of vascular damage in a given vertebral body fracture is sufficient to impair its bone regenerative potential. The definition of this vascular injury threshold, specifying the point beyond which the probability of bone healing is significantly reduced, and thus necessitating vertebral body replacement, represents the central challenge in designing such a therapeutic algorithm. Ultimately, the development of this method would allow integration of vascular biological factors into the clinical decision-making process for vertebral body fractures. This would significantly enhance the precision and effectiveness of treatment strategies and is expected to substantially reduce the incidence of non-union and post-traumatic vertebral necrosis, thereby addressing a rising and particularly difficult complication in spinal trauma care.

Several limitations of this study must be acknowledged. First, the selected animal model, although useful, presents inherent differences from humans, not only in metabolic physiology but also in biomechanics. Being quadrupeds, rats experience different patterns of spinal loading compared to the bipedal human posture. However, the use of the L1 vertebra, located at the transition between the rigid thoracic spine and the mobile lumbar region, which also happens in rats, was intended to replicate the biomechanical context of a region with high fracture prevalence in humans. Second, while the vertebral body fractures created in this model are not identical to human complete burst fractures, the experimental method employed closely reflects the mechanical characteristics of an A4-type complete burst fracture as defined by the AOSpine classification system and is considered to be reproducible for experimental purposes (9). In addition, although each group initially included 35 rats, the invasiveness of the surgical procedure led to some mortality. Secondly, the created vertebral bone fractures are certainly different from complete vertebral body burst fractures in humans. Nevertheless, we consider our fracture creation method to be reproducible and capable of generating a fracture closely resembling the mechanical definition of a complete burst fracture [AOSpine type A4 (9)]. In addition, although each group initially included 35 rats, the invasiveness of the surgical procedure led to some mortality. Moreover, some histological samples had to be excluded due to the absence of visible fractures. As a result, the final sample included a minimum of four rats per fracture week. While this number remains reasonable for analysis, it limits the strength of the statistical evaluation and precludes more robust conclusions. Another limitation of this study was the difficulty encountered in distinguishing between the hard callus and bone remodeling phases using the pixel classification algorithm. This challenge likely arose from the similar pixel coloration of bone tissue associated with both phases, which hindered the algorithm’s ability to reliably differentiate between them. Consequently, for analytical purposes, these two phases were grouped into a single category in order to maintain consistency and improve classification accuracy. While this approach was necessary for quantitative assessment, it limited the ability to detect more detailed differences between the two phases. Such distinctions were made only through qualitative and morphological analysis based on the identification of well-defined trabecular structures, which were used to define the bone remodeling phase. Furthermore, this study does not include imaging examinations, which could have been useful for assessing the morphology of the induced fracture, enabling a more accurate determination of its similarity to complete burst fractures, as well as for evaluating its progression in terms of bone healing up to the formation of mature bone. Additionally, the role of osteoclasts in bone remodeling and the process of revascularization in bone healing efficacy were not investigated in this study. These factors may provide valuable insights into the differences observed between groups and should be explored in future research. Despite these limitations, our analysis focused entirely on a detailed quantitative and qualitative histological assessment. In this context, we consider the pixel classification algorithm a valuable tool for high-resolution quantitative tissue analysis, as it effectively captures the continuous and progressive nature of tissue replacement that characterizes the bone healing process. Therefore, we believe the results of the present study are both reliable and informative, clearly demonstrating a delay in the bone healing stages in the intervention group compared to the control group within the Wistar rat L1 burst fracture model.

Conclusions

The findings of this study indicate that compromising the vertebral body anterolateral vascular supply negatively affects the biological bone repair of L1 vertebral body fractures in a Wistar rat model. This effect was reflected in a consistent delay across all key stages of the bone healing cascade, including the inflammatory phase, soft callus formation, transition to hard callus, and bone remodeling. This impairment in bone healing dynamics resulted in a slower rate of regeneration and may increase the likelihood of complications such as delayed union, non-union, or pseudarthrosis. Importantly, this research provides pioneering in vivo evidence, using an animal model with vertebral body vascular anatomy that closely resembles that of humans, to show that injury to the vertebral body anterolateral blood supply, an occurrence likely present in complete burst fractures in humans, has an unfavorable influence on the biological trajectory of bone healing. Based on these results, we propose that the vertebral body intraosseous vascular integrity should be carefully evaluated and factored into therapeutic decision-making, especially when choosing between vertebral body-conserving stabilization procedures and surgical vertebral body replacement. Incorporating vascular status into treatment algorithms may improve outcomes and help prevent the development of severe complications associated with impaired fracture healing. If it becomes possible to predict the likelihood of a vertebral body fracture progressing to union or non-union based on the extent of intraosseous vascular injury, this could enable the universal establishment of a threshold for vascular damage affecting bone healing. Such a threshold would help determine when to proceed immediately with interior or total vertebral body replacement in cases where spontaneous bone union is unlikely. This approach would enhance therapeutic decision-making algorithms by improving the ability to anticipate, prevent, and more effectively reduce the incidence of non-union and the progression to post-traumatic vertebral necrosis, along with their serious clinical consequences and treatment challenges. We propose that the present study may serve as a foundation for expanding research into the vascular contribution to vertebral body fracture biology. Understanding the pathophysiological impact of intraosseous vascular injury on vertebral bone regeneration could lead to significant improvements in the treatment algorithms for vertebral body fractures. In summary, our work seeks to contribute to the expanding body of scientific knowledge highlighting the importance of intraosseous blood supply integrity in the healing of vertebral body fractures. Further research on this topic is both necessary and strongly encouraged, beginning with animal models and eventually advancing to human studies, with the ultimate goal of translating these insights into clinical practice. Additionally, we believe this rat model reliably mirrors the physiological characteristics of bone healing in vertebral body burst fractures. The quantitative histological data it provides may prove useful in future investigations of fracture healing progression and how it is influenced by different factors.
