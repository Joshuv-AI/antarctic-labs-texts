Targeting the Sialic acid/Siglec axis: opportunities and challenges in colorectal cancer immunotherapy
Authors: Tao Wang, Chuixing Rong, Gaixia Sun, Yongxia Wang
Published: 2026

We read with great interest the recent publication by Aoise et al, titled “Stromal cells modulate innate immune cell phenotype and function in colorectal cancer via the Sialic acid/Siglec axis”.1 The authors provide compelling evidence that stromal cell sialylation, regulated by the sialyltransferase ST6GALNAC6, serves as a key driver of innate immune suppression via the sialic acid/Siglec axis. CMS4 colorectal cancer (CRC) stromal cells, including cancer-associated fibroblasts (CAFs) and tumor-conditioned mesenchymal stromal cells, exhibit high levels of sialylation—even higher than epithelial cancer cells—and induce Siglec-10 expression on macrophages and natural killer (NK) cells. This interaction hinders the phagocytic activity of macrophages and the cytotoxic function of NK cells, both of which are essential antitumor mechanisms within the innate immune system. This work provides valuable insights into the role of stromal cell sialylation in CRC immunosuppression, but several limitations merit discussion to enhance the interpretation and translation of its findings.

The authors used two strategies to target sialylation: the sialyltransferase inhibitor 3FAX and the sialidase E610. While both reduced stromal-mediated immunosuppression, the effects were not entirely consistent—particularly regarding Siglec-10 induction in macrophages. As noted in the Limitations section, these methods act broadly and transiently on sialic acids.1 This non-specificity makes it difficult to attribute observed effects solely to Siglec-10 ligands or ST6GALNAC6 activity. Future studies employing genetic tools (eg, CRISPR/Cas9 knockout of ST6GALNAC6 in CAFs) or Siglec-10-specific blocking antibodies would help establish a more direct causal link.

Although the study demonstrates that stromal cells induce Siglec-10 on macrophages and NK cells, the downstream signaling events remain unexplored. Siglec-10 contains an immunoreceptor tyrosine-based inhibition motif (ITIM) that recruits SHP1/2, but the study does not examine phosphorylation events or downstream transcriptional changes in immune cells. Validating the functional engagement of Siglec-10–ITIM signaling in co-culture models—for example, through phospho-flow cytometry or SHP1/2 inhibition—would solidify the mechanistic narrative.2 3

The in vivo model showed reduced tumor growth and metastasis on stromal desialylation, which is promising. However, the therapeutic window, dosing regimen, and potential toxicity of systemic sialidase or 3FAX administration were not addressed. Given that sialic acids are ubiquitously expressed, off-target effects on normal tissues could limit clinical applicability.4 Including data on immune cell profiles in non-tumor organs (eg, lung, liver) and assessing safety in longer-term studies would enhance the translational relevance.5

The study links tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α-conditioned stroma to increased Siglec-G expression and metastasis, but the role of other inflammatory cytokines (eg, interleukin (IL)-6, IL-1β) common in CMS4 tumor microenvironment (TME) is not investigated. Since inflammation drives stromal activation and immune evasion, a broader cytokine screen could reveal additional regulators of the sialic acid/Siglec axis.6 Moreover, the interplay between sialylation and other immune checkpoints (eg, programmed cell death protein-1 (PD-1)/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1)) in the same model remains unclear, which is relevant for designing combination therapies.7

The study establishes a connection between TNF-α-conditioned stroma and the upregulation of Siglec-G expression, as well as increased metastasis. However, it does not investigate the role of other inflammatory cytokines (eg, IL-6, IL-1β) that are prevalent in the CMS4 TME. Given that inflammation is a driving force behind stromal activation and immune evasion, conducting a more comprehensive cytokine screening could uncover additional regulators of the sialic acid/Siglec axis.8 Furthermore, the interaction between sialylation and other immune checkpoints (eg, PD-1/PD-L1) within this model remains ambiguous, which is pertinent for developing effective combination therapies.9

Despite these limitations, this study makes significant contributions to the field. It identifies the sialic acid/Siglec axis as a novel mechanism of stromal-mediated innate immune suppression and provides preclinical evidence for targeting stromal sialylation in CMS4 CRC. These findings open new avenues for developing combination immunotherapies for stroma-rich tumors. We believe addressing the above limitations in future studies will further strengthen the translational potential of this important work.
