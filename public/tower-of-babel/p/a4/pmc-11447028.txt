Analyses of whole-genome sequences from 185 North American Thoroughbred horses, spanning 5 generations
Authors: Ernie Bailey, Carrie J Finno, Jonah N Cullen, Ted Kalbfleisch, Jessica L Petersen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractWhole genome sequences (WGS) of 185 North American Thoroughbred horses were compared to quantify the number and frequency of variants, diversity of mitotypes, and autosomal runs of homozygosity (ROH). Of the samples, 82 horses were born between 1965 and 1986 (Group 1); the remaining 103, selected to maximize pedigree diversity, were born between 2000 and 2020 (Group 2). Over 14.3 million autosomal variants were identified with 4.5–5.0 million found per horse. Mitochondrial sequences associated the North American Thoroughbreds with 9 of 17 clades previously identified among diverse breeds. Individual coefficients of inbreeding, estimated from ROH, averaged 0.266 (Group 1) and 0.283 (Group 2). When SNP arrays were simulated using subsets of WGS markers, the arrays over-estimated lengths of ROH. WGS-based estimates of inbreeding were highly correlated (r > 0.98) with SNP array-based estimates, but only moderately correlated (r = 0.40) with inbreeding based on 5-generation pedigrees. On average, Group 1 horses had more heterozygous variants (P < 0.001), more total variants (P < 0.001), and lower individual inbreeding (FROH; P < 0.001) than horses in Group 2. However, the distribution of numbers of variants, allele frequency, and extent of ROH overlapped among all horses such that it was not possible to identify the group of origin of any single horse using these measures. Consequently, the Thoroughbred population would be better monitored by investigating changes in specific variants, rather than relying on broad measures of diversity. The WGS for these 185 horses is publicly available for comparison to other populations and as a foundation for modeling changes in population structure, breeding practices, or the appearance of deleterious variants.Keywords: Equus caballus, Genetic diversity, Inbreeding, Population genetics, mtDNA, Variant discoverySubject terms: Animal breeding, Inbreeding

Introduction

Genetic structure of the Thoroughbred horse population is of particular interest to breeders as they walk the fine line between selecting to improve the breed and avoiding inbreeding depression. In 2006, the Thoroughbred industry held the first in a series of Welfare and Safety of the Racehorse Summits and reported, “… the consensus of a broad representation of industry leaders was that Thoroughbreds today tend to be less sound, rugged, and durable than in the past” (GraysonJockeyClubDurabilityEssay); changes in genetics were identified as potentially contributing factors. The Thoroughbred population has undergone significant changes during the last few decades. The International Stud Book Committee (https://www.internationalstudbook.com/resource) reports that the worldwide registered foal crop has declined from 123,467 in 2005 to 86,417 in 2022. Similarly, the foal crop in the United States declined from 34,949 in 2005 to 17,877 in 2022. During that same period, the number of active stallions in the United States decreased from 3851 to 1166 (Jockey Club Fact Book: https://www.jockeyclub.com/Default.asp?section=Resources&area=11). To better understand and track changes in the population, we now can use genomic tools to assess and monitor diversity, measure inbreeding, and identify potential problems. This report shares genomic information from two groups of Thoroughbred horses in the United States, with the immediate goal of identifying extant variation.

The history of Thoroughbred horses is well documented. The genetic structure of the Thoroughbred horse was established by crossing European and Oriental horses during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries1,2. Due to this outcrossing, the genetic diversity among early Thoroughbred horses is likely to have been plentiful. Over the years, breeders selected the most athletic racehorses, chipping away at that diversity to increase the frequency of beneficial variants and purge deleterious or unwanted variants from the population. Early breeders also used inbreeding to increase the genetic contribution of high-quality stock3. Tracing the pedigrees of 211 randomly selected British Thoroughbreds back to their founders (20–30 generations), Cunningham and co-workers4 identified 158 foundation horses that contributed 81% of the genetic make-up to these 211 horses. They estimated that the top 10 founders contributed 45% of the genetic make-up for this sample. Not surprisingly, genomic studies have since identified the Thoroughbred among those horse breeds with the lowest genetic diversity (e.g., Petersen et al.5, Orlando and Librado6).

Historically, breeders utilized a variety of tools to make genetic improvement including the evaluation of performance records, phenotypic characteristics, and pedigree. Selection and mating decisions were then based upon horses that were deemed to have the most desirable and/or complementary characteristics. Through this process, they created a breed unsurpassed in its ability to race distances between 1000 m and 3000 m (~ 5/8 mi- ~ 1 7/8 mi)7,8. During the development of this specialized athlete, Thoroughbred breeders also appear to have been successful in eliminating some deleterious genetic variants; evidentiary of this, Orlando and Librado6 reported Thoroughbred horses among the breeds with evidence of low genetic load. Durward-Akhurst et al.9 compared 12 breeds and found the Thoroughbred with the lowest estimate of genetic burden (variants predicted to have a detrimental impact). The question remains, what more can breeders do to maintain the genetic health of the population as they continue to select for superior racehorses?

Across species and breeds, diversity will decrease in a closed studbook population undergoing selection as deleterious variants are culled and the frequency of beneficial variants increase. However, loss of diversity has also been correlated with inbreeding depression, often manifest as a decline in fertility, health, or performance (reviewed in Leroy)10. The precise causes of inbreeding depression vary among populations and are usually unknown. The traditional tool for management of inbreeding has been the prediction of the proportion of alleles identical by descent from ancestors as quantified by the coefficient of inbreeding (COI or F) determined by pedigree relationships11. This approach assumes random but equal transmission of variants from one generation to the next but does not account for the random inheritance of alleles each generation or for selection. Additionally, the assumption that all founders of a population are unrelated is unarguably violated. With the advent of genomic approaches, it became possible to accurately define regions of inbreeding by identifying runs of homozygosity (ROH)12. Indeed, several studies in cattle demonstrated that pedigree estimates of inbreeding are correlated with ROH measures, but correlations range greatly from 0.50 to 0.7213–15. Pedigree-based estimates of inbreeding also rely upon accurate pedigree information, which, prior to parentage investigation, was less certain16,17. Another advantage of using ROH to evaluate inbreeding is that the length of a ROH is expected to be correlated with the recency of inbreeding18,19. Finally, as the cost of whole genome sequencing (WGS) has dropped, it has been increasingly used to study horse populations20–23. The use of WGS allows for a more comprehensive survey of the genome including the characterization and consideration of rare variation.

Here, we report the investigation of diversity in two samples of Thoroughbred horses in the US using whole genome sequencing. The data provide the opportunity to identify variation present among individuals in the breed. While the samples do not represent a random subset of the population, these analyses allowed for a general comparison of pedigree and genomic estimates of individual inbreeding. The genomic data are publicly available to provide a resource for the development of genomic tools for an accurate quantification of genetic diversity and for intensive monitoring of the population. Finally, these data are a landmark for comparison of variation present in the current Thoroughbred population to that of the future.

Results and discussion

Discovery of variants

Genome coverage at measured polymorphic sites for the 185 samples averaged 20.8 X across the autosomes. Researchers can use the UK-UNL-ThoroughbredDiversity Globus collection for download of raw and derived data files. Raw data are also available as outlined in Data Availability.

Over 14.3 million autosomal variants were identified among the 185 North American Thoroughbreds studied (Group 1: 82 horses born between 1965 and 1986, and Group 2: 103 horses born between 2000 and 2020); the total number of variants identified is similar to that reported (14.2 million) among 101 Japanese Thoroughbred horses21 (Table 1). Differences among samples are geographic (US vs Japan), temporal (Group 1 vs Group2), a consequence of sampling, and differences in bioinformatic processing between studies. Not only the number of variants observed, but the frequency distribution of variants was similar, as may be expected given international gene flow among Thoroughbred horses (Fig. S1). Estimates of genome sharing (pi-hat) found that despite the intentional sampling for diversity, the mean relationship among Group 2 horses (0.034) was significantly (P < 0.001) greater than that among the Japanese horses (0.027), both of which were significantly (P < 0.001) greater than that of the horses in Group 1 (0.024). Variation among samples from the US and Japanese Thoroughbreds was compared using a principal component analysis; the distributions showed overlap, but not identity when comparing principal component 1 (8.6% of variation) and principal component 2 (7.4% of variation) (Fig. S2), similar to that observed in McGivney et al.24.

How do Thoroughbreds compare with other breeds? Studies involving multiple breeds identified a greater number of variants relative to the reference genome20,22,23. Jagannathan et al.20 investigated 88 horse breeds, including 1 Thoroughbred horse, and identified a total of 25.955 million variants. Durward-Akhurst et al.22 generated WGS for 534 horses of 46 breeds, including 54 Thoroughbreds, identifying 31.138 million variants; in that study the average number of variants per individual in 10 common breeds ranged from 5.0 M (Thoroughbred) to 6.1 M (Belgian, Icelandic). The results were similar in a subsequent study that bolstered sample size9. Likewise, Jagannathan et al., 201820 reported the total number of variants per individual, relative to the reference genome, that ranged from 5.1 million (Holsteiner) to 7.8 million (Icelandic). In that study a single Thoroughbred was sequenced and had 5.8 million variants. The comparatively lower number of variants for Thoroughbreds is likely due to 3 centuries of selective breeding with a closed studbook as well as a bias due to the use of a Thoroughbred for the equine reference genome (EquCab 3.0)25. As the reference genome is based upon a Thoroughbred, some of the variation present in other breeds is likely breed specific.

The numbers of SNPs unique to each group were identified for Groups 1 and 2, counting SNPs only if the alternative allele was found at least twice among the 185 horses. Using that criterion, Group 1 had 354,160 unique SNPs while Group 2 had 265,556. Most of the unique SNPs in both groups were infrequent (Fig. S3). Of the unique variants in Group 1, the Ensembl Variant Effect Predictor (VEP) predicted 2612 to have “MODERATE” impact and 436 were predicted to have “HIGH” impact on gene function. Classification of Group 2 variants found 2109 predicted to have “MODERATE” impact and 421, “HIGH.” In silico variant effect prediction is far from perfect26,27, and likely subject to bias due to the origin of the reference genome, however these data can help build hypotheses about a variant’s functional effect, whether it be a benefit or liability to health and performance. Despite Group 2 being selected for diversity of pedigree, the discovery of a greater number of both total and unique variants within Group 1 is consistent with decreasing diversity of the American Thoroughbred population over time, as expected for a population under selection. At the same time, as the selection criteria differ for inclusion of horses in each group, conclusions about the possible significance of these observations await further investigation.

Horses within Groups 1 and 2 were further compared for the numbers of homozygous and heterozygous genotypes at each polymorphic locus. For Groups 1 and 2 the average and range of homozygous variants per horse was 1,347,800 (1,231,095 to 1,495,499) and 1,354,455 (1,241,182 to 1,500,143), respectively (Fig. 1; Table S1). While there was no statistical difference in the mean number of homozygous variants identified in Group 1 as compared to Group 2 (P = 0.40), Group 1 horses had more (P < 0.001) heterozygous variants and total variants (P < 0.001) than horses in Group 2. This observation is consistent with selection and a decrease in diversity of individuals over time. However, > 87% of horses in Group 2 had a heterozygous variant count that fell within 2 standard deviations of the mean observed in Group 1; the differences are therefore not so great that one could determine to which group a horse belongs based on this distribution.

Count of heterozygous (blue) or homozygous (orange) variants (SNPs and indels) found per horse ordered by year of birth. On average, the total number of variants and count of heterozygous variants was greater (P < 0.001) in Group 1 than Group 2. The count of homozygous variants, however, did not differ (P = 0.40) by Group.

The frequency of each variant was also determined across all 185 horses, then identified as present or absent in each horse to examine the distribution of variants based upon their frequency (Fig. 2; Table S2). Group 1 had a greater (P < 0.001) proportion of alleles in the lowest minor allele frequency (maf) classes (< 0.01, 0.01 to 0.1 and 0.1 to 0.2) than found in Group 2. The proportion of alleles/horse with maf 0.4 to 0.5 was significantly greater in Group 2 than Group 1 (P = 0.001). Again, this observation is consistent with selection and loss of diversity over time as rare alleles are being lost or increasing in frequency. Even so, the distributions largely overlapped, (Fig. S4) and the differences were not so great that one could determine to which group a horse belonged based upon this distribution.

Variants associated with traits

Another application of WGS is to assess the prevalence of genetic variants for traits of interest. The website, Online Mendelian Inheritance in Animals (https://www.omia.org/), catalogs genetic variants identified for diverse traits in animals, including horses28. The existence of deleterious simple recessive variants is almost always unknown until an offspring inherits two copies of the variant. Examples of such variants include those causing severe combined immunodeficiency disease (SCID; OMIA:000220-9796) of Arabian horses, junctional epidermolysis bullosa (JEB) of Saddlebreds (OMIA:001677-9796) and Belgian horses (OMIA:001578-9796), hereditary equine regional dermal asthenia of Quarter Horses (HERDA; OMIA:000327-9796) and foal immunodeficiency syndrome (FIS; OMIA:001578-9796) of Dale and Fell ponies. Simple Mendelian disease traits that have been observed in Thoroughbreds include hypoparathyrodism (EEIH; OMIA:002458-9796) and fragile foal syndrome (FFS; OMIA:001982-9796). The accumulation of homozygous recessive alleles across the genome is collectively referred to as the mutational load or genetic load. One application of the WGS generated from these horses is to identify and track variation that could contribute to genetic load.

The WGS generated on these horses was queried for the presence of 29 genetic variants with known impact on health, coat color, and performance; the variant frequencies were compared for Group 1, Group 2 and the Japanese Thoroughbred data (Table 2). The variants chosen for study were simple nucleotide substitutions or short indels. Only 2 of 19 variants associated with/causative of disease (EEIH and FFS) were found among Thoroughbred horses, both at low frequencies. Among the three coat color variants identified in these Thoroughbreds, the variant frequency for W20 was significantly lower in Group 1 than Group 2 (P < 0.007) but not when comparing Group 2 to the Japanese Thoroughbred population, which represents horses that are similar in year of birth to those in Group 2. This variant is not known to have an impact on selection criteria for racehorses and the change may be a result of random drift or the sampling strategy. A significant increase in the intronic MSTN variant was found in Group 2 when comparing to Group 1 (P = 0.03) but again not when comparing Group 2 to the Japanese Thoroughbred population. This MSTN variant has been associated with sprinting ability and with precocity29–31. Therefore, this result may be the consequence of selection during the last 5 generations for horses that are sprinters rather than stayers. These observations, however, require reevaluation in a truly random sample of the population.

Mitochondrial DNA diversity

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is inherited almost exclusively through the female line and is thus a common indicator of maternal lineage. Figure 3 illustrates the diversity of mitochondrial genomes for the 185 Thoroughbreds based on comparison with results from a previous, multibreed study of mitochondrial haplotypes in modern and ancient horses, including 83 horses representing diverse breeds (> 32) and pan-global geographic regions32; that prior work identified 17 clades but found no association of mtDNA haplotype with geographic distributions or breed. In this study, the mtDNA haplotypes of the 185 horses contained 480 polymorphic sites within the 15,465 bp examined (excluding the D-loop) and resulted in 34 unique haplotypes. These 34 haplotypes clustered with 9 of 17 major clades previously identified32, including both major branches (A to L and M to Q) (Fig. 3; Table S2). Clades previously described that were not represented among the North American Thoroughbreds include Haplogroups C, F, J, K, O, P, Q, and R. A large number of haplotypes identified in the US sample represent unique variation stemming from clades L and N as described by Achilli et al.32. Thoroughbred sequences for clades A and I were identical or very similar to the reported sequences; sequences for horses that fell into the clades designated B, E, G, and M were similar but not identical to those previously reported32.

Neighbor joining network of the mtDNA sequences (D-loop excluded) of all 185 horses. Numbers indicate the contig to which they were assigned for this study (also see Table S1), and letter designations as identified in Achilli et al.32.

Prior to the use of WGS to investigate mitochondrial variation, it was often characterized by sequencing the hypervariable D-loop. Hill et al.33, and Bower et al.34 sequenced a 346 bp fragment of the D-loop in 296 Thoroughbred horses to identify 25 mitochondrial haplotypes. Comparing the 346 bp of the D-loop of the North American Thoroughbreds to those reported by Bower et al.34 found that the North American Thoroughbreds were represented by all but 6 of the previously described haplotypes (Fig. S5, Table S1). In addition, three haplotypes were identified in the US samples that were not reported by Bower et al.34 and Hill et al.33; two were each found in a single horse whereas one was found in two individuals.

The distributions of the 34 haplotypes identified by WGS in this study were also associated with the foundation dams in each pedigree. Tail-female pedigrees of the 185 North American Thoroughbred horses terminated with 21 European and 4 American dams (Table S1). Most of the families were represented by multiple mitotypes. Some of these differences may be due to variants introduced by mutation since the foundation of the pedigree. The observation of multiple mitochondrial haplotypes is also consistent with prior work suggesting that some exceptions were the result of errors in early pedigrees16,33,34.

Diversity/Inbreeding by runs of homozygosity

Inbreeding has traditionally been measured using statistical estimates of identity by descent based on pedigree11. However, genomic tools can identify actual runs of homozygosity (ROH), which reflect both identity by state and identity by descent (inbreeding)35. In these samples, FROH for each horse was calculated from all ROH ≥ 300 kb; A significant increase in ROH was observed in Group 2 (0.283) as compared to Group 1 (0.266; p < 0.001; Fig. 4).

Individual inbreeding estimates (FROH) and the average by year of birth (red diamonds) for 185 Thoroughbreds. Mean FROH was greater (P < 0.001) in Group 1 (0.266) compared to Group 2 (0.283).

The mean FROH found in these horses was roughly twice the inbreeding values estimated by pedigree-based methods in prior studies. For example, inbreeding from the pedigrees of 211 Thoroughbreds traced to the founders averaged 0.139 (Cunningham et al.4), and that from a study of Australian Thoroughbreds using an average of 22.9 generations of pedigree per horse was 0.14336. The difference is not surprising as FROH captures homozygosity that may have preceded breed formation. Furthermore, and as noted above, pedigree-based measures of inbreeding do not account for the effects of Mendelian sampling, random inheritance of alleles each generation, or selection.

During each generation, recombination breaks ancestral haplotypes into smaller segments. Consequently, long ROH are associated with recent inbreeding and short ROH associated with ancestral inbreeding37. Longer ROH are thought to contain a greater number of deleterious recessives as supported by several studies in humans38–40 and cattle41. The rationale for this assertion is that long ROH represent more recent inbreeding events upon which recombination or purifying selection would not have had time to act. In a prior study of Thoroughbreds, long ROH, defined as > 5 Mb, were associated with probability of racing, while short ROH, defined as < 5 Mb, had no impact42. However, effects of ROH length on production were not observed in other studies in cattle43 and dogs44. Statistically, the length of ROH is associated with time since inbreeding (in generations) as 100 Mb/2G, where G is the number of generations since the common ancestor with a recombination rate of 1 centiMorgan (cM)1 Mb18,19. Since the average recombination rate measured for the horse was 1.16 cM/1Mb45, then a ROH of 8 Mb, 4 Mb, 2 Mb would correspond to inbreeding that occurred approximately 5.4, 10.7 and 21.5 generations in the past, respectively.

These predictions cannot account for Mendelian sampling or for selection, so consequently, should only be regarded as rough estimates. Considering instead the proportion of the total inbreeding of each horse as determined by ROH of varying sizes, Group 2 horses had a significantly greater proportion of their total inbreeding accounted for in ROH of 1-2 Mb (P = 0.01) and 2-4 Mb (P = 0.03) than that of Group 1; no other comparisons of proportion of inbreeding were significant (Table 3). Still, there was no difference in ROH > 8 Mb, which argues against recent inbreeding.

Pairwise comparisons of genome-wide ROH revealed that any two horses, on average, shared homozygosity across 9.1% of their genomes (range 5.5 to 14.1%). In our data, the ROH shared between the 2 horses with the lowest estimates of FROH (0.213 and 0.215) totaled 0.066 while that between the horses with the highest average FROH was 0.130; these data exemplify the fact that even if two horses each have relatively high inbreeding, the regions of homozygosity are not necessarily shared. The proportion of overlap was only moderately (r = 0.33) correlated with the pedigree estimate of relationship between pairs, meaning it is difficult to predict the true proportion of homozygosity in the genome of a foal based upon pedigree relationship alone.

ROH determination by WGS vs SNP arrays

Most reports of ROH in horses used data from SNP arrays (e.g., Hill et al.42; McGivney et al.24; Lawson et al.46). Methods for measuring ROH with SNP arrays assume that loci between the assayed SNPs are non-variant (homozygous). As a result, spurious ROH may be identified and sets of smaller ROH may be concatenated into a single, large ROH47,48. These reports are consistent with the following observations.

To compare ROH from WGS data to those from SNP array data, we modeled SNP arrays by selecting subsets of markers from the WGS that were represented in the two most used equine SNP arrays, the Illumina GGP Equine (GGP, Neogen, Lincoln, NE) and the Axiom Equine Genotyping Array (HD). The comparison showed that SNP arrays overestimate ROH. With the parameters used, the total FROH was greatest with the GGP marker set and lowest using all markers from WGS (Table 3). The greatest difference was in ROH > 8 Mb, where the inbreeding attributed to these ROH in either SNP set accounted for 4 to 5 times that found in WGS (Fig. 5). The outcome, however, particularly the total FROH estimated based upon the data, varies depending upon the parameters set for ROH analyses. Regardless, the estimates of FROH based upon the SNP data are highly correlated (r > 0.98) to that determined by WGS (Fig. 6). This suggests that SNP arrays can be accurate for comparing relative differences between groups when the same method is employed but provide a less accurate characterization of ROH than WGS.

Inbreeding by length of ROH per horse ordered by year of birth for data based upon markers in the GGP (top) and HD (middle) arrays as well as considering the entire WGS (bottom).

Individual inbreeding (ROH) based upon the whole-genome sequence data (x-axis) compared to that estimated from markers represented on the GGP (Black) and HD (Red) arrays. Pearson’s correlations between the SNP and WGS estimates were 0.98 and 0.99 for the GGP and HD array, respectively.

The length of the maximum ROH also varied by marker set, attributed to the assumption noted above that markers intermediate to SNPs assayed on the arrays are homozygous. With the parameters used, each of the SNP arrays identified ROH over 48 Mb in length while the longest ROH identified by WGS only spanned 21 Mb. Furthermore, the SNP arrays identified 94 (GGP) and 153 (HD) ROH that were greater in length than the largest ROH found from WGS (21 Mb).

Inbreeding measured with ROH vs 5-generation pedigree data

The 5-generation pedigrees of the 185 horses sequenced consisted of 3938 individuals (Groups 1 and 2 combined) including 1264 unique sires and 2674 unique dams. There were 1167 horses considered founders of the population given that they extended to the final generation studied and therefore their ancestors were not recorded/analyzed. The generation interval based on the full pedigree data averaged 10.8 years, similar to that reported in 19887 (10.1 years). As found with genomic data, pedigree-based estimates of inbreeding (5-generation) were greater (P < 0.001) in Group 2 than in Group1 (Fig. S6). Estimates of inbreeding based upon the 5-generation pedigree of each individual were moderately correlated (r = 0.400) with inbreeding estimates based upon all ROH (Fig. 7). As longer ROH are associated with more recent inbreeding, which are expected to be captured in 5-generation pedigrees, the correlations between WGS-based FROH accounted for by ROH > 4 Mb, or those > 8 Mb were not improved, and numerically declined, over that using data from all ROH (r = 0.384 and 0.229, respectively). As expected, due to both the low pedigree depth used and assumptions with pedigree-based inbreeding estimates, ROH-based estimates (avg = 0.240) were significantly greater than those derived from the pedigree data (avg = 0.010).

Inbreeding estimates of each individual from their 5-generation pedigree (x-axis) vs that estimated from genome-wide ROH (y-axis).

ROH landmarks and islands

These data also demonstrated that despite SNP arrays identifying longer FROH, the SNP array markers miss many regions of ROH identified by WGS and identify SNPs that are not actually in ROH (Fig. 8; Fig. S7). ROH analyses, however, did identify genomic regions (ROH “islands”) shared among horses in Group 2 (Fig. S8). Although the consideration of diversity of individual markers has merit, the characterization of these ROH islands and haplotypes has the potential to inform better selection and mating decisions and allow for the elucidation of markers contributing to phenotypes of importance.

Summary and recommendations

This work provides a baseline for comparing the genomic characteristics of the current Thoroughbred horse population with future populations. The number of autosomal variants, distribution of heterozygotes and homozygotes, mitochondrial diversity and measures of ROH of future Thoroughbred samples can be compared to assess changes. On one hand, comparison is problematic because populations will vary based on geographic region, biases in sampling procedures, and because of the vagaries of sampling from a large population. The two populations under study in this report, Group 1 and Group 2, were separated temporally and were sampled for different purposes. Group 1 was selected at random in the 1980s but from a small number of farms. Group 2 was selected in 2021 from a broader group of farms than Group 1, and were chosen based on diversity as assessed from pedigree. Statistical differences were found for most comparisons of sample means and were consistent with greater diversity and less inbreeding among Group 1 horses than of those in Group 2. While the differences may be the result of selection over time, we cannot discount the potential effect of the different sampling criteria.

Importantly, these data provide a foundation for modeling the effect of changes in population number, and number of breeding stallions. Models can also simulate the appearance of deleterious recessive variants in sires and predict their simultaneous impacts on health and performance based on a variety of factors, including population size, the frequency of the variant in the population, and/or book size of the stallion. This study was a necessary step to characterize the extent of variation in the breed to better inform future models.

Group 1 horses had a greater number of total variants, more heterozygous variants, and lower FROH than Group 2 horses. Despite the differences in mean values, individual values overlapped between both groups. Using the variant counts, frequency distribution of variants, or that of ROH, one could not select a horse and identify whether it belonged to Group 1 or Group 2 (Figs. 1, 2, and 5). Certainly, differences as a result of selective breeding and changes over time are expected. However, managing measures of diversity and inbreeding may be less effective than attention to specific variants when evaluating the genetic health of the Thoroughbred population.

To that point, genomic data also helps to identify and track known variants in the population. For example, following the discovery of a variant associated with Fragile Foal Syndrome (FFS), a subsequent study was necessary to determine that the causative variant, PLOD1 2 g.39927817C > T (EquCab 3.0), was infrequent among Thoroughbred horses49. If genomic monitoring were in place, data, like that developed in this study, could be consulted to determine the presence and frequency when a new variant of interest is found in Thoroughbreds or another breed. Inbreeding is associated with unmasking variants that are deleterious to fertility, health and performance. Many studies have sought evidence of inbreeding depression for reproduction and performance traits in horses with mixed results for the Thoroughbred. Pedigree-based inbreeding depression was not found to be associated with foaling rate50,51, per cycle pregnancy rate50,52, or gestation length51 in Thoroughbred mares. As noted above, the relatively low accuracy of pedigree based inbreeding measures relative to that from genomics may partially explain these results. Using genomics, Lawson and coworkers46 reported an association of ROH with mid- and late- term pregnancy loss but not early pregnancy loss.

Analyses of inbreeding with respect to performance has also produced varied results. Using pedigrees, McGivney et al.24 did not find an association of inbreeding with Thoroughbred racing performance as measured by Timeform records. Todd and co-workers53 found that inbreeding, as measured by pedigree, had a negative impact on 5 measures of racing performance; however, no association was identified when genomic measures of inbreeding were used. Hill et al.42 used genomics (SNP array) in their report of an association of inbreeding with the probability of racing in European and AustralAsian Thoroughbred horses. However, this association did not hold true for the number of starts a horse had in its career. Conversely, the same group’s study of American Thoroughbred horses using genomic measures of inbreeding showed the opposite effect, reporting an association of increased inbreeding with number of starts but not likelihood of racing54.

Studies of ROH can lead to the identification of specific variants that may ultimately contribute to inbreeding depression. For example, Hill 202242 identified “large effect haplotypes” for the probability of racing and used ROH data from SNP arrays to identify a chromosome region and postulate genes that may be responsible for that effect. Investigations of this region may lead to identification of functional variants. This approach could also be applied to other discoveries of inbreeding depression. Lawson et al.46 reported association of increased ROH (SNP array) with midterm and late-term pregnancy losses in Thoroughbreds. Not all horses were homozygous for the same ROH reflecting genetic heterogeneity for pregnancy loss. A next step for that research might be to investigate the specific ROH for deleterious variants.

Application of genomic tools, compared to pedigree analyses, can provide more effective evaluations of the genetic health of the breed, allow for monitoring and modeling the effects of changes in management and breeding objectives, and lead to the discovery of incipient problems. Future studies to characterize and compare Thoroughbreds should focus on identifying deleterious variants and tracking changes in their frequencies since number of variants, homozygosity and ROH were similar among horses.

Materials and methods

Horses

Samples of horses were the result of two sampling periods, referenced as Group1 and Group 2 (Table S1). Group 1 consisted of 82 Thoroughbred mares born between 1965 and 1986 as part of commercial Thoroughbred breeding operations sampled in connection with studies at the Gluck Equine Research Center. Birthplaces were identified using information from Equibase (equibase.com) and reported as Kentucky (46), Maryland (9), Virginia (9), Florida (8), Canada (2), Ireland (2), Argentina (2) and 1 each from California, New York, Pennsylvania, and France.

Group 2 consisted of a subset of 103 Thoroughbred horses (77 mares, 14 stallions, 12 geldings) born between 2000 and 2020, selected from amongst 1,020 horses sampled from commercial Thoroughbred farms or training facilities in Kentucky (78), California (21), New York (3) and Florida (1). Birthplaces as identified using Equibase and included Kentucky (60), California (14), Ireland (4), Louisiana (3), Illinois (3), 2 each from Florida, New York, Pennsylvania, Argentina, United Kingdom and 1 each from Arizona, Indiana, Washington, France, Brazil, South Africa, New Zealand and Australia; the birthplace of 1 horse was not identified. The five-generation pedigree of each horse was obtained to calculate the additive relationship matrix, visualized by a principal component bi-plot (Fig. S9). Samples for WGS were chosen to represent variation among samples with primary focus on representing variation across the first PC, also considering WGS already available from 21 horses. Handling of the horses was conducted in connection with University of Kentucky IACUC protocol 2020–3590. Because the horses were from commercial breeding operations, the identities and specific pedigrees of the horses are confidential.

DNA sequencing

WGS data for 164 horses were generated on an Illumina NovaSeq using 150 bp paired-end reads subsequent to Illumina library preparation. Sequencing targeted a depth of 20X coverage per sample. Raw data were processed using containerized WAGS pipeline55. Briefly, each sample was aligned to EquCab325 (augmented with the male horse specific Y chromosome56) with BWA-MEM57, followed by left-aligning indels. SNPs and indels were called yielding genomic variant call format (GVCF) files for each sample. GVCFs were then combined and joint genotyped. SNPs were filtered using variant score recalibration (VQSR) with MNEc2M array sites58 and indels were filtered using the default hard-filtering approach. Sequence data from 21 horses was generated independently (Table S3) and raw data processed in the same manner. Variants were subset by group, and submitted to the ENSEMBL Variant Effect Predictor for annotation.

Analyses of data

The combined variant call format (VCF) file (Groups 1 and 2) was filtered to remove indels, multi-allelic variants, mitochondrial and sex chromosome markers, and markers on unplaced contigs. Twenty-five of the 26 male horses included (all in Group 2) were descended from the Darley Arabian; as prior studies demonstrated limited diversity for the Y chromosome, the diversity of Y chromosome DNA was not included in these analyses59. That file was converted to Plink (ped/map) format using VCFtools60. In Plink 1.961 only markers with a genotyping rate > 0.98 were kept. Runs of homozygosity were assessed with parameters to include ROH 300 kb or greater (–homozyg-kb 300) in 50 SNP windows (–homozyg-window-snp 50, requiring at least 100 SNPs in the ROH (–homozyg-snp 100), allowing up to 5 heterozygotes (–homozyg-window-het 5), 2 missing genotypes (–homozyg-window-missing 2), and allowing a gap of 400 kb between SNPs that could be considered within the same ROH. Minimum SNP density was left at the default (1 SNP/50 kb). The analyses included a total of 12,271,627 loci with an overall genotyping rate > 0.9999. The working genome size (2,270,643,051 bp) was calculated using the same commands but with a mock individual, homozygous for all loci. Estimates of inbreeding (FROH) were calculated based upon the ratio of the sum of all runs of homozygosity to the genome size. The contribution of runs of homozygosity of varying sizes (300-1 Mb, 1-2 Mb, 2-4 Mb, 4-8 Mb and > 8 Mb) was evaluated. Differences between mean values of the groups were evaluated using t-tests.

To allow for a comparison of diversity statistics derived from WGS data vs that which may be obtained using SNP arrays, 60,348 markers of the Illumina Equine GGP array (GGP), and 408,253 markers from the Affymetrix Equine 670 K array (HD) were subset from the WGS data. The Illumina and Affymetrix probes respectively were mapped to the EquCab3 reference genome25. The subset of markers used mapped uniquely to the reference genome and had maf > 0. In Plink, data were additionally pruned to remove loci genotyping at a rate of less than 98%. Additional visualization of ROH in the WGS of Group 2 was performed using the sliding RUNS option in detectRuns with parameters similar to those used in Plink (50 SNP window, min length 300 kb, max gap 400 kb, max het 5, min SNP/window 100, min density 1/50 kb, threshold 0.05). Runs of homozygosity using the markers on the SNP arrays were assayed using parameters adjusted to account for the lower marker density of the arrays guided in part by Duntsch et al.48 and Meyermans et al.47 For example, ~ 8 SNPs are expected per 300 kb on the GGP array and thus it is unreasonable to set a minimum number of SNPs in ROH to a number that exceeds the empirical distribution of markers. In the analysis of the GGP data, window size was set to 5 with a minimum number of homozygous SNPs set at 8 and the minimum SNP density set at 1 SNP per 100 kb. Due to low marker density, 1 missing and no heterozygous SNPs were allowed in a ROH. For analysis of the HD array, window size and minimum number of SNPs was 30 with 1 heterozygous locus allowed per ROH. The maximum allowed gap for both analyses was 400 kb. ROH were also evaluated within each group with plots identifying loci shared by at least 75% of each sample noted.

Analyses of inbreeding based upon pedigree data were conducted in Endog62 using the 5-generation pedigrees of all 185 horses. Individual estimates of inbreeding (F) were calculated using only 5-generations of pedigree information as well as all data/generations available for the studied horses. Pedigree-based estimates of inbreeding were compared to those derived from runs of homozygosity. The pedigrees of each horse were consulted using the online database “Thoroughbred Pedigree Query” (https://www.pedigreequery.com/) to identify the earliest recorded dam following the bottom female line (tail-female). Dam family designations were assigned using Bruce Lowe family numbers and the identity and histories of the horses were found at: https://www.tbheritage.com/Dams.html.

Data from 101 Japanese Thoroughbreds were obtained from the author21. VCF data from the Japanese horses was merged with that from the North American horses, keeping only bi-allelic SNP loci shared in both datasets (11.05 million). SNPs were selected every 250,000 bp (–bp-space 250,000) to reduce possible bias due to linkage disequilibrium (N = 9044). Estimates of relationship due to genome sharing (pi-hat) were calculated in Plink to allow for a comparison of average relationships within and among the three samples. A principal component plot was constructed in Plink using the same data set.

To assess variation in the mitochondrial genome, regions of the mtDNA genome mapping to autosomes (chr31:16,560,500–16,561,900, chr14:15,446,900–15,447,800, and chr27:37,569,800–37,576,100) were extracted from the bam files of each individual using BEDTools bamtofastq63. Combined with the reads initially mapping to the mitochondria, all reads were remapped to only the mtDNA genome as a reference. The data were processed as above for the whole-genome dataset except for variant calling in GATK, which included the -ploidy flag to indicate a haploid genome. The entire mtDNA sequence of each individual was then converted into a fasta file using GATK’s FastaAlternateReferenceMaker. The resulting sequence files were visualized in Sequencher ver5.4.6 (GeneCodes, Ann Arbor, MI) and compared to bam files visualized in IGV to manually correct (if needed) variant calling at position 357. In Sequencher, all sequences were aligned to those from Achilli et al.32, the D-loop removed (mt:15,469–16,660), and a Nexus file created to build a median joining network64 in PopArt65; as PopArt does not consider gaps, gap sites in the Nexus file were replaced with an artificial SNP to allow for the locus to be considered variable. Major haplotype groups were identified as per Achilli et al.32. Clades A-Q were represented by sequence from the following accessions, using a sample identical to that of a North American Thoroughbred when possible: (A) JN398381.1, (B) JN398391.1, (C) JN398393.1, (D) JN398398.1, (E) JN398401.1, (F) JN398402.1, (G) JN398408.1, (H) JN398413.1, (I) JN398417.1, (J) JN398419.1, (K) JN398420.1, (L) JN398429.1, (M) JN398435.1, (N) JN398440.1, (O) JN398445.1, (P) JN398447.1, (Q) JN398450.1.

A comparison of variation in mtDNA was also made to Bower et al.34, which included data from Hill et al.33. Representative haplotypes of the 25 unique, 346 bp D-loop sequences reported in Bower et al.34 were downloaded from NCBI and aligned to those from the US samples in a similar manner as described above. The aligned sequences were utilized to create a nexus file for the construction of Median Joining Networks as described above.
