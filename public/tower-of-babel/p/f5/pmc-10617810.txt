Mentorship refers to the relationship whereby a highly knowledgeable or experienced person shares or transform the information or content of interest to the less knowledgeable or experienced person in that particular area1,2. This relationship emphasizes on helping the individual grow and accomplish goals. It might involve professional and career development support, role modelling and psychosocial support. Such relationships help students to acclimatize to the medical school environments and enhance their social-academic prosperity1.

Mentorship relationship can be in different forms depending on how mentor and the mentee interacts1. In higher learning setting, mentorship can be between a teacher or faculty member and a student (hierarchical) or among students themselves (peer mentorship). In all of these scenarios, there can be one mentor (a person who mentors) to one mentee (a person who is mentored), one mentors and more than one mentees, one mentee and more than one mentors, more than one mentors and more than one mentee. The program can be with physically routine meetings or completely virtual or both, depending on the agreement between the participants. Each of the mentorship relationship above (teacher to student or student to student) has its advantages and its disadvantages. Despite the fact that peer mentorship is preferred more in limited resources settings especially with small ratio of faculty members or teacher to students, but they work more in harmony with hierarchical mentorship when practiced together in academic institutions1,3.

This article aims at highlighting and stressing on the need for mentorship programs in medical schools given their social-academic benefits.

Mentorship programs and medical schools

Why is it important to have mentorship programs in medical schools? Mentors can be considered as social-academic parents, sisters and brothers who are looking after students’ welfare more closely at the personal level. Medical field is very unique compared to a number of other professional fields in term of both learning and practicing. Learning from those who are more experienced in the field plays a very significant role in ensuring the competency, professionalism and prosperity of a learner in the medical field. Regardless of the level of an individual’s competence, but by associating with those who are more experienced in the area, a person is able to perform something to the possible best way and appropriately. Even if someone do not need a mentors now, but with time, someone will need mentors to increase chances of career’s prosperity. Mentorship is important because it ensures provision of both career support, career guidance, skill development, networking, sponsorship, psychosocial support, emotional support, confidence boosting, and role modelling1. Subjecting students under mentorship while at the medical school does not only ensure their social-academic prosperity and maintain their mental health, but also prepare them to mentor the coming generation of young doctors1,4,5.

The delicate nature of the medical field calls upon diligent mentors to guide and care for the students who without that, are more likely to become medical citizens who are not patriots to the field6. Since not every student is confident requesting of being a mentee to a certain college staff or senior student, establishing official mentorship programs and leadership at the medical schools can be helpful to such kind of students. Let the programs be participatory with the chances to student at least to express their interest on who to be their mentors instead of just allocating them randomly without them knowing their mentors prior. Both mentees and mentors should be sensitized about the necessity and the benefits of such programs and guideline handbook for the program should be available and accessible to both mentors and mentees. Through the guidelines, both mentees and mentors can be highlighted on what is mentorship and why is it important, expectations of the programs and so on.

Most medical schools in developing countries like in sub-Saharan Africa lack or poorly implement these mentorship programs and most of students and staffs in those institutions are neither aware nor practicing mentorship programs7,8. There is a need for revival and reformation towards mentorship programs in such institutions. In some situation, lack of mentorship experience among staff members might make them refrain from offering mentorship so that students can experience the same hardship. This is a serious professional crime among medical educators. If both students and staff members could be educated on the benefits of such programs, they could find them worth participating. Mentors (staff members) will not only get a chance to exercise and transfer their knowledge and skills, but also they will be doing a lifetime professional investment which might pay later enormously.

Implementing mentorship programs would address a number of issues around social and academic life of both students and staff members, reduce chances of academic bullying from teachers to students or students themselves, enhance professional development and satisfaction and ensure retention of students in the medical field post-graduation1,9. Most students are struggling with a number of issues and find none to share with, from social-academic to mental health challenges since not everyone can talk to the dean of students or communicate their personal matters to the University or College administration. One systematic review found that presence of mentorship programs in medical schools can strongly improve medical school satisfaction and career development10.

Conclusion and recommendation

There is an undeniable necessity of implementing mentorship programs in medical schools. All medical schools which do not have mentorship programs should consider the need of establishing them at different levels including undergraduate and post-graduate trainings. Both students and staff members should be informed and well highlighted on such kind of programs to ensure their maximum participation. Both hierarchical and peer mentorship can be executed at once to achieve maximum benefits of mentorship programs since they are complementing each other. When such programs are formally established, students should be encouraged to seek for mentors personally and staff members should be encouraged to be friendly and available to students who needs their support. Presence of mentorship programs in medical schools is of necessity since these mentorship programs will be transforming the hearts and minds of medical trainees into great and responsible physicians, surgeons, teachers, public health experts and so on in the future who cares and are ready to mentor their juniors too.
