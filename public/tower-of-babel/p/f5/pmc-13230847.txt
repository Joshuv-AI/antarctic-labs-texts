HCCaging: a liver physiological aging-related biomarker for hepatocellular carcinoma diagnosis based on transcriptome data
Authors: Bin Yu, Yajuan Zhang, Yong Tang, Meiling Hu, Jinfen Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aging is a fundamental biological process that influences cancer development in a context-dependent manner; however, how aging-related programs manifest in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) remains incompletely understood. Here, we systematically characterized aging-associated features in HCC by establishing a liver cancer-specific aging signature, termed HCCaging, across more than 2,000 tumor samples from 16 independent cohorts. We comprehensively evaluated its heterogeneity and associations with clinical outcomes, tumor stage, immune infiltration, and therapeutic response. The HCCaging score increased with chronological age, was higher in normal liver than tumor tissues, and elevated in early- versus late-stage tumors. In contrast, 13 previously reported aging- or senescence-related gene sets failed to show consistent patterns across these conditions in HCC. Machine learning models, including gradient boosting machines and random forests, achieved higher accuracy in distinguishing tumor from non-tumor samples using the HCCaging score compared with other 13 aging- or senescence-gene sets across eight independent HCC cohorts. Single-cell transcriptomic profiling revealed that HCCaging increased with age, particularly within epithelial compartments, reaching its highest levels in hepatocytes. Notably, although the proportion of T/NK cells declined with aging, their functional programs, including activated effector function, chemokine/chemokine receptor signaling, cytolytic activity, and pro-inflammatory pathways, were enhanced in older individuals. The HCCaging score, together with key genes ACAA1 and ESR1, were negatively correlated with T/NK cell infiltration, anti-inflammatory activity, and anti-apoptotic signatures, but positively correlated with pro-apoptotic, pro-inflammatory, chemokine, and cytolytic pathways. Furthermore, increased expression of XCL1 and XCL2 in T/NK cells with aging correlated positively with HCCaging, ACAA1, and ESR1, suggesting preserved or even enhanced antitumor potential of T/NK cells in older patients. Collectively, these findings highlight the dual role of aging in liver tumorigenesis. Hepatic aging and enhanced T/NK cell effector function may confer tumor-protective effects, whereas the concomitant decline in overall T/NK cell infiltration likely compromises immunosurveillance, thereby increasing carcinogenic susceptibility in the aging liver. This study provides new insights into the heterogeneity of hepatic aging and its complex interplay with the HCC tumor microenvironment and clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Contemporary cancer epidemiology underscores that age, together with metabolic and lifestyle factors, has become a predominant determinant of cancer risk, highlighting the critical importance of investigating these elements for effective tumor prevention1. Aging has therefore emerged as a major focus in life sciences, with increasing attention devoted to its multifaceted relationship with cancer. Rather than representing a single uniform process, aging influences tumor initiation and progression through diverse mechanisms that can exert either tumor-promoting or tumor-suppressive effects depending on tissue context and biological state2,3. Recent studies have begun to elucidate the molecular complexity underlying aging-associated tumor biology. For example, methylmalonic acid, a by-product of propionate metabolism, is upregulated in the serum of older individuals and serves as a mediator of tumor progression4. Additionally, aging has been shown to drive breast tumorigenesis by inducing epithelial and immune alterations in the mammary gland, with midkine identified as a key age-upregulated mediator that accelerates premalignant progression and tumor initiation through the PI3K-AKT-SREBF1 pathway5. The aged tumor microenvironment, with its ceramide-enriched lipid landscape, promotes metastatic liver tropism by activating the S1P–STAT3–IL-6 axis and suppressing oxidative phosphorylation in cancer cells6.

In parallel with experimental advances, the availability of large-scale omics datasets has facilitated the development of multiple aging-associated gene expression signatures aimed at quantifying biological aging processes. Representative examples include SenMayo7, CellAge8, GenAge9, ASIG10, SASP11, AgingAtlas12, SenUp12, SigRS12, DAS12, mSS12, hUSI12, SenFib13 and other classical senescence-associated gene signatures14. While these signatures have provided valuable insights into aging biology, they are largely derived from cross-tissue or cell-intrinsic senescence frameworks and may not fully capture tissue-specific aging programs. Indeed, recent studies have reported that tumors in younger patients can display prematurely aged molecular features, whereas tumors in older individuals often exhibit immunologically distinct microenvironments, underscoring the context-dependent nature of aging-associated signatures15.

Liver cancer, particularly hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), remains a significant global health challenge. According to the GLOBOCAN 2022 and the 2024 National Cancer Report of China, liver cancer ranks 6th globally in incidence and 3rd in mortality, with HCC accounting for 75% to 85% of all liver cancer cases16,17. Despite extensive research into aging and cancer, it remains unclear whether younger patients exhibit distinct aging-associated molecular features compared to older patients with liver cancer. Specifically, whether aging-related signatures derived from other tissues or biological contexts are applicable to liver cancer, and how age-associated molecular features relate to tumor stage, progression, immune landscape, and clinical outcome in HCC, remain largely unexplored.

In this study, we conducted a comprehensive investigation of aging-associated features in liver cancer. Through chronological analyses across multiple independent cohorts, we identified a core set of genes that define an HCC-specific aging signature, termed HCCaging, reflecting age-associated hepatic functional states rather than canonical cellular senescence alone. We integrated clinical data to examine the relationships between HCCaging and tumor stage, therapeutic response, and patient outcome, and systematically compared its performance with 13 established aging- or senescence-related signatures. Furthermore, we constructed machine-learning models to evaluate the predictive value of HCCaging across diverse HCC cohorts. Finally, leveraging single-cell transcriptomic data, we dissected cell-type-specific aging dynamics and characterized how aging-associated hepatic programs interact with immune cell infiltration and function within the HCC microenvironment. Together, this work provides a refined framework for understanding tissue-specific aging programs in HCC and their clinical relevance.

Results

We performed differential expression analysis on the 40 candidate genes across different age stages using TCGA data. This screening identified 16 genes (C1S, CYP2A6, CYP2C8, ESR1, SERPING1, HRG, SERPINC1, SLC27A5, TTR, GLYAT, HPR, HSD11B1, ABCA6, CYP4A11, ACAA1, and ALDH2) that demonstrated significant alterations in expression with age (P < 0.05, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 2). To independently validate the identified age-associated gene signature, we derived an HCCaging score via gene set variation analysis (GSVA) using the 16 core genes. This score consistently showed a positive correlation with patient age across six independent cohorts (Fig. 1C). A higher HCCaging score was associated with a trend toward improved prognosis (Fig. 1D). Moreover, elevated expression of 10 individual genes within this signature independently correlated with better patient outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 3). Moreover, the HCCaging score was significantly higher in normal liver tissues and early-stage tumors compared to late-stage tumors (Fig. 1E). Notably, it was elevated in patients responding to transcatheter arterial chemoembolization (TACE) but lower in those resistant to sorafenib, indicating a complex association with treatment response (Fig. 1E).

Analysis across six independent datasets confirmed that the expression of these 16 genes increased consistently with age (Fig. 2A, Supplementary Fig. 4A) and was predominantly downregulated in tumor versus normal tissues, as well as in late- versus early-stage tumors (Fig. 2B, Supplementary Fig. 4B). Machine learning-based construction of a diagnostic biomarker for HCC

Based on the observed expression patterns, specifically, the elevated levels of SenMayo, mSS, and HCCaging in normal tissues versus the upregulation of DAS, SASP and SigRS in tumors across multiple datasets, we employed machine learning to assess the diagnostic potential of these aging- or senescence-related signatures for distinguishing liver tumor from non-tumor samples. We systematically evaluated multiple signatures using random forest and gradient boosting machine (GBM) models, trained on the TCGA cohort and validated across seven independent cohorts (GSE14520, GSE76427, OEP000321, GSE10143, GSE25097, GSE63898, and LIRI).

In the GBM model, existing signatures such as ClassicalSs showed moderate performance (area under the curve (AUC) = 0.805 in TCGA, 0.776 in LIRI), while SASP and SigRS exhibited strong predictive power in specific datasets (e.g., SigRS AUC = 0.938 in OEP000321, SASP AUC = 0.850 in OEP000321) (Fig. 5A right panel, Supplementary Data 3). Notably, the HCCaging signature achieved consistently superior discrimination, attaining the highest AUC across all datasets (range: 0.841–0.983), including 0.983 in TCGA and 0.925 in both GSE14520 and OEP000321 (Fig. 5A left panel, Supplementary Data 3).Fig. 5Performance of the 14 senescence- or age-related gene sets in predicting tumor tissues based on machine learning.A AUC values of HCCaging across 8 datasets based on GBM and AUC values of the 14 gene set scores across 8 datasets based on GBM. Similarly, in the random forest model, among the various gene sets assessed, SenMayo and ClassicalSs demonstrated moderate discriminative capacity. For instance, SenMayo achieved AUC of 0.754 in GSE63898 and 0.704 in LIRI, while ClassicalSs yielded AUC of 0.541 in GSE63898 and 0.605 in LIRI (Fig. 5B right panel). The HCCaging signature again exhibited outstanding and stable predictive accuracy, with high AUC spanning 0.830–0.922 across all eight cohorts (e.g., 0.904 in TCGA, 0.949 in GSE14520) (Fig. 5B left panel, Supplementary Data 3).

Collectively, these results identify the HCCaging signature as a robust and generalizable biomarker for HCC detection, significantly outperforming established aging- senescence-related gene sets across independent cohorts and machine-learning frameworks. The consistency and magnitude of its performance underscore the potential of HCCaging as a reliable molecular tool for distinguishing tumor tissues from non-tumor liver tissues.

HCCaging score distribution at single-cell resolution and its association with tumor immune microenvironment

Using single-cell RNA sequencing data from 89 samples, we first performed clustering to identify major cell types and their subpopulations. The HCCaging score was predominantly enriched in epithelial cells (Fig. 6A), where its expression showed a gradual increase with advancing age (Fig. 6B, Supplementary Fig. 9A). This suggests that epithelial cells may serve as the principal site of HCCaging activity and exhibit a clear age-dependent accumulation of this signature (Fig. 6C). Further analysis of epithelial cell subtypes revealed that hepatocytes were the dominant source of HCCaging expression (Fig. 6D). We next explored the relationship between the HCCaging score and the tumor immune microenvironment by analyzing its correlation with immune cell infiltration. The HCCaging score was significantly negatively correlated with the overall abundance of immune cells, particularly with T/NK infiltration (Fig. 7A). In contrast, it was positively correlated with the proportions of epithelial and hepatocyte-like cells (Fig. 7A). This spatial pattern of exclusion was corroborated in four spatially resolved transcriptomic sections, where regions of high HCCaging activity inversely correlated with local T/NK cell abundance (Supplementary Fig. 12A, 12B). Notably, within these spatial transcriptomic datasets, the HCCaging score itself was also downregulated in tumor regions compared to adjacent non-tumor tissues (Supplementary Fig. 12C). These results suggest that aging-related changes in the HCC context impair T/NK cell infiltration. To resolve this apparent discrepancy, we further investigated the functional states of T/NK cells rather than their abundance. By quantifying functional gene signature scores within T/NK cells, we observed that increasing age was accompanied by a significant upregulation of multiple immune-related programs, including pro-apoptotic signaling, pro-inflammatory responses, chemokine and chemokine receptor pathways, activation and effector functions, as well as cytolytic effector pathways. Conversely, signatures related to anti-apoptosis, anti-inflammation, and T-regulatory function exhibited a downregulation with advancing age (Supplementary Fig. 14, Supplementary Fig. 15). Importantly, the HCCaging score in epithelial showed consistent positive correlations with these functional signatures in T/NK (Fig. 7B, Supplementary Fig. 16). Meanwhile, the expression levels of ACAA1 (Fig. 7C) and ESR1 (Fig. 7D) also showed correlations with the gene set scores in T cells. These findings indicate that, despite a decline in T/NK cell infiltration with age, the remaining T cells exhibit enhanced effector and inflammatory functional states. This suggests that aging-related protective mechanisms may operate through qualitative modulation of immune activity, characterized by heightened immune activation rather than increased immune cell abundance.

Consistent with this notion, expression levels of the chemokine genes XCL1 and XCL2, primarily expressed in T/NK cells, increased progressively with age, whereas levels of the T-cell marker genes CD3D and CD3E declined, further supporting the concurrent age-associated immune activation and reduction in overall T-cell abundance (Fig. 8A). Discussion

In this study, we constructed a novel liver-specific aging–associated gene expression signature, termed HCCaging, based on transcriptomic profiling of HCC. The HCCaging signature effectively discriminated tumor from normal liver tissues as well as early-stage from late-stage HCC, and consistently outperformed 13 previously reported aging- or senescence-related gene sets across 8 independent cohorts. Importantly, functional annotation revealed that HCCaging is predominantly composed of genes involved in hepatic metabolic, detoxification, and homeostatic processes, rather than canonical markers of cellular senescence. These findings indicate that HCCaging captures a physiological aging and functional state of hepatocytes, rather than a classical senescence-specific cellular program.

Notably, higher HCCaging scores were associated with favorable clinical outcomes, including prolonged survival and improved therapeutic response. While cellular senescence has traditionally been implicated in both tumor-suppressive and tumor-promoting roles, our results suggest that age-associated preservation of hepatic functional programs may confer tumor-restraining effects in HCC. In this context, HCCaging likely reflects a differentiated and metabolically competent hepatocyte state, which is progressively lost during tumor progression and dedifferentiation. This interpretation is further supported by the enrichment of HCCaging in normal liver tissues, early-stage tumors, and hepatocytes at the single-cell level.

Classical cellular senescence has been linked to tumorigenesis through mechanisms such as genomic instability, chronic inflammation, and microenvironmental remodeling18–20, while also serving as a tumor-suppressive barrier under certain conditions21. However, our comparative analyses demonstrate that established senescence-associated gene sets, including SASP, and cell cycle arrest–related signatures, do not exhibit consistent age-dependent trends in HCC across bulk and single-cell transcriptomic datasets. Moreover, these canonical senescence signatures exhibit limited concordance with tumor stage, tissue context, or clinical outcomes, underscoring the strong context dependency and tissue specificity of aging-related transcriptional programs and indicating that such age- or senescence-related signatures are unlikely to serve as universal markers of organ-specific aging.

In contrast to conventional senescence signatures, HCCaging is characterized by its consistent downregulation in tumor tissues, further reduction in advanced-stage disease, association with favorable prognosis, and enrichment in treatment-responsive patients. These features suggest that HCCaging reflects a protective hepatic aging program that is progressively attenuated during malignant transformation and tumor progression. Similar observations have been reported in prior pan-cancer studies, where certain aging- or senescence-related signatures are lower in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal samples and are associated with improved survival in specific cancer types, including LIHC12,22–25. These studies collectively suggest that tumor progression is not solely driven by the acquisition of oncogenic traits but may also involve active escape from protective senescence-like states that are essential for maintaining the malignant phenotype.

The HCCaging signature further reveals connections to age-associated metabolic reprogramming, with enhanced fatty acid oxidation and induction of detoxification genes such as those in the CYP450 family (e.g., CYP2A6, CYP2C8, CYP4A11). These findings are consistent with a growing body of evidence indicating that aging-associated gene programs can exert protective, anti-tumorigenic effects. For example, C1S has been associated with retinal aging26 and Alzheimer’s disease27, while CYP2A6 activity correlates with physiological age28. Similarly, SERPINC1 is a key gene in age-related muscle pathologies29 and as a pleiotropic gene involved in both inflammatory and senescence pathways, contributing to the shared genetic mechanism between aging and giant cell arteritis30. Circulating HRG levels demonstrate significant associations with both biological aging and all-cause mortality risk31. TTR deposition in aging cartilage exacerbates osteoarthritis severity by promoting inflammatory and catabolic responses32, while GLYAT expression peaks in the mouse liver during adolescence33. Additionally, HSD11B1 has been shown to play a role in sarcopenic muscle pathology and age-related neurotoxicity34,35. ALDH2, associated with aging through its role in clearing reactive aldehydes, has been identified as a promising therapeutic target for delaying aging-related cellular damage36 and ALDH2 expression in liver but not lung in rats increases with advancing age37. Emerging evidence indicates that ESR1 expression increases with age in estrogen receptor-positive breast cancer, which has been attributed to an uncontrolled feedback loop triggered by low postmenopausal estrogen levels38. In parallel, another recent study reported upregulation of ESR1 expression in senescent mammary glands39.

Given the established association between CD8 + T cell infiltration and both HCC progression and therapeutic response40, we further investigated the infiltration status of T/NK cells in our cohort of aging HCC samples. A particularly intriguing finding in our study is the significant negative correlation between the HCCaging score and T/NK cell infiltration, especially given that T/NK cells are shown to decline with age. Paradoxically, T/NK cells are considered to have anti-tumor activity, yet HCCaging is more prevalent in normal and early-stage tumors where one would theoretically expect higher infiltration. This apparent paradox suggests a complex interplay between aging-related gene signatures and immune surveillance in liver cancer development. It is possible that the aging process impairs the infiltration of T cells, thus reducing immune surveillance and promoting tumor progression. Interestingly, this pattern aligns with the observation that aged T/NK cells decline with aging, suggesting that age-related dysregulation may lead to the expansion of cell populations that contribute to cancer development25.

A novel finding of this study is that ESR1 expression is negatively correlated with T-cell infiltration but positively associated with T-cell immune activation scores. Consistent with recent reports showing that estrogen receptor signaling41 and ESR1 expression42 are linked to reduced T-cell infiltration in breast cancer based on scRNA-seq and multiplexed immunofluorescence analyses, we demonstrate that ESR1, as a component of the HCCaging signature, exhibits an age-dependent increase and a negative correlation with T/NK cell infiltration in HCC. Notably, ESR1 expression is lower in tumor tissues than in adjacent normal liver, in agreement with previous studies43. Together, these convergent observations across breast and liver cancers suggest that aging-related regulation of ESR1 may be partially conserved across organ systems. More broadly, these results align with emerging evidence that age-associated metabolic and immune remodeling, rather than canonical cellular senescence alone, plays a central role in shaping cancer risk and progression. As recently highlighted by Karsidag and Liu, global cancer epidemiology is undergoing a paradigm shift characterized by rising early-onset cancers and an increasing dominance of metabolic, hormonal, and inflammatory environments as key cancer drivers1. Within this evolving framework, our findings support the notion that genes comprising the HCCaging signature may contribute to maintaining tissue homeostasis, modulating immune functional states, and restraining malignant progression during early stages of liver cancer. Collectively, this positions HCCaging within a broader cancer–aging paradigm that emphasizes immune–metabolic regulation and tissue-specific functional aging, with important implications for precision prevention and early interception strategies in hepatocellular carcinoma.

Despite the comprehensive multi-omics analyses performed in this study, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we validated the HCCaging signature across multiple independent bulk RNA-seq, single-cell RNA-seq, and spatial transcriptomics datasets, all analyses were based on publicly available transcriptomic data. Due to limitations in experimental resources and time constraints, we were unable to perform additional experimental validation, such as sequencing of in-house patient samples, age-stratified human or murine single-cell profiling, or functional assays in normal hepatocytes, hepatocellular carcinoma cells, and their aged counterparts. While the consistency observed across diverse independent datasets supports the robustness of our findings, experimental validation remains an essential next step to establish causal relationships and mechanistic insights. Second, this study primarily relies on liver tissue transcriptome data. Although tissue-based analyses provide critical insights into the cellular and spatial heterogeneity of aging-associated hepatocellular carcinoma, they may limit the immediate translational applicability of the HCCaging signature for non-invasive clinical detection. Future studies incorporating body fluid transcriptome data, such as circulating RNA or other liquid biopsy–based approaches, would substantially enhance the clinical utility of aging-related biomarkers. Finally, future research should aim to integrate systemic aging-associated secretory phenotype profiling with tissue-specific transcriptomic analyses. A critical next step will be to bridge macro-scale secretory dynamics with their local tissue and cellular contexts, thereby enabling the translation of aging-related molecular signatures into clinically actionable tools for precision diagnosis, prognostic stratification, and therapeutic intervention in HCC.

In conclusion, in contrast to previous studies that failed to establish a clear positive correlation between aging and specific gene expression in HCC patients, our research identifies HCCaging as a novel and robust molecular signature that bridges this gap in a liver-specific context. Notably, although multiple aging- and senescence-related gene sets have been proposed based on peripheral blood and diverse tissues, our results suggest that such signatures do not function as universal markers of organ-specific aging. In this regard, HCCaging captures a context-dependent hepatic aging program rather than a generalized aging or senescence signal. Beyond serving as an indicator of age-associated molecular states, HCCaging reflects the activity of an intrinsic, aging-related protective program that appears to constrain early hepatocarcinogenesis. This signature demonstrates significant utility in distinguishing liver tumors from normal tissues, with its diagnostic performance further enhanced by the machine learning model developed for HCC classification. Collectively, these findings position HCCaging as a valuable framework for understanding aging-related functional remodeling in the liver and its relationship to tumor progression, while highlighting its potential translational relevance for diagnosis and risk stratification. To refine our understanding of the mechanistic links between aging, immune surveillance, and liver cancer development, further studies, particularly those focusing on younger patient populations and incorporating more normal tissue samples, are necessary.

Methods

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The analysis incorporated data from public repositories, including bulk sequencing and microarray data from TCGA, Cancer Genome Consortium (ICGC), OEP00032144, OMIX01047342 and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (GSE7642745, GSE12475146, GSE1014347, GSE1452048, GSE2509749 and GSE6389850). The single cell data was obtained from GSE14961451, CNP000065052, PRJCA00774453, GSE23586354, GSE15153055, GSE12544956 and GSE20237957. Spatially resolved transcriptomic data were downloaded from the CROST database (VISDP000084)58, as originally generated and reported in the associated study59.

Aging trajectory analysis

The temporal dynamics of gene expression across aging were analyzed with the Mfuzz package (v2.66.0). For each gene, the mean expression value was calculated within each of the ten ordered age groups. These averaged temporal profiles were subsequently clustered via a fuzzy c-means algorithm to identify gene modules sharing similar expression trajectories. This soft clustering framework allows genes to have probabilistic membership across multiple clusters, thereby accommodating inherent biological noise and robustly identifying coordinately regulated aging-associated gene programs.

Gene signatures score calculation

HCCaging gene set was derived from the present study. Specifically, through longitudinal trend analysis across multiple cohorts, we independently identified genes showing a progressive increase in expression with age, 272 genes in TCGA, 220 in a scRNA-seq dataset, and 145 in ICGC (Fig. 1A, Supplementary Data 1). Intersection of these candidate lists yielded 40 consistently age‑associated genes (Supplementary Data 1). From these, 16 genes exhibiting strictly consistent upregulation with age in TCGA were selected as the core HCCaging signature for all subsequent scoring analyses (Supplementary Fig. 1–2). The remaining other 13 gene sets were all obtained from published literature (Supplementary Data 2). Subsequently, GSVA scores for each of the 14 gene sets were calculated using the GSVA package (v2.0.0). These scores were then used for subsequent analysis and machine learning model construction. The other gene signatures (Supplementary Data 2) related T cell function was collated from previous studies60. We assessed the activity of specific transcriptional programs using module scoring implemented in Seurat. The AddModuleScore function was applied to calculate enrichment scores based on the average expression of input gene sets.

Enrichment analysis

The genes comprising the HCCaging signature was used for gene enrichment analysis using the clusterProfiler package (v. 4.6.0). The organism database used was org.Hs.eg.db, and the gene ontology annotations were provided by Gene Ontology. Enrichment magnitude was evaluated using a normalized enrichment score (NES) > 1, and statistical significance was assessed using a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05.

Machine learning-based prognostic modeling

To construct a machine learning model capable of distinguishing between normal and cancerous liver tissues, we employed the gradient boosting machine (GBM, r-gbm v 2.2.2) approach and randomForest (RF, r-randomforest v. 4.7_1.2). The workflow was designed to construct and validate binary classification models across multiple transcriptomic datasets.

The TCGA cohort was used as the training dataset, with normal and cancerous liver tissues were assigned as class labels. Independent validation was performed on external cohorts from the ICGC, OEP000321, GSE76427, GSE10143, GSE14520, GSE25097, and GSE63898. For each sample across all cohorts, the HCCaging score, together with a series of published senescence/aging-related expression signatures (SenMayo, CellAge, GenAge, ASIG, SASP, AgingAtlas, SenUp, SigRS, DAS, mSS, hUSI, ClassicalSs and SenFib), were used as predictive features for distinguishing normal and cancerous liver tissues. These signatures were incorporated individually into the machine learning framework to enable direct comparison of their classification performance.

Subsequently, the GBM and RF algorithms were implemented to construct predictive models. GBM was employed to iteratively optimize classification performance through sequential tree boosting, and the final predictive model was refined using hyperparameters 10,000 trees (shrinkage = 0.001, interaction depth = 3, min. observations = 10) and employed 5-fold cross-validation to identify the optimal number of iterations (n.trees) corresponding to the minimum CV error. RF was applied as a bagging-based ensemble approach that aggregates predictions from multiple decision trees, with fixed number of trees (ntree) at 500 and out-of-bag (OOB) estimates to assess internal prediction error.

For each signature, GBM and RF models were trained on TCGA data and evaluated by using aging-related expression signatures as the independent variables (predictors) and class labels as the dependent variable (response), Upon generating the predicted probabilities through the models, predictive performance was quantified using the AUC value. The trained models were further validated across the ICGC, OEP000321, and GEO cohorts to examine their robustness and cross-cohort reproducibility. Comparative analysis of classification metrics across all tested signatures enabled the benchmarking of HCCaging against other aging-related signatures.

Single cell and spatial RNA-seq data processing

The single-cell filtering parameters used in this study are as follows: (a) cells with a mitochondrial gene percentage higher than 20% were removed. (b) cells with fewer than 200 detected genes were removed. (c) cells with more than 10000 detected genes were also removed. Preprocessed single-cell data were analyzed using Seurat (v4.3.0). After LogNormalize normalization and scaling, the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for PCA. The first 30 principal components were computed, and an elbow plot was used to determine the optimal number of PCs for UMAP visualization and clustering. Cell clusters were identified via FindNeighbors and FindClusters, with resolution ranging from 0.05–0.2 for broad cell types and 0.5–1 for subtypes. The spatial transcriptome data used in current study was obtained from 10x Visium (4 tissue). For 10x Visium data, using Read10X and CreateSeuratObject function, the spatial transcriptome data was input and created as Seurat Object in Seurat (v. 5.0.1) package. Then, all subsequent data filtering, normalization, filtering, dimensional reduction, geneses score calculating and visualization were used in Seurat (v.5.0.1) and ggplot2 (v.3.4.4) package.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R and Python. Spearman’s rank correlation was employed to assess relationships between gene expression, signature scores, and cell proportions, with FDR < 0.05 defining significance. Group comparisons utilized Kruskal-Wallis tests for multiple groups and Wilcoxon rank-sum tests for pairwise comparisons, with adjusted p-values < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Survival analysis is performed using GEPIA261. Detailed statistical methods are provided in corresponding figure legends.
