Digital Light Processing of Thermoresponsive Hydrogels from Polyproline‐Based Star Polypeptides
Authors: Robert D Murphy, Muireann Cosgrave, Nicola Judge, Ernesto Tinajero‐Diaz, Giuseppe Portale, Bing Wu, Andreas Heise
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe first report of star poly(L‐proline) crosslinkers is disclosed for digital light processing 3D printing of thermoresponsive hydrogels. Through chain end functionalization of star poly(L‐proline)s with methacryloyl groups, access to high‐resolution defined 3D hydrogel structures via digital light processing is achieved through photoinitiated free radical polymerization. Changing the poly(L‐proline) molecular weight has a direct influence on both thermoresponsiveness and printability, while shape‐morphing behavior can be induced thermally.Keywords: 3D printing, digital light processing, hydrogels, polypeptides, thermoresponsive

Introduction

Thermoresponsive hydrogels are an important class of materials with applicability in many research areas from drug delivery[ 1 , 2 ] to sensors.[ 3 , 4 ] The extent of thermoresponsiveness can be dictated by a polymer's lower critical solution temperature (LCST) phase transition, whereby soluble polymer chains undergo aggregation out of solution.[ 5 ] For hydrogels, this enables hydrophilic to hydrophobic phase separation, which can promote changes in gel mechanical properties.[ 6 ] While polymers such as poly(ethylene glycol)‐poly(lactic acid) (PEG‐PLA),[ 7 , 8 ] and poly(2‐oxazoline) (POx)[ 9 , 10 ] have been explored as thermoresponsive hydrogels, poly(N‐isopropylacrylamide) (PNIPAm) remains the most universally studied, having a programmable LCST (∼32 °C), and desirable biological properties.[ 11 ] A common component of “smart” hydrogel systems, PNIPAm can directly influence actuation (shape morphing) properties of 2D materials.[ 12 ] However, conventional fabrication of 2D shape morphing materials is often costly, multi‐step, and limited to simple geometries.[ 13 ] To combat this, implementation of smart hydrogels within 3D printing has enabled the creation of responsive 3D objects with an unprecedented level of design and high spatial resolution.[ 14 ] This emerging “‘4D printing”’ toolbox can be expanded to other stimuli and bulk polymer networks, where pragmatic chemical design provides a handle to modulate 4D actuation of 3D printed structures.[ 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 ] For PNIPAm, multiple reports have successfully demonstrated the actuation capabilities of 3D printed structures,[ 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 ] while the advent of digital light processing (DLP) permits accelerated development of such 3D prototypes.[ 25 ] For DLP 3D part production, there are few alternatives to compete with PNIPAm, though poly(proline) (PP) materials are promising candidates due to their tunable thermoresponsive behavior[ 26 ] akin to natural proteins,[ 27 ] but their utility as 3D printable materials has not been explored to date. The thermoresponsive properties of PP originate from competing secondary structures, where the pyrrolidine motifs of PP may adopt a hydrophilic, left‐handed helix (PPII) or a hydrophobic right‐handed helix (PPI).[ 28 , 29 ] While such secondary structures have hindered previous efforts to synthesize conformationally pure PP via N‐carboxyanhydride ring‐opening polymerization (NCA ROP),[ 30 , 31 , 32 ] recently developed methodologies enable direct access to water‐soluble PPII polymers, with controlled molecular weights.[ 33 ] Such PPs can be programmed to have unique protein mimetic behaviors, such as anti‐freezing properties,[ 34 ] stemming from their intrinsic thermosensitivity. This behavior has also been exploited by Badreldin et al., where PP homopolymers were subject to solution‐based thermo‐induced phase separation.[ 35 ] The PPs underwent LCST driven aggregation, hysteresis, and slow thermoreversibility, with transition to a conformational intermediate between hydrophilic PPII and hydrophobic PPI observed. These unique properties of PPs are anticipated to be highly exploitable within 3D printing, complimenting our previous work toward developing synthetic polypeptides as high viscosity direct ink writable (DIW) hydrogels.[ 36 , 37 ] For DLP, PPs would be far more suitable by design as low viscosity hydrogel resins, where PPII to PPI transitions could be thermally exploited to induce physical and mechanical transformations of PP embedded 3D objects. Herein, the thermal behavior and DLP 3D printability of a series of star poly(L‐proline) (PLP) polypeptides is explored, analysing physicochemical and mechanical properties to identify the effect of increasing PLP chain length. Polymer solubilities and hydrogel mechanical properties for star PLPs were examined over changes in temperature, with DLP printed constructs capable of shape morphing via shrinkage.

Results and Discussion

Material Design and Synthesis

Pure L‐proline N‐carboxyanhydride monomer (LP NCA) was synthesized according to previously reported methods,[ 38 ] and characterized using 1H and 13C NMR spectroscopy (Figures S1 and S2, Supporting Information). In a similar fashion to our recent study,[ 39 ] functional star poly(proline)s were then prepared in a single vessel, open to air (Scheme 1 ). Using H2O/MeCN (1:1) as a solvent,[ 33 ] ring opening polymerization (ROP) of LP NCA using a four‐armed poly(propylene imine) (PPI) dendrimer proceeded rapidly (<2 min) and yielded a series of star poly(L‐proline) (4‐PLP) homopolypeptides with increasing molecular weights. The degree of polymerization (DP) was targeted at 80 (4‐PLP80), 160 (4‐PLP160), and 320 (4‐PLP320) – to impart the four‐armed topologies with 20 units, 40 units, and 80 units per arm respectively. In the same pot, the secondary amine chain ends of each 4‐PLP was then functionalized with methacryloyl groups, using methacrylic anhydride and tetramethylguanidine (TMG). Henceforth, the methacryloyl functionalized polypeptides, 4‐PLP80MA, 4‐PLP160MA, 4‐PLP320MA were referred to as 4‐PLP1, 4‐PLP2, and 4‐PLP3. Experimental DPs were confirmed in good agreement with stoichiometric feed ratio by 1H NMR spectroscopy (Figures S3, S5, and Table S1, Supporting Information), with DPs of 76, 141, and 282 observed. Vinyl protons from methacryloyl groups were observable at 5.75–6.00 ppm on 1H NMR spectra, while DOSY NMR spectra confirmed conjugation of these vinyl groups to the chain end (Figures S6–S8, Supporting Information). DOSY NMR was also used to observe changes in PLP molecular weight in accordance with recently established, universally applicable protocols for molecular weight determination.[ 40 ] A linear decrease in the diffusion coefficients for 4‐PLP1, 4‐PLP2, and4‐PLP3 reflected an increase in DP and molecular weight for the star PLP series (Table S1, Supporting Information).

Star PLP Properties in Solution

The impact of increasing DP on solution‐based properties of star PLP systems was determined via several characterization techniques including circular dichroism (CD), transmission electron microscopy (TEM), and small angle X‐ray scattering (SAXS). CD was initially used to clarify conformational differences for secondary structures (Figure 1A; Table S2, Supporting Information). BestSel, an online protein database program, was used to calculate secondary structural content from experimental CD spectra. All of the star PLPs displayed a minima at 205 nm and a maxima at 228 nm, with only slight changes observed for increasing DP. For example, 4‐PLP1 featured the highest helicity content at 60%, with 53% and 48% observed for 4‐PLP2 and 4‐PLP3 respectively.

UV/vis turbidimetry assays on thermoresponsive behavior of star polyprolines (5.0 mg mL−1) in deionized water (D), and in 0.25 mNa2SO4 (E).

Nanostructural morphological analysis of the star PLP series was then attempted by TEM. While TEM image resolution is low (Figure S9, Supporting Information), the star PLPs do appear to undergo self‐assembly. However, this lack of sufficient resolution corroborates the lack of analysis and/or observations from TEM for many previous studies on PLPs,[ 26 , 35 , 41 ] concluding that they are inherently difficult to image. Despite this, TEM did indicate that portions of the 4‐PLP1 and 4‐PLP2 appear as cylindrical rods, while 4‐PLP3 appeared as somewhat uniform clusters, possibly from packing of rod structures. Geometric sizes were then determined via SAXS. Background subtracted data were used to obtain Guinier plots (Figure S10, Supporting Information), showing two linear regions. Using equation Iq=I0exp−q2Rg2/3, two radii of gyration (Rg ) from the slopes of the Guinier plot curves were obtained. High R2 values of fitted lines for the low‐angle region and high‐angle region indicate mainly two sizes of highly monodisperse nanostructures in the samples. Here Rg is used for diameters (Table S3, Supporting Information) assuming a spherical shape for the scattering particle (D=2Rg5/3), as suggested by the TEM analyses. The first diameter (R g1) is related to the star PLPs as a whole (16.52 nm for 4‐PLP1), while the second diameter (R g2) may be related to the individual arm (2.86 nm for 4‐PLP1). It can be deduced tighter packing occurs upon increasing PLP DP, with Rg increases from 16.52 nm (4‐PLP1) to 18.19 nm (4‐PLP2), and 19.66 nm (4‐PLP3).

The thermoresponsive behavior of the star PLPs was then analyzed by a temperature induced turbidity (20–80 °C) assay using a UV Vis spectrometer at 550 nm (Figure 1B–E). The LCST was determined by observing the phase transition temperature (cloud point, TCP), evidenced by a sudden decrease in % transmittance (%T). The longest chain length (4‐PLP3) had the lowest TCP at 48 °C, while 57 and 66 °C were measured for 4‐PLP2 and 4‐PLP1 respectively. This behavior correlates to observations from Badreldin et al.,[ 35 ] where PPII (hydrophilic) appears to incompletely transform to PPI (hydrophobic) via aggregation and phase separation (Figure 1B). Interestingly, 4‐PLP1, with 80 PLP units, demonstrated a Tcp ∼ 10 °C higher than the linear equivalent (hexyl‐PLP70) synthesized in the same report, implying that polymer topology can impact thermoresponsive properties. Interestingly, star topologies of PNIPAm exhibit a lower LCST when compared to their linear analogues due faster collapse of polymer arms.[ 42 ] However, this is likely due to the high flexibility of its poly(acrylamide) backbone, opposing the highly rigid PLP backbone studied here. The LCST aggregation behavior of PLPs can also be programmed through changes in ionic strength, as previously demonstrated for triethylene glycol (TEG) PLP derivatives.[ 43 ] To this end, Na2SO4 was selected for its enhanced salting‐out potential in comparison to NaCl, as it is positioned higher in the Hofmeister ion series.[ 44 ] At 0.25 m Na2SO4, the Tcp of all star PLPs decreased, while at 0.5 m PLPs had low solubility. 4‐PLP1 changed from 66 to 53 °C, 4‐PLP2 changed from 57 to 46 °C, and 4‐PLP3 changed from 48 to 42 °C, demonstrating that ionic strength can modulate the PLP LCST.

Photorheology and DLP Printing

Each star PLP was formulated into a water‐based photocrosslinkable hydrogel resin using lithium phenyl‐2,4,6‐trimethylbenzoylphosphinate (LAP) as a photoinitiator and N‐hydroxyethylacrylamide (HEAAm) as a comonomer (Figure 2 ; Table S4, Supporting Information). Real‐time photocrosslinking regimes of the 4‐PLP resins using visible light irradiation were then observed rheologically. Resins were allowed 90 s to incubate, displaying fluid‐like properties, before subsequent photo‐irradiation using a collimated light source (405 nm, 4 mW cm−2). The evolution of the storage modulus (G’) and the characteristic gel point crossover of loss modulus (G’’) was observed within 30 s for liquid 4‐PLP resins (Figure 3A). All hydrogels underwent a G’ plateau after ∼300 s of irradiation, forming solid hydrogels with moduli of 6485, 3015, and 42 Pa for 4‐PLP1, 4PLP2, and 4‐PLP3, respectively. Lowering HEAAm concentrations to 5.0 wt.% significantly slowed crosslinking speeds by 50 s and afforded lower mechanical properties (Figure S11, Supporting Information). Increasing PLP chain length appears to impart the formed hydrogels with significantly weaker mechanical properties, evidenced by the fourfold difference in modulus between 4‐PLP2 (DP 160) and 4‐PLP3 (DP 320). This could stem from denser packing of 4‐PLP3 secondary structures resulting in inaccessibility to chain ends, while lower crosslink density would be anticipated due to longer PP chains between crosslink points of the star topology.[ 45 ] The swelling ratios (Q) and mesh sizes increased with increasing DP, with Q of 9.88 for 4‐PLP1 increasing to 18.66 for 4‐PLP3, having mesh sizes of 8.76 and 46.98 respectively (Table S5, Supporting Information).

For DLP printing of the resins, a simple resolution test was attempted with the 4‐PLP1 resin to print a multi‐featured 2D object. The initial test print displayed poor fidelity in terms of structural 2D features using a 10 s light exposure time per layer, though vastly improved feature resolution was observable upon increasing exposure time to 22 s per layer (Figure S12, Supporting Information). These printing conditions were then maintained for all PLP resins (Table S4, Supporting Information), where printing resolution changes significantly with increasing PLP chain length (Figure 3C). The 4‐PLP1 resin demonstrated high DLP printing structural fidelity to its corresponding CAD file of a 3D starfish and turtle (Figure S13, Supporting Information) at a low polymer concentration of 10 wt.%. However, the printing resolution of the 3D starfish from the 4‐PLP2 resin was much lower despite using the same polymer concentration. For the 4‐PLP3 resin, insufficient layer curing meant that resolution completely deteriorated for 4‐PLP3, with no observable features of the starfish CAD file. This correlates directly with the lower mechanical properties for formed 4‐PLP3 hydrogels as observed rheologically.

For printing of objects with distinct 3D features, it was necessary to adjust the lead 4‐PLP1 resin formulation. This was evident after attempting to DLP print a 3D gyroid structure, where the resin alone was incapable of yielding defined interstitial voids associated with the input CAD file (Figure S14, Supporting Information). This was rectified by the addition of Orange G (OG) to the resin, which is a photo absorbing dye (photomask) in the region of irradiation (405 nm). While OG addition did reduce the mechanical properties, from 6485 to 3476 Pa (Figure 3D), it provided necessary masking effects to produce well defined DLP printed 3D gyroids with porous voids (Figure S14, Supporting Information). High‐resolution bishop and rook 3D chess pieces could also be printed with high‐resolution features (Figure 3E). The thermoresponsive behavior of 3D printed structures could then be determined using aqueous incubation and temperature changes (Figure 3F). The extent of this behavior was displayed by first swelling a lyophilized DLP printed starfish in a 0.25 m Na2SO4 solution containing a blue dye, and subsequently incubating it in 0.25 m Na2SO4 aqueous solution. The aqueous solution was then heated up to 65 °C, and the 4‐PLP1 3D printed starfish, underwent a significant size shrinkage of 20% after the temperature equilibrated at 65 °C from the initial temperature of 21 °C, exhibiting shape morphing capabilities (Figure 3F; Video S1, Supporting Information). Under similar conditions, the 4‐PLP2 3D printed starfish also underwent size shrinkage up to 18% (Figure S15 and Video S2, Supporting Information). These observations correlate the thermoresponsive behavior exhibited in UV/vis spectra turbidimetry measurements, verifying the modular thermoresponsive behavior of star PLPs and their shape morphing capabilities.

Conclusion

To summarise, we report the first instance of the synthesis, characterization and use of thermoresponsive star poly(L‐proline) (PLP) as 3D printable hydrogels. Through increasing the chain length; the secondary structural conformations, microstructures, and mechanical properties of polyprolines could be readily modulated. Star PLPs could be readily formulated into hydrogel resins and 3D printed using digital light processing (DLP), while generation of high‐resolution 3D objects was possible in presence of an orange G photomask. The LCST of the star polyprolines decreased with increasing chain length, while changing aqueous ionic strength could successfully reduce the PLPs LCST in solution. After photocrosslinking via DLP 3D printing, the LCST handle could be used to induce shape morphing (shrinkage) of 3D objects, demonstrating the adaptability of star PLP as a new‐age thermoresponsive material for additive manufacturing.[ 46 , 47 ]
