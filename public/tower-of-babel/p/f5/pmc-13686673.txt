Ageism Against Older Adults and Associated Factors: A Multicenter Cross‐Sectional Study Among Dental Students in Midwestern Brazil
Authors: Eunice Ellen Gontijo de Menezes, Leonardo Marchini, Oscar Rysavy, Patrick Barlow, Cláudio Rodrigues Leles, Túlio Eduardo Nogueira
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to assess the occurrence of ageism towards older adults among dental students and investigate associated factors.Material and MethodsThis cross‐sectional observational study included dental students who had completed at least one clinical academic component. Participants completed, in person, a printed self‐administered questionnaire consisting of the Brazilian version of the Ageism Scale for Dental Students (ASDS‐Braz), the Jefferson Scale of Empathy (JSE‐Br) and the Duke University Religion Index (DUREL), as well as items addressing sociodemographic characteristics, intergenerational aspects, and academic experiences. Bivariate and multivariable models were used to explore associations between independent variables and the ASDS‐Braz score. All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.1, adopting a 5% significance level.ResultsA total of 795 dental students from seven Higher Education Institutions in Goiás, Brazil, participated in the study. Most participants were female (74.3%; n = 587), with a median age of 22.0 years (Q1 = 21.0; Q3 = 25.0). Only 35.6% reported previous exposure to geriatric dentistry content or related activities. Significant differences in ageism scores were observed between students with and without prior exposure to geriatric dentistry (p < 0.01), with those who had prior exposure demonstrating lower ageism scores. Additionally, a significant negative association was found between ageism and empathy scores (p < 0.001; β = −0.10), with higher empathy associated with lower ageism.ConclusionsDental students who had previous academic exposure to geriatric dentistry or care for older adults demonstrated significantly lower ageism scores. Furthermore, higher levels of empathy were associated with fewer ageist attitudes, underscoring the need to integrate geriatric content and empathy development into dental education.Keywords: aged, ageism, dental education, dental students, geriatric dentistry

Introduction

The reduction in fertility and mortality rates, along with increased life expectancy, has contributed to a global demographic transition [1]. This shift is associated with advances in science, improved nutrition, urbanization, expanded access to healthcare services and the development of vaccines [1, 2]. As a result, health services are experiencing a significant increase in the number of geriatric patients.

Older adults often face multiple chronic conditions, polypharmacy, and cognitive decline, which can complicate the delivery of appropriate healthcare [3]. Additionally, age‐related prejudice—known as ageism—can negatively impact both the experiences and quality of care provided to this population. Coined in 1969 by Robert Butler [4], the term ‘ageism’ was later defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) in 2021 as ‘stereotypes (how we think), prejudice (how we feel), and discrimination (how we act) against people based on their age’ [5]. In the same year, the WHO published the Global Report on Ageism [5], which inspired a global campaign and action plan within the framework of the Decade of Healthy Aging (2021–2030) [6].

There is growing evidence that ageism also affects dental care, influencing both the delivery of care to older adults and how it is received [7, 8]. Previous studies suggest that ageist attitudes are linked to dentists' academic training. For instance, Swiss dental students in more advanced years of study tend to express fewer ageist attitudes [9]. Similarly, intergenerational contact appears to be a protective factor; a Greek study found that frequent interaction with older adults was associated with reduced ageist attitudes [10]. Sex has also been identified as a relevant factor, with female U.S. dental students showing lower levels of ageism [11].

To assess ageism in the dental context, researchers have developed a specific tool: the Ageism Scale for Dental Students (ASDS) [11]. This scale has undergone cross‐cultural validation and adaptation in several countries, including Greece [12], Romania [13], France [14], Serbia [15], Switzerland [16], Iran [17], India [18], Poland [19], Malaysia [20], Japan [21] and Brazil [22]. In Brazil, the ASDS was translated and culturally adapted with a sample of 156 students from the Southern region, with the Brazilian version (ASDS‐Braz) demonstrating acceptable validity and reliability [22].

Despite these efforts, findings on factors associated with ageism among dental students remain inconsistent. Moreover, many studies have employed general ageism scales that are not specific to the dental field. Even among studies that have used the ASDS, most have primarily focused on its validation and cross‐cultural adaptation, rather than on explanatory analyses. Although a few studies have examined variables such as empathy and religiosity, these factors remain relatively underexplored despite their potential relevance.

Previous research has shown that religiosity may play a relevant role in shaping attitudes towards older adults. Beyond its well‐documented association with health and well‐being, religiosity has been described as a potential contextual and individual‐level protective factor in the face of ageism. Cross‐national evidence suggests that more religious environments may buffer the negative effects of ageism on well‐being, possibly through the promotion of social support, prosocial norms, and values that emphasize respect for older individuals [23]. Moreover, a study of Greek dentists found that a stronger belief in God was associated with lower ageist attitudes, reinforcing the hypothesis that individual religiosity may be linked to more positive perceptions of older adults [10].

Brazil is characterized by significant sociocultural diversity across its regions, which may influence attitudes and behaviours towards older adults. To date, however, no studies on ageism among dental students have been conducted in the country's Midwest region, underscoring the need for regionally representative research. Understanding the problem and its associations is a pivotal step in devising strategies to combat ageism.

Therefore, this study aimed to assess the occurrence of ageism towards older adults and investigate associated factors among dental students in the metropolitan region of Goiânia, Goiás, Brazil. We hypothesized that ageism is present among dental students and may be associated with variables such as sex (with lower levels expected among female students), intergenerational contact (with frequent contact linked to lower ageism), religiosity and empathy (with higher levels associated with lower ageism), and prior exposure to geriatric dentistry content in their academic curriculum.

Material and Methods

Study Design and Sample

This was a cross‐sectional observational study conducted at dental schools in the state of Goiás, located in Midwestern Brazil. Data were collected between April and June 2023. The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [24].

The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Goiás (UFG), Brazil (approval number: 66109222.7.0000.5083). Additionally, formal authorization was obtained from all selected higher education institutions (HEIs) prior to data collection. All participants provided written informed consent.

The target population consisted of undergraduate dental students enrolled in institutions located in the metropolitan region of Goiânia, Goiás, Brazil. HEIs were selected from a list provided by the Regional Council of Dentistry of Goiás. Of the 29 identified dental programs, 22 were excluded due to reasons such as the absence of students in clinical disciplines, locations outside the metropolitan area of Goiânia, or failure to respond to the study invitation. Ultimately, seven HEIs were included—five located in Goiânia and two in neighbouring municipalities within the metropolitan region.

Inclusion criteria were as follows: being an undergraduate student enrolled in one of the selected dental programs, having completed at least one clinical discipline, and agreeing to participate in the study by signing the informed consent form. Foreign students without proficiency in Portuguese were excluded, as limited language skills could compromise their understanding of the study questionnaires.

A non‐probabilistic sampling method was used, in which all students present during visits to the selected HEIs who met the eligibility criteria were invited to participate. No formal sample size calculation was performed, and the final sample was treated as a census of eligible students.

Data Collection

Upon acceptance to participate in the study, each HEI's administration was asked to appoint a key informant to assist the researchers in planning the data collection process. This individual could be the program coordinator or another person designated by the coordination team.

During this preparatory phase, the key informant provided information such as the number of eligible students (based on the previously described criteria) and suggested strategic days and times for data collection. Data were collected in person, preferably in classroom settings; clinical outpatient settings were used only when no other feasible option was available for a given class.

Data were collected using printed, self‐administered questionnaires consisting of four sections: (1) the Brazilian version of the Ageism Scale for Dental Students (ASDS‐Braz) [22]; (2) the Brazilian student version of the Jefferson Scale of Medical Empathy (JSE‐Br) [25]; (3) items related to sociodemographic characteristics, academic background, and intergenerational interaction with older adults; and (4) the Brazilian version of the Duke University Religion Index (DUREL) [26, 27]. All questionnaires were distributed by the same researcher across all HEIs, following a standardized protocol developed by the research team. In each class, the researcher introduced herself, explained the study's objectives, and provided instructions on how to complete the questionnaires. Participants were informed that participation was voluntary and were encouraged to respond to all items.

In addition, program‐related information for each HEI was gathered. This step involved reviewing the institution's website to extract data such as program duration and the presence of mandatory and/or elective courses related to geriatric dentistry.

Pilot Study

Before data collection, a pilot study was conducted with 17 first‐year master's students enrolled in the Graduate Program in Dentistry at the Federal University of Goiás, Brazil. The average completion time was 20 min, and all questionnaires were returned fully completed.

The pilot study was used to test the application process, estimate average response time, and identify potential difficulties in understanding the questionnaires. All participants were asked to share their impressions of the questionnaire and were invited to provide written comments and suggestions. Based on their feedback, minor adjustments were made, primarily related to formatting.

Dependent Variable

Ageism was defined as the dependent variable (primary outcome) in this study. It was assessed using the Brazilian version of the Ageism Scale for Dental Students (ASDS‐Braz), originally developed by Rucker et al. (2018) [11] and cross‐culturally adapted to Brazil by Rucker et al. (2020) [22].

The ASDS‐Braz consists of 12 statements, including 3 reverse‐scored items, distributed across three domains: negative perception of older adults, positive perception of older adults, and complexity of elder care. Participants indicated their level of agreement with each item using a six‐point Likert scale: 1 = strongly disagree, 2 = disagree, 3 = slightly disagree, 4 = slightly agree, 5 = agree, and 6 = strongly agree. A total score was calculated by summing all item scores, with higher scores indicating a greater level of ageism [22].

Independent Variables

The independent variables included sociodemographic characteristics, academic background, intergenerational interaction with older adults, empathy and religiosity. Each of these variables is described below:

Sociodemographic data included age, sex, family monthly income, marital status, race/ethnicity and the primary place of residence during the participant's childhood. Academic background variables included the student's current semester in the undergraduate program and prior participation in activities related to Geriatric Dentistry or oral healthcare for older adults. Participants were also asked whether they were afraid of getting older and if they had heard of the term ‘ageism’.

Intergenerational interaction with older adults was assessed through a set of questions based on the participants' past experiences and living arrangements. These questions covered aspects such as the existence of older adults in the family, the quality of relationships with older adult family members, current or past cohabitation with older adults, previous close contact with older adults, frequency of contact, and caregiving experiences involving older adults.

Empathy was measured using the Brazilian student version of the Jefferson Scale of Medical Empathy (JSE‐Br) [25]. The scale consists of 20 items, including 10 reverse‐scored items, rated on a seven‐point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The total score is calculated by summing all item scores, yielding a range from 20 to 140, with higher scores indicating greater empathy [25]. Permission to use the scale was formally obtained from the copyright holders prior to conducting this study.

Religiosity was assessed using the Brazilian version of the Duke University Religion Index (DUREL) [26, 27], which evaluates three dimensions: organizational religious activity (ORA), non‐organizational religious activity (NORA), and intrinsic religiosity (IR), also referred to as subjective religiosity [26]. The instrument contains five items: one item each for ORA and NORA, and three items for IR. Responses are given on a five‐point Likert scale for IR and a six‐point scale for ORA and NORA. Scores range from 1 to 6 for ORA and NORA, and from 3 to 15 for IR, with higher scores indicating greater religiosity in each dimension [27].

Statistical Analyses

The ASDS‐Braz score was calculated by summing all item responses, with reverse scoring applied to negatively worded items. Non‐response to any single item resulted in an invalid total score for that participant. The JSE‐Br score was also computed by summing all items after reverse scoring where appropriate. All participants met the imputation criteria defined in the JSE‐Br scoring algorithm. The three dimensions of the DUREL were scored separately. Non‐response to any single item resulted in exclusion from the analysis of all DUREL domains. Internal consistency for each multi‐item scale was evaluated using Cronbach's alpha.

Descriptive statistics were calculated for all collected variables, including medians and interquartile ranges for continuous variables and proportions for categorical variables. Bivariate and multivariable models were used to explore associations between independent variables and the ASDS‐Braz score. Bivariate models were fit using all available data for each predictor.

To account for potential variability in scores across institutions, linear mixed‐effects models with a random intercept for school were employed in both bivariate and multivariable analyses. These models were fit using residual maximum likelihood (REML) estimation in the lme4 package (v1.1–35.5) [28]. Assumptions of normality and homoscedasticity were visually assessed using quantile‐quantile (Q–Q) plots and residuals versus fitted values plots. Coefficient significance was evaluated using t‐tests with Satterthwaite estimated degrees of freedom, implemented via the lmerTest package (v3.1–3) [29].

All analyses were performed in R, version 4.4.1 [30]. A significance level of 5% was adopted for all inferential tests.

Discussion

This study assessed the occurrence of ageism towards older adults among dental students and explored potential associated factors. Our findings indicate a significant association between empathy and ageism, suggesting that students with higher empathy scores exhibited lower ageism scores. Additionally, students who reported prior participation in activities related to Geriatric Dentistry or the dental care of older adults showed significantly lower ageism scores than those without such experience.

These results are consistent with a study conducted in France involving 284 participants, including students and their families, which found that higher levels of empathy were significantly associated with reduced negative prejudice towards older adults [31]. A study conducted in Brazil with dental students also found a significant association between empathy and ageism, indicating that higher levels of empathy are associated with lower levels of ageism [32]. Empathy, recognized as a key element in building human connections and understanding others' experiences, plays a pivotal role in mitigating ageist attitudes [33]. In clinical settings, empathy contributes to the delivery of quality care for older patients, supporting thorough history taking and fostering a compassionate, responsive approach [34].

In our study, only one of the seven participating HEIs offered a formal Geriatric Dentistry component, and only 35.6% of students reported prior participation in an academic activity related to geriatric dentistry or oral healthcare for older adults. Furthermore, when asked about their prior knowledge of the term ageism, 84.1% of participants reported being unfamiliar with it. A lack of knowledge about aging and related issues has been strongly associated with more ageist attitudes, which is particularly concerning given the global trend of population aging and its implications for healthcare systems [35, 36]. Universities, which play a critical role in shaping future healthcare professionals, often fail to adequately address issues related to aging and ageism in their curricula [37, 38]. Our finding that students with prior exposure to geriatric content demonstrated lower levels of ageism underscores the importance of integrating such content into dental education to better prepare professionals for the care of an aging population.

No association was found between sex and ageism scores in this study, in contrast to previous research suggesting that female students are generally less ageist [10, 11]. A possible explanation for this discrepancy lies in the sample composition, which was predominantly female (over 73%), potentially limiting the variability necessary to detect differences by sex.

Religiosity has been linked to higher levels of empathy and positive attitudes towards older adults in several studies [39, 40, 41]. Some religious traditions have been empirically associated with more positive perceptions of older adults, particularly regarding their social and personal value, as higher levels of religiosity and specific religious practices have been linked to more positive attitudes towards older people [42]. A study of Greek dentists found that stronger belief in God was associated with lower ageist attitudes [10]. However, our study did not find an association between religiosity and ageism scores. This discrepancy may be partly explained by differences in study populations and measurement instruments, as the previous study evaluated practicing dentists and employed a different religiosity measure, limiting direct comparison.

Beyond identifying the occurrence and associated factors of ageism, it is essential to develop strategies to combat it. Although ageism has long been ingrained in society, the COVID‐19 pandemic brought renewed attention to this issue [43]. During this period, public policies aimed at addressing ageism were strengthened, particularly in response to extreme forms of ageism such as senicide, defined as the murder or abandonment to death of older adults [44]. Another front of the battle was the rationing of healthcare services for older individuals, a topic that gained prominence during the pandemic [45]. In dentistry, the shortage of specialists in Geriatric Dentistry and the limited presence of related coursework in undergraduate programs can also be viewed as expressions of structural ageism that must be addressed.

Multiple strategies have been proposed to combat ageism. Legislative action to protect older adults is one important approach. Additionally, educational interventions, particularly those that increase knowledge about aging, have proven effective in reducing ageist attitudes [35, 46]. Academic institutions have been identified as ideal settings for such interventions [47]. Intergenerational contact, especially in‐person interactions with older adults, has also demonstrated positive outcomes [47]. Burnes et al. [48] found that combining intergenerational contact with educational content on aging yields the strongest effects in reducing ageism.

While this study benefited from several methodological strengths, including a large sample size and the use of a standardized data collection using validated scales and questionnaires, it also presents limitations. The cross‐sectional design precludes causal inference, and the regional focus (Midwestern Brazil) limits generalizability. Given that ageism seems to be shaped by cultural and contextual factors, future research should explore the phenomenon in other regions of Brazil to broaden the evidence base. Additionally, intervention studies are needed to evaluate the effectiveness of specific educational and experiential strategies aimed at reducing ageism among dental students. Qualitative research is also recommended, as ageism is a cross‐cultural phenomenon. In‐depth studies could better explore how cultural background, religiosity, empathy and intergenerational experiences shape age‐related attitudes and help inform more comprehensive strategies to address ageism.

Our findings carry important implications for dental education and clinical practice. They highlight the urgent need to incorporate Geriatric Dentistry components into undergraduate curricula and to adopt more comprehensive educational approaches that promote not only technical proficiency but also empathy, ethical values and interpersonal skills.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study identified that dental students who had previously participated in academic activities related to Geriatric Dentistry or the care of older adults exhibited significantly lower ageism scores. Additionally, higher levels of empathy were associated with a reduction in ageism scores.
