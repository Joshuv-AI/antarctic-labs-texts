Assessment of knowledge, attitude, and practices of Lebanese diabetic patients and their relation to medication adherence and doctor-patient communication: A cross-sectional study
Authors: Hala Fahs, Sara Mansour, Diana Dakroub, Jana Hammoud, Aya Ibrahim, Aya Rida, Nada M Kassem, Sami El Khatib, Zeina Shrayteh, Sahar Obeid, Samar Younes, Mohamed Rahal, Samer Sakr, Souheil Hallit, Diana Malaeb, Hassan Hosseini
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetes mellitus, one of the major public health concerns worldwide, is a non-communicable metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia and associated with micro- and macro-vascular complications. This study aimed to assess knowledge, attitudes, and practices, as well as associated factors (medication adherence and doctor-patient communication), among Lebanese patients with diabetes.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among Lebanese patients from February to August 2021. The questionnaire included the Knowledge, Attitude, and Practice (KAP), the Lebanese Medication Adherence Scale (LMAS-14), and the Doctor-Patient Communication (DPC).ResultsA total of 125 patients with diabetes were enrolled. Linear regression models revealed that participants with higher doctor-patient communication scores had significantly higher knowledge scores in the unadjusted analysis (B = 0.055, p = 0.030). This association weakened after adjusting for socio-economic covariates, including gender, monthly income, and work status (p=0.078). No significant association was found between attitude/practice scores on diabetes and doctor-patient communication scores, in both unadjusted and adjusted analyses.ConclusionAlthough better doctor–patient communication was associated with higher knowledge in unadjusted analyses, this association was not statistically significant after adjustment. Our study found that positive attitudes and practices significantly correlated with better medication adherence. These findings emphasize the need for improved health literacy and patient education programs.Keywords: diabetes mellitus, medication adherence, knowledge, attitude, practices, communication, Lebanon

Background

Diabetes Mellitus (DM), a prevalent global public health concern, manifests as a non-communicable metabolic disorder characterized by hyperglycemia and various complications. Global estimates in 2019 indicated a prevalence ranging from 4% to 10.8%, influenced by factors like residence area and socioeconomic status. Effective management of diabetes involves crucial components, including tailored medication regimens based on glycemic levels and the adoption of healthy lifestyle behaviors such as a balanced diet, regular physical activity, and smoking cessation. Hence, achieving optimal glycemic control is imperative as it correlates with a reduced risk of complications, helps prevent hyperglycemic crises, improves quality of life, and reduces mortality.1–4

The therapeutic management of diabetes necessitates a comprehensive approach encompassing heightened patient awareness regarding medication adherence, diligent self-care practices, and regular monitoring. Hence, patients must possess a thorough understanding of various facets intrinsic to diabetes, encompassing the judicious implementation of non-pharmacological measures, strict adherence to medication guidelines, and compliance with established disease management protocols. These recommendations highlight the importance of patient engagement in achieving optimal therapeutic outcomes. 5

Enhancing patient education and awareness correlates with improved disease control that mitigates the progression of extensive detrimental consequences, preventing the development of both microvascular and macrovascular complications. 6 Effective diabetes management extends beyond professional consultations, necessitating active patient involvement to ensure comprehensive knowledge. Therefore, a thorough evaluation of the knowledge, attitude, and practices (KAP) of diabetic patients is imperative, with a focus on identifying areas requiring additional counseling.7,8

Knowledge, attitudes, and practices

Based on studies conducted in Western Nepal 9 and India, 10 diabetic patients exhibited adequate knowledge, insufficient attitude, and suboptimal practices concerning diabetes. Additionally, a study conducted in Saudi Arabia on 424 participants 11 found that 51.2% had insufficient knowledge, 7.8% showed negative attitudes, and 24.8% had negative practice levels. 11 The study also indicated that participants with higher education levels had better knowledge and attitudes compared to those without degrees. 11 Conversely, participants without degrees tended to demonstrate better practices. Hence, attending educational programs on diabetes was moderately linked to higher practice scores. 12 According to a study conducted in China, no significant association was found between patients’ knowledge and attitudes. 13 However, better knowledge and attitudes were associated with healthier diets and increased physical activity. Furthermore, research in Southern Benin 14 showed that approximately 53% of patients had good knowledge, 52% had a positive attitude, and 47% exhibited good practices towards diabetes. Factors such as being female, married, educated, employed, and having a longer duration of diabetes were significantly associated with good knowledge. Being married, having a longer duration of diabetes, and possessing good knowledge were associated with positive attitudes, while being educated, having a longer duration of diabetes, and having good knowledge were associated with good practices. 14

Therefore, this lack of awareness is not just an academic issue; it directly influences patients’ self-management practices, which in turn affects their glycemic control. 15 Poor self-management behaviors, including sedentary lifestyles, noncompliance with treatment plans, and tobacco use, are identified as significant factors contributing to poorly controlled diabetes among a considerable number of individuals. 16

Patient-provider communication

The relationship between physicians and patients is crucial in diabetes management. Therapeutic communication involves the exchange of information among healthcare providers, patients, and caregivers, aimed at fostering a relationship that promotes the individuals’ well-being. 17 Therefore, effective communication between healthcare providers and patients are essential for optimal disease management, treatment adherence, patient satisfaction, improved knowledge, and better health practices. 18 Conversely, poor communication hampers the delivery of standardized care. Furthermore, effective therapeutic communication skills help assess patients’ needs, provide appropriate physical care, support emotional stability, and allow for information exchange. 19 However, evidence suggests that this critical aspect of healthcare delivery may be compromised. 20 For example, a study in India revealed that many physicians have limited time for patients, potentially leading to the neglect of comprehensive assessments for complications. 21 This gap in physician-patient communication underscores the need to investigate how the quality of communication with healthcare providers influences self-management behaviors among diabetic patients.

Medication adherence

Another critical consideration that strongly influences therapeutic outcomes is medication adherence among diabetic patients. 22 Collaboration between patients and healthcare providers plays an essential role in enhancing medication adherence. 23 Research indicates that increasing disease knowledge correlates with greater adherence to medications. 24 Thus, educating patients about the importance of adhering to anti-diabetic medications empowers them to comply with prescribed therapies, recognize disease symptoms, and effectively manage them at home. In contrast, low disease literacy is linked to poor treatment outcomes and increased therapy costs. 25

The present study

Research exploring diabetes-related knowledge, attitude, and practices in the Middle East, particularly Lebanon, is scarce, noting that recent data showed that the prevalence of type II diabetes in Lebanon is approximately 15% 26 This study was guided by the Knowledge–Attitude–Practice (KAP) framework, which suggests that patients’ knowledge of their disease may influence their attitudes and practices toward disease management. In the present study, knowledge and attitude/practice were examined as outcome variables, while doctor–patient communication and medication adherence were assessed as associated factors.27,28

In the context of diabetes management, adequate awareness about disease complications and treatment benefits is highly associated with optimal glycemic control. 29 Furthermore, doctor–patient communication exerts a crucial role, as effective communication is fundamental in ensuring that diabetic patients are informed about disease complications and promotes adherence to medication regimens. 30 In this study, doctor–patient communication and medication adherence were examined as factors associated with diabetes-related knowledge and attitude/practice scores. Various studies were conducted in Lebanon among diabetic patients, which assessed the effectiveness of the collaborative practice between community pharmacists and physicians in diabetes management and evaluated the clinical implications of statins on blood glucose levels among hospitalized patients.31,32 Furthermore, other studies were conducted among diabetic patients to investigate factors associated with quality of life and assess factors contributing to diabetic-related distress.33,34 To our knowledge, there is a dearth of studies evaluating the associations among knowledge, attitudes, and practices, medication adherence, and doctor-patient communication among Lebanese diabetic patients. This study aims to thoroughly examine the knowledge, attitude, and practices of diabetic patients in Lebanon, exploring their relationship with Doctor-Patient Communication and Medication Adherence.

Methods

Study design and procedure

This cross-sectional study was conducted between February and August 2021. A snowball sampling technique was used to enroll diabetic patients from Lebanese governorates (Beirut, Mount Lebanon, North, South, and Beqaa). This non-probability sampling method was chosen due to COVID-19 restrictions; however, it may have introduced selection bias, as participation depended on social media access and network sharing. The questionnaire was created on Google Forms and was shared on social media (WhatsApp and Facebook) for self-administration in accordance with the COVID-19 lockdown imposed by the Lebanese government. The study link was circulated and sent to patients with diabetes. Diabetic patients (type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus) 18 years and above were considered eligible for enrollment in the study. Excluded were women with a history of gestational diabetes, patients with functional disorders of the thyroid or the adrenal glands, and patients on psychoactive medications, thyroid hormones, and corticosteroids.

Sample size calculation

The Epi Info software was used to calculate the required minimal sample size. Considering the diabetes prevalence of 7.9% in Lebanon, a minimum sample size of 112 patients was required to allow for a study power of 80% and a confidence interval of 95%.

Ethical aspects

The study protocol was approved by the Research Committee at the Lebanese International University (2020RC-002-LIUSOP). Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained from all participants before filling out the questionnaire. Privacy and confidentiality of participants were maintained as personal identifiers were not tracked.

Questionnaire

The questionnaire was written and distributed in Arabic and English, so as to reach as many Lebanese people as possible. It was made up of four sections that required 25 minutes to complete. The initial section of the survey included the socio-demographic including age, gender, marital status (single, married, divorced, or widowed), employment status (student, employed, unemployed, or retired), work type (medical versus non-medical), governorates and living region (rural versus urban), educational level (primary or less, intermediate, secondary, or university), family income, smoking and alcohol status, type of diabetes mellitus, and past medication history. The next section encompassed the scale that assessed knowledge, attitude, and practices towards diabetes, which is adapted from another study. 35 The questionnaire was self-administered with a total of 25 questions: 18 items for knowledge, 4 for attitude, and 3 for practice. Each correct answer was given a score of ‘one’, and the incorrect given a score of ‘zero’. Attitude and practices (A-P) were assessed using a score of 7 items, where the correct answer was coded by “1” and the incorrect answer was coded by “0”. The total AP score was 7. The internal consistency of the knowledge scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.60) and the attitude/practice scale (Cronbach’s α = 0.59) was moderate. These values may be explained by the limited number of items and the dichotomous (binary) scoring format used in the questionnaire. Therefore, results related to these scales should be interpreted with caution. The third section involved the Lebanese Medication Adherence Scale-14 (LMAS-14), which is used to evaluate medication adherence. This scale covers the occupational, psychological, annoyance, and economic domains; it was previously validated among Lebanese patients with hypertension and hypothyroidism8,36 (e.g., “Do you stop taking your medication without consulting your doctor if you feel better during the treatment period?”). The questionnaire included 14 items using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4, where a lower score indicates high adherence to treatment. The Cronbach’s alpha for the whole scale was 0.91 in this study.

The fourth section included Doctor-Patient Communication (DPC), 15 items scored on a Likert scale from 1 to 4 (1=No, 2= Possibly No, 3= Possibly Yes, and 4=Yes 7 (e.g., “Did the doctor involve you in the decision-making?”; “Do you feel you were given all the necessary information?”). Higher scores indicate better communication between the patient and the doctor (Cronbach’s alpha in this study = 0.98).

Translation procedure

The forward and backward translation methods were applied to different scales. The English version was translated into Arabic by a Lebanese translator who was completely unrelated to the study. Afterward, a Lebanese psychologist with full working proficiency in English translated the Arabic version back to English. The initial English version and the second English version were compared to detect and later eliminate any inconsistencies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical data analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences software (SPSS, version 25.0). Continuous variables were presented as mean and standard deviation, and 95% confidence interval. Categorical and ordinal variables were shown as frequencies (n) and percentages (%). Knowledge, attitude, and practice scores were considered dependent variables, while sociodemographic variables, medication adherence, and doctor–patient communication were treated as independent variables. Multivariable linear regression analyses were performed to identify factors associated with knowledge and attitude/practice scores. Variables with a p-value < 0.20 in the bivariate analysis were considered for inclusion in the multivariable models to reduce potential confounding. A stepwise regression approach was used to explore significant predictors given the relatively limited sample size. However, as stepwise methods are data-driven and may increase the risk of model instability, the results should be interpreted with caution. Basic model assumptions, including linearity, normality of residuals, and absence of multicollinearity, were assessed prior to analysis. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Discussion

Our study aimed to thoroughly examine the knowledge, attitude, and practices of diabetic patients in Lebanon, exploring their relationship with doctor-patient communication and adherence to medications.

Doctor-patient communication and knowledge

Although doctor–patient communication was associated with higher knowledge scores in the unadjusted analysis, this association did not remain statistically significant after adjusting for socio-demographic factors. This suggests that the observed relationship may be influenced by confounding variables such as income, gender, and employment status. Therefore, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and no definitive conclusion can be drawn regarding an independent association between doctor–patient communication and knowledge. Previous research has reported positive associations among knowledge, positive health outcomes, and effective patient–physician communication across various health conditions.37–45 While our unadjusted findings were consistent with this evidence, the lack of statistical significance after adjustment suggests that this relationship may not be independent and warrants further investigation. This attenuation of the association after adjustment suggests that socio-demographic factors such as income, gender, and employment status may play a more important role in determining patients’ knowledge levels than communication alone. Ensuring effective physicians’ communication skills and maintaining a professional relationship with the patients promotes patients’ knowledge about the treatment. Patient education by healthcare professionals on medication regimen and behavior towards the disease is essential to improve adherence and achieve a controlled level of HbA1c. Furthermore, health care professionals should be approachable, listen to patients’ concerns, inform patients about the cause of the disease, and manage side effects.46,47

Moreover, several previous studies have investigated the use of suitable medical explanations in conversation.48,49 Consequently, the medical terms utilized in patients’ referral letters can impact clinical communications, an aspect often overlooked by healthcare professionals. 50 Indeed, patients may interpret medical terminology differently, especially if they lack a full understanding, which is crucial for healthcare providers working with individuals from diverse scientific or cultural backgrounds. Therefore, healthcare professionals need to take the time to explain information clearly, allow patients adequate time to comprehend it, and encourage them to ask questions to enhance their knowledge about the disease. 50

Additionally, poor communication or unclear advice hinders patients’ understanding of their disease and thereby affects medical adherence. 51 Research has shown that ineffective communication can result in underestimating the severity of a patient’s illness and improper management of health-related risk factors. 52 Consequently, healthcare professionals should offer options during clinical decision-making processes. While there may be a clear preferred option from a healthcare professional’s perspective, it is crucial to explain to the patient why this option is recommended rather than simply expecting them to accept it.

The person-centered model of care is widely implemented in clinical settings.53–55 This approach emphasizes that healthcare providers should inform and empower patients to actively participate in managing their healthcare, rather than dictating what patients should do. Numerous studies have shown that person-centered care enhances patient autonomy, encourages patient involvement in healthcare decisions, and fosters increased interactions between patients and healthcare professionals.53,56 Given the documented benefits of person-centered care in managing chronic conditions, it is important to consider patients’ day-to-day experiences, knowledge, and actions over time as they manage their health. This approach aims to strengthen and enhance patients’ control over their health outcomes. Lastly, facilitating shared knowledge and decision-making processes are essential for promoting patient-centered care 53 and improving the patient-provider relationship. This study found a positive association between doctor-patient communication and knowledge, but not practice, since the communication mainly functions at the information transmission and cognitive levels, which is theoretically expected to influence knowledge only. The lack of significant association between doctor–patient communication and attitude/practice scores may indicate that communication primarily influences knowledge rather than behavioral implementation. Translating knowledge into practice may depend on additional factors such as economic constraints, access to medications, and healthcare system limitations. In the Lebanese context, ongoing economic challenges, limited access to medications, and constraints within the healthcare system may further hinder patients’ ability to translate knowledge into effective practices. These structural barriers may explain why improved communication does not necessarily result in better self-management behaviors.

Knowledge, attitudes, and practices

In our study, the knowledge, attitude, and practice scores are higher than a study conducted in Saudi Arabia (2016) 57 but lower than a study in Tehran (2018). 58 Our findings could be helpful to establish structured health education in the primary healthcare system in Lebanon. To avoid overloaded information, recent evidence has suggested that structured education, such as attending a structured course, could be efficient for patients with diabetes. 59

Clinical implications

Our research underscores the importance of the Lebanese Health Service and other pertinent stakeholders enhancing patient quality of life through effective therapeutic communication. For instance, hospital administrators should arrange ongoing training sessions for their personnel to familiarize them with commonly used medical terminology when interacting with patients. Furthermore, Lebanese doctors and healthcare providers must allocate sufficient time to educate, screen, and treat diabetic patients appropriately. It’s crucial that they tailor their communication to cultural backgrounds and educational levels and involve patients in decision-making to foster a collaborative treatment approach.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships between variables. Second, the use of snowball sampling through social media may have introduced selection bias, as individuals with internet access, higher digital literacy, or stronger social networks were more likely to participate. This may have resulted in the overrepresentation of certain groups, such as younger or more educated individuals, thereby limiting the generalizability of the findings to the broader Lebanese diabetic population, particularly those from underserved or rural areas. Third, the reliance on self-reported data, including clinical and behavioral variables, may be subject to recall and reporting bias. Fourth, the relatively small sample size in relation to the number of predictors included in the regression models may increase the risk of overfitting and limit the robustness of the findings. Finally, the internal consistency of the knowledge and attitude/practice scales was relatively low, which may have affected the reliability of these measures. Therefore, the results should be interpreted with caution, and further studies with larger, more representative samples and more robust measurement tools are recommended.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides important insights into the knowledge, attitude, and practices of Lebanese patients with diabetes and highlights the role of medication adherence and doctor–patient communication in diabetes management. While doctor–patient communication was associated with higher knowledge in unadjusted analyses, this relationship was not statistically significant after adjustment, suggesting that socio-demographic factors may play a more prominent role. Notably, better attitude and practice scores were significantly associated with improved medication adherence, underscoring the importance of promoting positive health behaviors. These findings emphasize the need for targeted patient education and structured interventions to enhance diabetes self-management. Future large-scale studies are warranted to further explore these relationships and inform effective healthcare strategies.
