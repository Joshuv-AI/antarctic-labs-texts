Adaptation in the Avian Middle Ear—The Columellar Annular Ligament in Aquatic and Diving Birds
Authors: John Peacock
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe effective detection of environmental vibrations such as sound waves depends on the transmission of tympanic membrane motion through the middle ear to the inner ear hair cells. In birds, the bony element of the middle ear is the columella; its distal end joins the cartilaginous extracolumella and tympanic membrane, while its basal expansion (the footplate) interfaces with inner ear fluid at the oval window, where it is held in place by the stapedial (columellar) annular ligament. Variation in footplate and oval window geometry can alter the annular ligament's size and shape, thereby influencing middle ear mechanics. Previous studies have noted relatively small footplates in aquatic birds as compared to their terrestrial relatives, and suggested that the adaptive significance of these may relate to their influence on the relative size of the annular ligament. Here, I examine a taxonomically and ecologically broad sample of bird species to test the hypothesis that aquatic lineages have convergently evolved proportionally larger annular ligaments. Results show larger ligaments are characteristic of aquatic, and particularly diving species, while narrower ligaments occur in high‐frequency specialists. These patterns are polyphyletic and, alongside their strong ecological associations, indicate repeated functional adaptation of the middle ear. Larger ligaments are consistent with reduced system stiffness and enhanced low‐frequency transmission, a hypothesis which is plausible for pelagic seabirds. In contrast, the extreme ligament enlargement in diving taxa is unlikely to be related to hearing, and instead may play a role in protection from barotrauma.

Introduction

The detection of environmental vibrations, including sound waves, provides an important survival advantage to many species. Vertebrates achieve this by having specialised cells that respond to the movement of fluid within the inner ear (Manley 1990). In birds, the reception of sound is facilitated by having a middle ear: a system which consists of a tympanic membrane, cartilaginous extracolumella, bony columella, one muscle and a variety of ligaments. Sound waves cause mechanical motion of the middle ear components which are then converted to pressure waves in the inner ear fluid. The part of the middle ear interfacing the inner ear fluid is the columellar footplate, which is seated in an opening called the oval window, and held in position by a ring of elastic, fibrous tissue called the columellar (or stapedial) annular ligament. This ligament provides a boundary that seals the inner ear while permitting relative footplate motion (see Smith 1904; Starck 1995; Wood et al. 2010; Peacock et al. 2024 for further details of avian ear anatomy).

The properties of the annular ligament are known to play a key role in determining middle ear response (Muyshondt et al. 2016; Jenkins et al. 2025), and the ligament's relative size will be of importance. Enlarging the ligament would reduce the stiffness of the system, which would tend to improve low‐frequency sound transmission and hinder high‐frequency transmission (Lynch et al. 1982), whereas a relatively smaller ligament would limit maximum footplate displacement, stiffening the system and potentially benefitting higher frequency sound transmission. Increased flexibility may also further modify sound transmission by encouraging the footplate to vibrate in different modes, as has been suggested for several bathygerid and spalacid mole‐rats as well as the murid Gerbillurus, all of which appear to have relatively large ligaments (Mason et al. 2010, 2016; Mason 2016a; Peacock et al. 2026). Alternatively, many animals regularly experience large static pressure variations from flying or diving (see do Val 2024). Changes in annular ligament stiffness could impact the ability of the middle ear system to provide protection from pressure variations experienced when diving, or impulsive pressure changes experienced by plunge divers. Taken together, these factors makes the annular ligament's size a plausible target for ecological selection.

Previous studies examining morphological disparity in the avian middle ear have noted ecological signal in the relative area of the footplate (Peacock et al. 2024), with this appearing smaller in aquatic species. The functional significance of changes to relative footplate area are normally discussed in terms of the area ratio: the ratio of tympanic membrane to footplate area that is considered a fundamental concept in understanding middle ear function (Mason 2016b). However, based on qualitative observations, Peacock et al. (2024) suggested the alternative explanation: that the reduced footplate area was not accompanied by a reduction in the oval window area, resulting in great variation in the relative area of the annular ligament. This hypothesis here is that the adaptive benefit to modification of columellar footplate area lies in how this alters the annular ligament, and how this in turn impacts middle ear function.

In this study, I directly examine the hypothesis that different avian lineages have convergently evolved proportionally larger annular ligaments in association with their ecological niche (namely aquatic lineages). I report measurements of oval window and footplate area in a taxonomically and ecologically diverse sample of birds, examine how these scale with columellar length, and explore how they differ in relative area. I demonstrate clear differences in the ratio of oval window to footplate area between different ecological niches and clades. In particular, I show that aquatic birds have proportionally larger ligaments than terrestrial species, that these are largest among diving species, and that species with proportionally large ligaments show a polyphyletic distribution. I discuss these results in terms of phylogeny and ecology, and suggest some biomechanical hypotheses for future experimental tests.

Methods

I measured the middle ear from μCT scans of 129 bird species collected entirely from MorphoSource (www.morphosource.org), and processed them using ImageJ (Schindelin et al. 2012) and 3D Slicer (Kikinis et al. 2013). Then, following digital segmentation, I made linear measurements of the length of the columella, and the area of both the footplate and oval window. Prior to analysis, I log‐transformed all variables.

To examine ecological patterns, I classified species into 17 foraging categories (derived from Pigot et al. 2020) and further collapsed these into three broad categories: diving species (which fully submerge themselves underwater, n = 36), non‐diving aquatic species (which feed, hunt, or spend significant amounts of time in aquatic environments, n = 22), and terrestrial species (n = 71).

To account for phylogenetic non‐independence, I performed phylogenetic generalised least squares regression analysis (PGLS) using the Caper package in R (Orme 2013), with a maximum likelihood estimate of Pagel's λ (Pagel 1999). The time‐scaled phylogenetic topology used for the PGLS analyses follow (Cooney et al. 2017) and (Prum et al. 2015) as performed by (Burton et al. 2023). For each response variable (footplate area and oval window area), I first fit a full PGLS model including the interaction between columellar length and ecological niche, and then compare this to a reduced model without the interaction when appropriate. To test whether the oval window‐to‐footplate area ratio (OW/FP ratio) varies among ecological niches, I used a Kruskal–Wallis test followed by pairwise Wilcoxon rank‐sum tests.

Results

There are very conspicuous differences in the relative area of the footplate and oval window between species. The footplate fits snugly within the oval window in the ground‐hunting invertivore Bubo africanus (Figure 1A,B), while a larger gap is clear in the small aerial insectivore Hirundo rustica (Figure 1B,D), and the footplate appears considerably smaller, and must be supported by a proportionally larger ligament, in the demersal/benthic pursuit diver Leucocarbo georgianus (Figure 1E,F).

The regression analysis reveals a strong and significant correlation between columellar length and the areas of both the footplate (Figure 2A) and the oval window (Figure 2B). The correlations found when performing the analyses with only diving birds are notably weaker and less significant than with only terrestrial species, for non‐diving aquatic species, or for all birds. A phylogenetic ANCOVA shows that footplate area increases strongly with columellar length (F₁,₁₂₃ = 420.47, p < 0.0001; λ = 0.90), but that ecological niche also has a significant effect (F₂,₁₂₃ = 9.02, p = 0.0002), and the columellar length × niche interaction is significant (F₂,₁₂₁ = 4.23, p = 0.0167). This indicates that both footplate area magnitude and the rate at which it scales with columellar length differ among niches. For oval window area, columellar length has a strong effect (F₁,₁₂₃ = 556.2, p < 0.0001; λ = 0.74), but the niche main effect is not significant (F₂,₁₂₃ = 0.225, p = 0.799), indicating no consistent differences in oval window area among niches at a given columellar length. However, the columellar length × niche interaction is highly significant (F₂,₁₂₁ = 15.26, p < 0.0001), indicating that the allometric slope varies among ecological groups. Thus, footplate area varies among niches in both intercept and slope, whereas oval window area varies among niches only in slope. However, these differing slopes imply that niche‐related differences in oval window area would only emerge outside the columellar length range found in my dataset, which covers the full range found in nature.

A small number of taxa depart from these trends. Megaceryle maxima (Giant Kingfisher) is a positive outlier among divers for footplate area, while Phaethon rubricauda (Red‐tailed Tropicbird) exhibits unusually small footplate and oval window areas relative to columellar length (the smallest in relative size for both measurements).

Comparing the absolute area of the annular ligament (the difference between oval window and footplate area) to columellar length shows a more variable relationship (Figure 2C). While correlations are much weaker than for footplate or oval window area alone, all are still statistically significant.

There is a very strong correlation between the area of the footplate and the area of the oval window across all birds, as well as across only terrestrial and non‐diving aquatic species (Figure 2D). However, the correlation is notably weaker when examining only diving birds, indicating more variation within this group. Separation of the three groups is visually apparent in Figure 2D where most diving birds are clustered with lower y‐axis values, terrestrial species have larger values, and non‐diving aquatic species are intermediate.

The ratio of oval window to footplate area (OW/FP ratio) is a measurement of the relative area of the annular ligament: a larger OW/FP ratio means a relatively larger ligament and a smaller ratio is a relatively smaller ligament. Upon dividing the sample into three groups based on their ecological niche, clear differences emerge in the distribution of OW/FP ratio values (Figure 3A). The median ratio across all species is 1.83: it is 1.50 for terrestrial species, 1.77 for non‐diving aquatic species, and 2.68 for diving species. A Kruskal‐Wallis test and pairwise Wilcoxon rank sum tests reveal significant differences between the three groups (p = 0.0003 for Non‐diving Aquatic vs. Terrestrial and <0.0001 for all others). The results from the PGLS regression analysis suggest no relationship between OW/FP ratio and columellar length (Figure 3B).

A larger OW/FP ratio is observable in various groups of aquatic species when compared to terrestrial ones (Figure 4). They are noticeably elevated in aquatic generalist and aquatic surface species, but even more so in those classified as aquatic aerial (which includes diving petrels and storm petrels as well as non‐diving skuas), aquatic plunge, and aquatic dive. The latter group has the highest median value of all. The aquatic groups which do not appear to have elevated values are the aquatic ground and aquatic perch groups. The latter contains two species: the diving alcedinid Megaceryle maxima and the non‐diving accipitrid Haliaeetus vocifer (African Fish Eagle), both have low OW/FP ratio values close to the mean for all terrestrial species, and Megaceryle maxima has the lowest OW/FP ratio of all diving species as described earlier. Other outlying points among the aquatic groups include Leucocarbo georgianus with the largest OW/FP ratio overall.

Among the terrestrial species, some niches have positive outliers. Among omnivores the biggest outlier is the shorebird Chionis minor (Black‐faced Sheathbill) followed by Struthio camelus (Common Ostrich). Also of note is the sole scavenger examined, Gyps coprotheres (Cape Vulture), which has a OW/FP ratio close to the median value for all aquatic diving species.

Clear differences are also visible in the OW/FP ratio when species are grouped by the clade to which each belongs (Figure 5). The largest OW/FP ratios are found in the diving Suliformes and Sphenisciformes, with other clades such as Anseriformes, Charadriiformes, Phaethontiformes and Procellariformes also appearing with noticeably elevated values. Meanwhile, some other clades appear to have low OW/FP ratio values, namely the Strigiformes.

A few terrestrial clades have OW/FP ratios that are notably elevated compared to other terrestrial taxa. This includes the Accipitriformes, Bucerotiformes and Falconiformes. The Accipitriformes are also notable for having one positive outlier in the vulture Gyps coprotheres.

Discussion

The results show significant differences in the relative area of the annular ligament across birds, including that larger ligaments appear characteristic of many aquatic species. This finding supports earlier hypotheses (Peacock et al. 2024). More specifically, the patterns shown in Figure 4 indicate ecological signal in the annular ligament's relative area, with this being particularly large in aquatic species in general, and even more so in diving species. In addition, the patterns shown in Figure 5 indicate that enlarged ligaments are likely convergent in multiple clades. Overall, the results suggest that the trait evolved independently multiple times in association with ecological niche, implying that the trait carries adaptive significance. Furthermore, there are reasons to expect that changes in the ligament's size would impact the mechanical behaviour of the middle ear system.

Statistical analysis further suggests significant interactions between columellar length and ecological niche for both footplate area and oval window area, indicating that the scaling relationships differ among ecological groups. However, while the footplate area showed strong and consistent ecological divergence across the whole sample, the effect in the oval window area was size‐dependent and not significant in the examined columellar size range. Thus, evolutionary changes in annular ligament area appear to reflect modification of footplate area rather than oval window area.

Functional Significance

Adaptation towards lower frequency, even infrasonic hearing has been suggested for pelagic seabirds as an aid to navigation (Zeyl et al. 2020; Patrick et al. 2021; Thorne 2023) and the increased relative ligament areas seen here across pelagic birds aligns with this notion. Further evidence that this could be an adaptation for hearing ability comes from the opposite direction: narrow ligaments would cause a reduction in hearing sensitivity, particularly at low‐frequencies, but may result in improvements to high‐frequency sound transmission. Strigiformes, most notably the tytonids, are well known for their acute hearing ability (Konishi 1973), and their OW/FP ratio is the smallest average of all clades in the sample, which would result in them having the stiffest middle ear (Figure 5). This is consistent with mechanical measurements in tytonids showing that the barn owl has a much lower group delay than other birds (Peacock et al. 2025). The lower OW/FP ratio is considerably lower than their nearest relatives, the Accipitriformes, from which they likely diverged in the middle Eocene (Cho et al. 2019), and may be hypothesised as an adaptive trait to help facilitate their well‐known high‐frequency hearing.

Tyto alba (Common Barn Owl) has the most sensitive hearing of any measured vertebrate, and can detect relatively high frequencies (Dooling et al. 2000; Dyson et al. 1998). However it does not have the smallest OW/FP ratio. The smallest value is found in another tytonid, Phodilus badius (Oriental Bay Owl), which is unknown in terms of hearing ability. Owls had three of the five smallest OW/FP ratios, this means that other species are comparable. These included the nocturnal Steatornis caripensis (Oilbird) but also others for which we might not immediately expect any high‐frequency specialisation.

Adaptation for low‐frequency hearing does appear the most likely adaptive explanation for the increased OW/FP ratios in the non‐diving or shallow‐diving pelagic species. However, it does not explain the greatly enlarged ligaments in coastal diving birds such as cormorants, which have some of the largest OW/FP ratios of all. An immediate thought might be that this is related to underwater hearing. Most birds are assumed to be visually guided while hunting underwater (Machovsky‐Capuska et al. 2012; Darby et al. 2022; and see commentary by Cronin 2012), but the extent to which birds may make use of underwater sound is underexplored. Some evidence had suggested poor eyesight and superior underwater hearing in cormorants (White et al. 2007; Larsen et al. 2020), and other studies have noted that penguins do vocalise while diving (Thiebault et al. 2019). However, the mechanism by which an enlarged annular ligament would facilitate underwater hearing is unclear. A columella that is much more loosely connected to the skull could, theoretically, be useful for a form of inertial hearing, however the small size and low mass of the columella makes this highly unlikely. It may alternatively allow the columella to vibrate in other ways in response to skull vibrations. Overall, an explanation in terms of hearing ability seems unlikely, and an explanation in terms of protection from barotrauma seems more plausible.

The Anseriformes generally have distinctly larger OW/FP ratios compared to the majority of terrestrial clades, including their nearest terrestrial relatives, the Galliformes, from which they diverged in the late Cretaceous (Chen et al. 2021; Claramunt et al. 2025). Although generally considered aquatic, many anseriform birds are only surface dwellers or dabbling species which do not fully immerse themselves underwater. My sample contains three diving ducks, including one deep diving sea duck Somateria mollissima, which forages a few metres below the surface (Guillemette et al. 2004). These three have the largest OW/FP ratios among the galloanserine birds, and Somateria mollissima has an OW/FP ratio that is the largest of all species outside the Phaethoquornithes. The hearing ability of Somateria mollissima does not appear different to other ducks in terms of frequencies they hear, but it may have elevated thresholds (Crowell et al. 2015). This lack of any difference in pitch perception (and overall poorer hearing) may support the notion that the highly enlarged ligament in diving birds is not a hearing adaptation but instead serves another, possibly protective purpose.

A protective function for the enlarged ligament in diving birds could manifest itself in several ways. A larger annular ligament may render the ear more compliant, thus having more give and permitting greater movement under static pressure loads. However, the increased mobility could allow the columella to be pushed further into the inner ear, displacing more fluid, which could be considered disadvantageous and may require further adaptation to protect the inner ear from large low‐frequency fluid motions. This may only be the case where static pressures cause the columella to displace in a piston‐like way and instead the larger ligament may encourage a rocking motion of the footplate, allowing for movement without excessively driving the columella or perilymph into the inner ear. A further, alternative explanation is that the larger ligament itself will provide a pressure relief mechanism by expanding into the middle ear space when the columella is driven into the inner ear. It's notable that almost all diving species have ligament areas that are equal to or greater than the footplate area, while this is not true for the majority of non‐diving aquatic and terrestrial species. This would appear to leave substantial room for the ligament to expand and move out of phase with the footplate.

While these possibilities could provide protection from barotrauma, this would be at the expense of efficient sound transmission. The out of phase ligament motion, rocking footplate motion, and other ideas would serve to diminish hearing sensitivity (in the absence of any other compensatory mechanism). It remains possible that diving birds have sacrificed hearing sensitivity in order to dive to greater depths, as appears to be the case for Somateria mollissima.

As noted earlier, Megaceryle maxima appeared as the biggest outlier among the diving birds with an OW/FP ratio that places it firmly among terrestrial species. One possible explanation is that this alcedinid is only a very shallow diver. Another explanation might be that its middle ear, with its derived columella (Feduccia 1975; Mayr 2003; Peacock et al. 2024), has other adaptations that compensate and protect it from trauma while still permitting a stiffer ear and improved high‐frequency perception. I was only able to measure in one species of alcedinid, and a broader sample ought to be examined before arriving at any conclusion.

Predictions of Hearing Specialisation

This data set contains a few curious data points, including the larger OW/FP ratios of Struthio camelus and Gyps coprotheres. While the hearing ability in Struthio camelus is not measured, it has been reported in other palaeognathous birds such as Dromaius novaehollandiae (Emu; Köppl and Manley 1997; Manley et al. 1997). The similarity between Struthio camelus and Dromaius novaehollandiae, including that both live in open environments and have similarly low frequency vocalisations, might suggest similar hearing ability, and we might further expect this to be skewed to even lower frequencies in Struthio camelus owing to its larger size. We may further add a prediction of low‐frequency hearing in Struthio camelus based on its relatively large annular ligament, which is not seen in any of the other palaeognathous birds measured for this study (Dromaius novaehollandiae, Casuarius casuarius (Southern Cassowary) and Crypturellus obsoletus (Brown Tinamou)). One might suggest a similar argument for low‐frequency specialisation in Gyps coprotheres however, to my knowledge, no published audiogram data exists for any vulture. These suggestions do need to be treated very cautiously. All the components of the middle ear act together as one system, and attempts to infer hearing from one element should be taken with the appropriate caution, especially when measurements here come from only one individual as in this study. Only direct measurements of hearing ability can confirm or reject these predictions.

As can be gleaned from the above discussion, interpretation of many points is limited by the availability of audiogram data which, while existing for many bird species (Dooling et al. 2000; Gleich and Langemann 2011), is lacking for the majority. This limits our ability to analyse potential relationships between morphology, mechanics and hearing ability, and this cannot be solved without gathering further audiogram data.

Conclusion

In this study, I demonstrated differences in the ratio of oval window to footplate area across a broad diversity of birds. I further showed that these differences appear aligned with different ecological niches: most aquatic birds have larger ratios than terrestrial birds and the largest ratios of all are found among diving species.

A relatively large ligament appears convergently in multiple lineages associated with the same ecological niche, strongly suggesting functional adaptation. The most likely explanation for pelagic seabirds is that annular ligament enlargement is an adaptation to improve low‐frequency hearing, which they may use to aid in navigation. However, the exceptionally large annular ligaments found in most diving species are unlikely to be adaptive for improved hearing. Instead, I propose that these are adapted to provide protection from barotrauma, which is likely at the expense of hearing sensitivity. The data shown here is purely correlative and future experimental and modelling studies are needed to test various hypotheses.

This work shows how studies of the relative size of the oval window and footplate could reveal new insights into the functional diversity of vertebrate ears, and further provide a window to study auditory adaptation and ecology, including in fossil species. More broadly, this study would not have been possible without open access to μCT datasets, and the work thus highlights the broader value of sharing data openly in comparative biology.
