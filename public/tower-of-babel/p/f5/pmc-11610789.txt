Ibrutinib-Induced Ventricular Electrical Storm Successfully Managed with Veno-Arterial ECMO and Intralipid Administration: A Rare Case Report
Authors: Debora E. Torre, Carla Stecco, Andrea Porzionato, Domenico Mangino, Veronica Macchi, Raffaele De Caro, Carmelo Pirri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report a 55-year-old men patient with a primitive central nervous system non-Hodgkin lymphoma B cell (LNH PNSLC), treated with chemotherapy rituximab, methotrexate, and ibrutinib (first treatment) who developed a refractory ventricular arrhythmic storm two hours after the ibrutinib intake. Indeed, ibrutinib could be associated with severe and occasionally fatal cardiac events. The swift emergence of a ventricular electrical storm with cardiac arrest demanded the prompt initiation of veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation to effectively navigate this critically ill patient toward recovery. This intervention was deemed imperative, given the absence of any available antidote for the effects of ibrutinib. Veno-arterial extracorporeal membrane oxygenation proved successful in rescuing this patient, resulting in a complete neurological recovery. Consequently, he was able to resume his chemotherapy treatment.

INTRODUCTION

Ibrutinib, an FDA-approved Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitor (BTKi), is used for various conditions, including mantle cell lymphoma, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, Waldenstrom’s macroglobulinemia, marginal zone lymphoma, and chronic graft-versus-host disease in allogeneic stem cell transplantation.[1] However, severe cardiac events, occasionally fatal, have been linked to ibrutinib exposure. Salem et al.[2] identified 303 ibrutinib-associated cardiovascular deaths, with increased occurrences of supraventricular arrhythmias, central nervous system (CNS) hemorrhagic events, heart failure, ventricular arrhythmias, conduction disorders, CNS ischemic events, and hypertension.

Cardiovascular adverse reactions associated with ibrutinib to fatalities ranging from approximately 10% (supraventricular and ventricular arrhythmias) to around 20% (CNS events, heart failure, and conduction disorders)[2] [Figure 1].

Ibrutinib side effects

Ibrutinib exhibits efficacy against various kinases. Its on-target effects lead to atrial-specific pro-arrhythmic effects, potentially associated with structural remodeling and calcium handling disorders. Off-target effects involve the PI3K pathway impacting sinus rhythm maintenance and increasing susceptibility to atrial fibrillation and ventricular arrhythmias.[3]

Risk factors include hypertension, prior atrial fibrillation, enlarged left atrial diameter, age over 65, and certain medication use (such as beta-blockers, ACEI/ARB, and Aspirin). Evaluation methods include cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) or echocardiography. Alternative BKT inhibitors, such as acalabrutinib, tirabrutinib, Zanubrutinib, and orelabrutinib, show good cardiovascular tolerability.[3]

Ventricular electrical storm, characterized by clustered sustained ventricular arrhythmias, poses risks of psychological disorders, heart failure decompensation, and elevated mortality.[4] Tachyarrhythmias, decreased ventricular filling time, and atrial contribution loss reduce cardiac output, causing cardiogenic shock. Managing ventricular electrical storm and resultant cardiogenic shock is challenging due to inotropic support exacerbating tachyarrhythmias and antiarrhythmic drugs having negative inotropic effects, leading to cardiac arrest.[4] Mechanical circulatory support (MCS), such as extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), can aid the management by providing time to understand the arrythmia’s cause and stabilizing hemodynamics for therapeutic interventions.

Extracorporeal life support is indicated for cardiogenic shock from acute myocardial infarction, myocarditis, cardiogenic shock postpulmonary embolism, drug intoxication, and postcardiac arrest syndrome with multi-organ failure risk. ECMO allows negative inotropic antiarrhythmic drug therapy, facilitating catecholamine weaning and breaking the catecholamine-driven electric storm cycle.[5] Intralipid, an intravenous lipid emulsion effective against lipophilic drug intoxications,[67] offered a possible therapeutic option. Hereby, we present a rare case of ibrutinib-related ventricular electrical storm treated with veno-arterial ECMO (V-A ECMO) and administration of intralipid.

CASE HISTORY

A 55-year-old, 75 kg men patient with recurrent primitive central nervous system non-Hodgkin lymphoma B cells (LNH PNSLC) experienced pulseless ventricular tachycardia and cardiac arrest two hours after receiving Ibrutinib treatment. Following DC 200J shock, 1 mg epinephrine, and an oro-tracheal tube placement, a return to spontaneous circulation (ROSC) occurred after six minutes.

Cerebral and thoracic CT scans with iv contrast were performed to rule out cerebral hemorrhage and pulmonary thrombo-embolism. Therefore, the patient was transported to the catheterization laboratory and the coronarography study revealed a 75% occlusion in the interventricular artery, not treated with a stent due to a Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) flow of 3.

A ventricular electrical storm during the coronary study led to 10 episodes of DC 200J shocks and initiation of femoro-femoral V-A ECMO support.

A Maquet femoral cannula (19Fr arterial, 21Fr venous), a Cardiohelp pump (Maquet), and a Maquet oxygenator were used [Figure 2]. ECMO provided stability for investigating the ventricular electrical storm’s root cause.

The 4 I and 4 T approach (European Resuscitation Council, advanced life support) ruled out various causes of cardiac arrest. We excluded 4 I: hypoxia (the patient was intubated), hypothermia (the patient was normothermic), hypovolemic status (the patient was normovolemic), and the alterations of electrolytes and pH serum concentration (using blood gas analysis). For about 4 T, we excluded cardiac tamponade and pneumothorax (using point-of-care ultrasound), thrombosis (coronary and pulmonary) performing the CT thorax scan with contrast, and the coronarography study. The most likely etiology was attributed to toxic/medication assumption (ibrutinib being the sole medication taken in the preceding 48 hours and the patient did not have cardiomyopathies that could explain the onset of an arrhythmic storm).

With no antidote for ibrutinib, ECMO was crucial to ensure adequate end-organ perfusion and potential cardiac recovery, all the while allowing the toxic agent to undergo metabolism. Continuous infusion of antiarrhythmic drugs (amiodarone 900 mg/24h iv, sulfate magnesium 10 g/24h iv, lidocaine 4 gr/24h iv) for the first 24 hours and off-label bolus of 150 ml of Intralipid 20% were administered. Continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was started and another DC shock in ECMO was performed, obtaining the ROSC for three hours.

A transthoracic echocardiogram revealed that an ejection fraction was <10%, and the aortic valve did not open, prompting epinephrine 0.05 mcg/kg/min and reduced ECMO blood flow 3.4 lpm to consent the aortic valve opening. Unfortunately, after three hours the patient had another ventricular storm so ECMO blood flow 4.6 lpm was restored, continuing antiarrhythmic drugs and deep sedation.

After 12 h, we administered another off-label bolus of 150 ml of Intralipid 20% and executed again a DC shock 200J. We obtained finally the ROSC and a definitive sinusal rhythm. No other arrhythmic events were observed, but the ejection fraction was <10%. We restarted adrenaline 0.05 mcg/kg/min and reduced again ECMO blood flow to 3.4 lpm to consent to the aortic valve opening and a heart systole-diastolic activity. After 36 hours of not observed arrhythmias, we administered a levosimendan infusion with consequent improvement of cardiac ejection fraction (35% after 5 days) and TAPSE (20 mm).

The ejection fraction was measured during ECMO with and without epinephrine. For this purpose, we performed an echocardiogram using the biplane Simpson’s method.

In ECMO without inotropic support, there was no aortic valve opening, resulting in no systolic/diastolic activity. The left ventricle was dilated and diffusely akinetic, with initial smoke observed in the left heart sections. Initiating epinephrine infusion and reducing ECMO blood flow to 3.4 lpm led to aortic valve opening, initial emptying of the left ventricle, and gradual improvement in contractile activity. The initial ejection fraction, less than 10%, improved to 35% subsequently. Besides left ventricular ejection fraction, left ventricular outflow tract velocity time integral (LVOT VTI) was also assessed during ECMO support via echocardiography (LVOT VTI was 12 cm when the ECMO was weaned off).

After seven days, the patient was weaned from V-A ECMO and after nine days, from mechanical ventilation. Discharged after 11 days, he continued chemotherapy to fight his lymphoma, with a cerebral performance status CPC score = 1 and no ECMO-related complications [Figure 3].

DISCUSSION

This case report details a rare instance of ibrutinib-related ventricular electrical storm managed with V-A ECMO and off-label intralipid administration.

In this case report, we effectively managed a severe adverse effect of ibrutinib using mechanical circulatory support (ECMO), stabilizing the critically ill patient and facilitating the detoxification of ibrutinib metabolism. Ibrutinib undergoes metabolism primarily in the liver by the enzymes CYP3A5 and CYP3A4, with a minor contribution from CYP2D6. Approximately 7.8% of the administered dose is excreted in urine, mainly within the first 24 h, and it has a half-life of four to six hours.[8] The off-label administration of intralipid 20% proved to be beneficial, as it is an intravenous lipid emulsion.

While the mechanism of action is not fully understood, it has demonstrated efficacy in treating lipophilic drug intoxications. It may create a lipid-rich compartment in the plasma (lipid sink theory), separating lipophilic drugs from target tissue. Another theory suggests that intralipid provides energy to the myocardium, activating the voltage-gated calcium channels in the myocytes (fat load and ionic channel theory).[9] The case highlights the efficacy of intralipid in rescuing patients from nonlocal anesthetic system toxicity, acting as a carrier, and possibly as a cardiac tonic.

The American College of Medical Toxicology (ACMT) recommends Lipid Resuscitation Therapy for poisoning by lipid-soluble cardiotoxic medication. Its use is discretionary based on clinical judgment, especially in cases of hemodynamic instability from highly lipid-soluble xenobiotics.[10] Reported adverse reactions include limitations in blood test, allergies allergic reactions, and fat overload syndrome, but our patient did not exhibit these reactions.[11]

Aware of risks of intravenous lipid emulsion (ILE) during ECMO,[12] precautions were taken: monitoring circuit parameters (and inspection monitoring for clotting and clogging) and machine indicators for malfunction, such as drop pressure, R membrane, and pressures (on arterial cannula, on venous cannula, and on oxygenator inlet), revealed no issues.

We believed that the clinical benefits obtained for the patient justify the risks of this ILE administration which has been carefully monitored, and ready to take action in case of malfunctions.

V-A ECMO played a crucial role in the patient’s life-saving, used as a rescue therapy during Extracorporeal Cardio-pulmonary Resuscitation (ECPR) in advanced life support. ESC guidelines recommend considering mechanical support for ventricular tachycardia and cardiogenic shock (class IIb, level C) after the administration of antiarrhythmic drugs (amiodarone, lidocaine, beta blocker, magnesium) (class I), deep sedation or oro-tracheal intubation (class IIa, level C).[13]

ECMO therapy management follows ELSO guidelines,[14] with relative contraindications including malignant tumor with poor life expectancy. Our patient had a malignant tumor, but the prognosis depended on chemotherapy response and by re-stadiation, making it challenging to categorize life expectancy.

ELSO guidelines emphasized clinician freedom in ECMO therapy administration, acknowledging that adherence to a guideline does not guarantee a successful outcome, requiring individualized clinical judgment.[14]

Recognizing that the patient’s only bridge was cardiac recovery, ineligible for a cardiac transplant or ventricular assist device implantation, we enabled him to continue fighting lymphoma, offering a chance to live as long as his lymphoma prognosis allows.
