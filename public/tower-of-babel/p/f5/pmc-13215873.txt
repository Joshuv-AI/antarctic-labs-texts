Early-life exposure to ambient temperature and polycystic ovary syndrome: a nationwide cohort study
Authors: Boya Zhang, Makiko Mitsunami, Elizabeth Peebles, Brent A Coull, Tamarra James-Todd, Jorge E Chavarro, Jaime E Hart, Francine Laden, Shruthi Mahalingaiah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly-life temperature exposure, especially during oogenesis, may impact ovarian function later in life. However, the effect of ambient temperature during sensitive developmental windows on ovulation disorders remains unknown.MethodsWe investigate the associations of early-life ambient temperature exposure with incident polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) in the Growing Up Today Study II, a United States (US) nationwide prospective cohort. We included females born after 1989 with available outcome and exposure data during the preconception (N = 3265), gestation (N = 3265), and childhood period (N = 3521). Monthly average residential mean temperatures were estimated using the PRISM Climate Group data. We considered exposure windows included preconception (3 months prior to conception), gestational (from conception month to birth), individual trimesters, and childhood (from birth to 1 year before age at menarche). PCOS was identified by self-reported clinical diagnosis. We estimated adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for PCOS per interquartile range (IQR) increase of mean temperature using Cox models.ResultsWe identified 234 (7.2%) PCOS cases over the 14.3 ± 4.2 years from age at menarche to 2021. Higher ambient temperatures during the preconception (HR 1.38 per 13.4°C, 95% CI, 0.84–2.27), gestation (HR 1.08 per 6.4°C, 95% CI, 0.74–1.60), and childhood (HR 1.20 per 4.1°C, 95% CI, 0.91–1.59) periods were suggestively associated with higher risk of PCOS. In gestation period, only the first-trimester temperature was associated with higher risks of PCOS (HR 1.75 per 14.4°C, 95% CI, 1.06–2.87).ConclusionIntervention to mitigate heat exposure during pregnancy, especially in the first trimester, may help reduce the risk of PCOS in offspring. Further research is needed to confirm these associations.Keywords: climate change, ambient temperature, preconception and gestation exposure, childhood exposure, polycystic ovary syndrome, reproductive health

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is the most common ovulation disorder among women of reproductive age, with an overall prevalence of between 8% and 20% worldwide [1]. Characterized by hyperandrogenism, irregular ovulation, and polycystic ovaries [2], women with PCOS have a 15 times higher risk of anovulatory infertility and a 2–3 times higher risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes than women without PCOS [3]. Beyond its impact on reproductive health, PCOS also contributes to other long-term health risks, including type 2 diabetes mellitus [4], cardiovascular disease (CVD) [5], and adverse mental health conditions [6]. Overall, PCOS profoundly affects individuals’ physical health and quality of life, while also imposing substantial economic costs on healthcare systems and society [7]. Despite its widespread impact, the etiology of PCOS remains elusive, and current treatments focus primarily on symptom management, underscoring the critical need to identify risk factors for developing effective early prevention strategies.

Temperature changes, particularly in the form of higher ambient temperatures, have had consequences for human health worldwide and are projected to have ongoing future impacts [8]. Increasing evidence indicates that exposure to high ambient temperatures during the early development period could increase the risk of childhood obesity and early menarche [9–12]. Given that both childhood obesity and early menarche are correlated with PCOS, it is plausible to hypothesize that higher early-life exposure to ambient temperatures may contribute to increased PCOS risk, though we did not identify any previous studies that have explored this potential link.

To address this gap, in this study, we leveraged a United States (US) nationwide cohort, the Growing Up Today Study II (GUTSII), to evaluate associations of preconception, gestation, and childhood exposure to ambient temperature with incident PCOS. As oogenesis has specific gestational phases and might be more vulnerable to the effect of temperature [13], we also evaluated the associations between PCOS and exposure during different trimesters.

Materials and methods

Study population

The GUTSII, founded in 2004, a prospective cohort of 10 923 children, was founded to study the health of adolescents and young adults. The cohort was established by inviting mothers in the ongoing Nurses’ Health Study II (NHSII) to enroll their children who were between the ages of 10 and 17 years. All subsequent correspondence was directly with GUTSII participants themselves. Follow-up questionnaires were mailed annually or biannually until the most recent in 2021. The questionnaires ask about demographic characteristics, updated lifestyle characteristics, and history of chronic diseases. The residential addresses of NHSII participants are distributed throughout the contiguous US during the biennial follow-up and have been geocoded to obtain latitude and longitude. We assumed that all participants lived with their mothers through age 18.

In the present study, we restricted the sample to only participants who reported as female at baseline. Since addresses information was first available for NHSII participants in 1989, we limited our analyses to those born after 1989. We also excluded participants who lost to follow-up before the first PCOS survey question in 2011, never reported age at menarche, or were missing in key covariates (Supplementary Fig. 1). The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of the Brigham and Women’s Hospital and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health and consent was implied through the return of the questionnaires.

Outcome assessment

On all questionnaires since 2011, participants were asked to report any diagnosis of PCOS by their healthcare provider and to recall the year of first diagnosis from a range of years depending on the year they took the survey. For example, in the 2011 questionnaire, the response options for the year of first diagnosis included “before 2004,” “2004 − 2006,” “2007 − 2009,” and “2010+.” Depending on the selected year range, we defined the incident date of PCOS as below: for those selecting the earliest options, we assigned 1 year prior to the earliest year options (e.g. Dec 2002 when reporting before 2004); for intervals given, we used the midpoint of the reported interval; and for those indicating the last year options, we set the incident dates as two months prior to the closest questionnaire return date (e.g. 2 months before the 2011 questionnaire return date).

Exposure assessment

Daily average ambient temperatures were estimated using data from the Parameter-elevation Regressions on Independent Slopes Model (PRISM) climate group at Oregon State University at 800-m resolution [14]. For the development of this model, temperature data were obtained from over 10 000 surface stations throughout the US and Canada. Monthly moving average temperatures were calculated from daily mean temperature predictions and assigned to participants’ residential addresses based on the 800 m grid cell that contained the geocoded address.

In 2009, mothers of GUTS participants reported their pregnancies through both the GUTS mother’s questionnaire and a pregnancy history summary in NHSII. When both questionnaires were available, we prioritized the NHSII responses, as they provided more detailed categories for gestational age (20–27, 28–31, 32–36, 37–39, 40–42, and 43 or more gestational weeks). In contrast, the GUTSII mother’s questionnaire included only broader classifications.

We then estimated the conception month with the birth month and the rounded gestational age in months. We defined the following exposure windows based on these estimated conception months: the preconception period (the three months prior to conception), the gestational period (from conception month to birth month), the first trimester (0–3 months after conception), the second trimester (4–6 months after conception), and the third trimester (from the seventh month after conception to birth). We calculated the childhood exposure as the average temperature from birth to one year before age at menarche. Age at menarche was self-reported by participants on questionnaires in 2004, 2006, and 2008. We used the first report to minimize recall bias. We also calculated other measures of temperature for sensitivity analyses, including average maximum temperature and average minimum temperature within the same developmental windows.

Other covariates

Information on potential confounders was obtained from self-reported questionnaires from participants in GUTII and their mothers in NHSII. We selected covariates that were associated with temperature exposures and might also be associated with the risk of developing PCOS but were unlikely to be mediators between the two, including demographic characteristics, socioeconomic status (SES) and lifestyle factors, and maternal characteristics. Demographic characteristics include age at baseline, year of birth, month, and season of conception. SES factors include neighborhood socioeconomic status (NSES) during the exposure period. Lifestyle factors include diet and physical activity. Maternal characteristics include maternal PCOS status before conception, maternal smoking status (never, past, and current smokers), maternal pre-pregnancy body mass index (BMI) either at baseline of NHSII (1989) or at next non-pregnant follow-up interview after past pregnancy. Detailed description of covariates could be found in Text S1.

Statistical analyses

We used Cox proportional hazards models, with age as the time axis, to follow participants from menarche until the earliest of the following events: first diagnosis of PCOS, loss to follow-up, or the end of the study period (2021). We estimated hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the risk of PCOS associated with each interquartile range (IQR) increase in average residential temperature during the preconception, gestation, and childhood periods. All models were stratified by age at baseline and adjusted for year of birth, season and month of conception, NSES, Census region, maternal PCOS status, smoking status, and maternal pre-pregnancy BMI. We evaluated potential effect modification by maternal PCOS status, Census region, and season of conception one at a time by including an interaction term between each exposure and the potential effect modifier in the fully adjusted models. Finally, we verified the proportional hazards assumption for all unstratified variables in the Cox models using scaled Schoenfeld residuals [15].

In sensitivity analyses, we first assessed potential deviations from linearity by fitting average mean temperatures with natural-splines with four degrees of freedom in our fully adjusted Cox models. We then further adjusted for participants’ lifestyle factors, maternal pre-pregnancy diabetes at NHSII baseline, and replaced maternal pre-pregnancy BMI with maternal BMI at age 18. To test the potential variations in association with more extreme measures of temperature, we replaced the average mean temperature with the average minimum and maximum temperature, respectively. We also accounted for inverse probability weighting (IPW) to address potential selection bias introduced by: (i) loss to follow-up of participants before the first PCOS survey or (ii) missing age at menarche. Given the incident date of PCOS may not be accurate and affected by the diagnosis pattern, we limited to subjects who were followed for at least 15 years after the age of menarche and treated PCOS diagnosis as a binary outcome to estimate the risk ratios (RRs) of PCOS using modified Poisson models with sandwich standard errors. Lastly, we used IPW in our modified Poisson models to account for potential selection bias introduced by loss to follow-up before 15 years since age at menarche. All analyses were conducted in SAS 9.4 or R (version 4.3).

Results

We respectively included 3265 and 3521 participants in our analyses of preconception/gestation and childhood exposure (Supplementary Fig. 1). Among the 3265 participants who were included in the analyses of preconception and gestation exposure (Table 1), the mean (standard deviation, SD) age at the menarche was 12.7 (1.6) years with 3103 (95.0%) White participants. We identified 234 (7.2%) PCOS cases during the 14.3 (4.2) years from age at menarche to the end of follow-up or 2021. Compared to those who did not develop PCOS, participants with PCOS were more likely to be conceived during the summer, reside in the Midwest region, with a family history of PCOS, and had higher ambient temperature levels at their addresses during the first trimester. We observed relatively high (Pearson r = 0.72, Supplementary Fig. 2) correlations among temperature during different exposure periods.

Associations of temperature with PCOS

Figure 1 shows HRs and 95% CIs for incident PCOS associated with an IQR increase in average mean temperature during different periods in the fully adjusted models. We observed a 38% (HR, 1.38 per 13.4°C, 95% CI, 0.84–2.27), 8% (HR, 1.08 per 6.4°C, 95% CI, 0.74–1.60), and 20% (HR, 1.20 per 4.1°C, 95% CI, 0.91–1.59) increase in the risk of incident PCOS per IQR of average mean temperature during the preconception, gestation, and childhood period respectively. When examining effect modification (Fig. 2), we found generally higher HRs for the average mean temperature, especially during the childhood period, among participants whose mothers reported diagnosed PCOS at baseline. Among subgroups residing in different regions, we find evidence of higher associations with temperature during the first trimester in the Northeast and South regions in the US. Regarding effect modification by the season of conception, we observed higher associations for average temperature during the first trimester among those conceived during summer.

In sensitivity analyses (Supplementary Table 1), our findings remained robust when further adjustment of maternal pre-pregnancy morbidity and participants’ lifestyle factors, replacing pre-pregnancy BMI with BMI at age 18, using average min and max temperatures as the exposure, estimating RRs of incident PCOS within 15 years of age at menarche with the modified Poisson model, as well as accounting for loss to follow-up or missingness in age at menarche in both the Cox and modified Poisson models. Across all analyses, the strongest associations continued to be observed during the first trimester, with 95% CIs not crossing 1. The smoothing curves of the relationships between temperature and PCOS are approximately linear across the temperature distribution, although there is an indication of a plateau or even a reversal in slope for temperatures exceeding 20°C during the first trimester (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

In this US nationwide cohort study, we found that higher exposure to the average ambient temperature during the preconception, gestation, and childhood periods was associated with a greater risk of developing PCOS, though the estimated associations were not different from null. The strength of the associations differed across gestation periods, with the most robust during the first trimester (1.75 per 14.4°C, 95% CI, 1.06–2.87). To the best of our knowledge, we are the first to report an association between ambient temperature and incident PCOS. Our findings indicate female fetus may be susceptible to higher temperatures during the first trimester, leading to ovarian disorders in adolescence and early adulthood. However, more research is needed to support our findings.

PCOS is one of the leading causes of ovulation disorders and infertility in women [16]. Although few studies have assessed the relationship between ambient temperature and PCOS, our findings may align with research on temperature and other female fertility outcomes. Season of birth, which correlates with gestational temperature, has been associated with fertility rates in different populations worldwide [17–19]. In a meta-analysis of 22 studies, most demonstrated that increased temperature at birth could disturb female fertility in adult life [20]. A time-series analysis of 65 countries also observed clear negative effects of temperatures on total fertility rates, which may persist into the following generation [21]. Beyond fertility rate, birth season has also been linked with age at menarche and menopause [20]. Furthermore, higher temperatures three months preceding ovarian function testing, as part of an infertility workup, were linked to lower ovarian antral follicle count (AFC) among 631 women in Massachusetts, further indicating the potential impact of increasing temperatures on ovarian function [22].

Our study further extends the literature by reporting associations of PCOS with ambient temperatures during sensitive developmental windows. Several studies have reported associations between gestation or childhood exposure to higher ambient temperature with various health outcomes, including higher risk of acute lymphoblastic leukemia [8], common cold [23], and earlier onset of menarche [10]. Among studies that evaluated the trimester-specific exposures, harmful associations of temperature during the first trimester were the greatest among the three trimesters [8, 23], which is consistent with our findings. This may be because oogenesis and ovarian development occur rapidly during the first trimester, making it highly susceptible to damage from temperature variation [24].

Several potential mechanisms may explain the association between ambient temperature and PCOS. PCOS is typically characterized by elevated markers of ovarian reserve, such as AFC, luteinizing hormone (LH) to follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) ratio, and anti-Müllerian hormone levels (AMH). Ambient temperature may impact PCOS development by disrupting ovarian function and cell signaling between the follicle, ovary, and hypothalamus [25]. For example, elevated temperatures can negatively affect follicular development and ovulation through hormonal disruption or increased oxidative stress, leading to anovulation, a hallmark of PCOS [26]. Consistent with the aromatase activation observed in patients with PCOS [27], in animal studies, heat stress consistently reduces estradiol levels across species (e.g. mice, rats, rabbits, pigs, cows), likely through suppression of aromatase expression in granulosa cells [28]. Another possible mechanism involves temperature-induced cell-signaling aberrations in the primordial follicle. Specifically, peak oocyte numbers occur in mid-gestation, with rapid oogenesis in the first trimester. Animal studies demonstrate that heat stress impairs folliculogenesis by reducing follicle number and viability, increasing atresia and apoptosis, and disrupting granulosa cell–oocyte coordination [29–31]. Studies in humans also observed low yield among individuals with PCOS, despite high AMH levels [32]. Therefore, initially induced signaling abnormalities may lead to increased AMH due to extreme temperature exposure, influencing the development of ovulatory disorder PCOS in reproductive age [33].

Our study has several limitations that need to be mentioned. First, we assigned exposure at the residential address without considering the use of mitigations like air conditioning, other indoor cooling systems, or time spent in other locations, which may lead to exposure measurement error. However, such measurement error was likely to be non-differential and would likely bias associations toward the null. Of note, PCOS diagnosis was based on self-report, which may underestimated the true proportion of cases. In this case, the true prevalence of PCOS might be higher than we observed (7.1%), as younger women may have delayed diagnosis due to lack of symptom awareness at the individual or care provider level [34]. However, self-reported data have been widely used in other US studies with the understanding that this underestimation is non-differential, potentially biasing our effect estimates toward the null [35, 36]. In addition, there is still no agreement on definitive biochemical or imaging markers for the clinical diagnosis of PCOS due to the diverse clinical and metabolic manifestations of PCOS [37, 38]. The generalizability of our findings may be limited due to the homogeneity of the GUTSII study population, which largely comprised non-Hispanic white participants (95%) and children of registered nurses. However, there is no evidence suggesting that the distribution of health status or health behaviors of the GUTSII participants differs importantly from that of the general population [39]. Additionally, our participants may be more proactive in seeking healthcare, making them less likely to have undiagnosed PCOS. This reduces the likelihood of underestimating our outcomes. Finally, as with all observational studies, we cannot completely rule out the possibility that our observed associations are attributable to unmeasured confounding.

Despite its limitations, our study is, to our knowledge, the first to prospectively evaluate the association between ambient temperature and risk of developing PCOS. Additionally, we also examined the life course effects of temperature by assessing preconception, gestation, and childhood exposures, including trimester-specific associations. Utilizing a nationwide cohort with participants residing across the US, we were able to adjust for a comprehensive list of potential confounders at both maternal and child levels to minimize residual confounding. Also, validation studies of numerous self-reported variables, such as body weight and hypertension [40, 41], have indicated highly accurate information in the cohort.

Conclusion

With the growing potential for global temperature changes, the health risks associated with ambient temperature have received increasing attention. Our findings suggest a possible association between higher average temperatures during the preconception, gestation, and childhood periods and PCOS risk. However, only first-trimester exposure showed evidence of an association different from the null. These results indicate that mitigating higher temperatures during pregnancy, especially in the first trimester, might be an effective strategy to lower the children’s risk of developing PCOS later in life. However, more research is needed to confirm our findings, and future work should also account for the role of adaptation when evaluating the impact of temperature.
