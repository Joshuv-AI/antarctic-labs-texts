Targeting GPA33 with CAR-macrophages: a novel immunotherapeutic approach for colorectal cancer
Authors: Daijun Wang, Wenzhang Wu, Luxi Yang, Caihao Qu, Yujie Wei, Yang Zhao, Yumin Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn recent years, tumor immunotherapy, exemplified by immune checkpoint inhibitors, has achieved remarkable success in certain patients. However, the efficacy of such therapies remains minimal in the majority of colorectal cancer patients. Overcoming the suppression of the tumor immune microenvironment and developing novel immunotherapeutic strategies have become focal points of research in this field. Adoptive cell therapy demonstrates immense potential, with chimeric antigen receptor macrophages exhibiting inherent advantages in combating solid tumors. GPA33, a surface antigen that is expressed in nearly all colorectal cancers, is a promising target for immunotherapy in colorectal cancer. This study aims to evaluate the potential therapeutic effects of GPA33-targeted CAR-macrophages in colorectal cancer.MethodsUsing single B cell isolation technology and single-cell PCR technology, we cloned the complete variable region sequences of mouse antibody light and heavy chains, which were subsequently employed to construct the scFv fragment of GPA33 CAR. CAR-Ms were generated by transfecting THP-1 cells with lentivirus, followed by PMA induction. The targeted phagocytic activity of CAR-Ms was observed using fluorescence microscopy, and their targeted phagocytosis, polarization trends, and tumoricidal effects were validated through flow cytometry. Cytokine secretion was quantified using ELISA assays. The in vitro and in vivo anti-tumor effects of CAR-Ms were assessed through bioluminescence measurements, while the safety of CAR-Ms was evaluated using HE staining and a small animal biochemical analyzer.ResultsBased on the successful development of a novel GPA33 antibody with high affinity and specificity, we constructed CAR-Ms targeting GPA33. For the in vitro experiments, GPA33-specific CAR-Ms were able to mediate antigen-dependent M1-like polarization of macrophages, enhancing their tumor phagocytic activity and pro-inflammatory cytokine secretion. In in vivo experiments, a xenograft model of colon cancer was established using immunodeficient mice, and the injection of CAR-Ms effectively inhibited tumor growth.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that the GPA33 CAR-M, constructed based on a novel GPA33 antibody, exhibits significant inhibitory effects on GPA33 antigen-positive colorectal cancer. This finding offers a proof-of-concept for a potential strategy for immunotherapy in colorectal cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-07897-6.Keywords: Chimeric antigen receptor, Macrophages, colorectal cancer, GPA33, Immunotherapy

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is the third most common malignancy worldwide and ranks second among cancer-related causes of death [1]. In recent years, the incidence of CRC has been rising progressively among young adults [2], constituting an urgent global public health concern. Early-stage CRC is often curable following surgical resection and adjuvant chemotherapy, whereas unresectable metastatic CRC is typically managed with systemic therapies, including immunotherapy. Although molecularly tailored immunotherapies targeting tumor-specific features have improved overall survival for some patients, five-year survival remains below 20% [3]. Thus, the development of novel immunotherapeutic strategies for CRC is of critical importance.

Adoptive cell transfer therapy (ACT) is an emerging cancer immunotherapy that augments host antitumor immunity by reinfusing host-derived immune cells with intrinsic antitumor activity or cells genetically modified to express chimeric antigen receptors (CARs). CAR-T cells have achieved remarkable success against hematologic malignancies but have not realized their full potential against epithelial tumors, including CRC. The principal limitations are insufficient intratumoral infiltration of CAR-T cells and their susceptibility to immunosuppressive mechanisms within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [4]. These constraints on CAR-T cell efficacy in solid tumors have driven exploration of alternative cellular platforms. Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), the most abundant immune cell subset infiltrating the CRC TME, play pivotal roles in CRC tumorigenesis and progression [5]. As key components of innate immunity, macrophages are characterized by phenotypic plasticity and heterogeneity and can polarize in response to TME cues toward classically activated (M1) or alternatively activated (M2) states. M1 macrophages possess pro-inflammatory and antitumor properties, secreting cytokines such as IL-12 and TNF-α, directly killing tumor cells and cooperating with dendritic cells to activate T cell–mediated immunity. In contrast, M2 macrophages promote tumor angiogenesis, immune suppression, and matrix remodeling by secreting factors including VEGF, TGF-β, and MMPs, which inhibit the functions of cytotoxic T lymphocytes and natural killer cells and thereby facilitate tumor progression and metastasis [6, 7]. In advanced tumors, M2-like TAMs often predominate and promote tumor growth and metastasis. Modulation of macrophage polarization—suppressing the M2 phenotype while promoting M1 polarization—has emerged as one of the key strategies in tumor immunotherapy [8, 9]. Previous studies have shown that chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) macrophages possess unique capabilities, including specific recognition, phagocytosis, and killing of tumor cells, as well as enhanced antigen presentation to bolster adaptive immunity. Consequently, engineering macrophages with CARs has become a feasible approach to combat solid tumors.

Glycoprotein A33 (GPA33), a cell-surface receptor of the immunoglobulin superfamily, is expressed in approximately 95% of colorectal cancers and is absent from most normal human tissues [10, 11]. Recent studies report GPA33 expression in up to 98% of pulmonary metastases from CRC, outperforming other gut-derived markers and indicating high diagnostic sensitivity and specificity [12]. Given its distinct tissue and cellular localization, GPA33 is considered an ideal target for CRC therapy. CAR-T cells directed against GPA33 have demonstrated potent antitumor activity in vitro and in vivo [13]. However, the potential of GPA33-targeted CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) in CRC has not yet been investigated.

In the present study, we constructed CAR-macrophages based on a novel anti-GPA33 antibody and evaluated their antigen-specific phagocytic and tumor-killing ability in defined preclinical models. Our results demonstrate that the engineered chimeric antigen receptor macrophages (CAR-M) induced by Thp-1 cells exhibit high antigen-targeting specificity and affinity, undergo antigen-driven polarization toward a proinflammatory phenotype in vitro, and show enhanced tumor growth inhibition in vitro and in immunodeficient xenograft models. These findings establish the foundational mechanisms of GPA33-specific CAR-M and support their further development as a potential immunotherapeutic approach for colorectal cancer.

Materials and methods

Preparation of recombinant antigen and animal immunization

To obtain specific antibodies targeting GPA33, we first constructed a recombinant GPA33 antigen. Specifically, the amino acid sequence of the extracellular region of human GPA33 (amino acids 22–235) was selected, and a sequence encoding a 6×His tag was fused to its N-terminus. Subsequently, protein expression was carried out using a mammalian expression system, and high-purity recombinant His-GPA33 protein was purified using nickel column affinity chromatography. This recombinant antigen was used to immunize 6-week-old female BALB/c mice (purchased from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd.). The immunization protocol was as follows: each mouse received a subcutaneous injection of an emulsion containing 50 µg of antigen mixed with Freund’s complete adjuvant (F5881, sigma) (for primary immunization) or incomplete adjuvant (F5506, sigma) (for booster immunizations), administered four times at two-week intervals. One week after the final immunization, serum was collected from the mice via orbital bleeding. The titer of anti-GPA33 antibodies in the serum was assessed using ELISA. Mice with the highest serum titers were selected for subsequent experiments.

Isolation of antigen-specific plasma cells and single B cell sorting

Upon confirmation of successful immunization, the aforementioned mouse was euthanized, and the spleen was aseptically removed. The single-cell suspension of splenic cells was prepared through mechanical grinding. Subsequently, using the mouse CD138 Positive Selection Kit (100–0601, STEMCELL Technologies) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, CD138 + cell populations were positively sorted from the splenic cell suspension. To obtain monoclonal B cells secreting antibodies targeting GPA33, single-cell isolation was performed using the CellCelector automated cell picking system from Sartorius (Germany). The fluorescence imaging system of the CellCelector automatically identified and selected single B cells secreting specific antibodies, which were directly sorted into a 96-well PCR plate containing cell lysis buffer for immediate use in subsequent reverse transcription.

Amplification of antibody variable region genes

Using the SuperScript™ IV Single Cell/Low-Input cDNA PreAmp Kit (1152048, Thermo Fisher), mRNA reverse transcription and cDNA pre-amplification were performed on the sorted single B cells. The procedure was strictly followed according to the kit’s instructions. Subsequently, using the pre-amplified products as templates, nested PCR was employed to amplify the variable region genes of the antibody light and heavy chains; the specific sequences of all primers used for PCR are provided in Additional file 3. All PCR reactions were conducted using the High-Fidelity DNA Polymerase Kit (P501, Vazyme) to ensure amplification fidelity. The PCR products were verified through agarose gel electrophoresis, and bands of the correct size were excised, purified, and sent to Anhui General Biol Co., Ltd for Sanger sequencing.

Production of recombinant monoclonal antibody

Antibody selection was performed based on sequence differences in the CDR regions, particularly the CDR3 region. From all successfully sequenced clones, five unique antibody sequences (designated as Ab1 to Ab5) were selected, characterized by low amino acid sequence similarity in the CDR regions and representative of the diversity observed. In brief, the selected heavy chain variable region and light chain variable region gene fragments were individually cloned into a humanized antibody expression vector containing the human IgG1 constant region, thereby constructing full-length heavy and light chain expression plasmids. These plasmids were then transfected into HEK-293 F cells for antibody expression and purification.

Antibody affinity assessment

The binding affinity of the recombinant antibody to the antigen was determined using an indirect ELISA method. The recombinant His-GPA33 antigen was diluted to a concentration of 1 µg/mL in a coating buffer and 100 µL was added to each well of a 96-well plate, followed by overnight incubation at 4 °C for coating. The purified antibody was subjected to a series of gradient dilutions, starting from 20 nM and performing threefold serial dilutions, which were then added to the corresponding wells for incubation. Flow cytometry was employed to assess the binding capacity of the antibody to cells expressing GPA33. The recombinant antibody at the corresponding dilution concentration was incubated with one million LS174T cells at 4 °C for one hour, followed by the addition of Goat anti-Human IgG Fc (FITC) antibody (RS2304, Immunoway) as a secondary antibody for flow cytometric analysis.

Cell lines

Human colorectal cancer cell lines (T84, LS174T, HCT116, RKO, SW480, SW620, Caco2, HT29) and the THP-1 cell line (human monocytic leukemia cell) were obtained from Procell Life Science and Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, China. NCM460 (human normal colon epithelial cells), HEK-293T, and HEK293-F cell lines are maintained in our laboratory. All cell lines were authenticated via short tandem repeat (STR) profiling. The cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 or DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator. The SW620-GPA33 cell line was established by infecting SW620 cells with a truncated version of human GPA33 without an intracellular domain overexpression lentivirus. GPA33-short hairpin RNA (shRNA) lentivirus was employed to generate the LS174T-GPA33 knockdown cell line. The targeting sequence for GPA33 shRNA#1 was: 5‘-GGGACTTATTCAATGGGATAATTCAAGAGATTATCCCATTGAATAAGTCCC-3’. The targeting sequence for GPA33 shRNA#2 was: 5‘-GGACGCAGTTCTGCAACATCATTCAAGAGATGATGTTGCAGAACTGCGTCC-3’.Corresponding cell lines expressing either the luciferase gene or mCherry red fluorescent protein were obtained by infecting the respective cell lines with luciferase-puromycin or mCherry-puromycin lentiviruses, and all cells transduced with the virus underwent puromycin selection to establish stable cell lines.

Molecular docking

The amino acid sequence of the GPA33 protein was retrieved from the UniProt database (https://www.uniprot.org/). The sequences of GPA33 and CAR-scFv were subsequently submitted to the AlphaFold3 server (https://deepmind.google/technologies/alphafold/alphafold-server/) for interaction modeling. Five interaction models were generated, and the model with the highest ranking score was selected for further analysis of its predicted interaction sites. Visualization of specific amino acid residues involved in the interaction was performed using the open-source version 2.6 of PyMOL.

Production of CARs and macrophages

The CAR construct comprises a CD8α signal peptide, a scFv targeting the extracellular domain of GPA33, the hinge and transmembrane regions of CD8α, the FcγI receptor ITAM domain, and the PI3K-recruitment domain of CD19, linked to eGFP via a P2A sequence. Synthesis of the CAR plasmid and lentiviral production were performed by Anhui General Biol Co., Ltd. THP-1 cells were transduced at an MOI of 50 in the presence of 2 µg/mL polybrene for 24 h, transduction efficiency was assessed 72 h later by flow cytometric detection of eGFP expression. THP-1 cells transduced or untransduced with lentivirus were induced with 50 µg/mL PMA (S1819, Beyotime) for 48 h, then replaced with complete RPMI-1640 medium and cultured for an additional 24 h. Successful M0 induction was assessed by flow cytometric analysis of CD14 and CD11b expression. PMA-differentiated THP-1 cells represent an artificial macrophage model with pre-activated characteristics that may not fully recapitulate the biology of primary tissue-resident or monocyte-derived macrophages. This model was selected for its reproducibility and ease of genetic manipulation for initial mechanistic proof-of-concept studies.

Phagocytosis assay based on flow cytometry

LS174T-mCherry cells and GFP-positive control macrophages (CON-M) or CAR-M were co-cultured at a 1:1 ratio. The co-culture systems were harvested using Accutase (00-4555-56, Invitrogen) at 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, and 4 h. Sample processing included multiple washes to ensure maximal dissociation of cell aggregates. The proportion of GFP/mCherry double-positive cells was used as the probability of phagocytic events. LS174T-mCherry, T84-mCherry, SW620-vector, or SW620-GPA33 cells were co-cultured with CON-M or CAR-M at a 1:1 ratio for 1 h. The co-culture was then harvested using Accutase, and flow cytometry was employed to determine the proportion of mCherry-positive cells within the GFP-positive population, which represents the phagocytosis rate of the respective macrophages. The gating strategy is shown in the Supplementary Fig. S11A.

Detection of the affinity between CAR-M and the GPA33 antigen

One million THP-1 cells, each transduced with different lentiviruses (CAR1, CAR3, CAR5, and GFP-CON), were co-incubated with 20 nM purified 6xHis-GPA33 antigen for 1 h. Cells were then stained with APC-conjugated anti-His Tag antibody (362605, BioLegend), and the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) in the APC channel was measured by flow cytometry to assess the binding capacity of CAR molecules on THP-1 cells to the extracellular domain of GPA33.

Cytokine analysis

The ELISA kits Human IL-6 (EK106, LiankeBiotech), Human TNF-α (EK182, LiankeBiotech), Human IL-1β (EK101b, LiankeBiotech), and Human IFN-γ (EK180, LiankeBiotech) were used to detect the levels of cytokines in the supernatants of the co-culture system. All operations were carried out according to the instructions.

In vitro cytotoxicity assay

LS174T, T84, and SW620 cell lines were transfected with a luciferase reporter gene. SW620 cell line was additionally transfected to overexpress a truncated fragment of GPA33 together with luciferase. Stable transfected cell lines were selected with puromycin. Macrophages and target cells were co-cultured in varying ratios in an incubator for 24 h. D-Luciferin (40901ES, Yeasen Biotechnology Co., Ltd.) was added to 96-well plates for bioluminescence imaging. Bioluminescent signals were captured using an IVIS system (NEWTON 7.0, France) and quantified to calculate cytotoxicity.

Lysis (%) = [(Sample signal–Tumor only signal)] / [(Background signal–Tumor only signal)] × 100.

Xenograft tumor model in nude mice

Six-week-old female BALB/c nude mice were purchased from Gempharmatech Co., Ltd. and maintained in the SPF animal facility of the Animal Experimental Center at Lanzhou University. All animal experiments were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Second Hospital of Lanzhou University and were conducted in strict accordance with ethical guidelines. A total of 1 × 10^6 luciferase-positive T84 cells were mixed at a 1:1 ratio with Matrigel (354234, Gibco) and injected into the left axilla of each mouse. One week after tumor transplantation, the mice were randomly assigned to three groups. Tumor-bearing mice received weekly tail vein injections of PBS, 1 × 10^7 CON-Ms, or 1 × 10^7 CAR-Ms. Bioluminescence imaging using the IVIS imaging system (NEWTON 7.0, France) was performed weekly. Tumor volumes were recorded with an electronic caliper and were calculated according to the formula (L×W×W)/2. Mice were weighed every three days. When the longest tumor diameter exceeded 10 mm, tumor-bearing mice were euthanized, and tumor tissues and major organs were collected for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining.

Flow cytometry

All THP-1 cells and macrophages were subjected to Fc receptor blocking with Human TruStain FcX (422302, BioLegend) before staining. Macrophage markers were assessed using APC/Cyanine7 anti-human CD11b (301341, Biolegend) and APC anti-human CD14 (301808, BioLegend). Markers of M1 macrophages were detected using PE anti-human CD80 (375410, BioLegend), APC anti-human CD86 (374208, BioLegend), and PE/Cyanine7 anti-human HLA-DR (361611, BioLegend). Markers of M2 macrophages were detected using APC anti-human CD163 (326509, BioLegend) and PE/Cyanine7 anti-human CD206 (321123, BioLegend). For the detection of M1/M2 markers of macrophages, first, the macrophages in the co - culture system were isolated using CD14 magnetic beads (17858, STEMCELL Technologies), and then the isolated and purified macrophages were subjected to routine staining. Apoptosis of target cells in the co-culture system was evaluated using Annexin V-APC/7-AAD Apoptosis Kit (AT105, LiankeBiotech). The gating strategy is shown in the Supplementary Fig. S11B. Flow cytometry experiments were performed on BD FACSLyric (BD Biosciences), and data were analyzed with Flowjo 10.8.1.

Western blot, IHC, and IF

All experiments were conducted following standard procedures. When the colon cancer cell lines reached 50% confluence, total proteins were extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C, Beyotime). GAPDH (60004-1-Ig, Proteintech) and GPA33 (ab108938, abcam) were used as primary antibodies. For immunohistochemical (IHC) staining, GPA33 (ab108938, Abcam) was used in a 1:1000 dilution. For immunofluorescence, Hoechst (C0030, Solarbio) was used for nuclear staining, and Goat anti-Human IgG Fc (FITC) antibody (RS2304, Immunoway) was employed as the secondary antibody. The sections were photographed with a fluorescence confocal microscope (Leica, Germany).

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism 10.6.0 was used to perform statistical analyses. P values for ANOVA multiple comparisons were obtained using Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Unless otherwise specified, all t-tests were two-sided. All data are presented as means ± standard deviation (means ± S.D.). For all figures in the study, * denotes P < 0.05, ** denotes P < 0.01, and *** denotes P < 0.001.

Results

High-affinity GPA33 monoclonal antibodies based on single B cell technology

To obtain high-affinity scFvs targeting human GPA33, we employed a murine monoclonal antibody generation workflow based on single B cell sorting and single-cell PCR techniques. The flow chart is depicted in Fig. 1A. First, the extracellular domain (ECD) amino acid sequence of human GPA33 (Ile22-Val235) was used to construct a recombinant gene with an N-terminal 6×His tag encoded in-frame. This gene was synthesized and the protein was expressed in HEK-293 F cells. The recombinant antigen was validated by SDS–PAGE (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Following immunization of mice with the GPA33 antigen, serum titers were evaluated by indirect ELISA (Additional file 1: Fig. S2), and the mouse with the highest serum titer was selected for subsequent antibody sequence retrieval. Plasma cells from the spleen were isolated using a CD138-positive cell sorting kit, and flow cytometry showed that the sorted plasma cell population exceeded 90% (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). GPA33-specific antibody-secreting plasma cells were identified and individually sorted into PCR tubes using the CellCelector (Fig. 1B). Reverse transcription was performed on each sorted single B cell to amplify cDNA, and primers were used to amplify the variable regions of the heavy and light chains. The relevant antibody sequences are currently the subject of a patent application (patent application No.202511700515.7). Recombinant antibodies were then produced according to the methods reported in the literature [14].

Indirect ELISA demonstrated that several produced antibodies (ab1, ab2, ab3, ab5) exhibited differing affinities for the GPA33 antigen (Fig. 1C). Considering that the recombinant antigen might not fully recapitulate the native conformation of GPA33, we further assessed the binding of these antibodies to LS174T cells, which overexpress GPA33 (Fig. 1D), by flow cytometry (Fig. 1E). Ab1, Ab3, and Ab5 bound to cell-surface GPA33, whereas Ab5 showed no detectable specific affinity. To confirm antigen specificity, Ab1 was used to label colorectal cancer cell lines and normal colonic epithelial cell lines. Flow cytometry histograms (Fig. 1F) were consistent with GPA33 expression results obtained by immunoblotting (Fig. 1D), indicating that the monoclonal antibody is GPA33-specific. We also used lentiviral vectors to generate LS174T-sh cells with stably knocked-down GPA33 expression (Additional file 1: Fig. S4A) and 293T cells with stable overexpression of GPA33 (Additional file 1: Fig. S4C). The sensitivity of Ab1, Ab3, and Ab5 to changes in GPA33 antigen expression was assessed by flow cytometry (Additional file 1: Figs. S4B, D). The results indicate that Ab1, Ab3, and Ab5 specifically recognize both endogenous and overexpressed GPA33 antigen and are capable of detecting dynamic changes in expression levels. Additionally, live-cell immunofluorescence (Fig. 1G) and frozen-section immunofluorescent staining of colorectal cancer tissues (Fig. 1H) validated the ability of the recombinant monoclonal antibody to recognize GPA33 antigen in vitro and in tumor tissues. Taken together, these results indicate that the generated monoclonal antibodies specifically and effectively recognize GPA33 and can serve as sources of scFv fragments for constructing GPA33-specific chimeric antigen receptors.

Construction and selection of high-affinity GPA33-specific chimeric antigen receptor macrophages

We constructed scFvs from the variable regions of the heavy and light chains of high-affinity GPA33 monoclonal antibodies (Ab1, Ab3, and Ab5). Each scFv was fused to a CAR comprising the intracellular domain of FcγRI containing ITAM motifs and the intracellular PI3K-recruitment domain of CD19. These CAR constructs were cloned into a lentiviral vector and linked to eGFP via a P2A sequence (Fig. 2A).

To generate chimeric antigen receptor macrophages, we first transduced THP-1 cells with CAR-encoding lentivirus. The eGFP expression was assessed by flow cytometry, and transduction efficiencies of the three CAR lentiviruses and the control GFP lentivirus all exceeded 95% (Fig. 2B). After co-incubation with 20 nM His-GPA33 antigen, CAR1 (with scFv derived from Ab1) exhibited the highest mean fluorescence intensity(MFI), more than fivefold greater than that of CAR5, whereas CAR3 showed no binding to the His-GPA33 antigen (Fig. 2C). To evaluate the phagocytic capacity of the three CAR-modified macrophages, the induced macrophages were co-cultured with mCherry+ LS174T cells at a 1:1 ratio. Flow cytometric analysis demonstrated that CAR1-modified macrophages exhibited the highest phagocytic activity (Additional file 1: Fig. S5). Consequently, CAR1 was selected for subsequent experiments and hereafter referred to as CAR. AlphaFold3 structural prediction combined with PyMOL analysis indicated a strong binding interaction between the CAR scFv and GPA33. As shown in Fig. 2F, the two molecules form a molecular recognition network through multiple key amino acid residues, including electrostatic/hydrogen-bond interactions Glu32–Gly102 and Glu105–Lys163, and a hydrogen bond Asn55–Asp105, with distances of 3.03 Å, 2.78 Å, and 2.79 Å, respectively, collectively maintaining the stability of the protein complex. THP-1, GFP-THP-1, and CAR-THP-1 cells were treated with 50 ng/mL PMA for 48 h, followed by a 24-hour resting period to obtain the corresponding macrophages. Compared with pre-induction cells, the expression of macrophage markers CD11b and CD14 was significantly increased (Figs. 2D-E), confirming successful macrophage induction. These cells are hereafter referred to as UTD-M, CON-M, and CAR-M. Regardless of transfection with GFP-control or CAR lentivirus, THP-1 cells and PMA-induced macrophages exhibited no significant differences in proliferation capacity, cell cycle, or cell viability (Additional file 1: Fig. S6).

To evaluate whether the GPA33 antigen can specifically activate CAR-modified macrophages, we stimulated CON-M and CAR-M with tissue culture plates coated with BSA or GPA33 antigen. Flow cytometric analysis of M1 macrophage markers CD80, CD86, and HLA-DR showed that CAR-M displayed a significant upregulation of CD80 and HLA-DR following GPA33 antigen stimulation, whereas CD86 expression did not change significantly (Fig. 2H). This pattern was not observed in CON-M (Fig. 2G). We assessed the nonspecific phagocytic capacity of both macrophage types using 1 μm red fluorescent beads and observed no change under either BSA or GPA33 antigen stimulation (Figs. 2I-J). Notably, CAR-M treated with GPA33 antigen exhibited pronounced M1-like morphological changes: cells enlarged substantially, transitioned from a rounded to an irregular polygonal shape, and extended numerous pseudopodia (Fig. 2J), indicating that GPA33 antigen can specifically induce CAR-mediated M1-like polarization of macrophages.

CAR-M demonstrated pronounced antigen-specific phagocytic activity against GPA33-positive tumor cells in vitro

To assess the targeted phagocytic activity of CAR-M against GPA33-positive colorectal cancer cells, we transduced LS174T, T84, and SW620 cell lines with mCherry red fluorescent protein. Additionally, the SW620 cell line was transduced with a truncated version of the GPA33 antigen. CAR-M or CON-M were co-cultured with LS174T-mCherry cells at an E: T ratio of 1:1, and at various time points, flow cytometry was used to quantify the proportion of GFP/mCherry double-positive cells as the rate of phagocytic events. Flow cytometric results showed that CAR-M exhibited significantly higher phagocytic efficiency than CON-M at all time points (Fig. 3A), with the peak efficiency occurring between 1 and 2 h. After one hour of co-culture, the phagocytic rate of CAR-M reached 20%, more than sixfold higher than that of CON-M (Fig. 3B). Similar results were observed when using T84-mCherry and SW620-GPA33 target cells (Figs. 3C-D). A summary of the phagocytic efficiency of macrophages after one hour of co-culture is shown in Fig. 3E.

The pictures of co-culture systems photographed by fluorescence microscopy further corroborated the antigen-specific phagocytosis of CAR-M, and demonstrated that a single effector cell can endocytose multiple target cells. After 24 h of co-culture, lysed target cells were observed within CAR-M but not within CON-M (Figs. 4A-C). Moreover, when GPA33-negative SW620 cells were co-cultured with CAR-M and CON-M, no phagocytic events were detected (Fig. 4C), indicating that phagocytosis of CAR-M is antigen-specific. Interestingly, CAR-M in the co-culture systems also displayed features of M1-like macrophage, including increased cell size and extension of pseudopods to capture target cells. Concurrently, we performed confocal fluorescence imaging on the co-culture system of CAR-M and LS174T-mCherry, acquiring Z-stack images to observe the processes of CAR-M capturing, engulfing, and degrading target cells. The observations confirm that CAR-M can directly kill tumor cells via phagocytosis (Additional file 1: Fig. S10).

CAR-M mediates potent, contact-dependent tumor cell killing in vitro

To investigate the cytotoxic effects of CAR-M against GPA33-positive colorectal cancer cells, COM-M and CAR-M were co-cultured with target cells at an E: T ratio of 5:1 for 24 h, and apoptosis of GFP− tumor cells within the co-culture system was assessed by flow cytometry. In LS174T, T84, and SW620 cells engineered to overexpress a truncated form of GPA33, CAR-M induced a significant increase in tumor cell apoptosis, with a particularly pronounced elevation in late apoptotic signals (Figs. 5A-C). By contrast, CAR-M did not exhibit significant targeted cytotoxicity against GPA33-negative SW620-Vector cells (Fig. 5C). A summary of the proportions of apoptotic cells is presented in Fig. 5D.

We next conducted a bioluminescence-based cytotoxicity assay to evaluate the in vitro cytolytic activity of CAR-M. Luciferase-transfected LS174T, T84, SW620-GPA33, and SW620-Vector cells were used as targets, and luminescence was measured after 24-hour co-culture with CON-M or CAR-M at varying E: T ratios. Consistent with the apoptosis assays, CAR-M demonstrated potent tumoricidal activity that increased with higher E: T ratios, producing approximately 80% inhibition after 24 h at an E: T of 10:1. CON-M did not display appreciable cytotoxicity (Figs. 5E-F). The selective killing of SW620-GPA33 but not SW620-Vector cells confirms that the tumor cell killing by CAR-M is antigen-specific and CAR-mediated (Fig. 5G). Additionally, flow cytometric analysis of remaining viable tumor cells in co-culture systems maintained at an E: T of 10:1 for 24 h revealed a significantly greater number of remaining tumor cells in the CON-M condition compared with the CAR-M condition (Figs. 5H-I). Collectively, these results indicate that CAR-M can efficiently and specifically target and kill GPA33-positive tumor cells in vitro, exhibiting robust antitumor activity. Given the direct cell-to-cell contact in the co-culture system utilized, the cytotoxic effect of CAR-M on target cells may be achieved via two mechanisms: cytokine mediation and direct phagocytic killing.

To assess potential off-target effects, we co-cultured NCM460-Luci cells with CON-M and CAR-M, respectively, at various E: T ratios for 24 h. Cell viability was subsequently determined by bioluminescence assay, which revealed no significant inhibition of cell viability at any of the tested E: T ratios (Additional file 1: Fig. S8 D, F). Given the extremely low expression level of GPA33 antigen in NCM460 cells, we further investigated GPA33 expression in human normal gastric mucosal epithelial cells (GES-1). Western blot analysis confirmed that the GES-1 cell line expresses GPA33 antigen (Additional file 1: Fig. S8 A-B). Similarly, cytotoxicity assays performed on GES-1-Luci cells demonstrated that CAR-M did not exert significant cytotoxic effects on these normal cells expressing GPA33 antigen (Additional file 1: Fig. S8 C, E).

CAR-M displays an M1-skewed phenotype upon engagement with GPA33-positive tumor cells in vitro

One characteristic of macrophages is their marked plasticity. In the tumor microenvironment, macrophages are typically polarized toward an M2-like, anti-inflammatory phenotype that promotes tumor progression. Therefore, reversing this polarization is a critical strategy for reprogramming macrophages. To determine whether GPA33 on tumor cells can specifically skew CAR-M toward an M1-like state and thereby enhance antitumor activity, we used flow cytometry to assess macrophage phenotypic marker levels in the presence or absence of GPA33-positive target cells.

In the resting state, CAR-M exhibited markedly higher expression levels of M1-associated markers of CD80, CD86, and HLA-DR compared with control UTD-M cells (Fig. 6A). After co-culture with LS174T cells, the expression of M1 markers in CAR-M was further enhanced, whereas UTD-M showed no detectable enhancement of M1 markers regardless of tumor cell presence (Fig. 6B). Stimulation by tumor cells increased the expression of the M2 markers CD163 and CD206 in both UTD-M and CAR-M. However, CD163 and CD206 levels in CAR-M remained substantially lower than those in CON-M under both conditions (Figs. 6C-D). Additionally, we quantified proinflammatory cytokine secretion by macrophages in the supernatant of resting and co-culture states. ELISA results demonstrated that CAR-M secreted significantly higher amounts of IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IFN-γ than controls, irrespective of tumor cell stimulation, whereas cytokine secretion by CON-M was significantly lower and unaffected by the presence of the tumor surface antigen GPA33(Figs. 6J-M). Taken together, these data indicate that antigen-specific recognition via the CAR drives macrophages toward a functional state characterized by heightened M1-associated surface markers, suppressed M2 markers, and increased proinflammatory cytokine secretion.

CAR-M inhibits tumor growth in a xenograft model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of CAR-M against colorectal cancer, we established a subcutaneous xenograft model using T84 luciferase+ cells. One week after inoculation of 1 × 10^6 T84 cells, mice were randomized into three groups receiving intravenous injections of PBS, control macrophages (UTD-M), or CAR-M weekly, starting on day 7 (Fig. 7A). Compared with the PBS and CON-M groups, tumors in the CAR-M group exhibited significantly slower volumetric growth (Fig. 7B). Serial in vivo imaging demonstrated a marked reduction in tumor burden in the CAR-M group (Fig. 7C). Quantitative analysis of tumor burden at the end of the experiment indicated that CAR-M suppresses colorectal tumor progression (Fig. 7D). HE staining of tumor sections showed that T84 cells retained typical colorectal carcinoma histological features after transplantation, thereby providing a better model to evaluate the in vivo efficacy of CAR-M (Fig. 7E). Moreover, extensive necrotic regions were observed in tumors from the CAR-M group, whereas only small or negligible necrotic foci were detected in the CON-M and UTD-M groups.

To assess safety, we monitored mouse body weight throughout the study and observed no significant differences among groups (Fig. 7F). Post-mortem H&E staining of major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lungs, kidneys) revealed no apparent pathological damage (Fig. 7G). Furthermore, comprehensive serum biochemical analysis (n = 5 per group) showed no significant impairment of hepatic, renal, cardiac, or metabolic functions attributable to CAR-M treatment (Additional file 2).

Collectively, these in vivo results demonstrate that GPA33-targeted CAR-Ms exert potent antitumor activity and show no detectable systemic toxicity in an immunodeficient xenograft model. This supports the further investigation of this approach in more complex, immunocompetent settings.

To investigate the in vivo biodistribution, persistence, and tumor infiltration of CAR-macrophages, we engineered a CAR-Luci vector, in which the eGFP sequence within the original CAR construct was replaced by luciferase. THP-1 cells were transduced with CAR-Luci lentivirus to generate CAR-M-Luci cells expressing the luciferase gene. For comparison, CON-M-Luci cells were constructed using a control luciferase gene viral vector. The in vivo biodistribution of intravenously administered CAR-M-Luci and CON-M-Luci macrophages was subsequently assessed at various time points in a T84 subcutaneous tumor mouse model. Results demonstrated that CAR-M-Luci effectively migrated to the tumor site, with its accumulation increasing over time, and remained detectable in tumor tissue two weeks post-injection. In contrast, CON-M-Luci consistently exhibited no observable accumulation within the tumor tissue (Additional file 1: Fig. S9).

Discussion

Immunotherapy, particularly adoptive cell transfer therapy (ACT), has emerged as a research focus in oncology due to its high specificity and relatively low adverse effects. ACT encompasses multiple modalities, including tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TIL), chimeric antigen receptor T cells (CAR-T), and T cell receptor-engineered T cells (TCR-T), which enhance the patient’s own immune system to recognize and eradicate tumor cells and have demonstrated substantial potential [15, 16]. In hematologic malignancies, CAR-T therapy has achieved breakthrough progress and obtained multiple FDA approvals [17]. Although CAR-T cells targeting antigens such as CEA, CD133, HER2, and GCC have shown potential antitumor activity against colorectal cancer [18–21], clinical application of CAR-T in colorectal cancer still faces multiple challenges. These challenges primarily include the scarcity of tumor-specific antigens, the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment that restricts immune cell infiltration and function, intratumoral antigen heterogeneity, and immune escape mechanisms [18, 22, 23]. Additionally, severe cytokine release syndrome remains a non-negligible factor limiting the application of CAR-T therapies.

Recently, the emerging direction of employing macrophages as CAR carriers has attracted widespread attention. CAR-macrophages (CAR-M) have demonstrated considerable potential in adoptive cell transfer (ACT) therapies: they not only possess inherent tumor tissue infiltration capabilities but also directly eliminate tumor cells via phagocytosis, secrete proinflammatory cytokines to activate adaptive immunity, and remodel the immune microenvironment [24, 25]. CAR-M exhibits distinct advantages in inhibiting tumor-associated macrophage M2 polarization and promoting M1 polarization, thereby overcoming immune tolerance in solid tumors and enhancing therapeutic efficacy [25]. A recent CAR-M cell therapy targeting HER2-overexpressing advanced solid tumors—CT-0508—has shown favorable safety and feasibility [26], providing confidence for researchers to explore CAR-M applications in solid malignancies. Although CAR-M are receiving increasing attention for the treatment of solid tumors, their application in colorectal cancer has not yet been investigated and warrants further study.

Since its discovery, GPA33 has been a prominent target in colorectal cancer research due to its distinctive expression pattern—high expression in primary gastrointestinal tumors and their metastases coupled with restricted expression in normal tissues—making it one of the preferred targets for colorectal cancer–directed therapies. Antibody-, radiopharmaceutical-, and CAR-T- based strategies targeting GPA33 have already shown preliminary progress [13, 27–29], underscoring GPA33’s translational potential in colorectal tumors. In this study, we performed IHC staining on colorectal cancer tissues and other normal tissues. Consistent with previous reports, GPA33 is not only abundantly and continuously expressed in tumor tissues but also has limited distribution in normal tissues (Additional file 1: Fig. S7), highlighting its effectiveness and safety as a CAR target. Therefore, we conducted a proof-of-concept study on GPA33-targeted CAR-M using THP-1-derived macrophages.

Chimeric antigen receptors (CARs) are fusion proteins composed principally of an antigen-recognition domain, a hinge region, a transmembrane domain, and intracellular signaling motifs. The antigen-recognition domain is typically a single-chain variable fragment (scFv) or a natural ligand and is responsible for specific recognition of the target antigen, thereby determining the affinity of the CAR for the antigenic epitope [30]. Affinity is one of the key parameters governing CAR-T cell functionality and therapeutic efficacy. High-affinity CAR-T cells exhibit enhanced sensitivity when recognizing tumor cells that express antigens at low levels, conferring a marked advantage in antitumor activity [31]. Studies have shown that computationally designed high-affinity binding domains used to construct CARs targeting EGFR or CD276 not only promote CAR-T cell proliferation and secretion of cytotoxic cytokines but also increase resistance to cellular exhaustion [32]. Optimizing scFv affinity can further improve efficacy against tumors with low antigen expression and help prevent relapse due to antigen loss [33]. However, high-affinity CAR-T cells have significant limitations, notably an increased risk of on-target, off-tumor toxicity: CAR-T cells may attack normal tissues that express the target antigen at low levels, resulting in severe damage to healthy organs. For example, high-affinity HER2-directed CAR-T cells, despite potent antitumor activity, have induced fatal toxicities caused by excessive activation and targeting of HER2 expressed on normal tissues, manifesting as inflammation and cellular injury in HER2-positive normal tissues and, in some cases, cytokine release syndrome (CRS) [34]. Affinity modulation can optimize CAR-T antitumor efficacy while reducing nonspecific adverse effects [31, 34].

Unlike CAR-T, prior studies have shown that cytokine release induced by CAR-M is relatively mild, and severe CRS has not been observed clinically. This is closely related to its more controllable immune activation profile and predominantly localized mode of action [35]. Therefore, rather than avoiding nonspecific activation and potential cytotoxic risks, increasing CAR affinity to enhance macrophage target-antigen recognition and tumoricidal function is of greater importance. To obtain optimal targeting fragments, we employed a single B cell screening platform to isolate antigen-specific B cells from the spleens of GPA33-immunized mice and directly retrieved naturally paired heavy- and light-chain variable-region sequences by single-cell PCR (Fig. 1A). This approach avoids affinity loss or framework bias that can arise from conventional phage-display library screening and yielded multiple recombinant anti-GPA33 monoclonal antibodies with nanomolar-range affinities (Figs. 1C, E). We then used scFv domains derived from several of these antibodies as antigen-recognition regions to construct CAR molecules for macrophages. The CAR intracellular activation domain comprised an FcγRI ITAM sequence, capable of triggering macrophage phagocytosis and immunoregulatory functions, together with a CD19 PI3K-recruitment sequence to enhance macrophage engulfment capacity [36]. CAR-Ms were generated by transducing the THP-1 cell line with CAR-expressing lentivirus, and phagocytosis assays demonstrated that CAR1 macrophages, which possessed the highest antigen affinity, also exhibited the strongest phagocytic activity (Additional file 1: Fig. S5).

This study provides initial proof-of-concept evidence, from both in vitro and in vivo experiments, for targeting GPA33 with CAR-M in colorectal cancer models. Our findings establish the foundational activity profile of GPA33 CAR-M and support its further investigation as a potential therapeutic strategy. The antitumor activity of GPA33 CAR-M is demonstrated by the following findings: upon engagement with the GPA33 antigen, CAR-M exhibited a skew toward an M1-like phenotype (Figs. 2G, H) and potent, antigen-dependent tumoricidal activity (Fig. 5). This functional shift was underpinned by enhanced phagocytic capacity and increased secretion of proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, IL-1β, IFN-γ, and TNF-α (Figs. 3, 4 and 6). Notably, the upregulation of MHC class II molecules (HLA-DR) on CAR-M suggests a potential for enhanced antigen presentation, a feature that, if preserved in immunocompetent settings, could contribute to adaptive immunity [24, 37]. This localized activity of proinflammatory cytokines is associated with a favorable safety profile, as increases in cytokine levels were observed only within the tumor and not systemically [38]. Studies have shown that even when macrophages are prepolarized to an M1 phenotype in vitro, the high levels of proinflammatory factors produced upon in vivo administration are insufficient to cause systemic cytotoxicity [39, 40]. While cytokine networks are central to macrophage-mediated effects, our study in immunodeficient models primarily demonstrates their direct role in tumor cell killing. The localized antitumor activity observed in our xenograft model was associated with a favorable safety profile, as no systemic toxicity was detected (Fig. 7, Additional file 2). Collectively, these data validate the core functional premise of GPA33-targeted CAR-M and warrant its future evaluation in models that permit assessment of immune microenvironment remodeling and systemic immunomodulation.

While the antitumor activity and preliminary safety profile of CAR-M have been established in our preclinical models, their translational development for colorectal cancer faces several key challenges. Antigen expression varies markedly both within and between CRC tumors, and tumor cells may evade immune surveillance through antigen-loss variants [16, 41]. Studies have shown that GPA33 can be lost at the invasive tumor margin, while inhibition of WNT activity can induce re-expression of GPA33 antigen in GPA33-negative tumor populations [13]. This phenomenon reflects the instability of tumor antigens within the complex immune microenvironment, where their expression is readily perturbed by local paracrine signaling. However, the plasticity of colorectal cancer cells also offers new avenues for ACT therapies. For example, researchers have proposed combining WNT inhibitors with EpCAM CAR-T cells to enhance CAR-T cell recognition of and infiltration into tumors [42]. This approach also suggests a potential strategy for future GPA33-targeted ACT therapies, whereby co-administration of WNT inhibitors might augment the efficacy of GPA33 CAR-M.

Study limitations and future directions

While this study primarily focused on the preliminary validation of GPA33 CAR-M in potential colorectal cancer therapeutic strategies, it is crucial to acknowledge that the reliance on PMA-induced differentiated THP-1 cell lines as a macrophage model constitutes a clear limitation. Although this model facilitates controlled and reproducible investigations into chimeric antigen receptor (CAR) expression and function, it cannot fully recapitulate the complexity of primary human macrophages. Consequently, the observed phenotypes, particularly the results about specific cytokines and polarization trends, may not be directly extrapolatable to primary human macrophages. Future work should validate these critical findings in primary human monocyte-derived macrophages (hMDMs) and ultimately in patient-derived macrophage models, which is essential for assessing the translational potential of our approach. Furthermore, investigating the performance of these CAR-engineered macrophages in more complex in vivo tumor microenvironment models will be the next crucial step. The antigen-presenting capacity and adaptive immune activation ability of CAR-M will be further validated in future humanized mouse models or syngeneic immunocompetent models. Our study on CAR-M in colorectal cancer represents a significant step towards exploring potential solutions for colorectal cancer immunotherapy.
