Imputation of arterial oxygen partial pressures using pulse oximetry in surgical patients under general anaesthesia: A prospective cohort study
Authors: Mohamad F. El-Khatib, Marwan Rizk, Amro Khalili, Thuraya HajAli, Ibrahim El Mallah, Rasha Shreim, Carine Zeeni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and Aims:Monitoring oxygen saturation (SpO2) during general anaesthesia using pulse oximetry is mandatory, though it may not always reflect the actual oxygenation status. Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis remains the gold standard for measuring the partial pressure of oxygen (PaO2), but it is invasive and limited by complications. This study aims to validate five existing equations for predicting PaO2 from SpO2 and, if applicable, to derive and validate a new equation in adult patients undergoing surgeries under general anaesthesia.Methods:A prospective cohort study was conducted on adult patients undergoing general anaesthesia and requiring ABG measurements at several time points intraoperatively. Using SPSS software, a Bland-Altman analysis was performed to assess the agreement between derived and measured PaO2 values using five existing equations. Regression analysis was then performed to develop a new equation for predicting PaO2, which was validated in a second cohort.Results:During the initial phase, 150 ABG samples were collected from 83 patients. Bland-Altman analysis revealed weak agreements with all existing equations. A new logarithmic equation, El-Khatib’s equation (PaO2 = 10−25.6 × SpO213.9), was derived. The validation phase involved 150 ABG samples from 65 patients, demonstrating strong agreement with El-Khatib’s equation (systematic bias of 13 mmHg, limits of agreement: −189 to 214 mmHg).Conclusion:Five existing equations for predicting PaO2 from SpO2 revealed weak agreement in adult patients under general anaesthesia. El-Khatib’s equation demonstrated strong potential for predicting PaO2, providing a reliable non-invasive alternative for determining PaO2 in clinical practice.

INTRODUCTION

Ensuring adequate oxygenation during general anaesthesia is paramount to patient safety and successful clinical outcomes. As such, anaesthesiologists must meticulously monitor oxygenation status to prevent hypoxemia and its related complications.[1]

Non-invasive pulse oximetry and invasive arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis are two methods for assessing oxygen saturation (SpO2) and partial pressure of oxygen in arterial blood (PaO2), respectively.[2] Due to its non-invasive, continuous, and real-time nature, monitoring SpO2 via pulse oximetry has become a standard of care during anaesthesia.[2] However, relying solely on SpO2 for assessing oxygenation can be misleading, especially in situations where peripheral perfusion is poor, in the event of carbon monoxide poisoning or methemoglobinemia, or when there is a significant leftward shift in the oxyhaemoglobin dissociation curve.[3] In addition, SpO2 is considered an indirect measurement of oxygenation and does not provide direct information about the actual amount of oxygen dissolved in blood plasma. Furthermore, SpO2 tends to be less reliable at lower oxygen saturations, especially in the presence of ventilation-perfusion mismatch or shunting. PaO2 helps to detect early signs of hypoxemia, identify the degree of impairment in oxygenation, distinguish between hypoxia and other causes of low SpO2, guide ventilatory support, and ensure that vital tissues and organs remain well-oxygenated throughout the surgical procedure.[4] However, arterial blood sampling for determining PaO2 has multiple contraindications, limitations, and complications.[5] Besides being invasive, it does not provide a continuous measure of the patient’s oxygenation and needs expensive blood gas analysers that might not be readily available in low-resource settings. Additionally, several complications are associated with ABG sampling, including iatrogenic anaemia, infection, haemorrhage, arterial occlusion, pseudoaneurysm formation, air or clotted blood emboli, and pain.[5]

As SpO2 is non-invasive and continuous, deriving PaO2 from SpO2 becomes a valuable and practical goal. Several attempts have been made to derive PaO2 from SpO2, and relevant mathematical expressions have been developed (Rice et al.,[6] Pandharipande et al.,[7] Gadrey et al.,[8] Hill,[9] and Severinghaus-Ellis[10]). However, all of these attempts were made in patients either breathing spontaneously or receiving mechanical ventilation for respiratory distress or failure, and none of the published studies have attempted to derive PaO2 from SpO2 values in adult patients under general anaesthesia. Therefore, this study aims to validate five established and commonly used equations for predicting PaO2 from SpO2 (Rice et al.,[6] Pandharipande et al.,[7] Gadrey et al.,[8] Hill,[9] and Severinghaus-Ellis[10]) in a cohort group of adult patients receiving general anaesthesia and if applicable, derive and validate a new equation for predicting PaO2 values from SpO2 values in this specific patient population. We hypothesise that none of the existing equations are valid for deriving PaO2 from SpO2 values in adult patients undergoing general anaesthesia and that a new imputation equation is needed for use during general anaesthesia.

METHODS

This prospective cohort study was conducted between 5 March 2024 and 7 August 2024. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the American University of Beirut (Approval #: BIO-2024-0011; dated 13 February 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki (2013) and the Good Clinical Practice guidelines. Written informed consent was obtained for participation in the study and the use of patient data for research purposes. The study was registered on Clinicaltrials.gov before patients’ enrolment (Registration #: NCT06283914; dated 28 February 2024; URL: https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06283914). It consisted of two phases: an initial assessment phase and, if indicated, a subsequent validation phase.

Adult patients (age > 18 years) undergoing surgical procedures exclusively under general anaesthesia in whom measurements of ABGs were planned or obtained according to the necessity of intraoperative assessment and clinical management, and not for the sole purpose of the study, were enroled in the initial assessment phase of the study [Figure 1a]. Patients requiring life-saving surgeries, those already intubated and/or tracheostomised, or those with American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status V and VI, were excluded from the study.

Flow diagram of patient enrolment in the assessment and validation data sets

Upon arrival in the operating rooms and before induction of anaesthesia, tracheal intubation, and mechanical ventilation, routine monitoring was applied, including electrocardiography (ECG), non-invasive blood pressure measurement, and pulse oximetry (Radical-7, Masimo Corporation, Irvine, California, USA). A standard general anaesthetic approach was induced and maintained. All patients received lung protective ventilation using initially a tidal volume of 6–8 mL/kg of ideal body weight, respiratory rate to maintain end-tidal carbon dioxide (EtCO2) of 30–40 mmHg, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) of 40%–60% via oxygen and air mixture with a fresh gas flow of 3 L/min, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of 5 cmH2O. Anaesthesia was maintained with sevoflurane at a minimal alveolar concentration of 0.8–1 and intravenous fentanyl and rocuronium, as needed. Ventilatory support changes during surgery were left at the discretion of the anaesthesia team in charge of patient management to maintain an EtCO2 level at 30–40 mmHg and SpO2 greater than 95%, with no intervention from the research team. Whenever an ABG measurement was deemed necessary by the anaesthesia team, the SpO2 reading on the pulse oximeter was recorded in parallel with the collection of the arterial blood sample of 1 mL through either an arterial puncture or from an indwelling arterial line by members of the anaesthesia team. Values of SpO2 readings were recorded only after confirming adequate photo-plethysmograph waveforms on the pulse oximeter and strong agreement of pulse rates measured from the pulse oximeter and the ECG signal. Collections of arterial blood samples were made using a specialised ABG sampler kit (Pres-set Vacutainer System, Beckton-Dickinson, Plymouth, United Kingdom). Immediately after collecting the arterial blood samples, measurements of ABGs, including PaO2, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (PaCO2), pH, and SaO2, were performed using a Siemens blood gas machine (RAPIDPoint 500, Siemens Healthcare Diagnostics, Newark, Delaware, USA). This machine undergoes regular maintenance and daily ad-hoc calibration. Arterial blood samples eligible for data analysis were those collected at least 30 minutes after intubation, as well as 30 minutes after any change in mechanical ventilation settings to allow for stabilisation of blood gas exchange. In cases where multiple ABGs were drawn from the same patient during the same surgery, they were considered distinct data points if the SpO2, PaO2, or ventilatory support settings had changed. However, if these were similar, only the first eligible ABG was included in the data analysis.

During the subsequent validation phase of the study, a similar number of arterial blood samples was collected for direct measurements of PaO2 in a group of patients meeting the same inclusion/exclusion criteria and undergoing our standard institutional general anaesthesia protocol similar to the group of patients included in the initial assessment phase of the study [Figure 1b].

Patient characteristics, including age, gender, weight, height, BMI, smoking status, type and duration of surgery, surgery acuity, ASA physical status, and comorbidities (specifically cardiac and respiratory), were collected for all patients enroled during both the initial assessment and subsequent validation phases of the study. Continuous variables were checked for normality by using histograms and Q-Q plots and were reported as means and standard deviations or medians and interquartile ranges as appropriate. Categorical variables were reported as frequencies and percentages.

During the initial assessment phase of the study, Bland-Altman analyses were performed to determine the degrees of agreement (i.e. systematic bias and range) between values of PaO2 directly obtained from ABG measurements and those derived from each of the predicting equations of Rice, Pandharipande, Gadrey, Hill, and Severinghaus-Ellis using the relevant and appropriate SpO2 value determined by pulse oximetry. Then, a regression analysis was performed using the directly obtained PaO2 values from ABG measurements and the respective SpO2 values determined by pulse oximetry to derive a new logarithmic equation (termed the El-Khatib equation) that best fit the two variables. During the validation phase of the study, the second set of PaO2 values directly obtained from ABG measurements was compared to those derived using El-Khatib’s equation. The comparison was performed using the Bland-Altman analysis to assess the degree of agreement and validity of El-Khatib’s equation in predicting PaO2 from SpO2 values. All data analyses were performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS), version 29.0. (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp, USA) statistical software. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

During the initial assessment phase of the study, 150 arterial blood samples were collected from 83 patients [Figure 1a]. Patients’ characteristics and perioperative variables are presented in Table 1, as well as the ventilatory support at the time of arterial blood sample collection, and the ABGs values are presented in Table 2. The Bland-Altman analyses revealed weak agreements with wide systematic biases between the actual measured and derived PaO2 values using Rice (systematic bias of 128 mmHg with a limit of agreement range of −122 mmHg to 378 mmHg), Pandharipande (systematic bias of −53 mmHg with a limit of agreement range of −255 mmHg to 149 mmHg), Gadrey (systematic bias of 100 mmHg with a limit of agreement range of −121 mmHg to 313 mmHg), Hill equations (systematic bias of 60 mmHg with a limit of agreement range of −121 mmHg to 255 mmHg), and Severinghaus-Ellis equation (systematic bias of 113 mmHg with a limit of agreement range of −83 mmHg to 309 mmHg) [Figure 2]. Subsequently, statistical modelling involving the 150 arterial blood samples obtained during the initial assessment phase was used to fit a new logarithmic equation. The new resulting equation, referred to as the El-Khatib equation, was PaO2 = 10−25.6 × SpO213.9.

DISCUSSION

Our study shows that the established and commonly used equations (e.g. Rice, Pandharipande, Gadrey, Hill, and Severinghaus-Ellis equations) for imputing PaO2 from SpO2 values demonstrate poor reliability and accuracy in adult surgical patients undergoing general anaesthesia. Furthermore, our results confirm that a newly derived equation for PaO2 expressed as PaO2 = 10−25.6 × SpO213.9 and referred to as El-Khatib’s equation can accurately predict, with minimal systematic bias and a small agreement range, PaO2 from SpO2 values in adult patients undergoing general anaesthesia.

The findings of the current study are somewhat not surprising and expected to some extent. The patients’ population in the current study consisted of adult patients with relatively healthy lungs undergoing surgery and receiving protective mechanical ventilation under general anaesthesia. In contrast, currently available equations for predicting PaO2 based on SpO2 values were derived either from mixed patient populations or under different strategies for mechanical ventilation support.[678910] Hill’s equation was derived using PaO2 and SpO2 values from spontaneously breathing rather than mechanically ventilated patients.[9] The Severinghaus-Ellis equation, which is mainly a modification/improvement of Hill’s equation, also utilises PaO2 and SpO2 values obtained from spontaneously breathing patients with healthy lungs.[6] Rice et al.[6] derived their equation from patients with acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) who were receiving protective lung ventilation with low tidal volumes and higher-than-normal PEEP levels. Pandharipande et al.[7] derived their PaO2-SpO2 equation from a heterogeneous group of patients that included a mix of patients receiving either mechanical ventilation for general anaesthesia in the operating room or lung-protective ventilation for ARDS in the intensive care unit (ICU). Most recently, Gadrey et al.[8] derived their equation from non-intubated and spontaneously breathing adult patients. As such, and to our knowledge, our equation remains the only available equation for imputing PaO2 by using SpO2 values in adult patients undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia and controlled ventilation.

In large, prospective, multi-centre studies of mechanically ventilated ICU patients, Brown et al. showed that non-linear imputations of PaO2/FiO2 from SpO2/FiO2 values appear to be more accurate and better at estimating the degree of hypoxaemia from pulse oximetry, and they recommended against the use of linear imputations.[1112] In our study, we developed a non-linear imputation of PaO2 from SpO2 values that showed a high prediction accuracy. In 1,004 patients presenting with pneumonia to 17 emergency departments, Sanz et al.[13] compared the Rice and Ellis regression equations to PaO2/FiO2 measured simultaneously from ABGs. They showed that the Ellis equation was more accurate than the Rice equation for estimating PaO2/FiO2.[13] In contrast, our study showed that both the Ellis and Rice equations yielded weak agreements and wide systematic biases between the derived and directly measured PaO2 values. Our study included a smaller sample from a single centre and focused on adult surgical patients undergoing general anaesthesia, rather than patients with pneumonia presenting to emergency departments for respiratory distress. Recently, Ren et al.[14] conducted a study to develop a calculator utilising machine learning algorithms to impute PaO2 by using non-invasive SpO2 measurements from mechanically ventilated patients in a large database. They compared the accuracy of the machine learning models with that of previously published non-linear and log-linear imputation equations. They demonstrated that machine learning-derived equations outperformed previously published log-linear and non-linear equations in imputing PaO2 values.[71214] Although we did not utilise machine learning to derive our equation, we showed that our newly developed equation for imputing PaO2 from SpO2 values in adult surgical patients receiving general anaesthesia performed better than some of the previously published equations,[678910] and a possible future study would be to compare machine learning derived equations in adult surgical patients under general anaesthesia to our newly developed imputation equation.

In contrast to our study, several previous studies imputed PaO2/FiO2 from SpO2/FiO2 values rather than imputing PaO2 from SpO2 values.[67111213] Although PaO2/FiO2 may be a more clinically meaningful parameter, particularly in ICU patients with severe hypoxemia and/or with ARDS, imputation of PaO2 from SpO2 values remains of clinical significance in the set-up of healthy adult surgical patients under general anaesthesia without anticipated frequent or major changes in FiO2.

The strong and significant correlations between SaO2 and SpO2 values during the assessment and validation phases of the study confirm the accuracy of the SpO2 measurements analysed throughout the study.

The current study has several limitations. First, it is a single-centre study conducted at an academic medical centre where general anaesthesia procedures are standardised. Second, patients included in both phases of the study had relatively healthy lungs with SpO2 values ranging from 96% to 100%. Third, our patients’ population may lack apparent homogeneity due to a high proportion of smokers, a significant proportion of cardiac and pulmonary comorbidities, and inclusion of ASA class IV patients. Fourth, the findings of the current study may not be applicable to patients who are spontaneously breathing. Finally, the number of samples may be considered small, and our findings may need to be confirmed in a larger patient group.

CONCLUSION

Our newly developed imputation equation, El-Khatib’s equation, is more accurate and precise than previously published equations for imputing PaO2 from SPo2 in adult patients undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia and controlled ventilation.
