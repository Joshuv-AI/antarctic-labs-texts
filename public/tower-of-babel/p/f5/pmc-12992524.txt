Evaluating diaphragm motor response variability in electric and magnetic phrenic nerve stimulations during passive expiration
Authors: José Castro
Published: 2026

To the editor,

We read with great interest the recently published study by Chakalov and colleagues (Chakalov et al., 2025), in which the authors compared electrical and magnetic stimulation of the right phrenic nerve at the neck, with motor evoked potential of the diaphragm after transcranial magnetic stimulation. Contrary to the standard approach using supramaximal stimuli, the authors chose to analyze stimuli just above individual thresholds.

Although magnetic stimulation of the phrenic nerve has gathered some attention in the last years, and can be useful in certain ICU settings, particularly in the presence of central catheters that impede an adequate electrical stimulation of the phrenic nerve in the neck, the clinical usefulness of just above individual thresholds is not clear.

Additionally, the authors make some statements and report findings that are debatable and can raise some questions regarding the validity of their findings.

Foremost, the authors claim that magnetic stimulation does not require extensive operator training, which is a somewhat surprising statement. The International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology (IFCN), recently published guidelines for training in the practice of noninvasive brain stimulation (NIBS) (Fried et al., 2021). From it, one can understand that either Technicians or Clinicians, should undergo specific training to perform NIBS. This claim is even more striking when one considers that the diaphragm has a very specific motor cortex representation.

The authors also suggest that neuroplasticity changes influencing diaphragm function can be more sensitively identified using “individually adjusted stimulation intensities”. However, the authors fail to provide any physiological basis for this claim. Furthermore, the authors claim that “Fixed supramaximal intensities without prior threshold determination may obscure or falsely amplify the detection of neuroplasticity-related changes, as response amplitude and latency are influenced by stimulation intensity rather than solely by neural adaptations or modulations”. Supporting this claim, the authors cite (Koseki et al., 2022, Kricheldorff et al., 2022), however the stimulation intensities referred in these works are pertained with the stimulation used to induce neuroplasticity, and not to measure peripheral responses.

When using just above threshold intensities, the peripheral axons stimulated are the ones with larger diameter and closer to the source of the stimulus (Hennings et al., 2005). When the intensity of the stimulus is increased to supramaximal, all the available axons are stimulated. This is a well-established axiom of clinical neurophysiology. Claiming that low intensity stimuli would be advantageous in studying peripheral nerves in this context, would only be adequate if, somehow, the plastic changes induced would only affect larger diameter axons.

Regarding the results presented, it is highly unlikely that, in a young healthy population, a phrenic nerve CMAP after electrical stimulation, could not be obtained in more than one third of the individuals. This is even more improbable when considering the upper limit of stimulation intensity that the authors report of 200 mA. In several papers reporting normative values of phrenic nerve with electrical stimulation (Castro and de Carvalho, 2025, Vincent et al., 2019, Younis et al., 2022), the percentage of unobtainable responses ranged from 0 to 4%. Furthermore, supramaximal intensities reported are in the range of 10 to 60 mA (Vincent et al., 2019), which is the range we also see in our laboratory.

Finally, the authors reported that the mean latency of CMAP obtained by electrical stimulation was 5.44 ms. Indeed, in one subject (S20), the latency was 2.73 ms, while in another subject (S05), the latency was 3.71 ms. For an experienced neurophysiologist, these latency values are physiologically improbable. Considering the mean length of the right phrenic nerve of 23 cm from the clavicle to the diaphragm (Fochtmann-Frana et al., 2023), and the estimated conduction velocity of the phrenic nerve of approximately 55 m/s (Morelot-Panzini et al., 2009), the expected mean conduction time would be approximately 4.2 ms. However, one must consider the time needed for the neuromuscular transmission, around 1 ms, as well as the slower conduction velocities of the terminal unmyelinated part of the peripheral nerve. With this in mind, it is unlikely that phrenic nerve CMAP could present a latency lower than 5 ms. In fact, some authors propose that such small latencies should be considered an artifact due to co-stimulation of the brachial plexus (Younis et al., 2022). Considering that the authors report latencies below 5 ms in 44% of the CMAPSs obtained, caution is warranted, since these responses could potentially reflect artifacts due to co-stimulation of the brachial plexus.

In summary, the findings reported by Chakalov et al. should be taken carefully, such as “with latency showing greater consistency than amplitude across methods” or “The electrical phrenic nerve stimulation demonstrated the highest rate of response failure compared to cervical magnetic stimulation and transcranial magnetic stimulation of the diaphragm, making diTMS a superior technique for assessing phrenic nerve conduction”.
