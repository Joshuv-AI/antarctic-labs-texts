Extended versus simple cholecystectomy in stage I gallbladder cancer: a SEER-based competing risk analysis with external validation
Authors: Ziqiang Wang, Yangyang Xie, Xiaowen Li, Xue Song, Danwei Du
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe optimal surgical strategy for early-stage gallbladder cancer (GBC) remains controversial. This study aimed to compare the long-term survival outcomes of simple cholecystectomy (SC) and extended cholecystectomy (EC) in patients with stage I GBC.MethodsPatients diagnosed with stage I GBC between 2010 and 2015 were identified from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database and categorized into SC and EC groups. Kaplan–Meier survival analyses, propensity score matching (PSM), and competing risk models were applied. Competing risk nomograms were subsequently constructed and externally validated.ResultsA total of 902 patients were included, along with 33 patients from Hangzhou TCM Hospital as the external validation cohort. After 1:1 PSM, the EC group demonstrated lower cancer-specific death (CSD) rates at 1, 3, and 5 years compared with the SC group (1-year: 6.43% vs. 11.5%; 3-year: 12.5% vs. 24.4%; 5-year: 15.3% vs. 28.9%; P = 0.002). Subgroup analyses indicated that SC was associated with increased CSD in patients with T1b disease, grade II–IV tumors, and adenocarcinoma. Nomograms incorporating grade, T stage, tumor size, and chemotherapy accurately predicted individualized 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSD probabilities. The models exhibited strong discriminative ability (AUC values) and good calibration performance.ConclusionsCompeting risk analysis suggested that EC should be recommended for patients with T1b GBC, whereas SC alone may be sufficient for T1a disease. The proposed nomograms demonstrated robust predictive performance and may aid clinical decision-making.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04092-7.Keywords: Gallbladder cancer, Simple cholecystectomy, Extended cholecystectomy, SEER, Nomogram, Competing risk analysis

Introduction

Gallbladder cancer (GBC) is the most common type of extrahepatic biliary malignancy and is particularly prevalent in countries such as China, India, Japan, and other regions of Asia. It is strongly associated with gallstone disease and a history of chronic cholecystitis [1, 2]. The 5-year survival rate remains dismal at approximately 5%–15%, largely due to its aggressive biology and the absence of symptoms in the early stages [3]. Surgical resection remains the only curative treatment, and decisions regarding surgical extent and adjuvant therapy are closely dependent on tumor stage [4].

Stage I GBC generally carries a more favorable prognosis compared with advanced disease, as it has not yet invaded regional lymph nodes or adjacent structures. The two most widely used surgical procedures for stage I GBC are simple cholecystectomy (SC) and extended cholecystectomy (EC). SC entails removal of the gallbladder alone, whereas EC additionally includes regional lymphadenectomy, hepatic resection, or resection of the common bile duct.

Although there is broad consensus that radical surgery is essential in early-stage GBC, the optimal surgical strategy for stage I disease remains controversial. This is largely attributable to the lack of specific clinical manifestations and the scarcity of high-quality prospective evidence, despite guideline recommendations favoring EC [5]. Recent studies have suggested that SC does not compromise long-term outcomes in patients with T1b GBC [6–8], and that the surgical extent is often determined by surgeon experience [9]. Thus, robust analytical approaches using large-scale datasets are needed to clarify the relative efficacy of EC and SC in patients with T1b GBC.

In the present study, we employed competing risk models rather than conventional survival analysis to evaluate surgical outcomes in stage I GBC. This method effectively accounts for the influence of competing causes of death on cancer-specific survival. Given the rarity of early-stage GBC, patient data were obtained from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database.

Materials and methods

Patient selection

Patients diagnosed with gallbladder cancer (GBC) between 2010 and 2015 were identified from the SEER database. External validation data were obtained from Hangzhou TCM Hospital between January 2013 and December 2018. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathologically confirmed GBC; (2) stage T1N0M0 disease, defined according to the eighth edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system (T1 included T1a, T1b, and T1NOS); and (3) a documented history of primary tumor resection. Exclusion criteria were: (1) survival less than one month and (2) incomplete clinical or survival information.

For the SEER coding, “RX Summ–Surg Prim Site” codes of 30–60 were used to confirm complete resection of the primary tumor. The variable “RX Summ–Scope Reg LN Sur” with codes “none” or “regional lymph nodes removed” was used to determine whether regional lymphadenectomy was performed. The variable “RX Summ–Surg Oth Reg/Dis” with codes “none” or “non-primary surgical procedure to other regional sites” was used to identify whether additional tissue or organs beyond the primary site were resected. Based on these criteria, patients were classified into SC and EC groups.

Clinicopathological variables

Clinicopathological and treatment data were obtained from the SEER database, including age, sex, marital status, tumor characteristics (size, T stage, histological type, and pathological grade), and treatment-related factors (radiotherapy, chemotherapy, survival time, cause of death, and vital status). Patients were divided into two groups—SC and EC—according to the extent of surgical resection. Tumor size was stratified as ≤ 2 cm, 2–5 cm, and > 5 cm, and pathological type was classified as adenocarcinoma or other histologies for subgroup analysis.

Histological classification

Histological subtypes were categorized according to the ICD-O-3 codes in SEER. Adenocarcinomas included adenocarcinoma, NOS (8140; unspecified subtype of adenocarcinoma) and its variants (8141–8147: scirrhous, gastric-type, superficial spreading, intestinal, diffuse, solid, basaloid), papillary adenocarcinoma and related subtypes (8260–8263), mucinous/colloid adenocarcinoma (8480–8481), and signet ring cell carcinoma (8490). Non-adenocarcinoma histologies comprised adenosquamous carcinoma (8560–8570), squamous cell carcinoma (8070–8078), small cell carcinoma (8040–8046), undifferentiated carcinoma (8020–8022), and unspecified carcinoma (8010–8015).

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, whereas continuous variables were expressed as means with standard deviations. Baseline characteristics were compared using the Fisher’s exact test or Pearson’s chi-square test for categorical variables, and the Student’s t-test or analysis of variance for continuous variables, as appropriate. Overall survival (OS) and cancer-specific survival (CSS) were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, with between-group differences assessed by the log-rank test.

For the competing risk analysis, patients were classified into SC or EC groups. Cancer-specific death (CSD) was considered the primary endpoint, with other causes of death (OCD) treated as competing events. The three outcomes of interest were survival, CSD, and OCD. Cumulative incidence functions (CIFs) were calculated for univariate analysis, and intergroup differences were tested using Gray’s test. Multivariate analysis was performed with the R package cmprsk, applying the Fine–Gray proportional subdistribution hazards model to identify independent prognostic factors [10].

Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to reduce baseline heterogeneity between groups [11]. Matching was performed at a 1:1 ratio with a caliper width of 0.01, adjusting for age, sex, race, marital status, grade, T stage, tumor size, histology, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. Balance was evaluated using standardized differences (SD), with SD ≤ 0.1 indicating adequate comparability [12].

Patients were then randomly assigned to a training cohort (n = 631, 70%) and a validation cohort (n = 271, 30%). Based on independent prognostic factors identified from the competing risk model, nomograms were constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year CSD in the training cohort. The development process followed Zhang’s stepwise approach [13]. Model performance was evaluated by discrimination and calibration, with discrimination quantified using the area under the curve (AUC) and decision curve analysis (DCA), and calibration assessed by comparing predicted versus observed probabilities using 1000 bootstrap resamples.

Statistical analyses and data visualization were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Patient and public involvement

Neither patients nor members of the public were involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination of this research.

Results

Clinicopathological characteristics

A total of 902 patients from the SEER database and 33 patients from Hangzhou TCM Hospital were included as the external validation cohort. The patient selection process is shown in Fig. 1. Among the SEER cohort, 284 patients (31.5%) underwent EC and 618 (68.5%) underwent SC. Significant baseline differences were observed between the two groups in age, race, T stage, and tumor size (all p < 0.05). Patients in the EC group were more likely to be younger (40.8% vs. 31.6%), of non-White race (30.6% vs. 19.9%), and diagnosed with T1b disease (64.8% vs. 57.1%). In contrast, the SC group had a higher proportion of patients with unknown tumor size (40.8% vs. 30.6%).

Survival analysis

Before PSM, patients who underwent EC had significantly better OS and CSS than those in the SC group (p < 0.05; Figures S2A, S2D). In subgroup analyses, EC conferred superior OS in patients with T1b disease, whereas no difference was observed in T1a patients (p = 0.4; Figures S2B, S2C). For CSS, SC was associated with worse outcomes in both T1a and T1b subgroups (p < 0.05; Figures S2E, S2F).

After PSM, EC continued to demonstrate improved OS and CSS compared with SC (p < 0.05; Fig. 2A and D). In the T1a subgroup, survival outcomes were comparable between EC and SC (OS: p = 0.959; CSS: p = 0.625; Fig. 2B and E). In contrast, EC was associated with significantly better OS and CSS in the T1b subgroup (both p < 0.05; Fig. 2C and F).

Kaplan–Meier survival analyses of stage I GBC patients after PSM. A OS in the overall cohort; B OS in T1a patients; C OS in T1b patients; D CSS in the overall cohort; E CSS in T1a patients; F CSS in T1b patients

Competing risk analyses showed a lower cumulative incidence of CSD in the EC group (p = 0.002; Figure S3A). At 1, 3, and 5 years, CSD rates were 6.04%, 13.6%, and 16.9% in the EC group, compared with 12.9%, 25.3%, and 29.9% in the SC group (all p < 0.001; Table S1).

Subgroup analyses (Table S2) further indicated that SC was associated with increased CSD in patients with T1a disease, T1b disease, grade III–IV tumors, and adenocarcinoma (Figures S3B–I). In multivariable competing risk regression, SC remained strongly associated with CSD (HR = 1.80, 95% CI: 1.33–2.44; p < 0.001; Table S4).

Following 1:1 PSM, significant differences persisted in the cumulative incidence of CSD at 1, 3, and 5 years (6.43% vs. 11.5%, 12.5% vs. 24.4%, and 15.3% vs. 28.9%, respectively; p = 0.002; Table S1). Subsequent subgroup analyses again showed higher CSD rates in the SC group for patients with T1b disease, grade II–IV tumors, and adenocarcinoma (Table S3, Figure S4). Multivariate regression confirmed a strong association between SC and CSD (HR = 1.99, 95% CI: 1.38–2.89; p < 0.001; Table S4). No significant difference was observed between the groups for OCD (HR = 0.88, 95% CI: 0.64–1.21; p = 0.430; Table S5).

Univariate and multivariate analyses

A secondary analysis was conducted by randomly assigning all patients into a training cohort (70%, n = 631) and a validation cohort (30%, n = 271) to develop prognostic models. In the training cohort, univariate analysis was performed to evaluate 1-, 3-, and 5-year CIF values for CSD. Significant associations were observed between CSD and age, grade, T stage, tumor size, type of surgery, and chemotherapy.

These variables were then entered into the Fine–Gray proportional subdistribution hazards model for multivariate analysis. The results demonstrated that grade, T stage, tumor size, surgical procedure, and chemotherapy were independent prognostic factors for CSD in stage I GBC patients (Table S6).

Construction and validation of the nomogram

Based on the variables identified in the multivariate analysis, two competing risk nomograms were developed to estimate 1-, 3-, and 5-year probabilities of CSD for patients in the SC and EC cohorts. These nomograms allow clinicians to calculate total scores from individual prognostic factors, thereby facilitating personalized prediction of CSD at different time points.

The predictive performance of the nomograms was evaluated in both internal and external validation cohorts. For the SC cohort (Fig. 3A), the AUC values were 0.727, 0.712, and 0.694 at 1, 3, and 5 years in the training cohort; 0.804, 0.802, and 0.782 in the internal validation cohort; and 0.921, 0.842, and 0.868 in the external validation cohort (Figs. 3B–D). For the EC cohort (Fig. 4A), the corresponding AUC values were 0.663, 0.679, and 0.682 in the training cohort; 0.797, 0.713, and 0.695 in the internal validation cohort; and 0.738, 0.742, and 0.766 in the external validation cohort (Figs. 4B–D).

Calibration plots demonstrated excellent agreement between predicted and observed probabilities across both training and validation datasets (Figs. 3E–G and 4E–G). Notably, actual 1-year CSD events occurred exclusively in the SC cohort across all datasets (Figs. 3E–F and 4E–F). These findings support the high reliability and strong predictive capability of the nomograms.

To address the potential influence of missing data, a sensitivity analysis was performed by excluding patients with unknown tumor size or grade. The results remained consistent with the primary analysis (Figure S5).

Discussion

In this study, we analyzed 902 patients with stage I GBC from the SEER database and compared outcomes following different surgical strategies. CSS was chosen as a more reliable endpoint than OS, given that OS may be diluted by deaths from other causes (OCD) [14]. This is particularly relevant in stage I patients, who generally have longer survival than those with advanced disease, thereby increasing the likelihood of competing events [15]. To address this, we performed competing risk analyses. After 1:1 PSM, patients in the EC group had significantly lower CSD rates than those in the SC group at 1 year (6.43% vs. 11.5%), 3 years (12.5% vs. 24.4%), and 5 years (15.3% vs. 28.9%). Subgroup analyses further revealed higher CSD incidence among SC patients with T1b tumors, grade II–IV disease, and adenocarcinoma. Based on these findings, we developed competing risk nomograms to predict individualized 1-, 3-, and 5-year probabilities of CSD. The models, incorporating grade, T stage, tumor size, and chemotherapy, demonstrated promising clinical applicability. To our knowledge, this is the first study to compare EC and SC in stage I GBC using competing risk methodology and to construct predictive nomograms.

Surgical resection remains the cornerstone of curative treatment for GBC across all stages. Over the past two decades, ample evidence has underscored the necessity of resection, often extending to adjacent structures in advanced disease [16, 17]. However, defining optimal surgical management in early-stage disease has been challenging because of tumor heterogeneity within stage I and the lack of consensus regarding the definitions of EC and SC. Studies from the USA and Asia have evaluated outcomes of patients with T0, T1a, and T1b GBC. While SC is considered sufficient for T0 and T1a disease [9, 18–21], controversy persists for T1b, despite current guidelines recommending EC [5]. Notably, recent studies suggest that SC does not adversely affect long-term survival in T1b patients [6–8]. Several high-quality studies have reported that SC does not compromise long-term survival in patients with T1b gallbladder cancer, which differs from the findings of our study. For example, Rhodin et al. analyzed 950 T1b cases from the National Cancer Database and found no significant difference in overall survival between SC and radical resection [22]. Similarly, Kim et al.’s international multicenter study (237 T1b cases) demonstrated that the 5-year cancer-specific survival exceeded 90% in both groups, with no significant differences [5]. In addition, a recent multicenter retrospective study from Chile also reported comparable long-term survival between SC and EC [23]. These results suggest that SC may be sufficient for carefully selected, low-risk cases. However, most of these studies relied on conventional OS or CSS analyses, without adequately accounting for competing non-cancer mortality. In contrast, by applying a competing risk model, our study revealed that SC was associated with a significantly higher risk of cancer-specific death in the T1b subgroup, indicating that some patients may benefit from EC. Thus, our findings provide a complementary perspective to the existing literature and emphasize that individualized surgical decision-making for T1b disease should integrate both tumor characteristics and competing risk factors.

Our competing risk analysis demonstrated that SC was associated with higher CSD at 1, 3, and 5 years, particularly in the T1b subgroup. Unlike prior studies that primarily focused on OS and CSS, our approach incorporated competing events, providing a more accurate assessment of GBC-related mortality. These findings support more aggressive surgical strategies for T1b disease. Thus, in cases of incidentally discovered T1b GBC, an additional EC should be strongly considered.

We subsequently developed a nomogram for individualized prediction of CSD in stage I GBC. The model integrated grade, T stage, tumor size, and treatment, identifying factors most strongly associated with risk. These findings are consistent with prior studies. Wen et al. developed a nomogram incorporating age, histological grade, TNM stage, surgical method, chemotherapy, and tumor size to predict survival in elderly GBC patients, consistent with our results [24]. Groot et al. reported that T stage, nodal status, margin involvement, and tumor differentiation significantly impact prognosis [25]. Similarly, our multivariate competing risk analysis identified high grade, larger tumor size, T1b stage, and SC as independent risk factors for CSD. Although patients who received chemotherapy in our study had poorer outcomes, we emphasize that these patients often had higher-risk disease. Prior research by Bohan et al. demonstrated no survival benefit from chemotherapy in stage I GBC, explaining the relatively low use of chemotherapy in this cohort [26, 27]. Unlike traditional regression models, our nomogram provided an intuitive visualization of individualized CSD risk at 1, 3, and 5 years. Calibration analyses confirmed excellent concordance between predicted and observed probabilities. Importantly, actual 1-year CSD events occurred exclusively in the SC group, reinforcing the survival benefit of EC. The nomogram also exhibited strong predictive accuracy in external validation, supporting its clinical utility.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, as a retrospective study, imbalances in baseline characteristics were inevitable, with significant differences across several parameters. Although PSM reduced selection bias, residual confounding from unmeasured variables may have remained. Additionally, case reduction during PSM may have introduced instability in outcomes.

Second, another limitation of this study is that the definition of EC in the SEER database relies on surgical coding, which may result in the inclusion of patients who underwent only minimal lymph node dissection. Current guidelines define EC as cholecystectomy combined with hepatic resection of the gallbladder bed or adjacent organs with regional lymphadenectomy, without stipulating a fixed lymph node count. While some studies have suggested > 3 or ≥ 6 nodes as references for adequate staging, these are primarily quality-control benchmarks rather than definitive criteria for EC. As in prior SEER-based studies, our classification approach enables comparability across research, but results should nonetheless be interpreted with this limitation in mind.

Third, important variables such as detailed chemotherapy regimens, surgical techniques, comorbidities, and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and alcohol use) were not available in SEER, leading to potential misclassification. Moreover, because SEER lacks detailed information on perioperative complications and causes of death, patients with survival shorter than one month were excluded. While this approach is commonly applied in SEER-based studies to avoid misclassification, it may underestimate the true perioperative mortality rate, and therefore results should be interpreted with caution.

Finally, the relatively small sample size of the external validation cohort highlights the need for larger multicenter prospective studies to confirm these findings.

Conclusion

Competing risk analyses demonstrated that stage I GBC patients undergoing SC had higher 1-, 3-, and 5-year cumulative incidences of CSD, particularly in the T1b subgroup. These findings highlight the necessity of adopting more aggressive surgical strategies for T1b disease. The nomogram we developed accurately predicted 1-, 3-, and 5-year probabilities of CSD, showing excellent discriminatory ability and calibration. Prospective studies and randomized controlled trials are warranted to further validate these results and guide clinical decision-making.
