A pH-sensitive binding modality allows successful transferrin receptor-mediated transcytosis of a bivalent antibody across brain barriers
Authors: Philipp Kuhn, Sabrina Petralla, Fatemeh Dabbagh, Valentina Pegoretti, Walter Muranyi, Hiroshi Ishikawa, Horst Schroten, Roman Fischer, André Frenzel, Thomas Schirrmann, Markus Rehm, Christian Schwerk, Gert Fricker, Roland Kontermann, Gavin Fullstone
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTEfficient delivery of therapeutics to the central nervous system (CNS) is one of the major challenges in treating neurological diseases due to brain barriers, which prevent entry of almost all potential therapeutic agents into the CNS. Targeting receptors that induce receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) across brain barriers has long been heralded as a potential solution to this problem, but this approach has yet to deliver clinical improvements for patients. Here, we set out to identify and characterize bivalent antibodies against the transferrin receptor 1 (TfR) as mediators of RMT. We identified the antibody YU904-F06 (hereafter referred to as F06) that showed efficient transcytosis as a bivalent IgG in two independent in vitro models of brain barriers. Despite its high affinity at extracellular pH levels, we determined that F06’s binding to TfR was greatly reduced at lower pH levels expected during endocytic acidification. We postulated, with the support of a validated predictive mathematical model of RMT, that the pH-sensitivity of F06 allowed it to overcome the lysosomal degradation that has been previously reported for high affinity bivalent binders of TfR. Finally, we demonstrated that F06 could mediate the transcytosis of scFvs that target TREM2 or EGFRvIII as potential therapeutic cargos. In conclusion, we present a proof-of-concept antibody and rationale for the design of high affinity bivalent anti-TfR antibodies that effectively induce RMT by exploiting pH-sensitivity in binding.

Introduction

Diseases of the central nervous system (CNS) affect 3.4 billion people worldwide, but treatment possibilities are often limited due to a combination of factors.1 One critical factor under investigation is the poor access of potential therapeutics into the CNS due to the presence of brain barriers, including the blood-brain barrier (BBB) and the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB). These barriers tightly regulate the entry of molecules into the CNS and therefore are bottlenecks in the development of effective CNS-targeted therapeutics.2,3 The BBB acts as the main interface between the CNS and the periphery, with barrier function occurring at the level of the endothelial cells that separate the blood from the CNS. On the contrary, the BCSFB consists of largely permeable fenestrated vasculature with barrier function instead occurring in epithelial cells that separate the interstitial fluid of the choroid plexus from the cerebrospinal fluid.4

Therapeutics based on molecules of biological origin (e.g., proteins), are increasingly used for a variety of disorders due to their high level of specificity against biological targets, tuning of half-life and malleability in including multiple biological targets within the same molecule. However, the presence of brain barriers has severely limited their impact as effective treatments of neurological disorders.5

Receptor-mediated transcytosis (RMT) is a process whereby the binding of specific ligands to their cognate receptors on cells of the brain barriers can enable the shuttling of cargo across the cell by a tightly controlled vesicular transport pathway. Previously, it has been suggested that this pathway offers a route to permit active transport of therapeutics into the CNS by specifically targeting RMT receptors at the brain barriers.6 Although RMT was discovered in the 1980s, this so-called “Trojan horse” approach has yet to lead to effective clinical intervention. In recent years, a multitude of studies have suggested that RMT is much more complex than initially thought, with factors including affinity,7,8 avidity,9,10 pH-sensitivity,11 and steric arrangement12 affecting RMT efficacy. Indeed, seminal work from Yu and colleagues suggested that reducing the overall affinity of antibodies against the TfR, either by introducing mutations into the binding sites to reduce the affinity or by reducing the valency, was necessary for improving RMT.7,13 Reports from researchers at Roche have suggested that the key criterion is valency, as a brain shuttle based on monovalent binding to the TfR showed superior RMT compared to a bivalent molecule, which was instead redirected to lysosomal compartments.9,10 These studies resulted in a renewed interest in the development of RMT-inducing therapeutics and several molecules based on the reduced affinity/avidity effect have entered clinical trials (trontinemab, RO7121932, tividenofusp alfa and TAK594/DNL593). Contradictorily, the most advanced RMT-targeted therapeutic, pabinafusp alfa (IZCARGO®), is an immunoconjugate comprising a high affinity bivalent IgG fused to an enzyme14 that has been approved in Japan for treatment of lysosomal storage disease. Altogether, this makes for an unclear picture of how to develop an effective RMT-targeted molecule.

Here, we investigated whether a bivalent RMT-inducer could be developed and successfully identified bivalent IgG candidates against the TfR with transcytosis activity in two independent in vitro brain barrier models. We compared our molecules to monovalent and bivalent formats of the Brainshuttle™ (BSMono and BSBi, respectively), the RMT-inducing anti-TfR entity in the molecule trontinemab, which is currently in clinical trials for treatment of Alzheimer’s disease (NCT04639050). We further show that our lead candidate, F06, demonstrates high affinity at extracellular pHs, but a strikingly reduced binding at lower pH (5.2), potentially providing a mechanism by which it can escape lysosomal degradation despite its high affinity.

Materials and methods

Biopanning, screening, and production for TfR specific antibodies

For the discovery of TfR antibodies, YUMAB’s universal human antibody-phage library was screened for antigen-specific antibodies. This antibody library contains a theoretical diversity of ~ 5 × 1010 and was constructed from B cells from more than 80 healthy human donors. The chemically biotinylated (Thermo Fisher Scientific, A39257) extracellular domain (ECD) of the human TfR (R&D systems, 2474-TR) was used as antigen.

Then, antibody-phage selections were performed. First, the antibody-phage library was cleared from polyreactive or cross-reactive antibody-phage by incubation with Streptavidin and bovine serum albumin (BSA). Afterward, the biotinylated TfR antigen was added (50 nM) and incubated for 1 h at room temperature (RT) with the antibody-phage library. Target-specific antibody-phage were isolated via pull-down with magnetic Streptavidin beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 11206D). After washing of the beads to remove nonspecific or weakly bound antibody-phage, target-specific phage were eluted and rescued by E. coli infection, and propagated by M13K07 Helperphage packaging. The amplified phage were used for another selection cycle.

In total, four rounds of selection were performed. At the end of the third and fourth selection cycles, single colonies of the infected bacteria were isolated and used to produce soluble monoclonal single-chain variable fragment (scFv) antibodies.

These were then used for ELISA and flow-cytometry screenings to identify target-specific clones. Antibodies with best binding properties were selected for an in-silico developability prediction analysis using an in-house software tool developed by YUMAB. In brief, key characteristics of the test antibodies were calculated (e.g., pI, hydrophobicity, charge, post-translational modifications, length of complementarity-determining regions [CDR]), and compared to a reference dataset of more than 200 clinical post-Phase 1 antibodies. Test antibodies with features comparable to the reference antibodies (e.g., within the 90% quantile) were selected as leads for full-length IgG1 production. Antibodies with rare feature characteristics that were not usually observed in the reference antibody dataset (e.g., outside of the 90% quantile) were flagged and de-prioritized. For production of full-length IgG1, the antibody coding genes were cloned into mammalian expression vectors and used for the transient transfection of HEK cells. After 7 days of cultivation, culture supernatants were cleared by centrifugation and antibodies were purified by Protein A affinity chromatography. The purified proteins were quantified by UV/VIS spectrometry and purity and integrity was confirmed by SDS-PAGE and SEC-HPLC analysis. As antibody control, the Brainshuttle™ module of trontinemab, an antibody under clinical evaluation, was produced in a bivalent as well as monovalent format.

Cell culture

Immortalized human cerebral microvascular endothelial cells (hCMEC/D3) were grown on rat tail collagen (0.1 mg/mL; Roche, Cat# 11 179 179 001) coated flasks in a humidified, 5% CO2 incubator at 37°C, in Endothelial Cell Growth Medium 2 (ECGM2; Promocell, Cat# C-22011), supplemented with penicillin/streptomycin 100 U/mL. The cultivation and passage of human epithelial choroid plexus papilloma (HIBCPP) cells were carried out following previously described protocol15,16 using the DMEM/F-12 (1:1) (Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium/Nutrient Mixture F-12) (Gibco™, Cat#31330038) cell culture medium supplemented with 5 µg/mL insulin, 100 U/mL penicillin, 100 µg/mL streptomycin and 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). Incubation was maintained at 37°C with 5% (v/v) CO2, and the culture medium was refreshed every alternate day.

For the generation of BCSFB model, HIBCPP cells were seeded onto cell culture filter inserts (ThinCert™ 12 Well, Pore ⌀ 3.0 µM, Translucent, PET, Greiner Bio-One, Cat #665631) using an inverted model system, with a seeding density of 250,000 cells per insert. The cells were allowed to attach for approximately 24 hours. Once attachment was established, the filter inserts were transferred to their respective 12-well plates. These plates were filled with fresh medium on both the apical and basolateral sides. Subsequently, starting from the 5th day of culture initiation, the trans-epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) was assessed daily. Upon surpassing 100 Ω cm2 in TEER values (typically observed around 5–6 days post-seeding) of HIBCPP-grown cell culture inserts, the cultivation process was sustained using DMEM/F-12 (1:1) medium (Gibco, Cat#11039021) supplemented with 1% FBS and 5 µg/mL insulin. Following that, filter inserts demonstrating TEER values exceeding 300 Ω cm2 were selectively chosen for subsequent transcytosis assays.

In vitro pulse-chase transcytosis assay

Transcytosis assays were performed in the “pulse chase” mode as previously described.11 High density pore (1x108 pores/cm2 PET membrane filter inserts (0.4 μm, 24 mm diameter; Greiner Bio-one, Cat# 662640) were used. Apical chambers of hanging filter inserts were coated with rat tail collagen. hCMEC/D3 cells were grown to confluent monolayers (2x105 cells/cm2 for 10–12 days in ECGM2 medium. Filter inserts were incubated apically with 20 nM of the antibodies for 1 h at 37°C. The monolayers were washed 3 times and prewarmed medium was added to the apical chamber. Media from apical and basolateral compartments were collected at various time points (from 0 h to 6 h) and cells lysed with RIPA buffer. The content of the antibodies in each compartment (apical, basolateral, and cell lysate) was quantified by ELISA. Transcytosed amount was given as the amount in the basolateral compartment.

A pulse-chase assay set-up, initially described by Raub and Newton,17 may effectively mitigate experimental artifacts stemming from potential cell-free regions or uneven cell growth on the filter inserts as observed in conventional transcytosis design. So, for the BCSFB model, to perform the transcytosis assay based on the pulse-chase set-up, the receiver plates were initially subjected to overnight blocking with 2% BSA in Dulbecco’s phosphate-buffered saline (DPBS) at 4°C. Subsequently, these plates were prepared by introducing 0.8 mL of warm serum-free medium into the individual wells. In tandem, the filter inserts underwent assessment for TEER measurement. Only inserts exhibiting TEER values surpassing 300 Ω cm2 were chosen for the assay. Then, to prepare the culture inserts for the assay, both their apical and basolateral compartments were thoroughly washed with serum-free medium. Following this, a one-hour incubation in serum-free medium was conducted.

At the next step, 400 µL of 20 nM antibodies diluted in serum-free medium were added to the donor (basolateral) chamber of the cell-grown culture inserts. BCSFB culture inserts were incubated at 37°C for 1 hour (pulse). After incubation, BCSFB culture inserts were washed apically and basolaterally three times using serum free medium at RT to remove unbound ligands. BCSFB culture inserts were then transferred to a new 12-well plate, previously blocked overnight with 2% BSA, filled with 800 µL warm culture media. The cells were then chased by adding 400 µL of medium to the upper (basolateral) chamber of BCSFB culture inserts and then incubated for further 6 hours at 37°C (chase). At each 1 hour-interval, BCSFB culture inserts were removed and the samples of the donor (basolateral chase) and receiver (apical chase) compartments, in addition to the cell lysate (intracellular) were harvested and stored frozen for further quantitation.

Transcytosis quantification with ELISA

A 96-well plate (Greiner Bio-one, Cat # 655061) was coated overnight at 4°C with 1 µg/ml of the AffiniPure F(ab’)2 Fragment Donkey Anti-Human IgG, Fcγ Fragment Specific (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #709–006–098) in 0.05 M carbonate buffer pH 9.6. The plate was then blocked for 2 h with 2% BSA in PBS at RT and washed three times with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS. Samples from the transcytosis assay and standard curves of the antibodies were incubated for 1 h at RT, washed three times with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS before 100 µl/well of peroxidase-conjugated AffiniPure F(ab’)2 Fragment Goat Anti-Human IgG diluted 1:25000 (Jackson ImmunoResearch, #109–036–098) were added for 1 h. Following 3 washes with 0.05% Tween-20 in PBS, 100 µl 1-StepTM Ultra TMB-ELISA substrate solution (Thermo Scientific, #34028) prewarmed to RT was added and incubated for 15 min. After addition of 1 M H2SO4, antibody-receptor complexes were detected by measuring the OD of the plate at 450 nm. All results were subjected to statistical analysis by using asymmetric sigmoidal, 5PL, X is log(concentration) analysis (Graphpad Prism version 8).

Binding ELISA to human and murine TfR

Streptavidin was diluted in PBS and immobilized to 96-well ELISA plates (2 µg/ml, 100 µl/well). After washing and blocking of the ELISA wells, biotinylated human (Acro Biosystems, TFR-H82E5) or murine TfR (Acro Biosystems, TFR-M82E3) was added (1 µg/ml in 2% BSA-PBST) and captured for 1 h at RT to the ELISA plate. After washing of the ELISA wells, an antibody concentration series was added for 1 h at RT. After another washing cycle, bound IgG were detected with a secondary horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-human Fc specific antibody (Sigma Aldrich, A0170) for 1 h at RT before TMB staining and absorbance readings were performed (see above) and used for EC50 calculations.

Affinity and avidity measurement by bio-layer interferometry

The binding kinetics of the antibodies were measured by biolayer interferometry (BLI), using an Octet Qke instrument. For the affinity measurement, the antibodies (5 µg/ml) were loaded on FAB2G sensors for 5 min. After baseline measurement in assay buffer, the antibody-loaded sensors were transferred into a TfR concentration series to measure the antigen association profile for 5 min. Then, the sensors were transferred into assay buffer to measure the antigen dissociation for 5 min. The association and dissociation profiles of the different antigen concentrations were fitted with a global 1:1 binding model to calculate the binding kinetics and the affinity (Data Analysis HT, version 11). For the measurement of the avidity, the setup was inverted. First, biotinylated TfR (5 µg/ml) was loaded on Streptavidin sensors for 5 min before the association profiles of the antibody concentration series were measured (5 min). Afterward, the dissociation of the antibodies from the sensor was measured for 5 min in assay buffer.

pH sensitivity measurement

Streptavidin was diluted in PBS and immobilized to 96-well ELISA plates (2 µg/ml, 100 µl/well). After washing and blocking of the ELISA wells, biotinylated human TfR (Acro Biosystems, TFR-H82E5) was added (1 µg/ml in 2% BSA-PBST) and captured to the ELISA plate. After washing of the ELISA wells, an antibody concentration series was added for 1 h at RT. The dilutions were prepared either in PBS buffer of pH 7.4, in citrate buffer of pH 5.2 or pH 4.0. Detection of bound IgG and TMB staining were performed as described above.

Computational modelling

Full details of the implementation and parameterization of the computational models of antibody RMT are given in the supplementary materials.

Generation of cargo-loaded antibodies

A ~30 kDa cargo protein (specifically, a TREM2-specific scFv antibody) was genetically fused C-terminally to one of the Fc chains of an IgG molecule. Correct heterodimerization of the Fc regions was promoted by using knob-into-hole mutations in the CH3 (knob Fc: S354C, T366W. hole Fc: Y349C, T366S, L368A, Y407V). Both Fab regions of the IgG part contained the TfR-specific VH/VL domains of F06 or COR101, a SARS-CoV-2 specific antibody as a negative control. This cargo-loaded IgG format is referred in the results as the 2 + 1 cargo format.

In parallel, a format with monovalent TfR binding valency was designed, referred to as 1 + 1 cargo format. Here, one of the Fab domains of an IgG molecule was exchanged with an scFv protein specific for epidermal growth factor receptor vIII (EGFRvIII) (~30 kDa). Again, knob-into-hole mutations were used to promote correct heterodimerization of the Fc. For the TfR-specific Fab domain, F06, the Brainshuttle (positive control), or COR101 (negative control) was used.

The combination of a TREM2-specific scFv antibody into the 1 + 1 format and the inclusion of an scFv against the EGFRvIII in the 2 + 1 format showed poor production yields and were therefore omitted from subsequent proof-of-concept studies.

Characterisation of HIBCPP and hCMEC/D3 cell model systems

Methods for the characterization of the HIBCPP and hCMEC/D3 cells are included in the supplementary materials.

Statistical analysis

All statistical testing was performed with GraphPad Prism version 8 except 3-way ANOVA, which was performed using the ‘anovan’ function in MATLAB 2024b (The MathWorks Inc., Natick, Massachusetts). All ELISA and flow cytometry binding curves were fitted using asymmetric sigmoidal, 5PL, X is log(concentration) analysis. For normalized ELISA binding data, the top plateau was constrained to 100%, except for pH-sensitive ELISA where the top plateau was constrained to ≤ 100%. Initial antibody screening in the HIBCPP model was performed using control of false-discovery rate (FDR) by the Benjamini, Krieger, and Yekutieli method,18 with the FDR set to 0.1. All other significance tests are specified in the respective figure legend.

Results

Discovery of anti-TfR antibodies

We set out to discover and select for novel antibodies that could induce RMT across brain barriers into the CNS. For this, we chose the highly studied and well-validated RMT target receptor TfR. We performed biopanning against the recombinant human TfR protein using YUMAB’s universal human antibody library, which contains a diversity of ~5 ×1010 and was constructed from B cells from more than 80 healthy human donors. Identified individual clones were then followed up by monoclonal screening for target-specific binding by ELISA (Supplementary Figure S1A). DNA sequence analysis revealed a total of 41 antibody clones with unique CDR sequences that bound the recombinant human TfR protein and to cellular displayed human TfR on transfected HEK cells (Supplementary Figure S1B-C). Among those, 37 clones were species cross-reactive to the murine TfR when displayed on HEK cells (Supplementary Figure S1B). Antibodies with best binding and predicted in silico developability features were produced as full-length human IgG1.

Candidate antibody F06 shows the strongest transcytosis potential

To functionally select our antibodies, we first evaluated the IgG antibodies for transcytosis activity in a novel model of the blood-cerebrospinal fluid barrier (BCSFB) using human choroid plexus papilloma epithelial cells (HIBCPP). While traditional transcytosis assays have focused on endothelial cells of the blood-brain barrier cells, commercial human blood-brain barrier cell lines, such as hCMEC/D3 cells, show poor in vitro barrier properties, necessitating the use of assays that reduce the confounding effects of passive paracellular transport.11 Importantly, the HIBCPP BSCFB model showed far superior barrier function than hCMEC/D3 cells and negligible nonspecific paracellular transport (Supplementary Figures S2A & B, S3A & B). We also demonstrated that HIBCPP cells express human TfR in high abundance (Supplementary Figure S2C & D) and a HIBCPP BCSFB model can transcytose the monovalent Brainshuttle (BSMono) positive control by a form of active transport (see Supplementary Figure S2E). Furthermore, we were able to verify that the HIBCPP BCSFB model shows far superior transcytosis capability of the BSMono control in a monovalent format, as compared to a bivalent format (BSBi), in line with previous studies,9 despite the expected superior binding of the BSBi (Supplementary Figure S2E & F). Furthermore, our data show broad agreement of the HIBCPP and hCMEC/D3 model in transcytosis assays (Spearman’s Rho = 0.825, see Supplementary Figure S2G), demonstrating that the HIBCPP BCSFB model can be used to evaluate human transcytosis activity without the confounding effects of paracellular flux.

From the tested IgG antibodies, nine clones showed notable transcytosis in the HIBCPP model (Figure 1A). For five antibodies, a higher transcytosis was measured as compared to the monovalent Brainshuttle BSMono reference antibody with one antibody, F06, identified as a hit with statistically improved transcytosis-capability. The lead antibody (F06) was also validated in a pulse-chase assay using the hCMEC/D3 BBB model (Figure 1B, general hCMEC/D3 model characterization in Supplementary Figure S3). In hCMEC/D3 cells, the lead antibody showed comparable transcytosis to the BSMono and showed a trend of higher transcytosis than the bivalent BSBi antibody. F06 shows species cross-reactivity

We next set out to evaluate the cross-reactivity of our lead candidate to the murine TfR. First, the human and murine TfR proteins were immobilized to ELISA plates and binding of the antibody was measured and quantified by EC50 calculation (Figure 2A). ELISA binding data revealed species cross-reactive binding of F06 to human and mouse TfR. In comparison, the Brainshuttle showed only binding specificity to human TfR, in line with previous studies of the Brainshuttle (Figure 2B). F06 has a higher affinity than the Brainshuttle

Previous studies have indicated that a reduced, intermediate affinity is required for effective transcytosis of bivalent antibodies. As our antibody showed improved transcytosis against the Brainshuttle, we therefore measured the binding affinity of our lead candidate antibody to TfR by BLI and compared it to BSBi (Figure 3A–B). Contrary to our expectations, the F06 antibody showed a significantly higher affinity (KD) and a lower dissociation constant (kdis) as compared to the Brainshuttle (Figure 3C), suggesting that the reduced affinity is not an absolute requirement for inducing RMT with a bivalent molecule. F06 shows high pH-sensitivity in binding

During RMT, molecules are trafficked via the endosomal pathway, which is associated with progressive acidification.19 pH-sensitivity has been reported to be of advantage for efficient RMT by allowing evasion of late endosomal/lysosomal degradation and successful redirection to transcytotic routes.11 Given that our lead candidate F06 shows high affinity and high transcytosis, we next investigated if it shows any difference in pH-sensitive binding as compared to the Brainshuttle. Hereby, TfR was immobilized to ELISA plates. Thereafter, binding of an antibody concentration series at different pHs was measured. F06 showed strong pH sensitivity (Figure 4A). At pH 7.4, reflecting conditions of binding to extracellular TfR, we demonstrated strong binding for F06. However, after shifting the pH to 5.2, reflecting acidification during endosomal transport, binding to TfR was strikingly reduced. At pH 4.0, no TfR binding was detectable. In contrast, we found that the Brainshuttle was less sensitive to pH change, with only a moderate decrease in TfR-binding between pH 7.4 and pH 5.2 (Figure 4B); a notable drop-off in binding efficiency was observed only at pH 4.0. As reported by others,20 we were unable to reliably determine kinetic parameters for F06 and Brainshuttle at reduced pH by BLI (discussed in the supplementary information). Taken together, this suggests that our lead candidate F06 molecule may escape lysosomal degradation by its strong pH-dependent reduction in affinity, which was notably much stronger than for the Brainshuttle. A data-driven predictive mathematical model demonstrates the importance of pH-sensitivity in F06 transcytosis

While pH-sensitivity has been previously suggested to allow improved RMT of antibodies against transferrin,11 a full mechanistic study of the role pH plays in RMT sorting has, to the best of our knowledge, never been performed. Therefore, we next set out to test the viability of our hypothesis by applying data-driven mechanistic mathematical modeling to elucidate the role of affinity, avidity and pH-sensitivity in RMT. Hereby, we first established a mathematical model of antibody RMT, incorporating receptor-binding, endocytosis, endosomal sorting, recycling, lysosomal degradation and transcytosis (see ‘Supporting Information for Modelling’ in Supplementary Materials for a full description). To mimic affinity-dependent sorting, we applied a stochastic competitive process whereby if the antibody disengages the receptor (based on kdis) before a time-dependent sorting step, then the antibody undergoes transcytosis. We parameterized the model to resemble the hCMEC/D3 cell culture filter insert setup by using absolute quantification of TfR on hCMEC/D3 cells (Supplementary Figure S2D) and the measured affinity of TfR to immobilized BSBi (Figure 3C). To identify unknown parameters, we applied a parameter estimation using time-course quantitative transcytosis assay of BSMono in hCMEC/D3 cells as training data (Figure 5A). Integration of the best fit parameters into the model recapitulated the experimental dynamics of BSMono transcytosis (Figure 5B). To validate the predictive capabilities of our model, we next used it to predict the transcytosis efficiency of BSBi. Hereby, all parameters were left identical to that of BSMono except the binding kinetics of BSBi to TfR, which were adjusted to account for avidity effects as measured by BLI (Figure 5Ci/iii). Measurement of avidity (Figure 5), as compared to affinity (Figure 3), was performed by switching the immobilized target protein between TfR (avidity) and anti-TfR (affinity) antibodies and probing with varying concentrations of the partner, as outlined in detail in the methods section. Notably, we found that the model predicted a vastly reduced transcytosis efficiency of BSBi compared to BSMono, with almost all BSBi retained in cells (Figure 5D). This stark reduction in transcytosis efficiency of BSBi aligns well to previous quantitative studies of BSBi in hCMEC/D3 cells, thus validating the predictive capability of our model.9 Next, we applied our validated model to investigate the role that pH plays in the transcytosis of F06. Hereby, we first parameterized the model with the binding kinetics of F06 to TfR based on BLI measurements (Figure 5C ii-iii). Thereafter, we used different kdis kinetic parameters for endosomal sorting to establish models without (same kdis as measured by BLI) and with pH-sensitivity (higher kdis to reflect differences in ELISA binding at different pHs (Figure 4A), details provided in Supplementary Material). Strikingly, we found that the model without pH-sensitivity predicted a poor efficiency of transcytosis, similar to BSBi (Figure 5E). On the contrary, in the model with pH-sensitivity, F06 was predicted to be highly efficiently transcytosed (Figure 5F). Taken together, our validated predictive mathematical model supports our hypothesis that the pH-sensitivity of F06 is integral for its efficient transcytosis and allows it to escape affinity/avidity-dependent late endosomal/lysosomal sorting.

Efficient transcytosis of F06 with mock cargo

Having established and characterized F06 as our lead candidate, we finally tested if it could be used to transport a potential therapeutic effector cargo molecule across brain barrier models. Simultaneously, we evaluated how our initial lead candidate F06 antibody would perform in bivalent and monovalent anti-TfR formats. Hereby, we produced our candidate in two bispecific formats. Firstly, we fused a TREM2-specific scFv antibody (~30 kDa) C-terminally to one of the Fc chains of an IgG, hereafter referred to as the 2 + 1 format (Figure 6A). Secondly, we exchanged one of the Fab domains of an IgG with a scFv from the huMR1.1 antibody, specific for variant III of EGFRvIII, hereafter referred to as the 1 + 1 format (Figure 6B). Correct heterodimerization of the different Fc was promoted by disulfide-stabilized knob-into-hole mutations of the CH3 domain. As controls, the 1 + 1 format was used with the BSMono Fab (positive control) and a SARS-CoV-2 specific antibody (COR101, negative control) was used in both bispecific formats. We used these cargo-loaded antibodies to perform a detailed study in the hCMEC/D3 BBB model (Figure 6C). The best transcytosis was achieved using the bivalent 2 + 1 format of our lead candidate F06. Notably, the transcytosis level was reduced using the monovalent format of F06 (1 + 1), likely due to the decreased avidity in binding. Importantly, no notable transcytosis was observed using the negative control COR101, regardless of the format, demonstrating the specificity of the CNS-targeting moieties. Finally, we also verified the transcytosis of the 2 + 1 F06 format in the HIBCPP BCSFB model (Figure 6D). While a slight drop-off in performance was observed compared to the original IgG, it still showed comparable transcytosis activity to the BSMono 1 + 1 positive control and higher transcytosis than the COR101 negative control. Taken together, this shows that bivalent anti-TfR antibody F06 can successfully transcytosis potential therapeutic cargo across brain barriers. Discussion

The efficient delivery of therapeutics into the CNS by RMT has long been heralded as a game changer in the effective treatment of a wide variety of neurological disorders.6,21 The first approval of an RMT-inducer, IZCARGO®/pabinafusp alfa (approved in Japan in 2021), suggests we are closer than ever to realizing this potential, with other therapeutics based on alternative RMT-inducing technology also advancing in clinical trials including trontinemab (BRAINSHUTTLE technology), RO7121932 (BRAINSHUTTLE technology), ALIA-1758 (Modular Delivery [MODEL] technology), tividenofusp alfa (transport vehicle [TV] technology) and TAK594/DNL593 (TV technology). However, fundamental questions remain as to the optimal design principles required to achieve efficient, robust, and universal RMT-inducers that can be routinely used in clinical settings. Here, we discovered the anti-TfR antibody, F06, a high-affinity, bivalent, pH-sensitive molecule that shows high transcytosis in two independent models of brain barriers, as both an IgG and in a therapeutic cargo format. Additionally, it was validated as a potential therapeutic fusion molecule for carrying therapeutic cargo, in this case an anti-TREM2 scFv.

To initially select F06, we applied an in vitro BCSFB model using HIBCPP cells. One of the key challenges in the discovery of RMT-inducing antibodies is in choosing an appropriate model for initial selection that balances robustness, reproducibility, throughput, and cost.22 For this, in vitro 2D cellular models are commonly used as they provide rapid, reproducible, inexpensive systems to carry out medium throughput studies to narrow down potential candidates. However, while human in vitro models of the BBB would be the optimal solution and are available, as of yet, commercial BBB cell lines, such as hCMEC/D3, show relatively poor physical barrier properties when grown in classical cell culture filter insert cultures.23,24 This necessitates the use of methodology to limit the confounding effects of paracellular flux. For example, Sade and colleagues demonstrated that a pulse-chase strategy, involving an initial incubation with the antibody (the pulse) before washing and collection of cell-released antibody over time (the chase), could be effectively used to help mitigate paracellular flux in a BBB model based on the hCMEC/D3 cell line.11 Alternatively, several strategies have been implemented to improve barrier function in in vitro models, ranging from using induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs), to co-culture with other cell types, to more sophisticated spheroid and micro-fluidic systems.23 These alternative strategies, while effective, are often more laborious and costly, and can introduce other confounding factors that make quality control and the determination of transport efficiency more complicated. The HIBCPP model used in this study demonstrated moderate to high TEER values of ~600 Ω·cm,2 comparable to levels seen in more sophisticated BBB models such as primary bovine brain microvascular endothelial cells or co-cultures.25–27 This allowed us to confidently screen initial candidate antibodies for transcytosis activity. Notably, when we validated our hits in a second in vitro model, using hCMEC/D3 cells with the pulse-chase protocol,11 we found good concordance between the two models. Interestingly, hCMEC/D3 cells showed generally higher levels of transcytosis (~2-fold), as expected for a BBB cell line, but also generally higher levels of variance that could be attributed to the expected higher levels of paracellular flux with this model.

From our functional selection, we identified a lead candidate, F06, that showed strong transcytosis in both the HIBCPP BCSFB and hCMEC/D3 BBB models. Next, we demonstrated that F06 also has good cross-reactivity to the mouse TfR. Many RMT-inducers show poor species cross-reactivity, as also demonstrated for the Brainshuttle, requiring the use of surrogate molecules,9,28,29 rodent models with human TfR or human-mouse chimeric TfR,14,30,31 or the use of higher primates in preclinical animal studies.14,30–33 Therefore, the cross-reactivity of F06 offers a strong opportunity to perform future in vivo work without these requirements.

We next demonstrated that F06 possessed higher affinity for TfR than the BSBi. Several studies have suggested that high affinity bivalent anti-TfR binders are sorted for lysosomal degradation, while reduced affinity binders are more efficiently transcytosed.7–9 Therefore, it was somewhat surprising that our lead candidate demonstrated a generally high affinity and high transcytosis capability. The exact sorting mechanism for transcytosis is still to be fully elucidated. However, studies using co-localization with endosomal markers, subcellular fractionation, and/or live microscopy have shed some light on the mechanism of transcytosis. Importantly, in one such study by Haqqani and colleagues, a high affinity variant (5 nM) of the antibody OX26, and indeed the TfR receptor itself was shown to heavily co-localize with late endosomal and lysosomal markers, while lower affinity variants (76 nM and 108 nM) with higher levels of transcytosis were predominantly located in early endosomal compartments, suggesting an affinity-related intracellular sorting mechanism.8 A similar affinity-related effect had been previously described by researchers at Genentech, where high affinity bispecific antibodies against TfR led to lysosomal degradation of the antibody and TfR.7,13 The work of Roche researchers went further, demonstrating that a bivalent Brainshuttle was targeted to the lysosome whereas the monovalent Brainshuttle was targeted for transcytosis by the formation of sorting tubules.9,10 Taken together, these studies suggest that high affinity bivalent binders may have limited application as RMT-inducers. Given that TfR is generally recycled back to the apical membrane during BBB transcytosis34 and that in these aforementioned studies the higher affinity/avidity molecules showed simultaneous re-targeting of not only the antibody but also the TfR receptor to the late endosome/lysosomal compartments, it would stand to reason that the ability to dissociate TfR:Ab complexes in the endosomal pathway is a necessary step to achieve efficient transcytosis. This may also explain why monovalent antibody binders have become the preferred format for RMT-inducers, as avidity has a disproportionately stronger effect on lower dissociation compared to higher association. Contrary to recent findings, the F06 format demonstrated the strongest transcytosis in a bivalent format. Therefore, the strong pH-sensitivity of our F06 may overcome the drawbacks of a high affinity bivalent binder with endosomal acidification facilitating the intracellular dissociation of F06:TfR.

We corroborated our hypothesis by applying a mathematical model of transcytosis. We based this model on current understanding of the mechanism of RMT and parameterized it on data from BSMono. To validate the predictive capabilities of the model we applied it to BSBi by altering only the binding kinetics, which correctly predicted the expected poor transcytosis of BSBi. Importantly, the ability to correctly predict the different transcytosis behavior based on binding kinetics alone suggests that the underlying assumptions in the model are valid and that kinetic parameters such as internalization rate and trafficking rates, which were maintained across simulations, can be generalized while still capturing avidity/affinity-dependent RMT processes. This is contrary to previous attempts to apply a simple mathematical model to capture the differential behavior of the mouse surrogates of BSMono and BSBi that relied on individual re-fittings for both molecules, suggesting that the underlying model used in this study cannot capture these effects.10 The model predicted that a pH-insensitive variant of F06 would be poorly transcytosed compared to the pH-sensitive scenario. The use of the validated mechanistic model allowed us to rapidly evaluate this hypothesis without the need for creating structurally guided pH-insensitive variants or relying on experiments using bafilomycin, which effectively interrupts endosomal acidification, but also introduces potential confounding factors from other impairments in endocytic trafficking and induction of cellular stress.35,36 A similar pH-sensitivity mechanism was previously suggested for the pH-sensitive antibody MEM-189, which showed improved transcytosis compared to non-pH sensitive antibodies that bind similar epitopes with similar affinity at pH 7.4.11 Notably, MEM-189 shows comparable transcytosis performance as compared to our F06 molecule of ~3.5 fmol/h (from 3 to 5 h) in similar pulse-chase assays with the same cell line (hCMEC/D3).11 Engineering pH-sensitivity into a monovalent RMT-inducer by introducing histidine mutations led to an improved in vivo transcytosis efficiency of an anti-TfR nanobody.20 While the Brainshuttle showed somewhat weaker pH-sensitivity than F06, it should be noted that this assay for technical reasons used the bivalent format, while the monovalent version currently in clinical trials may show stronger pH-sensitivity.

A potential downside of the high affinity but pH-sensitive approach, however, is that TfR also shows strong peripheral expression with previous anti-TfR antibodies being subject to target-mediated drug disposition (TMDD), a process that is dependent on affinity.7,29 Given that TfR in other cells usually mediates endocytosis of holo-transferrin before a pH-dependent release of iron and recycling of the apo-transferrin back to the surface where it dissociates due to the low affinity of apo-transferrin to TfR at pH 7.4,37,38 it is difficult to predict how a high affinity but pH-sensitive antibody will perform in in vivo biodistribution and pharmacokinetic studies.

Future investigations will aim to prove our concept of pH-sensitivity enabling effective transcytosis of bivalent antibodies in vivo. To this end, an integral first step will be to confirm that the unique features of the F06 candidate antibody are maintained cross-species, specifically the high affinity, strong pH-sensitivity, and transcytosis capability in relevant mouse models. Additionally, establishing the structural basis of F06’s epitope binding specificity to both the human and mouse TfR will be crucial to establish the translational potential of F06 itself and to obtain greater insight into the mechanism of pH-sensitivity itself. This could also enable the generation of structurally guided pH-insensitive variants as valuable controls to support both future in vitro and in vivo studies. An important aspect of future in vivo studies will be to ascertain not only the extent of biodistribution to the CNS, but also the relative contributions of affinity and pH-sensitivity to both TMDD and RMT-induction. The use of our mechanistic model as a foundation for building a multi-compartmental pharmacokinetic model can support these studies in separating the relative contributions of these intangible processes.

In conclusion, we have discovered and validated the bivalent antibody F06 as a proof-of-concept high affinity bivalent RMT-inducer. We further demonstrated that F06 can be used to effectively transport a model therapeutic cargo, in this case an scFv against the TREM2 receptor. The F06 antibody may overcome liabilities in the use of high affinity bivalent molecules for RMT-induction by a pH-dependent switch in binding affinity. We therefore demonstrate a rationale for the design of a high affinity bivalent antibody to induce effective RMT by exploiting pH-sensitivity. Future work will ascertain whether a translationally relevant RMT-inducer can be established based on the concept of pH-sensitivity in high affinity bivalent binders, using the F06 molecule as a basis for future studies, including effects on pharmacokinetics, in vivo biodistribution and whether pH-sensitivity can be upheld across species.
