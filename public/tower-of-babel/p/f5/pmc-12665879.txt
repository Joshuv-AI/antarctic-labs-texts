One‐Step Extreme Delignification to Simultaneously Produce High‐Purity Cellulose and Phenolated Lignin
Authors: Yongchao Zhang, Ruijie Wu, Shuzhen Ni, Xiaoqian Chen, Zhaojiang Wang, Zongquan Li, Yingjuan Fu, Menghua Qin, Chunlin Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCurrent forest product strategies, involving cooking and multistage bleaching processes, primarily focus on producing high‐quality (e.g., high brightness) cellulose fibers, generating low‐quality lignin that is typically burnt for energy production. Here, a formic acid–phenol–water system (FP) for achieving extreme delignification is developed, simultaneously producing high‐purity/brightness cellulose pulp fiber and phenolated lignin directly from lignocellulosic biomass. The strategy allows to establish a competitive reaction mechanism by introducing active phenol to replace lignin fragments and to react preferentially with lignin‐reactive intermediates, enhancing the lignin removal rate while avoiding its condensation. The directly produced cellulose pulp with superior purity enables it to be adapted to different application areas, e.g., dissolving pulp for textile. Importantly, the in situ phenolated lignin from FP fractionation possesses high reactivity and can be directly considered as bioadhesive for plywood production. This efficient one‐pot fractionation process demonstrates a sustainable pathway for building an economic and environmental biorefinery platform.Keywords: extreme delignification, formic acid–phenol–water system, high‐purity cellulose, lignocellulose biomass, phenolated lignin

Introduction

Currently, the energy requirement of human society development is largely met by fossil resources, such as petroleum, coal, and natural gas.[ 1 , 2 ] The urgent need to look for sustainable resources as alternatives to meet the increasing energy demand has attracted extensive attention.[ 3 , 4 ] Lignocellulosic biomass is one of the most promising resources to meet the challenges of sustainable and green energy systems. Its global annual production is ≈181.5 billion,[ 5 ] which also has enormous forthcoming potential to deliver human needs of all useable forms of chemicals and materials.[ 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 ] Lignocellulosic biomass is primarily composed of natural polymers (cellulose, hemicelluloses, and lignin) constituting the cell wall.[ 10 ] To achieve the full components utilization of lignocellulosic biomass, the fractionation process is the first and critical step in separating biomass into its main components. During most fractionation processes of lignocellulosic biomass, cellulose remained as a solid in the reaction system, while other components including lignin and hemicellulose are degraded and dissolved into cooking liquors.[ 11 , 12 , 13 ] The severely altered physical and chemical properties of fractionated biopolymers during fractionation restrict their subsequent valorization as raw materials to produce various biobased chemicals and materials. For example, the high concentrations of acids/bases or high reaction temperatures cause irreversible damage to the original structure of lignin that dramatically affects its subsequent utilization.[ 14 ] In particular, lignin molecules undergo a certain degree of condensation reaction during delignification process that leads to the formation of extremely recalcitrant carbon–carbon bonds.[ 15 , 16 , 17 ] The newly formed carbon–carbon bonds could result in an increase of molar mass and a decrease of chemical reaction activity, thus limiting its subsequent phenolic monomer production and further chemical modification.[ 16 , 17 ] Importantly, the condensed lignin macromolecules that remained in biomass raw materials could cause serious steric hindrance, hindering delignification progress and reducing lignin removal rate.

As the major industrial source of cellulose, raw pulp fibers (mainly cellulose) are usually produced from the traditional forest product industries, especially the pulp and papermaking industry. As the most common and conventional kraft pulping technologies, ≈90% of the lignin could be removed from raw materials during delignification stage.[ 18 ] However, the presence of residual lignin results in a darker or brown color and lowers the purity and quality of the cellulose pulp (Figure 1a). Importantly, most of the cellulose‐derived products, such as man‐made cellulosic fibers, require highly pure cellulose pulp as the raw material.[ 19 , 20 ] Aiming to enhance the brightness and purity of cellulose pulp to a satisfactory level, the further bleaching process must be conducted to remove residual lignin that remained in the raw pulp after the cooking or pulping process. Bleaching processes consume a large amount of chemicals, including chlorine‐based bleaching reagents, hydrogen peroxide, ozone, sodium hydroxide, and sulfuric acid, as well as energy.[ 21 , 22 , 23 ] For instance, 50% of the hydrogen peroxide produced annually in the world is used for pulp/paper bleaching, which is ≈1.1 million tons.[ 21 ] Moreover, the wastewater produced from bleaching process is the main target of environmental risk management in the pulp and paper industry.[ 24 ]

To upgrade lignin to well‐defined platform molecules along with the production of pure cellulose, the disruptive and targeted strategies to design a green integrated process via one‐step fractionation of biomass, e.g., which prevents pollution from its origins instead of dealing with pollution after generation, are appealing. We assume that one‐step fractionation shall enable almost complete removal of lignin existed in lignocellulosic biomass materials; thus, the high‐purity and high‐brightness cellulose pulp fiber could be directly obtained without subsequent bleaching procedures. However, the unwieldy issue impeding delignification of lignocellulosic biomass is that the formed recalcitrant condensed lignin remained in raw material is dramatically stable and difficult to be removed from lignocellulose by fractionation.[ 25 , 26 ] With this in mind, two fractionation strategies that were carried out by preventing the formation of the key reactive intermediate (benzylic carbocation) (strategy 1) or adding protecting agents to block the reactive benzylic positions (strategy 2) have been proposed to produce less condensed lignin.[ 13 , 16 , 27 , 28 , 29 ] Strategy 1 focuses on inhibiting the formation of Cα benzyl carbocations by chemically functionalizing benzyl alcohols through etherification, oxidation, or acetalization of the C α ‐OH.[ 13 , 16 , 28 ] For example, aldehydes (e.g., formaldehyde and acetaldehyde) and alcohols (such as ethylene glycol, secondary ethanol, and tertiary n‐pentanol) have been shown to react with C α ‐OH, resulting in highly reactive lignin with a high content of β‐O‐4 linkages.[ 6 , 13 , 16 , 28 ] The aim of these approaches was mainly to improve the yield and selectivity of aromatic monomers from subsequent catalytic depolymerization of the obtained lignin.[ 30 , 31 ] However, the extent of delignification of lignocellulosic biomass and the quality of residual cellulosic solids have not attracted sufficient attention or are not easy to achieve in a controlled manner. Furthermore, most of the research strategies for lignin isolation and depolymerization generally go at the expense of reducing delignification degree of lignocellulosic biomass, as the high lignin yields obtained from fractionation always undergo severe operating conditions. Significant removal of lignin from lignocellulosic biomass by various chemical treatment approaches typically required higher temperature or pressure, thus resulting in severe degradation of cellulose and irreversible condensation of lignin fragments.[ 14 , 32 ] Recent research reported that the addition of lignin‐like monomers during biomass fractionation limits the condensation between lignin macromolecules, achieving the catalytic conversion of lignin into biophenols.[ 27 ] Moreover, the yielded cellulosic solid could be easily upgraded to dissolving‐grade pulp via totally chlorine‐free four‐stage bleaching sequences. Therefore, the efficient control of lignin condensation provides a possibility to enhance delignification degree and simultaneously possess the selectivity for cellulose fibers.

A near‐complete removal of lignin during biomass fractionation process by suppressing the undesired condensation would facilitate the production of high‐purity cellulose pulp fiber and extend the feasible utilization of the obtained reactive lignin, thus increasing the economic viability and environmental sustainability of biorefinery strategies. The major challenge for achieving such a highly efficient fractionation technology of lignocellulosic biomass is to maintain extensive delignification and simultaneously fractionate reactive lignin. Our strategy is to establish a competitive reaction mechanism by introducing an active small molecule to replace lignin molecular fragments and react preferentially with reactive intermediates of lignin. We selected widely used phenol as the active molecules, which was due to two considerations. First, the nucleophilic phenol possesses higher chemical reactivity and molecular space advantage in comparison to lignin molecular fragments, thus suppressing condensation between lignin molecules or within the lignin molecule itself. Second, the reaction between lignin and phenol could further improve the chemical reaction activity of the fractionated lignin as the abundant phenolic hydroxyl were introduced in lignin molecules. This work involved a simple one‐pot process to simultaneously produce high‐purity, high‐brightness cellulose pulp and phenolic lignin. The resulting cellulose pulp met the standards for dissolving pulp without the need for any bleaching process. Meanwhile, the phenolic lignin obtained can be directly utilized to formulate high‐performance bioadhesives, eliminating the need for energy‐intensive catalytic depolymerization.

Results and Discussion

Biomass Fractionation for Fabricating High‐Purity Cellulose

For the initial evolution, we designed a formic acid–phenol–water fractionation system (FP), which is used for the component separation of lignocellulosic biomass (poplar, pine, and bamboo chips) (Figure 1b). The cellulose pulp obtained from FP fractionation system exhibited higher brightness compared with the brown pulp from formic acid fractionation (F), indicating most of the colored lignin has been removed from raw materials. We may thus infer that the FP fractionation system possesses an amazing delignification ability. More specifically, the brightness of the produced poplar (hardwood) pulp reached 78.8 ISO% without any further bleaching treatment, while the conventional kraft pulp could only achieve 30 ISO% (Figure 1c). Remarkably, the directly obtained poplar cellulose pulp possessed a super‐high purity of 98.95%. In contrast, a purity of only 73.24% could be obtained from conventional kraft pulping process (Figure 1d). The further chemical composition analysis showed that most of hemicellulose has been removed and only trace amount (0.86%) of lignin remained in cellulose pulp (Figure 1e,f). Furthermore, the FP fractionation system exhibited broad raw material adaptability, demonstrating excellent delignification efficiency also for the other two major types of biomasses for the forest industry, pine (softwood material) and bamboo (non‐wood material). The produced cellulose pulp without bleaching showed acceptable mechanical properties, which could be directly used to make paper sheets for versatile applications (Figure S1, Supporting Information). However, the super‐high purity of the obtained cellulose pulp means that there may be more possibilities for manufacturing other cellulosic high‐value products.

Comparison of Cellulose Pulp Fibers

As the raw materials for producing viscose and lyocell fibers, the commercial dissolving pulp fibers are manufactured by extensive bleaching of chemical pulps, which are mostly produced from hot‐water prehydrolysis followed by kraft pulping.[ 33 , 34 ] These chemical treatment processes require high temperatures and long reaction time, e.g., 160–180 °C for a total of 2–4 h (prehydrolysis and kraft pulping) and 70–90 °C for 4–6 h (conventional four bleaching stages), which is a typical high energy consumption process.[ 35 ] The obtained high‐purity cellulose pulps from FP fractionation system were further evaluated according to dissolving pulp standard. As the critical quality parameters for dissolving pulps, the α‐cellulose content in the obtained poplar and pine pulp exceeded the requirement of 90% (Chinese Standard QB/T 4898‐2015; Figure 2a). Another important criterion of dissolving pulp is the ash content (including iron content), which could generate negative impact on pulp dissolution. Results showed that both the ash and iron contents met the standard requirements (Figure 2b,c). This initial study revealed that the fractionation process with the presence of phenol exhibits strong “delignification” ability and selectivity, which is beneficial for the direct production of high‐quality cellulose pulp without additional chemical treatments. The processed fruition achieved by one‐step FP fractionation is almost competing with the traditional multistage treatment (including prehydrolysis, pulping, and bleaching), which undoubtedly greatly reduce the energy consumption of the process.

Lignin Structural Features

As the most abundant natural aromatic compounds, the fate and valorization prospects of the produced lignins have a decisive role for developing a promising biorefinery fractionation process. Based on the FP fractionation system established above, the essential target was to maintain the high efficiency of delignification for lignocellulosic biomass and simultaneously upgrade both the cellulose pulp and lignin to well‐defined platform molecules. Lignin fractions extracted via FP fractionation system showed 107.70%, 107.39%, and 92.29% yields from poplar, pine, and bamboo, respectively, which is significantly exceeded the total lignin content of raw materials and higher than that of fractionation process without phenol presence (Table S2, Supporting Information). Obviously, this result indicated that phenol also efficiently participated in the reaction with lignin during delignification process, confirming phenol's role as a stabilizer in lignin isolation.

One of the most distinctive features, which could be an index of lignin condensation, is the molar mass of lignin. Under FP fractionation of poplar, the molar mass of the recovered lignin (M W 9263 and M n 7470) slightly higher than that of milled wood lignin (MWL) (M W 8394 and M n 6564), while the molar mass of the extracted lignin without phenol addition was remarkably elevated (M W 31 290 and M n 25 440) (Figure 3b). The other two raw materials (pine and bamboo) showed similar trends. We could attribute this to the beneficial effect of phenol reaction participated in lignin by preventing the repolymerization or condensation of lignin fragments. This conclusion was further confirmed by the results of nuclear magentic resonance (NMR) analysis (Figure 3a, S3, and S4, Supporting Information). 2D HSQC spectra of the lignin isolated from different lignocellulosic biomass exhibited distinct signal in the aromatic region that are assigned to H unit of lignin, as compared to that of MWL analysis. Meanwhile, the total content of phenolic OH in lignin structure extracted from FP fractionation was extremely higher than that of MWL as well as the obtained lignin from the formic acid fractionation without phenol. Remarkably, the increased phenolic hydroxyl was mainly attributed to the increase in H‐OH. Therefore, we can conclude that the phenol has been grafted extensively into the lignin structure by reacting preferentially with isolated lignin molecules and their degraded fragments in the fractionation system. In the side‐chain region of the HSQC NMR spectrum (Figure 3), the poplar lignin extracted from FP fractionation presented signals typical for the β‐O‐4 with a content of 8.48 per 100 aromatic units, which is regarded as a key factor for its subsequent conversion to aromatic monomers. As a control, no relative signal representing β‐O‐4 structure could be observed from the formic acid fractionation without phenol addition.

MWL: milled wood lignin; FPL: lignin isolated from wood by FP process; FL: lignin isolated from wood by F process.

Directly Upgrading Lignin into Adhesive

Based on the above concluded lignin features, the extracted lignin polymer from FP fractionation remained a relative completed structure, simultaneously grafting a large amount of phenol. This kind of molecular structure is highly similar to that of crosslinked phenol, which might therefore be considered as phenolic resin. The obtained lignin was simply dissolved into NaOH solution and directly used to the preparation of plywood (Figure 4a). As the critical mechanical parameters for plywood, both of dry (applications in dry environments) and wet (applications in humid environments) bonding strengths were evaluated. The three‐layer plywoods prepared from pine FP lignin‐based adhesive showed excellent dry adhesion strengths, which could significantly exceed the requirement of 0.7 MPa under a wide range of hot‐pressing temperature from 110 to 190 °C (Chinese National Standard GB/T 9846‐2015; Figure 4b). However, the wet adhesion strength of plywood met the standard only under a high hot‐pressing temperature (190 °C). Therefore, the fully FP lignin‐based adhesive is more suitable for the application of indoor plywood, e.g., furniture and flooring, which could eliminate concerns about indoor formaldehyde emissions as no formaldehyde is used during the whole preparation process. In addition, the adhesion performance of adhesives prepared from lignins extracted from different raw materials (pine, poplar, and bamboo) was also assessed. The dry bonding strength of all the prepared plywoods with three kinds of adhesives could reach the requirement of 0.7 MPa under a range of hot‐pressing temperature from 110 to 170 °C. These advantages and excellent performance make the FP‐lignin‐based adhesives promising alternatives to traditional fossil‐based adhesives.

Competitive Reaction Mechanism

As we learned from the mechanism of delignification, depolymerization and condensation reaction occurred simultaneously during lignocellulosic biomass fractionation.[ 17 , 25 , 36 ] Under acidic conditions, α‐hydroxyl group is one of the most vulnerable functional groups in lignin linkages, which exert a crucial influence on the depolymerization and condensation or repolymerization of lignin molecules.[ 13 , 16 ] Specifically, the hydroxyl group is easily hydrolyzed and protonated, followed by dehydration to form benzylic carbocations.[ 15 ] Benzylic carbocation is the key intermediate, which is a strong electrophile that can attack nucleophilic lignin aromatic rings, forming stable carbon–carbon (C—C) bonds (Figure 5 ). To further reveal the competitive reaction mechanism, a model lignin dimer (veratrylglycerol‐β‐guaiacyl ether, VG, 1), possessing the β‐O‐4 ether bond linked units with available α‐ and γ‐hydroxyl groups, was used to simulate the occurred chemical reactions and structure changes during the FP fractionation process (Figure 6 ). The reaction product identification and quantification are described extensively in the Supporting Information. Previous researches have established that VG could be depolymerized into 1‐(3,4‐dimethoxyphenyl)‐3‐hydroxy‐2‐propanone (4) and guaiacol by breaking the β‐O‐4 linkage under acidic system. After the FP fractionation system treatment of VG, the yields of reaction products revealed that 78.04% (8 and 9) preserved the β‐O‐4 aryl ether bond, which is attributed to the reaction of phenol and benzylic carbocations. 10 (total proportion 14.51%) was also confirmed quantitatively, which is the products of the reaction between phenol and the intermediates originated from the broken β‐O‐4 aryl ether bond. Therefore, when the reaction system contains both phenol and lignin benzene ring active fragments 7, ortho and para positions of phenol benzene ring exhibited great competitivity to react preferentially with benzylic carbocations. These results were consistent with the results of above research analysis of lignocellulosic biomass fractionation using FP system. It is also further confirmed the competitive reaction mechanism by introducing phenol to replace lignin molecular fragments and react preferentially with lignin‐reactive intermediates.

Conclusion

In our study, we proposed a facile one‐step FP fractionation system to achieve the simultaneous production of phenolated lignin in high‐reactivity and cellulose pulp fibers in high purity, demonstrating that both cellulose pulp fibers and recovered lignin were directly upgraded to well‐defined platform macromolecules from lignocellulosic biomass. The mechanistic studies have shown that the presence of phenol in the FP solvent system can compete with lignin fragments to react preferentially with the benzyl carbocations as key intermediates, thereby reducing the C—C condensation of lignin molecules under acidic delignification. The building of the competitive reaction mechanism with the phenol presence provided a new strategy to suppress lignin condensation while improving the delignification efficiency and the quality of the recovered lignin.

Detailedly, the occurrence of this competitive reaction presented three amazing effects at the same time: 1) the lignin condensation is suppressed during delignification process and noncondensed lignin stabilized by phenol was obtained. Compared with the severely condensed formic acid lignin obtained from formic acid fractionation, the obtained noncondensed phenolic lignins had a smaller molecular weight and a higher content of phenolic hydroxyl groups, especially H‐type OH. Moreover, the obvious β‐O‐4 and H unit signals in the NMR spectra also indicated the role of phenol as a stabilizer in lignin isolation. 2) The suppressed condensation of lignin in turn greatly improves the thoroughness of delignification, producing pulp with high brightness and purity. The FP fractionation system can produce cellulose pulp fibers in high purity/brightness while achieving nearly complete delignification. The directly produced cellulose pulp with higher purity can be adapted directly for different applications, such as textile dissolving pulp, without multiple bleaching steps. The FP fractionation system is suitable for different biomass feedback (poplar, pine, and bamboo). 3) In situ phenolation modification produces high‐quality and highly reactive lignin polymer, which can be directly used for the preparation of fully lignin‐based adhesives by a simple procedure. The prepared formaldehyde‐free lignin‐based adhesive has good dry bonding strength and is expected to replace traditional fossil‐based phenolic resin and be directly used in the preparation of plywood.

These advantages suggest that FP fractionation could be easily transformed into a biorefinery platform for the high‐value application of the main components of biomass. Currently, the pilot production line of FP fractionation process has been built (Figure S5, Supporting Information), showing a great promise to the upscaling feasibility and industrialization potential of this process that could bring a disruptive and competitive route to biomass high‐value conversion offering a game‐changing bioeconomy scenario.
