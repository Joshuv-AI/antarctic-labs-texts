Operationalising prehospital management of exertional heat illness in athletes and team sports
Authors: Todd Leckie, F Stourton, D Fitzpatrick, R Hemingway, C Kipps, M J Stacey, F Wood, A Hartle, A Boalch, H Pynn, G Jones
Published: 2026

Exertional heat illness (EHI) is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in young athletes. It is likely the second most common cause of non-traumatic death in this population1 and may affect individuals exercising in both warm and cool environments. The incidence of EHI has risen over the past three decades,2 likely due to increased recognition, greater participation in strenuous activities and environmental risk from global warming. However, the full burden of disease remains uncertain due to the diverse clinical presentations of EHI, under-reporting and limited diagnostic capabilities in the prehospital environment.

Effective treatment of EHI provides an opportunity to reverse pathophysiology and improve outcomes. Severe EHI, or heatstroke, is characterised by a core temperature >40°C and central nervous system dysfunction. A ‘golden half-hour’ exists for severe EHI in which rapid cooling significantly improves outcomes by mitigating cellular damage.3

Patients with severe EHI present with a broad spectrum of symptoms, from subtle behavioural changes to critical multiorgan dysfunction. Clinical teams must consider the diagnosis in unwell athletes and ensure accurate core temperature measurement. Cooling must proceed alongside resuscitation, prioritising hyperthermia resolution.

Cold water immersion (CWI) is the preferred cooling method for severe EHI, providing the fastest cooling rate. When CWI is unavailable, shade, strip, spray and fan techniques can be effective. Complications that may arise during treatment include agitation and combative behaviour, while coma and airway compromise require special management during CWI. Rebound hyperthermia can occur after cooling, necessitating continued monitoring to prevent further tissue injury.

Optimal cooling may be hindered by the complex presentation of EHI, leading to failure of recognition or misattribution to another syndrome (eg, sepsis). Institutional and systemic barriers to effective EHI management include inadequate preparation and resources (eg, lack of continuous core temperature monitoring or CWI facilities), unfamiliarity with cooling strategies and reluctance to treat on-site (‘cool first, transport later’). Confusion over terminology (eg, heatstroke vs heat exhaustion) may also hinder appropriate prehospital intervention.

While advancements have improved EHI management at sporting events,4 EHI often occurs outside organised competitions where responders may lack specific expertise and be drawn from different organisations with diverse approaches to EHI care. The accompanying infographic, which is congruent with the recent UK Faculty of Pre-Hospital Care Consensus Guidelines,5 aims to support public awareness and equip responders at various skill levels to effectively manage suspected EHI cases in a range of sports-related settings.
