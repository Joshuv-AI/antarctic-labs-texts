A potential RNA–RNA distal interaction competing with a functional pseudoknot in the 3′-untranslated region of SARS-like coronaviruses
Authors: Sergio López-Núñez, Álvaro Simba-Lahuasi, Ángel Cantero-Camacho, Chengjin Ye, Ignacio Mena, Adolfo García-Sastre, Luis Martínez-Sobrido, José Gallego
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the 3′ untranslated region of betacoronaviruses, a pseudoknot involving stem–loop 3SL2 has been proposed to form a binding site for the viral RNA polymerase enabling the initiation of negative-sense RNA synthesis during the processes of virus replication and discontinuous transcription. Based on bioinformatic analyses and a mutagenesis strategy monitored with electrophoretic experiments and viral replication assays, we report that, in addition to the local pseudoknot in the 3′UTR, 3SL2 can establish a distal tertiary contact with an exposed sequence in the ORF7b gene located 1847 nucleotides upstream in the SARS-CoV-2 genome. The bases involved in this contact are highly conserved in all SARS-like coronaviruses and sequencing data indicate that this motif may form an alternative polymerase binding site enabling the synthesis of non-canonical subgenomic RNA. These findings provide a foundation for investigating the molecular mechanisms governing the processes of replication, discontinuous transcription, and recombination in these viruses.

Introduction

The 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of coronaviruses comprises cis-acting elements that regulate the initiation of negative-sense RNA synthesis during the processes of virus replication and discontinuous transcription [1–3]. In addition, this region has been proposed to bind microRNAs implicated in regulating the immune response of the host cell [4] and is likely involved in viral genome packaging [5].

Among the functional 3′UTR RNA motifs, a pseudoknot (PK) connecting a stem–loop located just after the N gene stop codon (usually identified as bulged stem–loop, BSL, or 3SL1) with the apical loop of an adjacent downstream hairpin (known as PK, or 3SL2) has been proposed to regulate negative-sense RNA synthesis in different betacoronaviruses, including bovine coronavirus [6], mouse hepatitis virus (MHV) [7, 8], and SARS-CoV-2 [9, 10] (Fig. 1). 3SL2 has also been described to form a three-helix junction with nucleotides (nt) of the 3′-terminal UTR segment [9–14], and this junction has been reported to be conserved along with 3SL1 in a variety of betacoronaviruses [11]. Since the three-helix intersection involving 3SL2 is incompatible with the 3SL1–3SL2 PK, a riboswitch model was proposed to explain the mechanism of replication–transcription initiation [11]. In this model, the junction is initially bound by nsp8 and other viral proteins to initiate negative-sense RNA synthesis. This priming activity promotes a transition from the three-helix junction to the PK conformation, allowing the association of the RNA-dependent RNA polymerase and the subsequent elongation of the nascent RNA chain [9, 11]. The 3SL1–3SL2 PK has not been detected by chemical probing studies involving full-length or minigenome SARS-CoV-2 RNA in vitro, in cells or in virions [13–16], and a biophysical analysis carried out with MHV sequences indicated that it had limited thermodynamic stability [17]. These results would be consistent with the proposed riboswitch model [9, 11], where the PK conformation is dynamic and requires the association of trans-acting factors to form. However, some of these studies have failed to detect the alternative 3SL2 junction conformation involved in the riboswitch mechanism [15].

(A) Schematic representation of a Sarbecovirus genome indicating the network of potential RNA–RNA contacts involving stem–loops 3SL2 and 3SL1 in the 3′UTR and ORF7b in the coding region. The corresponding SARS-CoV-2 sequences are shown in boxes.

Here, we use bioinformatic analyses and a mutagenesis strategy supported by electrophoretic experiments and viral replication assays to propose that, in addition to potentially forming a PK with 3SL1, the apical loop of 3SL2 can establish a distal kissing-loop contact with an exposed sequence in the ORF7b gene located 1847 nt upstream in the SARS-CoV-2 genome. This contact is conserved in all SARS-like coronaviruses and may represent an additional mechanism for regulating replication–transcription processes in these viruses.

Materials and methods

Sequences

The sequences experimentally analyzed in this study correspond to SARS-CoV-2 isolates Wuhan-Hu-1 (NCBI NC_045 512) or USA-WA1/2020 (NCBI MT246667), SARS-CoV-1 isolate tor2 (NCBI NC_004 718), and bat SARS-related coronavirus isolate Rp3 (NCBI DQ071615). Other coronavirus sequences were retrieved from the NCBI virus database, www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/labs/virus/vssi/.

Bioinformatic analyses

Secondary structure predictions were carried out with the RNAfold web server [18], and secondary structures were drawn using VARNA [19]. Reference coronavirus sequences were downloaded from the NCBI RefSeq database [20].

Assessment of long-range interactions in SARS-CoV-2

To identify potential RNA–RNA contacts, we applied LRIscan [21] on a Clustal Omega alignment [22] of four complete SARS-CoV-2 sequencing projects (NCBI NC_045 512, MT295464, MT295465, and MT324684), establishing a minimum interaction energy (ΔGi) of −10 kcal mol−1 and a minimum length of 7 nt for the contacting sequences [21]. To avoid cluttering the results with local interactions, a minimum distance of 200 nt between the interacting segments was also established. 16 923 hits obtained with this initial search were filtered with a more restrictive energy threshold of −15 kcal mol−1 to select 1029 potential interactions. For 48 contacts involving the 5′UTR, 3′UTR, or the programmed ribosomal frameshift (PRF) region, the secondary structure environment was assessed with RNAfold calculations involving 50 nt upstream and 50 nt downstream of each interacting sequence.

Analysis of the 3SL2–ORF7b distal interaction in coronaviruses

The conservation of the sequence and secondary structure of each interacting domain was evaluated through a BLASTn [23] alignment disregarding SARS-CoV-2 sequences other than NC_045512 to avoid the accumulation of highly similar sequences. These analyses included 115 nt (NC_045512 nt 29543–29657; 503 BLASTn hits) encompassing both the 3SL1 and 3SL2 3′UTR stem–loops to include the possibly competing 3SL1–3SL2 local PK, as well as 176 nt of ORF7b forming a well-defined secondary structure domain (NC_045512 nt 27694–27867; 466 BLASTn hits). Previous BLASTn analyses indicated that these regions were absolutely conserved among all SARS-CoV-2 isolates. Consensus secondary structures were then predicted with the RNAfold web server [18] using the consensus sequence of the alignment. To analyze the feasibility of the 3SL1+2–ORF7b contact, IntaRNA version 3.3.2 [24] was applied with default parameters to assess the thermodynamic stability of the interaction using a targeted search: 3SL1+2 as the query (NC_045512 nt 29543–29657) and ORF7b (NC_045512 nt 27694–27867) as the target, as well as general searches (either 3SL1+2 or ORF7b as the query and the full SARS-CoV-2 RNA genome as the target) to check whether any of the two elements could be involved in other thermodynamically favorable interactions. The IntaRNA results were compared with the output of a targeted interaction search using the RNAup webserver [18].

Preparation of RNA samples for experiments in vitro

The SARS-CoV-2 RNA sequences used in electrophoretic assays, 3SL2 (31 nt; NC_045512 29630–29656), 3SL1+2 (wild-type and mutant 3SL1+2A29641C and 3SL1+2A29644G; 115 nt; NC_045512 29543–29657), ORF7bs (69 nt; NC_045512 27758–27824), and ORF7b (wild-type and mutant ORF7bU27786C and ORF7bG27788A; 176 nt; 27694–27867), were prepared by T7-polymerase in vitro transcription from synthetic DNA templates or DNA sequences amplified by PCR with suitable primers. Additionally, two 3SL1+2 and ORF7b sequence pairs derived from wild-type SARS-CoV-1 tor2 and bat SARS-CoV Rp3 isolates, 3SL1+2tor2 (115 nt; NC_004718 29400–29514), ORF7btor2 (172 nt; NC_004718 27574–27745), 3SL1+2rp3 (115 nt; DQ071615 29389–29503), and ORF7brp3 (171 nt; DQ071615 27527–27697), were generated using the same methodology (Supplementary Tables S1–S4). To ensure the 3′ homogeneity of the RNA samples, most DNA templates contained 2′-methoxy modifications in the first two nt [25]. In addition, to increase transcription yield G:C pairs were added at the termini of the 3SL2 and ORF7bs hairpins and two U nt at the 5′ end of all ORF7b sequences were replaced with G, while G27750 and A27751 were substituted with C to maintain base-pairing at the base of the first stem–loop of SARS-CoV-2 ORF7b (Supplementary Table S1). All transcripts were subsequently purified on denaturing gels containing 12% acrylamide and 7 M urea. After electroelution from the gel, the RNAs were ethanol-precipitated two times, desalted with Sephadex G-25 cartridges, freeze-dried, and transferred into water stock solutions. The purity of all samples was assessed with denaturing electrophoresis experiments (Supplementary Fig. S1).

Electrophoresis experiments

Native gels assessing mobility shifts were run at 4°C for 4 h under constant voltage (90 V) in 89 mM Tris–borate (TB) or 89 mM Tris–borate and 2 mM MgCl2 (TBM) running buffers, using 12% 29:1 acrylamide:bisacrylamide or 16% 19:1 acrylamide:bisacrylamide. Prior to electrophoresis each RNA sample, at an approximate concentration of 2 μM, was heated at 80°C for 5 min and immediately placed on ice for 10 min before being incubated for 2 h at 37°C in the presence of 2 mM MgCl2, 25 mM potassium phosphate (pH 6.2), and 50 mM potassium chloride. Denaturing 12% 29:1 acrylamide:bisacrylamide or 20% 19:1 acrylamide:bisacrylamide gels containing 7 M urea were run for 1 h at 250 V in 89 mM Tris–borate–EDTA (TBE). All gels were carried out in triplicate to ensure the reproducibility of the results and were stained with methylene blue and destained with water. Preparation of mutant SARS-CoV-2 viruses

All SARS-CoV-2 infection experiments were performed according to experimental protocols approved by the Institutional Biosafety Committee at Texas Biomedical Research Institute. An infectious complementary DNA clone of the SARS-CoV-2 USA-WA1/2020 strain containing single mutations in either the 3′UTR 3SL2 stem–loop (A29641C, A29644G) or the ORF7b coding region (U27786C, G27788A), or a double-mutation in both the 3′UTR and ORF7b segments (A29644G and U27786C), were prepared based on the use of a bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) [26]. The mutations were introduced by site-directed mutagenesis in a pUC57 vector containing both the ORF7b and the 3′UTR of the viral genome [26] using primers designed with the NEBaseChanger tool (Supplementary Table S4). The presence of the mutations in the modified pUC57 was confirmed by Sanger sequencing in all cases. Since SARS-CoV-2 has been proposed to contain a small orphan ORF (ORF10; nt 29558–29674) partially overlapping with the 3′UTR 3SL1+2 segment [3, 27], all U27786C, G27788A, A29641C, and A29644G mutations inserted in both the ORF7b and 3′UTR sequences were synonymous. After sequence confirmation, the modified fragment from the pUC57 vector was substituted in the BAC [26]. To generate the rSARS-CoV-2 WT and 3SL1+2 (A29644G) mutant, confluent monolayers of Vero E6 cells in six-well plates (1.0 × 106 cells) were transfected using Lipofectamine 2000 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with 4 μg/well of the corresponding BAC plasmids. At 14 h post-transfection, transfection media was replaced with Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) containing 2% fetal bovine serum (FBS). At 24 h post-transfection, cells were scaled up into T75 flasks. At 72 h post-transfection, cell culture supernatants were collected and stored at −80°C. Viral rescues were confirmed by infecting fresh Vero E6 cells in six-well plates (1.0 × 106 cells). P1 viruses were passaged to generate a P2 stock in Vero E6 cells expressing ACE2 and TMPRSS2 (Vero AT, BEI, NR-54970) and titrated by plaque assay in Vero AT cells. RNA of rSARS-CoV-2 P2 stocks was extracted with TRIzol (Invitrogen). The extracted RNA samples were sequenced by NGS. Viral fitness of the rSARS-CoV-2 WT and 3SL1+2 (A29644G) mutant was determined by assessing viral growth kinetics and plaque assay in Vero AT cells.

Growth kinetics

Confluent monolayers of Vero AT cells in 24-well plates (2.0 × 105 cells, triplicates) were infected (MOI = 0.01) with rSARS-CoV-2 WT and 3SL1+2 (A29644G) mutant. After 1 h of virus adsorption at 37°C, cells were washed with phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and incubated with DMEM containing 2% FBS post-infection media at 37°C. At the indicated times after infection, viral titers in cell culture supernatants were determined by plaque assay in Vero AT cells. Viral growth kinetics experiments were conducted in triplicate, and the differences between wild-type and mutant 3SL1+2 (A29644G) viruses were analyzed with a multiple unpaired t-test analysis using GraphPad Prism.

Plaque assays

Vero AT cells in six-well plates (1.0 × 106 cells) were infected with serial 10-fold dilutions of rSARS-CoV-2 WT and 3SL1+2 (A29644G) mutant. Cells were incubated at 37°C for 1 h, washed with PBS, and overlaid with DMEM containing 2% FBS post-infection media and 1% low melting agar. At 72 h post-infection, cells were fixed overnight with 10% formaldehyde solution and stained with 1% crystal violet in 20% ethanol for 15 min. The wells were then rinsed with water and dried. Plaques were counted and viral titers were calculated.

Results

Bioinformatic assessment of potential long-range RNA–RNA interactions in SARS-CoV-2

In order to identify putative distal contacts involving known functional elements of the viral RNA, we explored the SARS-CoV-2 genome with LRIscan [21]. After defining an initial set of 1029 potential pairings with favorable interaction energy, we focused on 48 contacts involving the 5′UTR, 3′UTR, or PRF regions (Supplementary Fig. S2 and Supplementary Table S5), and analyzed the secondary structure environment of the corresponding sequences with RNAfold [18]. Upon selecting contacts where the interacting segments were likely exposed in a loop or located in an unstable stem, a predicted interaction involving 12 nt of the apical loop of 3′UTR hairpin 3SL2 (NC_045512 nt 29638–29649) and a likely exposed complementary sequence located 1847 nt upstream in the ORF7b coding region (27781–27792) (Fig. 1) attracted our attention.

The apical loop of 3SL2 had been proposed to form with the upstream hairpin 3SL1 a conserved PK regulating the initiation of the replication and transcription processes in the 3′UTR of betacoronaviruses [6–11] (Fig. 1). This local PK involved 7 nt of the 3SL2 apical loop that were encompassed within the 12-nt segment presumably contacting ORF7b (Figs 1 and 2). On the other hand, SARS-CoV-2 sequencing data [28] indicated that the complementary sequence in ORF7b could function as a recombination site for non-canonical subgenomic RNA generation. The exposed location of the 3′UTR interacting segment in the 13-nt apical loop of stem–loop 3SL2 had been established in SARS-CoV-2 by many structural analyses based on chemical probing [13–16, 29] and NMR spectroscopy [9, 12], and evidence supporting the location of the ORF7b segment in an exposed region was provided by some SHAPE mapping [29, 30] and bioinformatic analyses [31] (Supplementary Fig. S3). The functional relevance of the 3SL2 and ORF7b elements, together with the probable accessibility of the contacting nt potentially facilitating alternative base-pair formation, encouraged us to conduct further analyses of the 3SL2–ORF7b distal contact candidate.

Subsequent tests performed with IntaRNA [24] indicated that the 12-nt contact between 3SL2 and ORF7b was the best interaction match for both sequences in searches involving the full-length SARS-CoV-2 genome, and predicted that formation of the distal complex, with a calculated interaction energy ΔGi of −11.3 kcal mol−1, would be thermodynamically favorable (Fig. 2A). According to these calculations, there was no interaction involving ORF7b or 3SL2 inside the SARS-CoV-2 genome that was more stable than the 3SL2–ORF7b contact. Parallel computations with RNAup [18] confirmed these findings and likewise predicted a ΔGi value of −13.2 kcal mol−1, although in this case the interacting segment was predicted to comprise 11 instead of 12 nt as indicated by the LRIscan and IntaRNA calculations. Thus, these two algorithms predicted a favorable free energy for the interaction, concurring with LRIscan (−16.1 kcal mol−1; Supplementary Table S5).

Conservation of the putative 3SL2–ORF7b distal contact in coronaviruses

To assess the possible functional relevance of the interaction, we studied the conservation in coronaviruses of the sequence and secondary structure of each interacting domain with bioinformatic methods. These analyses involved a 176-nt domain within ORF7b including the pairing region flanked by two stem–loops, and a 115-nt segment within the 3′UTR encompassing the 3SL1 and 3SL2 hairpins. BLASTn alignments indicated that these elements were absolutely conserved in SARS-CoV-2 variants and highly conserved in the subgenus Sarbecovirus (Supplementary Fig. S4). Focusing on the 12-nt pairing sequences, conservation in sarbecoviruses averaged 99% for all positions of the ORF7b segment. The conservation in the 3SL2 sequence was 98% on average for all positions except for the 3′-terminal nt, which was G in the consensus sequence (84.1% identity rate) instead of A as observed in SARS-CoV-2 (14.1%). This A to G variation was not expected to have a significant effect on the stability of the complex as it replaced an A:U pair with G:U (Fig. 2A). Since all possibly disruptive mutations had frequency values below 2%, it is unlikely that 3SL2–ORF7b complementarity is altered in naturally occurring Sarbecovirus variants. While this low degree of variability impeded the possibility of carrying out a covariation analysis [32], the high degree of sequence conservation for both interacting segments supported a potential role for the interaction in the replication cycle of sarbecoviruses. On the other hand, RNAfold [18] calculations indicated that the secondary structures of the 3SL1, 3SL2, and ORF7b domains were also conserved in SARS-like viruses, and supported that the sequences involved in the presumed distal contact were exposed in the apical loop of 3SL2 and a partly unpaired region of ORF7b (Fig. 2B).

Sequence design and mutagenesis strategy

To experimentally confirm the putative 3SL2–ORF7b contact identified by the bioinformatic analyses, electrophoretic mobility shift experiments were conducted with SARS-CoV-2 sequences. In order to simulate a native folding context in the highest possible degree and at the same time account for the possibly competing 3SL1–3SL2 local PK in the 3′UTR, we transcribed a 115-nt construct comprising both the 3SL1 and 3SL2 stem–loops (hereafter identified as 3SL1+2), as well as the 176-nt ORF7b sequence encompassing the interacting segment in an unstructured region between two stem–loops (Fig. 3A). To assess whether the distal interaction was established in other sarbecoviruses as suggested by the bioinformatics analyses, electrophoretic mobility tests were likewise carried out with similar 3SL1+2 and ORF7b sequence pairs derived from SARS-CoV-1 and bat SARS-CoV Rp3 (Supplementary Fig. S5).

The stability and specificity of the 3SL1+2–ORF7b interaction were assessed with two sets of SARS-CoV-2 mutant sequences, where the base modifications were selected using as a guide the predicted secondary structure of the domains (Fig. 3A), supported by previous reports [12, 29, 31, 33] and our conservation analysis (Fig. 2). In addition, all mutations were synonymous to ensure no impact on protein sequence in the subsequent cellular assays involving full-length viruses (see below). In the 3SL1+2 domain, A29641 or A29644, occupying exposed positions in the 3SL2 apical loop, were changed to C or G, respectively. The first A29641C mutant would disrupt an A:U pair in the 3SL1+2–ORF7b complex, while the second A29644G mutant was expected to have a lesser effect since it would substitute an A:U pair with G:U in the complex (Fig. 3A). In the ORF7b domain, U27786 or G27788, both located in the unpaired interacting segment, were mutated to C or A, respectively. The aim of these U27786C and G27788A mutants was to disrupt either an A:U or G:C pair in the 3SL1+2–ORF7b complex, respectively (Fig. 3A). On the other hand, since U27786 and A29644 occupy complementary positions in the complex, the 3SL1+2–ORF7b interaction was expected to be restored when combining the 3SL1+2 A29644G and ORF7b U27786C mutants.

Electrophoresis analysis of the 3SL2–ORF7b interaction

Electrophoretic mobility shift assays indicated that the wild-type 3SL1+2 and ORF7b domains of SARS-CoV-2 formed a heterodimeric complex in the presence of 2 mM MgCl2, as clearly indicated by an intense delayed band that was absent in the wells containing isolated domain samples (Fig. 3B–D). The 3SL1+2–ORF7b interaction required the presence of Mg2+, since the delayed band was not observed in the absence of the cation (Supplementary Fig. S6). When MgCl2 was present in the experiments, the isolated 3SL1+2 domain gave also rise to an additional band that was absent in the absence of the cation, where all species adopted a single, monomeric structure. This effect was likely caused by 3SL1+2 homodimer formation driven by the presence of a partially self-complementary 12-nt sequence in the unpaired segment linking 3SL1 and 3SL2 (Supplementary Fig. S7). This self-complementary segment has not been described before and its functional relevance, if any, is unknown. Nevertheless, the presence of the 3SL1+2 homodimer did not affect 3SL1+2–ORF7b complex formation in our experiments (Fig. 3B–D), as expected since the predicted structure of the homodimer does not change the accessibility of the 3SL2 loops (Supplementary Fig. S7).

The effect of ORF7b mutants U27786C or G27788A on 3SL1+2–ORF7b complex formation was assessed by mixing each of these sequences with wild-type 3SL1+2. The G27788A mutant, disrupting a G:C pair in the complex, completely abolished the interaction, while the U27786C mutation, disrupting an A:U pair, had a strong deleterious effect but did not completely impede complex formation (Fig. 3B and E). The effect of the 3SL1+2 mutations was analyzed in the same way; the 3SL1+2 A29641C mutation, disrupting an A:U pair, had a strong but not completely deleterious effect on complex formation, closely matching the result obtained with the U27786C mutant. In contrast, the A29644G change led to only slightly less complex formation relative to wild type (Fig. 3C and E). This was expected since this mutation replaced an A:U pair with a wobble G:U pair in the complex. Finally, when the ORF7b U27786C and 3SL1+2 A29644G sequences containing compensatory mutations were combined, ​the complex was recovered in a slightly higher degree relative to wild type, likely because the corresponding A:U pair of the wild-type duplex was replaced by a G:C pair (Fig. 3D and E).

Altogether, these results showed that 3SL1+2–ORF7b complex formation was driven by the base-pairing of the complementary segments and indicated that the interaction was exquisitely specific, since disruption of a single G:C pair completely obliterated the 12-base pair interaction, single A:U disruptions had a strong deleterious effect, and even replacements of a single A:U pair with G:U, or a single A:U with G:C, modulated the degree of complex formation (Fig. 3B–E). At the same time, the stringent requirement for Mg2+ ions (Supplementary Fig. S6) indicated that the establishment of the interaction likely depended on tertiary interactions stabilized by the cation, as commonly observed in kissing-loop complexes involving structured RNA domains [34, 35]. Interestingly, initial electrophoresis tests conducted with shorter sequences lacking the upstream 3SL1 hairpin in the 3′UTR and the two stem–loops flanking the ORF7b pairing sequence indicated that 3SL2–ORF7b complex formation was significantly weaker in these conditions (Supplementary Fig. S8), revealing a significant stabilizing effect of the neighboring structures. Note also that the local 3SL1–3SL2 PK could form in the context of the 3SL1+2 sequence since the 3SL2 loop sequence was unhindered in the monomeric or homodimeric structures of 3SL1+2 (Supplementary Fig. S7). However, this potential interaction was outcompeted by the formation of the 3SL1+2–ORF7b complex (Fig. 3B–D). This suggested greater stability for the heterodimeric complex relative to the local 3SL1–3SL2 PK.

The 3SL1+2–ORF7b interaction was also detected in vitro when testing similar sequence pairs from SARS-CoV-1 and bat SARS-CoV Rp3, where the ORF7b pairing sequence was likely less exposed relative to SARS-CoV-2 (Supplementary Fig. S5). This result suggested that the 3SL1+2–ORF7b contact can form when the ORF7b segment is embedded in different secondary structure environments.

Impact of the 3SL2 and ORF7b distal contact sequences on SARS-CoV-2 replication

To assess the effect of the RNA sequences involved in the putative 3SL2–ORF7b distal interaction on viral growth kinetics, each of the four synonymous mutations studied in the electrophoretic assays, A29641C and A29644G in the 3′UTR 3SL2 loop, and U27786C and G27788A in the ORF7b segment, was inserted in a full-length SARS-CoV-2 virus following an approach based on the use of a BAC [26]. A virus sequence containing the double compensatory mutation in ORF7b and the 3′UTR, U27786C and A29644G, was likewise prepared. Since all mutations were synonymous, viral protein sequence was not affected and the observed effects were exclusively due to changes in the 3′UTR and ORF7b RNA elements.

When Vero cells were transfected with plasmids coding for the mutant virus sequences, all mutant viruses except A29644G failed to be rescued. While the plaque phenotype of the rSARS-CoV-2 3SL1+2 (A29644G) mutant virus was comparable to that of the rSARS-CoV-2 WT, the replication rate of the viable rSARS-CoV-2 3SL1+2 (A29644G) mutant virus was slightly below the rate observed for the rSARS-CoV-2 at early (24 h) times post-infection (Fig. 4). The difference decreased at longer post-infection times, but sequencing data indicated that the mutation was maintained by the virus in these experiments. In the electrophoretic tests assessing the interaction between 3SL1+2 and ORF7b, the A29644G mutation led to slightly less complex formation relative to wild type because it replaced an A:U pair with G:U in the complex (Fig. 3C and E), and the same effect (substitution of A:U with G:U) was expected for this mutation regarding the base-pairs driving formation of the 3SL1–3SL2 PK (Fig. 1). In contrast, the other mutation in the 3′UTR, A29641C, significantly abrogated the contact with ORF7b by disrupting an A:U pair (Fig. 3B), and would similarly affect an A:U pair in the 3SL1–3SL2 PK of the 3′UTR. Since this local PK is likely required for the initiation of negative-strand RNA synthesis during the processes of virus replication and discontinuous transcription [6–9, 11], the deleterious effect of this mutation on virus rescue was not surprising. Intriguingly, all nt changes affecting the ORF7b segment, including U27786C, G27788A, and the double A29644G/ U27786C mutation, were also lethal for virus rescue.

Discussion

The RNA genomes of SARS-CoV-2 and other coronaviruses have been reported to comprise a significant number of distal RNA–RNA interactions [14, 36–41]. While the functional significance of these complexes remains unknown in many cases, the long-range RNA–RNA contacts detected between transcription regulating sequences likely facilitate the process of polymerase template switch during discontinuous transcription [37]. Starting from a systematic search of distal contacts involving functional regions of SARS-CoV-2 with the bioinformatic tool LRIscan [21], we identified a potential base-pairing interaction between a 12-nt sequence in ORF7b and the apical region of stem–loop 3SL2 in the 3′UTR. 3SL2 had been previously reported to form with the neighboring hairpin 3SL1 a local RNA–RNA PK required for the initiation of negative-sense RNA synthesis in betacoronaviruses [6–9, 11], and the 7-nt 3SL2 sequence involved in PK formation was encompassed within the 12-nt segment pairing with ORF7b (Figs 1 and 2). Further bioinformatic analyses indicated that the interaction between 3SL2 and ORF7b was thermodynamically favorable (Fig. 2A) and the best possible match for each of both sequences within the SARS-CoV-2 genome. Additional evidence supporting the feasibility of the 3SL2–ORF7b contact was provided by a number of chemical footprinting and NMR reports indicating that the interacting segments of 3SL2 or ORF7b were exposed in unpaired or weakly paired regions within the full-length SARS-CoV-2 structurome [12, 29–31], (Supplementary Fig. S3), although we also found contradicting secondary structure maps regarding the ORF7b region [13]. We subsequently verified the formation of the SARS-CoV-2 3SL2–ORF7b complex in vitro through an analysis of the electrophoretic mobility shifts of 115- and 176-nt constructs simulating the native folding environment of the 3SL2 and ORF7b regions (Fig. 3). The 3SL2–ORF7b interaction requiring magnesium (Supplementary Fig. S6), as typically observed in kissing-loop complexes involving tertiary contacts [34, 35], was stabilized by stem–loops adjacent to the pairing sequences—an observation further supporting its likely tertiary character—(Supplementary Fig. S8), and outcompeted the local 3SL1–3SL2 PK (Fig. 3). The 3SL2–ORF7b contact was also highly specific, as it was sensitive not only to single base-pair disruptions (with G:C to A:C having a greater effect than A:U to A:C or C:U) but also to substitutions of A:U with G:U, or A:U with G:C (Fig. 3).

This 3SL2–ORF7b contact had not been described before, as it was not detected in systematic studies of SARS-CoV-2 contacts based on psoralen crosslinking of base-paired RNA and high-throughput sequencing [14, 36–38]. We note in this regard that some of these analyses did not detect the local PK between 3SL1 and 3SL2 either [14, 36, 38], suggesting that certain RNA–RNA contacts, particularly kissing-loop complexes as opposed to extended duplexes, may escape detection in these assays due to their structural or dynamic characteristics.

Upon assessing the conservation of the 3SL2 and ORF7b 12-nt pairing sequences in different coronaviruses, we found that they were absolutely conserved in SARS-CoV-2 variants and exhibited 98%–99% identity rates in the subgenus Sarbecovirus except for a 3′-terminal base with no impact on the stability of the duplex (Fig. 2A). Indeed, electrophoretic experiments involving SARS-CoV-1 and bat SARS-CoV Rp3 3SL1+2 and ORF7b domains similar to those employed for studying the SARS-CoV-2 contact confirmed the establishment of the interaction (Supplementary Fig. S5). This result and the high degree of sequence conservation within SARS-like viruses, which also encompassed the surrounding secondary structures (Fig. 2B and Supplementary Fig. S4), suggested a possible role in the replication cycle of sarbecoviruses. A potential clue in this regard was obtained from SARS-CoV-2 transcriptome data [28, 42], which indicated that the ORF7b sequence pairing with 3SL2 can function as a recombination or junction site for non-canonical subgenomic RNA generation. When the putative functional significance of the interaction was evaluated by introducing 3SL2 and ORF7b mutations in SARS-CoV-2, all mutant viruses failed to be rescued except that containing an A29644G change in the 3′UTR (Fig. 4). This mutant replaced an A:U pair with G:U in the 3SL2–ORF7b duplex and had the smallest effect on complex formation in vitro (Fig. 3C and E), and was also expected to have a minor impact on 3SL1–3SL2 PK formation in the 3′UTR (Fig. 1). The alternative A29641C mutation in the 3′UTR significantly abrogated ORF7b binding (Fig. 3B), but would similarly affect the 3SL1–3SL2 PK of the 3′UTR. Since this local PK is likely required for the initiation of negative-strand RNA synthesis during virus replication and discontinuous transcription [6–9, 11], the deleterious effect of this mutation on virus rescue was not surprising. However, all of the mutant viruses containing synonymous changes in the ORF7b segment were also lethal for virus rescue, suggesting a relevant function for this region as well.

In this regard, the 3SL2–ORF7b distal complex shares sequence and structural characteristics with the 3SL1–3SL2 local PK complex in the 3′UTR, including a similar duplex composition and the presence of 3SL1 and other nearby stems (Fig. 5). These similarities may favor the binding of the primed nsp12 polymerase to the ORF7b–3SL2 complex so that nascent negative-sense RNA chain is elongated from that alternative site. This hypothesis would explain why SARS-CoV-2 sequencing data [28, 42] indicated that the ORF7b sequence identified here functions as a junction site for non-canonical subgenomic RNA generation. The fact that an antisense oligonucleotide targeting the upstream stem–loop of the 176-nt ORF7b construct tested in our electrophoresis analyses has been recently reported to reduce SARS-CoV-2 fitness [40] further supports a functional role for this extended RNA–RNA tertiary contact. Nevertheless, this hypothesis needs to be tested with further research. In this regard, experiments involving protein components of the replication–transcription complex are currently under way in our laboratories.

Schematic depiction of the suggested riboswitch involving 3SL2 in the 3′UTR of SARS-like viruses. The apical loop of 3SL2 can interact with 3SL1 to form a local PK recognized by components of the viral polymerase complex to initiate the synthesis of negative-sense RNA during replication and discontinuous transcription [11]. Here, we propose that 3SL2 can alternatively form a distal complex with ORF7b; this complex shares structural characteristics with the 3SL2–3SL1 PK and could also be bound by the polymerase complex to synthesize non-canonical subgenomic RNA as deduced from previously reported sequencing data [28]. The model depicts binding of the nsp8 component of the polymerase complex and needs to be experimentally confirmed.

Since the kinetics of RNA folding tend to favor formation of secondary structures involving proximal sequences, it is plausible that many RNA–RNA contacts between distal genomic regions involve kissing-loop complexes. Our findings contribute information about the molecular mechanisms possibly governing the processes of replication, discontinuous transcription, and recombination in coronaviruses, underscoring the potential importance in all of these processes of local or distal RNA–RNA tertiary complexes as possible binding sites for the viral RNA polymerase complex. At the same time, the results described here may expand the scope of therapeutic strategies that could be followed against current or future infections by sarbecoviruses.
