The Antarctic coastal ocean heat budget is dominated by heat loss to land ice melt
Authors: Ruth Moorman, Andrew F. Thompson, Madeleine K. Youngs, Andrew L. Stewart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transport of warm Circumpolar Deep Water (CDW) across the Antarctic continental shelf break is the primary source of heat to Antarctica’s marginal seas. Net heat supplied by CDW is ultimately lost to: (i) the ocean surface, either to the atmosphere or sea ice, or (ii) the ice sheet, by melting the base of ice shelves and calved icebergs. Ocean models often neglect the heat exchange needed to melt ice shelves and icebergs. Simulations presented here indicate that this omits the largest ocean heat sink on the Antarctic continental shelf, representing 60% of heat supplied across the shelf break. Suppressing this heat sink in simulations drives enhanced heat loss to the atmosphere through thinned sea ice cover as well as nonlocal reductions in heat supply to the continental shelf via sea ice–mediated stratification changes. These results highlight a source of climate model bias and clarify the dynamics of heat transport to Antarctic ice shelves.

INTRODUCTION

Ocean heat is the ultimate driver of observed Antarctic Ice Sheet (AIS) mass loss. When the ice sheet is at mass equilibrium, ice gain from net snowfall accumulation in the continental interior is balanced by glacial discharge to the ice shelves that fan out over Antarctica’s coastal seas. Of that discharged ice (which we refer to as “land ice”), approximately half is lost to melting at the ice shelf base, where ice is exposed to ocean heat in the ice shelf cavity (“basal melt”), while the other half breaks off into icebergs (“calving”) that melt in the ocean further afield (1–3). In recent decades, the AIS has lost mass due to accelerating rates of discharge without compensatory increases in surface accumulation (4–6). The observed increase in discharge has been attributed to high rates of basal melting where ice shelves are exposed to particularly warm ocean waters, notably in the Amundsen and Bellingshausen Seas in West Antarctica (7–9). These high basal melt rates indirectly enhance ice sheet discharge by reducing the capacity for ice shelves to mechanically inhibit upstream ice flow across the grounding zone (10, 11). Ocean heat is thus central to recent AIS mass loss, yet the heat budget of Antarctic coastal waters remains poorly characterized.

Characterizing the Antarctic coastal ocean heat budget requires consideration of both heat supply and heat loss processes. Most of the ocean heat driving elevated ice shelf basal melt rates is supplied by intrusions of warm Circumpolar Deep Water (CDW) across the continental shelf break. This warm water mass fills the mid-depths of the ocean off shore of the Antarctic continental shelf, and dynamics over the shelf control the poleward flow and modification of CDW, determining the ice sheet’s exposure to this offshore ocean heat reservoir. The first dynamical “barrier” limiting CDW-driven ice shelf melt lies at the shelf break, where the steep bathymetry of the continental slope limits poleward geostrophic CDW transport and supports strong cross slope gradients in water mass properties [the Antarctic Slope Front (ASF)] and balanced along-slope flows (the Antarctic Slope Current) (12, 13). Warm CDW overcomes this topographic constraint and supplies heat to the continental shelf via numerous proposed pathways, including eddies (13–18), tides (13, 19), steering of along slope currents into cross slope troughs (20–23), and exchange flows at Dense Shelf Water (DSW) overflow sites (13, 24–26), among others. These mechanisms support a spatially and temporally varying supply of ocean heat, in the form of warm CDW intrusions, to the continental shelf.

The second major barrier-limiting CDW access to ice shelf cavities is the transformation of warm CDW to cooler water masses on the continental shelf. Once CDW breaches the shelf break, it may undergo modification by mixing with water masses kept cool by heat loss to the atmosphere, such as DSW, Antarctic Surface Waters, and Winter Waters. This water mass transformation pathway reroutes ocean heat bound for ice shelves into the surface ocean and ultimately into the atmosphere. Surface-driven transformation of warm CDW is particularly active in regions of weak stratification and enhanced vertical mixing (27, 28) such as coastal polynyas, where the ocean is exposed to the wintertime atmosphere and sea ice formation rates are anomalously high (29). There is evidence to suggest that rates of coastal CDW transformation exert an equal or greater control on coastal CDW heat content than cross-shelf break CDW supply. In high-resolution regional simulations of the Ross Sea and West Antarctic Peninsula continental shelves, differing rates of on-shelf CDW modification explain contrasting ice shelf basal melt rates better than differences in cross-shelf break CDW intrusion rates (27). More recently, in circumpolar simulations, cold DSW-forming regions were shown to experience higher rates of CDW supply, balanced by higher rates of CDW transformation, than warm West Antarctic regions, further indicating that cross-shelf break CDW transport is an insufficient metric of ice shelf ocean heat exposure (24, 30). In addition, simulations (13, 24, 25) and observations (26) indicate that DSW export mechanically drives onshore CDW return flows, hinting at dynamical feedbacks between the rate of CDW supply and surface-driven CDW transformation in cold shelf regions. Although previous studies have centered CDW supply as the leading control on ice shelf melt variability, a balanced consideration of both the sources and sinks of warm CDW, and ocean heat more broadly, will be necessary for the attribution of observed basal melt variations and projection of future trends.

Should warm CDW overcome these dynamical barriers, it may ultimately undergo water mass transformation by melting glacial ice within ice shelf cavities and mixing with meltwater. Many models, especially large-scale climate models, misrepresent this transformation pathway because they do not explicitly represent ocean circulation within ice shelf cavities or thermodynamic exchanges of heat and freshwater at the ice shelf-ocean boundary. Models without ice shelf cavities treat ice sheets, ice shelves, and ice shelf cavities as land and typically represent the combined freshening effect of basal and calved melt as a river runoff flux at the ice shelf calving front [details vary, see (31, 32)]. Such representations confer several caveats to simulated pathways of warm CDW and ocean heat transport. The common choice to inject meltwater at the ocean surface is associated with hydrographic biases that are substantially mitigated by distributing freshwater fluxes over the depth range of the ice shelf base (33). Under these surface meltwater injection schemes, deeper water masses such as CDW and DSW that directly melt glacial ice in the real ocean are only indirectly modified by meltwater in simulations via mixing with surface waters, altering the properties and dynamics of these key water masses (33). In addition, in lieu of ocean temperature–dependent melt parameterizations [e.g., (34)], glacial meltwater rates are often prescribed or determined by the ice sheet surface mass balance (31), decoupling melt rates from the ocean state and neglecting potential feedbacks between ice shelf melt and CDW supply (13, 23, 35) or surface-driven transformation (36, 37).

Another potentially important, yet poorly characterized, limitation of prescribed meltwater schemes directly concerns the coastal ocean heat budget. In half of recently surveyed Coupled Model Intercomparison Project (CMIP) class climate models (32), and in proposed baseline protocols for Antarctic meltwater perturbation experiments (31), ice shelf melt is treated as a pure freshwater or salinity flux without attempting to account for the latent or sensible heat exchanges required to melt solid ice. This commonly neglected heat sink is large, with the latent heat of fusion required to sustain observed rates of basal and calved land ice melt approaching 30 TW [29.4 ± 3.4 TW using estimates of basal mass balance and calving fluxes from Depoorter et al. (1) and Lf = 334 J/g as the latent heat of fusion for ice, 1 TW = 1012 W]. Given that the total cross-shelf break ocean heat transport to the Antarctic continental shelf has been estimated at between 22 and 44 TW in high-resolution simulations that omit heat loss to melting glacial ice (17, 24, 35, 38, 39), representing an additional ~30 TW heat sink in simulations can be expected to restructure the Antarctic coastal ocean heat budget. The impact of omitting heat loss to land ice melting on our current understanding of Antarctic ocean circulation, as well as our expectations for the effect of future melt on regional and large-scale climate, has not been assessed.

In this study, we characterize the heat budget of the Antarctic continental shelf in a coupled ocean–sea ice simulation of the Southern Ocean, the LLC270-SO model, that explicitly represents ice shelf thermodynamics and parameterizes the heat fluxes implied by a prescribed iceberg melt field (see Materials and Methods). Although including thermodynamic ice shelf cavities in Southern Ocean and global scale simulations is becoming increasingly feasible [e.g., (33, 40–43)], simpler melt parameterizations and prescribed meltwater fluxes are expected to remain in use. We therefore conduct two experiments to diagnose the impact of choosing to neglect heat loss to land ice melting in such configurations, one where heat loss to basal melting is suppressed and one where heat fluxes to all land ice melting (ice shelf basal melt and calved iceberg melt) is suppressed. The freshening effects of these melting processes are prescribed to match the control simulation, but this freshening flux is associated with no latent or sensible heat transfers (see Materials and Methods). Our results provide insights into the coastal Antarctic ocean heat budget and stress that neglecting to parameterize heat loss to land ice melt in ocean models without explicit representation of ice shelf thermodynamics modifies large-scale ocean and sea ice properties. Our findings additionally stress the coupled nature of Antarctic coastal ocean heat sources and sinks and, relatedly, the coupled nature CDW supply and transformation.

RESULTS

The Antarctic continental shelf heat budget

First, we characterize the ocean heat budget of the Antarctic continental shelf region in our reference simulation, where heat fluxes to basal and calved land ice melting are represented and basal melt rates evolve dynamically in response to ocean conditions. The time mean heat budget is evaluated for the 20-year period from 1999 to 2019 to exclude an adjustment period in the perturbed simulations (next section). A time mean net heat supply of 38.2 TW across the continental shelf boundary, here defined as a continuous approximation of the 1000-m isobath [following (24, 38)], is almost entirely balanced by time mean heat loss through the ocean surface (15.3 TW), to the base of ice shelves (15.2 TW), and to iceberg melt (7.6 TW) (Fig. 1). The remaining 0.1 TW drives a weak continental shelf warming over the assessed period (1999 to 2019). Combined, commonly neglected heat losses to land ice melting account for 60% (22.8 TW) of time mean heat sinks on the Antarctic continental shelf. This ocean heat sink is smaller than the minimum ~30 TW anticipated based on observational estimates of ice sheet discharge rates (see Introduction), since roughly half of iceberg mass is advected off the continental shelf before melting in the climatological pattern used here (44). The total land ice ocean heat sink in our simulation, including heat lost to melting icebergs that reach the open ocean, is 30.3 TW.

Regional differences in the balance between heat sources and sinks are revealed by transforming the heat budget into alongshore coordinates (Fig. 2). Our interpretation of the spatial distribution of cross-shelf break heat supply uses a reference temperature of 0°C for all advective heat flux calculations (see Materials and Methods for detailed discussion). In this framework, onshore heat transport includes onshore transport of warm (>0°C) waters as well as offshore transport of cold (<0°C) waters; thus, we effectively assess the balance of fluxes that supply and modify high melt potential waters. In our simulation, net heat supplied by warm waters is approximately balanced locally by heat sinks on the continental shelf, as evidenced by the mirroring of cross-shelf break heat supply and summed heat sinks in Fig. 2. This local balance does not appear when a cold reference temperature is used to ensure that the direction of heat and volume fluxes are aligned (fig. S4), as excess onshore volume flux in the West Antarctica must be redistributed alongshore both eastward and westward before flowing offshore at the tip of the Antarctic Peninsula. This suggests that cold waters are substantially redistributed between coastal basins, providing pathways for downstream feedbacks to local perturbations [e.g., (35, 45, 46)], but that the fate of warm water is primarily a function of local processes. Changing the advective heat flux reference temperature thus provides complementary information that deepens our understanding of the system, our findings are insensitive to the choice to center 0°C referenced fluxes here.

Figure 2 provides further evidence of the leading order role of surface heat loss variations in explaining regional patterns of ice shelf basal melt, previously suggested in (24, 27). This is particularly evident along the West Antarctic coast where the Amundsen, Bellingshausen, and Ross shelf seas have comparable rates of heat supply, yet these seas have contrasting mean ocean temperatures, which correlate with contrasting surface cooling rates. This relationship does not hold for the Weddell Sea shelf, the coldest coastal basin in our simulations, where a weak net heat supply is primarily balanced by heat loss to land ice sinks. In general, stronger rates of cross-shelf break heat supply are simulated in the Pacific sector (Bellingshausen, Amundsen, and Ross) than the Indian (Wilkes and Amery) and Atlantic (Queen Maud Land and Weddell) sectors. This may be a consequence of large-scale circulation features, such as subpolar gyres and Antarctic Circumpolar Current, influencing CDW properties at the shelf break [e.g., (47, 48)], or of differences in the strength and continuity of the ASF, which may act as a dynamical barrier to poleward heat transport at the shelf break (12). These spatial patterns are independent of the chosen advective heat flux reference temperature (fig. S4).

Returning to a spatially integrated picture, the total continental shelf temperature tendency varies widely on seasonal timescales, ranging from a maximum monthly averaged warming rate of 103 TW in February to minimum in April of −57 TW in the climatological mean (fig. S1). This seasonal variability is almost entirely explained by variations in the ocean surface heat flux (R2 = 0.96, P < 0.05) and is relatively uncorrelated with variations in heat supply (R2 = 0.13, P < 0.05) and heat loss to basal melting (R2 = 0.05, P < 0.05). The seasonal cycle of continental shelf heat budget terms resemble those presented by Dinniman et al. (40) but with a reduced amplitude. Interannual temperature tendency variations are more muted, ranging from +4 TW to −4 TW over the simulation, and are best explained by changes in heat supply (R2 = 0.31, P < 0.05) and surface heat loss (R2 = 0.26, P < 0.05) while remaining uncorrelated with heat loss to basal melting (R2 = 0.02, P > 0.05). Modeling evidence indicates that ice shelf mass changes are particularly sensitive to interannual to decadal scale fluctuations in coastal ocean temperatures (49), and our simulations suggest an approximately equal role for heat supply variability and heat consumption variability at these longer timescales. Note, however, that this does not account for potential feedbacks between heat loss and heat supply.

Impacts of neglecting ocean heat loss to land ice melting

In response to suppressing ocean heat loss to land ice melting in our simulations, the heat budget of the Antarctic continental shelf adjusts via a dual response consisting of (i) an increase in the rate of heat loss to the atmosphere and (ii) a decrease in the rate of heat supply from the open ocean (Fig. 1). At the continental shelf scale, these responses are of similar magnitude and scale linearly across the two perturbation experiments. Suppressing ocean heat fluxes to ice shelf basal melting removes a 15.2 TW heat sink from the Antarctic coastal oceans that is ultimately balanced by a 6.6 TW increase in heat loss to the atmosphere (43% of removed sink) and an 8.2 TW reduction in cross-shelf break heat supply (54% of removed sink) with a small residual contributing to increased temperature tendency. When heat fluxes to both basal and calved iceberg melting is suppressed (i.e., all land ice melting), the omitted 22.7 TW heat sink is similarly balanced by a 9.7 TW increase in surface heat loss (43% of removed sink) and a 12.3 TW decrease in cross-shelf break heat supply (54% of removed sink). The similarity of these responses motivates our focus on the latter experiment, where all heat loss to land ice melting is suppressed. Adjustments to heat budget terms occur over a 6-year transition period (fig. S5), during which the continental shelf warms substantially (Fig. 3) as the heat tendency term is transiently large. Time mean heat budgets are therefore calculated from 1999 to 2019, following this adjustment period. Heat loss to the atmosphere adjusts rapidly (after 2 years), relative to heat supply across the shelf break (after 6 years) (fig. S5).

When appropriately represented, heat loss to land ice melting cools the continental shelf surface ocean by extracting heat from buoyant meltwater plumes around icebergs and within ice shelf cavities (Fig. 3A) that typically mix into the upper water column. Failing to represent the cooling effect of melting ice results in warmer, more buoyant meltwater plumes (Fig. 3, A and B) that warm the continental shelf surface ocean relative to the reference simulation across all seasons (Figs. 3A and 4, A and D). Continental shelf sea surface temperatures (SSTs) experience an average warming of 0.07°C, with larger anomalies (>0.25°C) concentrated to the Ross and Cape Darnley (near Amery ice shelf) coastal polynyas. This warm SST anomaly is nearly ubiquitous on the continental shelf (Fig. 4A), excepting a small region at the tip of the Antarctic Peninsula, and results in widespread sea ice thinning across all seasons (Fig. 4, B and E). Both increased SST and reduced insulation of the surface ocean by sea ice support increased heat loss to the atmosphere in cool months (Fig. 4, C and F). In the brief Antarctic summer, reduced sea ice insulation has the opposite effect and ocean heat uptake is amplified (Fig. 4F), but the integrated effect over an annual cycle is enhanced surface ocean heat loss. This response is approximately circumpolar, although notably amplified in the Ross and Cape Darnley polynya regions (Fig. 4C).

In contrast to the circumpolar surface heat loss response, the simulated decline in cross-shelf break heat supply is concentrated to cold DSW formation and export regions, namely, the Ross Sea, Weddell Sea, and the region downstream of Amery ice shelf (Fig. 5). This localized reduction in poleward heat transport coincides with a reduction in the volume and density of waters exported from the shelf (Fig. 6A) and a decrease in the density of waters at the seafloor in DSW formation regions (Fig. 6, B and D) due to freshening (fig. S6). These responses are consistent with evidence that the flow of DSW off the continental shelf supports poleward cross-slope return flows of warm CDW (24–26), such that declines in DSW export are coupled with reductions in heat supply to the continental shelf by CDW intrusions (13). In our experiments, the contraction and freshening of the densest shelf waters are driven by a reduction in sea ice export from the Antarctic coast to the open ocean. Around Antarctica, sea ice formation exceeds melt near the coast, while melt exceeds formation further offshore, resulting in a net export of freshwater from the continental shelf to the open Southern Ocean in all simulations (fig. S7). Sea ice thinning, simulated in response to suppressing heat loss to land ice melt (Fig. 4, B and E), reduces the volume of freshwater that can be transported from the continental shelf to the open ocean by sea ice advection, leading to widespread freshening of the continental shelf (Fig. 3 and fig. S7). By altering the sea ice field, misrepresentation of the ocean heat budget can therefore lead to sizable biases in ocean salinity (Fig. 3),which may then alter stratification and circulation at the predominantly salinity-stratified Antarctic margins. The sea ice–driven freshening response is enhanced at the ocean surface (fig. S8) and thus stratifies the water column in DSW formation regions (Fig. 6, C and E), imposing a greater energetic barrier to the full depth convection required to form dense waters. Waters that manage to overcome this stratification barrier are fresher than in the reference simulation (fig. S6), since they derive from fresher surface waters and are less modified by sea ice formation. The freshening and contraction of exported dense waters weaken exchanges of DSW and CDW at the shelf break, leading to the localized reductions in cross-slope poleward heat transport seen in Fig. 5. We find consistent behavior in a supplementary simulation where basal melting is suppressed completely such that neither heat nor freshwater exchanges between the ocean and ice shelves are represented. In that simulation, an increase in the density of exported waters driven by increased salinity on the continental shelf is associated with an increase in poleward cross-slope heat transport (fig. S10). Despite experiencing a large perturbation to the local heat budget, heat supply to the Amundsen and Bellingshausen shelf seas is unaffected by neglecting heat loss to land ice melting (Fig. 5). As in DSW formation regions, reduced offshore sea ice export (fig. S7) freshens surface waters and stratifies the water column in these warm shelf regions (Fig. 6E), yet these hydrographic changes do not affect local cross-slope heat supply. This result contrasts with several recent simulation studies in which CDW supply to the Amundsen shelf increases in response to coastal freshening by reduced offshore sea ice export (50–52). Our findings suggest that coastal freshening alone is insufficient to influence CDW supply when decoupled from changes to ice shelf basal melt rates. We find that heat supply to the Amundsen and Bellingshausen shelf seas remains constant across model runs with the same ice shelf freshwater forcing and only decreases in our supplemental simulation where both ice shelf heat and freshwater fluxes are suppressed (fig. S10), implying that basal melt rate variations drive heat supply variations in the Amundsen and Bellingshausen seas. This is consistent with Si et al. (23), who demonstrate that cross-slope heat supply is driven by the rate of basal meltwater overturning within the ice shelf cavity of an idealized Amundsen-like simulation. In the fully coupled system, changes to surface forcing may still affect cross-slope CDW transport in ways that reconcile our findings with (50–52). For example, coastal surface freshening may increase basal melting by reducing heat loss from the available coastal warm water reservoir to the atmosphere (45); this enhanced melting may then draw more CDW onshore. The preceding discussion carries a caveat due to the use of the Amundsen Undercurrent velocity as a proxy for cross-slope poleward CDW transport in (50–52). Cross-slope heat transport to the Amundsen shelf remains stable in our perturbed simulations despite an accelerating Amundsen Undercurrent (fig. S11); we suggest heat supply by CDW intrusions should be quantified more directly where possible and acknowledge that our model may fail to capture all relevant Amundsen Undercurrent dynamics. Although further investigation is merited, our results support a growing consensus that representing dynamic melt within ice shelf cavities is necessary to appropriately simulate cross-slope heat transport in warm shelf regions.

While surface cooling is amplified in the Amundsen and Bellingshausen seas in response to omitting locally large heat fluxes to land ice melting (Fig. 4), the magnitude of this response is insufficient to rebalance the heat budget locally (Fig. 5). The excess heat supply (~4 TW) is redistributed westward toward the Ross Sea (fig. S9). This indicates that variations in interbasin exchanges, not captured in regional simulations, play a role in the adjustment of coastal Antarctic conditions to forcing perturbations. Further, this result suggests that warm water supplied locally is not always modified locally, cautioning against an overinterpretation of cross-slope heat supply variability before assessing where supplied heat is ultimately routed.

DISCUSSION

In this study, we characterize the heat budget of Antarctic continental shelf waters using an MITgcm configuration that represents the thermodynamic effects of melting ice discharged from the AIS. Beyond seasonal timescales, we find that all heat supplied to the Antarctic continental shelf originates from the open Southern Ocean, reaching coastal waters via heat transport across the continental shelf break. We find that most of this supplied heat (60%, 22.8 TW) is ultimately lost to the act of melting land ice, either at the base of floating ice shelves (40%, 15.2 TW) or the submerged portion of icebergs (20%, 7.6 TW). This heat sink is commonly neglected in ocean–sea ice and coupled climate models, and we investigate the impacts of this omission by artificially quashing heat fluxes to land ice in simulations. In response to suppressing ocean heat loss to land ice melting, the Antarctic continental shelf transiently warms before arriving at a new equilibrium characterized by reduced heat supply from the open ocean and enhanced heat loss through the ocean surface. As schematically summarized in Fig. 7, both these heat budget adjustments are the result of widespread sea ice thinning driven by surface ocean warming. Since sea ice typically insulates the ocean from the cold Antarctic atmosphere, reductions in sea ice cover amplifies ocean surface heat loss. Widespread sea ice thinning also reduces the export of sea ice from Antarctica’s coastal seas to the open Southern Ocean. This freshens coastal waters and inhibits DSW formation, reducing exchanges between DSW and CDW at the shelf break that supply heat to the continental shelf. While heat is approximately a passive tracer in the salinity-stratified Antarctic coastal ocean, misrepresenting the ocean heat budget can alter circulation by influencing temperature-dependent components of the freshwater budget. In our experiments, neglecting heat loss to land ice melting results in widespread continental shelf warming and freshening, reduced sea ice formation and cover, increased water column stratification, and decreased DSW formation and export. In models that omit ocean heat loss to land ice melting, these anomalies may arise as biases or be inadvertently offset by tuning parameters, with implications for model physics.

While diagnosing the impact of neglecting ocean heat loss to land ice melt is the central aim of this study, our analysis yields broader insights into the dynamics controlling ocean heat transport toward Antarctic ice shelves. In both dense and warm shelf regions, we find that rates of cross-slope heat supply are coupled to local warm water transformation processes. Dense shelf regions are characterized by the transformation of inflowing warm CDW and surface waters into outflowing DSW, an overturning circulation sustained by sea ice–driven surface buoyancy loss in coastal polynyas. In our experiments, we find that system perturbations that lighten overflowing dense waters drive reductions in cross-slope heat transport to dense shelf regions (primary experiments), while perturbations that increase the density of overflowing dense waters lead to increased local heat supply (supplementary experiment, fig. S10). The density of exported waters, rather than export volume, is a better predictor of poleward heat transport across our simulations (fig. S10). This behavior aligns with an idealized simulation study by Si et al. (13), who found that poleward heat transport across the continental shelf break increased in response to increasing DSW density in dense shelf configurations. Further investigation of how this coupling arises in different regional contexts, and the implications of such a feedback at longer timescales, is merited. Warm shelf regions are characterized by the transformation of warm CDW into lighter surface waters by mixing with buoyant glacial meltwater, a flipped overturning circulation relative to dense shelf regions. Our simulations suggest that basal melt rates exert control over the rate of CDW transport to the warm Amundsen and Bellingshausen shelf seas, at least in the absence of variable wind forcing. This interpretation is consistent with the mechanism of CDW transport to warm shelves proposed by Si et al. (23) in which meltwater-driven upwelling within ice shelf cavities steers warm CDW poleward at the shelf break by injecting cyclonic vorticity into the CDW layer. This implies a feedback wherein a transiently forced increase in basal melting by CDW may pull additional warm CDW toward the coast to sustain the elevated basal melt rate. The coupled nature of CDW supply and transformation processes, highlighted by our experiments, cautions against relying on correlations when identifying the drivers of observed or predicted CDW trends. These findings also stress the importance of conceptualizing ice shelf melt as an active driver of continental shelf circulation and hydrography, rather than a passive response to ambient ocean conditions.

Our results carry the following caveats due to modeling choices. First, since the simulated increases in surface heat loss and decreases in cross-shelf-break heat supply hinge on interactions with the atmosphere and sea ice fields, these adjustments would likely be modified by the inclusion of atmospheric feedbacks in a fully coupled climate model. Second, we have investigated decadal scale adjustments to the abrupt suppression of heat loss to land ice melting, and it is possible that this adjustment may not accurately reflect the differences between models spun up for centuries with and without this heat sink. Third, our model does not include a representation of tides, which have been shown to increase total Antarctic basal melt rates while decreasing continental shelf ocean temperatures (41), although their impact on cross-shelf break heat fluxes was found to be minimal and of mixed sign in high-resolution idealized simulations (13). Last, the horizontal (1/3° or ~15 km on the continental shelf) and vertical (3 m at the surface) grid of our model is insufficient to resolve the full suite dynamics relevant to heat transport in Antarctica’s marginal seas. DSW properties, formation rates, and shelf break overflow dynamics are sensitive to model grid structure (32) with studies suggesting horizontal grid spacing of ≤2 km (53) and surface grid cell thickness <1 m (54) may be required to achieve a realistic representation. On the basis of sensitivity studies by Si et al. (13), we anticipate that a higher-resolution representation of DSW overflows would increase the magnitude of cross-slope heat transport to dense shelf regions, likely balanced by additional surface heat loss, and perhaps the sensitivity of this transport to DSW density perturbations. Model resolution may also impede the representation of shelf break dynamics in warm shelf regions; our finding that cross-slope heat transport can remain stable while the Amundsen Undercurrent accelerates should be tested in higher-resolution configurations. Broadly, our model grid is too coarse to resolve the mesoscale eddy field on the Antarctic continental shelf and slope, so tracer transport by eddies and smaller scale motions is parameterized. In higher-resolution simulations, the coastal freshening simulated in regions characterized by a strong ASF (e.g., along the coastal of Wilkes Land; Fig. 3F) may enhance poleward cross-slope heat transport by eddies (13), a response not captured here. It is unclear whether direct resolution of eddies in a higher-resolution simulation would result in increased or reduced cross-shelf break heat transport. We remind the reader that a positive bias in CDW thickness (see Materials and Methods) does not necessarily imply an excess supply of CDW as the system is in steady state. We encourage further investigation of the Antarctic continental shelf heat budget and feedbacks between CDW supply and transformation processes in higher-resolution simulations and fully coupled climate models.

Although details of described feedbacks may vary based on modeling choices, the central point remains that models across varying degrees of complexity, resolution, and scale omit a 30 TW Antarctic ocean heat sink from representations of the current climate. Averaged over the globe, this is equivalent to a forcing imbalance of 0.06 W m–2. The magnitude of this neglected heat sink is amplified in freshwater perturbation experiments where prescribed glacial freshwater fluxes are increased without accounting for the heat needed to melt ice [e.g., (35, 55–70)]. For example, one high emissions Antarctic ice shelf melt pathway (63) used in numerous freshwater perturbation experiments that neglect heat fluxes to land ice melting (35, 63–66) should extract heat from the ocean at a rate of >80 TW (0.16 W m–2) by 2100. Perturbation experiments using the more extreme projected melt rates of DeConto and Pollard (71) omit latent heat fluxes to ice melting of approximately 200 TW (0.39 W m–2) by 2100 [0.6 Sv meltwater injection; e.g., (59, 60)] and in excess of 335 TW (0.66 W m–2) by 2150 [>1.0 Sv meltwater injection; e.g., (60)]. Commonly identified responses to meltwater perturbations in these experiments include increased water column stratification, subsurface ocean warming, reduced of DSW formation, and sea ice expansion (31); our results indicate that these responses are likely affected by the omission of ocean heat loss to melting ice. Latent heat conserving methods of prescribed meltwater injection have been described [e.g., as a flux as in (31, 33) or depressed meltwater temperature here and in (72) for iceberg melt] and implemented in a minority of meltwater perturbation experiments (73–79). Recently, the Southern Ocean Freshwater Input from Antarctica (SOFIA) initiative (31) has proposed standardized protocols for Antarctic glacial meltwater perturbation experiments. Of the 14 SOFIA protocols, only three experiments, all in the lowest priority “tier,” parameterize the extraction of latent heat needed to melt glacial ice. Our findings support the increased prioritization of these protocols, now framed as sensitivity studies, and widespread adoption of latent heat conserving methods of meltwater injection in hindcast, forecast, and perturbation simulations without thermodynamically interactive ice shelf cavities.

In this study, we have focused on the Antarctic continental shelf ocean heat budget and the near Antarctic implications of neglecting heat loss to land ice melt in simulations. However, a neglected ocean heat sink of this magnitude (30 TW or 0.06 W m–2) may be consequential at the global scale, particularly when the omission is amplified in perturbation experiments and emissions pathway forecasts (e.g., to O (100) TW, see above). To contextualize the magnitude of these neglected ocean heat sinks, we note that the estimated 1993 to 2018 total ocean heat gain translates to a mean ocean heat uptake rate of 334 ± 84 TW or 0.65 ± 0.16 W m–2 (80). This ocean heating constitutes 91% of the top of atmosphere energy imbalance, which has warmed the earth at a mean rate of 367 ± 87 TW or 0.72 ± 0.17 W m–2 over the same period (80). Neglecting ocean heat loss to melting Antarctic ice could therefore introduce a 10% error to simulated global ocean heat budgets. We urge modeling centers to consider the large-scale energy balance adjustments accommodating the neglect of this ocean heat sink. Should AIS mass loss continue to increase as projected, the simulated global ocean heat content changes may diverge from reality unless latent heat conserving parameterizations of land ice melt are broadly implemented.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Numerical model

LLC270-SO is a Southern Ocean–only configuration of the MITgcm ocean model complete with a dynamic/thermodynamic sea ice model (81), a thermodynamic ice shelf model (82), and parameterized iceberg melt (see below). LLC270-SO builds on the nominally 1/3° (~15 km on the Antarctic continental shelf) resolution version of the Latitude-Longitude-Polar Cap model grid geometry (LLC270) developed for the Estimating the Circulation and Climate of the Ocean (ECCO-MITgcm) project (83, 84). This horizontal resolution is too coarse to explicitly represent the mesoscale eddy field on the Antarctic continental shelf, continental slope, and within ice shelf cavities, where the first baroclinic Rossby radius of deformation [approximated by NH/πf, see (85)] can be as small as 1 km due to weak stratification (small N), high latitudes (large f), and shallow depths (small H). Parameterization of tracer transport by mesoscale eddies is therefore necessary in LLC270-SO; here, we use the MITgcm GM/Redi Eddy Parametrization Scheme with κGM = κρ = 100 m2s–1 (25, 86, 87). In the vertical, we prescribe a 113-level z* discretization scheme that concentrates resolution in the upper 2000 m of the water column, where the vast majority of ice shelf cavity and continental shelf waters are located. Of the 113 levels, 90 are dedicated to the upper 2000 m, ranging in spacing from 3 to 87 m with a mean spacing of 23 m, to better accommodate heat and salt fluxes across ocean-ice boundaries and related circulation at depth within ice shelf cavities. Tides are not represented.

The LLC270-SO configuration spans the Southern Ocean south of 50°S. Ocean bathymetry and ice draft fields are taken from RTopo-2 (88). Initial conditions and northern boundary conditions are taken from iteration 50 of the LLC270 ECCO solution (83, 84), with the northern boundary condition implemented over a 50° to 55°S sponge layer using the MITgcm Open Boundary Conditions Package. Within ice shelf cavities, where LLC270 ECCO does not extend, the model is initialized from linear temperature and salinity profiles with upper and lower end members tailored to four subdomains (1, Amundsen; 2, Bellingshausen; 3, East Antarctic; and 4, Ross, Weddell, and Antarctic Peninsula). Originally, all ice shelf cavities were prescribed the same initial conditions; however, this resulted in either spuriously cool and convective conditions in the Amundsen and Bellingshausen Seas or spurious CDW intrusions into the Ross and Weddell ice shelf cavities, suggesting hysteric, initial condition–dependent dynamics [as described in (37, 89, 90)] and necessitating the split initialization used here. At the surface, the model is forced by the European Center for Medium Range Forecasts (ECMWF) Re-Analysis Interim (ERA-Interim) atmospheric product (91, 92). Following initialization, LLC270-SO was spun up over three 1992 to 2019 atmospheric forcing cycles before branching off the control and experiment simulations. The first 60 days postinitialization were integrated with a halved timestep of 600 s and no external forcing to permit geostrophic adjustment of flow within and adjacent ice shelf cavities.

LLC270-SO dynamically solves for freezing and melting processes within fixed geometry ice shelf cavities using the MITgcm implementation of three-equation thermodynamics (82, 93, 94). Exchanges of heat and freshwater associated with basal melting and freezing are parameterized as diffusive fluxes of temperature and salinity at the ice ocean boundary and so do not alter cavity geometry or add mass to the ocean model. Velocity-dependent exchange coefficients [see (95)] are not used here, instead heat and salinity transfer coefficients are given constant values of 5 × 10−5 and 2.525 × 10−7 ms–1, respectively. This is typical for lower-resolution models [e.g., (95, 96)] since circulation features within ice shelf cavities are not well captured.

Iceberg melt is more simply represented using the MITgcm “AddMass” option as a time invariant spatial pattern of freshwater injection [S = 0 practical salinity units (PSU), θ = −92.5°C] into the ocean. The meltwater temperature of −92.5°C is determined by extrapolating the mixing line (“Gade line”) between CDW and glacial meltwater to 0 PSU using observations from the calving front of Pine Island Glacier (97). One can understand this value by first defining qmass, the heat transport into the ocean by meltwater mass injection, according toqmass [J/s]=m[kg/s]⋅Tmelt[°C]⋅cp[J/kg°C](1)where m is the melt rate and cp is the specific heat capacity of seawater (3994 J/kg °C). This value can be compared to ql, the latent heat that must be extracted from the ocean to melt that same mass of iceql [J/s]=−m[kg/s]⋅Lf[J/kg](2)where Lf is the latent heat of fusion of ice (334 × 103 J/kg). Equating qmass and ql gives a meltwater temperature of Tmelt = −Lf/cp = −83.6°C. Depression of Tmelt from −83.6° to −92.5°C approximately (and empirically) accounts for the small amount of sensible heating required to bring glacial ice to the freshwater freezing point, which may vary in space and time in ways not accounted for here. This iceberg meltwater is distributed horizontally according to the Merino et al. (44) time mean product and spread evenly over the upper 350 m of the water column following Naughten et al. (72), who used the same representation of iceberg melt and found this vertical distribution optimized Winter Water properties and water column stratification strength. Additional mass is then removed at the northern boundary of the simulation to balance this freshwater input.

The use of a time invariant iceberg meltwater field is a limitation of our study. On the basis of the climatology of small iceberg melt presented by Merino et al. (44), the continental shelf iceberg heat sink should vary seasonally by an order of magnitude from a minimum of 2.3 TW in midwinter to a maximum of 23 TW in midsummer. Here, we use the annual mean meltwater field, resulting in a constant heat sink of 7.6 TW. This choice, constrained by the AddMass version in these simulations, likely biases the seasonal cycle of the atmospheric heat sink, which has the opposite phase to the iceberg heat sink and dominates the seasonal variability of the system (fig. S1). Temporal variations of large tabular iceberg melt, likewise neglected, may introduce additional interannual-to-decadal variability to the system; however, observations indicate that these larger icebergs make up a small portion of the total Antarctic iceberg meltwater flux (98).

For the purposes of this study, the LLC270-SO control simulation reasonably represents observed Antarctic continental shelf hydrography and ice shelf basal melt rates for the simulated period. When compared to combined ship-borne, seal tag, and autonomous profiler hydrography (99) in six relatively well-observed shelf regions (the West Antarctic Peninsula, Bellingshausen Sea, Amundsen Sea Embayment, Filchner Trough, Terra Nova Bay, and Prydz Bay), end member water mass properties are well captured (fig. S12). However, we note that the hydrographic signature of mCDW on the continental shelf is more prominent in LLC270-SO than observed in all assessed regions. In warm West Antarctic coastal regions (the West Antarctic Peninsula, Bellingshausen Sea, and Amundsen Sea Embayment), mCDW properties are simulated at shallower depths than observed. In Terra Nova Bay, we likewise see mCDW signatures in the upper water column, either due to alongshore transport from the Amundsen Sea or excessive mCDW intrusion across the Ross Sea shelf break. In Prydz Bay and the Filchner trough, spurious mCDW signatures are seen at depth. This overrepresentation of mCDW on the shelf is likely, at least in part, a consequence of the modest horizontal grid spacing of LLC270-SO (1/3°), which is insufficient to capture sharp frontal structures separating CDW from shelf waters at the shelf break. In addition, Antarctic Surface Waters and Winter Waters have a warm, salty bias in the West Antarctic region. This may be suggestive of excessive vertical mixing of mCDW toward the surface, an underrepresentation of surface water mass modification, or both. We note that the collated observational product used contains temporal biases; however, we do not expect these to explain discrepancies with the simulated fields.

Despite the overrepresentation of warm mCDW on the continental shelf, LLC270-SO simulates reasonable ice shelf basal melt rates in all sectors (fig. S13). Spatial patterns of basal melt are in approximate agreement with observational estimates (9), although they tend to be less strongly concentrated to ice shelf grounding lines. Although time mean melt rates in each sector are reasonable, LLC270-SO does not capture observed interannual variations in melt rates. One possibility is that our choice of a relatively small thermal transfer coefficient [half the magnitude used in (93)] partially compensates for the overrepresentation of mCDW on the continental shelf when generating basal melt rates. This may have an additional effect of damping the influence of ocean thermal forcing variability on basal melt variability, resulting in smoother melt rates over time than observations suggest. While these discussed hydrographic and basal melt biases are important to acknowledge, this study focuses on signals much larger than the biases described here.

Simulation output from 1999 to 2019 is therefore used for all presented analysis.

Advective heat flux calculations

Advective heat fluxes through a two-dimensional boundary A associated with a nonzero net volume transport are calculated relative to a constant reference temperature θref according toFAθ=∫A(u • n)(θ−θref) dA=∫A(u • n) θ dA−θref∫A(u • n) dA(6)where u is the ocean velocity vector, n is the vector normal to A, and θ is the ocean potential temperature as a function of space and time (100). The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. 6 is computed online at the simulation timestep and thus includes contributions due to correlations between any resolved fluctuating temperature and velocity terms. The second term can be computed offline for a chosen θref from diagnosed volume fluxes. Time mean total advective heat fluxes across the boundary enclosing the continental shelf (e.g., in Fig. 1) are insensitive to the choice of θref since the continental shelf is approximately volume conserving. However, advective heat fluxes across open fractions of this boundary, as calculated when visualizing the spatial structure of heat supply in Figs. 2 and 5, do depend strongly on the choice of θref. This is especially true in polar regions where ocean temperatures are frequently negative. One choice would be to set θref to the lowest ocean temperature present in the study area or an approximation of that value [e.g., (24, 35, 38, 39)]. This choice has the advantage of ensuring the direction of heat and volume fluxes align, yielding some clarity, but can result in figures dominated by the transport of relatively cold waters that are not expected to substantially contribute to the ice melting processes motivating this study (see fig. S4). Instead, we choose to set θref to 0°C to explicitly track exchanges of waters with high melt potential. This choice results in time mean heat budgets that are approximately locally balanced in our simulations, and net heat supplied by warm (>0°C) waters to a given region of the continental shelf is roughly balanced by heat sinks within that same region (Figs. 2 and 5). Small variations in θref away from 0°C do not substantially alter onshore heat transport, but as θref becomes increasingly negative, Fig. 5 becomes increasingly dominated by the supply, alongshore redistribution, and eventual offshore flow of cool waters and conveys less information about the ocean heat content (see fig. S4). Combining information gleaned from using a range of θref values can enrich understanding; in our reference simulation, we find that heat carried by warm (>0°C) water is approximately locally balanced, but heat carried by cooler waters comes into balance via interbasin exchange. It should be noted when interpreting figures, however, that this choice of reference temperature means onshore flow of water with θ < 0°C across the continental shelf break registers as heat loss from the continental shelf, and offshore flows with θ < 0°C register as heat supply. In addition, while the spatial distribution of cross-shelf break advective heat transport is the most sensitive result to the choice of θref, this choice also influences heat fluxes associated with volume transfer, including those associated with iceberg melting and some surface flux components. However, these are negligible on the scale of provided figures.

Last, our simulations contain a ~9-Sv current, likely a southerly extension of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current, that intersects the continental shelf break at the tip of the Antarctic Peninsula, where the 1000-m isobath has a particularly complex geometry (fig. S3). For clarity, we average advective transports across this stretch of shelf break before any further analysis, as we found no net effect of this flow structure on the continental shelf although variability in this region dominated figures when not removed. The averaged segment of onshore heat transports can be seen in fig. S3.
