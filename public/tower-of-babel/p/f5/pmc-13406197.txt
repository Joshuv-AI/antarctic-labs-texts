Predictive value of right ventricular functional indices for bronchopulmonary dysplasia in preterm infants: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Enyu Wan, Qian Liu, Yuanxia He, Yun He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:To comprehensively determine whether echocardiographic measures of right-sided cardiac performance can identify premature neonates at risk of developing bronchopulmonary dysplasia.Methods:We systematically queried 4 electronic databases – PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Library, and Web of Science – from their respective launch dates through April 2025 to retrieve eligible studies examining whether echocardiographic markers of right-sided cardiac performance could anticipate bronchopulmonary dysplasia in premature neonates. Study rigor was evaluated with the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale, while the review was designed and reported in accordance with Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidance and the Cochrane Handbook. Pooled effect estimates were calculated in RevMan 5.3, whereas sensitivity analyses and Egger regression test were performed with Stata/SE 15.1.Results:Twelve studies were incorporated, involving 1316 preterm infants, with 414 of them diagnosed with bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD). Meta-analysis indicated that tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) (weighted mean difference [WMD] = −0.08, 95% confidence interval [CI] −0.12 to −0.04, P = .0002), myocardial performance index (MPI) (WMD = 0.05, 95% CI 0.02–0.08, P = .004), fractional area change (FAC) (WMD = −3.51, 95% CI −6.68 to −0.33, P = .03), pulmonary artery acceleration time (PAAT) (WMD = −6.54, 95% CI −10.71 to −2.37, P = .002), and PAAT to right ventricular ejection time ratio (PAAT/RVET) (WMD = −0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to −0.01, P = .005) can aid BPD prediction. Subgroup analyses showed that measurements obtained before 36 weeks’ postmenstrual age (PMA) had better predictive performance for TAPSE, MPI, and FAC than measurements obtained at 36 weeks’ PMA. PAAT/RVET remained predictive of BPD both before 36 weeks’ PMA and at 36 weeks’ PMA. Among infants with birth weight ≥1000 g, MPI demonstrated better predictive performance for BPD. Sensitivity analyses (leave-one-out) showed that exclusion of individual studies had no meaningful effect on the summary estimates, supporting the stability of the results.Conclusion:TAPSE, MPI, FAC, PAAT, and PAAT/RVET have predictive value for BPD in preterm infants.

1. Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a common chronic lung disease of prematurity that results from a combination of prenatal and postnatal factors, including lung immaturity, ventilator-induced lung injury, hyperoxia, inflammation, and abnormal pulmonary vascular development.[1,2] BPD impairs early respiratory function and is associated with cardiovascular complications such as pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular dysfunction, thereby reducing quality of life and long-term outcomes.[3] Even with advancements in perinatal care that have greatly improved the survival of very-low-birth-weight and extremely-low-birth-weight infants, the incidence of BPD has not decreased accordingly.[4,5]

Currently, BPD diagnosis and severity grading mainly rely on diagnostic criteria proposed by the National Institute of Child Health and Human Development (NICHD)/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute/Office of Rare Diseases workshop in 2001.[6] In their original form, the diagnostic requirement is “Treatment with oxygen ≥ 21% for at least 28 d plus” and severity is determined at the time point of assessment: for infants with gestational age < 32 weeks, the assessment is “36 wk postmenstrual age (PMA) or discharge to home, whichever comes first” and severity is classified as mild BPD – “Breathing room air at 36 wk PMA or discharge, whichever comes first”; moderate BPD – “Need for <30% oxygen at 36 wk PMA or discharge, whichever comes first”; severe BPD – “Need for ≥30% oxygen and/or positive pressure, (positive pressure ventilation [PPV] or nasal continuous positive airway pressure [NCPAP]) at 36 wk PMA or discharge, whichever comes first.” For infants with gestational age ≥32 weeks, after meeting the diagnostic requirement of “Treatment with oxygen ≥ 21% for at least 28 d plus,” severity is assessed >28 and <56 days after birth or discharge to home, whichever comes 1st; severity is classified as mild BPD – “Breathing room air by 56 d postnatal age or discharge, whichever comes first”; moderate BPD – “Need for <30% oxygen at 56 d postnatal age or discharge, whichever comes first”; severe BPD – “Need for ≥30% oxygen and/or positive pressure, (PPV or NCPAP) at 56 d postnatal age or discharge, whichever comes first.[6]” However, in clinical practice, early prediction of BPD remains challenging, and there is an urgent need for sensitive, reproducible, and noninvasive early predictive markers to guide timely intervention.[7]

In the pathogenesis and progression of BPD, pulmonary vascular abnormalities and impairment of right ventricular (RV) function play a pivotal role. Persistent pulmonary arterial hypertension increases RV afterload and leads to RV dysfunction.[3] Previous studies have revealed a correlation between the onset of BPD and the RV functional indices in preterm infants. Most studies are, however, limited to a single institution with an insufficient number of participants; hence, no extensive meta-analyses have been conducted, and it remains unclear which of the RV indices provide reliable and consistent forecasts. Therefore, we conducted this systematic review and meta-analysis to synthesize the available evidence on right ventricular functional indices in preterm infants and to determine their predictive value for BPD, thereby providing an evidence base for early risk stratification and timely clinical intervention in high-risk preterm infants.

2. Methods

2.1. Ethics statement

This study was a systematic review and meta-analysis of published data and did not involve the collection of new individual participant data; therefore, institutional ethics approval and informed consent were not required. The review was conducted in accordance with the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and reported in line with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines.

2.2. Strategy for searching literature

Registration of the study’s protocol occurred in International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (CRD420251021520). We searched through articles published starting at the beginning of the database up to April 2025 and included English studies that measured right ventricular functional indices to indicate BPD in premature newborns. Our search strategies combined structured and general terms and made corresponding corrections for the peculiarities of each database. 2.4. Evaluation of the quality of the study

The Newcastle–Ottawa Scale was employed to evaluate the methodological soundness of the included studies. This scale consists of 8 elements, peaking at 9; scores between 7 and 9, as set, signify superior studies.[9] At present, each study’s evidence level was assessed based on the Oxford Centre for Evidence-Based Medicine Levels of Evidence framework. Two independent evaluators assessed the quality of the studies, resolving any differences via discussion.

2.5. Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently and in parallel by 2 investigators; disagreements were adjudicated by a 3rd reviewer. From eligible studies, the following data were extracted: 1st author, year of publication, study period, country, study design, sample size, age, timing of echocardiographic assessment, gestational age, birth weight, and right ventricular functional indices (tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion [TAPSE], myocardial performance index [MPI], fractional area change [FAC], pulmonary artery acceleration time [PAAT], right ventricular ejection time [RVET], the ratio of pulmonary artery acceleration time to right ventricular ejection time [PAAT/RVET], tricuspid annular E/E′ ratio [E/E′], and tricuspid inflow E/A ratio [E/A]). In cases where continuous variables were presented as median values accompanied by ranges or interquartile ranges, established techniques were employed to calculate the means ± standard deviations.[10,11]

2.6. Statistical analysis

We performed the meta-analysis using Review Manager (RevMan), version 5.3 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The Cochrane Collaboration, Copenhagen, Denmark). For continuous outcomes, we calculated weighted mean differences (WMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Between-study heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran Q test and the I2 statistic.[12] If heterogeneity was significant (Q test P < .05 or I2 > 50%), we applied a random-effects model to pool the data. We also evaluated the influence of each individual study on the overall results using Stata/SE, version 15.1 (StataCorp LLC, College Station). Publication bias was initially assessed with funnel plots in RevMan 5.3, and then formally tested using Egger regression in Stata/SE 15.1.[13] In addition, we carried out predefined subgroup analyses by timing of assessment and birth weight to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. These were the only prespecified subgroup variables. Because the number of included studies was limited and reporting of other candidate modifiers (e.g., study setting/region, sample size, comorbidities, and clinical exposures) was inconsistent, we did not perform additional subgroup analyses or meta-regression.

3.3. Meta-analysis results

For TAPSE, 8 studies (total n = 786 preterm infants) were included.[15–17,19–22,25] TAPSE values were significantly lower in infants with BPD than in those without BPD (WMD = –0.08, 95% CI –0.12 to –0.04, P = .0002; I2 = 81%; Fig. 2A). The funnel plot for TAPSE appeared symmetrical (Fig. 3A), but Egger test indicated potential publication bias (P = .005).

For MPI, 7 studies involving 850 preterm infants were included.[15,16,18,20,22–24] Compared with infants without BPD, those with BPD had a significantly higher MPI (WMD = 0.05, 95% CI 0.02–0.08, P = .004), with substantial heterogeneity observed (I2 = 68%) (Fig. 2B). Visual inspection of the funnel plot showed symmetry (Fig. 3B), and Egger test indicated no evidence of publication bias (P = .392).

For FAC, 5 studies (including 435 preterm infants) were included.[16,19,21,22,25] FAC in infants with BPD was significantly lower than that in infants without BPD (WMD = −3.51, 95% CI −6.68 to −0.33, P = .03), with I2 = 74% (Fig. 2C). Symmetry was evident in the funnel plot (Fig. 3C), with Egger test indicating an absence of publication bias (P = .897).

For PAAT, 6 studies (including 465 preterm infants) were included.[14,17,21,22,24,25] Among patients with BPD, PAAT differed significantly from the non-BPD group (WMD = −6.54, 95% CI −10.71 to −2.37, P = .002), with I2 = 49% (Fig. 2D). The funnel plot appeared symmetrical (Fig. 3D), and Egger test suggested no publication bias (P = .819).

For PAAT/RVET, 5 studies (n = 407 preterm infants) were included.[14,17,21,22,24] The PAAT/RVET ratio was significantly lower in infants with BPD compared to those without BPD (WMD = –0.04, 95% CI –0.07 to –0.01, P = .005; I2 = 65%; Fig. 2E). The funnel plot for PAAT/RVET was symmetric (Fig. 3E), and Egger test showed no evidence of publication bias (P = .264).

For E/E′, 5 studies (n = 312 preterm infants) were included.[16,17,23–25] The E/E′ ratio did not differ significantly between infants with BPD and those without (WMD = 2.09, 95% CI 0.00–4.19, P = .05; I2 = 92%; Figure 4A).

For E/A, 4 studies (including 357 preterm infants) were included.[16,17,19,24] The E/A ratio did not differ significantly between infants with BPD and those without (WMD = 0.04, 95% CI −0.07 to 0.16], P = .46; I2 = 89%; Fig. 4B).

The sensitivity analyses showed that systematically omitting any study did not significantly alter the overall trend or statistical significance of the pooled effects, confirming the robustness of the findings for TAPSE, MPI, FAC, PAAT, and PAAT/RVET. This robustness further supports the reliability of these measures in predicting BPD in preterm infants (Fig. 5).

3.5. Subgroup analyses

Analyses of subgroups were categorized based on the time of evaluation (<36 vs 36 weeks) and the newborn’s weight (<1000 vs ≥1000 g) (Table 2). Findings indicated that TAPSE assessed at <36 weeks showed better predictive performance for BPD compared to 36 weeks. When exploring heterogeneity using the 2 prespecified subgroup variables, birth weight showed a more apparent between-subgroup difference for TAPSE, suggesting that birth weight may partially contribute to the observed variability; however, other potential sources of heterogeneity (e.g., study setting/region, sample size, comorbidities, and clinical exposures) could not be evaluated due to limited and inconsistently reported data. The predictive performance of MPI measured at <36 weeks was also superior to that at 36 weeks, and it was better in the subgroup with birth weight ≥1000 g than in the <1000 g subgroup; however, heterogeneity analyses did not support measurement time or birth weight as explanatory factors for MPI heterogeneity. The predictive performance of FAC measured at <36 weeks was higher than at 36 weeks, but heterogeneity analyses did not indicate that measurement time explained the heterogeneity related to FAC. Birth-weight subgroup analysis was not feasible for FAC because too few studies reported FAC with extractable birth-weight-stratified data. The PAAT/RVET ratio demonstrated predictive significance for BPD at both <36 and 36 weeks; however, heterogeneity analyses did not indicate that measurement time explained the heterogeneity related to PAAT/RVET. Similarly, birth-weight subgroup analysis could not be performed for PAAT/RVET due to the limited number of studies and insufficient reporting of birth-weight-stratified results.

4. Discussion

The identification of BPD in preterm infants in medical settings has consistently adhered to the 2001 NICHD/National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute/Office of Rare Diseases workshop diagnostic criteria, in which BPD is defined as “Treatment with oxygen ≥ 21% for at least 28 d plus,” and severity is categorized by gestational age and assessment time point: for infants with gestational age <32 weeks, the time point of assessment is “36 wk PMA or discharge to home, whichever comes first,” with mild BPD – “Breathing room air at 36 wk PMA or discharge, whichever comes first”; moderate BPD – “Need for <30% oxygen at 36 wk PMA or discharge, whichever comes first”; and severe BPD – “Need for ≥30% oxygen and/or positive pressure, (PPV or NCPAP) at 36 wk PMA or discharge, whichever comes first.” For infants with gestational age ≥32 weeks, once the diagnostic requirement (“Treatment with oxygen ≥ 21% for at least 28 d plus”) is satisfied, severity assessment is performed >28 and <56 days after birth or discharge to home, whichever comes 1st; mild BPD – “Breathing room air by 56 d postnatal age or discharge, whichever comes first”; moderate BPD – “Need for <30% oxygen at 56 d postnatal age or discharge, whichever comes first”; and severe BPD – “Need for ≥30% oxygen and/or positive pressure, (PPV or NCPAP) at 56 d postnatal age or discharge, whichever comes first.” In 2018, a NICHD workshop proposed a new skeleton definition of BPD (Table 1 in reference [26]): “A premature infant (<32 weeks’ gestational age) with BPD has persistent parenchymal lung disease, radiographic confirmation of parenchymal lung disease, and at 36 weeks PMA requires 1 of the following FiO2 ranges/oxygen levels/O2 concentrations for ≥3 consecutive days to maintain arterial oxygen saturation in the 90%–95% range.” The suggested grading system was: grade I – “Invasive IPPV: —; N-CPAP, NIPPV, or nasal cannula ≥ 3 L/min: 21; Nasal cannula flow of 1–<3 L/min: 22–29; Hood O2: 22–29; Nasal cannula flow of <1 L/min: 22–70”; grade II – “Invasive IPPV: 21; N-CPAP, NIPPV, or nasal cannula ≥ 3 L/min: 22–29; Nasal cannula flow of 1–<3 L/min: ≥30; Hood O2: ≥30; Nasal cannula flow of <1 L/min: >70”; grade III – “Invasive IPPV: >21; N-CPAP, NIPPV, or nasal cannula ≥ 3 L/min: ≥30”; and grade III(A) – “Early death (between 14 days of postnatal age and 36 weeks) owing to persistent parenchymal lung disease and respiratory failure that cannot be attributable to other neonatal morbidities (e.g., necrotizing enterocolitis, intraventricular hemorrhage, redirection of care, episodes of sepsis, etc.).[26]” It is important to note that the 2 sets of criteria are assessed at fixed time points (e.g., 36 weeks’ PMA), meaning that the formal diagnosis is often made relatively late; Peng et al emphasized that early identification of infants at risk of BPD would facilitate preventive interventions and support clinical decision-making, highlighting the need for reliable early prediction tools.[27]

Early prognostication of BPD risk is clinically important, as it may facilitate adaptation of clinical management and enable early identification of infants who might benefit from timely preventive or therapeutic interventions. Echocardiography is a practical, noninvasive bedside tool, and early right ventricular functional indices (e.g., TAPSE and right ventricular myocardial performance index [RIMP]/MPI) may help identify preterm infants at increased risk of developing BPD.[18,20,28] Although research studies have been done on the ability of different right ventricular factors to predict BPD, this study is the 1st meta-analysis to analyze the ability of different right ventricular indices to predict BPD.

In this meta-analysis, we examined 8 RV functional indices (TAPSE, MPI, FAC, PAAT, E/E′, E/A, RVET, and PAAT/RVET). Our findings indicate that 5 of these indices (TAPSE, MPI, FAC, PAAT, and PAAT/RVET) are strong predictors of BPD in preterm infants. Specifically, a low TAPSE, low FAC, low PAAT, low PAAT/RVET, and a high MPI were associated with an increased risk of BPD. The sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these 5 indices as predictors. Although funnel plots and Egger test suggested some publication bias for TAPSE, no significant publication bias was detected for MPI, FAC, PAAT, or PAAT/RVET.

First, TAPSE is an important parameter that enables the evaluation of longitudinal systolic ventricular performance of the right ventricle. Recently, researchers have investigated TAPSE as a marker for BPD risk and severity in preterm infants, but results have been mixed. For example, Neumann et al.[18] noted that on day 7 after birth, TAPSE was significantly lower in extremely preterm infants who later died or developed BPD compared to those with no adverse outcomes. However, at 28 days and near-term, TAPSE did not differ significantly between infants who later developed BPD and those who did not.[25] Additionally, some studies have found no direct association between TAPSE and BPD severity.[22,29] The meta-analysis revealed a notable reduction in TAPSE levels among preterm infants with BPD compared to those without, suggesting a dysfunction in the longitudinal systolic function of the right ventricle, aligning with irregular right-ventricular loading caused by increased pulmonary vascular resistance.[30] As a simple and reproducible marker of right ventricular pump function, TAPSE has gestational-age-specific reference ranges in neonates and can be standardized using z scores. The latest American Society of Echocardiography guidelines also recommend incorporating TAPSE into routine neonatal cardiac function assessment, providing a methodological basis for cross-study comparisons and clinical risk stratification.[31] Several studies reported lower TAPSE in infants who later developed BPD, supporting its potential role in early risk stratification.[18,20] Pathophysiologically, alveolar and microvascular developmental disturbances together with pulmonary vascular remodeling in BPD can chronically elevate pulmonary vascular resistance, increase right-ventricular afterload, and disrupt right ventricle–pulmonary artery (RV–PA) coupling, leading to reduced TAPSE – providing a plausible explanation for decreased TAPSE as an indicator of BPD risk.[30] Importantly, our funnel plots and Egger regression suggest small-study effects or selective reporting for TAPSE, indicating that the overall effect size may be overestimated. Thus, predictions based on TAPSE are to be approached with caution; further research is to be preregistered and proceed multicentrically, and TAPSE is to be combined with other parameters to enhance predictive specificity and strength.

The RIMP or MPI, is a comprehensive account of the state of right ventricular performance; it reflects the combined state of right ventricular systolic and diastolic performance. The index is based on the RV filling and ejection times and is applied to evaluate RV performance with different pulmonary arterial pressures and afterload statuses. In a prospective study with serial echocardiography, RIMP was significantly higher in infants who developed BPD at day of life 7, 14, and 28.[32] Our meta-analysis revealed a similar result in that RIMP is significantly greater in preterm infants who develop BPD than in infants who do not. The current finding is aligned with the pathophysiology of BPD: an increased MPI is a direct result of RV dysfunction, primarily due to increased pulmonary vascular resistance and increased RV afterload. The abnormal alteration of the pulmonary vascular network is a characteristic feature of BPD, where the RV is forced to oppose high levels of pulmonary arterial pressures in order to maintain normal levels of ejection; therefore, prolonged isovolumic contractions and relaxation make the MPI values high.[30]

Additionally, an echocardiographic measure known as RV FAC is used to measure the variability of both the end-systolic and end-diastolic areas of the RV cavity. Reduced FAC is directly related to RV systolic dysfunction, mostly because of augmented RV afterload by augmented pulmonary vascular resistance. Repeated inflammatory responses and alteration of pulmonary vessels that are associated with BPD may result in abnormal development of the vascular network of the lungs, which increases resistance to the ejection of the RV and greatly impairs the systolic performance of the RV.[30] The meta-analysis that we have performed demonstrates a significant decrease in FAC in the BPD group as compared to that of the non-BPD group and is consistent with the pathophysiological changes that accompany RV systolic dysfunction.

Furthermore, the echocardiographic measures of PAAT and PAAT/RVET are employed to assess the hemodynamics of newborns. Due to the absence of extensive validation in infants with healthy hearts, their present clinical use is somewhat restricted. Nonetheless, our research showed that PAAT and PAAT/RVET were predictive of BPD, with the BPD group’s values being notably less than those in the non-BPD group. Decreases in PAAT or PAAT/RVET reflect increases in pulmonary vascular resistance. The chronic pulmonary inflammation accompanying BPD can impair pulmonary microvascular development and trigger pulmonary vascular remodeling, increasing RV afterload and thereby shortening PA acceleration time and reducing PAAT and PAAT/RVET values.[3,31] Our subgroup analysis further showed that PAAT/RVET had good predictive performance for BPD regardless of gestational age <36 or ≥ 36 weeks. This may be because pulmonary vascular disease persists in preterm infants: even clinically asymptomatic preterm infants can exhibit abnormal PAAT/RVET due to delayed pulmonary vascular maturation, and the sensitivity of PAAT/RVET to changes in pulmonary hemodynamics is not constrained by developmental stage. Nevertheless, some studies have reported that when assessing the relationships of PAAT and PAAT/RVET with BPD severity at 36 weeks PMA or earlier, no significant differences were observed among groups stratified by BPD severity.[22,24] The variance indicates a need for additional studies to ascertain the dependability of PAAT and PAAT/RVET in forecasting the intensity of BPD at various stages of development.

This subgroup analysis had also shown that MPI was more accurate in predicting BPD among preterm babies born with a weight of >1000 g. This observation differs from most prior epidemiologic evidence showing that BPD prevalence is higher in infants born in lower birth-weight categories.[33] This observation is countered by the fact that most of the previous studies assume that the only factor affecting birth weight and BPD risk is categorizing based on low birth weight, without considering the contribution of gestational period, common comorbid conditions of preterm babies, RV functional measurements at birth, and maternal health in the course of pregnancy. Therefore, further research is necessary to establish the accuracy of this phenomenon and ensure an understanding of its basic mechanisms.

Although this study presents solid evidence on using RV functional indices as predictors of BPD through systematic review and meta-analysis, it has a number of limitations. To start with, there was a high level of heterogeneity among the studies involved. We handled this variability using the random-effects model, but residual heterogeneity is a possibility that can be detrimental to the findings’ strength. Since few studies provided sufficient data and heterogeneity remained after subgroup analyses, we were only able to explore heterogeneity using the 2 prespecified variables (timing of assessment and birth weight). Other potential contributors (e.g., study setting/region, sample size, comorbidities, and clinical exposures) may also play a role but could not be examined in the current study. Second, some of the analytic indices had publication bias, which may be associated with the preference to publish positive findings and a big variation in sample sizes among the studies. Third, clear evidence of the prediction value of these RV indices is not yet in the form of large-sample, prospective, multicenter studies. Subsequent research efforts must focus on standardizing echocardiographic measurement regimens, investigating multimarker predictive frameworks, and incorporating modern imaging features like RV strain to enhance predictive capability and clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, our results demonstrate that several echocardiographic measures of RV performance – specifically TAPSE, MPI, FAC, PAAT, and PAAT/RVET – are effective predictors of BPD in preterm infants. In particular, a pattern of low TAPSE, low FAC, low PAAT, low PAAT/RVET, and high MPI in a preterm infant may indicate an abnormally high risk of developing BPD. However, given the heterogeneity and potential publication biases in the current literature, further large-scale prospective studies in diverse populations are needed to confirm these findings and to determine the clinical utility of combining these indices for early BPD prediction.
