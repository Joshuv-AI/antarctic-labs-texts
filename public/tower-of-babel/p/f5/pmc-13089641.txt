Disparities in utilization of high-volume thyroid surgeons for Graves disease: a statewide analysis
Authors: Steven Xie, Catherine B Jensen, Aayushi Sinha, Elizabeth M Bacon, Lauren N Krumeich, Hunter J Underwood, David T Hughes, Paul G Gauger, Hari Nathan, Michael A Rubyan, Susan C Pitt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractContextFor patients with Graves disease undergoing surgery, the American Thyroid Association guidelines recommend referral to a high-volume thyroid surgeon (HVTS). However, factors limiting patient utilization of an HVTS are poorly understood.ObjectiveThis study aimed to identify factors associated with utilization of an HVTS for patients with Graves.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed adults with Graves who underwent thyroidectomy from January 2015 to December 2022 in a statewide claims registry. Surgeons were categorized as HV (>25 thyroidectomies/year) or low-volume (LVTS; ≤25 thyroidectomies/year). Logistic regression evaluated patient demographics, travel distance to treating HVTS hospital and closest endocrinologist, household income, area deprivation index, and education.ResultsAmong 764 patients treated by 130 surgeons, 17 HVTSs performed the majority (60.4%) of thyroidectomies. Most patients were female (84.3%) and White (51.0%). Patients treated by an HVTS traveled 16.8 miles farther (P < .001). On multivariable analysis, distance traveled to the treating hospital (odds ratio [OR] 1.01; 95% CI, 1.003-1.014) and education of a patient's home zip code (OR 0.87; 95% CI, 0.83-0.91) were significant predicators of using an HVTS. Proximity to an endocrinologist was not associated with utilization (P = .45). Surgery by an HVTS was associated with lower rates of hypocalcemia (18.2% vs 24.8%; P = .03) and cardiac events (10.4% vs 17.2%; P = .008).ConclusionJust more than half of patients with Graves disease received guideline-concordant surgical care by an HVTS. Enhancing patient knowledge and awareness of the benefits of HVTS care and solving transportation barriers may improve Graves patients’ use of HVTS and outcomes.

Graves disease is the leading cause of hyperthyroidism in the United States, with an annual incidence of 20 to 50 cases per 100 000 individuals [1, 2]. Most patients are managed with antithyroid medications, radioactive iodine (RAI) therapy, and/or thyroidectomy (reserved for patients who fail medical therapy or have contraindications to RAI) [3, 4]. Thyroidectomy as the initial treatment for Graves disease has been associated with reduced all-cause mortality and lower rates of cardiovascular disease, neuropsychiatric symptoms, diabetes, and hypertension [5]. However, Graves disease was a significant predictor of complications when performed by low- (LV) and intermediate-volume surgeons (low: <10 thyroidectomies; intermediate: 10-99 thyroidectomies during this study's 10-year period) [6]. The American Thyroid Association guidelines recommend referral to a high-volume thyroid surgeon (HVTS) when surgery is the chosen management strategy for Graves disease [4]. Undergoing thyroidectomy by an HVTS has been shown to improve patient outcomes, decrease length of stay, and decrease cost [7-9]. Therefore, it is crucial to consider patient access and utilization of an HVTS for management of Graves disease to optimize their short- and long-term outcomes.

The existing literature highlights substantial racial, socioeconomic, and geographic disparities in thyroid care [10-13]. Black patients were more likely to receive surgery as first-line therapy for Graves disease [14], suffer worse outcomes from thyroidectomy [15], and be treated by low-volume thyroid surgeons (LVTSs) [16]. Patients with Medicare insurance or lower income had a higher likelihood of being treated at a low-volume center [17]. Currently, there are limited data on geographic disparities and its interplay with social determinants in utilization of an HVTS for Graves disease. There are also knowledge gaps of the time and distance traveled by patients to an HVTS and how patient proximity to an endocrinologist may affect referral patterns and utilization of these surgeons.

This study aimed to identify disparities in utilization of an HVTS for patients with Graves disease, emphasizing geospatial factors such as distance to the closest endocrinologist, travel time and distance to the treatment hospital, and social determinants of health. These findings will inform future interventions and guide implementation strategies to improve utilization of an HVTS at a state and national level, potentially enhancing patient outcomes and reducing complications.

Materials and methods

Data source and study design

This retrospective cohort study evaluated data from the Michigan Value Collaborative (MVC) claims registry, a comprehensive database aggregating claims data from 106 hospitals [18]. The database currently captures approximately 84% of Michigan's insured population, consisting of deidentified claims data from Blue Cross Blue Shield of Michigan (BCBSM) preferred provider organization (PPO) Commercial and Medicare Advantage, Blue Care Network (BCN) Commercial and Medicare Advantage, Medicaid, and Medicare Fee-For-Service. The registry collects data on 30- and 90-day episodes of care, including index procedures, readmissions, and postoperative care claims.

The study cohort comprised adults (aged ≥18 years) with a diagnosis of Graves disease (International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision [ICD-10] E5.00) who underwent thyroidectomy between January 2015 and December 2022. Extent of surgery was categorized using current procedural terminology codes: thyroid lobectomy (60220), subtotal thyroidectomy (60225), total thyroidectomy (60240), and completion thyroidectomy (60260). Operations excluded from analysis included total thyroidectomy with central neck dissection (60252), total thyroidectomy with radical neck dissection (60254), and thyroidectomies involving a substernal thyroid gland (60270, 60271). These operations were excluded because of their low sample size, likely overlap with non-Graves pathologies, and higher risk of complications. To minimize confounding effects of out-of-state patients who traveled by air, patients were excluded if their home zip code was more than 550 miles (∼885 km) (approximate greatest driving distance in Michigan) from the treating surgical facility. This study was reviewed and approved under exempt status by the institutional review board (HUM00233914).

Primary outcome

The primary outcome was patient treatment by an HVTS for Graves disease. HVTSs were defined as those performing more than 25 thyroidectomies per year at any point in their career for any surgical indication, while LVTS were those performing 25 or fewer thyroidectomies per year, identified through surgeon national provider identifier number. This threshold for HVTSs is well-established by prior literature that demonstrated improved patient outcomes and shorter length of stay when thyroidectomies were performed by surgeons with at least 25 total thyroidectomies per year [19]. Surgeon subspecialty and years in practice were determined by residency or fellowship graduation year through publicly available data.

Variables

Patient demographic data (age, sex, race, insurance type, and home zip code) were extracted from the MVC registry. Socioeconomic variables were derived from US Census data at the level of patient's home zip code [20]. Patient household income was estimated using mean income at the residential zip code level, while educational attainment was defined as the percentage of residents aged 25 years or older without a high school diploma [21]. The area deprivation index (ADI), a validated measure of socioeconomic disadvantage of neighborhoods (measuring 17 factors including income, education, employment, and housing quality), was included in the analysis [22]. Surgical outcomes and postoperative complications were identified within the 90-day thyroidectomy episode of care using ICD-10 codes (supplemental material S1A) [23]. Thyroidectomy-specific outcomes included vocal cord dysfunction, hypocalcemia, tracheostomy, and bleeding/hematoma.

Distance metrics

Geospatial analyses were conducted to evaluate for geographic disparities in utilization of an HVTS for patients with Graves disease. Two primary distance metrics were calculated: the distance from the centroid of the patient's home zip code to the centroid of the 1) treating surgical facility and 2) nearest endocrinologist zip codes. We calculated two secondary distance metrics: 1) the distance from the closest HVTS to the nearest endocrinologist for patients treated by an HVTS vs an LVTS, and 2) the distance from the closest LVTS to the nearest endocrinologist for patients treated by an HVTS vs an LVTS. The shortest driving distances were calculated using Google Maps Application Programming Interface to ensure consistency of measurement across all patients [24, 25]. Travel distances to surgeons were stratified into quartiles (Q). To analyze how proximity to closest endocrinologist influenced utilization of an HVTS, quintiles were used for finer stratification and to capture potential nonlinear effects as distance to endocrinologist was highly skewed, allowing more detailed visualization and revealing patterns in the extreme tail [26]. Surgeon and endocrinologist data were obtained by linking national provider identifier number to the National Plan and Provider Enumeration System and public data [27, 28].

Statistical analysis

Univariate and bivariate analyses were performed to compare patient demographics, socioeconomic characteristics, surgical outcomes, and travel distances between patients treated by an HVTS vs an LVTS. Univariate analysis used descriptive statistics for the variables, while bivariate analysis used chi-square tests for categorical data and Mann-Whitney U tests and t test for continuous data. A multivariable logistic regression model was created to identify independent predictors of receiving surgical care from an HVTS. Covariates were selected based on univariate analyses, using patient-level variables that showed statistically significant associations with the outcome (P < .05). Because income, education, and ADI are derived from patient zip codes, only education was included in the model. The relationship between access to an HVTS and distance to closest endocrinologist was analyzed using quintiles and plotted into a distance matrix to demonstrate how the proximity to an endocrinologist influenced travel to an HVTS or an LVTS and the nonlinear relationship. All analyses were performed using Python 3.11.4 software, with statistical significance set at P less than .05.

Results

Overall, 764 patients with Graves disease underwent thyroidectomy by 130 surgeons during the study period. Among 764 patients, 462 (60.4%) were treated by an HVTS (n = 17 surgeons) and 302 (39.6%) were treated by an LVTS (n = 113 surgeons). Of the 130 surgeons, 88 (63.7%) were general surgeons, 34 (26.2%) were otolaryngologists, and 13 (10.0%) were general surgeons who completed endocrine surgery fellowships (Table 1). Surgeons with 21 years or more in practice treated 41.5% of the patients (n = 317), and general surgeons treated 58.1% (n = 444) of the patients.

Distance traveled to a high-volume thyroid surgeon vs a low-volume thyroid surgeon and closest endocrinologist

Patients treated by an HVTS also traveled significantly greater distances from their home zip codes than patients treated by an LVTS (mean distance: 43.1 ± 100.0 miles vs 26.3 ± 46.4 miles; P = .001; Table 3). This effect was most evident when travel distance was stratified into quartiles (Q1: 0-9 miles, Q2: 9-18 miles, Q3: 18-34 miles, Q4: >34 miles). A greater proportion of patients treated by an HVTS traveled 18 to 34 miles (Fig. 1, Q3: 28.6% vs 18.2%) and more than 34 miles (Q4: 29.9% vs 19.5%), while higher rates of patients treated by an LVTS traveled 0 to 9 miles (LVTS 36.4% vs HVTS 18.8%; P < .001). When examined by time traveled, a higher proportion of patients traveled more than 1 hour to undergo surgery by an HVTS (18.2% vs 8.9%; P < .001). Patients treated by an HVTS also had a significantly shorter distance between their closest HVTS and closest endocrinologist (supplemental S1B; HVTS 32.2 miles vs LVTS 54.6 miles; P < .001) [23]. This effect was not statistically significant for patients treated by an HVTS when looking at the closest distance between an LVTS and closest endocrinologist (HVTS 15.9 miles vs LVTS 17.7 miles; P = .24).

Logistic regression model of factors associated with thyroidectomy by high-volume thyroid surgeon

Multivariable logistic regression analysis identified factors associated with undergoing surgery by an HVTS (Fig. 4). Patients who traveled a greater distance to receive surgery had increased odds of being treated by an HVTS (odds ratio [OR] 1.01; 95% CI, 1.003-1.014). Lower educational attainment derived from zip-code data (higher rate of adults aged 25 year or older without high school diploma) was associated with decreased odds of being treated by an HVTS (OR 0.87; 95% CI, 0.83-0.91). Older age and type of insurance was not significantly associated with access to HVTS (see Fig. 4).

Extent of thyroidectomy and complications

The extent of thyroidectomy for Graves disease differed significantly for patients treated by an HVTS or an LVTS (Table 4). HVTSs performed a greater portion of total thyroidectomy than LVTSs (89.3% vs 82.4%; P < .01) and fewer lobectomies (3.3% vs 6.9%; P < .01). No statistically significant differences existed between HVTSs and LVTSs in the rates of vocal cord dysfunction (4.5% vs 6.6%; P = .27). However, patients operated on by an HVTS had significantly lower rates of hypocalcemia (18.2% vs 24.8%; P = .03) and cardiac events (10.4% vs 17.2%; P = .008). Rates of atrial fibrillation were lower in the group operated on by an HVTS, although not statistically significant (6.9% vs 10.9%; P = .07). The number needed to treat by an HVTS to prevent hypocalcemia or a cardiac event are 16 and 15 patients, respectively.

Discussion

This study identified factors that were independently associated with decreased odds of undergoing a thyroidectomy by an HVTS for Graves disease in Michigan: lower educational attainment and distance traveled to an HVTS hospital. Interestingly, no statistically significant association was found between proximity to the closest endocrinologist and utilization of an HVTS. This study also found that patients who were operated on by an LVTS did have significantly worse outcomes (hypocalcemia and cardiac events). These findings highlight the continued disparities in utilization and access to an HVTS for Graves disease with a unique focus on geospatial factors (distance to treating surgeon facility and closest endocrinologist), which are likely not isolated to Michigan and contribute to worse outcomes and higher complication rates in this patient population.

Our study demonstrated significant zip code–level disparities in receiving care from an HVTS. This finding aligns with prior work demonstrating various racial and/or socioeconomic disparities in the management of thyroid conditions [10-17]. For instance, Sosa et al [10] demonstrated that Black and Hispanic patients were significantly less likely to undergo a thyroidectomy for benign (Graves included) and malignant thyroid condition by an HVTS compared to White patients. Our study was unable to demonstrate any significant differences in rates of undergoing thyroidectomy by different races, likely from partially missing racial data collection by the MVC registry. Hauch et al [17] found that Black patients in the Midwest and West regions were at higher risk of being treated for benign (Graves included) and malignant thyroid conditions in an LV hospital, while households with the lowest Q income were more likely to be treated by an LVTS in the South and Midwest regions. Although our study did not demonstrate a statistically significant association between insurance type and utilization of an HVTS for Graves on regression analysis, prior studies have shown that Medicare and Medicaid beneficiaries were more likely to be managed at LV hospitals [17], while uninsured patients and Medicare or Medicaid beneficiaries were less likely to receive HV thyroid care for thyroid cancer [29, 30]. This study's inability to demonstrate a significant association between insurance type and HVTS utilization may reflect the modest cohort size (n = 764) of this statewide analysis, which may have limited statistical power compared to studies using national datasets [17, 29]. In addition, the unique geography of Michigan and the predominance of patients insured through BCBSM may have played a role. When data from the present study are examined in the context of prior literature, it is still critical to consider the interplay between a patient's insurance, race, and household income to receiving care by an HVTS. One potential solution to improve utilization of HVTSs for Graves disease is to tailor and implement interventions for patients from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, the uninsured patient population, or Medicare/Medicaid beneficiaries. Shared decision-making interventions have improved knowledge and increased informed choices among patients with other disease processes from lower socioeconomic or educational backgrounds [31-33]. A similar approach could be adapted for patients with Graves disease and utilization of an HVTS.

Geographic barriers were also identified as significant obstacles in utilization of HVTS care in our study. Patients who used an HVTS had to travel farther to receive care. Patients in our cohort who were treated by an LVTS had a significantly greater difference in distance (22.4 miles; supplemental S1B) [23] between the closest HVTS and their closest endocrinologist, suggesting practice and referral patterns may play a role in utilization. One geospatial analysis study by Alterio et al [34] found that nearly one-third of patients do not have access to an endocrine-focused surgeon within a 1-hour driving distance in the United States and Puerto Rico, with most surgeons clustered in metropolitan areas. Loyo et al [35] similarly demonstrated HVTSs are more likely to be in urban areas and less likely to operate in the Midwest or Southern regions. Given Michigan's unique geography and concentration of HVTSs in the Southeast, patients located in the Upper Peninsula and Northern regions of the state likely face considerable geographic barriers and travel burden compared to those residing in areas served by HVTSs (eg, Detroit, Lansing, or Grand Rapids). To address this geographic disparity, one potential solution is to leverage current hospital expansions and restructurings to strategically allow HVTSs to offer their expertise in these HVTS deserts. By partnering with affiliated hospitals and surgical centers, HVTSs can expand their geographic reach to serve patients at a location they can access. Additionally, collaboration and surgical coaching between HVTSs and LVTSs (and anesthesia teams) have the potential to improve the quality of care for patients unable to access and use an HVTS [36, 37]. Patient-level interventions [38, 39], such as education on the benefits of an HVTS and arranging transportation services to HVTSs, may further reduce these disparities [40, 41].

Despite the established benefits of surgical treatment by HVTSs, one study found 50% of patients who undergo thyroid surgery have it performed by a surgeon that averages 5 or fewer thyroid operations yearly [42]. Benefits of thyroidectomies performed by HVTSs include lower rates of recurrent laryngeal nerve injury, hypocalcemia, and decreased length of stay for benign and malignant thyroid conditions [43]. Thyroidectomy performed for Graves disease is known to be associated with a higher risk for complications like hypocalcemia and recurrent laryngeal nerve injury when performed by an LVTS [6]. Another study highlighted that patients with Graves disease undergoing surgery at HV centers had lower rates of thyroid-specific (postoperative hematomas, hypocalcemia, need for tracheostomy) and other major medical complications [44]. These results are similar to the present study, which revealed lower rates of hypocalcemia and cardiac events when operated on by an HVTS. These differences in outcomes may reflect differences in surgical expertise and technique, differences in management between LVTSs and HVTSs and/or their anesthesia teams, or due to the LVTS patient cohort being older and having lower average income and education. Preoperative preparation with Lugol's solution, calcium, and a β-blocker or achieving a euthyroid state prior to surgery as outlined by current guidelines may be less common among LVTSs [4]. Differences in surgical technique between LVTSs and HVTSs may also contribute to the increased risk of cardiovascular complications if LVTSs manipulate the thyroid more, releasing more thyroxine [45, 46]. Unfortunately, we could not examine these factors in the available data. One potential strategy to improve thyroidectomy outcomes for patients with Graves disease and mitigate the risk of complications is to develop a more nuanced stratification system to determine when referral to an HVTS may be appropriate.

A unique, previously unstudied component of the present study is the analysis of patient proximity to an endocrinologist and its relationship to utilization of an HVTS. While we did not find a statistically significant association between these variables, access to an endocrinologist is an important factor to consider as they are the most common type of physician to place a surgical referral for this disease. Future studies should further explore access to and referral patterns from endocrinologists, which may influence use of an HVTS.

While this study presents novel findings, it is not without limitations. The MVC claims database captures only 84% of the insured population of Michigan. Although unlikely, categorization of surgeons into HV and LV may be inaccurate if certain surgeons derive a large proportion of their patients from non-MVC payers. Claims data may also be subject to reporting or collection errors and limit the ability to assess true complication rates. There are no data on patient comorbidities due to the nature of the claims database, which could have accounted for differences in cardiovascular outcomes in our patient population. Additionally, race was available only for Medicare beneficiaries, weakening our ability to analyze race as an independent factor associated with utilization of an HVTS for Graves disease. Household income and educational attainment were based on zip code–level data, contributing to a lack of granularity on an individual level. Due to Michigan's unique geography and distribution of HVTSs, the results may not be entirely generalizable to all other US states. Lastly, this study was unable to determine whether patients visited their closest or any endocrinologist. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, no other study has examined the relationship between proximity to an endocrinologist and utilization of an HVTS for treatment of Graves disease.

Conclusion

This study found that just more than half of patients with Graves disease received guideline-concordant surgical care from an HVTS, leaving a substantial proportion of patients at high risk for thyroidectomy complications [4]. Significant factors associated with treatment by an HVTS were distance to the treating surgical facility and educational attainment. Interestingly, whether patients lived near an endocrinologist was not associated with being treated by an HVTS. While educational attainment is a nonmodifiable factor, distance to a treating HVTS hospital is potentially modifiable. Interventions include increasing patient, primary care, and endocrinology awareness of the guidelines and benefits of care by an HVTS, addressing transportation issues, leveraging ongoing hospital expansions to decrease HVTS deserts, and engaging HVTSs in surgical coaching of LVTSs. These interventions may contribute to improved utilization of HV surgical care, decrease complications, and improve overall outcomes for patients with Graves disease.
