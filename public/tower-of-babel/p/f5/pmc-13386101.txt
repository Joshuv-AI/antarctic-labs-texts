Serological Surveillance of Avian Influenza Virus Exposure Using Paired Sera Among Occupational Contacts in Taiwan, January–September 2025
Authors: Chieh‐Yu Lin, Tsung‐Pei Tsou, Po‐Wei Chu, Shu‐Er Yang, I‐Ling Yu, Yao‐Tsun Li, Ming‐Tsan Liu, Yi‐Chien Chih
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIn 2025, Taiwan implemented paired sera surveillance among occupational contacts of avian influenza viruses (AIVs). Of 122 contacts analyzed, 117 (95.9%) remained asymptomatic with high personal protective equipment compliance (91.8%). No seroconversion was detected against AIV A(H5N2) clade 2.3.4.4b. These findings suggest a low risk of human infection despite persistent poultry outbreaks, provide timely serological evidence during the ongoing outbreak, and demonstrate the effectiveness of Taiwan's integrated human‐animal surveillance in early risk characterization.Keywords: avian influenza; communicable diseases, emerging; public health surveillance; serology; Taiwan

Introduction

Since late 2020, highly pathogenic avian influenza virus (AIV) A(H5) clade 2.3.4.4b viruses have spread globally [1]. The first detection of AIV A(H5N1) infections in dairy cattle in the United States in March 2024, along with subsequent human cases, further heightened the virus's pandemic potential [2].

Avian influenza outbreaks in poultry farms occur annually in Taiwan. Following decades of endemic circulation of various AIV A(H5) lineages, a dynamic shift occurred during 2021–2022 with the introduction of AIV A(H5) clade 2.3.4.4b [3]. From 2023 to 2024 onwards, highly pathogenic avian influenza (HPAI) A(H5N1) emerged as the single predominant strain among HPAI outbreaks, predominantly affecting commercial poultry farms clustered in central and southern Taiwan [4]. Because occupational exposure is a known risk factor for acquiring AIV infection in humans, serologic surveillance among exposed poultry workers (contacts) has been conducted by Taiwan Centers for Disease Control (TCDC) since 2017 [5], expanding from solely including exposed poultry workers to incorporating unexposed controls in the following years. Since 2021, a longitudinal cohort has been established to track changes in antibody titers against AIVs up to 2 years post‐exposure among the contacts [6, 7]. From 2025, we started to collect paired acute‐ and convalescent‐phase serum from contacts, allowing timely diagnosis of acute infection and a more robust evaluation of antibody responses. We report results to inform zoonotic risk assessment and public health preparedness.

Methods

AIV outbreaks were detected through voluntary reporting of poultry operators, active surveillance in poultry abattoirs, and routine sampling from wild/migratory birds, conducted by Animal and Plant Health Inspection Agency, Ministry of Agriculture (APHIA, MOA), and were laboratory‐confirmed by the Veterinary Research Institute (VRI), MOA [8]. Field investigations were conducted by local health bureaus. Contacts exposed to affected farms from the day of outbreak identification until the completion of culling and disinfection were enrolled in TCDC's health monitoring program that concluded 10 days after their last exposure. Body temperature and respiratory or conjunctivitis symptoms were recorded daily. A suspected case of novel influenza A infection was defined as a contact presenting with fever≧ 38°C, cough or acute conjunctivitis, which required mandatory reporting to TCDC and specimen collection within 24 hours.

Paired sera (acute: ≤ 7 days; convalescent: 21 days–4 months post‐exposure) were obtained as part of routine surveillance activities. The TCDC laboratory performed hemagglutination inhibition (HI) assays using inactivated A(H5) viruses from representative clade 2.3.4.4b A(H5N2) (A/duck/TW/YL/21080001/2021; GISAID accession number EPI2584831) and clade 2.3.4.4c A(H5N8) viruses (A/goose/Taiwan/01003/2015; GenBank accession number KP714479) in a biosafety level 2 (BSL‐2) facility. Both viruses were provided by the VRI, MOA. Guinea pig red blood cells were used in the HI assays. Detailed laboratory procedures are described in S1. Seroconversion was defined according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria as a fourfold or greater rise in antibody titer with a convalescent‐phase titer of ≥ 1:40 [9].

Because many contacts were professional culling personnel conducting culling activities across multiple sites, we targeted only the first identified outbreak per worker for each AIV subtype. Contacts re‐exposed to AIVs of the same subtype and pathogenicity in subsequent missions would not be repeatedly enrolled for serological surveillance, ensuring a standardized timeline for evaluating seroconversion. Health monitoring would be performed on every single exposure event of all contacts and counted as contact events.

Demographics, work tasks, personal protective equipment (PPE) usage, health monitoring, and laboratory results of contacts were retrieved from the TCDC contact monitoring database. Seasonal influenza vaccination records were retrieved from the National Immunization Information System (NIIS) of TCDC.

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of TCDC (Approval No. 114202), and informed consent was waived because the data were collected as part of routine public health surveillance activities.

Health Monitoring Result of Contact Events (N = 836)

In the 76 outbreaks, a total of 836 contact events were identified and monitored. Among them, nine contacts from nine isolated events developed respiratory symptoms during the monitoring period. One contact met the criteria for a suspected novel influenza A infection and was reported to TCDC. RT‐PCR testing confirmed seasonal influenza A(H1N1) infection.

After excluding repeated exposures to the same AIV subtype and pathogenicity, these 836 contact events yielded 322 unique contacts. Among them, 182 completed paired serum sampling, and 122 met the sampling interval criteria described above and were included in the final analysis (Figure 1), including five of the nine symptomatic contacts.

Serological Monitoring Result of Contacts (N = 122)

The contacts were predominantly male (72.1%) with ages ranging from 16 to 76 years (mean age 41 years, SD = 12.3). Occupations included poultry workers (27.9%), animal health or inspection officers (23.8%), and personnel involved in culling or site‐cleaning operations (20.5%).

Most contacts (92.6%) were exposed during AIV A(H5N1) outbreaks, whereas 7.4% were exposed during AIV A(H6N1) outbreaks. Only 22.1% of the contacts had received the 2024–2025 seasonal influenza vaccine. The majority (91.8%) reported appropriate PPE use during exposure events, with gown (90.2%) and eye shields (51.6%) most commonly used.

HI assay results demonstrated uniformly low antibody titers against both AIV A(H5N2) (clade 2.3.4.4b) and AIV A(H5N8) (clade 2.3.4.4c) viruses (Figure 2). In the acute phase, all contacts exhibited antibody titers of < 1:10. During the convalescent phase, titers remained < 1:10 for 121 contacts (99.2%), whereas only one contact showed a titer of 1:10 against each subtype, who is a 43‐year‐old female poultry worker exposed in an AIV A(H5N1) outbreak of a poultry farm. She had no history of repeated exposure, did not use PPE, and had not received the 2024–2025 seasonal influenza vaccine. Overall, no seroconversion was identified among the exposed contacts.

Conclusions

We evaluated the zoonotic transmission risk among AIV outbreak contacts in Taiwan between January and September 2025. Despite the frequent outbreaks, analysis of 122 high‐risk contacts revealed zero seroconversion. Combined with results from health monitoring, our findings suggest the current risk of avian‐to‐human transmission of AIVs remains low, even in a cohort with significant occupational exposure.

A primary strength of our research lies in the provision of paired sera results from AIV contacts, which allows for the differentiation of recent from past infections and obviates the interference of baseline antibody titers [10] Leveraging years of experience in seroepidemiological surveillance, TCDC's robust public health framework fosters trust among contacts, thereby facilitating the collection of paired samples from over a hundred individuals. We did not perform routine molecular testing on asymptomatic contacts, which aligns with recommendations from international public health authorities [11], but may perform molecular testing in future outbreaks if indicated, for example, when a novel influenza A subtype is first identified in Taiwan.

Most contacts were associated with A(H5N1) HPAI outbreaks, as mandatory culling at all HPAI‐affected farms involved a substantially larger workforce (e.g., culling teams and disinfection personnel) compared to LPAI outbreaks, where only farm owners and hired workers were typically identified as contacts. Given this difference in exposure scale and the established pandemic potential of A(H5) subtypes, we prioritized HI testing against A(H5) viruses in the current study.

Limitations of the current study include selection bias because not all eligible contacts were included in the seroepidemiological study. Furthermore, PPE use was self‐reported and may not reflect actual compliance. The HI assay, although widely used as a standard serologic method for influenza, is less sensitive compared to microneutralization (MN) assays, particularly in cases of mild or asymptomatic infection [9]. Due to the unavailability of live influenza viruses and a biosafety level 3 (BSL‐3) laboratory, the MN assay was not performed, and we were unable to cross‐validate these findings with the HI results [15]. In addition, we did not assess potential seroconversion to non‐H5 subtypes such as A(H6N1), the predominant LPAI subtype in this study period. To address this limitation, serological testing against A(H6N1) has been incorporated into our surveillance program since 2026.

Overall, no evidence of AIV A(H5N1) seroconversion was identified among contacts of AIV outbreaks in Taiwan, validating the effectiveness of current biosafety protocols. Continued monitoring of high‐risk populations, promotion of seasonal influenza vaccination, and ongoing genomic surveillance of AIVs remain essential to detect potential zoonotic transmission at an early stage.
