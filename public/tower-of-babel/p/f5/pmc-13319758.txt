Actinidia Deliciosa mediated synthesis of antimony oxide nanoparticles and their incorporation into chitosan matrix for enhanced antibacterial and photocatalytic activity
Authors: Malik Taimur Khan, Wajid Rehman, Rida Zameer, Masab Saeed, Muhammed Shakeel, Thoraya A. Farghaly, Abdullah Y. A. Alzahrani, Zeeshan Arshid
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study examines the synthesis and characterization of antimony oxide (Sb2O3) nanoparticle and its combination with chitosan (Cs) to make Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposites. The green chemistry method of the Sb2O3 nanoparticles synthesis was supported by the Actinidia deliciosa (kiwi) extract that serves as reducing agent and stabilizing agent. The nanoparticles obtained were characterized by Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM), Energy Dispersive X-ray (EDX) analysis, X-ray Diffraction (XRD), and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) to study their morphology, elemental composition, crystallinity and functional groups respectively. The SEM images indicated a more compact, heterogeneous structure when the Sb2O3 nanoparticles were incorporated into the chitosan matrix to form Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposites, with the nanoparticles uniformly distributed in the chitosan matrix. XRD results established the formation of a pure senarmontite cubic phase of Sb2O3 in pure nanoparticles and nanocomposites. The findings indicated that the dispersion of Sb2O3 nanoparticles was highly enhanced by the presence of chitosan and this minimized aggregation and increased the stability of the material. The synthesized materials were tested against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli by zone of inhibition (ZOI) method of antibacterial activity as well as tested for photocatalytic degradation of rhodamine-B (rhB) under visible light. This study explains that Sb2O3 nanoparticles, and Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposites, possess significant potential in biomedical and pollution remediation applications.

Introduction

Nanotechnology is a fast-growing technology that involves the design, synthesis and use of materials in the nanoscale, which is normally between 1 and 100 nm (nm)1–5. Nanomaterials exhibit distinct physical, chemical, and biological properties that are quite different to those of the bulk material6,7. Nanomaterials have transformed various industries such as medicine, electronics and environmental science8,9. This capability to control materials on the atomic and molecular scale has created a new field of applications in bio sensing, drug delivery, photocatalysis and antimicrobial coatings10,11. Nanotechnology is a radical area that has significant contributions in other scientific disciplines and sectors12–14. It is significant in that it is able to modify materials at the atomic and molecular level and this results in the creation of new materials with improved properties like high strength, high conductivity, and high chemical reactivity14–16. These development have presented new opportunities in the fields of medicine, electronics, energy, and environmental science to the extent that nanotechnology is one of the most influential technological fields of the twenty-first century17.

Actinidia deliciosa, commonly known as kiwifruit, is a nutrient-rich fruit belonging to the Actinidiaceae family. It is originally from China, but is extensively cultivated in countries such as New Zealand, Italy, Greece, Iran and Chile due to its high commercial value, nutritional importance and health benefits18. Kiwifruit has a unique brown furry skin and bright green or golden flesh containing a high amount of vitamins, minerals and antioxidants19. Green synthesis using plant/fruit extracts has emerged as a sustainable alternative to conventional chemical routes because extract-derived biomolecules can act simultaneously as reducing, complexing, and stabilizing agents, enabling nanoparticle formation without harsh reagents. Several studies have reported biosynthesis of metal/metal-oxide nanoparticles using fruit or plant extracts due to their high content of polyphenols, organic acids, sugars, and vitamins, which can coordinate metal ions, promote nucleation, and limit uncontrolled aggregation. Similarly, polymer-assisted nanocomposites, especially chitosan-based systems, are widely explored because chitosan provides a biocompatible matrix, improves colloidal stability, and enhances surface interactions with bacterial cells and organic dyes through abundant –NH₂ and –OH groups20. Kiwi extract is rich in reported reducing/stabilizing biomolecules such as ascorbic acid and polyphenols, whose hydroxyl/carboxyl functionalities can donate electrons and adsorb onto growing nuclei, facilitating Sb₂O₃ formation and stabilizing nanoparticles against uncontrolled growth/aggregation21.

Antimony oxide (Sb2O3) nanoparticles have gained significant attention due to their multifunctional properties, such as optical transparency, electrical conductivity, antimicrobial activity and photocatalytic dye degradation properties. These nanoparticles exhibit unique properties due to their small size, high surface area and quantum effects making them suitable for various applications22. Recent reports have pointed out their use in biomedical and photocatalytic dye degradation applications, especially in the preparation of antibacterial coatings and wound dressings and water pollution remediations. The mechanism behind these activity is attributed to the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS)23. Although having promising characteristics, the green synthesis of antimony oxide nanoparticles has largely unexplored and more needs to be investigated. Chitosan is a biodegradable, biocompatible, and non-toxic polysaccharide derived from chitin. Chitosan is a linear polysaccharide made up of β-(1 → 4)-linked D-glucosamine (deacetylated unit) and N-acetyl-D-glucosamine (acetylated unit) units24–26. It is made by deacetylation of the shells of shrimp and other crustaceans with an alkaline substance, like sodium hydroxide27. Chitosan possesses several commercial and potential biomedical and photocatalytic applications. It can be used in agriculture as a seed coating and biopesticide, enabling the plants to resist fungal infections27. In wine production, it can serve as a fining agent, again preventing spoilage. In industry, it can be utilized in a self-healing polyurethane paint film. Medically, it find applications in bandages to stop bleeding and as an antibacterial agent; also it can be employed to assist in the delivery of drugs across the skin28. Chitosan is a biopolymer of chitin origin well known for its biocompatibility, biodegradability, and inherent antimicrobial properties29. Its chemical structure, consisting of amino and hydroxyl groups, allows for strong interactions with metal oxide nanoparticles and thus can be considered to be a good matrix for nanocomposite preparation30. The pure and modified Chitosan-based nanomaterials have found wide applications in drug delivery, tissue engineering, and food preservation and photocatalytic dye degradation in aqueous solution31. The incorporation of antimony oxide nanoparticles into a chitosan matrix can enhance its antibacterial and photocatalytic activity, providing a future role in medical coatings and pollution remediation31. Recent literature on cellulose acetate-based composites, such as Bi2WO6-incorporated CA and MoS2/NiFe-LDH-incorporated CA systems), demonstrates their potential for various applications. However, the Sb2O3-chitosan system offers advantages in terms of biocompatibility, biodegradability, and enhanced interfacial bonding, making it a promising candidate for biomedical and environmental applications32,33.

Green synthesis of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles using plant extracts is widely reported, particularly for systems such as ZnO, Ag, TiO₂, and Fe-based nanoparticles34. The reports on the green synthesis of Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles remain very limited, especially using fruit-based extracts. The novelty of the present work lies in the use of Actinidia deliciosa extract, which is rich in bioactive phytochemicals (e.g., phenolics and flavonoids) that act as both reducing and stabilizing agents, enabling controlled nanoparticle formation. the use of Actinidia deliciosa extract for Sb2O3 synthesis is novel compared to existing green synthesis reports of metal oxides (e.g., using plant extracts like Aloe vera or tea leaves). This work distinguishes itself through the unique phytochemical composition of the extract, which influences the particle size, shape, and properties of Sb2O3 nanoparticles. However, although metal-oxide nanomaterials and chitosan-based antimicrobial/photocatalytic composites are of growing interest, the gap in the body of existing information on this alternative remains that no one has reported the use of Actinidia deliciosa (kiwi) extract as a bioreductant/stabilizer to make Sb2O3 nanoparticles and no one has reported the systematic incorporation of kiwi-derived Sb2O3 in a chitosan-based matrix to create an antibacterial/ This gap is valuable because a sustainable strategy of synthesis in conjunction with a biopolymer stabilized nanostructure can diminish aggregation, enhance the stability of materials, and augment realistic work in biomedical coverings, and wastewater detoxification. Accordingly, this paper seeks to prepare Sb2O3 nanoparticles using green chemistry mediated by kiwi-extracts, and to prepare Sb2O3 chitosan (Sb2O3-Cs) nanocomposites, which have enhanced functionality. The objectives are as follows: (i) to produce Sb2O3 nanoparticles at two concentrations of the precursor (2 mM and 4 mM) with the help of kiwi extract; (ii) to embed Sb2O3 into chitosan to create Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposites; (iii) to verify the phase formation and morphology, elemental composition, and the interactions between Sb2O3 and the surface solutes with XRD, SEM/EDX, and FTIR; In this regard, the following research questions were answered in this study: (1) Does incorporation of chitosan enhance better dispersion and interfacial bonding of Sb2O3 in comparison with bare nanoparticles? (2) Does the concentration of precursors (2 mM and 4 mM) affect morphology and antibacterial action against Gram-positive and Gram-negative strains? (3) Does Sb2O3-Cs provide rapidvisible light RhB degradation with constant performance in repeated cycles? On the whole, the current work offers a sustainable synthesis pathway and shows a biopolymer-stabilized Sb2O3 platform, having both antibacterial and photocatalytic properties, which enhances the scientific and practical importance of Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposites use in medicine and the environment.

Experimental

Material

Antimony trichloride (SbCl3), Ethanol (99.9%), Antimony Trichloride (SbCl₃), Chitosan (Cs), Acetic Acid are purchased from Sigma Aldrich. Muller-Hinton Agar (MHA) for the Growth medium for antibacterial testing and Muller-Hinton Broth (MHB) for the Growth medium for antibacterial testing are purchased from Condalab, Laboratorios Conda Spain. All the solutions were prepared using distilled water. Actinidia deliciosa (Kiwi) Fruit was collected from Baffa in the Mansehra district of Khyber Pakhtunkhwa, Pakistan.

Preparation of Actinidia deliciosa extract

The Actinidia deliciosa (kiwi) extract was prepared using fresh peeled slices of fruit. First, 20g of the peeled slices were blended to obtain kiwi fruit juice. The juice was then filtered through a mesmerized cloth and subjected to centrifugation to remove any solid residues. Finally, 20mL of distilled water was added to the filtered extract to achieve the desired consistency. The resulting A. deliciosa extract was then ready for further use as shown in Fig. 1.Fig. 1Preparation of plant extract.

Preparation of plant extract.

Synthesis of A. deliciosa mediated antimony oxide (Sb2O3) nanoparticles

2mM antimony trichloride solution is prepared by dissolving 0.45g of SbCl3 in 20 mL 30:70 of water: ethanol. Solution was kept for the stirring at 300rpm for 2h at 60°C. Meanwhile 10mL of kiwi fruit extract was taken and added dropwise into the SbCl3 solution. By the addition of extract the white precipitates were appeared. The solution mixture was sonicated for 30min at 60°C. The solution mixture is then centrifuged at 1500rpm for 30min. The supernatant was removed and the pellets (Sb2O3 NPs) were washed with distilled water and ethanol for 4–5 times. The synthesized Sb2O3 Nanoparticles were calcined at 400°C. Sb2O3 nanoparticles were collected, crushed and collected for further use. Similar procedure was followed for the synthesis of 4mM Sb2O3 nanoparticles. 0.9g of SbCl3 in 20 mL 30:70 of water:ethanol followed by the stirring at 300rpm for 2h at 60°C. Meanwhile 10mL of kiwi fruit extract was taken and added dropwise into the SbCl3 solution. Precipitates were appeared and the sonicated, centrifugated, calcined, crushed and collected for further use. Synthesis of antimony oxide loaded chitosan (Sb2O3-Cs) nanocomposites

Initially, 0.25g of Sb2O3 NPs were added in 5mL distilled water and sonicated for 30 min. Meanwhile 0.1g chitosan (Cs) was taken and dissolved in 10mL of 10% acetic acid solution using hotplate at 300rpm. The Sb2O3 NPs were added to Cs solution to form Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite solution. The solution mixture was kept for the stirring for 30 min, followed by sonication for 45 min until the uniform distribution of Sb2O3 NPs in Cs matrix was achieved. The Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite was kept for drying overnight. The Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite was dried, crushed and collected for further use (Fig. 3).Fig. 3Synthesis of antimony oxide loaded chitosan (Sb2O3-Cs) nanocomposites.

Characterization techniques

The Sb2O3 nanoparticles and Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite were synthesized successfully and characterized using the following techniques. The surface morphology and shape of the Sb2O3 nanoparticles and Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite were studied using scanning electron microscope at centralized resource laboratory, University of Peshawar. Energy dispersive x-ray was coupled with scanning electron microscopy. FTIR was used to study the functional group present in the Sb2O3 nanoparticles and Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite. XRD analysis was carried out using diffractometer to study the crystalline structure of the synthesized materials. The Debye–Scherrer method was used to analyze the structure of crystalline materials by examining how X-rays are diffracted when they pass through a powdered sample.

Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of synthesized Sb2O3 nanoparticles and Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite was tested using Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureus. Muller-Hinton Agar and Muller-Hinton Broth were used as growth media. Bacterial suspensions were standardized to a 0.5 McFarland standard before testing. For the disk diffusion method, MHA plates were prepared inoculated with bacterial suspensions using sterile cotton swabs. Sterile 6 mm disks were impregnated with 2mm Sb2O3 nanoparticles and Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite and 4mm Sb2O3 nanoparticles and Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite and placed on the plates. The plates were incubated at 37C for 18–24 h, and the zone of inhibition was measured in millimeters. For the well diffusion method, agar plates were inoculated with bacterial suspensions.

Photocatalytic degradation of dyes

The photocatalytic performance of the Sb2O3-Cs, along with the reference materials, was evaluated under visible-light irradiation. For each experiment, 0.1 g of catalyst was added to 100 mL of RhB solution (20 mg L⁻1). The suspension was first kept in the dark to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium, after which it was illuminated using a Hg lamp equipped with a cutoff filter (λ > 420 nm) while continuously stirred. The light source was placed approximately 10 cm from the reaction vessel to ensure efficient activation of the photocatalytic process. At regular time intervals, aliquots of the reaction mixture were withdrawn, the catalyst particles were removed, and the remaining RhB concentration was determined using a colorimetric method. Control experiments confirmed that RhB did not degrade in the absence of either the catalyst or light irradiation. The extent of degradation was quantified by comparing the absorbance of the reaction solution with its initial value and correlating it to a standard calibration curve. To ensure reproducibility of the visible-light photocatalytic tests, the experimental setup and analytical method were clarified in detail. RhB degradation was carried out under visible irradiation using a Hg lamp equipped with a cutoff filter (λ > 420 nm). The catalyst–dye suspension was magnetically stirred throughout the experiment, and a dark adsorption step was performed prior to illumination to establish adsorption–desorption equilibrium. At fixed time intervals, aliquots were withdrawn and the catalyst was removed by centrifugation/filtration to avoid scattering effects. The remaining RhB concentration was quantified using UV–Vis spectrophotometry by monitoring the decrease in absorbance at RhB’s maximum absorption wavelength (λmax ≈ 554 nm) and converting absorbance to concentration using a calibration curve. Control experiments (light without catalyst, and catalyst in dark) confirmed that degradation was negligible without the combined action of catalyst and visible light.

Results and discussion

Scanning electron microscopy

The SEM images in Fig. 4 reveal the morphology of 2 mM Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles at varying magnifications (1,000 × to 60,000x). At lower magnifications (a-b), the nanoparticles appear aggregated into larger clusters, likely due to electrostatic interactions or drying effects during sample preparation. Higher magnifications (c-d) resolve individual nanoparticles, showing irregular spherical or polyhedral shapes. The variation in particle size suggests incomplete uniformity during synthesis, possibly influenced by the kiwi extract-mediated reduction process or calcination conditions. SEM images of 4 mM Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles Fig. 5a–d reveal densely packed, irregularly shaped particles with enhanced agglomeration compared to the 2 mM sample. At low magnification (a, b), the nanoparticles display a granular morphology with smaller particle sizes and closer packing. High-magnification images (c, d) show more pronounced clustering and surface roughness, possibly due to increased nucleation rate at higher precursor concentration. The particles remain non-uniform but appear finer, indicating that a higher molar concentration of SbCl₃ results in more abundant nucleation centers, producing smaller yet more aggregated Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles. The SEM images Fig. 6a–d of the 2 mM Sb₂O₃-Chitosan nanocomposite reveals a heterogeneous and compact structure. At lower magnification (a, b), the surface appears rough and layered, with Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles embedded within the chitosan matrix. The chitosan provides a continuous phase that hosts the dispersed Sb₂O₃ particles. Higher magnification images (c, d) show aggregated clusters of nanoparticles, indicating good interaction but some degree of particle agglomeration. The morphology suggests that the biopolymer matrix effectively encapsulates the nanoparticles, enhancing dispersion while maintaining nanoscale features, which is crucial for potential antibacterial activity and material stability. The SEM images in Fig. 7 demonstrate the morphology of 4mM Sb₂O₃-chitosan (Sb₂O₃-Cs) nanocomposites at magnifications from 1,000 × to 60,000 × . At lower magnifications (a-b), the nanocomposites exhibit reduced aggregation compared to pure Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles (Figs. 3), forming more defined, and interconnected structures. Higher magnifications (c-d) reveal a uniform distribution of Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles embedded within the chitosan matrix, confirming successful nanocomposite formation. Quantitative particle size distribution analysis reveals an average particle size of 84.8nm for 2mM Sb2O3 nanoparticles, 85.6nm for 4mM Sb2O3 nanoparticles, 88nm for 2mM Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite and 89.6nm for 4mM Sb2O3-Cs nanocomposite. The chitosan acts as a stabilizing and dispersing agent, mitigating particle aggregation observed in pure Sb₂O₃ samples. The improved homogeneity suggests effective encapsulation of Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles during synthesis, likely due to electrostatic interactions between positively charged chitosan (in acetic acid) and Sb₂O₃ surfaces. Antibacterial activity

The antibacterial activity of Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles and Cs-Sb₂O₃ nanocomposites was evaluated against two bacterial strains, Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli, by measuring the zone of inhibition (ZOI). The results indicate that both Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles and Cs-Sb₂O₃ nanocomposites possess significant antibacterial properties, with varying levels of efficacy depending on the concentration and type of bacterial strain. In the case of Staphylococcus aureus, the 4mM Sb2O3 nanoparticles had the highest zone of inhibition ZOI of 22.8 mm, which means that it has a high antibacterial activity against this Gram-positive bacterium. The 2mM Sb2O3 nanoparticles also showed a considerable activity having a ZOI of 15.7 mm, which indicates towards the dose-dependent effect of the nanoparticles. The Cs-Sb2O3 nanocomposites on the other hand were effective but slightly less active than the pure Sb2O3 nanoparticles with ZOI of 13.2 mm and 12.7 mm at the 4mM and 2mM concentrations respectively. This decrease can be explained by the interaction between antimony oxide nanoparticles and the chitosan matrix, which could prevent complete release of active components responsible for antibacterial properties. For Escherichia coli, the largest ZOI was recorded for the 2mM Cs-Sb₂O₃ nanocomposites at 13.2 mm, closely followed by the 2mM Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles at 12.9 mm. For 4mM Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles the efficacy was somewhat lower towards E. coli, with a ZOI of 11.4 mm, which suggests the Gram-negative bacteria are less sensitive towards the nanoparticles compared to the Gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus. The 4mM Cs-Sb₂O₃ nanocomposites exhibited the ZOI of 10.9 mm, reinforcing the observation that the presence of chitosan reduces the antimicrobial potency against Escherichia coli. The positive control exhibited moderate inhibition against both strains, while the negative control showed no antibacterial effect, verifying the success of the experiment. Overall, the results demonstrate the promising antibacterial efficacy of Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles and Cs-Sb₂O₃ nanocomposites, with differences in their activity against various bacterial strains. In some cases the SB2O3 nanoparticles have a greater ZOI than composite. This is due to their fully exposed reactive surface, which allows immediate interaction of nanoparticles with bacterial cell membranes. However, incorporation into the chitosan matrix provides stabilization and protection, resulting in a controlled and sustained antibacterial effect. The slight reduction in activity of the nanocomposite is likely due to gradual Sb3⁺ ion release and reduced direct surface exposure, rather than loss of functionality. Chitosan improves dispersion, reduces nanoparticle toxicity, and enhances adhesion to microbial cells. Thus the nanocomposite also offers improved stability, biocompatibility, and prolonged antibacterial performance, making it more suitable for practical applications. These results indicate that the modification of Sb₂O₃ with chitosan may be a potential method for promoting the material’s antibacterial properties for potential biomedical applications.

Photocatalytic degradation performance

The visible-light-driven photocatalytic degradation behavior of the SbO3-Cs nanocomposite and the corresponding reference materials is presented in Fig. 12a. In the absence of any catalyst, the concentration of RhB remains essentially constant during 45 min of illumination, confirming its photostability under these conditions. Once catalysts are introduced, the degradation efficiencies follow the sequence Sb2O3-Cs (2 mM) > Sb2O3-Cs (4 mM) > Sb2O3 (2 mM) > Sb2O3 (4 mM) Within 10 min of visible-light irradiation, Sb2O3 (2 mM) and Sb2O3 (4 mM) achieve only about 45% and 35% RhB degradation, respectively. Remarkably, the SbO3-Cs nanocomposite reaches degradation efficiencies up to 90% within 10 min, highlighting their outstanding performance. The enhanced photocatalytic behavior of the SbO3-Cs nanocomposite system under visible light primarily arises from the synergistic interaction between the two components and the favorable alignment of their band structures [67]. However, when the Sb2O3 content is excessively increased to 4 mM, the activity markedly decreases. This decline is attributed to structural interference, reduces the number of accessible active sites, and hinders charge transport. Furthermore, excessive Sb2O3 weakens the interfacial bonding, potentially compromising the heterojunction integrity. Advanced oxidation processes (AOPs) rely on the formation of highly reactive oxygen species (ROS), which aggressively oxidize organic pollutants. Under visible-light excitation, both Sb2O3 and Sb2O3-CS generate electron–hole pairs due to their suitable band edge positions. Upon illumination, electrons are promoted from the VB to the CB of both semiconductors. Since the CB of Sb2O3 lies at a more positive potential relative to Sb2O3-CS, electrons preferentially transfer from Sb2O3-CS to Sb2O3. Conversely, holes migrate from Sb2O3 to Sb2O3-CS due to the VB potential difference. This bidirectional transfer suppresses recombination and promotes efficient participation of both electrons and holes in the photocatalytic reactions. The main reactive species identified in the degradation process include photogenerated holes (h⁺), superoxide radicals (O₂⁻·), and hydroxyl radicals (·OH). Electrons accumulated in the CB reduce O₂ to O₂⁻·, which subsequently reacts with water or trapped holes to form ·OH radicals. Although antimony-based precursors can be considered less common than some widely used oxides, Sb₂O₃ offers a practical balance between functional performance and material stability, and it is markedly less expensive than noble-metal photocatalysts (e.g., Ag, Au, Pt). In this study, the overall approach is cost-aware because (i) the synthesis uses a green extract route that minimizes additional chemicals, (ii) chitosan is an inexpensive and abundant biopolymer, (iii) the catalyst dose is low and performance is high (rapid RhB degradation), and (iv) the composite shows strong reusability over multiple cycles, reducing the effective cost per treatment cycle. Thus, the selected material system is positioned as a scalable and recyclable platform for environmental remediation rather than a single-use laboratory catalyst.

Conclusions

This work effectively showed the green synthesis of Sb₂O₃ nanoparticles and their incorporation into chitosan (Cs) to form Sb₂O₃-Cs nanocomposites. The functional group, crystalline structure, elemental composition, and the effective addition of Sb2O3 to the chitosan framework were confirmed by the characterization techniques, including FTIR, SEM, EDS, and XRD. SEM showed irregular nanoparticles with agglomeration, more compact and denser at 4 mM, whereas the Sb₂O₃–Cs composites exhibited a compact and interconnected polymeric network with improved nanoparticle dispersion. EDX verified Sb and O as the dominant elements and a stronger C signal in the composites due to the chitosan matrix, while FTIR displayed chitosan bands (–OH/–NH and C–O–C) together with Sb–O vibrations; changes in band intensity and position supported interfacial interactions between Sb₂O₃ and chitosan. Antibacterial assays against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli revealed significant, strain-dependent inhibition. The highest activity was obtained for 4 mM Sb₂O₃ against S. aureus (22.8 mm ZOI), while the best response against E. coli was achieved by 2 mM Sb₂O₃–Cs (13.2 mm ZOI), underscoring the importance of optimized loading and polymer-assisted dispersion. In photocatalysis, Sb₂O₃–Cs outperformed bare Sb₂O₃ under visible light: within 10 min, Sb₂O₃ (2 mM) and Sb₂O₃ (4 mM) degraded ~ 45% and ~ 35% RhB, respectively, whereas Sb₂O₃–Cs reached up to ~ 90% (2 mM composite). Control tests confirmed negligible RhB loss without catalyst or in the dark. The composite maintained high efficiency over five consecutive cycles, confirming good stability and reusability. These findings demonstrate that Sb2O3 and Cs-Sb2O3 nanocomposites have enormous potential in many biomedical as well as photocatalytic applications.
