Enhanced recovery following neck of femur fractures – improving outcomes
Authors: Abbas See, Lauren A Taylor, Katie E Rollins, David W Hewson, Adam L Gordon, Andrew D Duckworth, Xavier L Griffin, Ben J Ollivere
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsThe number of hip fractures is increasing, with significant mortality and morbidity, particularly among frail and comorbid patients. Enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathways have proven effective in elective orthopaedics, but this has not been investigated in people with hip fractures. This study aimed to identify current perioperative practice and develop a cohesive ERAS pathway tailored for hip fracture patients, to standardize and optimize care.MethodsWe conducted a cross-sectional survey-based study across trauma professionals. The survey was disseminated to an expert panel via direct mailing through research collaboratives and social media. Net promoter scores (NPS) were used to quantify survey responses, with a score of over 30 indicating that respondents felt strongly that a particular intervention should be included in the ERAS pathway.ResultsResponses were received from 51 institutions and a variety of trauma professionals. Respondents rated preoperative, perioperative, and postoperative interventions for inclusion in an ERAS pathway. Highly rated preoperative intervention included nerve blockade (NPS = 81) and delirium risk assessment (NPS = 69). Avoidance of intraoperative hypotension (NPS = 45) was the only perioperative intervention scoring above 30. Key postoperative interventions included geriatric review (NPS = 74) and Day 0 mobilization (NPS = 53). Overall, 87.5% of respondents expressed willingness to join a trial comparing ERAS to standard care.ConclusionThis study highlights the need for a standardized ERAS pathway for people who experience hip fractures. Key recommendations include preoperative optimization, early mobilization, and multidisciplinary input, all of which align with existing ERAS guidelines. Further research should focus on conducting feasibility studies to refine and validate this pathway.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2025;6(9):1065–1072.Keywords: Hip fracture, Neck of femur fracture, Enhanced recovery, Perioperative care, neck of femur fractures, hip fractures, Delirium, morbidity, trauma, intraoperative hypotension, nerve, anaemia, hip and knee arthroplasty

Introduction

The number of hip fractures is increasing, from 67, 000 in 2019 to 72,160 in 2022.1 Further data have shown that people experiencing hip fractures are more likely to be frail and living with multiple long-term conditions.2 Frailty is associated with greater morbidity and mortality after hip fracture.3 Valdes et al3 reported that hip fracture patients who were frail had a six-month mortality rate of 27%, compared to 6% for those who were not frail. Hence, more people with neck of femur (NOF) fractures are at an increased risk of mortality and morbidity.

Recent estimates of one-year mortality rate following NOF fracture can reach as high as 28%, with inpatient stays extending to up to 41 days. However, various organizational strategies such as delirium prevention and early postoperative mobilization have the potential to reduce length of stay, mortality rates, and costs per patient.4 This highlights the need for a multicomponent system to optimize patient care in the form of an enhanced recovery after surgery (ERAS) pathway.

ERAS is a multimodal approach to optimizing perioperative care to achieve early recovery.5 There are several components, including enhancing nutrition, optimizing pain relief, reducing surgical stress response, and early postoperative mobilization.6

There is a consensus on the use of ERAS pathways within orthopaedics, but this is focused on elective hip and knee arthroplasty,7 which has been successfully implemented in several sites across the UK.8 There is limited evidence of ERAS pathways in hip fracture patients despite a positive effect on time to surgery, length of stay, and overall complication rate.9 Centres across other countries have introduced ERAS pathways for hip fractures with positive effects on length of stay and 30-day mortality, however data on long-term outcomes are limited.10

Data from the Hospital Episode Statistics for England highlight considerable variation in NOF management and subsequent outcomes at an institutional level;4 these variations highlight the need to improve and standardize care.

To address this uncertainty, this study aims to identify current perioperative practice and develop a cohesive ERAS pathway tailored specifically for hip fracture patients, with the goal of standardizing and optimizing care. The objectives of this survey were to determine standard clinical practice for managing NOF across UK trauma centres, and to seek opinions of trauma professionals to understand which elements of care they felt should be part of an ERAS intervention.

Methods

Study setting and participants

A survey was conducted across staff working in acute hospitals between 1 March and 30 April 2024. Responses were collected by inviting participants across Europe to complete an online survey hosted on Microsoft Forms (Supplementary Material). The survey was disseminated to an expert panel via direct mailing to research collaboratives (the NIHR WHITE Hip Fracture platform network, Academic Anaesthetists, British Geriatrics Society) and disseminated via social media (X/Twitter, Facebook).

A total of 52 responses were received from 51 institutions, 48 of which were across the UK, two in Spain, and one in Austria.

Study design

This is a cross-sectional, survey-based study. The reporting of this study followed the Consensus-based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS) guidelines (Supplementary Material). Ethical approval was not required as this was a survey of current practice.

Questionnaire

To assist in survey item selection, one investigator (KER) conducted an informal examination of previously published ERAS interventions. Interventions were extracted from existing guidelines in hip and knee arthroplasty as well as emergency laparotomy.7,11,12

The survey consisted of 38 items (Supplementary Material). Key demographic details of respondents, including hospital and speciality, were recorded.

In our survey, respondents were asked to indicate which intervention they would include in an ERAS pathway. Respondents were given a list of items encompassing different aspects of care and asked to rate each one on a Likert scale from 1 to 10, with 1 indicating ‘not at all likely’ and 10 indicating ‘extremely likely’.

Statistical analysis

A net promotor score (NPS) was used to quantify survey responses. NPS is a measure of respondent satisfaction and representative of how likely a respondent is to recommend the item.13 To calculate the NPS, the percentage of detractors (respondents scoring 0 to 6) is subtracted from the percentage of promoters (respondents scoring 9 or 10) (Figure 1).13 Hence, the value of the NPS can range from -100 to +100.13 NPS was chosen due to its simplicity, ease of interpretation, and ability to provide a single actionable metric, enabling clear benchmarking and prioritization of improvements.

Categorization and calculation of net promoter score (NPS).13

A NPS of over 30 indicated a strongly positive intervention that was included in our proposed ERAS pathway. A score of 30 is a generally accepted benchmark for a positive attitude towards a particular item and is comparable to similar thresholds used in traditional Delphi approaches.14,15

The NPS was used to capture attitudes towards each intervention, offering a quantifiable measure of support or opposition. Unlike the traditional Delphi method, which typically promotes or eliminates interventions through consensus, this approach enables all interventions to be retained and assessed based on the strength of opinion.

Discussion

The ERAS pathway we have recommended shares many similarities with the guidelines that have already been established for elective orthopaedics. Common components include preoperative education, avoiding prolonged fasting, correction of preoperative anaemia, enhancing nutrition, optimizing analgesia, intraoperative fluid maintenance, and early mobilization.7,8 There are obvious differences as well, which is unsurprising given that the NOF population is vastly different from patients undergoing elective surgery. These differences include delirium assessment, orthogeriatric assessment, information leaflets, avoidance of surgical drains or catheters, orthogeriatric review, nutritional assessment, and pharmacy-led medication reconciliation. Many of the perioperative interventions highlighted and recommended in this study, e.g. prompt admission to a specialist and early mobilization, are already internationally used performance indicators in hip fracture care.16

Each component of this pathway has individually been shown to positively affect outcomes in NOF patients. Fast-track admission into a specialist hip fracture unit has been shown to reduce length of stay and 30-day mortality, and improve return to pre-injury residence in UK centres.17 Admission within 12 hours and early mobilization are standard in South and Southeast Asia.18 Furthermore, it has also been shown to facilitate more comprehensive preoperative assessment and reduced mortality.19 These improvements highlight the need for more of these facilities.

Correction of preoperative anaemia is also key: 40% of hip fracture patients are admitted with anaemia, which is associated with increased mortality.20 Postoperative anaemia also impedes rehabilitative capacity.21

In older NOF patients, optimizing analgesia is synonymous with avoiding opiates due to side effects of respiratory depression, confusion, and constipation. Preoperative nerve blockade is recommended and has been shown to reduce pain and opiate use.22

Patient counselling is vital, but often limited in the NOF population due to concurrent delirium or cognitive impairment. It is advocated that family members and carers are involved to reduce readmission rates, length of stay, and increase care satisfaction.23 However, communication is often suboptimal, and lack of standardization in information-sharing is perceived as disorganized and poor-quality care.24 Hence, we advocate the use of patient information leaflets or apps to share information.

Perioperative orthogeriatric assessment is already a national guideline and a regular part of hip fracture care.25 It is linked with reduced length of stay, decreased one-year mortality, and better return to pre-injury function.26 Early orthogeriatric assessment also leads to reduced rate of major complications.27 This is likely due to experienced medical clinicians dealing with medical issues as soon as they occur postoperatively, and better preoperative medical optimization.

Delirium in NOF patients is highly prevalent, with one in seven hip fracture patients at risk of developing it.28 This is associated with increased risk of cerebrovascular events, adverse cardiac events and lung infection, and increased length of stay and one-year mortality.28 Pre- and postoperative cognitive assessment has also been shown to improve quality of life scores up to four months post injury.29 Initially, we suggest that ERAS pathways focus on screening and managing delirium. However, there will be future opportunities to standardize and refine aspects of the pathway around medication review, pain management, and minimization of environmental contributors to delirium, which could further improve outcomes.

In this study, the ‘sip til send’ protocol was highly favoured. With this, patients can drink small volumes of water or clear liquids until transfer to the operating theatre. This has been shown to lead to lower rates of postoperative nausea and vomiting and reduced fasting duration, with no significant risk of regurgitation or aspiration.30

Avoidance of intraoperative hypotension was the most popular intraoperative treatment with a NPS of 45. The Anaesthesia Sprint Audit of Practice (ASAP)-2 study showed that lower systolic blood pressures were associated with higher mortality at 30 days postoperatively, regardless of baseline mortality risk.31 Recent evidence has also shown that this intervention may reduce the incidence of postoperative cognitive dysfunction, such as delirium, which is highly prevalent among older adults.32

There was a higher proportion of negative NPS in the intraoperative recommendations. This is likely to reflect the variety of responders: only 31% of responders were anaesthetists, with other responders being less likely to promote a treatment that was beyond their field of expertise. In addition, there is little evidence to support one type of anaesthetic over the other.33 The Association of Anaesthetists of Great Britain and Ireland have also stated that differences between anaesthetics are unlikely to make significant differences in outcomes.33

The greatest proportion of positive responses was in the postoperative recommendations, with five out of seven recommendations receiving a NPS of over 30. Responders felt strongly that no surgical drains or catheters should be used. It has been shown that there is no difference in infection, wound problems, reoperation, or other complications with use of a drain in NOF patients.34 Prolonged duration of urinary catheter use is also associated with increased incidence of urinary tract infection (UTI) and postoperative urinary retention,35 and UTI is subsequently associated with increased risk of surgical site infection.36 Hence, we recommend that patients have urinary catheters removed as soon as possible and surgical drains not be used as part of standard care.

The goal of all NOF surgeries is to allow immediate postoperative mobilization. It has been shown that patients who do not mobilize on postoperative day 1 are at higher risk of not returning to their pre-injury mobility, and that they are 46% more likely to die in hospital.37,38 Multiple factors can affect this, including postoperative delirium, hypotension, and pain.39 Hence, it is vital that the perioperative management of these patients focuses on optimizing early mobilization.

Nutritional status is key in ERAS guidelines across multiple specialties, and was highly prioritized in this study with a NPS of 63.5,6 Poor nutritional status is associated with higher baseline morbidity, higher mortality, and higher readmission rates.40 Methods of improving nutrition include reducing fasting times, allowing relatives to bring in favourite foods and improving meal options to make them more appetizing.41 Further augmentation of the pathway might include addressing micronutrient deficiency, in particular exploring the role of vitamin B in preventing postoperative delirium.42

NOF patients are at high risk of adverse effects secondary to polypharmacy, with drug discrepancies on admission reaching as high as 78%.43 Hence, there is a clear need for intervention in the form of pharmacist-led reconciliation of medicines. This intervention also allows the appropriateness of medicines to be assessed. Involvement of the pharmacy team also leads to less variation in practice and outcomes when compared to reconciliations performed solely by physicians. This has been shown to reduce inappropriate medication prescriptions, and is already a recommendation in anaesthetic guidelines.44

A multidisciplinary approach to hip fracture rehabilitation has been shown to improve outcomes,45 but there is uncertainty and debate about optimal configuration. We recognize that this is an important area for further consideration going forward, since ERAS pathways can only improve patient outcomes if they are discharged into the most appropriate rehabilitation pathways.

Our study shows that an ERAS guideline is both needed and would be widely accepted by a range of healthcare staff. Many of the interventions recommended are already in place in several sites, as summarized in Figure 3. Our study also showed that most respondents (87.5%) would be willing to participate in further research in this field.

We are aware of the limitations of this study. The number of respondents was relatively small, but from a broad base of clinical and practice location backgrounds. Nevertheless, this study still highlights key interventions that can be included into an ERAS pathway that are also part of other ERAS guidelines. We would also like to highlight that the interventions promoted in this study are based on the NPS, which reflects the attitudes of the respondents. As such, a negative NPS will not disqualify a particular intervention from further inclusion, but more research is required to formulate a robust and thorough ERAS guideline.

In conclusion, given the high morbidity and mortality associated with NOF patients, there is a need to optimize care. We feel that this would be best done via a standardized ERAS pathway which is evidenced-based and pragmatic. Our study has highlighted key elements of current perioperative and intraoperative care that forms the basis of a recommended ERAS pathway. However, further work is required in the form of a feasibility or cohort study to further refine the pathway to improve outcomes of NOF patients.

Take home message

- This body of work highlights the variation in practice within the perioperative management of neck of femur fractures.

- A robust enhanced recovery protocol is required to standardize care and optimize outcomes.

- This paper is the first step in developing this guideline.
