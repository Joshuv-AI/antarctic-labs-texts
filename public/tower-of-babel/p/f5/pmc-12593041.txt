The Long-Range High-pH Proton Channel Facilitates the Nitrite Reduction in Copper-Containing Nitrite Reductase
Authors: Xin Qin, Li Li, Qiulan Zhou, Xiaohua Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Copper-containing nitrite reductase (CuNiR) catalyzes the conversion of nitrite (NO2 –) to nitric oxide (NO), requiring sequential proton delivery through long-range high-pH and primary proton channels. But the proton transfer (PT) and electron transfer (ET) mechanisms during the critical second protonation remain unclear. Here, we investigate these bioenergetic mechanisms using a two-layer ONIOM approach. Results reveal distinct pathways: the high-pH channel features a water-mediated three-stage PT from Glu113 to WS3, followed by direct PT to Asp98 and proton-coupled β-ET to nitrous acid (HNO2). In contrast, the primary channel proceeds via water-mediated three-stage PTs from Lys128 to His255 and then a water-mediated two-stage proton-coupled β-ET to HNO2. Comparative energy analysis shows that the second protonation of NO2 – is the overall rate-limiting step with the long-range high-pH channel serving as the preferred pathway in CuNiR catalysis. These fundamental mechanisms of CuNiR deepen our understanding of the biological denitrification.

Introduction

The nitrogen cycle is an important process that maintains the balance of inorganic and organic nitrogen in ecosystems, including nitrogen fixation and denitrification. The nitrogen-fixing microorganisms convert inorganic nitrogen from the atmosphere into organic forms, while nitrogen oxides in organisms are released back into the atmosphere through anaerobic respiration by microorganisms, such as bacteria and fungi. More recently, human activities, particularly the discovery of the Haber–Bosch process, have disrupted the nitrogen balance in ecosystems, leading to numerous environmental problems, especially eutrophication and algal blooms in aquatic ecosystems. Denitrification is currently the only proven process that converts nitrate into gaseous dinitrogen. , To mitigate the impact of human activities on the nitrogen balance, it is imperative to elucidate the detailed mechanisms of biological denitrification and uncover its intrinsic nature and patterns.

The catalytic reduction of nitrite (NO2 –) to nitric oxide (NO) by copper-containing nitrite reductase (CuNiR) is the second step of biological denitrification. CuNiR can be categorized into green and blue subtypes based on their distinct ultraviolet–visible spectral signatures and their specific interactions with exogenous electron donor proteins (pseudoazurin for green CuNiR and azurin or cytochrome c for blue CuNiR). , Both subclasses share a homotrimeric architecture, with each monomer harboring a type-one copper (T1Cu) center and a type-two copper (T2Cu) site. , The T1Cu center is coordinated by two histidine (His) residues, a cysteine (Cys) residue, and a methionine (Met) residue, enabling electron shuttling from the exogenous electron donor proteins to the T2Cu site during catalysis. The T2Cu site, which serves as the catalytic hub for nitrite reduction, is coordinated by three His residues and a water (H2O) ligand. Each catalytic cycle requires the consumption of one electron and two protons. The reaction initiates with the nitrite substrate displacing the H2O ligand and coordinating to the T2Cu site. , Subsequently, electron transfer (ET) from the T1Cu site to the oxidized T2Cu site proceeds via a conserved ∼12.5 Å Cys–His bridge, while proton transfer (PT) from the extracellular solution to the nitrite substrate is facilitated through the proton channels within CuNiR.

The mechanistic sequence of ET, PT, and nitrite binding at the T2Cu site remains a subject of active debate. Experimental and theoretical studies have proposed either an ordered sequential mechanism where inter-Cu ET precedes nitrite binding , or a concentration/pH-dependent random sequential mechanism. , Ghosh and coworkers constructed a two-dimensional reaction coordinate diagram for NO generation during CuNiR catalysis, revealing spontaneous HO–ON bond breaking concomitant with ET from T2Cu+ to nitrous acid (HNO2). Their findings suggest that T2Cu-mediated HO–ON bond cleavage occurs without requiring secondary protonation. Our prior work demonstrated that the first protonation of NO2 – generates the T2Cu+–HNO2 intermediate, necessitating subsequent proton delivery to complete NO formation. However, the first protonation at the substrate-binding pocket induces steric and electronic hindrance, gatekeeping subsequent proton ingress into the active site. The theoretical research by Silaghi-Dumitrescu revealed that HNO2 forms a stable coordination complex with oxidized T2Cu2+, triggering spontaneous cleavage of the HO–ON bond to generate NO+ and T2Cu2+–OH–. Maekawa et al. employed organometallic T2Cu model complexes to systematically map the pH-dependent reaction pathway in CuNiR. The corresponding results demonstrate that HNO2 reduction operates as a minor pathway under neutral conditions but becomes dominant under acidic conditions. While the binding of the first proton may impede the influx of a second proton, the long-range proton and electron transfer mechanisms underlying the second protonation of NO2 – remain mysteries from both computational and experimental perspectives.

Two conserved long-range proton transfer pathways, including the high-pH proton channel and the primary proton channel, have been shown to mediate sequential delivery of protons from the external aqueous solution to the T2Cu site in CuNiR. − These structurally ordered hydrogen-bond (H-bond) networks exhibit a high degree of conservation in green CuNiRs from Alcaligenes faecalis (AfNiR) , and Achromobacter cycloclastes (AcNiR), , as well as in the blue CuNiR from Alcaligenes xylosoxidans (AxNiR). , Structural analyses of the dual proton transfer channels reveal their distinct functional architectures. The high-pH proton channel originates from Asp98 through Ans96 (Asn90 in AxNiR), Gly109 (Gly103 in AxNiR), and Ala137 (Ala131 in AxNiR), via three water molecules then to the entrance gatekeeper residue (Glu113 in AfNiR, Gln113 in AcNiR, or Ans107 in AxNiR). Ans96, Gly109, and Ala137 from AfNiR and AcNiR critically stabilize this pathway. , The primary channel extends from the T2Cu site to surface-exposed Lys128 (Arg122 in AxNiR) through Asp98-His255-His260 and an interfacial water network. His260 (His254 in AxNiR) residing halfway along this channel regulates the flow of protons. ,, N90S substitution reduces enzymatic activity by ∼70%, confirming its dominance in the high-pH channel during nitrite reduction by CuNiR. H254F mutation shows minimal catalytic impact, suggesting the functional redundancy of this channel. Previous computational result identifies the primary channel as energetically favorable for the first protonation of NO2 – with a lower energy barrier (6.7 kcal/mol) of the rate-limiting step. But the major proton transfer pathway governing the second protonation of NO2 – remains to be elucidated. Therefore, the favorable proton channel responsible for the entire enzymatic catalytic reaction in CuNiR remains unclear.

Herein, we employed a two-layered quantum mechanical/quantum mechanical (QM1/QM2) approach to analyze the PT and ET mechanisms during the second protonation of NO2 – along two long-range proton channels. Our calculation results revealed that in the high-pH proton channel, Glu113 initiates water-mediated triple-proton transfer to WS3, WS3 directly transfers a proton to Asp98, and Asp98 enables proton-coupled β-electron transfer to HNO2. For the primary channel, Lys128-driven sequential water-mediated three-stage protons relay to His255, followed by His255-mediated double-proton-coupled β-electron transfer to HNO2. Both channels generate NO through T2Cu-derived β-electron transfer with energy barrier analysis identifying the primary channel as the rate-limiting pathway in the second protonation of NO2 –. Comparative analysis of energy barriers for each PT step reveals that the long-range high-pH channel is energetically more favorable for NO2 – catalytic reduction by CuNiR. These findings systematically elucidate the bioenergetic mechanisms of PT and ET in CuNiR-mediated nitrite reduction and further deepen our understanding of the biological denitrification.

Results and Discussion

The Second Protonation of NO2 – along the High-pH Proton Channel

Previous study has demonstrated that the first protonation of NO2 – produces HNO2, requiring additional proton transfer from protein surface residues to complete the catalytic reduction. To identify the dominant proton transfer pathway in the catalytic reaction by CuNiR, we systematically investigated the PT steps during the second protonation of NO2 – through the high-pH proton channel (Scheme ).

DFT calculations indicate predominant spin density localization at the T1Cu site in EW-r. This result is consistent with the previous report, which finds an oxidized T1Cu and a reduced T2Cu state after the first protonation of NO2 –. Structural analysis of the optimized EW-ts shows that the first migrating proton is positioned 1.37 Å from Glu113 and 1.09 Å from WS1. The second moving proton is located 1.32 Å from WS1 and 1.12 Å from WS2, while the third transferring proton resides 1.03 Å and 1.51 Å from O atoms of WS2 and WS3, respectively. The calculated EW-p reveals that WS3 undergoes protonation to generate a hydronium ion (H3O+) intermediate. Notably, the first PT pathway in the second protonation of NO2 – through the high-pH channel is identical to that observed during the first protonation event. However, as shown in Figure , the energy barrier (20.0 kcal/mol, blue curve) of PT from Glu113 to WS3 in the second protonation of NO2 – is significantly higher than that in the first protonation of NO2 – (14.5 kcal/mol). The elevated energy barrier may arise from charge–charge repulsion, where the positively charged T2Cu+-HNO2 complex electrostatically hinders approaching protons from accessing the active center. This suggests that an additional proton is required for Glu113 to facilitate HNO2 protonation.

Following reprotonation of Glu113, we computationally examined the second PT step from WS3 to Asp98 through the high-pH proton channel employing the WD model, where W and D correspond to WS3 and Asp98, respectively. Figure shows the optimized structures and spin density distributions of the reactant (WD-r), transition state (WD-ts), and product (WD-p) for this PT step. The reprotonated Glu113 in WD-r forms a water-mediated connection with WS3 via a WS1–WS2 bridge, with H-bond distances of 2.22, 1.80, and 1.50 Å. A well-ordered H-bond network stabilizes the high-pH proton channel, including Glu113-Asn96 (2.36 Å), Ala137-Asn96 (2.06 Å), and Ala137-WS2 (1.78 Å). The shortened distance (1.53 Å) promotes proton transfer from WS3 to Asp98. The optimized EW-ts configuration indicates the transferring proton occupies an intermediate position between WS3 and Asp98, with respective distances of 1.32 and 1.11 Å. In the final WD-p, Asp98 acquires the proton and forms a 1.51 Å H-bond with WS3. The well-organized H-bond network, comprising Glu113–WS1 (2.53 Å), WS1–WS2 (1.75 Å), and WS2–WS3 (1.60 Å) interactions, ensures efficient proton transfer through the high-pH channel. The calculated Mulliken population analysis reveals spin density localization at the T1Cu site throughout the WD-r to WD-p, confirming PT from WS3 to Asp98 without accompanying ET. This PT process exhibits a negligible energy barrier of −1.2 kcal/mol, characteristic of a low-barrier H-bond, indicating PT from WS3 to Asp98 occurs readily in the second NO2 – protonation via the high-pH channel. The energy barrier for this PT step is significantly lower than that of the corresponding step in the first NO2 – protonation (11.0 kcal/mol). This barrier reduction is likely attributed to the electrostatic driving force generated by Glu113 reprotonation.

In WD-p, the side chain of protonated Asp98 adopts a “gatekeeper” conformation, with its carbonyl HO group oriented toward the O atom of WS3. To complete the second protonation of NO2 –, Asp98 reorients its hydroxyl group toward HNO2 through proton tunneling (Figure S1). Our scanning calculations reveal that this hydroxyl group reorientation is energetically favorable (Figure S2). Subsequently, the PT step from Asp98 to HNO2 along the high-pH channel was theoretically examined using the DN model (D and N represent Asp98 and HNO2). Figure displays the optimized structures and spin density distributions of the reactant (DN-r), transition state (DN-ts), and product (DN-p) for the PT step. In DN-r, the protonated Asp98 forms a 1.81 Å H-bond with HNO2, while the bond lengths of HO–N and N–O in HNO2 are 1.43 and 1.17 Å, respectively. His255 interacts with Asp98 through the bridging WD, forming two hydrogen bonds of 1.78 and 1.72 Å. A water-mediated H-bond network (WS1–WS2–WS3) links Glu113 to Asp98. At DN-ts, proton transfer initiates with a 1.28 Å displacement from the O atom of Asp98, cleaving the HO–NO bond to yield NO with a 1.12 Å N–O bond. The optimized DN-p shows that HNO2 directly accepts a proton from Asp98, differing from the PT step observed during the first NO2 – protonation. This distinction is likely due to the inability of neutral HNO2 to attract a proton from distant His255. Upon proton dissociation, the structural stabilization is likely mediated by hydrogen-bonding interactions between the negatively charged Asp98 and surrounding water molecules, featuring a 1.81 Å H-bond with WS3 and a 1.58 Å H-bond with the regenerated H2O. Concurrently, the increased distance between NO and the T2Cu ion (2.64 Å) suggests NO dissociation.

Mulliken population analysis reveals 0.37 and 0.30 α-electrons at T1Cu in DN-r and DN-ts, respectively. In DN-p, a β-electron transfers from T2Cu to the NO fragment, indicating that PT from Asp98 to HNO2 triggers ET from T2Cu to NO, consistent with our previous findings. Additionally, DN-p shows 0.38 and 0.73 α-electrons at T1Cu and T2Cu, respectively (Table S5). These results demonstrate that the PT from Asp98 to HNO2 occurs via a proton-coupled β-ET mechanism. The forward energy barrier (8.2 kcal/mol) agrees well with the corresponding PT step in the first NO2 – protonation (6.4 kcal/mol).

Additionally, a comparative analysis of the energy barriers for the three PT steps reveals that the PT from Glu113 to WS3 with the highest energy barrier (20.0 kcal/mol) serves as the rate-limiting step in the second protonation of NO2 – along the high-pH proton channel.

The Second Protonation of NO2 – along the Primary Proton Channel

We further examined the PT steps in the second protonation of NO2 – along the primary proton channel featuring protonated Asp98 and deprotonated His255. Long-range PT from Lys128 to His255 proceeds via two sequential steps (Scheme ). The first PT from Lys128 to His260 occurs via a water-mediated three-stage mechanism involving WP1 and WP2 (Figure S6). This triple-PT step exhibits a remarkably low energy barrier (1.2 kcal/mol), indicative of facile proton movement facilitated by a remote transfer pathway that avoids electrostatic interference from the positively charged T2Cu center. The second PT from His260 to His255 proceeds via a water-mediated three-stage mechanism through WP3 and WP4 (Figure S7) with a higher energy barrier (17.8 kcal/mol), significantly exceeding those in the first NO2 – protonation (3.0 and −0.9 kcal/mol). Detailed descriptions of these two PT processes are provided in the Supporting Information.

The PT pathways for NO2 – reduction by CuNiR were evaluated by comparing the energy barriers of the rate-limiting steps in the first and second protonations. We previously shown that the primary proton channel exhibits greater efficacy in facilitating the first protonation of NO2 –. For the second protonation of NO2 –, as shown in Figure , our examinations revealed that the energy barrier (20.0 kcal/mol, blue curve) for PT from Glu113 to WS3 along the high-pH proton channel is lower than that (32.6 kcal/mol, pink curve) for PT from His255 to HNO2 along the primary proton channel, which confirms that the high-pH proton channel is more favorable for the second protonation of NO2 –. The barriers (20.0 and 32.6 kcal/mol) in the second protonation of NO2 – along the high-pH and the primary proton channels are significantly higher than those in the first protonation of NO2 – (14.5 and 6.7 kcal/mol), indicating that the second protonation of NO2 – serves as the rate-limiting process for the catalytic reaction in CuNiR. Therefore, taking into account all PT and ET steps in the overall nitrite reduction reaction, the lower activation energy in the second protonation of NO2 – along the high-pH proton channel suggests this channel may be a favorable PT pathway for the reduction of NO2 – by CuNiR. These results are consistent with the experimental observations. ,

More recently, a hypothetical alternative proton channel, which is invisible in AxNiR and AcNiR, has been proposed in the latest identified crystal structure of BjNirK from Bradyrhizobium japonicum USDA110. , The potential proton channel in BjNirK is currently suggested to involve only Asp308 and Pro135 residues near the channel entrance, and a complete pathway leading to the T2Cu active site has not yet been established. It is particularly noteworthy that the amino acid residues forming the proton channels, especially those at the channel entrance and the active residues (Asp98 and His255) exert a significant influence on both the PT and ET mechanisms in the catalytic reduction of NO2 – by CuNiR. Therefore, further mutagenesis experiments are required to validate the reliability of these putative proton channels and are essential for achieving a comprehensive mechanistic understanding of NO2 – reduction catalyzed by CuNiR. We hope that these findings may provide some valuable theoretical guidance for future site-directed mutagenesis experiments. ,

Additionally, the computational models intercepted from the protein structure may have limitations in interpreting the PT and ET mechanisms of CuNiR. Consequently, a QM/MM investigation of the complete protein system is imperative for elucidating the catalytic mechanisms in CuNiR. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that ET from T1Cu to T2Cu proceeds more rapidly than nitrite binding under acidic conditions or at low nitrite concentrations. In contrast, under alkaline conditions or at elevated nitrite concentrations, nitrite coordination to oxidized T2Cu precedes ET. ,, These findings suggest that pH and ionic strength significantly influence the proton and electron transfer mechanisms in CuNiR. Clarifying these effects is crucial for validating our hypothesis and will serve as the primary focus of our subsequent investigations.

Conclusion

Our theoretical investigation reveals that the second protonation of NO2 – proceeds via distinct long-range PT and ET mechanisms through the long-range high-pH and primary proton channels. Along the high-pH channel, PT from Glu113 to HNO2 occurs via three sequential steps: a water-mediated three-stage PT from Glu113 to WS3 mediated by WS1–WS2, a direct PT from WS3 to Asp98, and PT from Asp98 to HNO2 coupled with β-electron transfer from T2Cu to NO. Similarly, the primary channel features a three-step proton relay from Lys128 to HNO2, involving sequential water-mediated three-stage PT from Lys128 to His260 through WP1–WP2 and from His260 to His255 WP3–WP4 bridge, followed by water-mediated two-stage PT from His255 to HNO2 via WD with concomitant β-electron transfer from T2Cu to NO. A comprehensive comparison of the energy barriers for each catalytic step demonstrates that the second protonation of NO2 – serves as the overall rate-limiting step, while thermodynamic analysis indicates that the long-range high-pH channel provides the most favorable proton transfer pathway in CuNiR catalysis.

Computational Methods

Based on the X-ray structure of AfNiR from green CuNiR (PDB code: 1AS6), we constructed a series of computational models to examine the second protonation of NO2 – along the high-pH and the primary proton channels. To balance accuracy and computational efficiency, we employed a two-layer QM1/QM2 approach. , As shown in Figure , these models feature the same basic structures, including a T1Cu center coordinated by His95, His145, Met150, Cys136, and a T2Cu center ligated by His100, His135, His306, and HNO2. A sensor loop, comprising His95, Asn96, Ile97, Asp98, Phe99, and His100 residues, structurally bridges the two copper centers. Besides, the two copper sites are interconnected through a ∼12.5 Å Cys–His bridge, which has been identified as the putative electron transfer pathway. Ile257 acts as a critical modulator of nitrite binding coordination. An H-bond network formed by His255, H2O332 (labeled as WD), and Asp98 facilitates the protonation of the substrate, , while the protonation state of His255 is regulated by H-bonds with Glu279/Thr280 (Figure ).

All quantum mechanical calculations were performed using the Gaussian 09 (revision D.01) package in the gas phase. Geometry optimizations were conducted with the α-C atoms of all residues (or the first C atoms of retained side chains) constrained. The T1Cu, T2Cu, HNO2, six H2Os in the high-pH channel, five H2Os in the primary channel, and the side chains of inner-layer residues were optimized at the M06/SDD level for Cu atoms, − 6-31G­(d) for S, C, and 6-31G­(d, p) for O, N, and H. , The full systems were optimized using the PM6 semiempirical method. , Vibrational frequency analyses at the same levels confirmed stationary points as minima (all real frequencies) or transition states (one imaginary frequency). For the rate-determining step structures, single-point calculations were performed in three different continuum solvents, including water (ε = 78.36), dichloroethane (ε = 10.36), and diethyl ether (ε = 4.335) at the M06/6-311+G­(d, p) level by means of the conductor-like polarizable continuum model (CPCM). , Spin densities were analyzed via a Mulliken population analysis. Since the relative distribution of spin densities is a key point, the minor deviations from spin contamination were deemed negligible. All reported energies include zero-point vibrational corrections.
