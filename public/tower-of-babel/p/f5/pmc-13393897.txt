Hemoglobin–Albumin–Lymphocyte–Platelet (HALP) score as a low-cost adjunct for osteoporosis risk stratification in primary care
Authors: Mehmet Vatansever, Ersan Gürsoy, Serhat Hayme
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study aimed to evaluate the hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) score as an exploratory adjunctive marker associated with reduced bone density, particularly in resource-limited settings.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted between October 2022 and October 2023 at the Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation Hospital in Trabzon, Türkiye. This study included 267 patients who had not been previously diagnosed with osteopenia or osteoporosis and had undergone a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan within the previous year. The participants were categorized into the normal, osteopenic, and osteoporotic groups according to their DEXA results. Statistical analysis included receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve assessment, sensitivity and specificity calculations, and multiple logistic regression analysis to evaluate the discriminative performance of the HALP score and its adjusted association with reduced bone density.ResultsPatients with reduced bone density (osteopenia or osteoporosis) had substantially lower HALP scores than those with a normal bone density. The area under the curve (AUC) for the HALP score was 0.625, indicating a limited discriminative ability. The HALP score exhibited a sensitivity of 65.36% and a specificity of 55.26% for identifying reduced bone density. In multivariable analysis, higher HALP remained associated with lower odds of osteopenia/osteoporosis after adjustment (OR = 0.757, 95% CI 0.624–0.917, p = 0.004).ConclusionGiven its limited discriminative ability, HALP should not be interpreted as a stand-alone screening test. At most, it may represent a low-cost exploratory adjunctive signal that could help support consideration of confirmatory DEXA in resource-limited settings, pending external validation.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12875-026-03399-y.Keywords: HALP score, Bone density disorders, Inflammatory markers, Osteopenia, Osteoporosis, Screening tools

Introduction

Osteopenia and osteoporosis represent significant public health challenges, particularly in the elderly population, where they are major contributors to increased morbidity and mortality resulting from bone fractures [1, 2]. These conditions, characterized by reduced bone mineral density, increase the risk of fractures from minimal trauma and subsequently lead to greater health complications and elevated mortality rates [1, 3]. Given the aging global population, the rising incidence of osteopenia and osteoporosis highlights the need for accessible diagnostic strategies [4, 5].

Currently, dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) is the gold standard for diagnosing bone density disorders [6]. However, high costs and limited availability often limit DEXA scan accessibility in primary care settings, posing a significant barrier to the early diagnosis and timely treatment of at-risk populations [7, 8]. This gap underscores the necessity for alternative diagnostic approaches that are both accessible and cost-effective.

Recent advances in the identification and application of biochemical markers offer promising avenues for improving early detection of osteopenia and osteoporosis. Numerous studies have highlighted the roles of bone turnover markers (BTMs), such as bone-specific alkaline phosphatase (BALP), procollagen type I N-terminal propeptide (P1NP), and C-terminal cross-linked telopeptide (CTX), in reflecting bone metabolism and predicting changes in bone density [9, 10]. However, access to these markers may be limited in primary-care settings. In light of these challenges, there is growing interest in exploring biochemical markers that could serve as preliminary screening tools to identify individuals at high risk of osteopenia and osteoporosis [11].

Hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte, and platelet (HALP) scores, commonly employed to forecast outcomes in systemic illnesses such as coronary heart disease and different types of malignancies, predominantly indicate systemic inflammation [12–14]. The presence of this inflammation, which is known to affect bone metabolism, indicates that the HALP score may have consequences for bone health [15, 16]. Although the potential of HALP scores to reflect biological processes influencing bone metabolism is intriguing, studies directly linking HALP scores with bone mineral density (BMD) are currently limited [17]. This finding underscores a critical gap in research and highlights the need for further studies to explore how systemic inflammation, as measured by HALP score, might impact BMD.

Because HALP integrates routine hematologic and biochemical parameters, it can proxy inflammation–nutrition–hematopoietic axes that shape bone remodeling [18]. Pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g., IL-6, TNF-α) upregulate RANKL and downregulate osteoprotegerin, favoring osteoclastogenesis; lower albumin reflects protein–energy malnutrition and systemic inflammation that impair osteoblast function; lymphocyte perturbations modulate the RANK/RANKL/OPG pathway and skeletal turnover; platelets release mediators (e.g., TGF-β, PDGF) relevant to bone repair; and low hemoglobin/hypoxia may influence osteoblast metabolism [12, 19–23]. These links provide a biologically plausible rationale for the observed association between HALP and bone density.

Despite the established utility of HALP score in predicting the outcomes of systemic diseases, its role in bone health remains underexplored. This study aimed to assess the diagnostic potential of the HALP score as a supplementary tool for identifying individuals at risk of osteopenia and osteoporosis, particularly in settings where advanced diagnostic methods such as DEXA are not readily available. Accordingly, the present study evaluates HALP not as a replacement for diagnostic testing but as a potentially adjunctive, low-cost signal to inform risk prioritization for DEXA.

Materials and methods

Study design

This observational, retrospective, cross-sectional study analyzed patient records from the Maçka Ömer Burhanoğlu Physical Therapy and Rehabilitation Hospital, Trabzon, Türkiye. The data included were obtained from individuals who attended the hospital between October 2022 and October 2023. Ethical approval was secured before accessing any patient data. Data retrieval and analysis were performed retrospectively from anonymized medical records between November 1 and November 30, 2023. The study conforms to the STROBE guidelines for observational studies.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the Health Sciences University, Trabzon Faculty of Medicine Scientific Research Ethics Committee and the Trabzon Provincial Health Directorate, Türkiye (approval number: 2023/37; approval date: October 25, 2023). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the Health Sciences University, Trabzon Faculty of Medicine Scientific Research Ethics Committee because this retrospective study used anonymized medical records. Patient anonymity was maintained throughout the study, and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and relevant institutional and national regulations.

Participants

Participants were adults aged ≥ 18 years who underwent a dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA) scan within the previous year, along with concurrent laboratory assessments, including hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocyte count, and platelet count. Only individuals without prior diagnosis or treatment for osteopenia or osteoporosis were included.

Exclusion criteria

Participants were excluded if they had incomplete medical records; if there was a time interval greater than one month between the DEXA scan and biochemical testing; if they were on long-term use of medications known to affect bone metabolism, such as corticosteroids, anticonvulsants, or selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs); if they had chronic renal failure requiring erythropoietin therapy; if they had recently received vitamin D or iron supplementation; if they had a diagnosis of cancer or were undergoing cancer treatment; or if they had any conditions known to significantly influence systemic inflammation or bone metabolism.

Data collection

Data were collected retrospectively from the hospital’s electronic health records system. The variables retrieved included demographic details such as age and sex; bone density measurements in the form of T-scores obtained from DEXA scans; and a comprehensive panel of biochemical parameters, including hemoglobin, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, platelets, glucose, AST, ALT, GFR, uric acid, creatinine, triglycerides, total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, vitamin D, calcium, albumin, vitamin B12, TSH, and free T4.

Sample size

Due to the exploratory nature of the study, a formal sample size calculation was not conducted. Instead, all eligible participants meeting the inclusion criteria within the defined period were included, ensuring adequate representation of the population at risk. Although no a priori sample size calculation was conducted, the final sample size (n = 267) was considered adequate for exploratory statistical modeling based on similar observational studies.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (minimum–maximum) for continuous variables, and frequencies (%) for categorical variables. Normality distribution was tested using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, and homogeneity of variances was verified with Levene’s test.

Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. The diagnostic capability of the HALP score for osteopenia and osteoporosis was assessed through Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves, and the area under the curve (AUC) was calculated. Optimal cut-off points were determined using Youden’s index. Optimal exploratory cut-off points were determined post hoc using Youden’s index, with the understanding that clinically optimized thresholds would require external validation and may prioritize sensitivity over overall discrimination in primary-care triage. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were also computed.

Binary logistic regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between HALP score and reduced bone density (osteopenia/osteoporosis vs. normal bone density). Variables with p-values < 0.15 in univariate analyses were considered candidate covariates; however, age and sex were forced into the multivariable model a priori because of their established biological relevance to bone density and their potential confounding effect on the HALP–bone density association. The main multivariable model included age, sex, HALP score, glomerular filtration rate (GFR), triglycerides, and vitamin D. HALP score components were not entered separately into the model to avoid multicollinearity. Because some covariates had missing values, multivariable regression analyses were performed using complete-case data for each model. To further explore the potential impact of unmeasured menopausal status, two sensitivity analyses were performed using the same covariate structure: a female-only model and an age-based proxy analysis restricted to women aged 55 years and older. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

The study group comprised 267 adult patients, with a sex distribution of 17.6% males (n = 47) and 82.4% females (n = 220). The mean age of the subjects was 66.45 ± 8.60. The bone density exams revealed that the mean hip T score was − 1.17 ± 1.12 (minimum= -5.00, maximum = 2.80). On the basis of their T-scores, 42.7% of the participants (n = 114) were classified as having normal bone density, whereas the remaining 57.3% (n = 153) were classified as having either osteopenia or osteoporosis.

Multivariable regression analyses were performed on complete-case data; therefore, model-specific sample sizes were smaller than the overall analytic cohort of 267 participants. Table 3 presents the re-specified multivariable logistic regression model examining the association between HALP score and reduced bone density. Of the 267 participants with valid reduced bone density classification, 228 had complete data for all variables included in the adjusted model. After forced adjustment for age and sex, together with GFR, triglycerides, and vitamin D, higher HALP scores remained significantly associated with lower odds of reduced bone density (OR = 0.757, 95% CI: 0.624–0.917; p = 0.004). Age was positively associated with reduced bone density (OR = 1.121, 95% CI: 1.071–1.173; p < 0.001). Vitamin D showed a modest inverse association (OR = 0.964, 95% CI: 0.933–0.995; p = 0.025), whereas sex, GFR, and triglycerides were not statistically significant in the fully adjusted model. Because menopausal status was unavailable in the retrospective dataset, we performed two additional sensitivity analyses to examine the robustness of the HALP association. In the female-only model, which included 187 complete cases, HALP remained inversely associated with reduced bone density (OR = 0.736, 95% CI: 0.595–0.910; p = 0.005). In an age-based proxy analysis restricted to women aged 55 years and older, including 177 complete cases, the association also persisted (OR = 0.718, 95% CI: 0.576–0.894; p = 0.003) (Supplementary Table S1). Across both sensitivity analyses, age remained positively associated with reduced bone density, whereas GFR, triglycerides, and vitamin D were not consistently significant.

Re-specified multivariable logistic regression model for reduced bone density

To further clarify the contribution of individual HALP components, we performed additional sensitivity analyses. In the component-level adjusted model, lymphocyte count remained significantly associated with reduced bone density (OR = 0.534, 95% CI: 0.305–0.937; p = 0.029), whereas platelet count was not significant (OR = 1.000, 95% CI: 1.000–1.000; p = 0.786) (Supplementary Table S2). Consistently, Spearman correlation analysis showed weak positive correlations of hip T-score with lymphocyte count (rho = 0.216, p < 0.001), albumin (rho = 0.215, p < 0.001), and hemoglobin (rho = 0.134, p = 0.029), while platelet count was not significantly correlated (rho = 0.049, p = 0.427) (Supplementary Table S3). In a supplementary model-comparison analysis using the same 228 complete cases, the AUC of the logistic model including age, sex, GFR, triglycerides, and vitamin D was 0.757. After adding HALP to this model, the AUC increased to 0.774, indicating a modest incremental contribution of HALP (Supplementary Table S4).

Discussion

The primary finding of this study is that lower HALP scores were associated with reduced bone density; however, the clinical discriminative performance of HALP was limited. Although HALP is simple and inexpensive to calculate from routine laboratory parameters, our findings do not support its use as a screening or decision-making tool in isolation. Rather, HALP should be interpreted as an exploratory adjunctive marker that may warrant further study as part of broader risk-assessment strategies, particularly in settings where access to DEXA is constrained.

Systemic inflammation significantly affects bone metabolism, primarily by promoting bone resorption and inhibiting bone formation [18, 24]. Important inflammatory cytokines, including interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), promote osteoclast differentiation and activity, thereby accelerating bone resorption [25, 26]. The receptor activator of nuclear factor kappa-B ligand (RANKL) mediates this process by stimulating osteoclast formation and activity, which in turn decreases osteoprotegerin (OPG) levels and disrupts the balance of bone remodelling [19]. Additionally, cytokines can directly inhibit osteoblast function, reduce bone formation, and inhibit the effects of increased resorption [20]. This dual effect of systemic inflammation not only accelerates bone loss but also impedes bone regeneration, thereby increasing the risk of osteoporosis and associated fractures. Therefore, addressing systemic inflammation is crucial for the management and prevention of bone metabolic disorders [18].

The HALP score, which includes hemoglobin, albumin, lymphocytes, and platelets, is used as a measure of systemic inflammation [12]. Given that inflammation is a vital factor in bone resorption and reduced bone formation, the HALP score has the potential to serve as an indirect indicator of bone mineral density [18, 27, 28]. Although lower HALP scores (reflecting a more pro-inflammatory and/or poorer nutritional profile) were associated with reduced bone density, the ROC analysis showed limited discrimination (AUC = 0.625), with sensitivity of 65.36% and specificity of 55.26%; therefore, HALP should be interpreted as an adjunctive, weak signal to support consideration of confirmatory DEXA rather than a stand-alone screening tool. Although the original HALP formula was retained to preserve comparability with prior studies, additional component-level and non-parametric analyses suggested that the observed HALP signal in this cohort was not driven equally by all four components. In particular, platelet count did not show a significant independent association or significant correlation with hip T-score, whereas lymphocyte-related signals appeared more informative. Therefore, the platelet term should be interpreted as part of the original composite structure rather than as an equally contributory standalone marker in this dataset.

These findings should also be interpreted in the context of contemporary osteoporosis prediction research. Carvalho and Gavaia developed a stacking ensemble model using routinely available clinical and anthropometric data, explicitly excluding DXA as an input, and reported an AUC of 0.94 in 7,924 NHANES participants aged 50 years and older [29]. In contrast, the present study evaluates a single composite hematologic–nutritional index rather than a multivariable prediction framework. Accordingly, HALP alone should not be expected to match the discriminative performance of broader machine-learning models and should instead be viewed, at most, as a low-cost, hypothesis-generating adjunctive signal pending external validation.

After re-specifying the multivariable model to force age and sex into the analysis, the inverse association between HALP and reduced bone density remained statistically significant, although its magnitude was modest. This suggests that the HALP association was not fully explained by basic demographic structure alone. However, the explanatory capacity of the regression model remains limited by residual confounding, particularly because direct menopausal information was unavailable in a cohort composed predominantly of women. Menopause is a major determinant of bone loss, and its omission limits our ability to determine how much of the observed HALP association is truly independent of sex hormone status, age-related nutritional change, or systemic inflammation. Accordingly, the adjusted HALP odds ratio should be interpreted as evidence of a modest association within an incompletely specified model, rather than as proof of a fully independent effect.

To further explore this issue, we performed sensitivity analyses in women only and in women aged 55 years and older as an age-based proxy for likely postmenopausal status. In both analyses, the inverse HALP association persisted. This consistency reduces, but does not eliminate, concern that the main finding was solely driven by sex imbalance in the overall sample. At the same time, because menopausal status was not directly measured, residual confounding likely remains, and the direction of bias may have favored overestimation of the apparent HALP association if lower HALP values were more common among older or postmenopausal women in this cohort.

Şen et al. focused exclusively on hemodialysis patients who have unique metabolic and inflammatory profiles that do not necessarily reflect those of the general population [17, 30]. Their research demonstrated significant correlations between the HALP score and bone mineral density within this specialized cohort [17]. However, the absence of a normal population in their study limits the wider applicability of their findings. Compared with previous research, our study extends the evaluation of HALP beyond a hemodialysis population to a broader clinical sample. However, this broader sampling should not be interpreted as evidence that HALP is ready for routine screening use in primary care. Rather, it suggests that the HALP–bone density association may be observable outside highly selected renal populations, while still requiring validation in more diverse and prospectively characterized cohorts.

If validated prospectively and tested within broader multivariable models, biochemical markers such as HALP may contribute to more refined risk assessment strategies for reduced bone density [31]. Future studies should evaluate HALP longitudinally, test its performance within broader multivariable prediction frameworks, and determine whether it provides incremental value beyond established clinical and anthropometric risk factors.

Limitations

Despite these findings, several limitations warrant consideration. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference between HALP and bone density status. Second, reliance on retrospective records may have introduced selection and information bias. Third, the principal structural limitation is residual confounding due to the unavailability of key determinants of bone mineral density, including menopausal status (particularly relevant given that 82% of the sample were female), BMI, physical activity, calcium/vitamin D intake, smoking and alcohol use, chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, inflammatory bowel disease), and medication exposures known to affect bone health, which could not be comprehensively ascertained in the retrospective dataset. Accordingly, the regression model has limited explanatory capacity, and HALP may primarily reflect underlying inflammatory and/or nutritional status rather than an independent marker of bone metabolism. Fourth, discriminative performance was modest (AUC = 0.625), so HALP should be interpreted as an adjunctive signal rather than a stand-alone screening tool. In addition, because the cohort was predominantly female, the findings may not generalize directly to male populations or to settings with different patient demographics, and future sex-stratified studies are warranted [32].

Conclusion

In this retrospective cross-sectional cohort, HALP was inversely associated with osteopenia/osteoporosis; however, its ability to discriminate individuals with reduced bone density was limited (AUC = 0.625; sensitivity 65.36%; specificity 55.26%). Therefore, HALP should not be used as a stand-alone screening test. At most, it may serve as a weak, low-cost adjunctive signal that could support consideration of confirmatory DEXA assessment in resource-limited settings. Prospective studies with comprehensive confounder adjustment and external validation are required before any clinical implementation.
