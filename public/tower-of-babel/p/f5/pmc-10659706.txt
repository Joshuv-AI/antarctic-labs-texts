Effects of sham acupuncture for chronic musculoskeletal pain syndrome: A systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Chuan Yu, Rui Zhang, Bin Shen, Xiang Li, Yiyi Fang, Yiyun Jiang, Guofan Jian
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Background:Acupuncture has been widely used for chronic musculoskeletal pain syndrome (MPS). Due to the strong influence of sham acupuncture (SA) in clinical trials, the treatment of MPS by acupuncture remains controversial. Different types of SA procedures might produce different responses. The purpose of this systematic review was to assess the effect of SA on MPS.Methods:We searched 8 literature databases for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on acupuncture for chronic MPS with SA as a control from database inception to November 29, 2022. SA included superficial acupuncture on non-acupoints (SANAs), non-penetration on acupoints (NPAs), and non-penetration on non-acupoints (NPNAs). Two independent reviewers assessed the risk of bias and conducted the research selection, data extraction, and quality assessment of the included RCTs. We conducted data analysis using the RevMan 5.3 and STATA 14 software packages, and traditional meta-analysis was adopted for direct comparison. A network meta-analysis (NMA) was executed using frequency models in which we combined all available direct and indirect evidence from RCTs. The pain-related indicators were set as primary outcomes, and GRADEpro online was implemented for the assessment of evidence quality.Results:Forty-two RCTs were included in this study, encompassing a total of 6876 patients and incorporating 3 types of SA procedures. In our traditional meta-analysis, true acupuncture (TA) was more effective than SANAs, NPAs, and NPANAs concerning MPS. In the NMA, TA was the most effective modality, followed by SANAs, NPAs and NPANAs, and then the blank control (BC). In this NMA and according to the therapeutic effects in the pain indicators, the rankings of SA were as follows: SANA (surface under the cumulative ranking curve [SUCRA], 65.3%), NPA (SUCRA, 46.2%), and NPANA (SUCRA, 34.2%). The quality of the evidence for outcomes ranged from “low” to “moderate.”Conclusions:Compared with SA, TA was effective in treating MPS. The effects produced by different SA procedures were different, and the order of effects from greatest to least was as follows: SANA, NPA, and NPANA.

1. Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders and their related chronic pain constitute major causes of disability, with significant economic and societal implications globally.[1] Tension-type headache (TTH), neck pain (NP), shoulder pain (SP), low back pain (LBP), osteoarthritis (OA), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), and fibromyalgia (FM) comprise the most common types of chronic musculoskeletal pain syndromes (MPSs).[2–6]

Acupuncture has been widely used as an integrative or complementary therapy for chronic pain. Controlled trials have been published on chronic pain syndromes such as acupuncture for TTH,[7] knee OA,[8] FM,[9] and musculoskeletal pain.[10] However, the treatment for chronic musculoskeletal pain by acupuncture remains controversial.[11,12]

Sham acupuncture (SA) is a control placebo treatment in clinical trials that is used to assess the specific effects of true acupuncture (TA). A meta-analysis reflecting data from 29 of 31 eligible randomized controlled trials (RCTs) established that acupuncture was effective for the treatment of chronic pain, but there were small statistically significant differences between TA and SA.[13] Due to the robust influence of SA in clinical trials, the principal controversy was whether acupuncture was merely exerting a placebo effect. While Moffet postulated that SA conditions were related to clinical results,[14] SA could also exert specific analgesic effects.[15] The authors of a meta-analysis conducted a systematic review of 61 clinical trials to compare the efficacy of SA (19 trials) with that of other placebos (42 trials) and concluded that SA exerted a larger effect than did other placebos.[16] Multiple factors may contribute to variability in TA and SA therapeutic effects, including needling technique (de qi response), number of the number of points needled, and psychological factors. The most frequently applied SA conditions were superficial penetration of the non-acupoints (outside true acupoints) and non-penetration.[17]

Different types of SA procedures might produce different responses and thus affect the conclusions of clinical trials. A systematic review of the effects of SA on chronic MPS in RCTs would therefore help ascertain whether acupuncture only generated a placebo effect, and it would also facilitate the design of other appropriate forms of SA in future clinical trials. In this systematic review, we synthesized the currently available clinical evidence concerning TA versus SA in clinical trials of chronic MPS. Additionally, we posit that this will achieve an appreciable foundation for clinical practice.

2. Methods

2.1. Study registration

This systematic review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (Registration No. CRD42022363647). We conducted the study according to the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions and presented the results based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines.[18,19]

2.2. Literature search

We searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, EMBASE, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, the Wanfang database, the Chongqing VIP Chinese Science Technology Periodical database, and the China Biology Medicine database for articles published before November 29, 2022, and placed no restrictions on geographic region. A combination of subject words and free text words was applied to the searches, and the retrieval mode used was a combination of free words and medical subject heading (MeSH) terms. The PubMed search strategy is presented in Table 1, and appropriate adjustments to this strategy that were made for other databases are depicted in Supplementary Material S1, http://links.lww.com/MD/K702.

2.3. Eligibility criteria

This review included articles that met all of the following criteria:

2.3.1. Types of studies.

This review encompassed clinical RCTs on chronic MPS regardless of whether they had been published or not. In addition, the language was limited to English and Chinese.

2.3.2. Types of participants.

Patients suffering from pain associated with MPS, defined broadly as pain that affects the muscles, ligaments and tendons, and bones; with one of the following indications included: TTH, NP, shoulder pain, LBP, OA, RA, and FM. The diagnosis of the above diseases were carried out according to the relevant guidelines of Chinese medicine or Western medicine; patients were ≥ 18 years of age; and the current episode of pain must have had a duration of at least 3 months for musculoskeletal disorders, regardless of the individual sex, race, educational level, or economic status.

2.3.3. Types of TA procedures.

Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) acupuncture-related therapies (body acupuncture, electroacupuncture, and manual acupuncture) were used for the treatment group.

2.3.4. Types of SA procedures.

SA included: superficial acupuncture on non-acupoints (SANAs), the needles were inserted into points away from conventional acupoints or meridians. The depth of the needle insertion was superficially (1–10 mm in depth) without needle manipulation for de qi, non-penetration on acupoints (NPAs), the blunt tip needle was allowed to touch the acupoints but was not inserted into the skin, and non-penetration on non-acupoints (NPNAs), the blunt tip needle touched the non-acupoints and was not inserted into the skin.

2.3.5. Types of blank controls (BCs).

BCs included: waiting (without any treatment), routine care, and health education.

2.3.6. Types of outcome measures.

Pain-related scales such as the visual analog pain scale (VAS), the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index Pain Score (WOMAC), the Von Korff Chronic Pain Grade Scale (Von Korff), and the Neck Pain Questionnaire (NPQ) were used for evaluation. We used the VAS with a total score of 10 to convert the different scales into uniform units according to the international unit system to obtain robust results.

2.4. Exclusion criteria

Our exclusion criteria contained the following items.

Types of studies: studies in nonhuman animals, nonrandomized or quasi-randomized trials, review studies, observational studies, case reports/series, conference abstracts/reports, comments, or letters.

Types of participants: individuals with cancer-related pain, individuals with central neurological conditions, and individuals with systemic pain.

Types of participants: individuals with cancer-related pain, individuals with central neurological conditions, and individuals with systemic pain.

Types of TA procedures: auriculoacupuncture, catgut embedding, scalp acupuncture, acupoint injection, and warm needling.

Types of SA procedures: sham auricular, sham tongue, laser treatments, acupressure, and transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation.

2.5. Research screening and data extraction

All citations obtained from the search were imported into EndnoteX8 for management, and duplicate documents were removed. After screening titles and abstracts, all relevant full-text articles were retrieved for subsequent eligibility screening.

Two review authors (Y.C. and S.B.) independently initiated the search-results selection process according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria, and any dispute was resolved through consultation with a third author (Z.R.) to reach a consensus. The data were independently extracted from the selected articles by 2 review authors (L.X. and Y.C.) using an Excel spreadsheet. The extracted content included identification information (e.g., first author, publication date, publication source, country where the study was conducted, and source of financial support), characteristics of the research (e.g., number of groups, sample size, whether a randomized method was used, and blinding), participants (e.g., age, sex, original disease, and duration), details of treatment and control interventions (e.g., treatment procedures, frequency, and duration), and outcomes (e.g., primary outcome, time of measurement, adverse effects, length of follow-up, and drop-outs). Disagreements regarding the literature sources were resolved through discussions with a third author (Z.R.).

2.6. Assessment of risk of bias in included studies

The assessment of bias risk in each study was conducted by 2 review authors (F.Y.Y. and J.Y.Y.) using the “bias-risk assessment tool” recommended in the Cochran Manual 5.1.0 33. The contents comprised 7 items: random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete bias, reporting bias, and other sources of bias. The bias risk was classified as low risk, high risk, or unclear risk. If there were divergences in the results, a final decision was reached in consultation with a third author (J.G.F.).[20]

2.7. Data synthesis and statistical analysis

We adopted RevMan 5.3 (Cochrane Collaboration, Nordic Cochrane Center, Copenhagen, Denmark) software and Stata 14 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX) for statistical analyses and synthesis, and traditional meta-analysis was implemented for direct comparison. We employed the differences between the parameters after intervention and the baseline parameters as effects concerning continuous data. Standard mean differences (SMDs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used to analyze continuous data. We exploited the standard χ2 test and Higgins’ I squared (I2) statistic to detect statistical heterogeneity. If I2 < 50% and P > .1, the fixed-effects model was applied; but if I2 ≥ 50% and P < .1, studies were considered to reflect high heterogeneity, and the random-effects model was adopted. When the level of heterogeneity of the included studies was large, a sensitivity analysis was used, and the study that generated the heterogeneity was removed to determine whether the results of the meta-analysis changed significantly. A random-effects model was applied for overall and subgroup analyses if obvious heterogeneity was found; otherwise, we applied a fixed-effects model. When more than 10 trials were to be included, funnel plots were used to evaluate reporting biases, and Egger test was then executed to determine the asymmetry of the funnel plots.

To combine indirect and direct evidence, network meta-analysis (NMA) was conducted to allow evaluation of the effects of all interventions. We used Stata14.0 software to select the frequentist framework random-effects model for NMA, and the ranking-group command was used for data preprocessing. The nodal method was executed for the quantification and demonstration of the agreement between direct and indirect comparisons, and the consistency test was met at P > .05.[21] As illustrated in the network diagrams generated, each intervention was represented by a node, and the lines that connected the nodes embodied direct head-to-head comparisons between the interventions. The dot areas represent the number of patients with relevant interventions, and the thickness of the line between points represents the number of included studies. The intervention hierarchy was summed and reported as surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA) values, and these were used to measure the extent of certainty that one treatment was superior to another, averaged over all competing treatments. SUCRA values were expressed as percentages. When the SUCRA was 100%, this indicated that the intervention was absolutely effective, while 0 indicated that the intervention was absolutely ineffective.[22,23]

2.8. Assessment of reporting biases

We used funnel plots to evaluate reporting biases when more than 10 trials were to be included, and Egger test was executed to determine any asymmetry of the funnel plots in traditional meta-analysis. Concerning NMA, we used the asymmetry of the network funnel plot as the primary outcome to determine the probability of publication bias.

2.9. Management of missing data

We attempted to contact the trial authors to obtain any missing data. If we could not obtain the missing information, these particular studies were excluded from the analysis.

3. Results

3.1. Study selection

A total of 2046 records were retrieved from 8 databases, with 789 excluded due to duplication; after reading the titles and abstracts, 995 studies were further excluded. A total of 262 studies were subsequently evaluated in full text. Two evaluators read and cross-checked the studies: 211 studies were excluded due to the inclusions of ineligible TA, inappropriate SA, or inappropriate outcome indicators; not reflecting RCTs; and due to lack of available data. Forty-two studies were ultimately included in this review.[24–65] The PRISMA screening process is shown in Figure 1.

Flowchart of the selection process.

3.2. Characteristics of the included studies

This study encompassed 42 publicly published articles, comprising 8 Chinese articles and 34 English articles. There were 3 types of SA acupoints: SANAs, NPAs, and NPNAs. Twenty-seven articles were 2-arm studies, and 15 articles were 3-arm studies (the detailed characteristics of the included studies are shown in Supplementary Material S2, http://links.lww.com/MD/K703).

3.3. Bias risk of the included studies

In 34 articles, the authors reported the generation of random sequences, which was rated as low risk, and in 8 studies, the specific random methods were not described, which were rated as uncertain risk. Seventeen articles entailed the implementation of allocation concealment, which was rated as low risk, while 25 articles did not report the content of allocation concealment, which were rated as uncertain risk. All studies were blinded to the study subjects and rated as low risk: 9 articles had blinded statisticians, which was rated as low risk, while 33 articles did not specify whether statisticians were blinded as to treatment, which was rated as uncertain risk. In all 42 articles, the situation of dropout cases and follow-up were mentioned and analyzed, and they were rated as low risk. Twelve articles comprised clinical trial registration information in which the research data results were compared with the methodology section, and all expected outcome indicators of the studies were reported; these were rated as low risk. We could not obtain the original research protocol in 30 articles and could not determine whether there was selective reporting; we thus rated these as uncertain risk. In terms of other biases, for 42 articles we were unable to determine whether there were other sources of bias from the original texts, and these were thus also rated as uncertain risk. Overall, there was a low risk of bias in the literature included in the present analysis (the bias-risk information of the included studies is shown in Figs. 2 and 3).

3.4. Outcome measures

The primary outcome measure was the difference in pain scores (VAS, WOMAC, NPQ, Von Korff) before and after treatment.

3.5. Synthesis of results

3.5.1. Direct meta-analysis results.

3.5.1.1. TA versus SANA.

We noted 20 articles using SANA as SA, including 1957 cases in the TA group and 1968 cases in the SANA group. The heterogeneity of the pooled analysis was high (P < .00001, I2 = 81.7%), and we, therefore, adopted a random-effects model for the meta-analysis. The results showed that TA significantly reduced pain-related scale scores (SMD = 0.50; 95% CI, 0.33 to 0.66; Z = 5.91, P < .001) (Fig. 4), and sensitivity analysis showed that the results were robust (Supplementary Material S3, http://links.lww.com/MD/K704). Due to the large heterogeneity in the studies, we performed subgroup analyses according to the diseases and scales and divided the study components according to the diseases into an MPS group, an RA group, an FM group, and an OA group. We also partitioned the study according to the scales into an NPQ group, a VAS group, a WOMAC group, and a Von Korff group. However, subgroup analysis showed that there was no significant interaction among these factors (Supplementary Materials S4, http://links.lww.com/MD/K705 and S5, http://links.lww.com/MD/K706). We used funnel plots, Egger test, and Peter test to examine publication bias. The symmetrical shape of the funnel plot indicates a slight bias and the P values for the Egger and Peter tests revealed no significant publication bias (P = .083) (Fig. 5).

Funnel plot of TA versus SANA. SANAs = superficial acupuncture on non-acupoints, TA = true acupuncture.

3.5.1.2. TA versus NPA.

The authors adopted NPA for SA in 20 articles, including 906 cases in the TA group and 881 cases in the NPA group. The heterogeneity of the pooled analysis was high (P < .00001, I2 = 91.3%), and thus a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The results showed that TA significantly reduced pain-related scale scores (SMD = 0.75; 95% CI, 0.41 to 1.09; Z = 4.28, P < .001) (Fig. 6), and sensitivity analysis revealed that the results were robust (Supplementary Material S6, http://links.lww.com/MD/K707). Due to the large heterogeneity in the studies, we conducted subgroup analyses according to the diseases and scales, and partitioned the study according to the diseases into an MPS (TTH, NP, shoulder pain, LBP) group, an RA group, an FM group, and an OA group. The study was also divided according to the scales into a VAS group and a WOMAC group. However, subgroup analysis did not reveal any significant interaction among these factors (Supplementary Materials S7, http://links.lww.com/MD/K708 and S8, http://links.lww.com/MD/K709). We used funnel plots, Egger test, and Peter test to examine publication bias. The symmetrical shape of the funnel plot indicates a slight bias and the P values for the Egger and Peter tests revealed no significant publication bias (P = .078) (Fig. 7).

Forest plot of TA versus NPA. NPAs = non-penetration on acupoints, TA = true acupuncture.

Funnel plot of TA versus NPA. NPAs = non-penetration on acupoints, TA = true acupuncture.

3.5.1.3. TA versus NPNA.

In 3 articles, NPNA was used for SA, including 126 cases in the TA group and 125 cases in the NPNA group. The heterogeneity of the pooled analysis was high (P < .00001, I2 = 98.4%), and thus a random-effects model was used for the meta-analysis. The results showed that the TA significantly reduced pain-related scale scores (SMD = 2.32; 95% CI, −0.67 to 5.32; Z = 1.53, P = .128) (Fig. 8).

Forest plot of TA versus NPANA. TA = true acupuncture.

3.6. NMA

Forty-two RCTs reported pain-related scale scores, yielding 96 pairwise comparisons that involved 5 interventions, and the network relationship was generally centered on TA. The network map formed 4 closed loops: BC-NPA-TA + BC-SANA-TA + SANA-NPA-TA + BC-SANA-NPA as shown in Figure 9. As the results concerning inconsistency detection of the loop were not significant, the consistency model was acceptable (P = .4611). We used the node-splitting method for local inconsistency testing, and the results showed that there was no local inconsistency(Supplementary Material S11, http://links.lww.com/MD/K712). Based on the SUCRA and mean rank (Fig. 8), the priorities concerning effectiveness as measured by pain-related scale scores were as follows: TA (SUCRA, 99.9%), SANA (65.3%), NPA (46.2%), NPANA (34.2%), and BC (4.5%) (Fig. 10).

3.8. GRADE assessment

We strictly followed the GRADE guidelines to evaluate the NMA evidence, adopting the GRADE criteria to compare the direct and indirect evidence.[66,67] The quality of the evidence for outcomes ranged from “low” to “moderate,” with the principal reasons for the degradation being imprecise meta-analysis results and the risk of bias (Supplementary Material S9, http://links.lww.com/MD/K710 and S10, http://links.lww.com/MD/K711).

4. Discussion

4.1. Summary of the main results

In our meta-analysis, we included 42 trials for traditional and NMA. In traditional meta-analysis, TA was more effective than SANA, NPA, NPANA, or BC on MPS. Similar results were found in the NMA; we also analyzed all interventions using NMA, of which TA was the most effective, followed by SANA, NPA, NPANA, and then BC. In this NMA and according to the therapeutic effects in the pain-score results, the rankings were as follows: TA, SANA, NPA, NPANA, and BC.

4.2. Possible explanations for the current findings

There is accumulating evidence that the effects of different placebo interventions may vary considerably,[16] and our research results also confirmed that different types of SA procedures exerted various therapeutic effects. For example, in SA, SANA demonstrated the greatest therapeutic effect. However, needles that are inserted only superficially may also produce a cumulative, beneficial effect resulting from multiple stimulations of superficial sensory nerve endings or connective tissue.[68] Investigators using functional magnetic resonance imaging found that superficial and deep needling of an acupuncture point elicited similar blood oxygen level-dependent responses.[69] In TCM theory, superficial penetration is a type of acupuncture, and some Japanese acupuncturists consider superficial needling to be highly effective and, therefore, insert needles only a few millimeters into the skin in clinical practice.[70] Wu et al found that superficial needling produced a favorable therapeutic effect for knee joint pain compared with routine acupuncture,[71] and superficial acupuncture was also reported to be beneficial in shoulder periarthritis.[72] Needling at non-acupoints in the dysfunctional region might exert biological effects similar to the ones achieved when using needling acupuncture points and generate some effect on the pain.[73] In SA, NPA also possesses a certain therapeutic effect. Since the introduction of the blunt needle mimics real acupuncture without penetrating the skin, the credibility of this needling procedure has been tested in many ways.[74,75] A study revealed that a light touch of the skin stimulated mechanoreceptors that induced emotional and hormonal reactions that alleviated the pain.[76] When pressed at true points, this might constitute an effective form of acupressure and has been shown to be effective for postoperative nausea.[77] Zeng additionally concluded that non-penetrating SA reflected a different pathway of neuromodulation relative to penetrating SA.[78]

4.3. Comparison with previous studies

A previous meta-analysis confirmed that acupuncture produced a clinically relevant, persistent effect on chronic pain that was not completely explained by placebo effects.[79] In a systematic review, the authors sought to determine whether SA was as effective as TA and identified 38 acupuncture trials. These investigators did not discern any difference in outcomes, and SA proved to be as effective as TA, particularly when superficial needling was applied to non-points.[14] In a re-analysis, another group attempted to ascertain whether effects associated with SA differed from those of other “physical placebos.” Their pooled SMD was − 0.41 (95% CI, −0.56 to − 0.24) between SA and no treatment, while the SMD was − 0.26 (95% CI, −0.37 to − 0.15) between other physical placebos and no treatment. These authors suggested that SA might be associated with larger effects than pharmacologic or other physical placebos. One review provided low-quality evidence that real acupuncture exerted a moderate effect (an approximate 12-point reduction on the 100-mm VAS) on musculoskeletal pain.[10] Results of a recent NMA suggested that most acupuncture therapies—including acupuncture combined with other therapies—were effective in decreasing pain and improving physical function, but additional investigation on the safety of these therapies was required.[80] However, there was no comparison made of the effects of different types of SA procedures in the NMA.

5. Strengths and limitations

This study possesses several strengths. This was the first-ever meta-analysis in which different SA methods were compared with almost all musculoskeletal disorders related to pain; this systematic review was registered on PROSPERO and followed the PRISMA guidelines; and the majority of the studies were of high quality. In traditional meta-analysis, sensitivity analysis and subgroup analysis are conducted to explore possible sources of heterogeneity, while we herein conducted comparisons of different forms of SA procedures using NMA, thus achieving global and local consistency.

There were, however, several limitations to the present NMA. First, most studies included RCTs with a variety of treatment sessions and endpoints. Several systematic reviews indicated that factors such as acupuncture dosage, the manner of application, or the number of physician contacts could influence response rates.[81–83] If we were to adopt different treatment sessions and endpoints, our conclusions might have been better validated and have more guiding significance. Second, in most of the included studies, the authors did not evaluate the success of blinding the implementation of SA. Third, our review only comprised analyses of indicators related to pain and did not include indicators such as quality of life. Fourth, because most of the TA procedures in the studies that use sham laser acupuncture as a SA are laser acupuncture, in our review, TA procedures is limited to TCM acupuncture-related therapies, so we did not include sham laser acupuncture as one of the SA procedures. Lastly, our study only included 7 types of MPS and did not cover more diseases, such as temporomandibular disorders. We expect that there will be more high-quality RCTs implemented that will involve additional SA treatments to further explore the effects of acupuncture in treating MPS—including safety, effectiveness, stability, and durability. We hope that the above limitations can be addressed in our future updates systematic review.

6. Conclusions

To improve our understanding of how acupuncture specific effects differed from placebo, we found it necessary to evaluate expectancy in the context of real and different SA procedures. Our research thus focused on the effects of different SA procedures on MPS. Compared with SA, TA was effective in treating MPS. However, the therapeutic effects produced by different SA procedures were different, and the sequence of effects from greatest to least was SANA, NPA, and NPANA. The results of our study will provide additional targeted evidence to assist the clinical design of SA to evaluate the true efficacy of acupuncture. However, additional large-scale, high-quality, and diverse SA groups in RCTs are still needed to validate our findings.
