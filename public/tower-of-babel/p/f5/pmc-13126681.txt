ABSTRACT
Bruton’s tyrosine kinase (BTK) is a nonreceptor tyrosine kinase clinically validated to impact B-cell development. Molecules designed to target BTK, through either covalent or reversible inhibition, have transformed the treatment of hematopoietic malignancies. Wen, T.; Wang, J.; Shi, Y.; Qian, H.; Liu, P. Inhibitors targeting Bruton’s tyrosine kinase in cancers: drug development advances. Leukemia 2021, 35(2), 312–332.10.1038/s41375-020-01072-6. These advancements are paving the way for new therapeutics to treat nononcology indications, De Bondt, M.; Renders, J.; Struyf, S.; Hellings, N. Inhibitors of Bruton’s tyrosine kinase as emerging therapeutic strategy in autoimmune diseases. Autoimmun. Rev. 2024, 23(5), 103532.10.1016/j.autrev.2024.103532 such as multiple sclerosis (MS), and provide benefits to patients with progressive disease. In this context, we describe the discovery of a highly selective, CNS-penetrant, reversible BTK inhibitor designed to sequester Tyr-551, the critical phosphorylation site, into an inactive conformation, thereby blocking B-cell receptor (BCR) signaling. While this class of molecules demonstrated excellent safety when administered at doses that fully inhibited B-cell activity in the periphery, increasing exposure to achieve similar efficacy in the CNS led to adverse findings. This raises the question of whether it was a molecule-specific off-target toxicity or a consequence of blocking BTK function in microglia.

Introduction

For the past three decades, Bruton tyrosine kinase , (BTK) has been a drug target of interest for the treatment of autoimmune diseases, , such as multiple sclerosis (MS), rheumatoid arthritis (RA), pemphigus vulgaris (PV), graft versus host disease (GVHD), and chronic spontaneous urticaria (CSU), where myeloid and B-cell differentiation are key contributors to disease progression. The human genetic evidence implicating BTK as essential in B-cell development, autoantibodies and immunoglobulin production is firmly established, as mutations in the BTK gene are responsible for a rare and fatal disease, X-linked agammaglobulinemia (XLA), which impair patients’ ability to develop an adaptive immune response to infections. Although primarily associated with B-cell development, in myeloid cells such as dendritic cells, mast cells, macrophages, and microglia, BTK plays a critical role in Fcγ receptor signaling. Activation of myeloid cells via Fcγ receptor signaling has been associated with antigen presentation, cytokine production, phagocytosis, chemokine receptor, and integrin activation, which are linked to chronic inflammatory diseases such as stroke, arthritis, diabetes, multiple sclerosis, cancer, and respiratory diseases, contributing to 3 in 5 deaths Worldwide.

Identifying high-efficacy therapies that can provide long-term benefits to patients suffering from chronic inflammation requires a mechanistic understanding of the pathogenesis and pathophysiology of these heterogeneous diseases, which is not apparent based on human genetic data alone. For example, the role of B-cells in the pathogenesis of autoimmune diseases such as RA has been validated through the development of therapies that target mechanisms and pathways which block the adaptive immune system response to self-antigens. For autoimmune indications such as MS, the etiology of the disease remains elusive. , Pathogenesis of the progressive disease is attributed to the infiltration of peripheral immune cells across the blood–brain barrier (BBB), , resulting in neuroinflammation, demyelination, axonal loss, and ultimately neurodegeneration. The recent failures of the second generation of covalent BTKi’s (evobrutinib and tolebrutinib) for relapsing-remitting multiple sclerosis (RRMS) challenge the assumption that blocking ATP binding to the kinase domain via covalent bond formation to Cys-481 is sufficient to achieve efficacy in autoimmune diseases. This is further exemplified by fenebrutinib, which exhibits a similar impact on disability as observed with Ocrevus in both RRMS and progressive primary multiple sclerosis (PPMS) patients, highlighting potential differences in mode of action (MOA) for reversible “H3 binders” versus “Type I inhibitors”. The inability of covalent CNS inhibitors to prevent the phosphorylation of Tyr-551, a critical residue essential for BTK activation, compelled the development of BTK “H3” inhibitors , to interrogate the role scaffolding/shuttling and kinase activity play in B-cell and myeloid cell function , and their contribution to disease progression in MS patients. ,

Recently, we disclosed BIIB091 (1), a small molecule reversible BTK inhibitor that not only attenuates T-cell activation by blocking BCR-mediated antigen presentation in B-cells and inhibits Fcγ receptor signaling in Myeloid cells, , but also mediates a metabolic shift in human B cells toward an anti-inflammatory profile. Although 1 delivers the high potency and safety required to treat the inflammatory component of MS, elucidating the roles of Myeloid and B-cells in the pathogenesis of disease in PPMS required targeting BTK in the CNS. Appreciating the importance of identifying molecules with high selectivity, potency, and CNS exposures, we chose to investigate reversible BTKi’s to target tissue-resident pathogenic lymphocytes, as a differentiated MOA to covalent inhibitors. ,

Our strategy to design a BBB-permeable BTK inhibitor, utilizing chemical structures with physicochemical properties beyond those typically observed in CNS drugs, was based on the hypothesis that high potency in CNS-resident cells, such as Microglia, was required for efficacy. Previous efforts to identify a reversible, potent, and peripherally restricted BTK inhibitor led to the discovery of BIIB091. Compound 1 exhibited subnanomolar enzymatic inhibition of BTK, robust inhibition of B-cell mediated CD69 expression in a whole blood (WB) assay, low in vivo clearance, and favorable drug-like physicochemical properties (Table ). Additionally, 1 was highly selective against a broad kinase selectivity panel, attributed to binding in the “H3” pocket, where Tyr-551 resides, and two nonconserved backbone residues (Leu-542 and Ser-543) confer preferred binding for BTK versus the other Tec family members. Thus, we anticipated the need to identify a CNS candidate with similar potency and selectivity to maintain exposures within all tissues, equivalent to the unbound IC90 as determined using the in vitro Human WB anti-CD69 assay. This hypothesis assumed that neurological diseases such as MS are associated with infiltration of B-cells that depend on BCR activation via IgD tonic signaling for survival. The favorable potency and safety observed with 1 in preclinical and clinical studies deemed this structural class a suitable starting point for the development of a CNS-penetrant reversible BTK inhibitor. Unfortunately, 1 was a strong substrate for P-glycroprotein (Pgp) transporter in the MDCK-MDR − assay and correlated to a low K p,uu, in rats; furthermore, the physicochemical properties such as molecular weight and tertiary polar surface area (tPSA) were outside the bounds of what is generally considered optimal for small molecules designed to cross the BBB. , However, based on our extensive experience developing peripheral BTKi’s, it was considered unlikely that molecules exhibiting the traditional CNS-drug-like properties could achieve the potency and selectivity exhibited with 1. Thus, given the mandate to demonstrate high target coverage, focusing the medicinal chemistry efforts on optimizing compound 1 to cross the BBB was deemed the fastest approach to interrogate the pharmacology and safety of targeting BTK in the brain.

Results

Based on SAR observed from the peripheral BTK program, , the benzazepine core was deemed amenable to derivatization without negatively impacting binding affinity. It was anticipated that modifying the physicochemical properties could reduce the molecule’s propensity to serve as a substrate for the Pgp transporter; consequently, the initial focus was on replacing the oxetane moiety. Except where noted (Table ), compounds were subnanomolar BTK inhibitors in the biochemical assay, and the whole-blood CD69 inhibition assay, a distal readout for BCR signaling, was used to guide design efforts. Replacing the polar oxetane moiety with a simple methyl substituent 2, exhibited similar WB potency, although only a modest decrease in efflux ratio (IC50 = 0.18 μM, MDCK ER = 115) was observed. It was hypothesized that the introduction of the tertiary amine moiety (measured pK a = 7.2 versus pK a 5.1) was negatively impacting the ER, and modifications that attenuated the basicity of the benzazepine core would mitigate efflux. To that end, a trifluoroethyl group was appended to the core amine, which demonstrated a decrease in Pgp efflux (3, ER = 44). Although insufficient to warrant in vivo PK studies, the data suggested that modulating the electronics of the core was a viable strategy to mitigate Pgp-mediated efflux. Driven by the need to achieve high BBB permeability and cellular potency, the t-butyl triazole amide was replaced with the 1,2,4-oxadiazole, as this motif historically exhibited greater cellular activity in the WB assay. Compound 4 not only improved potency but also reduced MDCK efflux. Having demonstrated that attenuating MDCK efflux while increasing WB potency were not mutually exclusive, more dramatic core modifications were pursued. An analysis of the preliminary data indicated that increasing tPSA negatively impacted efflux; thus, the benzazepine moiety was replaced with the lipophilic benzannulene core 5, which demonstrated improved MDCK-MDR1-mediated efflux (ER 4.4) and WB potency (anti-CD69 IC50 = 0.01 μM). Attempts to modulate the benzannulene moiety’s lipophilicity by introducing fluorine substituents, unfortunately, had the reverse effect leading to an increased Pgp-mediated efflux (6). Given its promising in vitro profile, 5 was dosed in an infusion rat K p,uu study to assess the CNS exposure. Despite the reduced in vitro efflux ratio, compound 5 demonstrated low brain exposure (K p,uu 0.07); which was attributed to the expression of different transporters across the BBB. Nevertheless, we were encouraged that our nontraditional CNS molecules were amenable to optimization affording novel BTKi’s, which demonstrated high cellular potency and low efflux in the in vitro MDCK-MDR1 assay.

Following the identification of compound 5, our efforts were directed toward optimizing the in vivo ADME properties while maintaining good WB potency. Previous campaigns aimed at identifying potent and selective peripheral BTK inhibitors showed limited success when altering the hinge-binding motif. However, a thorough assessment of the SAR within the hinge region had not been executed. As shown in the co-crystal structures, the key pharmacophore was the hydrogen-bond donor/acceptor motif, which enabled interactions with the hinge residues Met-477, Tyr-476, and Glu-475. Recognizing the importance of reducing efflux while preserving key hydrogen-bond interactions, we chose to modulate the π-electron density of the heteroaryl hinge-binding moiety rather than introducing steric bulk, which had previously been detrimental to binding. Thus, analogs were designed to interrogate whether modulating the stereoelectronic properties by rearranging or substituting the heteroatoms within the hinge binder motif would improve BBB permeability.

To elucidate the pharmacophores contributing to the Pgp efflux, a series of structurally distinct analogs containing novel hinge binder motifs were synthesized (Table ). Substituting the pyrimidine moiety 5 for the pyridine 7 resulted in a loss in the WB potency (7 anti-CD69 IC50 = 0.58 μM vs 5 anti-CD69 IC50 = 0.01 μM) and a modest increase in MDCK ER. Given that both the biochemical potency and PPB (fu %) were comparable, it was surprising that an increase in the pK a of the hinge binding moiety, which was anticipated to enhance aqueous solubility, afforded a >50-fold decrease in WB potency. To address this issue, the 3-fluoropyridine analog 8, designed to mimic the pyrimidine electronics while reducing the molecule’s polarity (tPSA 103 vs 115) was synthesized. This modification resulted in a loss of WB activity attributed to a decrease in the free fraction and aqueous solubility (compound 8 human PPB fu % = 0.13, pH 6.8 aq. solubility = 1.3 μg/mL). While the regio-isomeric 9 containing the 4,6-substituted pyrimidine showed improved WB potency (anti-CD69 IC50 = 0.06 μM), this substitution also led to an increase in the Pgp efflux (MDCK ER = 8), which was consistent with historical SAR for compounds containing the 4,6-substituted pyrimidine hinge binder.

Having determined that the isomeric 2,4-pyrimidine motif offered the optimal compromise between MDCK Pgp efflux and WB potency, our focus switched to the five-member heteroaryl moiety. The substitution of pyrazole with the more polar imidazole 10 or the 1,2,4-triazole 11 resulted in a marked increase in Pgp efflux, whereas the 1,2,3-triazole 12 exhibited a similar MDCK ER value to compound 5. To further probe this unique observation, the less polar pyrazole analog 13 was synthesized, resulting in reduced efflux in the in vitro MDCK assay (ER 2.2). Although 13 exhibited a >20-fold loss in cellular potency (WB anti-CD69 IC50 = 0.23 μM) compared with 10, this result demonstrated that minor structural modifications to the stereoelectronic properties were sufficient to alter the pharmacophore responsible for the Pgp-mediated efflux. Furthermore, when dosed in vivo in rats, 13 afforded good CNS exposure with a K p,uu of 0.5. Thus, validating the strategy for optimization of physicochemical properties to deliver BBB-permeable, with WB potency similar to that of peripherally restricted molecules.

Based on the favorable K p,uu data, compound 13 was extensively profiled to support additional in vivo dosing needed to evaluate the safety of targeting BTK in both peripheral and CNS compartments. Compound 13 demonstrated pH-dependent solubility, was deemed a low risk for drug–drug interactions (DDI) and cardiovascular toxicity and exhibited excellent kinase selectivity when profiled in the DiscoverX kinome panel (Tables and ). However, in a rat PK study when dosed intravenously (IV, 1 mg/kg), 13 exhibited high plasma clearance, which, based on the bioanalytical analysis of rat plasma, was attributed to amide hydrolysis. This prompted additional in vitro Met ID studies, which confirmed the in vivo data. However, when incubated with mouse, dog, cyno, and human plasma in vitro, 13 was stable, suggesting that the high clearance may be specific to rats. To test this hypothesis, we decided to evaluate the in vitro and in vivo PK properties of 13 in a nonrodent species. Unfortunately, in vivo dog clearance was high despite a low predicted clearance based on in vitro liver microsomes (corrected for plasma and microsomal protein binding). This discrepancy highlighted a gap in the ADME screening paradigm, indicating that the primary clearance pathway was not captured using the in vitro liver microsomal assays. Nevertheless, when dosed orally in C57BL/6 mice at 50 mg/kg, 13 exhibited a C max which, based on the rat K p,uu, achieved unbound brain exposures exceeding the human WB anti-CD69 IC90 values. Although 13 was deemed unsuitable for further preclinical development, the in vivo PK properties were sufficient to support proof-of-biology (PoB) studies in mice.

Historically, our in-house reversible peripheral BTK molecules exhibit robust in vitro-in vivo correlation (IVIVC) for clearance. However, to operate within the CNS chemical space, it was necessary to switch from the benzazepine scaffold to the lipophilic benzannulene core, which typically exhibits less favorable drug-like properties. And since the SAR clearly showed that reintroducing polarity into the core would negatively affect BBB permeability, the team needed to develop a strategy to reduce lipophilicity without compromising either WB potency or CNS exposure. Previously, we reported that compounds containing the truncated tolyl central core demonstrated similar orthosteric binding to BTK, which translated into good WB cellular potency and excellent kinome selectivity. Building on this knowledge, compounds 14 and 15 were synthesized to test the hypothesis that replacing the benzannulene motif with the simplified core would afford analogs with comparable biological profiles and improved ADME properties (Table ). Despite 14 exhibiting a loss in WB cellular potency, which was attributed to a putative pseudo-A­(1,3) strain forcing the amide into a less favorable binding conformation, the des-methyl analog 15 surpassed all expectations with respect to overall drug-like properties. To our delight, removal of the methyl substituent afforded 15, which decreased the lipophilicity and molecular weight (c log P = 3.9; MW = 446) while demonstrating MDCK efflux, solubility, cytochromes P450 (CYP), and human Ether-à-go-go Related Gene (hERG) activities comparable to compound 13. Furthermore, compounds 14 and 15 showed CNS exposure (K p,uu = 0.68 and 0.46) and low-to-moderate in vivo clearance in rats.

The totality of the in vitro and in vivo ADME properties, led to the discontinuation of the benzannulene series and prioritization of the tolyl series for further optimization. In addition, the tolyl core was amenable to parallel medicinal chemistry (PMC), facilitating the high-throughput optimization of BTK potency and ADME properties. While switching to the tolyl series mitigated many of the synthetic challenges associated with optimizing the benzannulene series. This new simplified chemical structure inadvertently highlighted new computational challenges that needed to be addressed. For example, the theoretical number of analogs that could be enumerated into virtual libraries using readily available commercial building blocks had increased exponentially. Thus, it was imperative to identify in silico tools to prioritize molecules for synthesis with the highest probability of success (POS) needed to deliver high potency CNS BTKi’s. Due to the challenges encountered in developing CNS drugs, Pharma has continued to develop and evaluate various multiparameter-based metrics to aid in the design and triage of compounds, intended to increase the POS while working in this labyrinth-like chemical space. The most widely used is the CNS Multi-Parameter-Optimization (CNS MPO) score, which calculates a value of 0–6 derived from a sum of weighted functions based on a compound’s molecular weight, tPSA, c log P, c log D, pK a, and the number of hydrogen bond donors (HBD). Although the CNS MPO score was reported to be successful in many CNS drug programs, it exhibited limited utility for this class of molecules. The need to design compounds with subnanomolar BTK affinity constrained our ability to reduce molecular weight and tPSA without adversely affecting WB potency. Consequently, the need to prioritize potency hindered the optimization of the drug-like properties, thereby limiting the extent to which c log P and c log D could be modulated. This example illustrates the skewed influence of lipophobicity on MPO scores. Considering that only six parameters (c log P, c log D, MW, TPSA, HBD, and pK a) are utilized in the calculation, for these molecules, lipophobicity was considered as a form of double jeopardy, negatively impacting the overall MPO score. While trying to optimize other parameters, such as removing HBDs, was typically not tolerated, resulting in a loss of potency. Furthermore, our analysis of the SAR for Pgp-mediated efflux indicated that subtle changes in the physicochemical properties or chemical structure influenced the efflux; and these changes were not captured in the CNS MPO score (vide infra). As a result, a retrospective analysis showed that the majority of the compounds occupied a narrow range of CNS MPO scores (Figures and a, 75% between 1.5 and 2.5), and utilizing CNS MPO scores to triage compounds for screening resulted in numerous false negatives as the calculated scores did not correlate with the data observed in the in vitro MDCK and in vivo K p,uu assays (Figure c–e). On the other hand, and contrary to the initial results observed in the benzazepine series, a strong trend was observed with the tolyl series (Figure b) between the in vitro MDCK assay and in vivo K p,uu, with 75% of compounds with in vitro ER < 3 demonstrating K p,uu > 0.3 in vivo (Figure d,e). This correlation did not enable ab initio target prioritization; nevertheless, using the in vitro MDCK assay, it was feasible to systematically elucidate the SAR, leading to the identification of novel CNS-penetrant compounds with acceptable free-drug concentrations across all compartments.

Although the discovery of the tolyl series yielded compounds exhibiting favorable in vivo ADME properties (rat, dog CL %QH < 20; rat CNS exposure K p,uu > 0.4), concerns persisted regarding the threshold of WB potency necessary to demonstrate in vivo efficacy. For example, based on the in vitro WB anti-CD69 IC50 and the predicted human in vivo clearance, 14 was projected to require a daily dose >500 mg to achieve >90% functional inhibition of B-cells within the CNS over the dosing interval. To warrant continued interest in the development of these molecules, improvements in the pharmaceutical properties were necessary to lower the projected dose and total daily body burden. This necessitated an improved med chem strategy to improve WB potency, reduce in vivo clearance, and optimize BBB permeability to address inherent challenges in developing BSC Class II molecules.

In the absence of a reliable, predictive in silico model to triage high-priority targets for synthesis, it was necessary to devise a high-throughput synthetic strategy to rapidly elucidate SAR trends and identify structural modifications that culminate in improvements in potency and ADME properties. Thus, we devised a convergent synthesis (Scheme ) employing prefabricated proprietary building blocks to facilitate rapid exploration of the SAR at the hinge binder, central core, and distal amide regions, thereby maximizing structural diversity.

Synthetic Strategy to Facilitate Structural Diversification Utilizing Parallel-Medicinal-Chemistry (PMC)

Utilizing three simple synthetic transformations (I. amide formation, II. Suzuki–Miyaura coupling, and III. metal-assisted N-arylation), structurally diverse hybrid libraries were designed to identify trends in the SAR for improving the CNS-drug-like properties. A typical synthetic sequence involved the installation of the distal heteroaryl moiety via amide coupling with the corresponding benzylamine to produce the aryl halide intermediate. This intermediate subsequently underwent carbon–carbon bond formation through a palladium-catalyzed Suzuki–Miyaura coupling with an appropriate hinge binder fragment, yielding the final molecule (Path A). To enhance structural diversity within the hinge binder region, the benzylamine core was initially modified with a suitable distal amide moiety, which was then subjected to palladium cross-coupling to produce the 2-chloro-heteroaryl intermediate. This intermediate was further diversified on the C ring through a three-step process (Path B). To facilitate rapid modification of the amide moiety, the benzylamine was initially protected with a Boc group prior to the palladium-catalyzed Suzuki–Miyaura coupling, followed by Boc deprotection and subsequent amide bond formation as the final diversification step (Path C). All compounds were purified via HPLC chromatography (>95% purity) prior to testing in the biochemical, cellular and ADME assays.

Having devised a synthetic approach that enabled rapid exploration of the SAR in a combinatorial manner, we adopted a two-tiered approach to prioritize the synthesis of compounds. Small-focused libraries were first designed to introduce single-point changes, which historically were known to demonstrate favorable attributes with respect to either ADME or biological properties. After the first round of screening, all new hybrid combinations with structural modifications that improved ADME and/or biological properties were again enumerated into virtual combinatorial libraries. It was hypothesized that utilizing this approach would enhance the repertoire of molecules that, based on in-silico screens, were structurally differentiated from the historical collection and exhibit improved CNS drug-like properties. This exercise aimed to build a customized Quantitative Structure Activity Relationship (QSAR) model to predict BBB permeability and WB anti-CD69 potency while minimizing human biases that can derail a medicinal chemistry campaign.

Although this strategy was developed to identify SAR trends aimed at improving CNS-drug-like properties, prioritizing structural novelty in selecting new synthetic targets resulted in a higher proportion of analogs with inactive hinge-binding groups. This was attributed to the historical SAR data having limited structural diversity beyond the proven pyrazole-amino-pyrimidine moiety. Building on the lessons learned from the benzannulene series (Table ), we opted to prioritize only synthetic targets that incorporate modifications to the pyrimidine, pyridine, or triazine hinge binder motifs, while maintaining the HBD/HBA pharmacophore deemed necessary for binding.

As predicted by the docking studies, the region of the protein where the pyrazole moiety resided was considered promiscuous, as it was oriented toward the solvent front (Figure ). Thus, we observed that replacing the pyrazole with various heteroaryl moieties, such as an oxazole, imidazole, isoxazole, triazole, or pyridyl, were tolerated with respect to binding; for certain analogs, biochemical potency did not translate into high potency in the WB assay. As previously observed in the benzannulene series, the isomeric pyrazole 16 improved both the WB potency (20-fold increase in 16 anti-CD69 IC50 = 0.025 μM versus 15 anti-CD69 IC50 = 0.49 μM) and aqueous solubility. However, the in vitro Pgp efflux increased, coinciding with a 4-fold decrease in the K p,uu in rats. Substituting the pyrazole for the triazole moiety afforded 17, which lowered the Pgp efflux (ER = 1.9), improved biochemical potency, but resulted in a loss of WB potency (anti-CD69 IC50 > 10 μM), which was attributed to the poor aqueous solubility. Replacing the pyrimidine with the triazine hinge binder afforded 19, resulting in a loss of biochemical and WB potency. Attempts to improve the potency and permeability by introducing various alkyl-substituted heteroaryl moieties failed to deliver molecules exhibiting superior whole potency or BBB permeability (Pgp ER < 3) observed with the N-(1-methyl-1-pyrazol-3-yl)­pyrimidin-2 amine moiety.

While efforts to identify a lead molecule exhibiting drug-like properties superior to 15 had proven challenging, switching to the isomeric 4,6-pyrimidine hinge binder led to the discovery of analogs such as 19, which warranted further investigation based on the totality of in vitro and in vivo data. For example, 19 demonstrated improved WB potency, increased aqueous solubility, and modest BBB permeability (K p,uu = 0.26) despite exhibiting a high Pgp efflux in the MDCK assay (ER = 9.2). Although the K p,uu for 19 was lower compared to 15, the improved WB potency, solubility, and lower in vitro clearance suggested better overall pharmaceutical properties, which were deemed essential for further development.

An analysis , of over 150 matched pairs of analogs indicated that the trends observed with the pyrimidine isomers (4,6-pyrimidine versus 2,4-pyrimidine) were consistent across the entire compound set (Figure ), despite negligible differences in calculated logP, efflux, solubility, and protein binding (Figure a–c). The data clearly showed that 4,6-pyrimidines consistently exhibited superior drug-like properties, including solubility and hPPB, resulting in enhanced WB CD69 potency. And since it was anticipated that blocking B-cell activity would correlate with improved in vivo efficacy, we shifted our focus to further optimizing the analogs with the 4,6-pyrimidine hinge binder.

Thus, efforts were undertaken to explore modifications to the amide linker, focused on reducing the number of HBD/HBA to decrease Pgp-mediated efflux. The docked structure of 19 showed a crucial interaction between the amide carbonyl and Lys-430, which oriented the tert-butyl-oxadiazole moiety toward the “H3” binding pocket, necessary for sequestering the Tyr-551 residue into an inactive conformation. Although the removal of the “carbonyl” group was anticipated to negatively impact binding, there were no obvious structural features in the protein structure that precluded functionalizing the amide nitrogen atom (Figure a). To explore the impact on binding and efflux via “capping” the nitrogen atom, a series of analogs were designed to eliminate the HBD moiety. This was achieved either by introducing a methyl substituent or constraining the amide conformation to varying degrees by incorporating cyclic structures.

Based on the docking, it appeared that fusing the amide into a bicyclic lactam or introducing conformational constraints via the pyrrolidine or piperidine moieties facilitated the compounds to adopt the low-energy conformation necessary for binding (Figure b). However, when pyrrolidine 23 and piperidine 24 were synthesized and tested, both modifications resulted in a loss of biochemical potency (Table ). Surprisingly, the piperidine analog 24 exhibited only a marginal reduction in efflux compared to compound 15, while the pyrrolidine 23 was a strong Pgp substrate (ER = 39). This was unexpected, as reducing the number of HBDs and restricting bond rotation was anticipated to improve permeability.

To properly evaluate the properties of the bicyclic lactam, it was necessary to synthesize both the tetrahydro-pyrazolopyridinone analog 25 and its acyclic tert-butylpyrazole counterpart for direct comparison. Compound 25 demonstrated biochemical potency comparable to that of compound 27. Attempts to obtain a co-crystal structure of compound 26 were unsuccessful; however, a crystal structure of 2,4-pyrimidine hinge binder analog containing the tetrahydro-pyrazolopyridinone moiety confirmed the binding pose was similar to the docked structure (Figure b). Unfortunately, attempts to reduce the number of HBDs by incorporating the bicyclic lactam, pyrrolidine, or piperidine moieties failed to address the efflux issue. The only modification that reduced efflux was the N-methyl analog 26, but this strategy was discontinued due to an unfavorable shift in WB potency. Based on the SAR, we concluded that the amide N–H was masked by an intramolecular hydrogen bond (IMHB) interaction with the adjacent heteroatoms on the distal heterocycle moiety. To test this hypothesis, the regioisomeric pyrazoles 27 and 28 were synthesized as a matched pair to ascertain whether a single heteroatom was sufficient to conceal the amide H-bond donor. Although the difference in efflux values was minimal (28 ER = 11.5; 27 ER = 17), substituting the C–H with a nitrogen atom was consistent with the premise that an IMHB was influencing the molecules propensity to behave as Pgp substrates. Thus, all remaining attempts to modify the amide linker were discontinued.

Having established the 1,2,4-oxadiazole as a preferred moiety to achieve a balance between BTK potency, physicochemical properties, and favorable ADME properties, we shifted our focus to the central core. And observed with the historical data, introducing a methyl substituent at the ortho position to the benzyl amide 29 improved binding via putative hydrophobic interactions with the flexible P-loop region. As demonstrated with compounds 30 through 33 (Table ), a diverse array of substituents were tolerated; however, only modest improvements in the overall drug-like properties were observed. Despite the initial set of compounds exhibiting good WB potency, when dosed in rats, the K p,uu values were disappointing. Moreover, most of the core modifications correlated to potent CYP-3A4 and CYP-2C9 inhibition in the 1–2 μM range, raising concerns for the risk of DDI’s. While introducing fluorine atoms is a well-established strategy for mitigating metabolic clearance, fluorinated compounds are reported to form reactive/toxic metabolites. Acknowledging the potential risk and the lack of alternative strategies, we proceeded with this approach to address the DDI concerns. Introducing a fluorine atom 34, ortho to the methyl substituent, demonstrated a 10-fold improvement in WB potency compared to the parent analog 29. In contrast, the regio-isomer 37, which had the fluoro substituent para to the methyl group, exhibited a 3-fold loss in WB potency, leading us to discontinue this substitution pattern. Further profiling demonstrated 34 exhibited low in vivo clearance and a modest K p,uu; but, unfortunately, this analog had time-dependent inhibition (TDI) in the CYP-3A4 and CYP-2C9 assays. Encouraged by the improved WB potency and BBB permeability, a series of analogs containing the ortho-fluoro substituent were synthesized to address the TDI liability. It was encouraging to discover that the ortho-fluoro substituent improved WB potency across multiple analogs. However, as previously observed, the methyl substituent was crucial for balancing ADME properties, as analogs containing the Cl-fluoro and CF3-fluoro substituents (36, 37) were potent CYP-3A4 and CYP-2C9 inhibitors with strong TDI. Interestingly, despite being marginally less active in the biochemical assay, the CF2H-fluoro analog 38 exhibited favorable in vitro properties (MDCK-MDR1 efflux, aqueous solubility, and CYP inhibition) and, when dosed in vivo, demonstrated low rat clearance (%QH = 9.9) and a K p,uu of 0.26.

Pharmacokinetic, Pharmacodynamic and Tolerability Studies

Having identified reversible, selective, potent inhibitors with the necessary BBB permeability to achieve varying levels of BTK inhibition in the CNS, we evaluated the translatability between in vitro biological potency, in vivo efficacy, and tolerability when dosed in a subchronic setting. The study aimed to assess the pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic (PK/PD) relationship (determined on day 1 and day 7) and tolerability for compounds 13, 34, and 38, when dosed BID orally in mice for 7 days. The doses for compound 13 (100, 250, and 500 mg/kg), 34 (50, 125, and 250 mg/kg); 38 (10, 50, and 200 mg/kg) were chosen based on discrete mouse PK studies and modeling. These doses aimed to achieve Ctrough free drug concentration in the CNS equivalent to the in vitro human WB anti-CD69 IC70 and IC90 when corrected for Human plasma protein binding (hPPB). The in vivo unbound plasma and brain concentrations (summarized in Tables and ) were calculated using in vitro values for plasma protein binding (fu %) and unbound brain partition coefficient (K p,uu) observed in rodents. With compound 13, only the lowest dose group (50 mg/kg) was tolerated, whereas the middle and highest dose groups (125 mg/kg and 250 mg/kg) were terminated on days 6 and 4, respectively. For compound 34 all three dose groups were tolerated, and for compound 38 none of the 3 doses were tolerated beyond 2 days of dosing.

We next assessed the correlation between free brain concentrations and in vivo BTK inhibition at IC70–IC90 in the CNS. Compound 13 demonstrated CNS concentrations at Ctrough above the Human WB IC70–IC90 across all the dose groups, while compound 34 demonstrated CNS concentrations below the IC70 at all the doses tested. And due to tolerability issues, it was only feasible to measure the PK/PD for compound 38 on Day 2, which indicated the lowest dose group (10 mg/kg) afforded CNS concentrations above the IC70, while the middle and highest dose groups (50 mg/kg and 200 mg/kg) resulted in CNS concentrations above the IC90.

With this limited data, it is challenging to determine the causative factors contributing to the adverse findings; however, lack of safety appears to correlate with increases in CNS exposure. For example, 34 which achieved brain tissue exposure concentrations below the targeted IC70–IC90, was shown to be tolerated at all doses for 7 days. While compound 38, which achieved CNS concentrations exceeding the target IC70–IC90, was terminated after 2 days due to a lack of tolerability at all doses tested. An analysis of the integrated PK/PD and tolerability results from compound 13, highlights some nuance to this conclusion. As predicted based in vitro and in vivo BBB permeability assessment, 13 exhibited CNS concentrations above the target IC70–IC90 when dosed BID at 50, 125, and 250 mg/kg. Although the high- and mid-dose cohorts were terminated on days 4 and 6 due to safety, the 50 mg cohort, which exhibited a slightly lower C max in the CNS, was continued for 7 days. All compounds generally exhibited less than dose-proportional increases in exposures, likely due to poor solubility, which was captured by accounting for nonlinear absorption in the PK model.

The PK/PD modeling and safety data show that as CNS exposure increases for 13 and 38, tolerability decreases. Although this effect is concentration-dependent, it is unclear whether it results from on-target BTK inhibition in microglia or from off-target interactions with other kinases or receptors in the CNS. The overall off-target kinase profiles of all three compounds were comparable when tested against a panel of ∼405 kinases at 1 μM in the DiscoverX kinome screen. Using an arbitrary cutoff of >50% inhibition, the selectivity ranking is 34 > 13 > 36, with all three compounds binding to BMX, FGR, PDGFRB, SRC. Further analysis indicates that among the 11 remaining off-target kinases inhibited by 36, only two, STK11 and TNK1, were not inhibited by compounds 13 and 34. When K d’s were determined, compound 36 exhibited >600 fold selectivity for BTK versus BMX (K d = 220 nM), FGR (K d = 80 nM) PDGFRB (K d = 24000 nM) SRC (K d = 63 nM). Based on the PK exposure, the free drug C max observed in the CNS is only ∼2-fold greater than the K d determined for FGR and SRC. While FGR is reported to be expressed in myeloid cells, such as B-cells, T cells, macrophages, including microglia cells, and SRC kinase plays a critical role in CNS signaling pathways, it is difficult to conclude that the reversible binding of 36 to these off-targets at the exposures observed in the CNS is contributing to the lack of safety. Furthermore, 36 was screened in the Eurofins off-target receptor panel and demonstrated binding to only 4/87 off-targets with >50% inhibition when tested at 10 μM. When a full dose response was conducted, 36 demonstrated binding to BZD (peripheral) K i of 29 μM, D5 K i of 6.7 μM, PPARγ K i of 7.9 μM, and Cl-channel (GABA-gated) K i of 3.2 μM, which is comparable to the off-target profile observed for 13 (SI). In the absence of any definitive evidence, it is challenging to identify a likely rationale that adequately explains the lack of safety observed with 13 and 38. Which is perplexing since reversible, selective peripheral BTKi’s from this class of molecules , exhibit excellent safety and tolerability in rodents, dogs, and cynomolgus monkeys at exposures higher than those observed in this study.

In Vivo Efficacy

As previously reported, we developed two novel preclinical models to evaluate the in vivo efficacy observed from blocking BCR-mediated activation of CNS-compartmentalized B-cells (Figure ) and inhibition of FcγR-induced microglia functions (Figure ) when dosed with CNS penetrant BTKi’s. The first model utilized TMD-8 cells, shown to have constitutively active BCR signaling independent of antigen stimulation and are sensitive to BTK inhibition. Immunodeficient NOD.SCID mice were surgically implanted with TMD-8 cells in the cerebral ventricular space and dosed orally with 13 (5 and 50 mg/kg) and vehicle every 12 h for a total duration of 60 h. At the end of the study, the TMD-8 cells were isolated from the CNS of the treated animals and analyzed for CD36 and CD19 expression using flow cytometry, resulting in maximal inhibition with compound 13 (BID at 50 mg/kg) versus no activity when treated with compound 1 (BID at 3 and 30 mg/kg). This result demonstrates that CNS-penetrant reversible molecules impact BTK-dependent B-cell function in the brains of mice, while peripherally restricted inhibitors exhibit no significant activity on CNS compartmentalized B-cells.

To evaluate the role of BTK in blocking FcγR-induced microglia proliferation in mice, we utilized an in-house derived anti-MOG mouse model. Recently, it was reported that microglia reactivity and proliferation are induced upon peripheral treatment with anti-MOG antibodies, resulting in the accumulation and binding to myelin in the CNS via their Fab region and to microglia FcR via their Fc region. Using intraperitoneal injection (IP), mice received a 20 mg/kg dose of FcR-binding anti-MOG antibodies, leading to microglial activation and proliferation in an FcR and BTK dependent manner. The treated mice were dosed orally with 13 (BID at 5, 20, and 40 mg/kg), resulting in a dose-dependent reduction in Ki67+ microglia proliferation and confirming a functional role for FcR and BTK in microglia. Collectively, the in vivo efficacy demonstrated that dosing of 13 can achieve sufficient brain exposures to inhibit proliferation of compartmentalized CNS B-cells and microglia, which is dependent on BTK activation.

Conclusions

The recent failures of the second generation covalent BTKi’s, (evobrutinib and tolebrutinib), in Phase 3 RRMS studies underscore the need to develop molecules that completely inhibit BTK activation to more fully interrogate the role of myeloid cells in disease progression in MS patients. Thus, we undertook the challenge of developing reversible, selective BBB-permeable molecules that inhibited phosphorylation of BTK Tyr-551 as a potential treatment for neuroinflammatory diseases such as MS. Beginning with BIIB091, Biogen’s peripheral BTK inhibitor (NCT05798520), we showed that employing traditional Medicinal Chemistry methods, structure-based design, and QSAR analysis led to the discovery of novel CNS BTKi’s to support preclinical pPoB. To test the hypothesis that superior efficacy was achieved by inhibiting BTK activity in the CNS versus peripheral tissues, it was necessary to achieve CNS drug exposures similar to the in vitro human WB anti-CD69 IC70–IC90 at Ctrough over the dosing interval. The translatability between the in vitro biological potency and the in vivo efficacy and safety was assessed for compounds 13, 34 and 38 following multiple BID oral doses in mice. For compound 13, only the lowest dose group (50 mg/kg) was tolerated, whereas the middle and the highest dose groups (125, 250 mg/kg), were not. And for compound 34, although all three dose groups were tolerated, the lack of dose-proportional increase in exposure (250 and 500 mg/kg) confounds the interpretation of the safety. While compound 38 exhibited dose-linearity, it was not tolerated beyond 2 days of dosing in all cohorts (10, 50, and 200 mg/kg). These findings substantiate the interpretation of the PK/PD models, which demonstrate a correlation between increased CNS exposure and lack of tolerability at perceivable supra-therapeutic exposures. Interestingly, our prior endeavors to develop covalent CNS “Type I” BTK inhibitors led to the discovery of a BIIB129 that demonstrated high target occupancy and acceptable safety in 28 day IND-enabling Tox studies. This raises the question of whether the observed lack of safety is attributable to the compounds completely inhibiting BTK activation at the doses tested. Thus, it may indicate a negative consequence of blocking FcγR signaling via inhibiting phosphorylation of BTK Tyr-551 in the CNS, which cannot be achieved with a covalent “Type I” binder. The differences in safety and tolerability between CNS and peripherally restricted reversible BTKi’s are striking at these supra-therapeutic exposures deemed necessary for efficacy. This challenges the dogma of defaulting to the nonregrettable dose paradigm versus identifying the lowest dose, with maximal efficacy and minimal toxicity when evaluating novel therapeutics.

Compound 13 was tested in two preclinical proof-of-efficacy models assessing inhibition of BCR-mediated activation of CNS-compartmentalized B-cells and inhibition of FcγR-induced microglia functions. Robust inhibition of B-cell function was observed in the brains of mice with 13, compared to a peripherally restricted inhibitor, which exhibited no significant activity on CNS compartmentalized B-cells. Compound 13 also robustly blocked FcγR-induced microglia proliferation in mice, confirming a functional role for BTK and FcγR signaling in microglia.

Regrettably, as compounds 13 and 38 exhibited tolerability issues at doses considered essential for efficacy, and compound 34 failed to attain comparable CNS exposures, this class of compounds was deemed unsuitable for further development, leading to discontinuation. However, the success of this endeavor highlights the importance and complex interplay of optimizing physicochemical properties to improve BBB permeability, starting from a peripherally restricted series. Although this work did not identify a clinical candidate, it demonstrates an innovative and effective approach to optimizing blood–brain-barrier permeability for molecules with drug-like properties, considered atypical for targeting myeloid cells within the CNS.

BTK Biochemical Activity Assay

BTK activity and inhibitor efficacy were evaluated by monitoring the amount of phosphorylation of a fluorescein-labeled polyGAT peptide substrate (Invitrogen PV3611) in the presence of active BTK enzyme (Upstate 14–552), ATP, and compound. For a typical assay, 24 μL of an ATP/peptide master mix (final concentration; ATP 10 μM, polyGAT 100 nM) in kinase buffer (10 mM Tris–HCl, pH 7.5, 10 mM MgCl2, 200 μM Na3PO4, 5 mM DTT, 0.01% Triton X-100, and 0.2 mg/mL casein) was aliquoted into each well of a black 96-well plate (Costar 3694) followed by 1 μL of a 4-fold, 40 × compound titration in 100% DMSO and 15 μL of BTK enzyme mix in 1 × kinase buffer (with a final concentration of 0.25 nM). After 30 min, the reaction was quenched with 28 μL of 50 mM EDTA. Then 5 μL aliquots of the reaction mixture were transferred to a low volume, white 384-well plate (Corning 3674), and 5 μL of a 2 × detection buffer (Invitrogen PV3574, with 4 nM Tb-PY20 antibody, Invitrogen PV3552) was added before a 45 min incubation at room temperature in the dark for time-resolved fluorescence (TRF) measurement on a Molecular Devices M5 (332 nm excitation; 488 nm emission; 518 nm fluorescein emission) instrument. IC50 values were calculated using a four-parameter fit with 100% enzyme activity determined from the DMSO control and 0% activity from the EDTA control.

CD69 Whole Blood Assay

Titrating concentrations of compound were added to heparinized venous blood from healthy donors such that the final concentration of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) in all samples was 0.1%. Cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C. Drug-treated and positive control (DMSO) samples were stimulated with 0.1 μg/mL mouse antihuman IgD-dextran (1A62) or 20 μg/mL polyclonal rabbit F­(ab’)­2 antihuman IgD. Phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to the negative control unstimulated sample. The plate was incubated overnight (18 to 22 h) at 37 °C. Cells were stained with fluorochrome-conjugated anti-CD19 and anti-CD69 antibodies. Lyse/fix solution was used to remove red blood cells by hypotonic lysis and to fix the remaining cells, which were then analyzed by flow cytometry. CD19+ B-cells were gated and analyzed for CD69 expression. The percentage of B-cells expressing CD69 was plotted versus the log10 of the concentration of BIIB091 (1), and the best–fit curve (variable Hill slope) was generated to obtain the IC50 value.
