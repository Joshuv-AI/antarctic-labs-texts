Harnessing high-throughput phenotyping and artificial intelligence for soybean breeding: From trait assessment to data-driven decisions
Authors: José TB Chagas, Maurício S Araújo, João PS Pavan, André O Françani, Gérson NC Ferreira, André A Stella, Marcela Martinez, Yasmim I Retore, Bruno F Fregonezi, Gyanlucca M Martins, Tong Zhao, Jianye Zhou, Dayana R Sousa, Yuefang Gao, Donghong Ji, Kaizhou Gao, Bo Li, Shouyang Liu, Liang Zhao, José B Pinheiro

ABSTRACT
AbstractSoybean (Glycine max (L.) Merrill) is a highly important crop widely used for food, edible oil, animal feed, and microbial fermentation products. Traditional phenotypic measurement methods are often time-consuming, labor-intensive, destructive to plants, and prone to human error. High-Throughput Phenotyping (HTP) enables precise assessment of multiple soybean phenotypic features, including morphology, physiology, diseases, pests, and agronomic traits. Artificial Intelligence (AI) is a research field dedicated to developing algorithms for multiple tasks. This review highlights the application of HTP and AI in soybean breeding programs. We discuss the challenges of implementing HTP in soybean breeding and focus on the potential and limitations of Deep Learning (DL) to support soybean breeding goals. We demonstrate the application of HTP to key soybean traits, several HTP platforms, as well as DL applications across different datasets and strategies for developing large foundation models. While integrating AI into soybean breeding programs remains a challenge, leveraging HTP data and Large Language Models (LLMs) could reshape soybean breeding.Keywords: Phenomics, Glycine max (L.) Merrill, Machine learning, Deep learning, Computer vision, Remote sensing

Introduction

Soybean (Glycine max (L.) Merrill) is one of the most important agricultural crops worldwide, playing a central role in grain production and global food security [1]. The pursuit of high yield potential and desirable agronomic and nutritional traits remains a primary medium- and long-term goal in crop breeding. However, this process faces significant challenges due to the sensitivity of the crop to environmental factors [2,3], which indicates the complex interaction between biotic and abiotic components. This complexity is represented by the genotype × environment × management (G×E×M) interactions [[4], [5], [6]]. Across different stages of plant breeding, these combined effects result in a ”phenotypic bottleneck”, characterized by the difficulty of accurately and reliably quantifying phenotypic variation in large breeding populations. Traditionally, phenotypic selection, an essential step in the breeding process, has relied on visual assessments and manual measurements conducted across multiple field trials, a procedure that requires substantial time, labor, and financial resources, thereby constraining genetic gain when compared to modern High-Throughput Phenotyping (HTP) approaches [7].

Climate change has intensified the occurrence of emerging diseases and extreme weather events, imposing new challenges on plant breeding programs [8,9]. In this context, there is a growing demand for soybean germplasm that is better adapted to environmental stresses and capable of sustaining productivity under adverse conditions. To address this demand, multiple strategies have been incorporated into soybean breeding pipelines, with a particular emphasis on accelerating selection cycles through the integration of genomic selection and predictive modeling approaches [10]. Both approaches leverage statistical and machine learning models that facilitate more accurate prediction of genetic values of individuals. Although genomic prediction depends on molecular information, phenotypic selection remains extensively employed, particularly when integrated with HTP technologies. These platforms facilitate direct, frequent, and non-destructive measurements of agronomic traits, thereby substantially increasing the volume, accuracy, and temporal resolution of phenotypic data while reducing operational costs and enhancing genetic gain [7]. HTP has emerged as an integrated solution, allowing rapid and objective analysis of plant physiological and morphological traits. By supporting large-scale data collection, HTP facilitates early identification of key traits for selection in breeding programs [11].

Decision-making in plant breeding is becoming data-driven [12]. With the advent of artificial intelligence (AI) at Breeding 4.0, it has become possible to explain phenotypic variation through integration of digital agriculture, big data, machine learning, robotics, and remote sensing [13]. The integration of AI and HTP tools enables the creation of 3D maps that analyze vegetal spectral reflectance and estimate phosphorous and nitrogen content [14,15]. There is also multiple traits that can be estimated from different sensors (hyperspectral) and platforms (satellites) [14,16]. For soybean breeding, integrating these layers of information has become essential to manage the complexity of data generated by modern platforms [10]. Genomics, driven by next-generation sequencing technologies, produces millions of molecular markers distributed across the entire genome. Phenomics collects high-resolution data obtained from imaging sensors, capable of capturing subtle variations in plant development [7,17]. Meanwhile, metabolomics and enviromics expand understanding of physiological mechanisms and genotype-by-environment (G×E) interaction, adding new dimensions to the selection process.

This review provides an overview of HTP integration across multiple domains of soybean breeding goals. We explore machine and deep learning (ML and DL) methods, HTP applications, and hardware evolution to deploy AI models from a soybean breeding program perspective.

Phenomics in soybean breeding

High Throughput Plant Phenotyping measures traits at several levels, from large populations to few Recombinant Inbred Lines (RIL), accessing morphological and physiological attributes such as height, leaf area, biomass, root architecture, flowering time, and pod number. Architectural traits such as number of branches per plant, canopy height, leaf angle, and leaf area index are key for breeding high-yielding and adaptive cultivars (Fig. 1). Traditional manual measurements are labor-intensive, time-consuming, subjective, and can be destructive, limiting scalability and adoption at early stages [[18], [19], [20]]. In this sense, several platforms can be used to get phenotypic information from satellites to ground based HTP systems [21,22]. In soybean breeding, major work is carried out using UAV, with only minor work being done using satellites and other platforms [23].

Key traits in soybean breeding

HTP has been extensively applied to estimate key traits such as plant height, biomass, and yield [24]. In soybean, yield is determined by the interaction between morphological and physiological components, including pod number, seed size, plant architecture, days to reach critical phenology stages (flowering and maturity), photosynthetic efficiency, reproductive performance, and nitrogen fixation efficiency, making phenotypic characterization fundamental for improving yield potential and stability across environments [25]. Phenomics-assisted breeding allows integration of unused genetic diversity into cultivar development and can enhance genetic gain by increasing selection intensity. The estimation of vegetation indices from UAV can be correlated with important soybean traits like resistance to pest [26]. On Fig. 2 is established the pipeline to extract different vegetation indices from spectral reflectance and correlation with soybean key traits. Establishing relationships between phenomic traits and yield using HTP and advanced analytics, including machine learning and in-season yield prediction, is key for genotype ranking and trait selection [27].

Common secondary traits include physiological indicators (chlorophyll, nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium deficiency in leaves) and vegetation indices, which help interpret genotype × environment interactions [28]. Integrating highly heritable and genetically correlated secondary traits into multi-trait models can enhance indirect selection of low-heritability traits such as yield or biomass [28,29].

Plant height

Plant height (PH) is an important structural trait closely related to soybean growth habit, yield, and aboveground biomass (AGB) estimation [30,31]. However, obtaining crop height rapidly over large areas, especially for multi-temporal assessments, remains challenging [32]. In traditional plant breeding programs, canopy height is often measured only once before harvest, limiting its usefulness.

In soybean, PH influences light competition, biomass, and overall yield [25,33]. Taller plants are more susceptible to lodging, yet increased height is often accompanied by more internodes, positively correlated with pod number [34]. The ideal PH for improved soybean cultivars is approximately 1 m to reduce lodging and harvest losses during mechanization [24]. Soybean growth habit further affects PH. Cultivars with indeterminate habit continue vegetative growth and node formation until the beginning of seed fill (R5), typically culminating in taller plants with higher yield but also increased lodging risk, while determinate cultivars stop vegetative growth before flowering and tend to be shorter [10].

UAV-derived PH estimates based on subtracting the digital elevation model (DEM) from the digital surface model (DSM) demonstrate strong correlations with ground measurements (R2 = 0.72–0.84; RMSE = 3.35–6.13 cm), particularly during the R1–R6 stages, confirming the accuracy and reliability of this low-cost approach [31]. RGB-based phenotyping is widely used due to its user-friendly and cost-effective nature [25], but can overestimate PH due to limited canopy penetration [35]. LiDAR provides more accurate vertical measurements because its laser pulses penetrate dense canopies and differentiate vegetation from ground points, making it effective across forests, shrubs, and crop canopies [36,37]. UAV-based LiDAR outperforms RGB imagery for mid-season soybean PH estimation, particularly from pod development to seed filling (R2 = 0.83 vs. 0.53), although RGB cameras are more reliable at physiological maturity when LiDAR underperforms [10,25].

Plant lodging

Lodging is a major factor contributing to yield reduction in crops [38], and selection of lodging-tolerant cultivars is crucial for developing high-yielding soybean cultivars. In soybean, lodging can result from environmental factors such as fertile or moist soils, high planting density, stem borer infestation [39], and adverse meteorological conditions. Conventional breeding programs rely on visual scoring (1–5 scale) to assess lodging resistance, a method that is subjective, labor-intensive, and time-consuming [40,41]. Increasing the number of branches per plant boosts pod formation and yield, while lodging resistance is crucial, with the fifth internode identified as a key structural point influencing plant stability under dense planting conditions [42,43].

UAV imagery combined with ML algorithms has proven effective in classifying lodging severity and predicting yield-related traits such as grain number and grain weight per plant [41,44]. High classification accuracy (96%) was achieved using an RGB-based sensor and an artificial neural network (ANN) model with SMOTE-ENN sampling [41]. Lodging discrimination was also proposed combining RGB, multispectral, and a multimodal DL framework using a self-supervised contrastive learning strategy with point cloud data collected by a lightweight UAV, achieving F1-scores up to 0.64 under low-density planting conditions [45]. These studies show that UAV imaging combined with DL effectively quantifies soybean lodging, links it to key traits, and improves precision breeding. The fifth internode, plant height, lodging degree, and vegetation indices (VIs) are critical for accurate yield component estimation across planting conditions [[41], [42], [43], [44], [45], [46]].

Biomass estimation

Pioneering studies revealed that canopy cover is closely related to aboveground biomass (AGB), offering potential for early yield prediction [47]. Early studies in soybean identified strong genetic and phenotypic relationships between canopy traits and yield. Imagery from ground and aerial platforms across a diverse panel of soybean lines identified a major QTL on chromosome 19, increasing yield by 47.3 ha−1. Moreover, average canopy coverage, a highly heritable trait (H2 = 0.77) [48], showed a very high correlation with grain yield (r = 0.87) [48], suggesting its potential as an indirect marker of biomass and yield performance [48].

Field-based hyperspectral reflectance (350–2500 nm) measurements were also used to assess multiple traits, such as leaf area index, biomass, yield, and seed composition. While leaf area index showed the strongest association with reflectance, biomass prediction was less accurate, reinforcing the need to merge spectral and structural features for more powerful estimations [49]. UAV-derived RGB and multispectral data were combined with genomic information to predict AGB longitudinally using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) and partial least squares regression (PLSR) models. This approach revealed that AGB heritability fluctuates over time and is controlled by multiple small-effect loci and their interactions, emphasizing the importance of temporal and genetic modeling in biomass monitoring [50].

UAV-derived canopy height and area have also been incorporated into genomic prediction models. By modeling growth curves of canopy height and area as secondary traits, improved prediction accuracies for soybean biomass were achieved using multitrait genomic selection [51]. Most recently, fusion of UAV-derived spectral, textural, and structural features, including canopy height models (CHM), significantly enhanced AGB estimation (R2 = 0.85) [31], outperforming models using only dual features of VI, texture, and structure indices combinations (R2 = 0.79–0.81) [31]. Ensemble learning algorithms achieved robust biomass prediction across growth stages and genotypes. The study revealed that integrating complementary canopy traits provides higher accuracy than any single data source, emphasizing the synergy between canopy structure and reflectance for soybean biomass quantification [31].

Relative maturity group

Accurate identification of crop maturity is a key component of soybean breeding programs, as it supports informed selection and advancement decisions [52,53]. In soybean, maturity assessment is particularly important due to its broad geographic adaptability from tropical to temperate environments and its intense sensitivity to photoperiod [54]. Soybean is classified into maturity groups (MGs) corresponding to latitudinal zones [55]. These MGs and their finer subdivisions into relative maturity classes help breeders position genotypes for optimal performance across environments [56].

Timely determination of days to maturity is essential for breeding and management, as early-maturing genotypes reduce disease exposure (e.g., Asian soybean rust) and allow double cropping or cover crop establishment [24,56]. Conversely, excessively late cultivars may suffer frost or harvest losses [57]. The progression of canopy color during soybean maturation, usually lasting around three weeks, varies with genotype and environmental factors [58]. Physiological maturity (R8 stage) is typically determined when 95% of pods display their brown or gray color at sink-driven senescence [59]. As senescence advances, chlorophyll degradation leads to visible changes in canopy color from green to yellow and brown, altering spectral reflectance [52]. Since pod color and canopy senescence are strongly correlated, spectral reflectance features can serve as indirect indicators of physiological maturity [56].

These spectral changes can be captured through VIs sensitive to chlorophyll, nitrogen, and water content, enabling more accurate maturity estimation [56]. Recent studies have integrated UAVs equipped with RGB and multispectral sensors with ML approaches to predict maturity by analyzing temporal changes in canopy reflectance and color patterns [53,60,61]. UAV-based imaging with regression models has proven particularly effective for estimating maturity as a continuous trait rather than by categorical classification [56]. Thus, UAV-based imaging has become a powerful tool to monitor color dynamics and predict maturity in soybean breeding programs [58].

Secondary traits

Current progress highlights HTP application for monitoring soybean fertility and nutrient dynamics. Multispectral and hyperspectral data integrated with ML enable spatial assessment of macronutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, attaining highest accuracy around the R5 stage [62,63]. NDVI has also proven useful for tracking biomass and nutrient uptake across management systems, showing strong correlations with phosphorus and potassium absorption in initial growth stages [64].

Additionally, remote sensing assists early detection and mapping of iron deficiency chlorosis through convolutional neural network (CNN)-based models [65,66]. Beyond nutrient monitoring, satellite and UAV vegetation indices such as EVI2 have successfully captured yield gains associated with Bacillus-based microbial inoculants that enhance drought resilience, emphasizing the role of remote sensing in sustainable fertility management [67]. These approaches can help breeders assess genotype performance and select superior lines related to nutrient efficiency.

Phenomics applied to soybean breeding goals

Seed quality

Conventional seed-testing methods for quality, vigor, and germination have severe limitations, being labor-intensive, destructive, and susceptible to operational errors [68,69]. Additionally, manual acquisition of morphological measurements is slow and error-prone, making it unfeasible for large-scale collections [70]. Consequently, HTP combined with ML methods emerges as a non-destructive computer-vision alternative for seed analysis, reducing time, cost, and subjectivity compared to traditional routines [68].

Imaging sensors enable pixel-by-pixel mapping of chemical differences and features related to plant tissue (water/moisture, pigments, reserves) through specific reflectance bands, increasing sensitivity to physicochemical variations linked to quality and vigor in a non-destructive manner [69]. For specific purposes such as aging and stress evaluation, collection of volatile compounds using gas chromatography coupled to ion-mobility spectrometry may provide further information, aiding in batch classification complementary to RGB/hyperspectral imaging [71].

Current pipelines combine preprocessing (segmentation, normalization, feature engineering, or band selection) with ML algorithms. PCA, random forests, SVM, and multilayer perceptrons have been employed for variable selection and classification of seed vigor and quality, achieving high accuracy in distinguishing seed lots [71]. Beyond purely supervised models, incorporating interpretability criteria makes it possible to prioritize predictive descriptors (e.g., RGB color and saturation components) that remain stable across seasons and genotypes. For hyperspectral data, dimensionality reduction and selection routines mitigate multicollinearity and redundancy among spectral bands prior to model training and classification [71].

Applied to soybean, these tools separate individual seeds even under overlap and variable lighting and allow accurate estimation of seed length, width, and area [70]. At the population level [72], evaluated 164 genotypes, extracted eight morphological traits from RGB images acquired with a flatbed scanner, and compared seven ML models. Random forests and multiple linear regression showed the best performance for seed weight prediction (R2 up to 0.98 in training and 0.92–0.94 in testing), while size-related traits outperformed shape-related traits for predicting hundred-seed weight. Using the same morphological descriptors, hundred-seed weight was predicted with a capacity of 0.71 [73].

Physiology

Physiological characteristics are direct measurements of plant organ functions, including photosynthesis, respiration, nutrient assimilation, hormonal activity, stress resistance, and water relations. Leaf water potential is associated with plant hydration status, while stomatal conductance reflects leaf and canopy gas exchange. Stomatal aperture responds quickly to soil water status, controlling photosynthetic activity and growth [74].

An application of these physiological estimates is that the amount of green pixels and canopy green index were compared between plants inoculated or not with Bradyrhizobium strains. Inoculated plants, more tolerant to water stress, showed higher canopy green index values, explained by better chlorophyll status measured non-destructively with a SPAD meter [75]. Thermal imaging has proven highly effective for identifying physiological responses related to tolerance to environmental stresses such as drought and heat. Lower canopy temperatures indicate plants with greater efficiency in controlling water stress [75]. Leaf-air temperature differences from thermal images showed negative correlation with stomatal conductance and transpiration rate, with soybean plants showing greater thermal differences demonstrating greater drought resistance [76].

Thermography has been associated with photosynthetic efficiency through quantum efficiency of Photosystem II, estimated by chlorophyll fluorescence [75]. Fluorescence measurements obtained with a fluorometer were positively correlated with concentrations of nitrogen, phosphorus, iron, and molybdenum in leaves. Plants under nutritional deficiency showed lower photosynthetic rates, indicating potential for indirect, non-destructive nutritional status estimation [77]. Another study demonstrated applicability of chlorophyll a fluorescence for distinguishing soybean maturity groups [78]. Spectral and hyperspectral bands can be used for physiological phenotyping in soybeans. Infrared shows strong correlation with leaf relative water content and gas exchange [79], useful for identifying soybean genotypes more tolerant to prolonged drought. Another study demonstrated estimation of rubisco-catalyzed carboxylation rate based on multispectral data, although additional validations are needed [80]. Metabolite measurements often require destructive sampling, reinforcing the importance of non-destructive alternatives. One such alternative is correlation between leaf spectroscopy and biochemical composition. By modeling spectral data obtained with portable field spectroradiometers, it is possible to infer stomatal conductance (sc), abscisic acid (ABA), and jasmonic acid with good accuracy. ABA levels estimated by spectroscopy allowed early detection of water stress up to two days earlier than methods based on sc [81].

Pests resistance

Several insects are pests within soybean crops. Species from the Noctuidae family of Lepidoptera order stand out, including Chrysodeixis includens, Anticarsia gemmatalis, and Spodoptera species (S. frugiperda and S. eridania). These species predominantly cause leaf area reduction and, less frequently, damage to reproductive structures [82]. In addition to defoliating insects, sucking insects such as Aphis glycines and whitefly Bemisia tabaci affect plant development through sap extraction and pathogen transmission [83,84]. The ”soybean stink bug complex” (Pentatomidae family) is considered one of the main limitations to soybean yield stability, causing direct damage to grains and reproductive structures [[82], [83], [84], [85]].

Among stink bug complex species, Euschistus heros (Fabricius) stands out as the most frequently reported species across different cultivation areas [85]. Breeding for resistance to the stink bug complex is limited by correct identification of species infestation in initial crop stages. HTP approaches based on RGB images have been progressively incorporated into plant breeding pipelines [[86], [87], [88]], enabling extraction of spectral bands and derived indices associated with morphophysiological changes in plants, allowing modeling of quantitative relationships between spectral signals and stink bug complex incidence [89].

Recent advances in HTP combined with AI have expanded potential of remote sensing for detecting and quantifying biotic stresses [90]. used spectral data from a handheld spectroradiometer combined with SVM algorithms to classify soybean plots under different aphid infestation levels. Comparable findings were documented by Ref. [91] using multispectral UAV images. UAV-based approaches have also assessed damage caused by defoliating insects [92]. In the soybean stink bug complex context [93], evaluated damage caused by stink bugs and caterpillars using hyperspectral remote sensing under controlled conditions, finding that caterpillar infestation caused significant changes in reflectance patterns while stink bug damage did not. These studies reinforce the feasibility of using HTP with ML methods for indirect identification of biotic stresses, especially when integrated with time series, multiple spectral bands, and complementary plant phenology and architecture information.

Disease resistance

Although about 200 pathogens infect soybeans, only about 35 species are economically significant [94]. In tropical regions, large losses have been caused by stem canker, brown spot, Cercospora Leaf Blight/Purple Seed Stain, charcoal rot, seedling diseases, and Sclerotinia stem rot [94]. Since its introduction, Asian soybean rust has become the most significant threat to major producing countries such as Brazil, reducing grain yield while increasing chemical costs. Due to numerous diseases affecting the crop at various stages, evaluating symptoms and desirable agronomic traits becomes exhaustive, slow, and impractical on a large scale [95].

HTP tools can be useful for assessing disease severity, incidence, and monitoring epidemiological development, generating comprehensive field datasets and increasing genetic gain for disease resistance [96]. employed scanners and image processing to construct a standard area diagram for identification of Asian rust disease severity. Hyperspectral reflectance for Asian rust severity levels showed that more severe disease corresponds to higher reflectance [97]. Combining imaging sensors and ML algorithms can select superior lines and improve resistance through accurate assessment of disease incidence and severity [98].

Nematodes resistance

Over the past few decades, Heterodera glycines (Soybean Cyst Nematode, SCN) has been recognized as the major pest causing soybean yield reduction. In 2024 alone, it led to a yield loss of 108,008 thousand bushels in the northern United States [99]. Heterodera glycines is a soil-borne endoparasitic nematode that invades plant roots to establish feeding sites and forms cysts, impairing normal growth and development [100]. A syncytium provides nutrients for nematode development [101]. When soybeans are infected, above-ground symptoms include stunted growth, yellowing of cotyledons, shortened internodes, delayed flowering, or failure to form pods. Below ground, root growth is affected with retarded taproot and lateral root development, increased fibrous roots, and yellowish-white cysts on roots [99]. Integrating ML and HTP enables more accurate assessment from model establishment [102]. Sensors capture above-ground abnormalities of SCN-infested soybeans, including morphological indicators (plant dwarfing, leaf yellowing, reduced podding, and ”patchy” diseased plant areas) and spectral indicators (chlorophyll and moisture content), thereby monitoring Heterodera glycines [103].

Artificial intelligence integration in soybean breeding

Artificial intelligence in agriculture

Artificial intelligence (AI) encompasses algorithms capable of interpreting the world, making automated decisions, and assisting humans in complex tasks across domains such as speech recognition [108], fraud detection [110], video surveillance [111], and others [112]. In addition, AI has become an important tool for processing complex agricultural data and supporting more precise, scalable, and data-driven decisions. Its progress in precision agriculture came together with the advance of deep learning (DL) techniques, which enabled models to learn relevant patterns from large datasets instead of relying exclusively on handcrafted features. In computer vision, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and Vision Transformers [113] have advanced image classification, object detection, and semantic segmentation tasks. In the natural language processing (NLP) field, Transformer models [114] enabled the development of large language models (LLMs), which can generate, summarize, and organize textual information. These advances contributed to the emergence of foundation models (FMs) [115], which are large-scale models pre-trained on broad datasets and later adapted to downstream tasks through fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), or multimodal fusion [116].

In agriculture, DL has been applied to many tasks, namely crop classification, disease and pest detection, weed recognition, crop yield prediction, irrigation management, phenotyping, and remote sensing [117,118]. These applications are favored by the diversity of agricultural data, including RGB images, multispectral and hyperspectral imagery, thermal sensors, UAV and satellite data collection, weather records, soil sensors, management information, genomic markers, and high-throughput phenotyping traits. Therefore, agriculture is naturally suited to multimodal AI approaches, in which different data sources are combined to better describe crop performance [119].

However, LLMs and FMs may not be directly reliable for specialized agricultural use. Since these models are trained on broad datasets, they may provide generic answers, fail to capture crop-specific symptoms, or even ignore local environmental and management conditions [120]. For this reason, agricultural applications often require domain adaptation, fine-tuning, or RAG techniques. Examples include AgriBERT [121] for agricultural language representation, AgroGPT [120] for vision-language interaction in expert agricultural conversations, AgriCLIP [122] vision-language model for agriculture and livestock, and ShizishanGPT [123] for agricultural question answering.

AI-driven decision-making

In the decision-making context, an AI model becomes useful when it helps, for example, defining where to scout, whether to apply control measures, or which genotypes show resistance. Furthermore, a yield prediction model becomes decision-oriented when it supports harvest planning, resource allocation, food-security assessment, or selection of superior lines. This means that the value of AI in agriculture increases when the model outputs are translated into practical decision to improve crop yield and reduce losses.

In soybean breeding, AI can support decision-making by connecting phenotypic data acquisition, model training and analysis, and selection decisions. The large volume of field data from UAVs, ground sensors, environmental measurements, and image systems can be processed by an AI model to generate trait estimates, genotype rankings, crop yield maps, disease or pest resistance, maturity, and stress tolerance. These processed outputs can help breeders decide which lines should be advanced, discarded, crossed, or evaluated in other environments.

Several applications illustrate this role. Computer vision models have been used for plant disease detection and diagnosis, supporting earlier disease identification and management decisions [124]. Remote sensing and DL models have been applied to crop yield prediction, including soybean yield estimation from satellite-derived information and learned representations [125]. Furthermore, AlphaEarth Foundations have been evaluated for soybean and corn yield estimation, showing the potential of reusable Earth-observation embeddings for agricultural monitoring and planning [126].

LLMs and multimodal FMs can complement AI models by improving how information is accessed, interpreted, and communicated to the users. LLMs can retrieve literature, summarize field reports, organize breeding records, and explain model outputs [127]. RAG-based systems can base those responses in local reliable documents, management guidelines, or experimental data, reducing the risk of generic or wrong recommendations [123]. Computer vision FMs can support large-scale trait extraction from images, while multimodal FMs can combine images, environmental data, genomic information, and text to support more integrated and reliable decisions [122,128]. Therefore, multimodal FMs and LLMs are essential tools for supporting farmers in data-driven decision, following the evolution of deep learning techniques and the large volume of multimodal data in agriculture applications.

Future directions

AI application faces several challenges for soybean breeding goals. First, storage of field data from multispectral and hyperspectral sensors becomes larger when combined with RGB data, with hundreds of gigabytes requiring processing and analysis. Second, hyperparameter tuning for ML and DL model interpretability could benefit from XAI. Third, hardware requirements for AI model deployment make this methodology more restrictive and expensive for breeding programs with limited resources. Phenomics can assign superior soybean inbred lines, enabling faster decision-making during breeding pipelines. Identifying features strictly related to soybean breeding goals (grain yield components, disease and pest resistance, relative maturity group) can harness genetic potential of soybean genotypes. Early use of cameras to predict yield and forecast epidemiological diseases and pests embraces smart technology use in agriculture.

Given massive and complex information, AI acts as a bridge between different omics layers (genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics, enviromics), accelerating predictive selection and promoting a new era of data-driven breeding in soybean [140,141]. However, there is a lack of information regarding to understood the behavior of multiples of genotypes in discriminant environments and under diverse selection intensity. This G x E effect needs to be deeply understand in HTP derived traits from beginning until the end of cultivar development at breeding programs. In this sense, the integration of HTP, Large Language Models (LLMs), and Large Computer Vision Foundation models may reshape soybean breeding accounting for this challenge. This HTP techniques assist breeders in accessing phenotypic traits, enhancing selection intensity. AI models leverage large field datasets to identify patterns, classify, and select superior soybean lines according to breeder-defined idiotypes. This combination can harness breeding program efficiency, allowing evaluation of thousands of genotypes and improving genetic gain based on data-driven decisions.
