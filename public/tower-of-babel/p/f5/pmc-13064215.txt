Transcriptomic and functional comparison of adipose-and bone marrow-derived mesenchymal stem cells for tendon regeneration
Authors: Hadeer Khaled, Mohammed Zayed, Bumseok Kim, Byung-Hoon Jeong, Sang-Ik Oh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTendons are specialized connective tissues with limited intrinsic healing properties due to hypovascularity and low metabolic activity. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) possess regenerative potential for tendon injuries. Among the diverse sources of MSCs, those derived from the bone marrow (BM-MSCs) and adipose tissue (AD-MSCs) are the leading candidates. However, their relative efficacy remains underexplored, particularly in terms of biological characteristics and tenogenic differentiation. In addition, the signaling pathways driving tenogenic differentiation processes remain poorly understood. This study aimed to comprehensively investigate the regenerative potential of AD-MSCs and BM-MSCs for tendon repair.MethodsAD- and BM-MSCs were isolated from rats and evaluated for stemness based on morphology, viability, immunophenotyping, and tri-lineage differentiation. RNA sequencing was performed to obtain a baseline molecular profile of each MSC type prior to tenogenic differentiation. We evaluated the potential of AD- and BM-MSCs to differentiate into tendon-like cells in vitro using gene expression and immunofluorescence. A rat model of Achilles tendon injury was created to evaluate the regenerative efficacy following each MSC-type injection. After 6 weeks, tendon regeneration was assessed using histological and immunohistochemical analyses.ResultsBoth MSCs displayed similarities in morphology, immunophenotyping, and tri-lineage differentiation capacity. AD-MSCs exhibited a superior proliferation rate and tenogenic differentiation compared with BM-MSCs in vitro. Transcriptomic profiling revealed that AD-MSCs significantly upregulated genes related to extracellular matrix remodeling (COL1, TN-C), immunoregulation (IL-33), and tenogenic signaling (FGF18, TGF-β1). AD-MSCs were also enriched in the PI3K-Akt signaling, MAPK signaling, and focal adhesion pathways, indicating enhanced regenerative and tenogenic potential compared to BM-MSCs. Moreover, our data show that AD-MSCs were more effective than BM-MSCs in improving collagen organization and COL1 expression in an animal model of tendon injury.ConclusionOur findings not only highlight the superior regenerative potential of AD-MSCs for tendon repair but also provide a basis for future mechanistic and therapeutic advancements in regenerative medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13287-026-04961-2.Keywords: Tendinopathy, Mesenchymal stem cell, Adipose tissue, Bone marrow, Achilles tendon, RNA-Seq, Tendon regeneration

Background

Tendinopathy is a common disorder of the musculoskeletal system, especially among athletes and older individuals [1]. This condition is characterized by inflammation and degenerative changes in the tendon, which can ultimately result in its tearing [2]. Tendons are specialized connective structures that connect muscles to bones, transmit mechanical forces, and stabilize joints [3]. Although the tendon tissue is composed of approximately 20% cellular content (mainly tendon fibroblasts) and 80% extracellular matrix (ECM; primarily type I collagen along with elastin, proteoglycans, and water), its intrinsic repair capacity is limited owing to its hypovascularity and low metabolic activity [3]. Aging, mechanical overuse, endocrine changes, and acute trauma can disrupt the collagen architecture and ECM organization, causing inflammation and functional deficits in the tendons [4]. Traditional standard treatments, such as surgical repair and physical therapy, often result in biomechanically inferior scar tissue and a high risk of re-rupture [5]. Therefore, stem cell-based therapies have emerged as a transformative approach to address the regenerative demands of tendon repair.

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are promising candidates for tissue regeneration owing to their multipotency, self-renewal, and ability to differentiate into osteogenic, chondrogenic, adipogenic, and tenogenic lineages [6–9]. In addition, MSCs secrete various growth factors, cytokines, and extracellular vesicles that enhance the reparative environment by promoting angiogenesis, recruiting endogenous cells, and attenuating excessive inflammation [10–13]. In tendon injury, MSCs improve tendon architecture and tensile strength​, suppress inflammation, enhance tenogenic differentiation, and promote injured tendon healing [14–17]. MSCs reside in almost all mesodermal tissues, including the bone marrow, adipose tissue, tendons, dental pulp, synovial fluid, and umbilical cord [10, 18, 19]. However, their differentiation potential and gene expression profiles vary considerably, depending on the tissue source [20]. These differences highlight the importance of selecting an MSC source with characteristics best suited for each therapeutic application [21]. Bone marrow-derived MSCs (BM-MSCs) and adipose tissue-derived MSCs (AD-MSCs) are the leading candidates for tendon repair [10]. BM-MSCs are well characterized and have strong osteogenic potential for tendon-to-bone healing; however, they pose a risk of ectopic ossification and require invasive harvesting techniques [10]. AD-MSCs offer a compelling alternative, as they can be easily harvested with minimal donor-site morbidity and are known to exhibit higher proliferation rates and colony-forming efficiency [10, 22]. Preliminary data have shown that both cell types hold potential for tendon regeneration; however, their relative efficacy remains underexplored, particularly regarding their biological characteristics and tenogenic differentiation.

Moreover, an insufficient understanding of the molecular mechanisms that regulate tenogenic differentiation is a significant challenge in advancing stem cell therapies for tendon repair. Although previous studies have demonstrated that MSCs can adopt a tenocyte-like phenotype, the signaling pathways that drive this process remain poorly elucidated [23, 24]. High-throughput RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) is a powerful tool to overcome these challenges by profiling gene expression, identifying functional markers, and elucidating lineage-specific differentiation pathways [23, 24]. Transcriptomic analysis of AD- and BM-MSCs revealed key differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and signaling pathways relevant to tendon regeneration.

To address these knowledge gaps, we performed a detailed investigation of the regenerative capabilities of AD- and BM-MSCs for tendon repair. We systematically analyzed their stemness, tenogenic differentiation, and baseline transcriptomic signatures. We further evaluated their therapeutic potential in an Achilles tendon injury model using Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats to translate these findings into a practical setting. The results of this study provide robust scientific evidence for selecting an optimal MSC source for stem cell-based interventions targeting tendon injuries in both humans and animals.

Methods

Animals and ethics approval

Seven-week-old specific pathogen-free outbred male rats (245 ± 16 g) were purchased from Taconic Farms, Inc. (Samtako Bio Korea, Republic of Korea) and used as MSC donors. The rats were housed under conventional conditions with water and food pellets provided ad libitum in a pathogen-free environment at Animal Biosafety Facility 2 (ABL2) at the Korea Research Institute for Veterinary Biologics (KRIVB; Iksan, Republic of Korea). All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Jeonbuk National University, Republic of Korea (approval number: NON2024-093). The experimental scheme is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Schematic overview of experimental design. Isolation and characterization of MSCs, RNA sequencing, tenogenic differentiation, and in vivo analysis

Isolation and expansion of rat MSCs

All experimental animals were euthanized via cervical dislocation. MSCs were isolated and cultured as previously described [25, 26]. In brief, AD-MSCs were derived from inguinal fat tissue. Tissues were harvested and rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) to eliminate debris and contaminating hematopoietic cells. Adipose tissue was minced and enzymatically digested in collagenase type I (Fujifilm Wako Chemical, Tokyo, Japan) for 45 min at 37 °C. The stromal cells were pelleted by centrifugation (2,000 rpm, 5 min at 4 °C). The final cell pellet was resuspended in filtered Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% antimycotic-antibiotic and cultured at 37 °C with 5% CO₂. To isolate BM-MSCs, femurs were aseptically dissected and briefly sterilized in 70% isopropanol/ethanol. Bone marrow was extracted using an 18G syringe through a 70 μm cell strainer and then centrifuged (2,000 rpm, 5 min at 4 °C). The cell pellets were cultured under the same conditions as AD-MSCs. After 48 h, the medium was replaced, and non-adherent cells were removed. Subsequently, the adherent cells were cultured to 80–90% confluence and detached using 0.25% trypsin-EDTA. All MSCs were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination using the PCR-based Smart Gene Mycoplasma Detection Kit (Cat. SG-PCR-MYC) following the manufacturer’s protocol (Fig. S1). All MSCs isolated in this experiment were maintained in identical medium and conditions. The cells were expanded, cryopreserved in CellBanker 1 (Zenogen Pharma Co., Tokyo, Japan), and stored in liquid nitrogen for future use.

Flow cytometry

For flow cytometry, surface antigens of AD- and BM-MSCs were analyzed using antibodies against CD90, CD73, CD105, CD45, and CD34, as previously described [26]. Anti-Rat CD90/Mouse CD90.1 (FITC, Cat. #: 561973; BD Pharmingen™), Purified Mouse Anti-Rat CD73 (Cat. #: 551123; BD Pharmingen™), Rabbit Anti-Rat CD105 (FITC, Cat. #: GTX60377; GeneTex), Mouse Anti-Rat CD45 (FITC Cat. #: 561867; BD Pharmingen™, Franklin Lakes, NJ, USA), Mouse Anti-Rat CD34 (FITC Cat. #: 560238; BD Pharmingen™) were used. Isotype-matched IgG1 served as a negative control. The raw fluorescence data were acquired using a NovoCyte® flow cytometer (NovoCyte 3000, ACEA Bioscience, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). Data was analyzed using the NovoExpress Software (Agilent Technologies, Inc., Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Colony-forming unit and proliferation assay

A colony-forming unit (CFU) assay was conducted to compare the in vitro self-renewal potential of AD- and BM-MSCs. MSCs were seeded at a density of 1,000 cells/cm² into 35 mm culture dishes and maintained for 10 d under standard culture conditions. Colonies were assessed using 1% crystal violet staining, and quantification was performed by cell counting using ImageJ software version 1.37 (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA).

Cell proliferation was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8; Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Briefly, MSCs were seeded in 96-well plates (200 µL/well) and allowed to adhere for 24 h before the assay. Subsequently, CCK-8 solution (10 µL) was added to each well under dark conditions, and the plates were incubated for 2 h in a 5% CO2 incubator at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the optical density (OD) value was correlated with the cell number using a standard curve generated on a SpectraMax Plus 384 spectrophotometer (Molecular Devices, LLC, San Jose, CA, USA).

Tri-lineage differentiation assay

MSC multipotency was confirmed by adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic differentiation under specific in vitro conditions as previously described [26]. For adipogenic differentiation, the cells were treated with an induction medium (DMEM supplemented with 1 µM dexamethasone, 10 µg/mL insulin, 100 µM indomethacin, and 50 µg/mL L-ascorbic acid). After complete differentiation, the cells were fixed and stained with Oil Red O to detect lipid droplets. For chondrogenic differentiation, the pelleted MSCs were incubated in a chondrogenic medium (DMEM + 100 nM dexamethasone, 25 µg/mL ascorbic acid, and 10 ng/mL TGF-β1). After differentiation, cartilage matrix production was visualized using Alcian Blue staining to detect glycosaminoglycans. For osteogenic differentiation, MSCs were cultured in an osteogenic medium (DMEM supplemented with 50 µM ascorbic acid, 10 mM β-glycerophosphate, and 0.5 µM dexamethasone), followed by staining with Alizarin Red S Solution for calcium deposits.

RNA sequencing (RNA-Seq) and bioinformatics analysis

To compare the baseline transcriptomic profiles of AD- and BM-MSCs, we performed high-throughput RNA-Seq on second-passage (P2) cells. Total RNA was extracted from AD- and BM-MSCs (n = 3 per group) using an RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). A minimum total RNA yield of 0.5 µg was obtained. The RNA quality (RIN > 7) was assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer. Libraries were prepared using the TruSeq Stranded Total RNA Library Prep Kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) and then sequenced on an Illumina platform (paired-end, 2 × 100 bp). Raw reads were quality trimmed (Phred score ≥ Q30) and aligned to the Rattus norvegicus reference genome (rn6) using HISAT2. Gene counts were obtained using StringTie software and normalized to transcripts per million (TPM). For exploratory visualization purposes (e.g., distribution plots and unsupervised clustering), we analyzed genes with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and uncorrected p-values (raw p-value) < 0.05 to provide an unbiased overview of transcriptional variations between AD- and BM-MSCs. For formal differential expression analysis, we used DESeq2 with Benjamini-Hochberg false discovery rate [FDR; adjusted p-value (padj)] correction. DEGs were defined strictly as genes with |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. Functional enrichment analyses were performed on the DEGs to elucidate the biological differences between the two MSCs. First, we conducted Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis using g: Profiler to identify overrepresented terms in three GO categories: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). p-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR < 0.05). Next, we mapped the DEGs to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/pathway.html) to identify the signaling cascades that were significantly enriched between the two groups. The enriched pathways were ranked based on their adjusted p‑values and gene ratios, which were defined as the proportion of DEGs within each pathway relative to the total number of genes in that pathway.

Validation for RNA-Seq results

Quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) was performed to assess the expression levels of the selected genes and to validate the RNA-Seq results. The extracted RNAs from two MSCs were reverse transcribed into complementary DNAs (cDNAs) using ReverTra Ace® qPCR RT Master Mix with gDNA Remover (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). The cDNAs were amplified using primers for the eight selected genes (Table S1).

Evaluation of in vitro tenogenic differentiation

Tenogenic differentiation was performed as described in a previous study [26, 27]. Briefly, MSCs were cultured in DMEM (ThermoFisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 until they reached 80–90% confluence. Following confluence, the cells were cultured in a complete medium containing 100 ng/mL CTGF (PeproTech®, Rocky Hill, NJ, USA), 50 µg/mL ascorbic acid (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA), and 50 ng/mL BMP-12 (BioLegend®, San Diego, CA, USA). The medium was changed every 3 d, and the cells were monitored for differentiation. After 8 d, qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence analyses were performed to assess tenogenic differentiation.

qRT-PCR of key tenogenic marker genes

Total RNAs were extracted from tenogenic-differentiated AD- and BM-MSCs using an RNA extraction kit (GeneAll Biotechnology, Seoul, Republic of Korea). RNA concentration and purity were analyzed using a NanoDrop-2000 (ThermoFisher Scientific). mRNA was reverse-transcribed to cDNA as described above. qRT-PCR analysis was performed using a CFX Opus 96™ Real-Time PCR System (Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc., Hercules, CA, USA) and 2× qPCR BIO SyGreen (PCR Biosystems, London, UK) for key tenogenic marker genes [scleraxis (SCX), collagen type I (COL1), collagen type III (COL3), tenomodulin (TNMD), tenascin-C (TN-C), SOX9, thrombospondin 4 (THBS-4), and Decorin (DCN)], with specific primers (Table 1).

Immunofluorescence of tenogenic-differentiated AD- and BM-MSCs

After tenogenic differentiation, the cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), followed by rinsing with PBS. The cells were then permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 in PBS and blocked with Superblock (ScyTek Laboratories, West Logan, UT, USA). Immunostaining was performed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-SCX A-7 (1:50; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, USA), anti-COL1 (1:200; Abcam, Cambridge, UK), and anti-COL3 (1:200; Abcam). Subsequently, the cells were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with the following fluorescence-conjugated secondary antibodies: Alexa Fluor 555-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG for Scx and Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG for COL1 and COL3 (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA, USA). Cell nuclei were counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Vectashield®, Vector Laboratories, Newark, CA, USA), and fluorescence images were acquired using a BX51 microscope (Olympus Corp., Tokyo, Japan).

Animal experiments

Twelve SD rats were randomly assigned to three groups (n = 4 per group): control (untreated), AD-MSCs-treated, and BM-MSCs-treated. All experimental rats were anesthetized by intramuscular injection of xylazine (10 mg/kg) and tiletamine/zolazepam (50 mg/kg). The rats were positioned in sternal recumbency, and the surgical site was shaved and disinfected. A small longitudinal skin incision (approximately 1 cm) was made on the right hind limb to expose the Achilles tendon. A 3 mm transverse defect was created at the midpoint of the tendon. Subsequently, naïve (undifferentiated) 1 × 106 MSCs in 50 µL PBS were injected into the defect site immediately after skin suture and closure. The control group received a 50 µL PBS injection without MSCs. Postoperative analgesia was provided using ketorolac (3 mg/kg, IM), and antibiotic prophylaxis was administered with ampicillin (50 mg/kg, IM) for 3 d consecutively. Animals were allowed free cage activity but were prevented from excessive climbing or jumping. The work has been reported in line with the ARRIVE guidelines 2.0.

Histopathological evaluation

Six weeks post-injection, all rats were euthanized in a CO2 euthanasia chamber by gradual introduction of CO2 for 10–15 min, consistent with human endpoints and AVMA guidelines. Achilles tendon tissue samples were collected from each group, fixed in 4% PFA for 48 h, processed routinely, and embedded in paraffin. Five-micrometre sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) to assess cell morphology. H&E-stained sections were semi-quantitatively scored according to a previous study [28]. Each of the six parameters (fiber structure, fiber arrangement, vascularity, nuclear rounding, inflammation, and cell density) was scored from 0 (intact) to 3 (severely abnormal), with a maximum total score of 18. Collagen structure and organization were evaluated using Masson’s trichrome and Picro-Sirius Red staining, following the manufacturer’s protocols.

For immunohistochemistry (IHC), the slides were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with proteinase K (120 mg, GeneAll Biotechnology) for 10 min at 37 °C as an antigen retrieval solution. They were then blocked with Super Block (ScyTek Laboratories). Subsequently, the slides were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies, including anti-SCX A-7 (Santa Cruz Biotechnology), anti-COL1, and anti-COL3 (Abcam) at a dilution of 1:200. Next, the slides were incubated at room temperature with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody (Vector Laboratories) for 1 h. Immune complexes were visualized using the Vectastain DAB Substrate Kit (Vector Laboratories), according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and the sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. A BX51 light microscope (Olympus Corp.) was used for histopathological evaluations. IHC staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ (FIJI) software.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using the GraphPad Prism version 8 (GraphPad Software, Inc., USA). Student’s t-test and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey’s post hoc test were used to evaluate group differences. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with statistical significance values denoted as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001.

Results

Morphology and surface marker expression of MSCs

After the isolation and culturing of primary AD- and BM-MSCs, the morphology of the MSCs was assessed using inverted phase-contrast microscopy. Both MSC populations exhibited relatively homogeneous morphology of spindle-shaped fibroblasts (Fig. 2A). AD-MSCs achieved a higher confluence than BM-MSCs. These observations suggest that MSCs may differ in their proliferative potential and growth patterns based on their tissue of origin. Flow cytometric analysis was performed according to the MSC criteria proposed by the International Society for Cellular Therapies [29] (Fig. 2B and C). AD- and BM-MSCs showed high expression of the MSC markers CD90 (94.1 and 98.4%, respectively), CD73 (99.19 and 93.24%, respectively), and CD105 (99.00 and 98.26%, respectively), whereas both cell types were negative for CD45 (1.9 and 1.7%, respectively) and CD34 (0.5 and 0.4%, respectively). These results confirm the successful isolation of pure MSC populations from each tissue source.

CFU and proliferation capacity of MSCs

Following crystal violet staining, stained colonies were counted and analyzed. The staining results revealed that AD-MSCs exhibited a significantly higher colony-forming capacity than BM-MSCs (p < 0.01) at identical seeding densities (Fig. 2D). A proliferation assay was performed to compare the proliferative potential of AD- and BM-MSCs. AD-MSCs consistently showed significantly higher proliferation rates than BM-MSCs at all experimental time points: days 1 (p < 0.01), 2 (p < 0.0001), and 4 (p < 0.001) (Fig. 2E). These findings reflect that AD-MSCs possess enhanced proliferative and colony-forming capabilities compared with BM-MSCs.

Tri-lineage differentiation assay

Both AD-MSCs and BM-MSCs successfully differentiated into adipogenic, osteogenic, and chondrogenic lineages (Fig. 2F). After the induction of adipogenic differentiation, both MSC types accumulated Oil Red O-positive lipid droplets, indicating their ability to differentiate into adipocytes. Both cell types produced a mineralized matrix after osteogenic induction, and the staining intensity was similar between the two MSC types; however, AD-MSCs appeared to form denser mineral nodules (Fig. 2F). In chondrogenic differentiation, pellet cultures of each MSC type were stained blue with Alcian Blue after induction, indicating the formation of a glycosaminoglycan-rich cartilage matrix (Fig. 2F). These findings confirmed the tri-lineage differentiation potential of both MSC types, supporting their applicability in tissue regeneration strategies.

RNA-Seq analysis revealed distinct transcriptomic profiles of AD- and BM-MSCs

RNA-Seq was performed on AD- and BM-MSCs to compare their baseline transcriptomic profiles (File S1). As an exploratory step, we visualized gene-level differences with |log2 FC| ≥ 1 and a raw p-value < 0.05 (Fig. 3A and B, Fig. S2A and S2B), illustrating the distinct gene expression distributions between AD- and BM-MSCs. This separation was mainly driven by AD-MSCs, which exhibited a more pronounced upregulation of numerous genes in AD-MSCs than in BM-MSCs, with a raw p-value < 0.05. All formal DEG identifications and downstream analyses used |log2FC| ≥ 1 and FDR < 0.05. Volcano plot analysis revealed the distribution of DEGs between AD- and BM-MSCs, and identified significantly upregulated genes in AD-MSCs, including IL-33, TN-C, BMP6, MMP12, and ITGA2 (Fig. 3C).

GO term and KEGG enrichment analyses revealed the biological processes and signaling pathways associated with the DEGs. GO term analysis of AD-MSCs (relative to BM-MSCs) revealed strong enrichment in GO: BP related to ECM organization, collagen fibril assembly, and positive regulation of cell migration (Fig. 3D). In GO: CC, the collagen-containing extracellular matrix was significantly enriched, while GO: MF showed enrichment in integrin, collagen, and growth factor binding. These findings support the role of AD-MSCs in ECM-driven tendon repair. In contrast, upregulated DEGs in BM-MSCs were enriched in processes related to skeletal development, cell cycle regulation, and various developmental processes that were not directly related to tenogenesis. In addition, the KEGG pathway analysis results further supported the functional divergence between MSC types (Fig. 3E). The upregulated DEGs in AD-MSCs were prominently enriched in regenerative pathways, including PI3K-Akt signaling, MAPK signaling, focal adhesion, ECM-receptor interaction, and TGF-β signaling pathway (all FDR < 0.001). These pathways are critical for cellular proliferation, survival, ECM production, and differentiation during tissue regeneration [30]. However, BM-MSCs were enriched in fewer pathways directly linked to ECM remodeling or cell-matrix interactions, reflecting their distinct biological predispositions.

Tenogenic-associated gene expression

Targeted analysis of tenogenic-related genes revealed differences in the intrinsic tenogenic potential between AD- and BM-MSCs (Fig. 3F). AD-MSCs showed a significantly higher expression of critical tendon ECM genes than BM-MSCs. Notably, AD-MSCs presented substantial upregulation of COL12A1 (7.2-fold; a vital role in fibril formation and ECM integrity) [31], and COL1A1 (9.4-fold; a primary structural collagen in tendons) [3, 32], TN-C (25.6-fold; a critical glycoprotein modulating cell-ECM interactions during tendon healing) [61], and DCN (4.4-fold) as well as BGN (5.5-fold), which are key small leucine-rich proteoglycans regulating collagen fibrillogenesis [32]. Moreover, several growth factors and cytokine-related genes were also significantly elevated in AD-MSCs, including FGF18 (183-fold), FGF2 (12-fold), TGFβ1 (4.1-fold), and BMP6 (115-fold). In addition, IL-33 (289.9-fold), IL4R (19.7-fold), and IL6 (5.9-fold) showed significant increases in expression, which is crucial for modulating the inflammatory environment during tendon repair. The transcription factor CEBPB (3.4-fold) was also upregulated in AD-MSCs, which plays a vital role in fibroblast differentiation [33]. The overall results showed that AD-MSCs had a transcriptional profile indicative of increased tendon ECM synthesis and activation of tenogenic signaling pathways, whereas BM-MSCs showed lower expression of these genes. These gene expression patterns reflect the inherently stronger tenogenic differentiation capacity of AD-MSCs than that of BM-MSCs.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of tenogenic-associated DEGs

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of tenogenic-associated DEGs further supported our findings. In the GO: BP terms, AD-MSCs showed significant enrichment in the GO terms “cellular response to growth factor stimulus” and “developmental process” (involving FGF18 and BMP6) and “extracellular matrix organization” (COL1A1 and FMOD) (Fig. 4A). These processes are essential for ECM remodeling, collagen synthesis, and cell motility, directly contributing to tendon regeneration and structural integrity [10, 30]. For GO: CC terms, “extracellular matrix,” “collagen-containing extracellular matrix” (TN-C, COL1A1, COL4A1, COL15A1), and “focal adhesion” (ITGA2, ITGA5) were significantly enriched (Fig. 4B). In terms of GO: MF, the “binding” (ITGA2), “collagen binding” (ITGA2), and “integrin binding” (ITGA5) terms were enriched, all of which are crucial for tenocyte differentiation, ECM stabilization, and functional matrix synthesis in tendon tissue (Fig. 4C). These results suggested that AD-MSCs possess intrinsic molecular functionalities that enhance their tenogenic differentiation potential.

AD-MSCs showed specific upregulation of tenogenic-associated genes (COL1A1, TN-C, FGF2 & 18, and TGFβ1) within key KEGG pathways, including PI3K-Akt, MAPK, and focal adhesion pathways (Fig. 4D). Pathway-level comparisons between AD- and BM-MSCs (Fig. S2C-E) represented enhanced activation of multiple tenogenic-relevant cascades in AD-MSCs. These AD-MSCs-enriched pathways are recognized for their essential roles in cell proliferation, differentiation, and ECM interactions, which are necessary for tendon repair. Moreover, the “TGF-β signaling pathway” enrichment highlighted the pivotal role in supporting tenogenic differentiation and collagen synthesis in AD-MSCs compared to BM-MSCs.

Validation of RNA-Seq

To validate the RNA-Seq findings, we performed qRT-PCR on randomly selected representative upregulated DEGs in AD-MSCs compared with BM-MSCs. The expression levels of LOX-1, BMP6, COL7A1, CH25H, IL-33, ITGA2, SLC16A3, and STAR were significantly higher in AD-MSCs than in BM-MSCs, which was consistent with the transcriptomic data obtained by RNA-Seq (Fig. 5A-H).

mRNA expression of tenogenic markers

Based on baseline molecular profile analysis, we evaluated the tenogenic differentiation potential of each MSC type by quantifying the expression of key tenogenic markers following tenogenic stimulation (Fig. 6A). This analysis was designed to determine whether baseline RNA-Seq differences corresponded to variations in in vitro differentiation capacity. Upon stimulation, AD-MSCs showed a significant upregulation of SCX compared to BM-MSCs (p < 0.0001), as well as COL1, COL3 (p < 0.001), TNMD, and TN-C (p < 0.0001). THBS-4 and DCN levels remained steady (p > 0.05), whereas SOX9 expression was downregulated compared to that in BM-MSCs (p < 0.05) (Fig. 6A). These changes reflect the ability of AD-MSCs to differentiate into tenocyte-like cells upon stimulation. In contrast, BM-MSCs showed no substantial alterations in the expression of tenogenic markers and a lower tenogenic differentiation potential.

Immunofluorescence staining of key tenogenic markers

The tenogenic capacity of each MSC type was evaluated by measuring the mean fluorescence intensity of tenogenic differentiation-related markers. Figure 6B-D represented the immunostaining results for SCX (red), COL1, and COL3 (green) in differentiated cultures. AD-MSCs showed a significant increase in SCX-positive nuclei as well as abundant COL1 and COL3 expression (Fig. 6B-D). The quantification confirmed that AD-MSCs showed significantly higher fluorescence intensities for SCX (p < 0.001), COL1 (p < 0.01), and COL3 (p < 0.01) than BM-MSCs (Fig. 6B-D). In contrast, BM-MSCs exhibited minimal changes in SCX, COL1, and COL3 expression following tenogenic induction (Fig. 6B-D). The results showed that AD-MSCs exhibited higher tenogenic marker expression under current induction conditions, whereas BM-MSCs exhibited a more limited tenogenic response.

In vivo tendon regeneration outcomes

The regenerative potential of different MSC types was evaluated following tendon injury in 12 SD rats that underwent complete 3 mm transverse transection of the Achilles tendon and were randomly assigned to three treatment groups. Undifferentiated (naïve) MSCs were injected into the space between the transected tendon and overlying skin to simulate a clinically relevant scenario (Fig. 7A). Tendon healing was assessed by H&E staining, modified Masson’s trichrome staining, Picro-Sirius Red staining, and immunohistochemistry (IHC). Histopathological analysis showed that AD-MSCs improved tissue organization and collagen fiber arrangement compared with untreated controls (p < 0.001) and BM-MSC-treated animals (p < 0.01) (Fig. 7B, D). Masson’s trichrome and Picro-Sirius Red staining further confirmed the enhanced COL1 deposition in AD-MSC-treated animals (Fig. 7B). IHC analysis demonstrated significantly increased COL1 expression in AD-MSC-treated tendons compared to that in their BM-MSC-treated counterparts (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 7C, E). In contrast, COL3 expression was significantly lower in the AD-MSC group (p < 0.05), whereas no significant difference in SCX expression was observed between the two groups (Fig. 7C, E).

Discussion

Tendon repair is a multifaceted biological process involving inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling. However, this is often hindered by poor vascularity, low cellularity, and fibrotic scarring [30, 34]. MSCs, including AD- and BM-MSCs, have the potential to modulate inflammation, enhance ECM remodeling, and improve tenocyte function [10]. Although individual investigations have highlighted the potential of different tissue-derived MSCs, the molecular mechanisms driving tenogenic differentiation remain unclear, limiting their ability to refine differentiation protocols and maximize therapeutic outcomes. This study addresses these gaps by integrating transcriptomic profiling with tenogenic differentiation analysis. Our findings suggest that AD- and BM-MSCs possess distinct biological characteristics, which is consistent with previous studies suggesting that tissue origin influences the properties of MSCs and their suitability for specific therapeutic applications [20, 35].

Both MSC types display similar fundamental characteristics, including morphology, expression of immunophenotypic markers, and differentiation potential of mesodermal derivatives. These results confirmed successful MSC isolation according to the criteria established in a previous study [36]. In this study, AD-MSCs demonstrated enhanced growth properties, including faster confluence, higher colony-forming efficiency, and increased proliferation, compared to BM-MSCs. These findings are consistent with those of previous studies, which reported that AD-MSCs possess intrinsic properties favorable for rapid expansion and regenerative applications, likely owing to their ease of harvest, abundance, and multi-lineage differentiation capacity [37, 38]. Their robust proliferation and early passage growth rates further support their scalability and long-term expansion.

Tenogenic differentiation, the process by which cells become tenocyte-like, is crucial for MSC-based tendon healing because it directly affects the formation and repair of damaged tendon tissue [39]. Previous studies have reported positive results for AD-MSCs in terms of tenogenic differentiation potential in both rat and human cell lineages [27, 40]. The functional differentiation potential of each MSC type was assessed by culturing them in tenogenic induction medium and validating their responsiveness to tenogenic cues. Our comparative analysis demonstrated that AD-MSCs showed higher tenogenic differentiation potential than BM-MSCs. Following tenogenic induction, AD-MSCs exhibited higher expression of tenogenic markers (SCX, COL1, COL3, TNMD, and TN-C) than BM-MSCs, suggesting a stronger lineage commitment toward tenocyte-like cells. SCX is a tendon progenitor-specific transcription marker that regulates COL1 and TNMD expression, essential for tendon structure and maturation [41, 42]. COL1 is the main collagen secreted by mature tenocytes, whereas COL3 aids in early wound healing and supports epitenon and endotenon formation [32]. The upregulation of these markers, as confirmed by qRT-PCR and immunofluorescence staining, was associated with the highest SCX and collagen-related protein expression in AD-MSCs. Moreover, AD-MSCs showed significant SOX9 downregulation, which is consistent with previous studies suggesting that tenogenesis requires the suppression of chondrogenic pathways [32, 34]. Although these findings contradict previous reports suggesting that AD-MSCs primarily favor adipogenesis [35, 38], our results indicate that AD-MSCs have considerable tenogenic differentiation potential, conforming to their reported plasticity and adaptability [37]. In contrast, BM-MSCs appear less responsive and show limited tenogenic differentiation, possibly because of their inherent osteogenic and chondrogenic propensities [32, 38]. These results highlighted the impact of tissue origin on lineage commitment, revealing the adaptability of AD-MSCs beyond the expected differentiation pathways.

Despite their pivotal role in tissue regeneration due to self-renewal and pluripotency, the therapeutic potential of MSCs is limited by challenges such as donor aging, individual variability, and poorly understood molecular mechanisms [43–46].

Although MSCs share common characteristics, they comprise diverse subpopulations with varying functions, morphologies, and gene expression profiles. This inherent heterogeneity resulting from cellular physiological variations, differentiation states, and clonality complicates their characterization and clinical applications [47]. Transcriptomic approaches, especially RNA-Seq, can help clarify these complexities by mapping gene expression patterns and identifying pathways relevant to lineage specification [48]. In addition, tissue-engineered tendon repair remains challenging because of the limited understanding of the cellular signaling and molecular mechanisms regulating the tenogenic potential of stem cells [49]. In this study, we aimed to identify the baseline transcriptional profiles that may underlie the distinct biological properties of the two primary sources of MSCs.

Transcriptomic analysis revealed that AD- and BM-MSCs exhibited distinct gene expression patterns. The results also highlighted that the DEGs and their enriched pathways in AD-MSCs were critical for tendon repair, including PI3K-Akt signaling, MAPK signaling, and focal adhesion pathways. These pathways are essential for ECM remodeling, cellular adhesion, proliferation, tenogenesis, collagen synthesis, and tenocyte migration [30, 50, 51]. Heatmap clustering analysis has shown that AD-MSCs have specific transcriptomic signatures linked to tenogenic potential, providing a molecular framework for assessing lineage-promising and functional maturation [48]. These findings suggest that the unique intrinsic transcriptomic signature of AD-MSCs may contribute to the gene expression patterns involved in MSC differentiation, supporting their advantage in creating a regenerative microenvironment conducive to tendon healing and tenogenic potential.

GO and KEGG analyses identified increased ECM-related genes and integrin-mediated signaling in AD-MSCs, suggesting an enhanced interaction with the microenvironment. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway is significantly enriched in AD-MSCs, which regulates COL1 production and cell cycle progression during healing [50]. Our results showed significant upregulation of downstream effectors, including TGFβ1, COL1A1, and IL4R, which are essential for ECM production, cellular adhesion, and anti-inflammatory modulation. While TGFβ/Smad2/3 represented the canonical tenogenic pathway, PI3K/Akt may facilitate TGFβ-driven tendon repair independently of Smad signaling [52]. TGFβ1 could activate Akt, which regulates cell survival, growth, and protein synthesis [53, 54], and may activate Akt without Smad2/3 involvement [55], suggesting a Smad-independent tenogenic route. Furthermore, PI3K-Akt signaling could enhance MSC survival by promoting anti-apoptotic properties during tendon injury [49]. TGFβ isoforms guide musculoskeletal lineage commitment, and TGFβ2 relates to tenogenesis [56]. These isoforms may act simultaneously but trigger different pathways, given TGFβ1’s potential fibrotic and chondrogenic tendencies [57, 58]. While TGFβ2 signaling pathways are complex and involve interactions with the BMP, MAPK, and Wnt pathways [59], the specific role of PI3K/Akt in TGFβ2-mediated tenogenesis requires further investigation. Enhanced activation of the PI3K/Akt signaling pathway in AD-MSCs may indicate dual potential benefits for ECM remodeling and cell viability.

The MAPK signaling pathway is vital for tenogenesis and regulates cellular stress responses and proliferation. Our findings on the upregulation of this pathway in AD-MSCs are consistent with those of previous studies demonstrating its role in FGF signaling, collagen synthesis, ECM remodeling, and fibroblast differentiation into tendon-like cells [34, 50]. The increased expression of FGF18 and FGF2 observed in this study supports the importance of this pathway. FGF activation of the MAPK/ERK pathway not only promotes tendon lineage commitment and differentiation but also encourages neovascularization, inflammation modulation, and cellular proliferation [34]. In addition, MAPK/ERK phosphorylates transcription factors such as Myc, reinforcing its role in tendon regeneration [60]. The upregulation of the PI3K-Akt and MAPK signaling pathways suggests a molecular basis for the enhanced regenerative capacity of AD-MSCs in tendon repair.

Activation of the focal adhesion pathway in AD-MSCs indicated improved mechanotransduction and ECM interaction capabilities. Our findings showed upregulated integrins and associated molecules (ITGA2, ITGA8, ITGA5, LAMA5, TN-C), which may improve cell-ECM adhesion and migration, enabling MSCs to respond more effectively to the biomechanical signals essential for tendon remodeling [61]. DEGs showed increased levels of matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), particularly MMP12, essential for ECM remodeling, scar degradation, fibrosis inhibition, and regenerative healing. Tendon repair entails intrinsic and extrinsic pathways, with BMP/Smad signaling being a key component, while ECM proteins like laminin-α1 facilitate muscle-tendon junction formation [34]. The connection between MMP activity and collagen synthesis (COL1A1) ensures a proper tendon matrix structure, reduces adhesion, and enhances functional recovery. The balance among MMP activity, collagen production (COL1A1), adhesion molecules, and growth factor signaling is crucial for tendon matrix structure and functional recovery.

Notably, IL-33 (an alarmin cytokine) was significantly upregulated in the DEGs between AD-MSCs and BM-MSCs, suggesting a novel potential immunoregulatory role in tendon repair. The function of IL-33 in tendon healing is still being clarified, but it may enhance MSC-mediated repair during the early remodeling phases. However, prolonged elevation may promote fibrosis and COL3 deposition, potentially compromising tendon integrity [62, 63]. Previous studies have reported that IL-33 promotes inflammation and extracellular matrix remodeling, contributing to tendon pathology. IL-33 communicates via the ST2 receptor and may modulate immune responses by promoting macrophage polarization and recruiting regulatory T cells; however, its intracellular versus extracellular functions remain unclear [64, 65]. Elevated IL-33 expression in AD-MSCs warrants further investigation as a potential therapeutic mediator and prognostic biomarker for tendinopathy.

Histological analysis indicated that AD-MSC-treated tendons had more favorable regenerative outcomes than BM-MSC, as evidenced by improved collagen organization, lower histopathological lesion scores, and elevated COL1 expression. These results suggest that AD-MSC treatment of Achilles tendon injury enhances matrix remodeling and tenogenic potential. In contrast, BM-MSCs showed less favorable effects, possibly because of their established benefits for cartilage and bone repair and their tendency toward non-tenogenic lineage differentiation, which poses a risk of heterotopic matrix formation during tendon repair, even under tenogenic induction [66].

Although our in vitro and transcriptomic analyses demonstrated the enhanced tenogenic potential of AD-MSCs, several limitations should be considered. The sample size was relatively small, and long-term in vivo follow-up studies would be valuable for assessing the therapeutic durability. Functional locomotion and gait analyses were not performed, which limited the behavioral assessment of tendon healing. Stem cell labeling was not conducted, thereby restricting the evaluation of MSC engraftment. In addition, dose-response studies on MSCs should be performed to optimize therapeutic applications. Future studies should utilize larger animal models, extended follow-up periods, and comprehensive functional assessments to improve the clinical translatability.

Conclusion

This comprehensive comparison of AD- and BM-MSCs demonstrated that AD-MSCs possess a superior intrinsic capacity for tendon regeneration. Our transcriptomic analysis revealed that AD-MSCs exhibit distinct molecular signatures with significant enrichment in the PI3K-Akt, MAPK, focal adhesion, and ECM remodeling pathways. The upregulation of key tenogenic-associated genes, including COL1A1, TN-C, and FGF18 in AD-MSCs translated into enhanced in vivo therapeutic efficacy, as evidenced by improved collagen organization, favorable COL1/COL3 ratios, and improved histological outcomes. These molecular and functional advantages and practical considerations (e.g., abundance, plasticity, and ease of harvest), suggest that AD-MSCs constitute an optimal cell source for tendon tissue regenerative medicine applications. In contrast, BM-MSCs exhibited lineage-specific tenogenic characteristics but showed less pronounced activation of repair-related pathways, which may explain their comparatively limited regenerative efficacy.
