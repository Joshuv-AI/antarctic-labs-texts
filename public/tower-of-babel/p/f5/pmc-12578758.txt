Development and evaluation of a nutritional risk prediction model for patients with hepatocellular carcinoma undergoing transarterial chemoembolisation
Authors: Chun-Xia Chen, Xiang-Fan Chen, Tian-Le Wang, Hai-Yan Gu, Guan-Hua Xu, Zhi-Ming Cui
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
To study the risk factors for malnutrition in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma undergoing transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE), construct a risk prediction model and evaluate its predictive effectiveness. A retrospective study was conducted, selecting 194 cases of patients with liver cancer who received their first TACE treatment in the interventional department of a tertiary first-class hospital in Nantong, Jiangsu Province, between January 2019 and December 2023. General data and factors related to malnutrition were retrospectively collected, and the occurrence of malnutrition within 3 months following TACE surgery was observed. Univariate analysis and multivariate logistic regression analysis were performed on the data, a predictive model was constructed and the model performance was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The incidence of malnutrition was 36.08%. Multivariate logistic regression analysis showed that haemoglobin (Hb) (P = 0.048, odds ratio [OR] = 0.971), age (P = 0.017, OR = 1.068), Child–Pugh (CP) class score (P = 0.000, OR = 11.780), L3-psoas muscle index (L3-PMI) (P = 0.036, OR = 0.062) and albumin (ALB) (P = 0.000, OR = 0.804) were independent risk factors for malnutrition. The area under the ROC curve of the constructed model was 0.958, indicating its strong predictive power. The HL goodness-of-fit test (P > 0.05) suggested that the model fit was good. Advanced age, high CP grade, low L3-PMI, low ALB and low Hb are independent risk factors for malnutrition in patients undergoing TACE for liver cancer. The constructed model can provide a scientific basis for nutritional management of patients.

Introduction

The latest statistics from the Global Cancer Research Agency show that in 2020, there were approximately 906,000 new cases of liver cancer worldwide, with around 830,000 deaths, of which more than 50% occurred in China [1]. The main risk factors for liver cancer include infections with viral hepatitis B and C. Currently, there are approximately 90 million carriers of the hepatitis B virus in China [2], predominantly in the eastern regions [3].

Primary liver cancer (PLC) is the fourth most common malignant tumour and the second leading cause of cancer-related death in China [4]. Because many patients with liver cancer also suffer from chronic hepatitis or cirrhosis, the progression of the disease is often insidious, and by the time of diagnosis, most patients are already in the middle to late stages, resulting in poor prognosis. Among the various treatment modalities, transcatheter arterial chemoembolisation (TACE) is one of the primary treatments for patients with advanced PLC. This technique can be used alone or in combination with other treatment methods, playing an increasingly important role in the comprehensive treatment of liver cancer.

However, malnutrition is a common issue among patients with PLC, with the prevalence of malnutrition ranging from 33.5% to 80% [5–9]. Protein malnutrition not only reduces the patient’s immune function and exacerbates the condition but may also weaken the effectiveness of anti-tumour treatments, thereby accelerating the spread and growth of tumour cells [10]. Additionally, malnutrition is closely related to the incidence of postoperative complications and is an important independent risk factor affecting the mortality rate of patients with liver cancer [11]. Patients undergoing TACE treatment for liver cancer are typically at a more advanced stage, with a greater tumour burden and more severe liver function damage, meaning malnutrition may be more common and more severe in these patients [12]. For those with nutritional risk or malnutrition, nutritional therapy should commence preoperatively and become an important component of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) treatment. However, preoperative nutritional support for patients with HCC without malnutrition remains controversial and may even increase patient burden.

Early identification and intervention for malnutrition are key to improving outcomes for patients with cancer. Studies have shown that the use of nutritional risk screening tools can detect patients’ malnutrition status early, allowing for implementation of effective interventions and thereby improving treatment outcomes [13–16]. Although nutritional risk prediction models have been applied in other disease areas, research on nutritional risk prediction for patients undergoing TACE surgery for liver cancer remains scarce, with a lack of large-scale studies on nutritional assessment indicators [17]. Therefore, this study explores the key factors affecting malnutrition in patients with liver cancer following TACE surgery and constructs precise and efficient prediction models to provide a scientific basis for clinical nutritional management.

Methods and materials

Study participants

This study adopts a retrospective research design, selecting 194 patients with liver cancer who first received TACE treatment in the interventional department of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nantong University, between January 2019 and December 2023. All patients were diagnosed with PLC through imaging, pathological examination or tumour marker testing.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: patients (1) diagnosed with PLC based on imaging, pathology or tumour marker tests; (2) undergoing first-time TACE treatment, with no history of other liver cancer-related surgeries; (3) aged ≥ 18 years; (4) with liver function classified as Child–Pugh (CP) grade A or B and/or Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group score of 0–2; (5) with no serious cardiovascular, pulmonary, renal or haematological diseases; (6) with no preoperative diagnosis of malnutrition and no history of severe gastrointestinal diseases (e.g. inflammatory bowel disease, gastrointestinal tumours); (7) with complete clinical examination records and laboratory data; and (8) with normal cognitive function, capable of independently completing relevant assessments and having signed informed consent forms.

The exclusion criteria included patients (1) with preoperative diagnosis of moderate to severe malnutrition; (2) with severe infections, other malignancies or chronic diseases that affect nutritional status (e.g. chronic kidney disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease); (3) with psychiatric or neurological disorders that may interfere with data collection or follow-up; and (4) with more than 20% missing critical data, preventing a complete assessment.

Screening of malnutrition risk factors

This study conducted a systematic literature review (meta-analysis), incorporating 13 articles, 12 in Chinese and one in a foreign language, involving a total of 2,353 patients. Potential malnutrition risk factors for patients with liver cancer were identified, with results indicating the following seven factors as closely associated with malnutrition in such patients: decreased body mass index (BMI), advanced TNM stage of liver cancer (stage III or above), CP grade B or C, decreased haemoglobin (Hb) (anaemia), reduced serum albumin (ALB), reduced serum prealbumin and advanced age (≥ 65 years). Due to the complex aetiology of malnutrition in patients with liver cancer, it is challenging to analyse potential interactions among these factors. Additionally, since liver cancer is highly prevalent in East Asia, particularly in China, there is limited mention of related foreign literature studies, which may introduce bias in the research sample.

As such, semi-structured interviews were conducted with patients with liver cancer who had a history of malnutrition post-TACE and frontline medical staff in the interventional department to further refine other potential influencing factors for malnutrition. The Delphi method was employed for expert consultation, with two rounds of expert discussions. A total of 24 malnutrition risk factors specific to patients with liver cancer undergoing TACE were identified, providing foundational data for subsequent model construction.

Transarterial chemoembolisation procedure for study participants

In the 194 cases of TACE surgery, the following procedures were used. Under digital subtraction angiography, Seldinger catheterisation was performed through the femoral artery to access the celiac trunk for imaging. Subsequently, a microcatheter was superselectively inserted into the artery supplying the liver tumour, and emulsified oil (iodised opium injection) was injected. The chemotherapy drug dose (platinum derivatives + anthracyclines) was determined based on the size of the lesion, blood supply and patient’s surface index. Finally, gelatin sponge particles were injected as embolic agents.

Research method

Diagnostic criteria for malnutrition

Based on the ‘Expert Consensus on Malnutrition Diagnosis Criteria’ published by ESPEN in 2015, this study adopted the following criteria to diagnose the malnutrition status of patients with liver cancer undergoing TACE treatment: (1) BMI < 18.5 kg/m2; (2) uncontrolled weight loss > 10% (time unspecified), or weight loss > 5% within 3 months; and (3) on the basis of meeting one of the above two points, malnutrition can be diagnosed by satisfying any one of the following criteria:

a. BMI < 20 kg/m2 (age < 70 years) or BMI < 22 kg/m2 (age ≥ 70 years);

b. Fat-free mass index (FFMI) < 15 kg/m2 (women) or FFMI < 17 kg/m2 (men).

Data collection method

The general data and malnutrition-related factors of the patients were retrospectively collected through the electronic medical record system. Additionally, follow-up data on the occurrence of malnutrition within 3 months after TACE surgery were recorded.

The collected information included the following: (1) patient demographics (gender, age, marital status, education level, medical insurance status); (2) clinical characteristics (BMI, appetite, smoking and alcohol use, TNM staging, CP classification, hepatitis B virus infection status, history of liver cancer resection surgery, TACE-related complications and postoperative recovery); (3) laboratory test results (lymphocyte count, Hb, ALB, prealbumin, total bilirubin, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase); and (4) L3 Psoas Muscle Index (L3-PMI) to evaluate muscle mass.

Statistical methods

All data were analysed using SPSS 23.0 statistical software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics were used for general data and results. Normally distributed continuous data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (x̄ ± s), and independent sample t-tests were used for inter-group comparisons. Categorical data were presented as percentages (%), and chi-square tests were used for inter-group comparisons.

Univariate analysis was conducted using univariate logistic regression to screen for factors associated with malnutrition. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed using the backward stepwise method with maximum likelihood estimation to build a malnutrition risk prediction model for patients with liver cancer undergoing TACE. The model’s performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis to assess the predictive ability of the logistic regression model and its variables, and the goodness of fit of the model was evaluated using the Hosmer–Lemeshow (HL) test.

Results

General information of the research participants

This study collected data from 210 patients who met the inclusion criteria between January 2019 and December 2023. Exclusions included five patients transferred from other hospitals who immediately underwent TACE procedures and who had more than 20% preoperative-related data missing in electronic medical records; three patients experiencing HCC rupture with haemorrhage who received massive blood product transfusions upon admission, rendering laboratory indicators unable to reflect actual nutritional status; and eight patients lost to follow-up following discharge without documented malnutrition during postoperative hospitalisation. Ultimately, data from 194 patients were included for statistical analysis (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Recruitment flowchart of research subjects

Recruitment flowchart of research subjects

Of the total 194 patients with HCC who underwent TACE, 148 were men (76.29%) and 46 were women (23.71%). The patients’ age range was 27–85 years, with an average age of 64.64 ± 9.892 years. Regarding marital status, 190 patients were married (97.94%) and four were unmarried (2.06%). The distribution of educational levels was as follows: 85 patients (43.81%) had an education level of primary school or below, 84 (43.3%) had a middle school education and 25 (12.89%) had a college or higher education.

The medical insurance situation indicated that there were 116 patients (59.79%) with medical insurance and 78 (40.21%) without medical insurance. The distribution of TNM stages was as follows: stage I, 31 cases (15.98%), stage II, 46 cases (23.71%), stage III, 54 cases (27.84%) and stage IV, 63 cases (32.47%). In terms of CP classification, 77 patients (39.69%) were at grade A, 85 (43.81%) were at grade B and 32 (16.49%) were at grade C.

In terms of nutrition-related indicators, the BMI range of the study participants was 18.59–35.16 kg/m2, with an average of 22.85 ± 2.981 kg/m2; the L3-PMI range was 1.625–10.229 cm2/m2, with an average of 5.36 ± 1.109 cm2/m2; ALB levels ranged from 20 to 48.8 g/L, with an average of 35.71 ± 4.047 g/L; and Hb levels ranged from 64 to 171 g/L, with an average of 124.76 ± 21.261 g/L.

Based on the diagnostic criteria for malnutrition, patients were divided into a malnourished group and a non-malnourished group. There was no statistically significant difference in the general data between the two groups of patients (P > 0.05). Among the 194 patients, 70 experienced malnutrition, with an incidence rate of 36.08%.

Univariate analysis

Using the patient’s baseline risk factors as independent variables and the occurrence of malnutrition as the dependent variable, a univariate analysis was conducted. The results showed that age, BMI, CP grade, TNM stage, L3-PMI, ALB and Hb were all significantly associated with the occurrence of malnutrition following TACE (P < 0.05), indicating that these may be important factors affecting the patient’s nutritional status post-surgery.

In the malnutrition group, the average age of patients was 67.84 ± 10.670 years, which was significantly higher than the 62.12 ± 9.832 years in the non-malnutrition group (P < 0.001), suggesting that older individuals are more prone to malnutrition. The mean BMI was significantly lower in the malnutrition group (22.715 ± 3.232 kg/m2) than in the non-malnutrition group (23.64 ± 2.939 kg/m2) (P = 0.044), indicating that a lower BMI may be associated with the occurrence of postoperative malnutrition.

In terms of CP classification, the incidence of malnutrition was highest in patients classified as grade C (96.88%), whereas it was lowest in patients classified as grade A (5.19%), with a statistically significant difference (P < 0.001), indicating that patients with poorer liver function are more likely to experience malnutrition. Moreover, TNM staging is also significantly associated with the occurrence of malnutrition, with the incidence of malnutrition in patients at stage IV (55.56%) being much higher than in those at stage I (12.90%) (P < 0.001), suggesting that the higher the degree of tumour progression is, the poorer the nutritional status of the patients may be.

In terms of laboratory indicators, the mean L3-PMI (4.16 ± 0.973 cm2/m2) was significantly lower in the malnutrition group than in the non-malnutrition group (6.58 ± 1.219 cm2/m2) (P < 0.001), suggesting that lower muscle mass may increase the risk of malnutrition. The ALB level was significantly lower in the malnutrition group (33.76 ± 4.063 g/L) than in the non-malnutrition group (37.17 ± 4.347 g/L) (P < 0.001), indicating that hypoalbuminaemia may be closely related to postoperative malnutrition. Similarly, the Hb level in the malnutrition group (114.76 ± 21.852 g/L) was significantly lower than that in the non-malnutrition group (131.19 ± 21.281 g/L) (P < 0.001), suggesting that anaemia may be one of the risk factors for malnutrition.

The results of univariate analysis indicated that patients with older age, lower BMI, higher CP grade, later TNM stage, lower L3-PMI, lower ALB and lower Hb are more prone to malnutrition, suggesting that these factors may be important predictors of malnutrition in patients with HCC undergoing TACE. For every additional year of age, the risk of malnutrition in patients increases by 6.8% (odds ratio [OR] = 1.068, 95%CI: 1.012–1.128, P = 0.017). Patients with CP grade B and C have a significantly higher risk of malnutrition, especially those with grade C, whose OR value was as high as 11.780 (95%CI: 4.071–34.082, P < 0.001), indicating that the poorer the liver function is, the higher the risk of malnutrition following surgery.

Patients with lower L3-PMI levels are more prone to malnutrition (OR = 0.062, 95%CI: 0.017–0.230, P = 0.036), indicating that sarcopenia may be an important influencing factor for malnutrition following TACE surgery for liver cancer. Lower ALB levels (OR = 0.804, 95%CI: 0.726–0.890, P < 0.001) and lower Hb levels (OR = 0.971, 95%CI: 0.943–1.000, P = 0.048) are both closely related to malnutrition, suggesting that hypoalbuminaemia and anaemia may increase the risk of postoperative malnutrition.

A malnutrition risk prediction model for patients with HCC undergoing TACE treatment was constructed based on logistic regression analysis, with the regression equation as follows:

Logit(P) = 3.388–0.030 × Hb + 0.066 × Age + 2.466 × CP + 0.218 × ALB − 2.774(L3-PMI).

The results showed a likelihood ratio chi-square value of 8.584 (df = 8, P = 0.378), with a P-value of > 0.05, indicating that the model’s goodness-of-fit is satisfactory and the match between the predicted results and the actual observed outcomes is high (Fig. 2).Fig. 2ROC Curve Analysis of Regression Models and Variables in the Models

Further adoption of ROC curve analysis was used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model. The results showed that the area under the ROC curve (AUC) of the entire model was 0.958 (95%CI: 0.933–0.982, P < 0.001), indicating that the model has high predictive performance.

The ROC curve analysis was performed on the variables in the logistic regression model, and the results indicated that the AUC value for CP classification ranged between 0.85 and 0.95 (AUC = 0.853, 95%CI: 0.798–0.908, P < 0.001), indicating a relatively good individual predictive efficacy. The AUC values for age and TNM staging were between 0.5 and 0.7 (AUC = 0.669 and 0.694, P < 0.001), suggesting lower predictive efficacy. The AUC values for Hb, BMI, L3-PMI and ALB were all < 0.5 (P < 0.001), indicating that these individual indicators have lower predictive efficacy and are not as effective as the comprehensive model in predicting outcomes.

Discussion

The results of this study indicate that advanced age, low L3-PMI, low ALB, low Hb and higher CؘP scores are independent risk factors for malnutrition in patients undergoing TACE for liver cancer. These results underscore the multifactorial nature of malnutrition in TACE-treated patients with HCC. Unlike conventional nutritional assessments that rely on isolated parameters (e.g. BMI or ALB), our model uniquely integrates L3-PMI (reflecting sarcopenia), CP grade (indicating liver functional reserve) and Hb (reflecting oxygen-carrying capacity), which are critical in the context of post-TACE metabolic stress. This comprehensive approach addresses the limitations of existing tools and aligns with ESPEN guidelines emphasising the need for disease-specific nutritional models in chronic liver disease.

Advanced age is an important risk factor for malnutrition in patients undergoing TACE for liver cancer. The mechanisms of malnutrition in elderly patients are complex, involving reduced protein intake, changes in body metabolism, decreased digestive function and socioeconomic factors. With increasing age, the body’s protein metabolism falls into a negative balance, leading to increased nitrogen loss and muscle reduction, which, in turn, causes a decline in immune function, thereby increasing the risk of malnutrition. Additionally, elderly patients often experience loose teeth and reduced secretion of digestive fluids, which further affects the dissolution and absorption of various nutrients, such as calcium, iron, vitamin B2, folic acid and vitamin B6 [17–19]. Socioeconomic factors are also significant contributors to malnutrition in the elderly; for example, living alone or declining income may lead to a decrease in dietary quality and insufficient nutrient intake [20]. Studies have shown that the incidence of malnutrition in patients aged ≥ 60 years is 1.34 times that of patients < 60 years old [21], and in patients > 65 years old, the risk of malnutrition increases by 7% for each additional year of age [22].

The findings of this study are consistent with previous research, suggesting that individualised nutritional support should be implemented preoperatively for elderly patients undergoing TACE. This includes calculating daily nutritional requirements, selecting appropriate nutritional supplementation methods, adjusting dietary structure, choosing nutrients, modifying the frequency of nutrient intake and providing activity guidance. These measures aim to improve nutritional status and enhance treatment tolerance.

This study found that a low L3-PMI is an important influencing factor for malnutrition in patients undergoing TACE for liver cancer. The research results showed that the L3-PMI of patients with malnutrition was 4.16 ± 0.973 cm2/m2, significantly lower than that of the non-malnourished group at 6.58 ± 1.219 cm2/m2 (P < 0.001). The L3-PMI is an objective indicator used in recent years to assess muscle mass, and its reduction typically suggests sarcopenia, which has been recognised as one of the important factors for poor prognosis in patients with malignant tumours. In HCC, muscle wasting is not merely a consequence of nutritional deficits but also reflects systemic inflammation and metabolic derangements driven by tumour progression. Unlike traditional serum markers (e.g. ALB), L3-PMI captures structural muscle depletion, which may correlate more closely with functional outcomes and treatment tolerance. This study utilised abdominal computed tomography imaging to measure the cross-sectional area of the psoas muscle at the level of the L3 vertebra and calculated the ratio to height squared. This method incurs no additional economic burden and no extra radiation exposure, and it can objectively reflect muscle reserves. It has been widely used in nutritional assessment for patients with cancer [23–26]. The results of this study indicate that patients with a lower L3-PMI have a significantly increased risk of malnutrition, suggesting that L3-PMI can be incorporated into the nutritional assessment system post-TACE in clinical practice to identify high-risk groups early and adopt targeted muscle protection and nutritional support strategies.

Low ALB and low Hb (anaemia) are significant risk factors for malnutrition in patients with liver cancer undergoing TACE. Albumin levels in these patients are influenced by multiple factors, including impaired hepatocyte synthetic function, decreased appetite, reduced digestive and absorptive capacity, and inflammatory status. In this study, the ALB level of malnourished patients was 33.76 ± 4.063 g/L, significantly lower than that of non-malnourished patients at 37.17 ± 4.347 g/L (P < 0.001), indicating that low ALB levels can serve as a sensitive indicator of malnutrition. Studies have shown that ALB levels not only reflect nutritional status but are also an important factor affecting the prognosis of patients with chronic diseases. Preoperative hypoalbuminaemia is significantly associated with the incidence of postoperative complications [27–29], suggesting that protein nutritional status should be actively improved before surgery.

Furthermore, the results of this study indicate that the Hb levels of patients in the malnutrition group (114.76 ± 21.852 g/L) were significantly lower than in those in the non-malnutrition group (131.19 ± 21.281 g/L, P < 0.001), suggesting that anaemia may increase the risk of malnutrition. Haemoglobin is a key protein in the body’s blood that carries oxygen, and its reduction leads to tissue hypoxia and impaired metabolic function, thereby affecting the nutritional status of patients [8]. The mechanism of anaemia in patients with liver cancer is complex, including gastrointestinal bleeding, portal hypertension with splenomegaly, chronic blood loss and inadequate nutritional intake. Studies have shown that the risk of malnutrition in hospitalised patients with liver cancer and anaemia is 2.311 times higher than that of those who are non-anaemic [8], indicating that when conducting nutritional assessments, the impact of anaemia on the overall health status of patients should be fully considered, and supplementation of nutrients such as iron, vitamin B12 and folic acid should be actively implemented.

A CP grade of B or above is a strong predictor of malnutrition in patients undergoing TACE for HCC, consistent with previous studies [8, 30]. The CP score is the most commonly used clinical indicator for liver function assessment, which includes five key parameters: hepatic encephalopathy, ascites, serum bilirubin, ALB and prothrombin time [31]. This study found that a higher CP score correlates with an increased risk of malnutrition, potentially due to the following factors: impaired liver function leading to decreased digestive and absorptive capacity; hepatic encephalopathy and ascites limiting the intake of protein and sodium; and cholestasis causing a deficiency in fat-soluble vitamins. Therefore, early nutritional intervention and liver function protective treatment are crucial for patients with a CP grade of B or above.

This study found that hepatitis B infection status and BMI are not significant influencing factors for malnutrition following TACE surgery. Possible reasons for this include that, with the widespread application of antiviral therapy, effective control of hepatitis B has reduced its impact on liver function, with the nutritional status of young and middle-aged patients with liver cancer particularly improved [32]. In addition, although BMI is a traditional indicator for nutritional assessment, it may be affected by factors such as edema and ascites in patients with liver cancer, and in some stages, BMI may even increase, leading to its inability to accurately reflect the true nutritional status of patients [33]. Therefore, relying solely on BMI in the nutritional assessment of patients undergoing TACE may have limitations and should be combined with other more sensitive indicators, such as L3-PMI and ALB levels, for a more comprehensive evaluation.

This study systematically reviewed domestic and international literature on risk factors for malnutrition in patients with liver cancer. The research employed comprehensive database searches with well-designed retrieval strategies, conducted scientifically rigorous systematic evaluations and followed standardised literature screening and data extraction protocols to ensure reliable research outcomes [34]. However, among the 13 eligible studies, only one was in a foreign language, and there was limited focus on the specific population of patients with liver cancer undergoing TACE procedures. Therefore, semi-structured interviews were conducted with frontline medical staff and patients, with the interview results serving as supplementary data.

The 15 consulting experts selected for this study comprehensively represented the research field. All had over 10 years of professional experience and held at least a bachelor’s degree, with 80% possessing associate senior or higher titles, demonstrating strong representativeness. The response rates of the two rounds of questionnaires related to risk factors were 100% and 93.33%, meeting good standards, with 12 experts providing valuable feedback, indicating high engagement. The average expert authority coefficient was 0.88, reflecting strong expert credibility. As such, the malnutrition risk factors identified for patients with liver cancer undergoing TACE in this study are comprehensive, reliable and scientifically credible.

However, as a retrospective study, certain risk factors were difficult to collect due to limitations in the existing electronic medical record system, including important indicators such as China Liver Cancer and Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer staging and prealbumin levels in patients with HCC, thereby introducing study limitations. Additionally, this was a single-centre study with a limited number of cases, and only internal validation of the model was performed. The next steps will involve multicentre prospective studies with more cases and both internal and external validation to ensure more rigorous results. Subsequently, plans include developing a user-friendly system app to embed the model for clinical use, ultimately benefiting patients. At present, our medical team has comprehensively grasped and evaluated the nutritional risks of patients through the collection of detailed information in the clinical diagnosis and treatment process of this institution. Based on the principle of prevention, we have provided necessary nutritional management guidance for patients.

Conclusion

This study constructed a malnutrition risk prediction model for patients with HCC undergoing TACE. The five independent predictive factors ultimately included in the model were Hb, age, CP grade, ALB and L3-PMI. The constructed risk prediction model is as follows: Logit(P) = 3.388 − 0.030 × Hb + 0.066 × Age + 2.466 × CP grade + 0.218 × ALB − 2.774(L3-PMI), with good calibration of the model. The HL test (P = 0.378) indicated a high goodness of fit. The ROC curve analysis showed that the AUC was 0.958 (95%CI: 0.933–0.982), indicating that the model has strong discriminant ability. The risk prediction model constructed in this study can be used for malnutrition risk assessment of patients prior to TACE, providing a scientific basis for early clinical identification of high-risk patients and optimisation of preoperative nutritional intervention plans.
