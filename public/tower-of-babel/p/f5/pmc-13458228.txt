Are respiratory physicians adherent to guidelines when evaluating pulmonary nodules?
Authors: Laura Tu, Raymond Wong, Amy Baker, Fergal O’Donoghue, Christine F McDonald, Tracy L Leong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionAlthough fewer than 1% of pulmonary nodules are malignant, appropriate evaluation is essential in optimising patient outcomes. The 2017 Fleischner Society Guidelines provide recommendations on management of incidentally detected nodules; however, studies to date have shown variable guideline adherence. Adherence to guidelines in Australian nodule clinics has not been reported.MethodsA retrospective analysis of medical records was conducted for new patients evaluated in a pulmonary nodule clinic between 1 July 2017 and 1 July 2020. Patient demographics, past medical history, family history of malignancy, smoking history, baseline lung function tests, reasons for referral and lung nodule characteristics were analysed. Follow‐up recommendations were evaluated to determine Guideline adherence. Final diagnoses, where available, were extracted from multidisciplinary meeting (MDM) data.ResultsThree hundred sixty‐one patients were included (mean age 68.2 years, female 44.3%). One hundred ten patients (30.5%) were never smokers, 106 (29.3%) current smokers, and 140 (38.8%) former smokers. Most lung nodules were identified incidentally (77.0%) with mean diameter (±SD) 12.1 ± 6.4 mm averaged from the long and short axes. Adherence to guidelines was 97.2%. Ninety‐seven patients (26.9%) were diagnosed with malignancy. Among patients diagnosed with lung cancer, former smokers had a higher risk (48.1%, relative risk (RR) 1.8; 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.08–3.1; P = 0.02) of lung cancer than never smokers (20.3%), with current smokers being intermediate in risk (31.6%, RR 1.6; 95% CI 0.96–2.84; P = 0.07).ConclusionOur findings from this first‐in‐Australia study demonstrate appropriate lung nodule follow‐up in accordance with Fleischner Society Guidelines in a dedicated respiratory physician‐led nodule clinic.Keywords: pulmonary nodule, lung cancer, Fleischner, physician adherence

Introduction

Increasing use and sensitivity of imaging modalities has seen a rise in detection of pulmonary nodules defined as lesions ≤3 cm in diameter. 1 , 2 Worldwide, the prevalence of non‐calcified lung nodules in screened and non‐screened populations is 33% and 13% respectively, 3 with mean lung cancer prevalence 1.4% and 1.5% respectively. 3

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer‐related mortality. It is often diagnosed at an advanced stage, where treatment options are limited. 4 , 5 , 6 Appropriate management of pulmonary nodules, of which a critical minority may represent early‐stage disease, has potential for significant impacts. 7

Evidence‐based recommendations to guide pulmonary nodule evaluation include those from the Fleischner Society, 8 British Thoracic Society (BTS) 3 and American College of Chest Physicians (ACCP). 9 These recommend assessment of nodule characteristics and patient‐related risk factors. Fleischner Society Guidelines provide recommendations regarding incidentally detected nodules only, whereas BTS and ACCP guidelines can be applied to incidentally and screen‐detected nodules. 10 Within Australia, Fleischner Society Guidelines are widely used and are endorsed by the Thoracic Society of Australia and New Zealand (TSANZ) and Choosing Wisely Australia initiative. 11 Fleischner Society Guidelines for the management of solid nodules were first published in 2005, 12 with further guidance in 2013 to address subsolid nodules 13 and the most recent 2017 iteration incorporating multidisciplinary input. 8

Adherence to guidelines is important in improving patient outcomes and minimising healthcare costs. 14 , 15 Consistency in nodule evaluation across healthcare professionals has been generally poor. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Studies assessing adherence to Fleischner Society Guidelines have largely been conducted from the radiologist perspective and showed variable concordance with recommendations, ranging from 30% to 70%. 19 , 21 , 22 On the other hand, there is a paucity of studies with an emphasis on respiratory physician adherence, in particular, to the most recent iteration of the guidelines. A recent US study (n = 113) in a multidisciplinary nodule clinic using the 2005 Fleischner Society Guidelines demonstrated that only 67% of respiratory physicians recommended guideline‐concordant nodule evaluation. 23 In this study, we examined respiratory physician adherence to the 2017 Fleischner Society Guidelines with respect to the evaluation of newly referred nodules within a dedicated nodule service in a quaternary institution.

Methods

Study population

All new patients assessed in the pulmonary nodule clinic between 1 July 2017 and 1 July 2020, who completed the minimum 24‐month follow‐up, were included. In alignment with Fleischner Society Guidelines, patients over 35 years of age with nodules ≤3 cm in diameter were included (Fig. 1).

Consort flow diagram.

Data collection

Eligible patients were identified from medical records. Demographics, past medical history, family history, occupational exposure history, smoking history and baseline respiratory function were extracted. Lung nodule characteristics including size, shape, appearance and location were gathered from radiology reports. Follow‐up recommendations from initial clinic review were identified from correspondence and categorised as further imaging, bronchoscopy, biopsy, fine needle aspiration, surgical treatment, radiotherapy or discharge from clinic.

Risk stratification

In line with Fleischner Society Guidelines, a dichotomized risk assignment on the probability of malignancy was performed according to categories proposed by ACCP. This involved consideration of nodule‐ and patient‐related factors, including age, smoking history, occupational exposures and existing lung disease(s). 8 , 9 , 24 High‐risk radiological features included larger nodule size, irregular or spiculated margin and upper lobe localisation. As outlined in the Fleischner Society Guidelines, management of nodules must incorporate the clinician's perception of whether the patient is at low risk (<5% chance per year) or high risk (>5% chance per year) of developing lung cancer. The ACCP intermediate‐risk (5%–65% risk) and high‐risk (>65%) categories were combined for estimation of those who were at increased risk for neoplasm (>5% risk). The ACCP low‐risk category corresponded to an estimated cancer risk of less than 5%.

Defining guideline concordance

Clinical decisions on nodule management were considered guideline‐concordant when all of the following criteria were met in alignment with the Fleischner recommendations: (1) decision to proceed with further investigation or discharge from clinic, (2) appropriate choice on modality of investigation such as imaging or tissue sampling and (3) appropriate time interval for follow‐up investigation. Where clinical decisions made by the physician deviated from the Fleischner Society Guidelines, the reasons for their recommendation were assessed to determine the cause. This was further broken down into patient factor and physician's discretion on clinical grounds.

‘Symptomatic’ patients continued to be managed under the Fleischner Society Guidelines and deemed to have received guideline‐concordant evaluation if the clinician believed the symptoms were most likely explained by a non‐malignant pathology such as airways disease, restrictive lung disease, infection or inflammation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using Microsoft Excel (version 2018). The primary outcome was determination of respiratory physicians’ adherence to the 2017 Fleischner Society Guidelines. Secondary outcomes included defining the most common follow‐up recommendation, proportion of patients who ultimately received a malignant diagnosis, number of incidentally discovered malignant lung nodules and other histological or radiological diagnosis where available.

Ethics approval

This study (Study/19/Austin/77) was approved by the Austin Health Human Research Ethics Committee.

Results

Patient characteristics

Three hundred sixty‐one patients, mean age 68 (±12) years, 56% (n = 201) males, were included in the final analysis. One hundred ten patients (30%) were never smokers, 106 (29%) current smokers (44 ± 23 mean pack‐years) and 104 (39%) former smokers (32 ± 24 mean pack‐years). Smoking status was not recorded in three patients. Sixteen per cent of patients reported occupational exposures (Table 1). Eight per cent of the study cohort recounted a family history of malignancy.

Seventy‐seven per cent of patients (n = 278) were asymptomatic and had incidentally detected lung nodules, of whom 140 patients had solitary nodules. Twenty‐two per cent (n = 78) described haemoptysis, new dyspnoea or new cough at the time of referral. Forty‐two per cent had normal ventilatory function, 28% had an obstructive ventilatory defect, 4% had a restrictive ventilatory defect, and 1% had a mixed obstructive/restrictive ventilatory defect. One quarter of patients did not have baseline respiratory function test results at the first clinic assessment.

Nodule characteristics

A majority of nodules (n = 264 cases; 73%) were identified as high risk for malignancy according to Fleischner criteria. Mean nodule size calculated by the average of short and long axes was 12.1 mm ± 6.3 mm and 160 nodules measured >8 mm. One hundred thirty‐eight patients (38.2%) were found to have multiple lung nodules, where 101 patients (73.2%) had at least one nodule classified as high‐risk. Spiculation was noted in 12% of lesions, and upper lobe location in 44% of cases. Nodule characteristics are summarised in Table 2.

First clinic assessment outcome

Following initial clinic assessment, 22 patients were discharged. Of these, eight were considered low‐risk with nodules <6 mm, 10 patients had imaging within the past 9 years demonstrating the same lesion with no interval change, and four patients elected not to have follow‐up due to age and comorbidities.

Continued management was recommended for the remainder. Further imaging with computed tomography (CT) or fluorodeoxyglucose‐positron emission tomography (FDG‐PET) was the most common outcome (n = 316). Of note, 99 patients were triaged on referral to undergo FDG‐PET prior to their first clinic attendance. Twenty patients were recommended to proceed to a tissue sampling procedure. Four patients were referred for definitive treatment with surgical resection or stereotactic ablative body radiation based on high‐risk radiological nodule characteristics (Fig. 2).

Nodule management deviated from Fleischner Society Guidelines in 10 patients. Seven patients (2%) received investigations in excess of recommendations. Two low‐risk patients with nodules <6 mm underwent further imaging, in one case due to significant patient anxiety and in the other due to imminent organ transplant work‐up. The remaining five patients received more intense follow‐up at physician discretion due to other high‐risk radiological features such as lymphadenopathy, multiple lesions, nodule distribution and presentation with weight loss, haemoptysis and unexplained dyspnoea. Of the three patients (0.8%) whose follow‐up was less frequent than recommended, a shared decision was reached due to patient preferences or demonstration of stable lesions compared to prior images. Overall, 97% of follow‐up decisions were in strict alignment with the 2017 Fleischner Society Guidelines. Increased rate of adherence was also observed over time. Lung nodule diagnoses

At the end of the 24‐month follow‐up period, a definitive diagnosis was reached in 135 patients through either pattern of radiological progression or histology/cytology (Fig. 3).

Ninety‐seven patients were diagnosed with cancer, with an average nodule size of 16.8 ± 5.7 mm. Seventy‐eight patients received a tissue diagnosis, while 19 were diagnosed based on radiological morphology and FDG‐PET avidity, with tissue sampling procedures deemed unsafe in the context of patient comorbidities/anaesthetic risks (Table 4). Sixty‐six patients (68%) with malignancy had an incidentally discovered lung nodule. One patient received more intense nodule evaluation due to suspicious symptoms and distant history of malignancy. Eighty‐four patients were diagnosed with lung cancer, including five with metastatic disease, and another 15 were diagnosed with metastatic cancer from a non‐lung cancer origin. Thirty‐two per cent of the former smokers and 25% of the current smokers were diagnosed with cancer. There was no significant difference in relative risk of developing cancer between these two groups of ever smokers (95% CI, 0.84–1.89; P = 0.26). The relative risk of developing lung cancer was 1.8 (95% CI, 1.08–3.01; P = 0.02) in former smokers and 1.6 (95% CI, 0.96–2.84; P = 0.07) in current smokers compared to never smokers. Discussion

Our study demonstrates that in a respiratory physician‐led nodule clinic, there was a high level of concordance with current Fleischner Society Guidelines at first clinic assessment. Further imaging was the most common recommendation thereafter. A high proportion of nodules were malignant, noting that this included 23.7% of those detected incidentally. Over a quarter of ever smokers and over a fifth of never smokers were diagnosed with malignant nodules.

Few studies have assessed physician adherence to nodule guidelines. To our knowledge, this is the first such study in an Australian cohort. In a prospective study assessing US physician adherence to the 2005 Fleischner Society Guidelines, 67% of patients assessed over 3 years received guideline‐concordant care, 23 while a multicentre observational North American study of 337 patients found that physician guideline adherence in dedicated nodule clinics was as low as 49%. 18 In comparison, our study demonstrated highly guideline‐concordant care. In patients receiving more intensive nodule follow‐up, decisions were based on clinical judgement with consideration of risk factors, symptoms, and high‐risk radiological characteristics. Conversely, in those who received less intensive nodule care, the most common reasons were old age, frailty, comorbidities limiting therapies and investigations, and patient preferences. This is not unexpected as it reflects the emphasis on shared decision‐making. 25 , 26

Among the 135 patients with a diagnosis made by the conclusion of the follow‐up period, malignancy was confirmed upon MDM review of histology/cytology in 80% cases, representing 22% of total patients studied. One in five of the incidental nodules referred were malignant. This is consistent with previous studies that report prevalence of malignancy in incidentally detected pulmonary nodules between 2% and 23%. 27 , 28 Importantly, the majority of patients diagnosed with cancer during follow‐up received guideline‐concordant care. 23 , 28 It is important to be cognisant of the potential harms of over‐management where, in a US study, biopsy was performed on 33.2% (n = 125) of patients and 20.4% (n = 77) underwent surgery, of whom 35% (n = 27) had benign lesions. 28 Adherence to guidelines serves to reduce variation in nodule management while improving communication between healthcare professionals, avoiding unwarranted invasive procedures and resulting in better patient outcomes. 29

There was a higher frequency of malignant nodules diagnosed among ever smokers compared to never smokers. No significant difference was found in the rate of malignant lung nodules between former and current smokers. Only 23 never smokers were diagnosed with malignancy (20.9%), but of the 84 patients with primary lung cancer, 20% were never smokers. In their analysis of the Framingham Heart Study (n = 5002) which evaluated the conferred cumulative risk for lung cancer based on smoking history, Tindle et al. reported incidence rates of lung cancers in descending order of current (1.97/1000 person‐years), former (1.61/1000 person‐years) and never smokers (0.26/1000 person‐years). 30 In contrast, our study showed no difference between the proportion of former and current smokers diagnosed with malignant pulmonary nodules. This might be attributable to the small sample size and overlapping mean smoking pack‐year history between the two groups.

Several international guidelines provide recommendations on the evaluation of pulmonary nodules. These differ in their study population, measurement of nodule size and growth, threshold for discharge from follow‐up and management of non‐solid nodules. The Fleischner Society Guidelines are the most recently revised and target incidentally detected nodules. 8 , 24 Nodule size is reported as the average of long and short axes. Further follow‐up is not recommended in nodules <6 mm. On the other hand, the American College of Radiology Lung‐RADS tool (2014) informs the assessment of screen‐detected nodules. Nodules are again evaluated based on the average of the long and short axes. 31 Lesions ≥6 mm are recommended to undergo annual screening rather than discharge. Guidelines published by the BTS (2015) and the ACCP (2013) were directed at incidentally and screen‐detected nodules. 3 , 9 BTS utilises semi‐automated volumetric reporting for nodules, while ACCP guidance largely mirrors Fleischner. Nodules with volumes <8 mm3 can be discharged per BTS, and lesions <5 mm in size averaged from long and short axes per ACCP. Suggested duration of surveillance for non‐solid nodules is annual screening for 5 years (unless >20 mm) for Fleischner, 4 years for Lung‐RADS and 3 years for BTS and ACCP. Despite varied approaches, all guidelines emphasise the importance of accurate risk prediction models. Examples include the Brock model, 32 derived from a screening population and commonly used alongside BTS guidelines, and the Herder model 33 that calculates risks with the aid of PET‐CT findings.

Following the landmark U.S. National Lung Screening Trial (NLST) and the Dutch–Belgian Randomised Lung Cancer Screening Trial (NELSON), which demonstrated mortality benefit from CT screening in high‐risk populations, 34 , 35 Australia has also commenced its own screening programme in 2025. 36 We must now urgently address the question as to which is the most appropriate scheme to guide our local practice in the management of both screening‐ and non‐screening‐detected nodules. Findings from the US DELUGE study suggest that low‐dose CT screening and multidisciplinary nodule programmes are complementary in achieving the goal of early lung cancer detection and treatment. 37 Consideration must be given to cost‐effectiveness, ease of implementation, adherence and applicability to the population. A US study estimated an average downstream management cost of US$860 per nodule associated with over‐management relative to the Fleischner Society Guidelines, which was significantly higher than the cost of guideline‐concordant care (US$292 per nodule). 38 With the launch of the National Lung Cancer Screening Program (NLCSP) in Australia in 2025 36 and a growing number of dedicated nodule clinics, it is vital that approaches to nodule evaluation in Australia continue to be monitored and benchmarked.

Guideline‐concordant management of incidentally detected pulmonary nodules will be crucial in complementing the NLCSP in Australia to ensure early lung cancer detection in those who are at risk but not enrolled in the screening programme. Observational data from the US have highlighted current real‐world challenges associated with lung cancer screening, including low uptake and follow‐up rates, 39 , 40 , 41 poor access to multidisciplinary care in disadvantaged and culturally and linguistically diverse communities, suboptimal delivery in remote regions and among certain racial groups due to language or cultural barriers, as well as exclusion of non‐smokers due to strict entry criteria. 37 Eligibility criteria for the Australian NLCSP include (1) age 50–70 years; (2) current tobacco cigarette smoker or former tobacco cigarette smoker having quite less than 10 years ago; (3) having a history of tobacco cigarette smoking of at least 30 pack‐years and (4) being asymptomatic. 36 Up to 46% of patients diagnosed with lung cancer during follow‐up in dedicated nodule clinics or multidisciplinary programmes were otherwise ineligible for the screening programme. 37 Research has shown the potential for dedicated multidisciplinary nodule programmes to improve rates of early lung cancer diagnosis in up to 33%–38% of incidentally found nodules. 42 , 43 , 44 The key messages echoed in these studies underscore the imperative to address the management of incidental nodules in parallel to formal lung cancer screening programmes in order to drive a stage shift in the detection of early lung cancers that can be treated with curative intent.

This study has limitations. It was a single‐centre study focusing on pulmonary nodule evaluation at first visit to a respiratory physician‐led nodule clinic, which limits its generalisability and reflection of broader practice. Data collected may have been influenced by referral bias as only those who were referred to this nodule clinic could be accounted for. In addition, the overall percentage of malignancy in patients referred for evaluation of pulmonary nodules could be overestimated due to the relatively small sample size. While the Fleischner Society Guidelines are directed at incidentally detected pulmonary nodules, Fleischner recommendations were applied to all nodule cases referred to the clinic in this study irrespective of the presence of symptoms. This was a consensus‐based decision within this nodule clinic, adopted for the purpose of consistency, efficiency and workflow. However, such practice may not necessarily be echoed in other centres. Questions like, for example, the demographics of non‐smokers who were diagnosed with malignant nodules would be better addressed in multicentre studies with a larger sample size. Potential confounding factors may not be adequately accounted for given this is an observational study. Nonetheless, the strength of the study is that it informs on real‐world practice, providing reliable data on a more diverse patient population, including those who were excluded from randomised controlled trials due to their vulnerabilities. 45 , 46 , 47

Conclusion

To our knowledge, this is the first published Australian study assessing respiratory physicians’ adherence to Fleischner Society Guidelines for the management of lung nodules within a structured specialist physician‐led nodule clinic, demonstrating a high level of guideline‐concordant care. With increasing CT scans in the current era, a parallel rise in incidental nodule detection is inevitable. Appropriate nodule management is paramount to reduce patient anxiety, minimise unnecessary investigations and ensure prudent allocation of limited resources.
