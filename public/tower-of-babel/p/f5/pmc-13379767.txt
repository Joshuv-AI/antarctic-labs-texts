Retirement Changed My Laboratory, Not My Mission
Authors: José A. Gil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRetirement need not mark the end of a scientific career. This opinion article describes how the MicroMundo citizen science programme transformed a secondary‐school laboratory into a place where students search for new antibiotics while experiencing authentic scientific research. The experience highlights the educational value of citizen science, promotes microbial literacy and STEM vocations, and illustrates how retired scientists can continue to contribute meaningfully by connecting universities, schools and society.

When I retired after more than four decades as a university microbiologist, I imagined that my days of teaching in laboratories had come to an end. I returned to Torrejoncillo, the small town in western Spain where I was born, looking forward to a quieter life. My plans were simple: to write about the history of science, tend my vegetable garden, prune my vineyard and care for my olive trees.

I did not expect that, only a few months later, I would once again be sterilising inoculating loops, preparing agar plates and discussing bacterial colonies—not with university students or PhD candidates, but with 15‐year‐old teenagers.

An unexpected invitation from the principal of the local secondary school “Via Dalmacia” led me back into a modest school laboratory. It was nothing like the university laboratories I had known throughout my career. Yet it possessed something that some sophisticated research laboratories gradually lose: the excitement of discovery.

At about the same time, colleagues from the Spanish Society for Microbiology encouraged me to establish the MicroMundo programme in Extremadura. The two opportunities complemented each other perfectly.

Looking back, I have come to realise that retirement did not mark the end of my scientific career; rather, it offered a new context in which research, education and public engagement could naturally converge. The timing could hardly have been more appropriate. At a moment when antimicrobial resistance is undermining one of medicine's greatest achievements and the discovery of new antibiotics has slowed dramatically, initiatives such as MicroMundo demonstrate that the search for tomorrow's antibiotics can also become a powerful educational experience. Rather than simply learning about microbiology, students contribute to one of the most pressing biomedical challenges of our time.

A Classroom That Became a Research Laboratory

MicroMundo was created around a simple idea: if discovering new antibiotics is one of the greatest scientific challenges of our time, why not invite students to become part of the search? As part of the international Tiny Earth (Hurley et al. 2021) and Small World Initiative (Davis et al. 2017) networks, the project combines citizen science with education, enabling secondary‐school students to participate in authentic microbiological research while contributing to the global effort to combat antimicrobial resistance.

Rather than performing predefined classroom exercises, students follow the same basic workflow used in antibiotic discovery programmes. They collect soil samples, isolate environmental bacteria, screen them for antimicrobial activity, and contribute their findings to a shared database. Every isolate reminds students that the microbial world still holds countless unexplored molecules—and perhaps the next antibiotic.

Today, more than 30 universities and research centres form the Spanish MicroMundo network. Together, participants have analysed over 10,000 soil samples and screened more than 150,000 bacterial isolates for antimicrobial activity (Gil‐Serna et al. 2025; Gil‐Serna and Cid 2026).

The project has demonstrated that citizen science can generate genuine scientific discoveries. One striking example is the identification of Bacillus altitudinis ECC22, isolated from a soil sample collected by participating students, which led to the discovery of the novel circular bacteriocin altitudin A (Lafuente et al. 2024). At the same time, it has inspired thousands of young people to experience science not as a body of knowledge to be memorised, but as a process of exploration and discovery.

I still remember the excitement in the laboratory when one of the students noticed a clear inhibition halo surrounding a bacterial colony. For a few moments, the laboratory fell silent. Then came the inevitable question: “Could this be a new antibiotic?” That moment captured the essence of scientific discovery.

Long before its existence can be demonstrated, every new antibiotic begins as an idea. For my students, that idea already has a name: Dalmacin.

MicroMundo has taught me that the most valuable discovery is not always a new antibiotic. Sometimes it is the moment when a student realises that scientific knowledge is not simply something to be learned—it is something they can help create.

In this sense, MicroMundo has become an effective vehicle for promoting microbial literacy beyond the laboratory (IMiLI) (Timmis et al. 2024; Ramos et al. 2025). Students have taken part in seminars on antimicrobial resistance, sexually transmitted infections, the human microbiome, and microbial biodiversity; organised exhibitions of bacterial and fungal images; and have participated in a forensic‐style scientific treasure hunt at the Institute of Functional Biology and Genomics (IBFG‐CSIC, Salamanca), where they firsthand explored research laboratories and experienced scientific inquiry. Together, these activities reinforce a simple idea: students do not simply study science—they experience it.

In an age when microorganisms are still widely perceived primarily as agents of disease, helping students understand their essential roles in ecosystems, food production, biotechnology and human health may be just as important as teaching them laboratory techniques.

None of this would have been possible without the enthusiasm of the teachers and staff at IESO Vía Dalmacia, whose commitment transformed a small laboratory into a place where science could flourish.

A Second Life in Science

In many countries, retirement marks the end of formal academic activity but not the end of scientific curiosity. Thousands of experienced researchers leave universities every year carrying with them decades of accumulated knowledge, mentoring skills and laboratory expertise. Finding ways to reconnect this intellectual capital with schools and society may be one of the most overlooked opportunities in science education.

Somewhere among the thousands of soil bacteria collected by secondary‐school students may be the microorganism that produces tomorrow's life‐saving antibiotic. Whether or not that discovery occurs is almost secondary.

What matters is that thousands of young people have already discovered something equally valuable: Science is not something that happens somewhere else. Science is something they can do. Sometimes, it begins with a handful of soil collected on the way home from school.

The smallest laboratory of my career became the place where I could have the greatest educational impact.
