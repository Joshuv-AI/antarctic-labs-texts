Comparative study of trace metal accumulation in native and invasive plant species
Authors: Małgorzata Dambiec, Ludmiła Polechońska, Agnieszka Klink
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGlobal ecosystems are rapidly changing under human pressures such as land-use change, degradation, and trace metal pollution. These conditions often favor invasive plants, yet the links between invasiveness and metal contamination remain insufficiently understood. This study aimed to compare the biogeochemical responses of a native species (Tanacetum vulgare) and an invasive species (Solidago gigantea). Specifically, their capacity for metal uptake and translocation was investigated to assess whether certain traits may facilitate the performance of invasive plants in contaminated sites. Concentrations of Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, Zn, Ni, Fe, and Mn were determined in soils and in the roots and aboveground organs of both species sampled in areas with and without industrial impact. The results showed that both species are capable of inhabiting anthropogenically altered and metal-contaminated sites. Importantly, they both exhibited reduced uptake of metals in polluted soils, indicating the utilization of a metal-excluder strategy. T. vulgare was more likely to restrict metal uptake at the root level, whereas S. gigantea appeared to limit metal translocation to aboveground parts. Moreover, S. gigantea contained significantly lower levels of Cd, Ni, and Pb in its organs than T. vulgare, suggesting greater efficiency in avoiding metal accumulation. These findings support the classification of both species as excluders and highlight the adaptive capacity of invasive species in disturbed environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11356-026-37807-8.Keywords: Solidago gigantea, Tanacetum vulgare, Soil pollution, Industrial areas, Bioaccumulation factor , Translocation factor

Introduction

Global ecosystems are experiencing rapid and extraordinary changes as a result of various human activities, such as land development, pollution, the spread of invasive species, increased CO2 emissions, changes in disturbance regimes, and shifts in climate patterns (Sun et al. 2023). In recent decades, human activities have led to the pollution of numerous regions across the globe. Trace elements are pollutants that raise a specific concern. Among them, metals, being non-degradable, accumulate in soil, sediment, and living organisms, posing a significant risk to both the environment and human health. Industrial processes, agriculture, and urban areas are considered to be the primary sources of trace metal pollution (Kabata-Pendias 2011; Luo et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2025).

Changes in land use, degradation, and human disturbance provide opportunities for invasions (Mashhadi and Radosevich 2004). The invasion of plant species is considered one of the most significant threats to natural biodiversity, disrupting ecosystems worldwide. Recent studies have shown that invasive plant species do not share a common suite of attributes that underlie their invasive abilities, and many different mechanisms can contribute to the success of the invasion (Gulezian and Nyberg 2010). According to recent studies, environmental pollutants can alter ecosystem composition and functioning and thus play a significant role in shaping the invasiveness of invasive species (Sun et al. 2023). Certain invasive plants thrive in challenging environmental and stressful conditions due to their fast growth, high reproductive capacity, and specialized physiological adaptations. As a result, they overtake native species and disrupt ecosystem processes. Plant invasion is frequently driven by certain traits that allow invasive species to achieve dominance within a community. However, the mechanisms behind plant invasions in relation to soil-chemical pollution are still not well understood. Invasive species may have beneficial characteristics that help them either avoid or tolerate chemical pollution more effectively, potentially making them less vulnerable to pollutants compared to non-invasive species (Lozano and Rillig 2024; Sun et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2020). Chen et al. (2024), based on the results of a greenhouse experiment, suggest that adding trace elements (Cu 102.29 mg kg−1 and Zn 148.32 mg kg−1) weakened the competitiveness of invasive species. Nonetheless, trace-element additions did not change the outcome of competition, consistently favoring invasion successfully. Some researchers suggest that plant invasions are driven more by environmental conditions, particularly those resulting from human disturbance, than by the plants’ own invasiveness. For example, heavy-metal contamination can have a positive or negative impact on invading plants. Although some studies have demonstrated the influence of trace metals on invasive plants, the mechanisms underlying interactions between invasive species and environmental pollutants, including metals, remain poorly understood (Li et al. 2021; Lozano and Rillig 2024; Sun et al. 2023). There is still no consensus on whether the elemental composition of invasive plants differs from that of native species in polluted environments. Therefore, quantitative studies comparing contaminant contents in plants, litter, and soils associated with native and invasive species at a global scale remain an important direction for future research (Li et al. 2022).

Despite numerous studies on metal accumulation in invasive plant species, comparative analyses of native and invasive species growing under the same environmental conditions remain limited. Therefore, this study aimed to compare two species (native and invasive) in terms of metal uptake and translocation in polluted and unpolluted areas. Further we aimed to determine their metal accumulation strategies that facilitate their colonization and thriving in human-affected areas. Two plants representing each group were chosen: the native Tanacetum vulgare L. and the alien, invasive Solidago gigantea Aiton., which are both perennial, herbaceous flowering plants from the family Asteraceae. Solidago gigantea is native to North America and is considered an exceptionally successful invader worldwide (Szymura and Szymura 2013). A growing number of studies on Solidago gigantea indicate that this species can colonize highly polluted, human-altered areas, usually maintaining a relatively low level of metals in roots and shoots (Bąba et al. 2014; Dambiec et al. 2022; Kowalska et al. 2012; Wójcik et al. 2014) and significantly alter habitat conditions, including soil properties (Bobuľská et al. 2019; Stefanowicz et al. 2017). Tanacetum vulgare, on the other hand, is a widely distributed, common plant native to Europe and Asia, where it grows along roadsides, hedgerows, and waste places (Aćimović and Puvača 2020). Its wide distribution indicates a high degree of adaptability and ecological plasticity in variable environmental conditions (Adamcová et al. 2017). This species readily colonizes disturbed areas, including lignite mine waste dumps characterized by the following trace metal concentrations: Cd 0.2, Cr 16, Cu 13, Fe 7340, Mn 280, Ni 34, Pb 60, and Zn 41 mg kg−1 (Jasion et al. 2013). Metal accumulation and translocation in T. vulgare have been relatively well described. Tanacetum vulgare is known to effectively accumulate metals such as Cd, Hg, Cr, Ni, Fe, Mn, and Zn, making it a useful ecological indicator of metal contamination in industrial environments (Jasion et al. 2013). The species has also been used in phytoremediation of a wide range of pollutants in both urban (Nikolić and Stevović, 2015) and industrial areas (Stevović et al. 2010). Tanacetum vulgare occurs in habitats similar to those of S. gigantea and has been recognized as a good bioindicator of environmental pollution, particularly trace metals which makes it suitable for comparative studies with S. gigantea.

Methods

Sampling

Soil and plant samples were collected during the peak of the vegetation season (September 2024), with plants sampled at the flowering stage, from 30 study sites located in Lower Silesia, SW Poland. Seventeen sites (14–30) were situated in residential or agricultural areas, away from sources of industrial pollution (unpolluted study sites) and 13 sites (1–13) were affected by emissions from various industrial plants (polluted study sites) (Fig. 1, Tab. S1). The main industrial sources of trace metal in study areas were as follows: non-ferrous metal processing plant (study sites 1–5), cogeneration plant and heap left from the former ferrochrome steel facility (6–10), ironworks processing Zn, Pb and Cd alloys (11–13). In each sampling site a square of 25 m2 was chosen, where both T. vulgare and S. gigantea occurred simultaneously. Ten mature (in generative stage), undamaged individuals of each species and 5 sub-samples of soil from the surface (0 to 10 cm in depth) were collected randomly within each sampling square. Soil sub-samples were then mixed into one sample (about 1 kg).

Location of the study sites: unpolluted (14–30) and polluted (1–13) (basemap source: OpenStreetMap Standard, 05/2025)

Chemical analyses

Plant samples were thoroughly washed with tap water and rinsed with deionized water to remove any particles attached to the surface of the plants. Then the plants were divided into roots, stems and leaves and oven-dried (70 °C) to a constant weight (Kalra 2019). The dried samples were ground into fine powder using a POLYMIXPX-MFC 90 D laboratory mill (Kinematica AG, Switzerland). Air-dried soil samples were cleaned of stones and organic remains, sieved through a 2-mm sieve and homogenized in a Pulverisette 2 laboratory grinder (FRITSH GmbH Mahlen und Messen, Germany).

Soil pH was determined potentiometrically (soil to distilled water ratio of 1:2.5) using an HI9107 microprocessor pH meter (Hanna Instruments, USA). To determine the content of bioavailable P fractions as well as Ca, K and Mg in soil, samples were extracted with Mehlich 3 reagent (Benton Jones 2001) and P was analyzed using a flow-injection analyzer (FIA compact, MLE GmbH) while Ca, K and Mg were analyzed by flame absorption spectrophotometry (FAAS). Carbon content was determined in a combustion furnace LECO CS (LECO Corporate). Soil and plant samples (0.5 g) were digested with nitric acid (ultra-pure, 5 mL, 65%) and hydrogen peroxide (ultra-pure, 0.5 mL, 30%) in an open system (95 °C). The digests were diluted to 50 mL with deionized water and then analyzed for Fe, Mn and Zn using flame atomic absorption spectrophotometry (FAAS) and for Cd, Cr, Cu, Ni and Pb using electrothermal atomic absorption spectrometry (ETAAS) with a GF3000 Graphite Furnace (AVANTA PM AAS, GBC Scientific Equipment, Australia) (Kalra 2019). The bioavailable fraction of trace metals in soils was determined after DTPA-extraction. The extracts were prepared by shaking air-dried soils with extracting solution (0.005 M DTPA, 0.01 M CaCl2 and 0.1 M TEA adjusted to pH 7.3), filtered (Benton Jones 2001) and analyzed for metals using the AAS methods described above. All the elements were tested against atomic absorption spectrometry standard solutions (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and blanks. The results were calculated on a dry-weight basis. The accuracy of the methods was checked against certified reference materials: IPE 952 Grass (mixture)/Poaceae (Wageningen Evaluating Programs for Analytical Laboratories, The Netherlands) for plants and METRANALQCM 31 light sandy soil (trace elements) (ANALYTIKA® spol. s r.o., Czech Republic) for soil samples. The recovery rates (percent) for IPE 952 were as follows: Cd 97.5, Cr 93.3, Cu 103, Fe 101, Mn 97.3, Ni 94.2, Pb 97.3, Zn 98.4 and for METRANALQCM 31: Cd 96.8, Cr 102, Cu 104, Fe 99.2, Mn 97.0, Ni 106, Pb 107, Zn 94.7.

Data analyses

The Shapiro–Wilk W-test was conducted to assess the normality of the data (Zar 1999). As the variables did not follow a normal distribution, non-parametric tests were selected. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to determine the differences in terms of element contents between the two groups of study sites (unpolluted and polluted) in soil and plants and between the two species (T. vulgare and S. gigantea) within the same group of study sites. The differences in metal contents between the organs within each species were determined using a non-parametric Kruskal–Wallis ANOVA with a median post hoc test. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Following the Box-Cox transformation of the variables, the matrices containing the concentrations of 8 elements (Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, Pb, Zn) in plant organs from 30 locations were analyzed using principal component and classification analysis (PCCA) (Legendre and Legendre 1998) to identify potential gradients in element levels.

The Pollution Load Index (PLI) was used to assess the overall level of trace-metal contamination in soils at the study sites. It was calculated as the geometric mean of contamination factors determined for individual metals (Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, Mn, Ni, Pb, Zn) (Hołtra and Zamorska-Wojdyła 2020). The contamination factor expressed the ratio between the measured metal concentration in soil and a reference value derived from geochemical background data for Polish soils (Lis et al. 2012).

To assess the capacity of T. vulgare and S. gigantea to absorb elements from the soil, the bioaccumulation factor (BF) (Bidar et al. 2009) was determined by calculating the ratio of metal concentrations in the plant roots to the bioavailable fraction of those metals in the soil. Values greater than one suggest the plant’s capacity to absorb metals from the soil (Galal and Shehata 2015). The translocation factor (TF), which evaluates the mobility of metals within the plant and their internal transport, was calculated as the ratio of metal concentrations in the stems or leaves to those in the roots (Bonanno et al. 2018; Bidar et al. 2009).

The bioaccumulation coefficient (BAC) was calculated as the ratio of metal concentrations in plants’ aboveground parts (separately for leaves and stems) to those in the soil (bioavailable forms). It enables categorization of plants as accumulators (BAC > 1) or excluders (BAC < 1) of trace elements (Hołtra and Zamorska-Wojdyła 2020).

All calculations were done with Statistica 13 (TIBCO Software Inc. 2016).

Results and discussion

Soil properties and metal concentrations

Metal contents in soil at the study sites are given in Table 1. Significantly higher total concentrations of trace metals (Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ni, Pb, and Zn) were found in soil in polluted sites compared to unpolluted ones. Only total Mn concentrations did not differ significantly between these two groups (Table 1). The median Mn and Fe contents in both groups of the study sites did not exceed the mean content in soils in Poland (399 and 12,000 mg kg−1, respectively) (Kabata-Pendias 2011) and the geochemical background (370 and 16,000 mg kg−1, respectively mg kg−1) (Lis et al. 2012), which indicated no Mn and Fe pollution in either group. For other metals, mean contents in soil in polluted sites were remarkably higher than the mean content in soils in Poland and the geochemical background, indicating serious metal pollution (Table 1). High concentrations of Cd, Cu, Pb, and Zn in the soils in the vicinity of study sites 1–5 (polluted) were previously noted by Meinhardt (2016) and are related to the impact of industrial processing of copper products and their alloys. Other polluted study sites were located near the zinc smelter (sites 11–13), which emitted metalliferous dust responsible for Zn and Pb pollution (Cuske et al. 2013), or in the vicinity of the slag heap remaining from the former ferrochrome smelter (sites 6–10), which emitted trace metals, including Cr (Karczewska and Bortniak 2008). The pollution of these study sites is confirmed by the PLI values; all these sites were classified as moderately or very heavily polluted (Fig. S1). On the other hand, metal contents in unpolluted study sites did not exceed the mean content in soils in Poland and the geochemical background (Cd, Cr, Fe, Mn, Zn) or were only slightly higher (Cu, Ni, Pb), which suggests that only some sites had slight metal pollution. Unpolluted study sites (14–30) were characterized by PLI lower than 1 (indicating no pollution) or lower than 2 (indicating no to medium pollution) (Fig. S1).

The ability of both T. vulgare and S. gigantea to inhabit strongly polluted sites, where metal content in the soil was significantly higher than the geochemical background, was confirmed by other studies. Jasion et al. (2013) found T. vulgare in brown coal mine areas. Adamcová et al. (2017), studying T. vulgare growing on landfill bodies, found that this species tolerated high metal content in soil, ranging: Cd 0.067–0.204; Cr 64.1–191; Cu 32.4–58.6; Fe 12,389–39,382; Mn 513–701; Ni 32.7–140; Pb 2.6–8.5; and Zn 25.7–72.5 mg kg−1. Stevović et al. (2010) reported similarly high as in our study metal contents in soil in T. vulgare stands from industrial and recreational areas (Cd 2.1–8.3; Cr 4.6–15.4; Ni 4.3–30.3; and Pb 6–125 mg kg−1). Similarly, S. gigantea was reported from strongly polluted sites, e.g., Zn–Pb waste deposits (Cd 66–1464; Pb 1390–2226; Zn 7300–171790 mg kg−1) (Wójcik et al. 2014), industrial areas (Cr 140; Cu 110; Ni 80; Pb 70 mg kg−1) (Kowalska et al. 2012), lead and zinc spoil heap (total Cd 75.7, Cu 92, Pb 4365, Zn 13,070 mg kg−1, bioavailable Cd 1.1, Cu 0.09, Pb 0.8, Zn 13.8 mg kg−1) (Bąba et al. 2014).

The total metal content in the soil indicates the degree of metal saturation within the soil matrix. However, organisms are impacted only by the bioavailable fraction of the total metal content, while the sequestered or irreversibly bound metals within the soil matrix pose no direct adverse effects (Kim et al. 2015). The bioavailability of metals is determined by several factors, among which soil organic matter content, pH, eH, clay content, nutrient balance, concentration of other elements, soil moisture and temperature are the most important (Bąba et al. 2014, Peijnenburg 2025). The contents of elements which can potentially affect metal bioavailability in studied soils are given in Table 1. A significantly higher soil carbon content was observed in polluted sites compared to the unpolluted sites (Table 1), potentially influencing the bioavailability of trace metals as high levels of organic matter in soil are considered to reduce the bioavailability of metals for plants (Bąba et al. 2014). Calcium and Mg contents also differed significantly between the groups (Table 1). However, no significant differences were found for other parameters potentially affecting metal bioavailability, including P and K contents and soil pH.

Median soil pH in unpolluted study sites ranged from 5.7 to 7.7, while in polluted sites—from 6.0 to 7.7 (Table 1). According to the literature, both S. gigantea and T. vulgare tolerate a wide range of soil pH. The soil pH ranges reported by Bąba et al. (2014), Bobuľská et al. (2019), Stefanowicz et al. (2017) and Szymura and Szymura (2013) for habitats occupied by S. gigantea were similar to those observed in our study. Tanacetum vulgare was found in soils with pH ranging from 5 to 7.5 (Nurzyńska-Wierdak et al. 2022) as well as in brown coal mine areas where soil pH ranged from 4 to 8.4 (Jasion et al. 2013).

Since some differences in soil properties were observed between the polluted and unpolluted study sites, which may influence the bioavailability of trace metals to plants, the concentrations of bioavailable forms were determined in the soils (Table 1). In terms of bioavailable fractions, metal concentrations were significantly higher at the polluted sites than at the unpolluted ones, except for Mn, Fe, and Ni. Manganese bioavailable content in soil was higher in unpolluted study sites, and Fe and Ni bioavailable contents did not differ significantly between the two groups (Table 1).

Metal content in plants

Trace metal content in the organs of T. vulgare and S. gigantea in the polluted and unpolluted study sites is given in Table 2. The concentrations of some elements in the roots of the studied plants in polluted sites were higher than in unpolluted sites; however, they cannot be considered toxic as they were mainly within the ranges of the average levels in plants (Markert 1992). Only Cr in both species and Cu in S. gigantea were slightly higher than the average concentration of these metals in plants given by Markert (1992). The content of Cd in S. gigantea was slightly higher than the average levels in plants, while in T. vulgare it was about 3 times higher than the average content in plants (Markert 1992), with the maximum being as much as 10 times higher than the average content. Solidago gigantea was characterized by significantly higher content of Cu and Fe in roots than T. vulgare, while T. vulgare contained more Pb and Zn but only in unpolluted sites.

In both species studied, the content of trace elements in aboveground organs (stems and leaves) in polluted and unpolluted sites was mainly within the ranges of normal contents of metals in the leaves of plants given by Kabata-Pendias (2011) (mg kg−1): Cd (0.05–0.2), Cr (0.1–0.5), Cu (5–30), Fe (18–1000), Mn (30–300), Ni (0,1–5), Pb (5–10), and Zn (27–150). Only the Cd content in stems and leaves of T. vulgare was higher. However, as high Cd content in T. vulgare was observed both in unpolluted and polluted areas, it can be assumed that it is typical for this species. This is in accordance with Jasion et al. (2013) who reported that T. vulgare growing in a relatively clean site accumulated high amounts of Cd. Wójcik et al. (2014) reported lower trace metal concentrations in S. gigantea than in co-occurring species on Zn–Pb waste deposits. Metal concentrations in shoots were generally comparable to our results, except for higher Pb levels (mg kg−1): Zn 80; Pb 12.6; Cd 0.9; Cu 6.2; Ni 0.3; and Cr 0.3. On the other hand, Bąba et al. (2014) noticed remarkably higher contents of metals in the leaves of S. gigantea in a lead and zinc spoil heap (Cd 8.5, Cu 8.8, Pb 89.5, Zn 454 mg kg−1) indicating high bioaccumulation capacity in case of high exposure.

In unpolluted sites, the uptake efficiency from soil to roots of most trace metals was of similar order of magnitude in T. vulgare and S. gigantea (bioaccumulation factor values in Fig. 2). Solidago gigantea took up Cu, Cr, Fe, and Zn slightly more effectively than T. vulgare while T. vulgare took up Ni slightly better and Cd much better than S. gigantea. The differences in the uptake efficiency were confirmed by significant differences in metal contents in roots between these two species (Table 2). The remarkable ability of T. vulgare to absorb Cd was previously observed by Jasion et al. (2013), who reported BF values for Cd as high as 15 and 45 in plants growing in control areas and colonizing open-pit lignite mines, respectively. Adamcová et al. (2017) noted that Cd showed the highest degree of accumulation among all the metals studied. Both species were characterized by BF values higher than one for Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, and Zn, which indicates their ability to take up these elements from the soil (Galal and Shehata 2015). Manganese, Ni, and Pb were taken up much less intensively (BF values lower than 1). This partially agrees with the general behavioral properties of trace metals in soil media given by Kabata-Pendias (2011), who states that Cd, Cu, Mn, Ni, and Zn are readily taken up by plants, while Cr and Pb are slightly soluble in soil solution and thus not easily taken up by plants.

Mean values of the bioaccumulation factor (BF) (root-to-soil ratio) of elements in Solidago gigantea and Tanacetum vulgare for bioavailable metal concentration in soil in unpolluted and polluted study sites

Bioaccumulation factor values (Fig. 2) for most metals (Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb and Zn) in S. gigantea and T. vulgare growing in polluted sites were lower than in the same species growing in unpolluted sites. This suggests that both species were able to reduce the uptake efficiency of these elements from soil with high metal concentrations. A similar phenomenon was reported by Jasion et al. (2013) for BF values in T. vulgare growing in areas with varying levels of pollution and by Bielecka and Królak (2019) for Pb and Zn accumulation in S. canadensis. A species closely related to S. gigantea, Solidago canadensis, was suggested to be able to exclude Pb or reduce the uptake of Pb when growing in Pb-polluted soil (Yang et al. 2007).

Despite the lower intensity of metal uptake from soils in polluted sites than in unpolluted sites, the content of Cd, Cu, Ni in S. gigantea roots and Pb and Zn in the roots of both species growing in polluted areas were higher than in unpolluted areas (Table 2). This pattern can be seen on the projection of the variables on the factor plane in the principal component and classification analysis (PCCA) ordination for roots (Fig. 3). Despite the fact that the samples did not form clearly separated groups, most of the root samples of both species from unpolluted sites were characterized by positive scores of factor 1, negatively correlated with concentrations of Cd, Pb and Zn. Factor 2 was correlated negatively with Cu, Fe, Ni, Pb, and Zn concentrations. Samples of S. gigantea from polluted sites were generally characterized by negative scores of factor 2 and most of the samples of this species from unpolluted sites had positive values of factor 2. Samples of T. vulgare from polluted sites were mostly characterized by positive scores of factor 2 and negative scores of factor 1 (positively correlated with Cr, Fe, and Mn concentrations).

The translocation factor values (Fig. 4) for leaves in T. vulgare from unpolluted sites were higher than 1 for Cu, Mn, Ni, Pb, and Zn, and close to 1 for Cd. TF values for stems, on the other hand, were lower than 1 for all metals except Zn, indicating that the plant mainly translocated the absorbed metals to the leaves rather than to the stems. Only Cr and Fe were clearly retained in the roots (TF < 1), resulting in significantly higher concentrations of these metals in the roots of T. vulgare compared to aboveground organs (Table 2). Copper concentrations were significantly higher in leaves and roots than in stems, while Mn, Ni, Pb, and Zn concentrations were significantly highest in leaves. The concentration of Cd (TF close to 1) did not differ significantly among the studied organs. According to Kabata-Pendias (2011), the distribution and accumulation patterns of trace elements vary considerably for each element, kind of plant, and growth season. However, it also depends on the electrochemical variables of the elements. In general, Mn, Ni, Cd, and Zn can be considered moderately mobile, and Cu, Cr, Pb, and Fe are strongly bound in root cells.

Mean values of the translocation factor (TF) (leaf-to-root ratio and stem-to-root ratio) of elements in Solidago gigantea and Tanacetum vulgare in unpolluted and polluted study sites

In polluted sites, similar trends in metals translocation were observed as in unpolluted areas; however, Cd was more actively translocated to the leaves of T. vulgare (TF > 1), while Pb was more strongly retained in the roots (TF < 1). The TF value for Zn in stems was also markedly lower. Comparing metal concentrations among plant organs from polluted sites indicates that, similarly to unpolluted sites, roots contained significantly higher concentrations of Cr and Fe than aboveground organs, leaves had significantly higher concentrations of Zn and Mn, while concentrations of Cu, Ni, and Pb did not differ between roots and leaves and were significantly higher in these organs than in stems. Similarly, as in this study, Jasion et al. (2013) reported that Cr and Fe were mostly retained in the roots, and Cd, Cu, Mn, Ni, Pb, Zn were translocated to the shoots of T. vulgare. However, Stevović et al. (2010) noticed that Cd and Pb content decreased in the order: leaves > roots > stems while Cr and Ni content decreased in the order: roots > stems > leaves, which only partially agrees with our results. According to Adamcová et al. (2017) Cd, Cr, Ni and Zn are accumulated mostly in the leaves and Cu, Fe, Mn and Pb are accumulated mostly in the roots of T. vulgare.

Unlike T. vulgare, S. gigantea growing in both unpolluted and polluted study sites retained most metals in roots (TF < 1). Only Mn was actively translocated to leaves, and Zn both to stems and leaves (TF > 1). Manganese and Zn are essential micronutrients for plant growth and therefore, both of these elements are actively transported to the leaves (Wang et al. 2019). Remarkably high TF values compared to other metals were noticed for Pb (close to 1 in leaves and above 1 in stems), but only in unpolluted areas. Despite the high TF value for Pb, the concentration of this metal in leaves and stems of S. gigantea did not exceed the normal value in plants given by Kabata-Pendias (2011). For some invasive plants including S. gigantea, high root retention of heavy metals was previously reported (Li et al. 2021). In line with our results, Abramowicz and Rahmonov (2024) found TF values < 1 for Fe, Cu, Pb, Ni, and Cr, and > 1 for Mn and Cd in S. canadensis from coal-waste dumps. For Zn, values varied depending on site conditions. The limited mobility of elements between roots and aboveground organs is considered the mechanism of resistance to trace metal pollution in excluder plants (Baker 1981; Mandzhieva et al. 2016).

Differences in trace metal content in stems and leaves between the studied plants were presented using PCCA (Figs. 5 and 6). An ordination plot based on the content of elements in stem samples clearly discriminated between T. vulgare (characterized by negative scores of factor 2) and S. gigantea (characterized by positive scores of factor 2). Factor 2 was correlated mainly with Cd and Fe concentrations (positively). This is in accordance with Table 2, where significant differences in Cd and Fe content in stems between T. vulgare and S. gigantea can be seen. Factor 1 discriminated between samples from unpolluted and from polluted sites; however, the groups were not clearly separated. Both S. gigantea and T. vulgare from polluted sites were characterized mainly by positive scores of factor 1 (correlated with high Zn concentration), and from sites not affected by industry—mainly by negative scores of the same factor (correlated with high Mn concentration).

Significantly higher concentrations of Cd, Cu, Ni, Pb and Zn in T. vulgare in unpolluted sites and of Cd, Ni and Pb in polluted sites compared with S. gigantea are shown in Table 2. Samples from unpolluted and polluted sites within the same species were much less clearly separated. This is consistent with the results shown in Table 2, which indicated significant differences only in the concentrations of Cu, Mn, and Zn for S. gigantea and Fe, Mn, and Zn for T. vulgare.

The results of our study indicate that even high levels of metal pollution in the soil do not significantly affect the concentrations of these elements in the aboveground parts of either species. This suggests that both species can be classified as excluder plants (Baker 1981). Bioaccumulation coefficients (BAC) (Table 3) exceeding 1 were observed only at unpolluted sites, and solely for elements that are essential micronutrients for plant growth (Cu and Zn) in both studied species. Exceptionally high accumulation of Cd in T. vulgare was further confirmed by BAC values above 1 for this metal. At polluted sites, BAC values were generally below 1 (except for Cd and Ni in T. vulgare), which supports the classification of both T. vulgare and S. gigantea as metal excluders (Hołtra and Zamorska-Wojdyła 2020). It is also worth noting that, for most metals, the BAC values were similar in both species, suggesting comparable efficiency in limiting the translocation of toxic elements to the aboveground tissues. While both species accumulate potentially toxic elements at relatively low levels, the results indicate differences in metal uptake and translocation between the native T. vulgare and invasive S. gigantea. These differences may be related to species-specific traits, mainly physiological characteristics (Li et al. 2022) which influence their ability to cope with metal pollution. Tanacetum vulgare was more likely to restrict metal uptake at the root level, whereas S. gigantea was more likely to limit the translocation of metals to the leaves. Although the defense mechanisms against elevated metal content appear to differ between T. vulgare and S. gigantea, both species ultimately maintain similar, near-natural metal concentrations in their organs, including the leaves, which are important for assimilation (Kabata-Pendias 2011). However, S. gigantea appears to be slightly more effective in maintaining low levels of toxic metals in its assimilatory organs, as the concentrations of Cd, Ni, and Pb in its leaves were significantly lower than in those of T. vulgare. These findings contribute to current research on the relationship between plant invasiveness and environmental pollution and may help to better understand the biogeochemical behavior of invasive species in polluted environments.

Conclusions

It was found that both T. vulgare and S. gigantea were able to inhabit habitats that are heavily transformed and polluted by human activity. Importantly, both species were able to reduce the uptake efficiency of several metals (Cd, Cr, Cu, Pb, Zn) from soil with high metal concentrations, which suggests that they have developed mechanisms to protect themselves from an excess of trace metals. The concentrations of the majority of metals studied (Fe, Mn, Ni, Pb, Zn) in both species were close to the natural contents of these elements in plants and did not exceed the toxicity threshold. Tanacetum vulgare appears to limit the uptake of most trace metals (Cu, Cr, Fe, Pb, Zn) by roots, but the translocation of these elements to aboveground parts, especially to the leaves, is less limited. Only concentrations of physiologically essential elements such as Zn and Mn were higher in leaves than in roots. Solidago gigantea, on the other hand, can likely be classified as an excluder plant, whose resistance to trace metal pollution is based on the limited mobility of elements between roots and aboveground organs. The concentrations of the majority of trace metals (Cd, Cr, Cu, Fe, Ni, Pb) were the highest in the roots of this species. Such a mechanism may be advantageous for invasive plants to conquer anthropogenic habitats and polluted areas where they are exposed to high levels of toxic metals. What is more, S. gigantea was characterized by significantly lower content of some toxic metals (Cd, Ni, Pb) in its organs than T. vulgare.

Although the overall tolerance of both species to trace metal pollution appears comparable, S. gigantea seems to restrict the translocation of some metals to assimilatory organs more effectively than T. vulgare. Consequently, this invasive species shows a comparable—and for some elements even greater—capacity to avoid toxic trace metal accumulation and to thrive in heavily human-transformed environments. This finding is particularly noteworthy given that the native T. vulgare is widely recognized for its high tolerance to metal pollution and its frequent occurrence in anthropogenically disturbed and contaminated habitats.
