Electroencephalography (EEG) has been with us for almost a century. Since Hans Berger first recorded the human EEG in 1929, this deceptively simple measurement of scalp potentials has shaped modern neurology in a way few diagnostic tools ever have. 1 Long before computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging transformed structural brain imaging, EEG offered the first noninvasive window into human brain function. It became indispensable in the diagnosis of epilepsy, the classification of seizure types, the localization of epileptogenic zones, and ultimately in enabling epilepsy surgery, often curative, for thousands of patients worldwide. 2

Even today, in an era dominated by high‐resolution imaging, EEG remains uniquely valuable. It is inexpensive, widely available, repeatable, portable, and provides real‐time measurement of physiological brain processes. No other clinical tool captures brain function with such temporal resolution. Beyond routine scalp EEG, the spectrum now extends from high‐density whole‐brain recordings to intracranial stereo‐EEG, electrocorticography, and even single‐neuron recordings. Few techniques span such scales, from macroscopic networks to individual neurons, while remaining clinically relevant. In neuroscience research, the value of EEG is immense. It allows repeated longitudinal measurements, pharmacodynamic assessment, sleep and vigilance monitoring, and the study of large‐scale brain dynamics in health and disease. Yet, despite all this progress, the answer we expect from a routine EEG in daily clinical practice has remained the same since Berger's time.

Beyond diagnosis and prognosis, EEG also holds unrealized potential as an active therapeutic tool. When combined with neuromodulation strategies, closed‐loop stimulation, neurofeedback, and rehabilitation paradigms, EEG‐derived signals can be used not only to observe brain dysfunction but to actively shape recovery, plasticity, and functional reorganization. In this context, EEG becomes not merely a diagnostic instrument but a bidirectional interface capable of guiding treatment, monitoring response, and enabling personalized neurorestorative interventions.

A CENTURY‐OLD QUESTION ASKED WITH 21ST‐CENTURY TOOLS

The typical EEG report today is descriptive. A trained neurologist visually inspects waveforms and writes descriptions such as “symmetric posterior alpha rhythm,” “intermittent right frontal slowing,” or “left temporal sharp waves.” Occasionally, an interpretative label, “normal” or “abnormal,” is added. This approach relies almost entirely on expert pattern recognition. It is labor‐intensive, subjective, and dependent on years of experience. More importantly, although International Federation of Clinical Neurophysiology and American Clinical Neurophysiology Society guidelines mandate that each EEG report include an interpretive impression addressing the clinical question, in practice this impression is commonly framed in descriptive EEG terminology rather than as a direct, quantitative clinical prediction. Interrater agreement between EEG experts on pattern‐level visual interpretation of short EEG segments is notoriously low, even among experienced epileptologists. 3 , 4 However, when experts evaluate entire routine recordings using standardized common data element systems such as SCORE, agreement on the diagnostic significance of EEGs has been shown to be substantial to almost perfect. 5 , 6 This distinction itself underscores our argument: shifting from pattern description to diagnostic classification improves reliability. If artificial intelligence (AI) systems are trained merely to replicate the pattern‐level reading, they inherit its ceiling of uncertainty. And yet, most contemporary EEG‐AI efforts aim precisely for this: to replicate or approximate the human expert. A notable exception is SCORE‐AI, which was trained to determine the diagnostic significance of entire EEG recordings and was validated against external clinical diagnostic standards, exemplifying the kind of paradigm shift we advocate. 5 , 6 Automated spike detectors, background classifiers, or AI‐based “EEG scores” are designed to be as good as the neurologist. This is often framed as progress. We argue that this ambition is far too modest.

WHY AIM LOW?

Why should AI strive to reproduce a 100‐year‐old descriptive paradigm when it is capable of much more? As neurologists, and even more so as patients, we do not ultimately care whether alpha rhythms are symmetric or whether slowing is frontal or temporal. These are intermediate observations, not answers. What we actually want to know is: Does this patient have epilepsy? What is the risk of future seizures? Will this patient recover consciousness? Is this dementia, and if so, which type? Is this Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease? Is this encephalopathy reversible?

EEG, by its nature, reflects global and local brain physiology. Advances in machine learning, deep neural networks, and large‐scale data integration allow us to compare an individual EEG not to one expert's mental template, but to hundreds of thousands of EEGs linked to real clinical outcomes. This is a fundamentally different epistemological approach.

Contemporary AI systems do not operate on features recognizable to the human eye. Through tokenization, high‐dimensional embeddings, and representations derived directly from raw EEG data, these models extract information that is inaccessible to traditional visual inspection or classical EEG terminology. As a result, clinicians are increasingly confronted with accurate yet opaque answers: powerful predictions derived from vast feature spaces that are largely uninterpretable and clinically meaningless at the level of individual features.

This introduces a paradox. As EEG‐AI models grow increasingly complex and rely on thousands of distributed features, the underlying neurophysiology may recede rather than be clarified. Many models achieve impressive classification performance through correlations that are statistically robust but biologically opaque. In such settings, the physiological mechanisms and causal pathways through which diseases emerge, propagate, and respond to treatment are no longer directly interrogated or even identifiable. We risk replacing mechanistic understanding with predictive accuracy, highly correlated outputs without insight into why the brain fails, how pathology is initiated, or which pathways might be therapeutically modulated.

Large EEG databases already exist, and others are rapidly emerging. Quantitative EEG, connectivity analysis, complexity metrics, and nonlinear dynamics have demonstrated sensitivity to neurodegenerative disease, coma outcomes, dementia subtypes, and pharmacological effects. 7 , 8 Classification performance for established Alzheimer disease and dementia with Lewy bodies reaches area under the curve values of approximately .80, with characteristic slowing, reduced complexity, and altered connectivity patterns. 9 EEG changes have been documented in Parkinson disease, Huntington disease, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, and Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, often reflecting underlying pathophysiology long before structural imaging becomes diagnostic. Yet, in clinical routine, these insights remain largely unused.

FROM PATTERN RECOGNITION TO DIAGNOSTIC ANSWERS

The central limitation is not EEG. It is our expectations. We continue to ask EEG the wrong question: “What do you look like?” instead of “What do you mean?” AI allows us to invert the process. Instead of describing patterns and leaving interpretation to the clinician, EEG‐AI systems could be trained end‐to‐end to answer clinically meaningful questions directly: probability of epilepsy versus nonepileptic events, risk stratification for seizure recurrence, likelihood of awakening from coma, probability distribution across dementia subtypes, or estimated disease stage or trajectory. This does not eliminate the neurologist, the neurophysiologist, or the need for comprehensive EEG training, which remains essential both for meaningful oversight of AI systems and as an indispensable competency when AI tools are unavailable. On the contrary, it elevates the role of clinical judgment, contextualization, and ethical decision‐making. The neurologist interprets answers, not waveforms. EEG data can be analyzed using novel information–theoretic frameworks, as exemplified by Shor et al., 10 using mathematical tools inspired by quantum information theory applied to routine clinical EEG recordings. By capturing contextual and distributed properties of brain information processing, this approach demonstrates that clinically meaningful diagnostic distinctions can emerge from nonclassical informational representations, rather than from traditional waveform features alone. 10 Furthermore, recent developments in high‐density EEG have enabled detailed brain mapping and analysis of brain connectivity, providing new opportunities for identifying specific biomarkers across various neurological conditions. 11 The evolution from conventional spectral analysis to advanced nonlinear methods represents genuine technological progress, and data‐driven approaches to EEG biomarker development have shown particular promise for neurodegenerative diseases. 12 Yet these advances remain largely confined to research settings, their potential unrealized in routine clinical practice.

A NECESSARY PARADIGM SHIFT

EEG began as a revolutionary functional tool. Over time, it was overshadowed by imaging, then confined to a descriptive niche. AI offers a chance to reclaim and redefine its purpose. We believe the goal of EEG interpretation must change. There should be less emphasis on visual pattern recognition, less reverence for century‐old terminology, and more focus on diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic relevance. This shift will not happen automatically. It will require deliberate action by researchers, clinicians, journals, industry, and professional societies.
