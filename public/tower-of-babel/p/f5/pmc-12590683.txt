Sleep quality during pregnancy: the role of anxiety, fear of childbirth, and obstetric factors – a multicenter cross-sectional study
Authors: Mojdeh Banaei, Shiva Alizadeh, Nasibeh Roozbeh, Marzieh Saei Ghare-Naz, Elahe Taghvaei, Leila Parviz, Vida Ghasemi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPregnancy is a vulnerable period for poor sleep quality, and sleep disturbances might increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify the prevalence of poor sleep quality and examine the association of pregnancy-related anxiety, fear of childbirth, sociodemographic and obstetric factors with sleep quality among Iranian pregnant women.MethodsA multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted using multi-stage cluster sampling among 424 pregnant women attending health centers in Bandar Abbas, Iran, from 2023 to 2024. Data collection tools included a demographic and obstetric questionnaire, a checklist of pregnancy complications, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (PRAQ), and the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ). Data were analyzed using both univariate and multivariate linear regression in SPSS version 25 and quantile regression in R software version 4.3.0 with the quantreg package.ResultsThe mean age and gestational age of the participants were 29.10 ± 6.81 years and 22.35 ± 9.28 weeks, respectively. Poor sleep quality was observed in 70.8% of the participants, with rates of 69.7%, 65.6%, and 79.1% in the first, second, and third trimesters, respectively. Pregnancy-related anxiety (B = 0.053, P < 0.001), fear of childbirth (B = 0.019, P = 0.006), occupation (B=-0.898, P = 0.026), unwanted pregnancy (B = 1.364, P < 0.001), hospitalization during pregnancy (B = 1.379, P < 0.001), and intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR) (B = 1.114, P = 0.033) were associated with sleep quality in pregnant women. The results of the quantile regression confirmed the significant association of these factors with the median PSQI score.ConclusionsPoor sleep quality was common among pregnant women in this study. Housewives and those reporting higher levels of pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth, unwanted pregnancies, hospitalization, and IUGR during pregnancy, were more likely to experience poorer sleep quality. Given the adverse effects of sleep disorders on maternal and fetal health, screening and managing sleep disturbances during pregnancy are essential.Keywords: Sleep quality, Anxiety, Fear, Obstetric labor, Pregnancy

Introduction

Pregnancy is a vulnerable period for poor sleep quality, and sleep disturbances are frequent complaints among pregnant women [1]. Good sleep quality during pregnancy is essential for fetal development and maternal health [2]. According to the definition from the Encyclopedia of Behavioral Medicine, sleep quality is “one’s satisfaction with the sleep experience, integrating aspects of sleep initiation, sleep maintenance, sleep quantity, and refreshment upon awakening.” [3]. Approximately half of pregnant women may experience poor sleep quality [1], which might increase the risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, including gestational diabetes mellitus, preeclampsia, cesarean section, preterm birth, and depression [4].

Various factors, including hormonal fluctuations and physical complaints, might negatively affect sleep quality during pregnancy [5]. Previous studies have shown that various factors can predict sleep quality in pregnant women, but there is inconsistency among the findings. According to the results of a longitudinal study of Saudi women, low socioeconomic levels and lifestyle factors are considered determinants of sleep quality throughout the first, second, and third trimesters [6]. Another study in Ethiopia showed that older age, increased gestational age, and multiparity are predictors of sleep quality in pregnant women [7]. A prospective birth cohort study demonstrated that women with a history of pregnancy loss, insufficient physical activity, smoking, and a vegetarian diet are more likely to experience poor sleep quality [8]. Moreover, it is proposed that psychological factors, including fear of childbirth and maternal stress, anxiety, and well-being, can contribute to sleep quality disturbances during pregnancy [9, 10]. As pregnancy progresses, risk factors for poor sleep quality can change. Zhang et al. reported that in the second trimester, only a high level of nausea and vomiting was a known risk factor for poor sleep. However, in the last trimester, in addition to the high level of nausea and vomiting, having more years of education and exercising fewer than three days per week were also risk factors [11].

Considering the high prevalence of poor sleep quality during pregnancy and its impact on pregnancy outcomes, a better understanding of the factors associated with sleep quality among Iranian pregnant women may help identify high-risk groups and optimize prevention and management strategies to improve their sleep quality. To the best of our knowledge, no comprehensive study has explored the impact of psychosocial and obstetric factors on sleep quality among pregnant women in Iran. Therefore, the present study aimed to identify the prevalence of poor sleep quality and examine the association of pregnancy-related anxiety, fear of childbirth, sociodemographic and obstetric factors with sleep quality among Iranian pregnant women.

Materials and methods

Study design

This descriptive-analytical multicenter cross-sectional study was conducted in Bandar Abbas, Iran, from November 2023 to September 2024 to explore the prevalence of poor sleep quality and the association of pregnancy-related anxiety, fear of childbirth, sociodemographic and obstetric factors, with sleep quality among Iranian pregnant women.

To determine the sample size, we used Cochran’s formula, which calculates a minimum of 424 eligible pregnant women with a 5% sampling error, a 0.5 ratio, a 0.95 confidence level, and a potential 10% dropout rate.

Participants and procedure

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Iranian pregnant women aged ≥ 18 years, with a gestational age of 7 weeks or more from the time of their last menstrual period (LMP), (2) being married, (3) had no acute or chronic physical or mental diseases, (4) had no recent traumatic events affecting themselves or those around them in the past month, (5) had no history of addiction, (6) no taking sedatives or other sleep-disrupting medications in the past month, (7) willingness to participate in the study, (8) literate. The exclusion criterion was an incomplete response to the questionnaires.

Using a multi-stage cluster sampling method, eight health centers were randomly selected out of 22 centers in Bandar Abbas city. This constituted the first stage, in which clusters (health centers) were randomly chosen. In the second stage, within each selected center, a list of pregnant women was extracted from the integrated health system database. Then, approximately 50–55 eligible women were randomly selected from each center using a computer-generated random number list. This second step represented a simple random sampling within each cluster to identify the final study participants.

Eligible participants were enrolled in the study after the objectives were explained, informed consent was obtained, and confidentiality was assured. The demographic and obstetric questionnaires, a checklist of pregnancy complications, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI), the Pregnancy-Related Anxiety Questionnaire (PRAQ), and the Wijma Delivery Expectancy/Experience Questionnaire (W-DEQ) were subsequently completed in a separate room during face-to-face individual interviews by a trained midwife (ET) to enhance the accuracy of data collection. Additionally, the medical records of the mothers were checked to verify pregnancy complications and reduce recall bias.

Instruments

Demographic and obstetric questionnaire

This questionnaire collects demographic and obstetric information, including the age and education level of the participant and her spouse, occupation, economic status, gestational age, gravidity, parity, number of living children, unwanted pregnancy, sex of the fetus, satisfaction with the sex of the fetus, twin pregnancy, and hospitalization during pregnancy.

Checklist of pregnancy complications

This checklist includes a history of infertility, intrauterine fetal death (IUFD), fetal anomalies, abortion, low birth weight (LBW)/intrauterine growth restriction (IUGR), preterm labor, and premature rupture of membranes (PPROM), as well as experiences of itching, nausea and vomiting, back pain, and medical conditions such as diabetes, hypertension (preeclampsia and other types), anemia, hypothyroidism, etc., during the current pregnancy. It is designed in a Yes/No format to ensure consistency of results, where “Yes” indicates individuals who have experienced the specified complication, and “No” indicates those who have not. Specifically, for conditions such as IUGR and preeclampsia, which are typically diagnosed later in pregnancy, data on these complications were collected from participants in the second and third trimesters.

Pittsburgh sleep quality index

The PSQI measures sleep quality over the past 4 weeks using seven subscales: subjective sleep quality, sleep latency, sleep duration, habitual sleep efficiency, sleep disturbances, use of sleeping medication, and daytime dysfunction. Each subscale is scored from 0 to 3. The scores from each component are summed to give a total score ranging from 0 to 21. A higher score indicates poorer sleep quality [12]. The reliability of the Persian version of the PSQI was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.77 in a study by Farrahi Moghaddami et al. In addition, this study, based on ROC analysis, determined a cut-off value of 6 to be the optimal point, with high sensitivity (93.6%) and specificity (72.2%) [13]. In this study, the reliability of this questionnaire was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.91.

Pregnancy-related anxiety questionnaire (PRAQ)

Developed by Vanden Berg in 1989, this questionnaire assesses fears and concerns related to pregnancy with 17 items. The exploratory factor analysis identified five factors: fear of mood changes and their impact on the child, fear for the baby’s well-being, fear of childbirth, concerns about changes in marital relationships, and self-concerns. The Likert scale for each question ranges from 1 to 7, with total scores ranging from 17 to 119 [14]. The reliability of the Persian version of the PRAQ-17 was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.78 [15]. In this study, the reliability was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.81.

Wijma delivery expectancy/experience questionnaire (W-DEQ)

Designed by Wijma in 1998, this questionnaire measures fear of childbirth through 33 items across six components: lack of positive anticipation, fear, lack of self-efficacy, loneliness, concerns for the child, and negative appraisal. The questionnaire uses a six-point Likert scale from 0 to 5, with total scores ranging from 0 to 165, where higher scores indicate greater fear [16]. The reliability of the Persian version of the W-DEQ was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.84 [17]. In this study, the reliability was confirmed with a Cronbach’s alpha coefficient of 0.87.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 25 and R software version 4.3.0 with the quantreg package. Descriptive statistics, including frequency, mean, and standard deviation, were used to describe the variables.

The normality of the PSQI total score distribution was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and visual inspection of histograms and Q-Q plots. The PSQI score was found to be non-normally distributed (P < 0.05). Although Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) linear regression is relatively robust to violations of normality assumptions with large sample sizes, we also employed quantile regression at the median (0.5 quantile) as a complementary analysis to enhance the robustness of our findings, as it does not assume normality or homoscedasticity and is less sensitive to outliers [18].

Univariate linear regression analysis was first conducted to examine the crude associations between the total PSQI score and each independent variable. Multicollinearity was evaluated using the variance inflation factor (VIF) [19]. Subsequently, variables showing association (P < 0.05) in the univariate linear regression analysis with VIF < 10 were entered into a multiple linear regression model using the Enter method to control for potential confounders and to identify variables independently associated with sleep quality [20]. The results of the multivariate OLS regression are presented as the primary findings for ease of interpretation and consistency with common practice in the field. Finally, to address the non-normal distribution of the PSQI score, a quantile regression analysis was conducted at the median (0.5 quantile) using R software with the quantreg package.

Results

Sociodemographic, obstetric, and pregnancy complications characteristics of the participants

This study included 424 Iranian pregnant women, with an overall response rate of 97.1%. The mean ± standard deviation (SD) age of the participants and their spouses was 29.10 ± 6.81 years and 33.36 ± 6.22 years, respectively. The majority of the participants (43.4%) had completed high school or held a diploma, and 81.3% were housewives. The average gestational age was 22.35 ± 9.28 weeks. The participants were distributed across trimesters as follows: 99 (23.3%) in the first trimester, 186 (43.9%) in the second trimester, and 139 (32.8%) in the third trimester.

More than half of the participants (65.1%) reported experiencing at least one medical condition during their pregnancy. For example, 98 (23.1%) had diabetes, 15 (3.5%) had preeclampsia, 91 (21.4%) had hypothyroidism, and 173 (40.7%) had anemia. Prevalence and dimensions of sleep quality

The mean scores of the PSQI and its dimensions are presented in Table 2. The prevalence of poor sleep quality (PSQI score ≥ 6) among the participants was 301 (70.8%). This prevalence was 69 (69.7%) in the first trimester, 122 (65.6%) in the second trimester, and 110 (79.1%) in the third trimester (Fig. 2). Regarding the dimensions of sleep quality, 24 (5.6%) participants reported very poor subjective sleep quality, while 192 (45.2%) reported sleeping more than 7 h per night. Most participants (92.7%) did not use sleeping medication to improve sleep quality, and 250 (58.9%) had sleep efficiency greater than 85%. Additionally, 149 (35.1%) participants took 31–40 min to fall asleep in the past month.

Factors associated with sleep quality

Univariate linear regression analysis indicated significant association between the total PSQI score and gestational age (P = 0.007), employment status (P = 0.008), IUGR (P = 0.002), itching (P = 0.025), twin pregnancy (P = 0.007), hospitalization (P < 0.001), unwanted pregnancy (P < 0.001), pregnancy-related anxiety (P < 0.001), and fear of childbirth (P < 0.001) (Table 3). A subsequent multiple linear regression model was used to adjust for potential confounding among these associated factors and identify those independently associated with the PSQI score. All VIF values were below 10, indicating no significant multicollinearity. The multiple linear regression model explained 22.6% of the variance in the PSQI score (R² = 0.226, Adjusted R² = 0.209). In the multivariate OLS model, higher pregnancy-related anxiety (B = 0.053, 95% CI: 0.036 to 0.069, P < 0.001), greater fear of childbirth (B = 0.019, 95% CI: 0.006 to 0.033, P = 0.006), being a housewife (compared to employed; B=−0.898, 95% CI: −1.691 to −0.106, P = 0.026), having an unwanted pregnancy (B = 1.364, 95% CI: 0.563 to 2.165, P < 0.001), experiencing hospitalization during pregnancy (B = 1.379, 95% CI: 0.611 to 2.147, P < 0.001), and having IUGR (B = 1.114, 95% CI: 0.087 to 2.140, P = 0.033) were independently associated with higher PSQI scores, indicating poorer sleep quality (Table 4). The coefficients represent the difference in the PSQI score associated with each factor after adjusting for other variables in the model.

For each unit increase in pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth, the PSQI score increased by 0.053 and 0.019, respectively. The employed women had a PSQI score 0.898 points lower than housewives. Pregnant women with unwanted pregnancies, those hospitalized during pregnancy, and those with IUGR had PSQI scores that were higher by 1.364, 1.379, and 1.114 points, respectively.

Additionally, the results of the quantile regression analysis which is conducted to address the non-normal distribution of the PSQI score, confirming the significant associations of pregnancy-related anxiety (B = 0.050, P < 0.001), fear of childbirth (B = 0.020, P = 0.003), occupation (B=−0.850, P = 0.038), unwanted pregnancy (B = 1.300, P = 0.002), hospitalization (B = 1.350, P < 0.001), and IUGR (B = 1.100, P = 0.028) with the median PSQI score. These results confirmed that the direction and significance of the associated factors were consistent with those of the OLS model. This consistency across regression techniques reinforces the robustness of the results.

Discussion

In this study, we aimed to determine the prevalence of poor sleep quality and the association of psychosocial and obstetric factors with sleep quality among Iranian pregnant women. The study results showed that approximately two-thirds of the participants experienced poor sleep quality and pregnancy-related anxiety, fear of childbirth, occupation, unwanted pregnancy, hospitalization during pregnancy, and IUGR were associated with sleep quality in our sample.

The results indicated a high prevalence of poor sleep quality in pregnant women. Some studies have reported that the prevalence of poor sleep quality during pregnancy is greater than ours, such as 87% in China [19], whereas others have reported a lower prevalence, such as 54.6% in Ethiopia [21]. The prevalence of poor sleep quality in Iranian pregnant women was previously reported to be 76.3% and 59.2%, indicating a consistently high rate of this disorder during pregnancy in this population [22, 23]. These differences in the prevalence of poor sleep quality across various studies can be attributed to variations in sample size, study design, socioeconomic status, and other contextual factors. In the Iranian context, the high prevalence may be partly attributed to the lack of systematic screening for sleep disturbances during prenatal care. Sleep problems are often underrecognized or considered a normal aspect of pregnancy, leading to underdiagnosis and insufficient management. The incorporation of sleep quality assessments into routine prenatal care is beneficial and essential [22, 24].

Our results also indicate that the prevalence of poor sleep quality in the first and third trimesters is greater than that in the second trimester. This finding is consistent with the study by Takelle et al. [25]. This may be because, in the first trimester, women often experience discomfort, such as nausea and vomiting, nocturia, threatened abortion, spotting, and fatigue. In the third trimester, issues such as fear or worry about impending childbirth occur, back pain, shortness of breath, fetal movements, nocturia, pressure from a large abdomen, and leg cramps become more common, all of which can interfere with sleep quality [26–28].

Consistent with our findings regarding the association between pregnancy-related anxiety and sleep quality during pregnancy, other studies have reported similar results [26, 29]. A study in Iran showed that 54.2% of pregnant women experience anxiety during pregnancy [30]. The results of a meta-analysis also showed that anxiety during pregnancy is prevalent at 29.9% in low and middle-income countries [31]. Anxiety during pregnancy is common and can stem from concerns about the fetus’s health, the ability to care for the baby, changes in body image, alterations in the relationship with the spouse, acceptance and adaptation to the new role, and the responsibilities of parenthood, which can be associated with poor sleep quality during this period [22]. Anxiety and stress can affect sleep quality through mechanisms such as hyperactivity of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis, elevated cortisol levels, arginine vasopressin release, and disruption of the circadian rhythm [32, 33]. In addition, the relationship between anxiety and sleep quality is bidirectional: high levels of anxiety often lead to poor sleep quality and insomnia, whereas poor sleep quality can, in turn, increase anxiety [34].

In support of our findings, several studies have shown an association between the fear of childbirth and sleep quality during pregnancy [10, 35]. In addition, in the present study, severe fear of childbirth and poor sleep quality were both more prevalent in pregnant women during the third trimester compared to other trimesters. A study by Mei et al. (2019) indicated that pregnant women experiencing high levels of fear are more likely to suffer from suboptimal sleep quality, restlessness, insomnia, and inadequate sleep at night [10]. Additionally, fear of childbirth can lead to psychological distress [36], which further negatively impacts sleep quality [25, 37]. It seems that interventions such as mindfulness, interpersonal psychotherapy, and psychological and cognitive-behavioral training can be effective in reducing the fear of childbirth and the psychological problems that negatively affect sleep quality during pregnancy [25, 37–39].

Although pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth were significantly associated with sleep quality in this study, to assess whether the results are clinically significant, we used the Minimal Clinically Important Difference (MCID). Therefore, we estimated the expected change in the PSQI score associated with a one-SD increase in the scores of these psychological factors [40]. A one-SD increase in pregnancy-related anxiety (19.11 points) and fear of childbirth (23.22 points) was associated with expected 1.01 and 0.44 point increases in the PSQI score, respectively. While the MCID of the PSQI in pregnant women is currently lacking, these estimated changes are below the MCID ≥ 3 for PSQI, which is commonly accepted in studies [41, 42]. Our estimates suggest that for women with low to moderate pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth, the associated deterioration in sleep quality may be of limited clinical significance; however, for those with severe levels of these stressors, a more substantial and clinically meaningful worsening of sleep quality is expected. This underscores the critical importance of screening for severe psychological distress during pregnancy, as these individuals stand to benefit the most from targeted interventions to improve sleep quality.

The results of the present study showed that employed women experienced better sleep quality compared to housewives. Consistent with our findings, Hung et al.‘s study showed that unemployed pregnant women had a greater prevalence of poor sleep quality compared to those who were employed [43]. There is a possible explanation for this. Employed women tend to have better socioeconomic status, which reduces the psychological stress caused by economic pressure. Additionally, working women may have a support network that allows them to express their sleep problems during pregnancy with their female colleagues and seek help to address these issues. Adherence to a regular work schedule and engagement in balanced physical activity are other possible explanations [43]. Furthermore, in Iran, recent family protection laws (enacted in 2021) provide employed pregnant women with extended maternity leave, reduced working hours, exemption from night shifts, and support for remote work [44]. These provisions might be associated with reduced occupational stressors, which could be linked to lower anxiety levels and better sleep quality among this group. Conversely, Du et al. reported no statistically significant association between sleep quality and the employment status of pregnant women [8]. These differing findings might be related to methodological variations, such as the cohort study design used by Du et al. and their exclusive focus on the first trimester, compared to the cross-sectional design and inclusion of all trimesters in our study.

The IUGR was another factor associated with sleep quality in this study. IUGR is associated with many factors, such as small for gestational age, stillbirth, preterm labor, obstetric complications, and maternal nutritional disorders, all of which can indirectly affect the comfort and quality of sleep during pregnancy [45]. IUGR and poor sleep quality can affect each other. In this context, Fan Wu et al. (2025) reported that the fetuses of pregnant women with poor sleep quality during pregnancy had reduced abdominal circumference and femur length during the second and third trimesters, and their birth weight was lower [46]. On the other hand, mothers with IUGR tend to experience higher levels of anxiety, which is associated with reduced sleep quality in pregnant women [47].

According to the results of this study, an unwanted pregnancy was another factor associated with poor sleep quality. The reason could be that an unwanted pregnancy has caused psychological and emotional distress due to insufficient preparation for pregnancy, childbirth, and support of the new child [48]. Mahini et al. (2023) found that unwanted pregnancy is associated with a 49% increase in the risk of anxiety during pregnancy [49], and anxiety is associated with poorer sleep quality in pregnant women [9].

The present study also showed that pregnant women who were hospitalized were more likely to experience sleep disorders. Pregnant women who experience hospitalization due to pregnancy complications tend to have higher levels of stress than those who have not been hospitalized, because of the fear of these complications recurring [50]. This stress is a factor associated with poorer sleep quality during pregnancy. Pia Burger et al. (2022) reported that the quality of sleep in hospitalized patients is generally poor, supporting the results of the present study. Factors such as nighttime awakening, noise, the number of patients, pain, and anxiety can affect sleep quality in a hospital setting [51]. In addition, sleep disturbances following hospitalization can lead to persistent insomnia and poor sleep quality even after discharge [52].

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the use of validated Persian versions of questionnaires that have been specifically adapted for the Iranian population, which show high reliability as demonstrated by a strong Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. This enhances both the cultural relevance and accuracy of the assessments. Additionally, assessing sleep quality across multiple pregnancy trimesters and employing advanced statistical methods such as linear and quantile regression analyses adds to its robustness.

However, this study has several limitations. The cross-sectional design of this study restricts its ability to establish causal relationships. The absence of a control group of nonpregnant women limits comparative insights, suggesting that larger longitudinal studies with control groups are needed to enhance validity. Additionally, recruiting exclusively from health centers in a single city may limit the sample’s representativeness of all pregnant women in the community. Potential self-report bias is another limitation. Furthermore, since more than 83% of the participants were housewives, the generalizability of the findings to employed women may be limited. Finally, it is recommended that, in addition to questionnaires, objective measures such as polysomnography be utilized to assess sleep disorders more accurately during pregnancy.

Conclusions

Our study showed that poor sleep quality was common among pregnant women in our sample. Housewives and those reporting higher levels of pregnancy-related anxiety and fear of childbirth, unwanted pregnancies, hospitalization, and IUGR during pregnancy, were more likely to experience poorer sleep quality. Given the high prevalence of poor sleep quality and its documented correlations with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes in other research, integrating routine screening for sleep quality and pregnancy-related psychological stressors into prenatal care across the Iranian healthcare system could be beneficial. The incorporation of sleep hygiene education and psychological support into prenatal visits, along with the establishment of referral pathways to specialized care, could be important components of such programs.

Additionally, development of tailored, culturally sensitive, and evidence-based interventions targeted at high-risk groups, including women in late pregnancy or those experiencing a high level of psychological stress, is essential to improve maternal sleep health.
