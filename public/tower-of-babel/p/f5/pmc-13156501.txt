Effectiveness of Cognitive Behavioral Therapies Targeting Cognitive Biases and Beliefs in Schizophrenia-Spectrum Disorders: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Donald Massicotte, Audrey Livet, Maryam Lahlou, Stéphane Potvin, Tania Lecomte
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisCognitive models of schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (SSD) posit dysfunctional beliefs and cognitive biases as maintenance mechanisms of positive and negative symptoms. Although cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT) targets these processes, its effects on mechanism-level outcomes remain unclear. This review examined whether CBT modifies dysfunctional beliefs and cognitive biases in SSD using rigorous randomized evidence.Study DesignPRISMA 2020—compliant systematic review and meta-analysis (PROSPERO registered). Primary analyses were restricted to intention-to-treat (ITT) randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in SSD samples, using random-effects models and between-group post-treatment estimates. Pre–post and nonrandomized studies were analyzed separately as secondary evidence. Subgroup and meta-regression analyses were conducted.Study ResultsThirty-three studies met inclusion criteria. Fourteen ITT RCTs contributed to the primary pooled analysis of dysfunctional beliefs, yielding a small but statistically significant effect favoring CBT (g = 0.154, 95% CI, 0.049-0.259). Effects were strongest for delusional conviction (g = 0.450) and self-related schemas (positive-self g = 0.278; negative-self g = 0.298). Voice-related beliefs did not reach statistical significance. Too few RCTs assessed cognitive biases to support primary pooled analyses; exploratory findings suggested small effects for belief inflexibility and no reliable effect for jumping-to-conclusions. Greater reductions in dysfunctional beliefs were associated with greater improvements in positive symptoms across trials.ConclusionsCBT produces small but reliable improvements in dysfunctional beliefs in SSD, although effects vary by domains particularly for delusional conviction and self-schemas, supporting their role as modifiable therapeutic targets and plausible mechanisms of change. Effects on cognitive biases remain limited and understudied.Keywords: schizophrenia spectrum disorders, cognitive behavioral therapy, cognitive biases, beliefs, meta-analysis

Introduction

Schizophrenia-spectrum disorders (SSD) are severe mental illnesses that significantly impair psychosocial functioning, reduce quality of life, and shorten life expectancy, 1 reflecting their high personal and societal burden. These disorders affect roughly 24 million people worldwide,2 with individuals often facing increased suicide risk,3–5 poor employability,6 with negative effects extending to family members.7 SSD are increasingly conceptualized as heterogeneous conditions,8 characterized by positive (eg, hallucinations, delusions), negative (eg, anhedonia, avolition), and disorganized thinking symptoms.9

Beyond clinical symptoms, SSD involve marked cognitive dysfunction,7 including cognitive biases.10 A central framework, derived from Beck’s cognitive model, emphasizes the role of dysfunctional beliefs in both positive and negative symptoms.11–16 Psychosis-specific models17–19 likewise highlight cognitive biases as interrelated mechanisms contributing to the onset and persistence of delusions.

Within this framework, dysfunctional and core beliefs refer to maladaptive cognitions, from rigid distortions to deep-seated schemas, that shape emotions and behaviors.20–22 These beliefs have been linked to the frequency and severity of both positive and negative symptoms. Examples include defeatist performance beliefs (DPB),13,23,24 negative self- and other-related schemas,25–27 and beliefs about emotion uncontrollability.28 Beliefs regarding the malevolence and omnipotence of auditory hallucinations have been shown to correlate with their severity,29 as does delusional conviction. Illness-related beliefs may further influence both symptomatology and emotion regulation strategies.30

Alongside dysfunctional beliefs, cognitive biases—systematic thinking errors often arising from heuristic misuse31—represent preferential distortions in processing, interpreting, or storing information.32 In SSD, reasoning and interpretation biases are frequently observed, such as the jumping-to-conclusions (JTC) bias, reflecting premature decision-making based on insufficient evidence.33–35 Meta-analytic data indicate that JTC occurs in about 60% of SSD patients versus 29% of healthy controls.36 A recent umbrella review,37 synthesizing 15 meta-analyses, reported moderate- to high-quality evidence linking specific cognitive biases to psychotic features, including interpretation biases when grouped together (ie, attributional, self-serving, hostility attribution, and aberrant salience biases), and reasoning biases (cognitive inflexibility and jumping to conclusions [JTC]).

These findings support the rationale for cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), which targets such biases and beliefs. CBT is grounded in collaborative, nonjudgmental principles that help individuals examine links between thoughts, feelings, and behaviors and evaluate symptom-related cognitions38–40 Building on these foundations, CBT for psychosis (CBTp) has demonstrated efficacy in reducing core symptoms of SSD.41,42 Several recent umbrella and meta-analytic reviews have examined the efficacy of CBT and psychotherapy for psychosis, primarily in relation to symptom outcomes.43,44 In parallel, umbrella syntheses have confirmed robust associations between cognitive biases and psychotic features.37 More focused meta-analyses have addressed mechanism-related constructs, but within restricted scopes: a prior meta-analysis of psychological interventions targeting cognitive biases excluded CBT45; meta-analyses of metacognitive training (MCT/MCT+)46,47 targeted cognitive biases reported small-to-moderate effects; and a recent CBT-focused meta-analysis examined core schemas only, showing small self-schema improvements but no effect on negative-other beliefs.48

Contemporary cognitive models of psychosis conceptualize maladaptive belief appraisal, threat anticipation, and biased evidence evaluation as maintenance mechanisms of delusions and related distress, making mechanism-level outcome evaluation clinically and theoretically essential. Yet, to date, no meta-analysis has quantified CBT’s effects on both cognitive biases and dysfunctional beliefs using primary evidence from randomized controlled trials with intention-to-treat estimates, a critical and unresolved gap.

The present systematic review and meta-analysis had three aims: (1) to identify and characterize CBT interventions targeting dysfunctional beliefs and cognitive biases in SSD; (2) to estimate their effects using randomized controlled trials with intention-to-treat between-group estimates as the primary evidence base, with uncontrolled pre–post findings examined separately as secondary evidence; and (3) to test whether changes in beliefs and biases are associated with symptom change using meta-regression analyses. This approach supports a mechanism-focused synthesis while prioritizing methodological rigor in primary effect estimates.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines49 and was registered with PROSPERO (CRD 42024524104). Primary meta-analyses were restricted to randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in SSD samples using intention-to-treat (ITT) between-group post-treatment estimates. Nonrandomized and pre–post studies were synthesized separately and treated as secondary exploratory evidence. The complete PRISMA checklist is provided in Table S1.

Literature Search Strategy

Five electronic databases were searched up to February 13-14, 2024: MEDLINE (Ovid), PsycINFO (Ovid), Embase (Ovid), CINAHL (EBSCOhost), and Web of Science (Clarivate Analytics). Search terms combined three concepts: (1) schizophrenia-spectrum and other psychotic disorders, (2) cognitive-behavioral and related therapies, and (3) cognitive biases and dysfunctional beliefs. Both free-text keywords and controlled vocabulary (eg, MeSH, APA Thesaurus, Emtree, CINAHL Headings) were applied. Complete search strategies for each database are provided in Table S2. Only peer-reviewed studies were included. Gray literature was explored through Google Scholar and cross-referencing was done with Connected Papers.50 Reference lists of all included studies were hand-searched to identify additional eligible records.

Eligibility Criteria

Eligible participants were individuals diagnosed with SSD, including those with a first-episode of psychosis or at ultra-high risk (UHR) for psychosis. Studies with mixed diagnostic groups were excluded if participants with SSD represented a minority relative to other diagnostic categories (eg, mood, schizotypal/schizoid personality, or delusional disorders). Trials conducted exclusively in UHR samples were not included in the primary pooled RCT analyses and were retained only for descriptive and sensitivity analyses, in order to avoid mixing risk-state and diagnosed SSD populations.

Both conventional CBTp (second-wave approaches) and third-wave interventions (eg, mindfulness-based interventions, acceptance and commitment therapy [ACT], compassion-focused therapy) were eligible when explicitly grounded in a cognitive-behavioral framework. CBT eligibility criteria were defined a priori based on established cognitive-behavioral therapy definitions and applied consistently during full-text screening (Table S3). To meet inclusion criteria, interventions had to explicitly target cognitive biases or dysfunctional beliefs, consistent with the primary objective of this review. Both individual and group formats were included.

Interventions focused on cognitive remediation or social-cognition training were excluded unless they specifically targeted cognitive biases or beliefs. Psychological interventions such as cognitive bias modification and the Maudsley Review Training Programme were excluded. MCT programs were also excluded because recent meta-analyses have already evaluated their effects on cognitive biases, and the present review aimed to synthesize evidence on CBT-based psychotherapeutic interventions targeting beliefs and biases within broader cognitive-behavioral treatment frameworks.

Eligible cognitive biases were those most likely to be targeted by CBT and included reasoning biases—specifically, JTC and data-gathering bias-belief inflexibility (biases against disconfirmatory or confirmatory evidence, liberal acceptance), and interpretation biases (externalization of cognitive events, source monitoring, self-serving, personalizing, hostility attribution, aberrant salience). Eligible dysfunctional beliefs encompassed DPB, threat-related appraisals, and beliefs about voices (omnipotence, malevolence, benevolence, resistance, engagement, origin). They also included delusional conviction (ie, the perceived veracity of delusional beliefs), schemas about self and others (negative-self, positive-self, negative-others, positive-others), and broader dysfunctional appraisals such as illness-related, stress-control, and asocial beliefs.

Study Selection and Data Extraction

Covidence systematic-review software51 was used to manage study screening and selection. The PRISMA flow diagram is presented in Figure S1. Data were extracted by one author and verified for 27% (9 of 33 studies) by a second author; disagreements were resolved by consensus among the author team.

A standardized form was used to extract data from the included studies. Extracted information included study design, control condition, sample characteristics, setting, intervention format, number and duration of sessions, type of CBT, targeted cognitive biases and beliefs, measurement tools, outcomes, means, standard deviations, sample size, correlations and P-values.

Risk of Bias Assessment

Three authors independently assessed study quality using the Risk of Bias 2 (ROB-2) tool for randomized trials52 and the Risk of Bias in Nonrandomized Studies of Intervention (ROBINS-I) tool for nonrandomized studies.53 Discrepancies were resolved through consensus discussions. Quality ratings are reported in Table S4.

Certainty of Evidence

Certainty of evidence for each primary outcome was assessed using the GRADE approach.54 Evidence from randomized controlled trials was initially rated as high certainty and could be downgraded across five domains: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. Risk-of-bias judgments were informed by study-level Cochrane RoB 2 assessments. Detailed ratings and justifications are provided in Table S5.

Statistical Analysis

Meta-analyses were conducted when at least two studies were available, as pooling is not meaningful for a single study. However, pooled estimates based on very small numbers of studies were interpreted cautiously due to potential statistical instability and vulnerability to small-study effects. Separate meta-analyses were conducted by bias type (interpretation or reasoning biases) and belief type (beliefs about voices, delusional conviction, self-related, and other-related beliefs). See Table S6 for the mapping of instrument/subscales to outcome constructs and domains (study-level). Analyses were performed using Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (version 4).55 Primary meta-analyses synthesized between-group post-treatment effects from RCTs only.

Pre-post meta-analyses were limited to uncontrolled studies and conducted separately as secondary analyses.

Hedge g was used to estimate effect sizes, correcting for small-sample bias,56 with conventional thresholds of 0.2 (small), 0.5 (moderate), and 0.8 (large); intermediate values were interpreted accordingly. All meta-analyses used random-effects models given the expected clinical and methodological heterogeneity across trials. Heterogeneity was assessed via Cochran Q57 and I2 statistics.58 By convention, a Q-statistic P-value below .10 indicates heterogeneity, whereas I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% correspond to low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.59

Meta-Regression Analyses

Meta-regression analyses examined whether between-group effect sizes for dysfunctional beliefs were associated with between-group effect sizes for symptom outcomes and with number of CBT sessions. Analyses were restricted to randomized controlled trials and used between-group effect-size estimates (CBT vs control). For positive symptoms, to avoid multiple dependent effects, one outcome per study was selected using a predefined hierarchy (PANSS positive scale, BPRS psychotic subscale, PSYRATS delusion/hallucination score or, GPTS). Meta-regression could not be conducted for negative symptoms due to the limited number of eligible RCTs and incomplete reporting. Study-level effect sizes were computed in Comprehensive Meta-Analysis (version 4) and entered as predictors in the meta-regression models.

Publication Bias

Publication bias was assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and the Egger test,60 was interpreted using a liberal significance threshold (P < .10) given the limited number of trials per meta-analysis.

Results

Search Outcomes

The search yielded a total of 1030 records. After removing 426 duplicates, 604 titles and abstracts were screened independently by three authors, and 493 were excluded at this stage. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus. A total of 111 full-texts articles were assessed, and 33 studies met the inclusion criteria. The main reasons for exclusion included publication type (n = 28), irrelevant outcomes (n = 15), or ineligible interventions (n = 12).

Systematic Review

Conducted across 9 countries on four continents, most often in the United Kingdom, the 33 included trials examined the effects of CBT on dysfunctional beliefs and/or cognitive biases in SSD. Dysfunctional beliefs were assessed in all studies, whereas only 6 (18%) investigated cognitive biases. Most designs were randomized controlled trials (74%), with beliefs and biases frequently examined as secondary outcomes. Total sample size ranged from 12 to 361, whereas CBT studies reporting between-group comparisons had baseline samples ranging from 13 to 181 for the CBT condition. Participants were mainly recruited from outpatient settings (79%) and were diagnosed with SSD, except for two studies focusing on individuals at UHR of psychosis, which were retained for descriptive purposes only and excluded from primary pooled RCT analyses.

Interventions consisted predominantly of adapted second-wave CBT protocols (eg, CBTp, CBT focused on emotions, CBT-n for negative symptoms). Four studies (12%) applied third-wave approaches, including Mindfulness-Based Cognitive Therapy and ACT.

In 70% of trials, participants received fewer than 16 sessions, below recommended guidelines, indicating substantial variability in treatment dose, underscoring the heterogeneity of CBT approaches.

Cognitive biases were examined in a limited subset of trials and are summarized narratively due to insufficient RCT data for primary pooling. The most frequently examined cognitive biases were reasoning biases—JTC and belief-inflexibility biases (possibility of being mistaken) whereas interpretation biases (externalization of cognitive events, personalizing bias, and hostile attributions) were less frequently assessed. Among RCTs, three trials assessing JTC did not show consistent significant between-group effects. Four RCTs assessed belief inflexibility; most reported improvements favoring CBT. An exploratory pooled estimate is provided in Table S7 and should be interpreted cautiously due to the small number of studies and high heterogeneity.

Regarding dysfunctional beliefs, the most frequently assessed constructs were beliefs about voices (omnipotence, malevolence, benevolence) measured with the Beliefs About Voices Questionnaire (BAVQ)61 or its revised version (BAVQ-R)62; core self- and other-related schemas measured with the Brief Core Schema Scale25; and conviction in delusional beliefs measured with multiple instruments. Other beliefs domains (eg, beliefs about safety, voice origin, voice persecution, defeatist beliefs performance, illness-related beliefs, stress control, and asocial beliefs) were less frequently studied. Overall patterns across individual trials suggested more consistent effects for delusional conviction and self-related schemas than for voice-related belief dimensions.

Meta-Analysis Results—RCT (Primary Analyses)

Cognitive Biases: RCTs Evidence

Insufficient RCTs assessed cognitive biases to support primary pooled meta-analyses. Results are therefore reported narratively (above), with exploratory pooled estimates provided in the Supplementary material only (Table S7).

Dysfunctional Beliefs—RCT (ITT, Between-Group)

Beliefs about Voices

CBT effects on beliefs about voices were examined in RCTs using ITT between-group estimates. Benevolence was assessed in five trials, malevolence in four trials, and omnipotence in five trials. Pooled effects were small and not statistically significant for benevolence (g = 0.025, 95% CI, −0.233 to 0.282) and malevolence (g = 0.060, 95% CI, −0.322 to 0.442). The pooled effect for omnipotence was small-to-moderate but not statistically significant (g = 0.245, 95% CI, −0.096 to 0.586). Heterogeneity ranged from low to substantial across subdomains. Egger tests were non-significant but underpowered (k < 10).

Delusional Belief Conviction

Five RCTs examined the effect of CBT on the degree of conviction that the person’s delusional beliefs were true. As illustrated in Table 2, results revealed a significant small-to-moderate effect (g = 0.450, 95% CI, 0.214-0.686). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate but not statistically significant, and no publication bias was detected (Table 2).

Self-Related Beliefs

Seven RCTs assessed positive-self beliefs and seven assessed negative-self beliefs. Pooled effects were small but statistically significant for both positive-self (g = 0.278, 95% CI, 0.055-0.500) and negative-self beliefs (g = 0.298, 95% CI, 0.060-0.536). Heterogeneity was low-to-moderate. Egger tests suggested possible small-study effects but were underpowered.

Combined Dysfunctional Beliefs (Primary Outcomes)

Fourteen ITT RCTs contributed to the primary pooled analysis of combined dysfunctional beliefs. The pooled effect was small but statistically significant (Hedges g = 0.154, 95% CI, 0.049-0.259, P = .004), with low between-study heterogeneity and no evidence of publication bias (Table 2; Figure 1).

Certainty of Evidence

For the primary pooled outcome (combined dysfunctional beliefs), certainty of evidence was rated as moderate using the GRADE approach, downgraded one level for risk of bias because 7 of 14 contributing ITT RCTs were rated as “some concerns” on RoB 2, with concerns driven mainly by domains relevant to subjective outcomes (outcome measurement and/or missing outcome data, and occasionally selective reporting) in unblinded psychotherapy trials. Certainty ratings across outcome domains ranged from low (delusional conviction; additional downgrading for imprecision due to small k and wider confidence intervals) to moderate (self-related schemas). No downgrading for indirectness was applied because all included studies involved SSD samples receiving CBT-based interventions directly relevant to the review question. Detailed ratings are presented in Table S5.

Subgroup Analyses by Control Condition

To address potential heterogeneity related to comparator type, subgroup analyses were conducted according to control condition (treatment as usual, active comparator, waiting list). Across ITT RCTs, pooled effects were of similar magnitude across control types, and the between-subgroup heterogeneity test was not statistically significant (Q between = 1.349, df = 2, P = .509). This indicates that the effect of CBT on dysfunctional beliefs did not significantly differ as a function of control group category. Detailed subgroup estimates are reported in Table S8.

Sensitivity Analysis

A sensitivity analysis was conducted by adding SSD RCTs reporting completer-only outcomes to the primary ITT RCT set, while excluding the high risk-of-bias trial by Dai et al.67 This expanded model yielded a larger but directionally consistent pooled effect for combined dysfunctional beliefs (k = 18; g = 0.242; 95% CI, 0.198-0.376). Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (Q = 25.821, P = .078; I2 = 34.2%). Evidence of small-study effects, potentially reflecting publication bias, was suggested by Egger’s test (t = 2.868, P = .011). The larger effect observed when completer-only trials were included may reflect attrition-related bias, greater treatment exposure among completers, or other methodological differences between studies. Because these factors cannot be disentangled, results should be interpreted with caution, supporting the decision to prioritize ITT-based estimates in the primary analysis.

Meta-Analysis Results—Pre-Post Analyses (Secondary Outcomes)

Combined Dysfunctional Beliefs

Separate uncontrolled pre-post meta-analyses indicated a moderate within-group improvement in combined dysfunctional beliefs (k = 9; Hedges g = 0.460, 95% CI, 0.266-0.654, P = .001). Between-study heterogeneity was moderate to substantial (Q = 20.128, P = .001; I2 = 60.3%). Evidence of small-study effects, potentially reflecting publication bias, was suggested by Egger’s test (t = 1.026, P = .034). Because these estimates derive from noncontrolled designs, they are considered secondary and were not included in primary inferences. See the forest plot of pre-post change in combined dysfunctional beliefs (Figure S3).

Meta-Regression Results

Meta-regression analyses based on RCT between-group effect sizes indicated that greater treatment-related reductions in positive symptoms were significantly associated with larger improvements in dysfunctional beliefs (β = 0.44, SE = 0.20, 95% CI, 0.05-0.83, P = .028; k = 10). This analysis reflects between-study associations in treatment effects and does not imply a causal or directional relationship at the individual level.

Meta-regression for negative symptoms was not conducted due to an insufficient number of eligible RCTs. No significant association was observed between the number of CBT sessions and changes in dysfunctional beliefs (Table 3).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide a mechanism-focused synthesis of CBT effects on dysfunctional beliefs and cognitive biases in SSD, with primary inferences based on intention-to-treat randomized controlled trials and secondary evidence from pre–post designs examined separately. Stratified analyses by control condition did not show significant subgroup differences, suggesting that belief-related effects were broadly consistent across comparator types.

Summary of Main Results

In primary RCT analyses, pooled effects were small and varied across belief domains. No established minimal important difference thresholds exist for dysfunctional-belief or cognitive-bias outcomes in SSD, as these represent mechanism-level rather than direct clinical endpoints. Despite modest effect sizes, even small shifts in dysfunctional beliefs may be clinically meaningful in chronic and treatment-resistant psychotic disorders, where baseline functioning is severely impaired and outcomes reflect multiple interacting factors. Such changes may be particularly relevant when they contribute to downstream reductions in distress, delusional conviction, or symptom persistence. The strongest and most consistent effects were observed for delusional conviction and positive and negative self-related schemas. A recent meta-analysis of schema outcomes48 reported similar improvements in negative- and positive-self schemas. In contrast, effects on voice-related beliefs were smaller and more heterogeneous: although point estimates suggested reductions in omnipotence, malevolence and benevolence, none of these belief dimensions reached statistical significance in RCTs.

Overall, CBT more consistently modified evaluative belief appraisals than voice-related beliefs. Primary ITT findings were directionally stable across sensitivity models.

Secondary pre–post analyses of combined belief outcomes suggested larger within-group improvements; however, because these estimates derive from uncontrolled designs, they were not used for primary inference.

Findings for reasoning biases were mixed. Exploratory ITT RCT subgroup analyses (k = 3-4 per construct) did not show a reliable pooled effect of CBT on JTC, despite its established association with delusion formation.34,36,37 In contrast, a small but statistically significant effect was observed for belief inflexibility using “possibility of being mistaken” measures,71,73,94 whereas tasks requiring “alternative explanations” showed no consistent change and were examined in only a small number of studies.73,94 Given the limited number of RCTs and construct heterogeneity, these subgroups estimates should be interpreted cautiously. Evidence concerning interpretation biases also remains sparse, preventing reliable inferences about CBT effects in this domain.

Meta-regression findings indicated that greater reductions in dysfunctional beliefs were associated with greater improvements in positive symptoms across trials. This association is consistent with cognitive models that position belief appraisal and conviction as maintenance mechanisms, although causal interpretation cannot be inferred from cross-trial meta-regression.

Notably, the number of sessions was not significantly associated with treatment efficacy. Nearly three-quarters of trials offered fewer than the 16 sessions recommended by clinical guidelines.42 Although brief CBT can be effective,97 lower dose may limit opportunities for behavioral experiments and cognitive restructuring needed for durable belief change.

Theoretical Implications

Although modest in magnitude, these findings align with cognitive models of psychosis emphasizing the role of maladaptive beliefs and cognitive biases in symptom maintenance. The relatively stronger effects observed for delusional conviction and self-related schemas suggest that CBT preferentially modifies evaluative belief appraisals through collaborative and reflective techniques that promote alternative interpretations and reduce maladaptive certainty. The significant improvements in negative-others schemas may also be relevant for interpersonal models of persecutory delusions, such as Freeman’s threat anticipation framework.98

Although based on exploratory analyses, the absence of a significant effect on JTC bias is not unexpected. When extreme responses are excluded, most studies are inconsistent to detect association between JTC and delusion intensity.99 Even interventions specifically targeting biases, such as MCT, yield only small effects.37 Yet qualitative reports indicate that CBT strategies, such as gathering data and cognitive restructuring, are perceived as helpful in reducing distress and belief conviction.100 Two interpretations remain plausible: rapid reasoning processes may be relatively resistant to change, or commonly used bias tasks may lack sensitivity to detect clinical improvement.37

Voice-related beliefs appeared more resistant to change. Similar results have been reported for AVATAR therapy, which applies CBT principles and demonstrates moderate effects on voice severity but limited impact on beliefs about voices.101 This pattern may reflect CBT’s stronger influence on distress reduction than on altering the phenomenological content of the voices.102,103 Some patients may cease fearing their voices yet continue to hold irrational appraisals, a phenomenon consistent with the long-described “double bookkeeping” in psychosis, which may help explain the coexistence of contradictory beliefs.104 Collectively, the results support tailored CBT protocols addressing specific beliefs domains, such as coping with command hallucinations.35,64

From a neurocognitive perspective, the results converge with Bayesian models conceptualizing psychotic symptoms as consequences of abnormal belief updating105 and overly rigid priors dominating inference and perceptions.106 CBT’s stronger effects on dysfunctional beliefs than on reasoning biases may reflect its capacity to enhance cognitive flexibility and adaptative updating of priors rather than to modify early inferential biases. Reductions in delusional conviction, DPB, and maladaptive self- and other-related schemas11,13,107 may thus promote more balanced evidence integration, contributing to symptom reduction. Neuroimaging data are consistent with CBT’s impact on beliefs updating.108 These interpretations remain theoretical and should be considered hypothesis-generating.

Clinical Implications

First, delusional conviction and self-related schemas emerge as reliable and modifiable treatment targets. Routine assessment and focused interventions to reduce conviction and challenge defeatist beliefs are warranted.11,13,15 Second, while cognitive restructuring and behavioral experiments remain central to CBT, incorporating work on evidence integration, self-esteem, and metacognitive awareness may strengthen outcomes.109 These components can be integrated within existing CBT frameworks rather than treated as distinct interventions, as exemplified in group CBT programs39 or MCT programs.110

Third, although the meta-regression did not reveal a dose–response relationship, most trials delivered fewer than 16 sessions, below guideline recommendations. Although brief CBT can offer some benefit,97 extending therapy or offering booster sessions may help consolidate gains, particularly for entrenched beliefs.111 Fourth, the weaker and inconsistent effects on voice-related beliefs suggest that CBT may reduce distress more readily than content, highlighting the need for targeted protocols addressing malevolence, benevolence, and command hallucinations.92,102,103

Finally, the observed covariation between belief and symptoms change supports mechanism-based clinical monitoring, encouraging therapists to track dysfunctional beliefs and reasoning styles as both therapeutic targets and indicators of progress.

Strengths

This review has several strengths. It is, to our knowledge, the first meta-analysis to jointly synthesize CBT effects on both dysfunctional beliefs and cognitive biases in SSD, directly addressing mechanism-level targets central to cognitive models of psychosis. A major strength is the explicit separation of randomized ITT evidence from nonrandomized and within-arm designs, with primary conclusions based on ITT RCT estimates only, thereby reducing attrition-related bias.

Methodological rigor was reinforced through PRISMA-guided procedures, structured risk-of-bias assessment, and refined analytic strategies designed to prioritize ITT RCT evidence. Meta-regression analyses linking belief change to symptom improvement provide a novel quantitative contribution to the mechanisms-of-change literature.

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered. First, the number of RCTs per construct was modest, and no belief or bias domain reached ten trials, limiting precision of domain-specific estimates. Although subgroup analyses by control type were conducted, comparator-specific strata remained small, reducing power to detect subgroup differences.

Second, heterogeneity was moderate to high for voice-related beliefs, likely reflecting variability in CBT protocols and outcome measures. Third, most trials delivered fewer than recommended session numbers, which may have attenuated observed effects.

Fourth, samples were predominantly outpatient, limiting generalizability to inpatient settings. Trials conducted exclusively in ultra-high-risk samples were excluded from primary pooled analyses to avoid mixing risk-state and diagnosed SSD populations, but stage-specific analyses were not feasible due to limited data.

Fifth, many trials assessed dysfunctional beliefs as secondary or mediator outcomes, which may have reduced statistical power and sensitivity to change. Trials designed specifically to target these constructs as primary outcomes may yield larger effects. Finally, although publication bias was formally assessed, reporting bias cannot be fully excluded.

Future Directions

Several research avenues emerge. Adequately powered, trials that explicitly target dysfunctional beliefs and cognitive biases as primary outcomes are needed to test cognitive models more stringently. Beyond content-focused CBT, future research should evaluate metacognitive interventions and processes such as belief updating as complementary mechanisms of change. Integration of these approaches may maximize CBT’s efficacy and broaden its impact.

Clarifying whether changes in dysfunctional beliefs mediate symptom reduction remains a key priority. Current evidence is inconsistent, highlighting the need for longitudinal within-subject designs capable of establishing temporal precedence in mechanism–symptom relationships.112 Given the heterogeneity of effects, moderators such as symptom subtype, illness stage, and treatment duration should be examined to inform more personalized intervention strategies.

Conclusion

Primary randomized evidence indicates that CBT produces small but reliable improvements in dysfunctional beliefs in SSD, particularly for delusional conviction and self-related schemas, whereas effects on cognitive biases, including reasoning and interpretation biases, remain limited and inconsistently studied. Improvements in dysfunctional beliefs covary with symptom improvement, supporting cognitive models that position maladaptive beliefs as maintenance mechanisms. Overall, the findings support CBT’s capacity to modify key belief targets while underscoring important theoretical, methodological, and measurement challenges that remain for translating these mechanism-level changes into broader clinical outcomes.
