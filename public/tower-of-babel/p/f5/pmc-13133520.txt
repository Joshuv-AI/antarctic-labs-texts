Finding the genes that fit CPX-351
Authors: Ameya Hanamshet, Jad Othman
Published: 2026

In this issue of Blood Advances, Shimony et al1 describe DNA-sequencing results and molecular reclassification of patients treated on the phase 3 registration study of CPX-351.

CPX-351 is a liposomal formulation of cytarabine and daunorubicin in a preclinically optimized synergistic 5:1 ratio. In a phase 2 study of older patients with newly diagnosed acute myeloid leukemia (AML), CPX-351 demonstrated particular efficacy in the subgroup with clinical secondary AML (sAML).2 This led to a phase 3 registration study (CLTR0310-301) of patients aged 60 to 75 years with either therapy-related AML (t-AML), AML with antecedent myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) or chronic myelomonocytic leukemia (CMML), or AML with MDS-related cytogenetic abnormalities. Compared with a control arm of 7+3 induction with 5+2 consolidation, CPX-351 was associated with improved overall and event-free survival, higher remission rates, and more patients receiving stem cell transplantation.3

CLTR0310-301 limited enrollment to patients with clinical sAML, a group enriched for adverse cytogenetics, MDS-related (MR) genomics, and TP53 mutations, but not a homogeneous or biologically distinct entity. Indeed, the patients included in the trial varied from favorable-risk diseases such as core-binding factor, CEBPA bZIP, and NPM1-mutant AML to the extremely adverse group with complex karyotype and TP53 mutations and everything in between. These groups have vastly different outcomes after intensive chemotherapy and would not be expected to derive similar benefits from novel therapies. Modern classification systems strongly prioritize genomic results to define leukemia subgroups.4,5

Shimony et al have deciphered this heterogeneity by sequencing available diagnostic samples (184/309 patients) from CLTR0310-301. As expected, AML-MR (48%) and TP53 mutations (34%) were common. The results allowed for reclassification of patients into the following 4 groups: (1) TP53-AML; (2) DDX41-AML; (3) AML-MR (1 or more mutations in ASXL1, BCOR, EZH2, SF3B1, SRSF2, STAG2, U2AF1, or ZRSR2); and (4) other-AML. Although these groups do not strictly align with the classifications of the World Health Organization or International Consensus Classification, they were reasonable choices in the context of the study population.

The original clinical subgroups (t-AML, prior MDS/CMML, MDS cytogenetics) had similar survival, reinforcing that these groups are both heterogeneous and overlapping. In contrast, the new molecular subgroups had significantly different remission rates and 2-year overall survival (OS) (TP53-AML, 7%; AML-MR, 19%; other-AML, 37%; DDX41-AML, 70%). More importantly, the molecular classification identified a subgroup, those with AML-MR, who derived the most benefit from the experimental arm, with median OS after CPX-351 at 9.7 months compared with 6.8 months with 7+3. The survival benefit with CPX-351 in AML-MR was only apparent in patients who received allogeneic transplant, with no difference among patients who did not undergo transplantation, as had been noted in the overall study population. Meanwhile, no survival difference was found in TP53-AML, with poor OS regardless of therapy (4.5 vs 5.1 months).

The reclassification performed by Shimony demonstrates how genetic features, rather than clinical categorization, more accurately represent biology and prognosis, including within clinical sAML. Although this analysis was restricted to older patients, similar results were found in the NCRI AML19 trial, comparing CPX-351 with FLAG-Ida in younger patients with adverse cytogenetic AML or high-risk MDS.6 In this study there was no difference in outcomes overall, however an exploratory subgroup of patients with MDS-related gene mutations saw significantly improved OS with CPX-351. Real-world cohorts have also reported promising outcomes in patients with AML-MR treated with CPX-351.7

An important, as yet unanswered, question is why patients with AML-MR defined by mutations derive particular benefit from CPX-351. Lower toxicity is a potential explanation, although Shimony reported that, if anything, the prolongation of count recovery with CPX-351 was more pronounced in the AML-MR group, and early mortality was similar. Transplant-related mortality was lower after CPX-351, so it is possible that CPX-351 delivers patients to transplant in a better state.8 Antileukemic efficacy seems not to be the answer, with remission rates within AML-MR not significantly higher, and post-transplant relapse not reduced by CPX-351.8 A potential impact of deeper remissions could not be assessed due to a lack of measurable residual disease (MRD) data. Could the explanation instead be related to the removal of the TP53-mutant patients from the analysis? Patients with TP53 mutation had equally poor outcomes with both treatment arms, and once they are removed, the benefit of CPX-351 becomes more apparent in the remaining patients, of whom >70% are from the AML-MR group.

The results of this study bring forth the tantalizing possibility of molecularly personalized treatments in AML. However, it is important that clinicians do not generalize these results beyond the populations treated in the studies, to all patients with AML-MR. CLTR0310-301 only included clinical sAML, and AML19 only adverse cytogenetic AML and high-risk MDS. A significant proportion of AML-MR cases occur in clinical de novo AML, including with favorable or intermediate cytogenetics.9 Whether these patients (de novo AML with MR mutations but not MR cytogenetics) also benefit from CPX-351 is unresolved. Indeed, in the only reported randomized controlled trial (RCT) of de novo AML without adverse cytogenetics (NCRI AML18), CPX-351 was associated with worse morphological and MRD response rates, EFS, and OS compared with standard therapy (daunorubicin, cytarabine, and gemtuzumab ozogamicin), including in the subgroup with MDS-related mutations. The ongoing ALFA-2101 and AMLSG30-18 trials will further clarify this, but in the meantime, clinicians should be cautious not to extrapolate these findings beyond the populations in which they were demonstrated.

Finally, these results need to be considered in light of increasing data suggesting that hypomethylating agents and venetoclax may be an ideal approach for adverse-risk AML, even if fit for intensive therapy. In particular, the recently reported results from PARADIGM, a phase 2 RCT comparing venetoclax-azacitidine with chemotherapy (7+3 or CPX-351), suggest that lower intensity approaches may be a less toxic and equally or more effective way to deliver these patients to allogeneic transplant.10 Further analysis of molecular subgroups in PARADIGM, along with other similar trials, is awaited.

Shimony et al are to be commended on a thorough and clearly presented study. Their results advance the field further in its goal to treat the right patients with the right therapies. They also emphasize the urgent need for rapid and accurate molecular diagnostics, with results available before the start of induction therapy.
