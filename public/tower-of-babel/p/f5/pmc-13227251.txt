Early-onset multisystem Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis with pulmonary and cutaneous involvement in a 3.5-month-old infant: A case report
Authors: Narges khazaei, Ruhollah Shirzadi, Seyed Hossein Mirlohi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLangerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare clonal proliferative disorder characterized by the accumulation of Langerhans-type dendritic cells in various tissues, including the lungs and skin [1]. One of the major challenges in this disease is its early diagnosis, the most important reason being that pulmonary involvement is rare in this disease. [2].Case PresentationA 3.5-month-old male infant presented to the emergency department of a children's hospital with symptoms of respiratory distress, mild fever, and vesicular-pustular skin lesions. Imaging studies revealed diffuse interstitial lung disease with bronchiectasis and cystic changes. Skin biopsy was positive for immunohistochemical markers CD1a and S-100, confirming the diagnosis of LCH. The patient initially required invasive mechanical ventilation and was subsequently managed with noninvasive mechanical ventilation and systemic corticosteroids, resulting in clinical improvement.ConclusionConsidering the patient presented, attention should be paid to Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis in infants who present with respiratory distress and skin lesions to enable early diagnosis and timely intervention.

Introduction

Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) is a rare clonal proliferative disorder characterized by the accumulation of Langerhans-type dendritic cells in various tissues, including the lungs and skin [1]. Signs and symptoms of LCH can begin at any age; however, the onset of the disease and the appearance of symptoms in infancy are very rare. If it does occur in infancy, it will be accompanied by the involvement of multiple body systems and a worse prognosis [2]. Due to the rarity of this disease in infants, clinical signs begin nonspecifically, including cough, tachypnea, and respiratory distress, which can delay diagnosis [3].

The pathogenesis of LCH remains incompletely understood but involves somatic mutations in the MAPK pathway, notably BRAF V600E and immune dysregulation [4]. The diagnosis of the disease is based on clinical suspicion, which can be confirmed with imaging findings. However, the final confirmation of the diagnosis is achieved through histopathological analysis, accompanied by immunohistochemical staining for CD1a and S-100 markers. [5]. There are different treatment plans for the disease, and the choice of treatment type varies based on the involvement of different organs. Among the available treatments is the use of systemic corticosteroids or systemic chemotherapy for multi-organ involvement [6].

This case report describes a 3.5-month-old infant presenting with respiratory distress and vesiculopustular skin lesions, whose diagnostic workup revealed pulmonary LCH, highlighting the challenges in diagnosis and management of this rare entity in early infancy.

Case Presentation

A 3.5-month-old male infant, the first child of non-consanguineous healthy parents, born full-term via spontaneous vaginal delivery without perinatal complications, presented to the pediatric emergency department with a 3-day history of low-grade fever and non-productive cough, progressing to respiratory distress. There was no prior history of recurrent infections, diarrhea, poor feeding, or failure to thrive. The family history was negative for immunodeficiency, autoimmune disorders, or consanguinity. Maternal history during pregnancy was unremarkable for infections or drug exposure.

On physical examination, the patient was ill-appearing, febrile (T 38.2 °C), tachypneic (respiratory rate: 66/min), tachycardic (heart rate: 172/min), with subcostal retractions and nasal flaring. Room-air oxygen saturation was 90%. Notably, multiple discrete vesiculopustular lesions were observed on the trunk, abdomen, and extremities. Cardiovascular and abdominal examinations were unremarkable; no hepatosplenomegaly was identified.

Given the severity of respiratory distress and hypoxemia, the patient was initiated on non-invasive ventilatory support, which was later escalated to endotracheal intubation due to clinical deterioration. Empirical broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics were commenced, and differential diagnoses at this stage included severe pneumonia, viral bronchiolitis, sepsis, myocarditis, and underlying immunodeficiency.

These findings were consistent with interstitial lung disease (ILD), and the differential diagnosis in infants includes congenital immunodeficiencies, surfactant protein dysfunction, NEHI, metabolic and immunologic diseases, infectious etiologies, and histiocytic disorders such as LCH [7,8].

The immunological workup—including serum immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM, IgE), complement levels (C3, C4, CH50), Nitroblue Tetrazolium (NBT) test, and HIV serology—was within normal limits. Bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) was non-diagnostic.

Given the persistent and characteristic skin lesions, a skin biopsy was performed. Histopathological evaluation revealed dermal infiltration by large histiocytic cells with pale eosinophilic cytoplasm and reniform, grooved nuclei. Immunohistochemical staining was positive for CD1a, S-100, and CD68—findings diagnostic of Langerhans Cell Histiocytosis [1,9].

Following histopathological confirmation, systemic corticosteroid therapy was initiated with clinical improvement.

The patient was discharged on supportive care with home NIV and scheduled outpatient follow-up. In subsequent visits, respiratory symptoms gradually resolved, and the patient became independent of ventilatory support.

This case represents an early-onset, multisystem LCH with pulmonary and cutaneous manifestations, emphasizing the diagnostic complexity and need for high clinical suspicion in similar presentations.

Discussion

Pulmonary LCH is considered a rare disease in infancy and most often occurs in childhood or adulthood. [1,3]. Patients present with nonspecific respiratory symptoms such as cough and distress, along with rare skin involvement, which is why the diagnosis is often delayed [3,9].

High-resolution CT scan findings in LCH vary with disease progression. In infants, features such as cystic bronchiectasis, ground-glass opacities, and a “crazy-paving” appearance may be seen, similar to other interstitial or infectious diseases. [7]. Therefore, if the aforementioned findings on lung CT scan are present in patients with skin lesions, LCH should be considered as a differential diagnosis.

Histopathological confirmation is essential, and immunohistochemical positivity for CD1a, S-100, and CD68 is the diagnostic hallmark [1,5,9]. In this patient, a skin biopsy provided the key to diagnosis, avoiding the need for an invasive lung biopsy.

Treatment for LCH varies depending on the extent of organ involvement. Single-system disease may respond to local therapy or even resolve spontaneously. Multisystem LCH, especially if major organs such as the lungs are involved, often requires systemic corticosteroids or chemotherapy. [6,10]. In our case, systemic corticosteroids led to marked clinical improvement.

Although BRAF V600E mutation testing was not performed in this case due to limited resources, molecular evaluation remains an important aspect of diagnosis and risk stratification in LCH [4].

Long-term follow-up is crucial, given risks of disease reactivation, chronic lung dysfunction, and late sequelae [11,12]. Clinical stability and radiologic improvement in our patient are encouraging, though continued surveillance is warranted.

This case reinforces the value of early skin biopsy, multidisciplinary evaluation, and consideration of LCH in the differential diagnosis of infantile respiratory distress with atypical skin findings.
