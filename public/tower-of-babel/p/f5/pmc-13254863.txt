Effect of Orthopedic Resident Surgical Education on Surgical Case Length and Healthcare Costs
Authors: Robert W. Gomez, Kevin G. Valdes, Kristofer S. Matullo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeThe study investigates the impact of resident involvement in orthopedic hand surgery, focusing on operative and room times, as well as the associated opportunity costs to physicians and the hospital. The primary objective was to examine the impact of resident participation on operative times and total surgical room times in comparison to attending-only procedures. The secondary objective was to assess the cost of resident education by analyzing differences in surgical efficiency for the procedures studied.MethodsData from a single academic teaching hospital were analyzed from 2020 to 2023. All procedures were done by a single fellowship-trained orthopedic hand surgeon, either with or without resident assistance. Procedures assessed included isolated endoscopic carpal tunnel release, isolated trigger finger release, thumb carpometacarpal joint interpositional arthroplasty, and isolated De Quervain release. Statistical differences in operative and total room times were evaluated, and opportunity costs were estimated using the identified differences found in these times.ResultsA total of 1,057 surgical cases were identified. The mean operative and room times were significantly longer when residents were involved in endoscopic carpal tunnel release, trigger finger release, and carpometacarpal procedures. No significant differences were found in mean operative and room times for De Quervain procedures. The estimated total cost, over the entire time period studied, to the hospital for resident involvement was $71,000.07.ConclusionsThis novel study highlights the cost of resident education in orthopedic hand surgery. Resident surgical involvement is critical to their training. It is evident that there is a substantial cost associated with educating resident physicians that impacts both teaching surgeons and hospitals. The continued development of appropriate compensation models is necessary to continue to encourage resident participation while managing costs effectively.Type of study/level of evidenceEconomic and decision analysis II

There is a lack of information regarding the impact on resident education or surgical efficiency within the field of orthopedic hand surgery, specifically measured by operative time. Orthopedic surgery is a growing field, and the number of orthopedic residency training programs increased from 162 in 2014 to 173 in 2024.1,2 Teaching hospitals face the unique challenge of providing quality and efficient care while training future professionals in an era in which institutions are concerned with managing costs and maximizing patient volume.

Active participation in the operating room plays a crucial role in the development of highly skilled resident physicians. Several orthopedic studies have analyzed large data sets from the American College of Surgeons National Quality Improvement Program database and have found no significant difference in patient outcomes, but have noted increased operative times when residents are involved compared with attending-only procedures.3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 Other studies have reported similar findings based on single-center cohorts.10, 11, 12 In contrast to these findings, a study utilizing the American College of Surgeons National Surgery Quality Improvement Project (ACS-NSQIP) database specifically measured the economic impact associated with resident involvement in shoulder arthroplasty, and they reported shorter operative times.13 The authors have not found any studies that specifically focus on these issues in hand and wrist surgery.

The primary objective of our study was to investigate the impact of resident involvement on operative times and total surgical room times in orthopedic hand cases as compared with attending-only procedures. The secondary objective was to evaluate the cost of resident education by examining differences in operative and room times for these procedures.

Methods

Institutional review board approval was obtained. A retrospective analysis of cases from January 2020 to June 2023, performed by a single attending orthopedic hand surgeon at a single academic teaching hospital, was performed. Data collected included procedure type, presence of a resident, training year of the resident (if present), in-room time, procedure start time, procedure complete time, and out-of-room time. Procedures assessed were isolated endoscopic carpal tunnel release (ECTR), isolated trigger finger release (TFR), thumb carpometacarpal (CMC) joint interpositional arthroplasty, or isolated De Quervain (DeQ) release. Resident involvement was examined as a binary variable with cases sorted as either resident involvement with attending or attending without resident. Resident involvement varied between postgraduate year 3 (PGY3) and PGY5 cases. Postgraduate year 5 residents completed all procedures skin-to-skin. PGY3 residents completed fewer technical procedures skin-to-skin. In more complicated surgical procedures, PGY3 residents completed all noncritical portions and were given the opportunity to complete the critical aspects of the case with education and guidance from the attending surgeon. However, instances where the procedural difficulty surpassed the resident’s skill level were handled by the attending surgeon. Cases in which the attending was supported by a physician assistant were designated as cases without resident involvement. Cases that were supported by a physician assistant and a resident were excluded from analysis. Subgroups were made for cases with resident involvement by postgraduate year (PGY).

Statistical analysis was performed using statistical computing software IBM SPSS Statistics (v 29.0.1.0). An independent sample t test was used to compare the mean operative and room times in cases with residents versus those without. A secondary independent sample t test analysis was then utilized to compare the mean operative and room times based on resident PGY training year.

The calculation of opportunity cost to the hospital was performed for each year. A subgroup analysis of opportunity costs was conducted for each procedure using differences in room time. To ensure greater accuracy in cost analysis, appropriate work and facility relative value units and Medicare conversion factors adjusted for each year were utilized (Supplemental Table 1, available online on the Journal’s website at https://www.jhsgo.org). The cost calculations were based on the operating room cost per minute.14

Results

A total of 1,057 patients were identified from the electronic medical record. This included 686 patients who underwent isolated ECTR, 284 patients with TFR, 47 patients with isolated CMC, and 40 patients with isolated DeQ release. Discussion

This study aimed to identify and quantify significant differences in operative times based on resident involvement. At our institution, PGY-3 and PGY-5 residents participate in hand rotations. The results revealed that cases involving ECTR, TFR, and CMC procedures exhibited significantly longer durations when residents were involved. Specifically, ECTR and CMC cases experienced the highest percentage increase in operative time, at 24.3% and 24.5%, respectively. This implies that an attending surgeon could potentially complete five cases for every four done with a resident. Increased surgical time with resident involvement can be attributed to multiple causes mainly including increased surgical dissection time, pause in cases for guidance from attending surgeon, or other applicable teaching moments. Interestingly, our results demonstrate no significant differences in operative times for DeQ releases among cases with and without resident involvement. It is important to note that the analyzed procedures were relatively simple and quick surgical procedures, suggesting that differences in surgical times may be even more pronounced for longer and more complex surgeries, as seen with the increase in complexity from an ECTR to a CMC procedure. However, it is also plausible that resident involvement would be limited in longer and more complex procedures. There is a clear opportunity cost to the hospital and attending physicians. Furthermore, in a model where physicians are rewarded for billing relative value units, these costs may be exacerbated. This raises concerns about disincentives for resident participation within a productivity-based attending compensation model, as residents require time to develop their surgical approach, speed, and efficiency. Nevertheless, operative experience remains critical for training future attending surgeons.

The study is novel in its focus on the cost of resident education within the field of orthopedic hand surgery. The finding regarding increased surgical times with resident involvement aligns with the majority of orthopedic literature in other subspecialties.4, 5, 6, 7,9, 10, 11, 12,15 Of note, one study concluded that resident involvement was associated with shorter operative times in total shoulder and hemishoulder arthroplasties with the exception of cases performed by PGY-3 and PGY-4 residents.13 Moreover, a study in plastic surgery analyzed operative times for various hand procedures using the ACS-NSQIP database.16 Although most research on this topic utilized the ACS-NSQIP database, which allows for a greater number of confounding variables, our study provides better control over these confounders as all surgeries were performed at a single academic teaching hospital by a single attending orthopedic hand surgeon. Consequently, we feel our study uniquely offers a greater consistency in accounting for resident involvement than the ACS-NSQIP, which is unable to determine the extent of resident participation.

This retrospective review is subject to inherent limitations, including missing data, coding errors, and the inability to examine specific surgical steps performed by residents. Additionally, the study’s focus on a single institution and an individual surgeon may limit its generalizability. However, one benefit is that the single surgeon provides consistency in the steps and progression that a resident proceeds through during training. We also recognize that our institution’s costs are variable when compared with others, and this may have influenced the outcomes of the study. It is also worth noting that Graduate Medical Education does receive funding from Medicare at teaching hospitals educating resident physicians; however, there is a consensus that the cost of training a resident, especially at academic hospitals, is greater than the funding provided by Medicare.17, 18, 19 Despite these limitations, this research provides a meaningful contribution to the literature by offering information that does not solely rely on the ACS-NSQIP database, which is susceptible to the same limitations and a greater number of confounding variables. Additional studies on the topic of resident hand surgery education should be completed with opportunities for insight into complication rates and patient outcomes in resident versus nonresident procedures.

In conclusion, resident surgical involvement is critical to training and the betterment of the field of orthopedic hand surgery. However, when considering simple and quick orthopedic hand surgeries, the study demonstrates a significant increase in operative and room times when residents are involved. The increase comes at a considerable opportunity cost to attending physicians and hospitals within a productivity-based care model. Consequently, it is possible that this additional time may create disincentives for resident participation in surgeries, thereby hindering their learning experiences. Further research is needed to clearly identify the degree to which attending physicians are discouraged from allowing resident involvement due to increased surgical times. This may facilitate the development of appropriate compensation models that encourage resident participation while managing costs effectively. Additionally, investigations into resident hand surgery education are needed, with opportunities for insight into complication rates and patient outcomes in resident versus nonresident procedures that may also compound costs associated with resident education.
