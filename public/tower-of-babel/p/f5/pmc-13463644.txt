Design and therapeutic rationale of antibody-peptide conjugates: insights from maridebart cafraglutide (AMG133) and emerging applications
Authors: Junming Yie, Zongchun Mou, Xiaohong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTherapeutic peptides have become an important class of pharmaceuticals, driven in part by the clinical success of long-acting incretin therapies. Despite their potency and specificity, peptides are intrinsically limited by rapid proteolytic degradation and renal clearance, resulting in short plasma half-lives and frequent dosing. Antibody-peptide conjugates represent an emerging class of biotherapeutics that combine the long half-life and target specificity of monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) with the chemical tunability and pharmacological activity of peptides. Unlike fusion proteins, antibody-peptide conjugates decouple peptide synthesis from antibody expression, enabling incorporation of non-proteinogenic amino acids, cyclic structures, and other chemical features that enhance peptide stability, potency, and half-life. This review examines the biological rationale, molecular design principles, and enabling technologies underlying antibody-peptide conjugates development, using the GIPR antagonist/GLP-1R agonist conjugate maridebart cafraglutide (AMG133) as a central case study. We illustrate how antibody-peptide conjugates can integrate complementary mechanisms of action into a single molecular entity and achieve sustained exposure profiles that are difficult to realize with fusion-based approaches. The discussion is extended to antibody-peptide conjugates programs across metabolic disease, oncology, neurology, pain, ocular, and infectious disease, highlighting recurring design themes such as half-life extension, targeted delivery, receptor-mediated internalization, and synergistic activity. Collectively, antibody-peptide conjugates represent a biology-driven and strategically important therapeutic modality that bridges antibody engineering and peptide medicinal chemistry. When guided by strong mechanistic rationale, antibody-peptide conjugates offer a powerful solution for therapeutic challenges that cannot be adequately addressed by peptides or antibodies alone.Keywords: antibody-peptide conjugate, AMG133, chemical tunability, pharmacokinetics, synergism

Introduction

Therapeutic peptides have become an increasingly important class of pharmaceuticals, with more than 80 peptide-based drugs approved globally [1, 2]. The clinical success of glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) agonists has further accelerated interest in peptide therapeutics, particularly for metabolic diseases [3]. However, peptides suffer from inherent limitations, including rapid proteolytic degradation and renal clearance due to their small molecular size [4]. These properties result in poor pharmacokinetics (PK) such as short plasma half-lives (often measured in minutes) [5], thereby necessitating frequent injections, limiting their broader therapeutic utility. Multiple strategies have been developed to overcome these challenges, such as amino acid substitution, glycosylation, cyclization, PEGylation, lipidation, and fusion to large carrier proteins [5]. Among these, approaches that exploit the neonatal Fc receptor (FcRn) recycling pathway [6], through Fc, albumin fusion or albumin-binding lipidation [7, 8], have proven especially effective. FcRn-mediated recycling protects molecules from lysosomal degradation while increased peptide size reduces kidney filtration, collectively enabling substantial half-life extension. To date, more than 25 peptide drugs using these strategies have been clinically approved [9].

Peptibodies, which genetically fuse peptides to an Fc domain, represent a major advance by combining peptide activity with antibody-like PK properties [10–12]. Their antibody-like manufacturing process makes this a very cost-effective approach. Approved examples such as romiplostim and dulaglutide demonstrate the clinical value of this format [11, 12]. However, fusion-based approaches are constrained by recombinant expression systems and are therefore limited to proteinogenic amino acids. This restricts the incorporation of unnatural amino acids, cyclic structures, and other chemical features that are increasingly needed for optimizing peptide stability and potency [11]. Peptide chemical conjugation offers a more flexible alternative (Table 1). In this approach, peptides are synthesized independently, typically using solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS), allowing incorporation of unnatural amino acids, or the formation of cyclic structures [1, 13]. These chemically optimized peptides are then site-specifically conjugated to a delivery vehicle such as an Fc domain or antibody. By decoupling peptide synthesis from protein expression, conjugation-based approaches greatly expand the accessible chemical space while retaining the PK advantages of large biologics. In parallel, antibody conjugation technologies have advanced rapidly, driven by the clinical success of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs). To date, 13 ADCs have been approved by the FDA, supported by major innovations in antibody engineering, linker chemistry, payload design, and site-specific conjugation [14, 15]. These technological advances have broadened the antibody conjugate landscape beyond ADCs to include antibody-radionuclide conjugates (ARCs) [16], antibody-oligonucleotide conjugates (AOCs) [14, 17], peptide-drug conjugates (PDCs) [18], and, more recently, antibody-peptide conjugates (APCs).

APCs represent an innovative class of biotherapeutics that strategically merge the distinct advantages of the two powerful modalities [12]. The antibody component serves a dual role: it confers a dramatically extended circulatory half-life, overcoming the rapid clearance characteristic of free peptides, and it provides additional biological functionality such as precise tissue or cell targeting, target-mediated internalization, synergistic and cooperative activity with the peptide, or independent pharmacological activity. The peptide component offers chemical tunability and access to mechanisms of action that are difficult to achieve with antibodies alone. Together, APCs exemplify the convergence of antibody engineering and peptide medicinal chemistry. Critically, APC development must be guided by strong biological rationale and careful molecular design. Unlike fusion proteins, APCs allow fine control over conjugation site, stoichiometry, and linker chemistry [19], and better PK matching between functional components. APCs also uniquely enable the incorporation of chemically modified peptides with enhanced potency or half-life, an advantage that is not always achievable through fusion-based formats.

Recent years have witnessed rapid progress in APC development, with multiple candidates advancing into clinical trials. These developments highlight the increasing scientific and investment interest in APCs, particularly in metabolic diseases and oncology. This review examines the biological rationale, enabling technologies, and drug development strategies underlying APCs, using AMG133, a GIPR-Ab/GLP-1 conjugate, as a case study. As a representative example of this emerging modality, the discovery of the AMG133 illustrates how APC design can integrate complementary biological mechanisms within a single long-acting therapeutic molecule, enabling synergistic effects and improved pharmacokinetic matching between GIPR antibody and GLP-1 components. These properties have translated into promising clinical outcomes. We then extend the discussion to other APC programs currently under investigation.

Discovery of AMG133 for the treatment of metabolic disorders

Biology background

Glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) are two incretin hormones, as both are secreted by the small intestine in response to food intake and enhance glucose-dependent insulin secretion (Fig. 1a). Despite this shared role, their anatomical distribution and physiological functions diverge considerably. Anatomically, GIP is secreted by K-cells, the enteroendocrine cells located in the upper tract of small intestines, and its secretion is primarily induced by fat ingestion and to a much less extent by glucose [20, 21]. In contrast, GLP-1 is secreted by L-cells, the enteroendocrine cells located in the lower tract of small intestines, and its secretion can be induced by various nutrients, most robustly by a mixed meal [22]. Functionally, GIP promotes adipocyte fat storage in concert with insulin, in addition to its incretin effect, and can stimulate glucagon secretion under hypoglycemic conditions [23], although this occurs only when pure fat is ingested, which is uncommon in daily life. GIP also exerts central effects, including promoting satiety, suppressing emesis, and supporting cognition. In contrast, GLP-1 primarily induces satiety, delays gastric emptying, and may exert direct anti-inflammatory effects, in addition to its incretin action (Fig. 1a) [24]. GLP-1’s role in appetite suppression is underscored by its remarkable commercial success in obesity therapeutics, although its potent anti-satiety effect largely reflects pharmacological actions achieved through long-acting GLP-1 analogs at supra-physiological levels. These effects are commonly accompanied by gastrointestinal side effects, which can be mitigated through gradual dose escalation. Moreover, GLP-1-induced weight loss is associated with reductions in lean mass, ranging from approximately 15% to 40% of total weight loss [25, 26], as well as decreases in resting metabolic rate [27, 28], likely representing a physiological adaptation to reduced caloric intake. High discontinuation rates, driven by tolerability and economic factors, and rapid weight regain following treatment cessation, often within 1 year [29–33], highlight the limitations of GLP-1 monotherapy.

The role of the GIP/GIP receptor (GIPR) pathway in obesity was elucidated from two complementary lines of evidence. In rodents, GIPR knockout mice are protected from high-fat-diet-induced weight gain [34, 35]. These mice preferentially oxidized fat rather than carbohydrate as an energy source during short-term high-fat feeding, as reflected by a lower respiratory quotient (RQ), and exhibit increased whole-body energy expenditure during prolonged high-fat feeding, indicated by elevated oxygen consumption [34, 35]. Interestingly, although GIPR knockout mice maintain body weights comparable to wild-type mice when fed a chow diet throughout their lifespan, they display reduced fat mass, increased lean mass, and lower intramuscular fat accumulation with aging, resulting in improved insulin sensitivity and an apparent extension of lifespan [36, 37]. These findings support the long-term metabolic benefits of GIPR blockade. In humans, GIP hypersecretion in obese individuals has been recognized for decades [38–40]; however, its association with obesity was more firmly established through recent human genetic studies. Large-scale genome-wide association studies (GWAS), together with more recent whole-exome sequencing studies, have provided insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying obesity and related metabolic diseases. Numerous loci associated with body mass index (BMI) have been identified, among which GIPR consistently emerges as one of the strongest signals [33, 41–45], alongside genes involved in central regulation of food intake. In line with these findings, multiple classes of GIPR inhibitors, including peptides [46–51], small molecules [52, 53], and blocking antibodies, have been developed. However, when administered as monotherapies, their overall effects on weight loss have generally been modest.

Rationale to combine GIPR antagonism and GLP-1 agonism and molecular design

Gastric bypass surgery is highly effective in producing sustained weight loss [54]. The standard proximal Roux-en-Y gastric bypass (RYGB) induces gastric restriction and reroutes ingested nutrients to bypass most of the stomach and part of the proximal intestine, primarily the duodenum, while leaving sufficient small intestine intact to avoid clinically significant malabsorption. In a study with 14 years of follow-up, substantial weight loss was achieved and maintained, with 37% weight loss at 1 year and 30% at 14 years [55]. In addition to calorie restriction, RYGB induces rapid and profound hormonal changes that contribute to diabetes remission and sustained weight loss. Two major hypotheses have been proposed: the lower intestinal hypothesis and the upper intestinal hypothesis. The lower intestinal hypothesis emphasizes enhanced secretion of GLP-1, which is markedly increased after RYGB [54]. Under the upper intestinal hypothesis, although exclusion of a short segment of proximal small intestine from contact with ingested nutrients is well known to confer significant metabolic benefits, the underlying mechanisms remain less well defined. Decreased GIP levels have been reported in some studies following RYGB surgery [56]; however, these findings have not been consistent, and both the magnitude and direction of change appear to depending on the extent of proximal intestinal bypass. Notably, consistently reduced GIP levels have been observed following biliopancreatic diversion (BPD), a bypass procedure that produces similar weight loss benefits and involves exclusion of the proximal intestine [57, 58]. In contrast, GLP-1 levels were not significantly changed following BPD surgeries. Although reduced GIP secretion is unlikely to be a driver of immediate diabetes resolution, it may contribute to sustained weight loss. Given the distinct anatomical distribution of K cells versus L cells along the small intestine and the functional differences between GIP and GLP-1, the concept of combining GIP antagonism with GLP-1 agonism for weight loss is biologically plausible and warrants further investigation.

Consistent with this idea, combining a GIPR-neutralizing antibody with GLP-1R agonist such as liraglutide or dulaglutide in diet-induced obese (DIO) mice resulted in significant, synergistic weight loss that exceeded the additive effects of either treatment alone [59, 60]. This synergy was further confirmed in-house using a murine GIPR-neutralizing monoclonal antibody (mGIPR mAb) in combination with another GLP-1R agonist semaglutide. As shown in Fig. 1b, mGIPR mAb alone reduced body weight gain, while its combination with a low-dose semaglutide produced greater weight loss than either agent alone, consistent with a synergistic effect as measured using the Bliss independent model (Bliss score = 0.083; Bliss score > 0 indicates synergy). Notably, a high-dose semaglutide alone achieved a similar magnitude of weight loss as the combination. However, upon treatment withdrawal, body weight rebound rapidly in the high-dose semaglutide group, whereas the combination group exhibited a marked slower regain. Interestingly, when the combination group (Semaglutide-L + mGIPR mAb) were further divided into subgroups receiving either mGIPR mAb alone or vehicle alone, continued treatment with mGIPR mAb alone did not significantly differ from the vehicle group in maintaining this delayed weight rebound. This delayed rebound could not be simply explained by the long half-life of mGIPR mAb (~7 days), as attenuated weight regain persisted for at least 8 weeks. Although the underlying mechanism remains unclear, these findings suggest that GIPR blockade may induce a sustained alteration in weight regulation, potentially resembling a form of “weight loss memory,” in contrast to the classical set-point theory in which weight is strongly defended following reduction [61, 62]. Moreover, administration of mGIPR mAb following high-dose semaglutide treatment also significantly reduced weight regain. Collectively, these findings support a potential role for GIPR blockade not only in enhancing weight loss but also promoting long-term weight maintenance.

In simple terms, weight loss can be achieved either by reducing food intake or by increasing energy expenditure. Considerable research has been devoted to identifying strategies that enhance energy expenditure. After all, the idea of losing weight while eating freely is inherently appealing. However, approaches based solely on increasing energy expenditure have consistently been associated with significant side effects. For example, a famous natural product ephedra, derived from herb Ephedra sinica (Ma Huang), sold under brand name Metabolife 356, was marketed for weight loss by increasing energy expenditure. It was later found to cause serious adverse effects including cardiovascular side effects, neurological disorders, and deaths, and was subsequently withdrawn from the market. Similarly, strong PPARα agonism has been associated with muscle degeneration without producing meaningful weight loss. Indeed, GIPR antagonism appeared to be one of the few approaches capable of mildly increasing energy expenditure without suppressing food intake. However, the resulting weight loss is modest, limiting its utility as a stand-alone therapy. In contrast, reducing food intake is generally a more effective strategy. For perspective, running a full marathon expends ~3000 calories in an average individual, an amount that can be easily offset by a single large meal. This imbalance helps explain why most therapeutic approaches to weight loss have focused on reducing food intake. Historically, however, many appetite-suppressing therapies acted by inhibiting reward pathways in the central nervous system, often leading to limited efficacy or unacceptable safety risks, including increased suicidality, until the advent of GLP-1 agonism. By combining GLP-1 agonism with GIPR antagonism, it is therefore possible to address weight loss through complementary mechanisms, targeting both reduced energy intake and increased energy expenditure.

Building on this understanding and experimental validation, efforts were undertaken to combine a GIPR mAb with GLP-1 into a single molecule. At the time, substantial internal expertise had been established at Amgen through earlier GLP-1 programs, complemented by insights from the literature. It was well recognized that the N-terminus of GLP-1 must remain free, as it is essential for biological activity and cannot be sterically blocked. Accordingly, a straightforward strategy was to fuse a GLP-1 analog to the N-terminus of either the heavy or light chain of a GIPR mAb. Dulaglutide, a GLP-1 analogue fused to the Fc fragment of IgG4, extends the half-life of GLP-1 from 1 to 2 minutes to 5 days, but its pharmacokinetics can only support once-weekly administration in patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) [63]. Similarly, romiplostim (AMG531), a thrombopoietin analogue fused to the Fc fragment of IgG1, prolongs its half-life to achieve once-weekly administration [64, 65]. However, regardless of the GLP-1 peptide design in the fusion protein, its ~5 day half-life is much shorter than the antibody’s typical ~21-day half-life. To address this mismatch, an antibody-peptide conjugate (APC) approach was adopted (Fig. 1c). Compared with fusion proteins, APCs offer greater design flexibility (Table 1), enabling incorporation of unnatural amino acids. However, this comes at the cost of more complex manufacturing and higher production costs. Numerous studies have shown that incorporation of the unnatural amino acid α-aminoisobutyric acid (Aib) at the second position of GLP-1 markedly increased resistance to proteolytic degradation, thereby improving serum stability. AMG133, an anti-GIPR Ab/GLP-1 conjugate, showed sustained weight loss and improved metabolic parameters in diet-induced obese mice and monkeys after weekly administration [66]. Clinical pharmacokinetic studies further demonstrated that the half-life of the GLP-1 moiety (~15 days) was closely aligned with that of the antibody (~22 days) [67]. This matched PK profile supported its progression into human clinical evaluation.

Clinical development of AMG133

In Phase 1 placebo-controlled trials for obesity, AMG133 demonstrated acceptable safety and pronounced dose-dependent weight loss [67]. The intact half-life of AMG133 is ~15 days, while the total antibody half-life is ~21 days, indicating a well-matched pharmacokinetic profile between the GLP-1 and antibody components. Across single ascending dose (SAD) and multiple ascending dose (MAD) cohorts, AMG133 reduced body weight (BW), BMI, and waist circumference compared with placebo, with a peak BW reduction of 14.5% in the highest MAD group. Notably, weight-lowering effects persisted for up to 150 days after the last dose across dosing regimens. Phase 2 studies were subsequently conducted in obese patients with and without T2D [68]. AMG133, later renamed maridebart cafraglutide (MariTide), achieved up to ~20% mean weight loss in non-T2D obese patients (vs. 2.6% with placebo) and ~17% in obese patients with T2D (vs. 1.4% with placebo), with weight loss continuing through Week 52 without reaching a plateau. In addition to substantial weight reduction, AMG133 produced a robust and sustained maximum HbA1c reduction of 2.2% in patients with T2D, along with improvements in key cardiometabolic measures, including waist circumference, blood pressure, hs-CRP, and select lipid parameters. No new safety signals emerged in Phase 2, with the most common adverse events being mild-to-moderate gastrointestinal reactions (Table 2). In summary, AMG133 has demonstrated significant dose-dependent weight loss with acceptable safety and tolerability in clinical studies, and multiple Phase 3 trials are underway.

Given its substantial potential as a weight-loss therapy, several potential side effects of AMG133 need to be discussed. First, reduction in bone mineral density (BMD) is a possible concern. GIPR is expressed in both osteoblasts and osteoclasts and plays a role in the regulation of bone density [69]. GIP-overexpressing mice exhibit enhanced osteoblast activity and resistance to age-related bone loss, whereas GIPR knockout mice show reduced cortical bone mass and impaired bone quality. Consistent with these findings, early studies linked carriers of the GIPR loss-of-function (LOF) variant rs1800437 (Glu354Gln) to lower BMD and increased fracture risk [70]. However, a large-scale human study involving ~1.2 million participants found no significant associations between rs1800437 or the rare missense variants rs139215588 (Arg190Gln) and rs143430880 (Glu288Gly) and either fracture risk or reduced BMD [71]. Moreover, recent clinical trials of AMG133, which included close BMD monitoring, detected no meaningful declines in BMD. Collectively, these data suggest that the impact of GIPR modulation on BMD is modest. As BMD reduction is a common physiological adaptation to weight loss, AMG133 appears comparable to existing therapies, and any potential effects can likely be mitigated through nutritional supplementation or other interventions.

A more concerning issue relates to the gastrointestinal (GI) side effects associated with GLP-1 therapy. Early clinical trials suggest that AMG133 may produce more pronounced GI adverse effects compared with semaglutide or tirzepatide. It is well established that GLP-1-related GI side effects can be mitigated through dose titration, whereby initiating treatment at lower doses allows for improved tolerability. However, the GLP-1 component of AMG133 has a substantially longer half-life than that of semaglutide or tirzepatide, and without a carefully optimized titration regimen, GI side effects may appear more severe. In addition, AMG133 exhibits more potent intracellular signaling, such as cAMP activation, in cells co-expressing GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) and GIPR [72]. While such co-expression occurs in pancreatic β cells and may also occur in the brain, most neuronal populations do not co-express both receptors. For example, GIPR is predominantly expressed in GABAergic neurons [73], whereas GLP-1R is mainly expressed in distinct neuronal populations that activate vagal pathways associated with nausea and vomiting. Nonetheless, the possibility of limited co-expression in certain cells cannot be excluded, and this may lead to unanticipated enhancement of GLP-1-mediated effects.

Reconciling the paradox: GIPR agonism versus antagonism in combination with GLP-1 therapy

There appears to be a paradoxical observation that both GIPR agonism and GIPR antagonism produce pronounced weight loss when combined with GLP-1 therapy, as exemplified by tirzepatide and AMG133. Several explanations have been proposed in the literature. One hypothesis suggests that GIPR is readily internalized upon agonist binding, such that sustained GIPR agonism induces receptor internalization and functional antagonism over time. However, while prolonged agonist exposure may lead to receptor desensitization and agonism-lead GIPR internalization is well documented [69, 74, 75], it cannot truly convert an agonist into an antagonist, as existing and newly synthesized receptors could still traffic back to the cell surface. A more plausible explanation might be provided by studies from Regmi and colleagues at Eli Lilly [76] and Yu and colleagues at UT Southwestern [77]. These studies demonstrate that pharmacological GIPR agonism can induce lipolysis in white adipose tissue and activate futile calcium cycling, thereby increasing energy expenditure. These findings help explain an important pharmacological phenomenon. Under physiological conditions, endogenous GIP secretion is food-induced and almost always accompanied by increased insulin levels, and together they promote fat storage in adipose tissue. Moreover, endogenous GIP is rapidly degraded, with a half-life of only 2–5 minutes. In contrast, pharmacological administration of a long-acting GIPR agonist can act independently on adipose tissue when there is no sufficient insulin around and induce lipolysis rather than fat storage. Additionally, long-acting GIPR agonists do not stimulate insulin secretion during fasting or low blood-glucose conditions, but may instead promote glucagon secretion [23], which could further enhance hepatic fatty acid oxidation. An additional and particularly intriguing finding is that GIP acts as a potent anti-emetic by signaling through GABAergic neurons in the area postrema of the hindbrain [78, 79]. Finally, GIPR agonist could also induce satiety independent of GLP-1, even though the degree of satiety provided may not be as pronounced as GLP-1 [80]. Together with GLP-1R agonism, GLP-1R/GIPR co-agonism emerges as a highly effective weight-loss strategy by conferring four key benefits of supraphysiological GIP signaling, besides well-known GLP-1 effects, including enhanced adipose lipolysis, glucagon induction in α cells, anti-emetic and satiety effects in the hindbrain, none of which seem to be prominent under physiological conditions. Extending this concept, GLP-1 itself could also be viewed as a hormone that is not strictly essential under normal physiological conditions and tends to exert relatively modest metabolic effects at endogenous levels. However, pharmacological administration of long-acting, supraphysiological GLP-1R agonists significantly amplifies these effects. In contrast, GIPR antagonism directly blocks the physiological actions of GIP. When combined with a long-acting GLP-1R agonist, as in the case of AMG133, this approach also produces synergistic weight loss. Both strategies have produced substantial weight loss in clinical trials; however, which approach offers superior long-term benefit and safety remains unclear and will require further study.

Other antibody-peptide conjugates across disease areas

Beyond AMG133, APCs have been explored across a wide range of therapeutic areas, each imposing distinct design requirements related to disease and target characteristics (Fig. 2a–l). Full antibodies or Fc fragments are commonly used as carriers to extend peptide half-life and improve pharmacokinetics. In more complex cases, the antibody may act synergistically with the peptide to modulate distinct pathways and enhance pharmacodynamic effects, as exemplified by AMG133 in metabolic diseases. In oncology, the high specificity of antibodies can also be leveraged to deliver peptides to targeted cells and facilitate internalization, analogous to ADC strategies. Furthermore, certain peptides capable of penetrating biological barriers enable APCs to cross barriers such as the blood–brain barrier (BBB) or ocular tissues and deliver the active component to exert its effect. OXM analog-based APCs for metabolic diseases

Oxyntomodulin (OXM) is a 37-amino acid peptide hormone derived from proglucagon and secreted by intestinal L cells and pancreatic alpha cells [96]. It activates both the GLP-1 receptor (GLP-1R) and glucagon receptor (GCGR), making it a natural template for dual-agonist strategies in metabolic disease. However, native OXM is rapidly cleared in humans, with a plasma half-life of ~12 minutes [96], necessitating substantial molecular engineering for therapeutic application. Most long-acting OXM analogs rely on two core design principles: incorporation of non-proteinogenic amino acids to improve proteolytic stability, and attachment to half-life extension elements such as albumin binders or Fc domains [97]. Substitution at position 2 with non-natural residue, confers resistance to dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) cleavage and has become a standard modification across GLP-1-derived peptides. These substitutions are frequently combined with fatty-acid acylation to enhance albumin binding and prolong systemic exposure [8, 33, 98].

Fc conjugation offers an alternative approach that leverages FcRn-mediated recycling while enabling extensive chemical optimization of the peptide component. Efinopegdutide (MK-6024) [81] exemplifies this design. The APC consists of a chemically synthesized 30-amino acid GLP-1R/GCGR dual agonist incorporating an Aib residue at position 2, an internal lactam bridge to stabilize secondary structure, and a terminal cysteine for site-specific conjugation. The peptide is linked via a 10 kDa polyethylene glycol (PEG) spacer to a non-glycosylated human IgG4 Fc produced in Escherichia coli (Fig. 2a). This modular architecture highlights a defining advantage of APCs: independent optimization of peptide pharmacology and antibody-derived pharmacokinetics. In clinical, efinopegdutide exhibits a prolonged pharmacokinetic profile in humans, with a half-life of approximately 131–176 hours [82] supporting once-weekly or longer dosing intervals. In Phase 2 studies [99, 100], weekly subcutaneous administration resulted in meaningful weight loss in individuals with obesity, with slightly lower efficacy observed in those with concomitant T2D. In patients with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), efinopegdutide produced greater reductions in liver fat content than semaglutide, despite comparable weight loss [81]. Efinopegdutide is currently being evaluated in a Phase 2 study in patients with metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH) (NCT06465186).

Beyond Fc fragments, intact immunoglobulins have also been explored as inert scaffolds for OXM peptide conjugation. Researchers at Janssen Biotherapeutics [84] described the chemical conjugation of an OXM analogue to an immunologically silent IgG4 antibody that lacks antigen-binding activity and functions solely as a carrier (Fig. 2e). Site-specific conjugation was achieved by introducing a unique cysteine residue into the heavy-chain CDR3 loop. The OXM peptide was chemically optimized with a non-natural Aib residue and C-terminal amidation, and equipped with a bromoacetylated lysine for selective linkage to the engineered cysteine. This homodimeric APC exhibited markedly prolonged circulation in both rodents and nonhuman primates, supporting the feasibility of once-weekly dosing and demonstrating the utility of inert antibody scaffolds for long-acting peptide delivery [84].

APCs for targeted cancer therapy

Cancer represents a major area of APC innovation, driven by the need for precise spatial and cellular control of peptide activity. One of the earliest APC platforms was Pfizer’s CovX-Body technology [101], which employs a universal antibody scaffold for peptide conjugation. PF-5057459 (CVX-241) was designed as a bispecific APC targeting vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and angiopoietin-2 (Ang2) [85]. Two independently synthesized peptides, a cyclic VEGF-binding peptide and a linear Ang2-binding peptide, were joined together using a branched azetidinone linker, and then site-specifically conjugated to defined lysine residues within the Fab arms of the scaffold antibody CVX-2000 (Fig. 2f). Animal PK studies demonstrated sustained exposure, with half-lives of approximately 84–143 h for Ang2 activity and 73–95 h for VEGF activity [85]. Although PF-5057459 was discontinued in Phase 1 (NCT01004822), it represents an early clinical exploration of the APC modality and highlights its design versatility, particularly for chemical modification and bispecific targeting.

More recently, APCs have been developed to enable cell-type-specific delivery of intracellularly active peptides. Petruzzella and colleagues [86] described an antibody-peptide inhibitor conjugate (APIC) platform in which non-natural peptide inhibitors (NNPIs) targeting cysteine cathepsins were conjugated to tumor-specific antibodies (Fig. 2h). These NNPIs were chemically designed for high selectivity and stability, and antibody conjugation enabled targeted delivery to cancer cells or osteoclasts, with receptor-mediated internalization facilitating intracellular peptide activity. This work underscores a central APC design rationale: antibodies can act not only as PK enhancers but also as delivery vehicles for peptides that are otherwise pharmacologically inaccessible.

A related but conceptually distinct strategy is antibody-peptide epitope conjugates (APECs), which redirect preexisting antiviral T-cell immunity toward tumor cells [87, 102]. In this approach, viral peptide epitopes, commonly derived from cytomegalovirus (CMV), are conjugated to tumor-targeting antibodies via protease-cleavable linkers (Fig. 2i). Following antibody-mediated tumor localization, proteolytic cleavage releases the viral peptide, which binds surface HLA class I molecules and renders tumor cells susceptible to cytotoxic T lymphocytes [87, 88]. This APC format illustrates how peptide conjugation can be used to manipulate immune recognition rather than direct receptor signaling.

RGD-based APCs further highlight the importance of peptide stabilization and multivalent targeting in oncology. RGD peptides, commonly used for cancer targeting, bind integrins such as αVβ3 but suffer from poor solution stability [103, 104]. Chemical cyclization and conjugation to Fc domains (Fig. 2b) markedly improve stability while preserving integrin binding. Recent designs have combined cyclic internalizing RGD peptides with immune checkpoint antibodies (Fig. 2j), enabling simultaneous engagement of tumor vasculature and immune effector cells [89]. These constructs demonstrate how APCs can be engineered to integrate targeting, penetration, and immune modulation within a single molecular framework.

APCs in neurology, pain, ocular and infectious diseases

Beyond oncology and metabolism, APCs have been explored in neurological, autoimmune, and infectious disease settings where peptide activity must be tightly controlled in space and time. In multiple sclerosis (MS), bifunctional peptide inhibitors (BPIs) were designed to simultaneously engage antigen presentation (signal 1) and block costimulatory signaling (signal 2). Early BPI constructs exhibited rapid clearance, and fusion-based approaches were limited by low expression yields and proteolytic instability [90, 105, 106]. Chemical conjugation of synthetic peptides to Fc domains (Fig. 2c) overcame these challenges, improving molecular stability and circulation time while preserving biological function [90, 91].

In pain research, APCs targeting the voltage-gated sodium channel NaV1.7 illustrate the use of antibodies primarily as delivery vehicles. Potent inhibitory peptides are highly selective for NaV1.7 but suffer from short half-life and poor tissue distribution. Site-specific conjugation of these peptides to antibodies via engineered cysteine residues (Fig. 2g) extended circulation time and enabled accumulation at NaV1.7-expressing neurons [92]. These studies also highlight key APC design challenges, including tissue penetration barriers and the difficulty of balancing potency, exposure, and off-target accumulation [93].

The impermeability of the BBB presents another opportunity for APC-based delivery strategies [107]. Fc conjugation of chemically modified BBB shuttle peptides (Fig. 2d) has been explored to generate modular carriers capable of transporting therapeutic payloads into the brain. In this context, the peptide functions primarily as a transport ligand for brain penetration, while the Fc domain provides systemic stability and long circulation time.

Ocular barriers pose a major challenge to the non-invasive drug delivery of macromolecular antibodies to targeted lesion sites. To address this limitation, a novel APC was developed, termed TAT-MPEG-antibody [94], for precise antibody delivery to the posterior segment and controlled release. Specifically, a therapeutic antibody (e.g. anti-VEGFA or anti-PDL1) was conjugated with the transactivator of transcription (TAT), a cell-penetrating peptide, via a reactive oxygen species (ROS)-responsive linker (Fig. 2k). Following topical administration, TAT facilitates enhanced ocular penetration, while the antibody is selectively released under oxidative conditions at the disease site. This strategy enables targeted delivery to deep fundus lesions for treating chorioretinal disorders.

Finally, antibody-bactericidal macrocyclic peptide conjugates (Fig. 2l) represent an emerging application in infectious disease [95]. Antimicrobial peptides are often limited by proteolytic instability and off-target toxicity [108]. Macrocyclization improves protease resistance, while antibody conjugation confers pathogen specificity and prolongs circulation [95]. This dual optimization demonstrates how APCs can simultaneously address stability, selectivity, and delivery challenges in antimicrobial therapy.

Conjugation strategies and design flexibility of APCs

Antibody-based conjugation technologies have matured significantly following the rapid development of ADCs, providing a foundation for APCs. In APCs, site-specific conjugation can be achieved at multiple positions on the intact antibody or Fc fragment, including engineered cysteine or lysine residues, the N297 glycosylation site, the N- or C-termini, or via interchain disulfide bonds for higher peptide-to-antibody ratios (PARs). A range of chemoselective and bioorthogonal conjugation methods is employed, such as maleimide-thiol reactions [87, 88, 94], click chemistry [86, 89], azetidinone acylation [85], bromoacetamide-thiol reactions [66, 67, 84, 92, 93], Sortase A-mediated ligation [90, 91, 95], and streamlined expressed protein ligation (SEPL) [107].

Despite sharing similar conjugation chemistries, APCs differ fundamentally from ADCs in design principles. While ADCs are typically optimized for targeted tumor cell delivery and intracellular release of cytotoxic payloads, APCs are more versatile and can be tailored according to biological function, as illustrated in Table 3 and summarized as the following aspects:

(i) Antibody component: unlike ADCs, where antibodies primarily mediate targeted delivery, the antibody in APCs may act as a carrier to extend peptide half-life or actively contribute to biological function through targeting or synergy with the peptide.

(ii) Linker design: APCs often employ non-cleavable linkers to maintain stability and allow the intact molecule to function systemically. However, cleavable linkers responsive to specific biologic conditions (e.g. proteases [87], redox environments [94], or others) can be introduced when controlled release is desired.

(iii) Peptide component: peptides are typically synthesized chemically, enabling incorporation of unnatural amino acids, cyclization, and other modifications that enhance stability, potency, and tunability.

Together, these features confer high modularity and design flexibility. This has enabled expansion of APC concept into emerging modalities such as lysosome-targeting chimeras (LYTACs), where the peptide recruits degradation machinery while the antibody provides target specificity. Examples include GLP-1-based LYTACs [109], cyclic RGD-based integrin targeting chimeras (ITACs) [110], and antibody-chaperone-mediated autophagy constructs (Ab-CMAs) [111], all of which demonstrate the potential of APC-based designs to drive targeted protein degradation.

Conclusion and perspective

APCs represent a versatile and biology-driven therapeutic modality that bridges the strengths of antibodies and chemically optimized peptides. From a functional standpoint, APCs can be broadly categorized into two classes. In the first, the primary objective is to enhance peptide pharmacokinetics, most notably half-life extension, where the antibody functions mainly as a long-acting carrier (Figs 1c, 2a–g) and physical linkage between antibody and peptide is not strictly required for biological synergy [81, 84, 85, 90, 92, 93, 104–107]. AMG133 exemplifies this class, in which Fc conjugation, rather than fusion, enables peptide exposure beyond what fusion proteins can practically achieve [67]. In the second class, physical linkage between antibody and peptide is essential for therapeutic efficacy (Fig. 2h–l), as it enables targeted delivery [87, 88, 94, 95, 102, 112], target-mediated internalization for intracellular activity [84, 86] or targeted degradation [109–111], or synergistic activity of the two components [89].

Beyond PK improvement, the APC platform has substantially expanded accessible peptide biology. Chemical synthesis enables incorporation of unnatural amino acids that are not available through proteinogenic expression systems, allowing fine-tuning of peptide stability, receptor bias, potency, and half-life [113, 114]. In parallel, advances in cyclic and conformationally constrained peptides, through head-to-tail cyclization, side-chain stapling, or macrocyclization, have significantly improved proteolytic stability and target engagement [13, 115], making these peptides particularly well suited for APC delivery strategies.

Despite these advantages, APC development remains constrained by several key challenges. Foremost, APCs require strong biological justification: they are most appropriate when multiple targets must be engaged within a single molecule, when at least one mechanism is best addressed by a peptide, and when fusion protein approaches are not viable due to biological, structural, or PK limitations [116]. In addition, APCs typically involve higher cost of goods and greater manufacturing complexity, as their production requires multiple coordinated steps, including peptide synthesis, antibody generation, and chemical conjugation, compared with the single, antibody-like process used for fusion proteins. Consequently, APCs are unlikely to broadly replace fusion-based modalities and will remain a targeted solution for specific, high-value applications.

Encouragingly, the economic landscape for APCs is improving. The growing availability of diverse unnatural amino acid building blocks [115, 117], advances in the research and development of cyclic or macrocyclic peptides [118, 119], together with the maturation of peptide synthesis technologies [120, 121] and the emergence of specialized contract research and manufacturing organizations, has significantly reduced peptide production costs. As access to large-scale, cost-effective peptide synthesis continues to expand, APC manufacturing is expected to become increasingly economical and scalable.

Clinically, the most advanced APC programs to date have emerged in metabolic diseases, where biological understanding is deep and peptide pharmacology is well established. The clinical progress of AMG133 has catalyzed renewed interest in APCs targeting obesity and related metabolic disorders. This momentum has extended beyond GLP-1 to dual- and multi-agonist strategies, including GLP-1/GIP, GLP-1/glucagon, and other peptide–receptor combinations, exemplified by programs such as Innovent Biologics’ IBI-3012 (αGIPR-GLP-1/GCG conjugate) and IBI3030 (αPCSK9-GLP-1/GCG/GIP conjugate) [83], and SixPeaks’ αActR2A/B-GLP-1 conjugates (WO2025027052A1). These efforts highlight the relative ease of expanding APC strategies within metabolic disease, particularly where unnatural amino acids can be leveraged to modulate peptide half-life and activity.

Looking beyond metabolism, broader application of APCs will require multiple conditions to align: the need to integrate more than one target into a single molecule, a clear advantage of using a peptide for at least one mechanism of action, the inability of fusion proteins to meet biological or manufacturing requirements, and the necessity of chemical peptide modification for activity or PK optimization. Given these constraints, APCs are likely to remain a specialized, but powerful, modality rather than a universal platform.

Finally, the choice between conjugation and fusion is ultimately dictated by scientific and practical considerations. Conjugation is preferred when peptides require precise chemical modifications or cannot be co-expressed with antibodies in mammalian systems such as CHO cells. This is particularly relevant for proteins expressed in alternative hosts (e.g. E. coli) due to toxicity [122, 123] or instability [124] in eukaryotic systems, enabling modular assembly through post-expression conjugation. Together, these considerations position APCs as a strategically valuable platform for addressing complex therapeutic challenges that cannot be adequately solved by conventional biologic formats.

All studies were conducted in accordance with an approved IACUC protocol. Male C57BL/6J DIO mice at 21 weeks of age, after 17 weeks of high-fat diet feeding, were randomized into several groups (n = 10 per group) based on body weight. In the mGIPR mAb monotherapy study, mice were treated once weekly with either vehicle (PBS) or mGIPR mAb (25 mg/kg) for 76 days. For the combination study, mice received low-dose semaglutide (0.04 mg/kg, every 3 days) either alone or in combination with mGIPR mAb (25 mg/kg, once weekly) for 3 weeks. On day 22, mice in the semaglutide-only group were switched to vehicle (PBS) and followed for an additional 8 weeks. Mice in the combination group were further divided into 2 groups and continued for 8 weeks with either vehicle (PBS) or mGIPR mAb (25 mg/kg) alone. In the semaglutide pretreatment study, mice were first treated with high-dose semaglutide (0.14 mg/kg, every 3 days) for 3 weeks. On day 22, they were randomized into 2 groups and treated for 8 weeks with either vehicle (PBS) or mGIPR mAb (25 mg/kg) alone. All treatments were administered by subcutaneous injection. Body weight was monitored from day 0 through day 76. For statistical analysis, all values are presented as mean ± SEM. For comparison between two groups, one-way ANOVA was performed.
