Genetic factors complicating recovery in patients with brain injury: Possible link between injury severity and posttraumatic neurodegeneration related to intracerebral amyloid deposition – A narrative review
Authors: Bruno Splavski, Matija Zupan, Tomaž Velnar, Senta Frol
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background: Traumatic brain injury (TBI) may initiate long-term neurodegenerative processes that complicate postinjury recovery. Both severe and repetitive mild TBI are associated with dysregulated amyloid precursor protein processing and intracranial deposition of amyloid-beta (Aβ), a hallmark of neurodegenerative disease. The objective of the study is to summarize current evidence linking TBI with posttraumatic neurodegeneration, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), and genetically mediated susceptibility factors influencing recovery.Methods: A narrative review of relevant literature was performed with a focus on mechanisms of posttraumatic vascular amyloid deposition and the role of genetic predisposition.Results: TBI triggers secondary injury mechanisms, including persistent neuroinflammation, excitotoxicity, mitochondrial dysfunction, and blood–brain barrier disruption, which promote sustained intracerebral Aβ deposition and impaired clearance, which is closely associated with CAA and increased risk of lobar intracerebral hemorrhage. Iatrogenic CAA represents a distinct Aβ-related entity with prolonged incubation and heterogeneous clinical presentation. It seems that genetic susceptibility, particularly apolipoprotein E epsilon 4 (APOEε4), is associated with increased amyloid burden, more severe cerebral contusions, and poorer long-term functional outcomes following TBI.Conclusion: TBI may accelerate neurodegenerative and amyloid-related vascular processes that adversely affect recovery. Genetic predisposition, especially APOEε4, appears to modulate posttraumatic intracranial amyloid deposition and long-term outcomes, highlighting the importance of individualized risk assessment in patients with TBI.

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) exerts profound acute and long-term effects on brain pathophysiology and structure, potentially initiating chronic neurodegenerative processes and complicating postinjury recovery. Mechanistic evidence indicates that TBI may accelerate neurodegenerative and amyloid-related vascular processes. Both a single severe TBI and repetitive mild TBI have been shown to accelerate neurodegeneration, in part by promoting intracranial amyloid-beta (Aβ) protein deposition – a hallmark of neurodegenerative diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD). This pathological cascade is thought to arise from injury-induced alterations in amyloid precursor protein (APP) processing, as TBI increases Aβ production from APP within damaged axons during both the acute and chronic phases of injury.[17,18] APP is a transmembrane protein essential for synapse formation and neuroplasticity; however, dysregulation of its proteolytic processing leads to the generation of neurotoxic Aβ peptides and activation of downstream degenerative pathways.[28]

TBI can initiate degenerative processes in the brain through a combination of mechanical damage, biochemical changes, and long-term disruptions to normal brain function. When the brain experiences a traumatic impact, such as from a fall, collision, or blast, neurons, blood vessels, and supporting cells can be stretched, torn, or compressed. This immediate physical damage sets off a cascade of secondary injury mechanisms that continue long after the initial trauma. One major process is neuroinflammation. After injury, microglia and astrocytes become activated to clear damaged tissue. While this response is protective in the short term, prolonged or excessive inflammation can damage healthy neurons and synapses. TBI can cause excitotoxicity and excessive release of neurotransmitters such as glutamate, which overstimulates neurons.[1] This overstimulation allows too much calcium to enter cells, triggering cellular stress and, in severe cases, cell death. Over time, repeated or unresolved excitotoxic damage can weaken neural networks. TBI also disrupts mitochondrial function, reducing the brain’s ability to produce energy efficiently. Neurons are highly energy-dependent, so impaired mitochondria make them more vulnerable to degeneration. In addition, damage to the blood–brain barrier after injury allows harmful substances to enter brain tissue, further increasing oxidative stress and inflammation. A shared molecular mechanism underlying many neurodegenerative diseases involves the intracranial deposition and propagation of misfolded proteins, including Aβ.[38,46] When triggered by TBI, this pathogenic cascade may hinder neurorehabilitation and adversely affect functional recovery.

Increasing evidence links neuroinflammation and neurotoxicity to cerebral microvascular amyloid deposition, commonly referred to as cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA).[27] CAA is a cerebrovascular disorder characterized by the deposition of Aβ proteins within the walls of small leptomeningeal and cortical vessels, resulting in vascular fragility and a predisposition to lobar intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH).[4,35] Iatrogenic CAA (iCAA) represents a distinct and recently recognized Aβ prion-like disease that predominantly affects younger patients with documented iatrogenic Aβ exposure and prolonged incubation periods.[35] Clinically, iCAA most often manifests with recurrent lobar ICH but may also present with seizures and progressive cognitive impairment.[2,9]

Irrespective of etiology, sustained intracerebral deposition of Aβ – frequently accompanied by tau pathology following TBI – is associated with an increased risk of subsequent neurodegenerative disease, reflecting a disruption in the balance between Aβ production and clearance after brain injury.[17]

In recent years, an increasing number of CAA and iCAA cases have been observed in clinical practice, underscoring the need for further focused investigation into these entities. At the same time, an increasing number of diagnosed cases of CAA and iCAA in clinical practice has drawn greater attention to these conditions and exposed important gaps in current knowledge. This apparent rise likely reflects an aging population, advances in neuroimaging, and improved clinical awareness.[43] Modern MRI techniques, particularly susceptibility-weighted imaging and amyloid-sensitive sequences, have enhanced the detection of microbleeds, cortical superficial siderosis, and inflammatory changes characteristic of CAA and iCAA, leading to more frequent recognition of cases previously underdiagnosed or misclassified. Despite these advances, the true incidence and prevalence of both conditions remain uncertain, and diagnostic criteria continue to evolve. iCAA presents challenges due to its heterogeneous clinical presentation and overlap with other inflammatory and neurodegenerative disorders, which can delay diagnosis and management. This is clinically significant, as timely immunosuppressive therapy may substantially improve outcomes in selected patients. The growing recognition of CAA and iCAA also raises unresolved questions regarding disease mechanisms, triggers of vascular inflammation, and their relationship to AD and other amyloid-related pathologies. Moreover, prognostic factors, long-term outcomes, and optimal treatment strategies, especially for recurrent or treatment-resistant cases, remain poorly defined.[24] These challenges highlight the need for focused, multidisciplinary research integrating clinical, imaging, biomarker, and pathological data to improve diagnosis, treatment, and patient outcomes.[7,10,11,31,33,39,47] It seems that clinical TBI outcomes are also modulated by genetic predisposition.[6] Accordingly, apolipoprotein E epsilon 4 (APOEε4) appeared as the genetic factor that has been linked to posttraumatic cerebral contusions but also to an increased intracranial amyloid deposition.[30]

Therefore, we sought to investigate further the potential links between TBI and neurodegenerative proteinopathies, as well as the contribution of genetically determined factors that may influence injury severity and posttraumatic recovery.

METHODS

We searched PubMed up to May 21, 2026, for studies reporting associations between injury severity and posttraumatic neurodegeneration related to genetically associated factors complicating recovery in patients with TBI. To be eligible for the present narrative review, the studies had to be published as full-text articles in English language. All PubMed abstracts related to the objective but not written in English, as well as those unrelated to the search terms, were excluded from the analysis.

DISCUSSION

The existing studies on this matter are mostly burdened with conflicting evidence, heterogeneity of TBI phenotypes, variability in APOE findings, and challenges in translating mechanistic findings into clinical prognostication. To address these limitations, we paid particular attention to investigate the association between TBI and neurodegeneration, as well as between TBI and genetic risk factors.

Association between TBI and neurodegeneration

TBI is increasingly recognized as a chronic condition that may deteriorate over time and jeopardize long-term recovery,[5] potentially through mechanisms related to posttraumatic neurodegeneration. However, establishing a clinical diagnosis of posttraumatic neurodegeneration remains challenging, as it is often difficult to disentangle the direct consequences of TBI from progressive neurodegenerative processes or other late posttraumatic sequelae, such as cognitive impairment,[14] which may further complicate rehabilitation and recovery. Moreover, TBI and neurodegeneration exhibit a bidirectional relationship, whereby TBI increases susceptibility to neurodegeneration, while preexisting or evolving neurodegenerative pathology may, in turn, exacerbate TBI outcomes.[14]

Several pathophysiological mechanisms have been proposed to link TBI with the later development of neurodegenerative diseases, including neuronal loss,[25,37] persistent neuroinflammation,[13] and cytoskeletal pathology.[12,36] Among these, altered production, deposition, and clearance of Aβ peptides following TBI appear to represent particularly plausible mechanisms,[18] either by amplifying neuronal injury and cell death,[15] or through direct amyloid deposition leading to CAA and affecting the TBI outcomes. Both environmental and genetic factors contribute to the development and progression of CAA[4] and may also influence recovery after TBI.

Association between TBI and genetic risk factors

Encouraging evidence links specific genetic variants to the pathophysiology of brain trauma, as TBI severity and clinical outcomes appear to be modulated by genetic susceptibility,[6] influencing recovery trajectories, rehabilitation outcomes, the risk of posttraumatic seizures, and long-term cognitive and behavioral function.[19]

Variability in disease course and outcome across neurodegenerative disorders and TBI suggests a contributory role for genetic factors as potential prognostic markers.[24] Among genetic factors, APOEε4 is the most extensively studied variant in this context.[40] It is a key regulator of lipid metabolism and one of the strongest genetic risk factors for neurodegenerative diseases,[24] primarily due to its association with misfolded Aβ deposition.

The introductory observational study demonstrated that APOEε4 increases genetic susceptibility to depositing Aβ protein in the brain after TBI. Clinicopathologically, it has been linked to more severe cerebral contusions following TBI and to increased intracranial amyloid deposition.[30] It also plays a central role in Aβ metabolism, promoting amyloid deposition within small cerebral vessels. This genetic association that confers an increased risk for CAA and CAA-related lobar ICH was confirmed by a large-scale case– control multiple-cohort study.[3]

In this context, CAA-related subdural hematoma (SDH) should also be considered, given the under-recognized association between acute lobar ICH and concomitant SDH in patients with CAA.[47] Furthermore, cases of CAA with recurrent ICH in individuals with a history of severe childhood TBI have been reported, suggesting a potential long-term interaction between early brain injury and subsequent amyloid-related vascular pathology.[31]

Beyond amyloid deposition, the mechanisms by which APOEε4 influences TBI outcomes are likely multifactorial, encompassing cytoskeletal destabilization, cholinergic dysfunction, oxidative stress, impaired neuroprotection, and altered cerebral plasticity in response to injury.[19] Accordingly, genetic predisposition related to APOEε4 polymorphism may modulate the risk of posttraumatic neurodegeneration,[17] adversely affecting recovery and long-term outcomes. Importantly, the risk of developing neurodegenerative disease appears to be proportional to TBI severity,[34] with moderate and severe TBIs – particularly in younger individuals – conferring increased lifetime risk. These observations support a synergistic interaction between TBI severity and the APOEε4 genotype as determinants of neurodegenerative risk.[34]

Despite comparable acute pathology, TBI outcomes can vary substantially between individuals. Genetic vulnerability may partially account for this variability, alongside established prognostic factors such as age, injury severity, and therapeutic interventions.[16] APOEε4 has been implicated in persistent post-TBI impairment,[8] prolonged recovery, and poorer long-term functional outcomes.[20-22] It has therefore been proposed as a potential biomarker for TBI prognosis,[44] given its association with reduced verbal memory[16,42] and increased risk of unfavorable long-term outcomes.[45]

As an important lipid transporter, APOEε4 also represents a potential therapeutic target and has been associated with asymptomatic CAA,[23] whose major clinical manifestation is lobar ICH.[29] However, the differential effects of APOEε4 genotypes on distinct CAA phenotypes remain incompletely understood.[41] Proposed mechanisms include modulation of inflammatory responses, cellular repair processes, and broad regulation of Aβ deposition. Nevertheless, the ultimate impact of APOEε4 on neuropsychological outcomes following TBI remains complex and incompletely defined.[26,32]

In summary, we explored the potential association between genetically mediated posttraumatic neurodegeneration and intracerebral amyloid deposition following TBI and their combined impact on recovery. The available evidence supports a meaningful link between these processes, highlighting an underexplored yet clinically relevant area. Awareness of these interactions may help anticipate postinjury complications and inform individualized management and rehabilitation strategies.

This manuscript synthesizes current evidence on the relationship between TBI, genetically determined susceptibility – particularly APOEε4 polymorphism – and posttraumatic neurodegeneration associated with intracerebral amyloid deposition. It is focused on mechanistic links between injury severity, amyloid-related vascular and parenchymal pathology, and long-term recovery, highlighting CAA, including its emerging iatrogenic forms, as a clinically relevant and underexplored contributor to adverse outcomes after TBI, which differentiates this paper from prior reviews.

This narrative review may be of particular interest to clinicians and researchers in neurology, neurosurgery, stroke medicine, and neurorehabilitation, as well as to those involved in the care of patients with traumatic and vascular brain injury. The topic is timely, given increasing recognition of TBI as a chronic condition with long-term neurodegenerative sequelae, advances in neuroimaging and diagnostic criteria for amyloid-related disorders, and growing awareness of genetic factors that may modulate recovery trajectories. The paper’s limitations mainly arise from its narrative review character, which may restrict its inclusiveness. Therefore, further large-scale modeling studies are needed to confirm our findings.

CONCLUSION

TBI may initiate long-term neurodegenerative processes that complicate postinjury recovery, in part by accelerating intracranial Aβ deposition during both the acute and chronic phases of injury.

It appears that posttraumatic neurodegeneration, neuroinflammation, and neurotoxicity are closely associated with cerebral microvascular amyloid deposition, commonly referred to as CAA. It also seems that genetic predisposition related to APOEε4 polymorphism is linked to increased amyloid deposition and more severe cerebral contusions following brain injury.

This genotype plays a key role in neural responses to injury, may promote the development of neurodegenerative disease after TBI, and is likely associated with prolonged recovery and poorer functional outcomes.
