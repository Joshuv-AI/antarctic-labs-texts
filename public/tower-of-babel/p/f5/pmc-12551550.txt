Natural history of coronary atherosclerosis based on multimodal imaging and physiological fusion techniques: study protocol and rationale for the NASCENT study
Authors: Yao Jiang, Zhongwei Sun, Bo Yu, Wei Liu, Hai Gao, Jianping Li, Zening Jin, Huai Yu, Bo Zheng, Changdong Guan, Han Zhang, Yin Zhang, Lijian Gao, Cheng Cui, Ying Song, Jingjing Xu, Kefei Dou, Weixian Yang, Jie Qian, Yongjian Wu, Lei Song
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPatients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and multivessel disease are at elevated risk of recurrent events. Radial wall strain (RWS), a novel indicator derived from angiography, has emerged as a potentially useful adjunct to optical coherence tomography (OCT) for assessing plaque vulnerability. The NASCENT trial is a prospective, multicentre cohort study designed to assess the natural history of coronary plaque in this high-risk AMI population and investigate the predictive value of angiography-based RWS for lesion progression, compared with OCT-assessed vulnerable plaque.Methods and analysisFollowing successful culprit lesion revascularisation for AMI patients with multivessel disease, we assessed eligible non-culprit lesions (30%–80% diameter stenosis) in non-flow-limiting, non-infarct-related arteries (Murray law-based quantitative flow ratio >0.80) using OCT and offline RWS analysis. The primary endpoint is lesion progression at 1 year, defined as a ≥20% increase in diameter stenosis percentage measured by quantitative coronary angiography. Between April 2024 and April 2025, 131 patients were enrolled. The 1-year angiographic and OCT follow-ups will be completed by May 2026. Clinical follow-ups are planned at 1 month, 6 months, 1 year and annually up to 3 years. As the first prospective trial comparing angiography-based RWS with OCT for predicting lesion progression in the AMI population, this study may provide crucial evidence for RWS as a valuable tool for risk stratification and clinical decision-making.Ethics and disseminationThe protocol has been approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (Fuwai Hospital Approval No. 2023-2039) and will be conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study results will undergo peer-reviewed publication.Trial registration numberNCT06040073.

Background

Patients with acute myocardial infarction (AMI) and multivessel disease are at a significantly elevated risk of adverse cardiovascular events compared with stable patients.1–3 Notably, nearly half of these adverse events originate from non-culprit lesions, which are often characterised by a substantial lipid burden and a thin fibrous cap.4–6 Emerging evidence has demonstrated that atherosclerosis represents a dynamic physiological process encompassing both gradual and rapid stepwise plaque progression.7–9 Serial angiographic investigations have consistently shown rapid plaque progression before the onset of acute coronary syndrome.3 10 11 The underlying mechanisms are believed to involve recurrent plaque disruption with subsequent healing, accompanied by thrombus organisation, intraplaque neovascularisation and intraplaque haemorrhage.12 Consequently, plaques with vulnerable morphological features are more susceptible to rapid progression, and lesions exhibiting rapid progression are more likely to evolve into clinically adverse events. Traditionally, plaque vulnerability has been evaluated using intravascular imaging modalities, including intravascular ultrasound, optical coherence tomography (OCT) and near-infrared spectroscopy, along with their derivative techniques. These methods provide precise identification of high-risk plaques and support early screening.2 13–15 However, the clinical adoption is impeded by challenges such as invasiveness, procedural complexity and costs.

Radial wall strain (RWS) constitutes a novel method for quantifying vessel wall deformation during the cardiac cycle using an angiography-derived haemodynamic model.16 This technique enables real-time, quantitative assessment of coronary biomechanics. Prior studies have shown that RWSmax >12% was significantly associated with 1-year vessel-oriented adverse events and OCT-defined vulnerability features such as thinner fibrous caps, higher prevalence of thin-cap fibroatheroma (TCFA) and larger lipidic plaque burden.16 17 In addition, a post hoc analysis demonstrated that RWSmax >12.6% independently predicted lesion progression with an area under the receiver-operating-characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.75.18 Although the available evidence remained limited and largely retrospective with a small sample size, an alternative method was provided to address the limitation of conventional intravascular imaging. Accordingly, we designed this first multicentre prospective cohort study and selected ≥13% as the exploratory cut-off to evaluate the predictive value of RWS for lesion progression in comparison to OCT assessments in AMI patients.

Methods

Study design

This prospective, multicentre cohort study aims to assess whether angiography-based RWSmax ≥13% is non-inferior to OCT-assessed TCFA in predicting 1-year lesion progression. Lesion progression is defined as an increase in the diameter stenosis of more than 20%, as measured by quantitative coronary analysis (QCA) (figure 1). Patients presenting with AMI within 60 days following successful percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) for the infarct-related artery (IRA) were enrolled. Lesions in non-infarct-related arteries (non-IRA) with 30%–80% diameter stenosis and no functional significance (defined by Murray law-based quantitative flow ratio (μQFR) >0.80) were assessed by OCT, QCA and RWS.

The study protocol has been approved by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee at each participating centre (Fuwai Hospital Approval No. 2023-2039), in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study has been registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06040073).

Study population

Eligible participants were enrolled in five centres in China, and the detailed enrolment process is shown in online supplemental figure 1. Adult patients (≥18 years old) who had experienced an AMI within 60 days, including ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), were enrolled after undergoing successful PCI for all culprit lesions in the IRA. Non-culprit lesions in non-IRA, with a visual diameter stenosis between 30% and 80%, vessel μQFR >0.80 and reference vessel diameter ≥2.5 mm, were investigated in this study. Patients with procedural failure or peri-procedural complications for IRA-PCI were excluded. Other exclusion criteria included the presence of cardiogenic shock, mechanical circulatory support devices, expected coronary artery bypass grafting or insufficient image quality for reliable RWS and μQFR measurements. Procedure

Eligible patients for emergency PCI were recommended to receive an initial loading dose of aspirin (300 mg) and ticagrelor (180 mg), followed by 100 mg of aspirin daily and 90 mg of ticagrelor two times per day for maintenance. Non-emergency patients were advised to take 100 mg of aspirin daily and 90 mg of ticagrelor two times per day. If aspirin and ticagrelor have been administered for <72 hours or 24 hours prior to the procedure, an additional loading dose of 300 mg of aspirin and 180 mg of ticagrelor was warranted. Patients who were intolerant to ticagrelor could substitute clopidogrel as an alternative. Procedural anticoagulation was achieved using unfractionated heparin at a dose of 100 units/kg. All patients received optimal medical therapy, including standard medications for the secondary prevention of coronary artery disease.

Angiography, IRA intervention and non-IRA assessment

Coronary angiography of the three major epicardial coronary vessels was performed following standard operating procedures. Non-overlapping images with minimal foreshortening were acquired in a standardised position at a frame rate of ≥15 frames/s, ensuring contrast medium injection for at least one full cardiac cycle to optimise image quality.

Patients presenting with AMI underwent IRA-PCI according to local standard practice. Revascularisation of the IRA was required within 24 hours for patients with STEMI or within 72 hours for those with NSTEMI. Successful PCI was defined as achieving residual stenosis <30% with Thrombolysis in Myocardial Infarction (TIMI) grade 3 flow after stent implantation or residual stenosis <50% with TIMI grade 3 flow after balloon angioplasty.

Following successful PCI of IRA, a non-IRA OCT evaluation was performed by the Cornaris P80 (Vivolight, Shenzhen, China) if the patient remained haemodynamically stable. Otherwise, OCT evaluation was permitted within 60 days. OCT images were acquired at 200 frames/s at a pull-back speed of 40 mm/s. All angiography and OCT data were transferred to an independent imaging core laboratory (Interventional Cardiovascular Imaging Core Laboratory, National Centre for Cardiovascular Diseases, Beijing, China) for offline analysis.19 All eligible lesions were offline analysed by two independent, experienced groups of core laboratory technicians. Any discordance was resolved through consensus with a third reviewer.

μQFR and QCA analysis

The acquired angiographic images were transmitted to the AngioPlus system (Pulse Medical, Shanghai, China) for real-time μQFR analysis, as previously described. In brief, the angiographic view with minimal overlap of the target vessel and its branch ostia was selected. The software automatically delineated lumen contours, reconstructed the reference vessel diameter using the Murray bifurcation fractal law and estimated contrast flow velocity based on the TIMI frame count. μQFR was then calculated by hyperaemic flow velocity, and a pullback curve was generated to derive the final μQFR value.20

Off-line QCA was analysed, including reference vessel diameter, diameter stenosis, minimal lumen diameter and lesion length at the core laboratory using Medis (Medis Medical Imaging, Leiden, the Netherlands).

RWS assessment

Offline RWS assessment was performed using the AngioPlus Pro II system (Pulse Medical, Shanghai, China) based on a single angiographic view.16–18 21 Flow velocity was automatically quantified by analysing contrast dye filling from a key frame, typically selected at end-diastole. The software automatically delineated the lumen contour, with manual adjustments permitted when necessary. In addition to the end-diastolic key frame, three additional high-quality frames were acquired during early systole, end-systole and mid-diastole to enhance measurement accuracy. OCT analysis

Cross-sectional OCT images were analysed at intervals of 0.4 mm (every two frames) using Cornaris® P80 (Vivolight, Shenzhen, China), of which the feasibility and safety have been well validated.22 The lumen area was measured automatically throughout each lesion and manually adjusted when necessary, from which the minimum lumen area was determined. Proximal and distal reference sites were identified within 5 mm proximal and distal to the lesion as the largest and most normal-appearing vessel segments, and the corresponding reference lumen areas were measured.

Fibrous cap thickness (FCT), defined as the thickness of the signal-rich zone separating the lipid core from the lumen, was measured at the thinnest site (FCTmin) three times, with the average value calculated. Non-culprit plaque composition was categorised based on OCT criteria as follows: lipid-rich plaques displayed signal-poor regions with diffuse borders, fibrous plaques showed homogeneous signal-rich regions and calcified plaques appeared as signal-poor regions with sharply demarcated borders. The extent of lipid and calcification was quantified by measuring their angular extent in OCT cross-sectional images, reported in degrees (lipid arc, calcification arc). TCFA was defined by OCT as lipid-rich plaques with a maximum lipid arc >90° and an FCTmin <65 µm. Additional vulnerability indices noted during OCT analysis included macrophage infiltration (signal-rich, punctate or linear regions with cast shadows), cholesterol crystals (thin, high-intensity linear structures usually located within the fibrous cap or lipid core), neovascularisation (signal-poor voids with distinct margins across multiple frames), plaque rupture (fibrous cap disruption with a hollow cavity) and thrombus (an irregular mass adherent to the luminal surface, classified into white thrombus with homogeneous low backscatter and red thrombus with high backscatter, increased attenuation and cast shadows).23

In addition to the aforementioned vulnerability parameters, the Vivolight multimodality OCT system provided a novel quantitative metric—the index of plaque attenuation (IPA)—that evaluated plaque vulnerability based on the optical attenuation properties of various tissue types. Unstable plaques, characterised by a large lipid core, macrophage infiltration and cholesterol crystal, exhibited elevated IPA values, whereas plaques predominantly composed of calcified and fibrous tissue showed lower IPA values.24 The IPA was defined as the ratio of the number of pixels in the attenuation map with an attenuation coefficient exceeding a specified threshold (x) to the total number of pixels, multiplied by 1000, and expressed per 4 mm segment. Endpoints and follow-up

The primary endpoint is lesion progression at 1 year, defined as a ≥20% increase in diameter stenosis assessed by QCA.3 25 Secondary endpoints included: (1) Clinical: major adverse cardiac events (MACE) and its components at 1, 2 and 3 years, defined as the composite of all-cause mortality, myocardial infarction and unplanned revascularisation, (2) Angiographic: diameter stenosis, minimal lumen diameter and lesion length assessed by QCA, μQFR and RWSmax at 1 year and (3) OCT: FCTmin, lipid arc, plaque burden, IPA and mean/minimum lumen area at 1 year. Additional details on endpoints and definitions are provided in the online supplemental table 1.

All eligible participants are scheduled to receive angiography and OCT follow-up at 1 year after the index procedure. For patients who experience recurrent AMI or death before 1-year follow-up, any emergency angiography and/or OCT performed will be retrieved and adjudicated by the independent core laboratory for use as the follow-up assessment; if no evaluable imaging is available, the data will be censored from imaging-based analyses. Clinical follow-up will be conducted via office visit or telephone at 1 month (with a window of 7 days), 6 months (with a window of 14 days), 1 year, and annually up to 3 years (with a window of 30 days).

Statistical analysis

Due to the limited studies regarding the predictive performance of OCT-defined TCFA for lesion progression, we re-extracted lesion-level data from a prior OCT study to estimate accuracy. In that dataset,26 TCFA prevalence was 20.0% among lesions with progression (n=50) and 5.8% among lesions without progression (n=467), corresponding to 10 true positives (50×20.0%) and 440 true negatives (467×(1–5.8%)). This yielded an overall predictive accuracy of (10+440)/517=87.0%. Accordingly, we conservatively assumed an accuracy of 85% for OCT-defined TCFA in our sample size estimation and hypothesised that elevated RWS (RWSmax ≥13%) was non-inferior to OCT with a predefined non-inferiority margin of 10%. A minimum sample size of 200 lesions was calculated to achieve 80% statistical power at a two-sided Type I error rate of 0.05. Accounting for the potential correlation among multiple eligible lesions within patients, a design effect of 1.5 was applied, increasing the required sample size to 300 analysable lesions. According to published literature,2 we anticipated an average of 3 eligible lesions per patient. Thus, approximately 100 participants are required. Under the assumption of a 20% dropout at 1 year, at least 125 patients will be enrolled to ensure at least 300 eligible lesions for final analysis.

Categorical variables are presented as counts and percentages and analysed using the χ² test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD for normally distributed data, or median (IQR) for non-normally distributed data, and analysed using t-test or Mann-Whitney U test accordingly. The two-sided 95% CIs for predictive accuracy of the angiography-based RWS and OCT-derived FCTmin regarding the primary endpoint are estimated using an asymptotic normal method and the Clopper-Pearson exact method, respectively. A Cohen’s kappa statistic was used to assess the intra- and interobserver agreements in the diagnosis of RWS and OCT analyses based on 30 randomly selected plaques.26 Adverse events are reported as frequency and incidence and analysed using the likelihood ratio χ² test or Fisher’s exact test. To account for the potential cluster effect of multiple lesions in the same patient, lesion-level analyses will be conducted using generalised estimating equations (GEEs). The GEE model is specified with a working correlation structure of compound symmetry and a two-level nested clustering structure, accounting for patients and lesions within patients.27 Both univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses within the GEE model are performed to identify independent predictors of plaque progression.28 Statistical analyses for the primary endpoint are conducted using a one-sided significance level of 0.025, whereas analyses for all other endpoints employ a two-sided significance level of 0.05. All statistical analyses are performed using SAS software, V.9.4 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina).

Study organisation

The NASCENT trial is an investigator-initiated trial, and the authors are responsible for the design and conduct of this trial, statistical analyses, data interpretation and manuscript preparation, including drafting, editing and final content. This trial is supervised by an executive committee responsible for developing the study protocol and overseeing all aspects of trial execution, data analysis and publications. All clinical events are adjudicated by an independent clinical events committee. An independent core laboratory is responsible for analysing all angiographic and OCT data.

Current status

Between April 2024 and April 2025, a total of 131 patients were enrolled from five sites in China. Follow-up for the last enrolled patient will be completed in May 2026, and the primary results of the NASCENT trial are anticipated to be reported in late 2026. Discussion

The NASCENT trial is a prospective, multicentre study designed to establish the non-inferiority of angiography-based RWS, an easy-to-perform method, compared with OCT in predicting plaque progression at 1 year. Serial studies with angiographic data obtained before and within 1–3 months of myocardial infarction have suggested that non-obstructive lesions exhibited more rapid and pronounced enlargement prior to adverse events.3 9 10 Thus, plaque progression represents a pivotal process by which non-culprit plaques evolve into cardiovascular events. Recurrent cycles of subclinical rupture followed by reparative healing are considered a principal mechanism driving this progression.12 28 TCFA, regarded as a precursor to plaque rupture, has been demonstrated to be an independent predictor of lesion progression, associated with a higher risk of recurrent cardiovascular events.13 26 28 29 These findings highlight the critical clinical imperatives of (1) Developing practical tools for comprehensive plaque characterisation and (2) Identifying vulnerable lesion phenotypes beyond luminal stenosis assessment. The NASCENT trial was designed to address these unmet needs through systematic validation of quantitative angiographic signatures, with the potential to establish a new paradigm for post-AMI risk stratification.

Numerous high-quality clinical studies have confirmed the superiority of OCT assessment of plaque vulnerability.2 3 23 30 A serial OCT study found that TCFA and other vulnerable features—macrophage infiltration, microchannels and layered plaque—were independent predictors of non-culprit lesion progression in patients with acute coronary syndrome.28 Corroborating studies implicate lipid-rich plaques and TCFAs in plaque progression, thereby supporting the selection of OCT-defined vulnerability as the reference standard in this study.26 31 However, the widespread clinical adoption of OCT has been limited by high-cost consumables, technical complexity in operational procedures, requiring contrast injection and a steep learning curve for image interpretation.

RWS is a quantitative angiographic parameter derived from the dynamic change in vessel diameter over a cardiac cycle. It reflects vessel wall deformation driven by pulsatile blood pressure and is influenced by plaque composition, including lipid content, inflammation, fibrous tissue and calcification.32 Hong et al were the first to demonstrate that angiography-based RWS was associated with OCT-identified vulnerable features.16 Elevated RWS has consistently been associated with an increased risk of future MACE, providing both diagnostic and prognostic value in assessing plaque vulnerability.17 21 Furthermore, RWS values are significantly higher in atherosclerotic segments and show strong agreement with OCT-derived biomechanical assessments using finite element analysis,33 supporting its role as a non-invasive surrogate marker for vulnerability.

Beyond identifying high-risk lesions, RWS also shows potential in predicting plaque progression. A post hoc analysis of 603 patients with untreated intermediate lesions demonstrated that RWSmax >12.6% was independently associated with lesion progression (AUC=0.75; adjusted HR=6.82; p<0.001).18 Similarly, another study reported that RWSmax >12% predicted an increase in lesion-specific QFR.34 However, most prior investigations have been retrospective and conducted in stable or lower-risk populations with mild to intermediate stenoses. Evidence in higher-risk AMI cohorts remains limited. The NASCENT trial addresses this gap by prospectively evaluating whether angiographic RWS (RWSmax ≥13%) can predict plaque progression in AMI patients, with OCT-defined vulnerability serving as the reference standard. This approach may provide a practical, non-invasive means to improve risk stratification and guide secondary prevention in patients with acute coronary syndrome.

There are several limitations. First, since enrolment was restricted to patients with successful PCI of the IRA, those with procedural failure or peri-procedural complications were not included. This design may introduce a degree of survival bias, which should be considered when interpreting the generalisability of our findings. Second, despite an appropriate sample size calculation, it was powered for a surrogate endpoint, lesion progression, but not clinical endpoints. The potential benefit of RWS-guided management in clinical outcomes requires validation in larger, event-driven studies. While follow-up data were collected for up to 3 years, the number of adverse events may be insufficient to establish a robust association between RWS and long-term outcomes. Third, since both RWS calculations and OCT image interpretation relied on image quality and reviewer expertise, which could introduce confounding factors, all analyses were performed offline by an independent core laboratory, ensuring standardised analysis and minimising bias. Fourth, based on the feasibility and ethical considerations, the angiographic criteria of diameter stenosis between 30% and 80% excluded those extremely mild or severe lesions, while these lesions retained the potential for progression. Finally, the present study did not assess cost-effectiveness, which remains an important consideration for the clinical adoption of RWS-guided strategies.

The NASCENT trial will first evaluate the non-inferiority of RWS-defined vulnerable plaques (RWSmax ≥13%) compared with OCT-identified TCFA in predicting 1-year plaque progression, to establish a new paradigm based on angiography-derived RWS, as a practical tool for risk stratification in AMI patients after successful culprit lesion revascularisation.
