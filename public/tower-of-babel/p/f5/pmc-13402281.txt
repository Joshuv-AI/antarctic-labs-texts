Exploratory multiomics analysis identifies WDR17 as a potential biomarker of tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance in lung adenocarcinoma
Authors: Yuan Xu, Liping Li, Zhouhua Li, Yanqiang Yang, Hongquan Xing, Xuehua Ma, Xinyi Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveDrug resistance remains a major clinical challenge in advanced lung adenocarcinoma. Identifying commonly dysregulated resistance-associated genes may provide mechanistic insights for novel therapeutic strategies.MethodsRNA-seq data from eight TCGA LUAD patients with documented TKI treatment were analyzed using the limma empirical Bayes framework for exploratory differential expression analysis. WDR17 association with clinicopathological features was evaluated in the TCGA cohort. Single-cell transcriptomics from publicly available datasets (GSE139386 and GSE260499) was used to characterize WDR17 distribution in TKI-resistant cell lines. In vitro experiments assessed the functional relevance of WDR17 in drug sensitivity.ResultsExploratory differential expression analysis identified WDR17 as significantly downregulated in the non-responding group (log2 FC = − 1.11, adjusted P = 0.026). In the broader TCGA-LUAD cohort, high WDR17 expression was associated with longer overall survival (HR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.67–0.91, P < 0.001). Single-cell analysis revealed that WDR17 was predominantly expressed in a cell state characterized by protein homeostasis and metabolic stress. Computational estimation using the EPIC algorithm suggested a positive correlation between WDR17 expression and CD8 + T-cell infiltration (P < 0.05). In vitro experiments confirmed that WDR17 overexpression enhanced crizotinib sensitivity in resistant cells, whereas knockdown reduced sensitivity in parental cells.ConclusionThis exploratory study identified WDR17 as a candidate biomarker associated with TKI resistance in lung adenocarcinoma, potentially involved in maintaining protein homeostasis and metabolic balance. These preliminary findings warrant validation in larger, independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s12672-026-05286-5.

Introduction

According to 2022 global cancer statistics from the World Health Organization (WHO), lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, as this cancer type accounts for 18.7% of all cancer deaths [1]. In addition, non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) comprises more than 85% of all lung cancer cases, among which lung adenocarcinoma is the most common subtype [2]. In recent years, tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) targeting epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) and anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) have become the standard treatment for advanced or unresectable stage III NSCLC due to their significant improvement in prognosis in patients with the corresponding mutations [3]. However, resistance to targeted therapy is almost inevitable, as durable responses rarely persist beyond two years in most patients [4]. In response to this, considerable effort has been devoted to investigating resistance mechanisms, including secondary mutations in EGFR and amplification of other genes (such as MET, ERBB2, and KRAS). Notably, approximately 30% of the resistance mechanisms remain unexplained [5, 6].

With the continuous advancement of sequencing technologies, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) has emerged as a powerful and widely adopted tool in biomedical research [7]. Its unparalleled ability to resolve cellular heterogeneity has rendered scRNA-seq an indispensable means for determining the mechanisms that underlie the initiation and progression of diverse diseases [8].

Furthermore, increasing evidence has indicated that resistance to targeted therapy arises from the intrinsic rewiring of oncogenic signaling pathways within tumor cells and is intimately linked to the dynamic remodeling of the tumor microenvironment (TME) [9]. Specifically, key TME alterations associated with resistance include exacerbated hypoxia, heightened immunosuppression, and altered patterns of immune cell infiltration [10]. However, the complex intercellular interactions between tumor cells and TME components [11], along with the critical regulatory nodes that govern these interactions during the development of drug resistance remain poorly understood [12]. Since the precise molecular networks that drive this adaptive ecosystem have not yet been fully elucidated, the development of effective postresistance treatment strategies has been significantly impeded [13]. Therefore, following the emergence of resistance to targeted therapies, a comprehensive understanding of the TME based on scRNA-seq holds considerable promise for the identification of novel avenues to overcome treatment failure [14]. Patient information

This study integrates RNA-seq data from the differential expression analysis of eight patients in the TCGA LUAD cohort, who had documented responses to erlotinib treatment, Subsequently, the association between the core gene WDR17 and clinicopathological features was assessed using the TCGA lung adenocarcinoma cohort, and its prognostic significance was independently validated via the Kaplan–Meier plotter. Furthermore, scRNA -seq was employed to pinpoint the cellular localization of WDR17 within the drug-resistant cells. This study aims to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying resistance to targeted therapy in lung adenocarcinoma, thereby revealing novel theoretical insights and potential therapeutic targets to overcome drug resistance and to ultimately facilitate clinical translation and improve patient outcomes.

Methods

Data acquisition

In this study, we obtained TKI treatment information and gene expression data for lung adenocarcinoma patients (TCGA-LUAD) from The Cancer Genome Atlas database (https://www.cancer.gov/ccg/research/genome-sequencing/tcga, accessed 10 December 2024). Patient selection was based on the following criteria: (1) histologically confirmed lung adenocarcinoma; (2) documented treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (including erlotinib) as recorded in the TCGA pharmaceutical data; and (3) availability of matched RNA-seq expression data. A total of eight patients met these criteria (Alive group, n = 6; Dead group, n = 2). Given the extremely limited sample size, this analysis was considered exploratory in nature. Differential expression analysis was performed using the limma empirical Bayes framework, which borrows variance information across the entire transcriptome to stabilize gene-specific dispersion estimates, thereby conferring improved statistical performance under small-sample conditions. Genes with low expression (mean counts < 1 across all samples) were filtered prior to analysis. Multiple testing correction was applied using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure. A threshold of |log2 FC|> 1 and adjusted P < 0.05 was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis

The genes related to WDR17 were obtained from the UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html] [15]. Genes were converted to Entrez IDs through the org.Hs.eg.db database. KEGG analysis was performed using the enrichKEGG function, with a significance threshold of p < 0.05. GO analysis was performed using the enrichGO function and, covered the BP, CC, and MF categories, with a significance criterion of p < 0.05. The enrichplot and ggplot2 packages were used to construct bar charts and bubble charts to display the top pathways [16, 17].

Construction of protein–protein interaction (PPI) networks and gene module analysis

We used GeneMANIA (https://www.genemania.org) to identify genes that are functionally similar to WDR17 and to construct a gene‒gene interaction network on the basis of physical interactions, coexpression, colocalization, pathways and shared protein domains [18]. STRING (https://string-db.org) (version 11.0) was used to construct a protein–protein interaction (PPI) network between WDR17 and its coexpressed genes [18, 19], and to visualize the functional interactions among them. A confidence score threshold of 0.4 was considered a critical point [20].

Expression and prognostic significance of WDR17 in LUAD patients

The UALCAN database (https://ualcan.path.uab.edu/index.html) [21] was used to evaluate the expression of WDR17 in lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) tissues and its association with clinicopathological variables. WDR17 was initially identified as a candidate gene based on its membership in the WD40 repeat protein family and its potential scaffolding role in signal transduction [22], and its differential expression was subsequently confirmed through TCGA data analysis.

Single-cell RNA sequencing analysis

In this study, we retrieved data from two publicly available GEO datasets derived from targeted therapy-resistant NSCLC cell lines: GSE139386 (ceritinib -sensitive and -resistant, ALK-translocated H3122 cells) and GSE260499 (osimertinib -sensitive and -resistant, EGFR-mutant PC9-C2 cells). The scRNA-seq data were processed and analyzed in the R statistical environment (version 4.2.1) [23]. Dimensionality reduction analysis was performed using the Seurat package. By establishing thresholds of 200–5500 gene counts and a mitochondrial gene ratio less than 25%, we conducted initial quality control steps, including the removal of low-quality cells and genes. This high-dimensional dataset was normalized, and key information was extracted using principal component analysis (PCA) to convert it into a low-dimensional representation, distributed on a two-dimensional plane. The Harmony algorithm (theta = 2, lambda = 1, nclust = 50) was applied for batch effect correction to eliminate potential technical variation between the two datasets. Integration quality was assessed by visual inspection of UMAP embeddings to confirm mixing of cells from different batches within shared clusters. In the elbow plot, we selected the first 15 principal components on the basis of the inflection point where the curve transitions from steep to flat for subsequent analysis. Cell clustering was conducted with the “runUMAP” “FindClusters” and “FindNeighbors” functions, and cell state annotation was performed on the basis of the top 100 differentially expressed genes identified by the “FindAllMarkers” function (Wilcoxon rank-sum test, min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25). With respect to the pseudotemporal developmental trajectory, a violin plot of WDR17 expression was generated using the Monocle package [24]. Additionally, the TIMER2.0 database (http://timer.comp-genomics.org/) was used to investigate the correlation between WDR17 expression levels and CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration using the EPIC algorithm [25]. Pseudotime trajectory analysis was performed using the Monocle algorithm to reconstruct the developmental trajectory of targeted therapy resistant lung adenocarcinoma cells. Metabolic pathway activity scores in targeted therapy resistant cells were calculated using the scMetabolism package [26]. CellChat was used to identify cell communication based on a human database [27].

Cell viability assay

The human lung adenocarcinoma cell line H2228 and the crizotinib-resistant lung adenocarcinoma cell line were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells/well and cultured overnight. Afterward, the cells were transfected with 100 ng of WDR17 plasmids or with WDR17-targeting RNAi constructs (siWDR17) (Jian, Suzhou, China) using Lipofectamine® 2000(Invitrogen, Carlsbad, USA). After transfection, fresh medium containing varying concentrations of crizotinib (Aladdin, Shanghai, China) was added, and the cells were incubated for 24 h. Subsequently, 10 μL of Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) reagent (Yeasen, Shanghai, China) was added to each well, and the cells were cultured at 37 °C, in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator for 1 h. Then, the absorbance of each well was measured at 450 nm with a microplate plate reader (Tecan, Männedorf, Switzerland). Wells without transfected cells served as blank wells, and the percentage of cell viability (%) was calculated as follows: cell viability (%) = [A450 (treated wells) − A450 (blank wells)]/[A450 (control wells) − A450 (blank wells)] × 100%.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT‒PCR) analysis

Cells were collected and kept on ice, followed by lysis with TRIzol reagent to isolate total RNA, after which the RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using a reverse transcription kit (Saiwen Biotech, Nanchang, China). qRT‒PCR was then performed using the cDNA as a template in a 10 μL reaction mixture containing: 5 μL of SYBR® Premix, 2.5 μL of 5 × cDNA, 0.25 μL each of forward and reverse primers, 0.2 μL of ROX reference dye, and 1.8 μL of nuclease-free H₂O. The amplification protocol consisted of initial denaturation at 95 °C for 15 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 3 s and 60 °C for 30 s, and a final extension at 95 °C for 15 s, 60 °C for 1 min, and 72 °C for 10 min. The sequences of primers used were as follows: WDR17: forward: 5′-CGAGAAGGAACTTGTGTGGATAC-3′, reverse: 5′-GAGTCTCACTGTAGAGTCACGG-3′. GAPDH (internal control): forward: 5′-GGGAGCCAAAAGGGTCAT-3′, reverse: 5′-GAGTCCTTCCACGATACCAA-3′. GAPDH was used as the reference gene for normalization. qRT‒PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems (ABI, USA) real-time PCR instrument.

Cell lines and equipment

The human lung adenocarcinoma cell line (H2228) used in this study was purchased from Wuhan Procell Life Science & Technology Co, Ltd. The ALK-positive, crizotinib-resistant lung adenocarcinoma cell line was kindly provided by the Affiliated Cancer Hospital of Xiangya Hospital, Central South University. Cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Cat. No. A3160801) and were maintained in a humidified incubator at 37 °C with 5% CO₂.

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0, and statistical graphs were generated with GraphPad Prism version 8.0. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using Student’s t-test. A P value < 0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Results

Identification of DEGs associated with resistance to targeted therapy

To investigate the molecular features underlying resistance to targeted therapy, we first performed an exploratory differential expression analysis of eight patients from the TCGA LUAD cohort with documented responses to erlotinib treatment. Given the small sample size, this analysis was considered exploratory. The limma empirical Bayes framework identified potential resistance-associated genes in the TCGA dataset. WDR17 (Fig. 1A), a gene with no prior link to human cancer, demonstrated significant prognostic value and was therefore prioritized for mechanistic exploration. The genes related to WDR17 were obtained from the UALCAN database (Table S1). GO enrichment analysis indicated that these genes were significantly enriched in molecular functions related to microtubule polymerization/depolymerization, mitotic chromosome segregation, and cell cycle regulation (Fig. 1C). KEGG pathway analysis further revealed enrichment in the cell cycle, DNA replication, and Fanconi anemia pathways, which suggests that cell cycle dysregulation may represent a key mechanism underlying acquired targeted therapy resistance in lung adenocarcinoma (Fig. 1D). Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network analysis revealed 20 highly connected hub genes, including SHISA8, CHD5, SLC39A12, KIAA0408, STN, ASTN1, GRIK1, CORO6, PDILT, WDR19, TRHDE, KLHL1, AQP6, OR2L13, ANAPC4, COX7B2, WDR35, SCGB1D1, RPE65, and PLPPR3 (Fig. 1E). Notably, WDR17 was identified as the top-ranked central node with the highest connectivity, which suggests a pivotal regulatory role within the network. Additionally, several other highly connected genes—such as WDR1, MRTO4, EBNA1BP2, WDR3, BRIX1, NOC2L, GRWD1, GTPBP4, RPF2, TSR1, and SDAD1—also exhibited strong interaction capacities across the full network (Fig. 1F), which indicates potential cooperative roles in resistance-related pathways. On the basis of the aforementioned findings, WDR17 was prioritized for further functional investigation.

Association of WDR17 expression with clinicopathological variables

An integrated analysis of transcriptomic data from the TCGA and GEO databases revealed that WDR17 expression was significantly lower in lung adenocarcinoma tissues than in normal lung tissues (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2A). Clinical correlation analyses revealed that patients aged 21–40 years had significantly higher WDR17 expression than did those aged ≥ 41 years (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2B). Although female patients tended to have slightly higher WDR17 expression than did male patients, this difference did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.10) (Fig. 2C). WDR17 expression was significantly downregulated as early as Stage I (p = 4.43 × 10⁻⁷) and remained consistently low in Stage II (p = 7.18 × 10⁻⁸) and Stage III (p = 1.28 × 10⁻⁷) disease. A decreasing trend was also observed in Stage IV disease, although this trend did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.32), possibly because of the limited sample size (Fig. 2D). Pancancer analysis using the UALCAN database indicated that WDR17 expression is tissue-specific, as significantly lower levels were observed in lung adenocarcinoma than in several other cancer types, such as pheochromocytoma and glioblastoma (Fig. 2E). WDR17 is predominantly expressed in a cell state associated with protein homeostasis and metabolic stress in TKI-resistant cell lines

To investigate the distribution of WDR17 in lung adenocarcinomas resistant to targeted therapy, we analyzed scRNA-seq datasets from GSE139386 and GSE260499. After quality control and correction for potential batch effects, the predominant cell types across different datasets exhibited largely consistent distributions in the UMAP plot, which indicates that the integration was successful (Figure SA-B). Dimensionality reduction and clustering revealed 13 distinct cell states: secretory epithelial cells, differentiated epithelial cells, proliferating epithelial cells, actively proliferating epithelial cells, highly proliferating epithelial cells, Ki-67 positive highly proliferating epithelial cells, high metabolic activity cells, actively proliferating cells, metabolically active epithelial cells, differentiation associated epithelial cells, stem like epithelial cells, cells with proteostasis-induced metabolic stress, and cells with endoplasmic reticulum-induced metabolic stress (Fig. 3A). In the cell state annotation process, we systematically assigned identities to epithelial subpopulations on the basis of the biological functions of differentially expressed genes and literature-supported marker gene profiles. The cells under protein homeostasis–metabolic stress were characterized by high expression of HSP90AA1 and HSPD1, which encode cytoplasmic and mitochondrial heat shock proteins involved in protein folding under stress. They also highly expressed: G3BP1, a core component of stress granules that regulates RNA stability, and PSMC1, a proteasome subunit that mediates degradation of misfolded proteins. These findings—collectively indicate disrupted proteostasis in these cells. The concurrent upregulation of the expression of the glycolytic enzymes LDHA and ENO1 further suggests metabolic reprogramming toward energy stress adaptation. Highly proliferative epithelial cells were defined by significant enrichment of CDC6 (a key initiator of DNA replication), MCM3 (a licensing factor for DNA replication), and the histone gene HIST1H1B. These markers strongly differed in expression (avg_log2FC = 1.50–3.00, p.val.adj = 0), which indicates active DNA replication and a robust proliferative state. A bubble plot illustrates the scaled expression levels and proportions of canonical markers for each cell type (Fig. 3B). The violin plot shows that WDR17 expression is highest in the protein homeostasis–metabolic stress cell state (Fig. 3C). Computational estimation using the EPIC algorithm from TIMER2.0 (based on bulk RNA-seq deconvolution) suggested a positive correlation between WDR17 expression and estimated CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration proportion (P = 5.13 × 10⁻3) (Fig. 3D). It should be noted that this correlation is derived from computational deconvolution and represents indirect evidence that requires validation by orthogonal approaches. Cells with protein homeostasis–metabolic stress related to WDR17 rely on the citric acid cycle, whereas proliferating cells exhibit increased glycolytic activity

Using the scMetabolism algorithm, we evaluated the activity of metabolic pathways across distinct cell states including ceritinib resistant H3122 cells (GSE139386) and osimertinib resistant EGFR-mutant PC9-C2 cells (GSE260499). We found that cells with protein homeostasis—metabolic stress related to WDR17 exhibited markedly increased activity in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, which suggests a greater reliance on this pathway to meet the energy demands of these cells (Fig. 4A). This TCA cycle activity was significantly greater than that of other metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation and glycolysis/gluconeogenesis. Further analysis revealed that metabolic modules associated with glycolytic intermediates were predominantly enriched in cell subpopulations with high energy demands or strong stress-response capacity, such as proliferating epithelial cells and Ki-67 + highly proliferative epithelial cells, which exhibited glycolysis/gluconeogenesis activity scores as high as 0.2. Hypoxia induces cell proliferation, and boxplots of both glycolytic activity and UMAP spatial distribution maps consistently corroborated the abovementioned findings (Fig. 4B–C). Notably, actively proliferating and Ki–67 + cells also exhibited prominent oxidative phosphorylation activity (Fig. 4C). In vitro experimental validation

CCK-8 assay results showed that the IC₅₀ value of CRZ in H2228 cells was 2.2 μg/mL (Fig. 6A), while the IC₅₀ value in CRZ-resistant (CR) cells was significantly increased to 8.6 μg/mL (Fig. 6B), demonstrating the successful establishment of CR cells with obvious resistance to CRZ. The results of the qRT‒PCR analysis revealed that the expression level of WDR17 in the CRZ-resistant cells was significantly lower than that in the H2228 parental cells (P < 0.001) (Fig. 6C), which is consistent with the results of our bioinformatics analysis. We found that in crizotinib-resistant lung adenocarcinoma, increased WDR17 expression enhanced the anticancer activity of crizotinib(Fig. 6D), whereas decreased WDR17 expression attenuated this activity in the lung adenocarcinoma cell line H2228(Fig. 6E). Discussion

Through an integrated multiomics analysis, our study consistently revealed that WDR17 is a candidate hub gene potentially associated with TKI resistance based on exploratory analyses of publicly available datasets, including the TCGA clinical cohort [28], GEO cell line datasets [29], and the Kaplan–Meier Plotter database [30]. WDR17 expression is widely downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma, and its low expression is significantly correlated with advanced disease stage, a younger patient subgroup, and poor prognosis. These findings suggest that WDR17 may serve as a candidate molecular biomarker, although its functional role in resistance development requires further experimental validation.

GO, KEGG, and PPI network analyses further revealed that WDR17 is a highly interconnected central node. Notably, several WDR family members such as WDR1, WDR3, and WDR19, have been implicated in ribosome biogenesis and proteostasis regulation [31]. These shared functional associations suggest that WDR17 may contribute to the maintenance of tumor cell sensitivity to targeted therapies by coordinating proteostasis and cell cycle progression.

This discovery expands the conventional understanding of resistance mechanisms, which has largely focused on secondary kinase mutations or bypass pathway activation [32], and has shifted attention toward upstream regulatory networks governing cellular homeostasis [33]. Single-cell resolution metabolic activity analysis revealed that cells under WDR17-related protein homeostasis–metabolic stress are significantly more inclined to derive energy from the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle than from glycolysis or oxidative phosphorylation. This metabolic signature suggests that this subpopulation may sustain energy (ATP) production and supply biosynthetic precursors such as lipids and amino acids derived from citrate through highly efficient mitochondrial oxidative metabolism to cope with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and the burden of protein folding [33, 34]. Notably, active TCA cycle activity is typically associated with cellular homeostasis and enhanced stress resistance. As a WD40 repeat-containing protein, WDR17 could theoretically contribute to metabolic regulation, although the precise mechanism remains to be determined. One speculative possibility is that WDR17 may interact with mitochondria-associated ER membranes (MAMs) or TCA cycle enzyme complexes [35]; however, this hypothesis requires direct experimental evidence. This “high efficiency, low-proliferation” metabolic program stands in stark contrast to the classical Warburg effect [36] and may represent an adaptive strategy that prioritizes cell survival over rapid proliferation under therapeutic pressure. Additionally, computational analyses suggest that WDR17 deficiency may be associated with altered metabolic programming. It is important to note that the single-cell data were derived from cell lines rather than patient tumors; therefore, observations related to cell state heterogeneity should not be directly extrapolated to the in vivo tumor microenvironment (TME). On the one hand, highly proliferative epithelial cells exhibit concurrent activation of both glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation and are enriched in proinvasive and proangiogenic signaling pathways, including those mediated by LAMININ [37], COLLAGEN [38], NOTCH [39], and EGF [40], which suggests a potential role in driving aggressive tumor phenotypes. On the other hand, cells under WDR17-related protein homeostasis-related metabolic stress are significantly enriched in immune modulatory pathways such as those mediated by CD46 [41] and MK [42]: CD46 can suppress T-cell activation and promote regulatory T-cell differentiation [43], whereas MK has dual proinflammatory and immunosuppressive functions [44].

Computational deconvolution using the EPIC algorithm suggested a positive correlation between WDR17 expression and estimated CD8⁺ T-cell infiltration [45]; however, this finding is based on bulk RNA-seq deconvolution and represents indirect evidence. The simple introduction of misfolded proteins into normal effector T cells (Teffs) is sufficient to directly drive their conversion into exhausted T cells (Tex), which demonstrates that disruption of proteostasis itself acts as a fundamental trigger for T-cell exhaustion [46]. Importantly, this Tex protein stress response (PSR) signature is recapitulated in exhausted T cells from human cancer patients and is correlated with poor clinical response to immunotherapy [47]. In support of this mechanistic link, while our computational analyses identified WDR17 expression in cell states associated with protein homeostasis and metabolic stress, whether WDR17 is directly expressed in CD8⁺ T-cells and functionally mediates T-cell exhaustion remains to be determined through cell-type-specific analyses such as patient-derived single-cell RNA-seq or multiplex immunohistochemistry.

Based on these observations, we propose a tentative hypothesis that the loss of WDR17 may impair the intrinsic ability of tumor cells to manage proteotoxic stress and could potentially be associated with altered immune cell composition, as suggested by computational deconvolution. However, this hypothesis remains speculative and requires direct experimental validation, including in vivo studies and patient-derived single-cell analyses.

In vitro experiments revealed that WDR17 expression was significantly downregulated in crizotinib-resistant cells, which is highly consistent with bioinformatics predictions, and further supports its functional role in drug resistance. Given its early and pronounced downregulation and the association between the low expression of WDR17 and poor prognosis, this protein may serve as a potential biomarker for predicting the response to tyrosine kinase inhibitors as well as resistance.

As a future research direction, the observation that WDR17-associated cells may preferentially utilize the TCA cycle raises the possibility of exploring metabolic intervention strategies. For instance, whether TCA cycle modulators (e.g., CPI-613) [48] could affect drug sensitivity in WDR17-low tumors warrants investigation. Similarly, the potential for restoring WDR17 expression through epigenetic modulation represents an area for future exploration. These therapeutic concepts remain speculative and would require extensive preclinical validation before any clinical consideration.

Although the precise molecular mechanisms of WDR17 remain to be elucidated, such as its potential role in regulating mitochondrial function or CD46 membrane localization, this study has established a critical foundation for its translational application in precision therapy for non-small cell lung cancer.

This study has several important limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the TCGA discovery cohort consists of only eight patients with TKI treatment records (Alive n = 6, Dead n = 2), which severely limits statistical power; therefore, the differential expression results should be regarded as exploratory and require validation in larger cohorts. Second, the discovery and validation datasets involve different TKI types and oncogenic drivers (erlotinib/EGFR in TCGA, osimertinib/EGFR and ceritinib/ALK in single-cell data, crizotinib/ALK in vitro), which limits the ability to draw conclusions specific to any single resistance mechanism; instead, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory evidence for a potential shared resistance-associated biomarker across TKI classes. Third, the single-cell data were derived from cell lines rather than patient tumor samples and therefore cannot be used to draw definitive conclusions about the tumor microenvironment or immune cell biology. Fourth, the correlation between WDR17 expression and CD8 + T-cell infiltration is based on computational deconvolution (EPIC algorithm) and requires validation by orthogonal methods such as multiplex immunohistochemistry or patient-derived single-cell RNA-seq. Fifth, the in vitro functional validation lacks protein-level confirmation, rescue experiments, and dose–response quantification (AUC). Finally, the molecular mechanism by which WDR17 influences drug sensitivity remains unexplored; future studies should include co-immunoprecipitation/mass spectrometry to identify binding partners, rescue experiments (knockdown followed by re-expression), and metabolic functional assays (e.g., Seahorse analysis). Comprehensive validation in larger independent cohorts with in vivo studies is essential before any clinical translation.

Conclusion

In summary, this exploratory study identified WDR17 as a candidate hub gene potentially associated with TKI resistance in lung adenocarcinoma through integrated multi-omics analysis. WDR17 expression was found to be downregulated in lung adenocarcinoma, and low expression was associated with poor prognosis in the TCGA-LUAD cohort. Single-cell analysis of TKI-resistant cell lines revealed that WDR17 was predominantly expressed in a cell state characterized by protein homeostasis and metabolic stress, with computational evidence suggesting preferential utilization of the TCA cycle. Deconvolution-based analysis indicated a positive correlation between WDR17 expression and estimated CD8 + T-cell infiltration, although this requires validation by orthogonal methods. In vitro experiments confirmed the downregulation of WDR17 in crizotinib-resistant cells and demonstrated that modulation of WDR17 expression affected drug sensitivity. These findings position WDR17 as a promising candidate biomarker warranting further validation in larger, independent cohorts with comprehensive mechanistic studies.
