Comparative analysis of complete mitochondrial genomes of fourteen moray eels (Anguilliformes: Muraenidae) and primary exploration of their phylogenetic relationship and temperature adaptation
Authors: Wei Tan, Fuxiao Chen, Yongbo Wang, Rongxia Wang, Shuyuan Fu, Shengtao Guo, Hongtao Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Moray eels (Anguilliformes: Muraenidae) are commonly found in subtropical and tropical waters, where they favor sheltered habitats and occasionally exhibit aggressive behavior. Nowadays, mitochondrial genomes (mitogenomes) information of moray eels remains poorly understood, and analysis of gene re-arrangements and evolutionary history within Anguilloidei remain largely unknown. In this study, mitochondrial genomes from 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp species were sequenced, assembled and annotated, and most of which were not published before. Our results showed that the mitochondrial genomes of the 14 species were approximately 16.5 kb in length, with GC content ranging from 41.1 to 46.1%. The mitochondrial genomes of these 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp are circular molecules that encode a total of 37 genes, including 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 22 tRNA genes, 2 rRNA (small and large subunit rRNA encoding genes) and a non-coding control region (CR). In addition, three types of gene arrangement have been identified for the first time in the mitogenomes of Anguilloidei. Phylogenetic relationships of 51 moray eel mitogenomes based on the protein-coding genes indicate that mitogenome sequences reveals the higher-order relationships of different Anguilloidei. Gene order and phylogenetic data are valuable for elucidating the evolutionary connections among various Anguilloidei species. Cox3 exhibited the smallest non-synonymous mutation value among all the 13 protein-coding genes, indicating that the role of Cox3 in maintaining energy metabolism under warm water conditions is a hypothesis based on observed evolutionary conservation. The complete information of mitogenomes in these 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp species provides genetic markers for species identification and temperature adaptation studies in Anguilloidei.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12862-025-02417-4.

Introduction

Fishes in the families Anguillidae (freshwater eels) and Muraenidae (moray eels) are among the richest species groups in the order Anguilliformes [1] and are widely distributed across tropical and subtropical oceans worldwide [2]. Moray eels are a relatively large fish species known for their ability to lurk in holes, twisting like snakes in crevices under rocks or coral and swallowing huge preys [3]. Currently, approximately 210 species of moray eels have been identified [4]. Moray eels have elongated bodies and small cross-sectional areas without pelvic fins [5]. They are carnivorous, consuming fish, crabs, and octopuses, and are crucial for maintaining the population balance of groundfish species [6]. Simultaneously, moray eels exhibit a variety of color patterns, ranging from a uniform pattern of unique spots to different spots, bars, and mesh patterns [4]. Additionally, moray eels were popular in marine aquariums due to their spectacular exterior coloration [7].

Previous studies showed that mitochondrial genomes of most animals are circular, approximately 16 kb in size, comprising 13 protein-coding genes, 2 rRNA genes, 22 tRNA genes, and a single major noncoding region [8]. The configuration of mitochondrial genomes, particularly protein-coding and rRNA genes, tends to be preserved within a phylum but shows considerable variation across different phyla [9]. Nevertheless, variations in the organization of protein-coding and rRNA genes were observed at finer taxonomic scales [10].

Mitochondria are an important organelle for energy production in a single cell [11]. Therefore, mitochondria are cellular heat sources and result in the warm-blooded nature of mammalian cells and organisms [12]. Indeed, the fundamental process of thermogenesis and thermoregulation involves separating chemical reactions within the mitochondrial matrix from ATP synthesis, a phenomenon referred to as ‘mitochondrial uncoupling’ [13, 14]. Therefore, chemical exothermic reactions occur unimpeded, thereby maintaining core body temperature [15]. Chemical uncoupling of mitochondria in vitro also results in an increase of the cell temperature [16]. In endothermic species, part of the energy released from the oxidation of respiratory substrates is used to drive ATP synthesis and metabolite transport, with variations influenced by the specific environmental conditions each species experiences [16]. The heat generated from metabolic processes preserves a consistent internal temperature within the organism. Ultimately, mitochondria are essential in the thermogenic process that regulates body temperature [17].

For the majority of fish, body temperature approximates that of their surrounding environment [18]. The diversity of thermal habitats and their abilities adapted to the thermal changes of habitats make them excellent organisms for studies on the evolutionary and phenotypic responses to environment temperature [19]. Given that moray eels are found in tropical and subtropical regions, they exhibited higher temperature requirements. Previous studies showed that moray eels are active at 24 °C to 28 °C and less active below 15 °C, what is more, there is a risk of death when moray eels are exposed to environments below 10 °C [20, 21]. Given that mitochondria function as cellular powerhouses, providing most eukaryotic cells with ATP through oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and sustaining a stable body temperature [22]. Production of ATP and energy transfer through phosphotransferases play an important role in the maintain of body temperature via mitochondria [23]. A series of recent studies have mainly focused on the morphology, taxonomy and germplasm resources of moray eels [24], limited genetic resources have restricted further researches on the temperature adaptability in moray eels.

Based on morphological classification, the ecologically diverse group, order Anguilliformes (eels), is divided into three suborders: Anguilloidei, Muraenoidei, and Congroidei [25, 26]. Congroidei are characterized by fused frontal bones, while Muraenoidei are identified by separated frontal bones, reduced branchial elements, and the absence of a lateral line or scales. Anguilloidei, on the other hand, are recognized by separated frontal bones [27]. Eels, belonging to the order Anguilliformes, are distinguished by their notably elongated bodies, slim cross-sections, and the typical lack of pelvic fins [5]. However, previous studies of the phylogenetic relationships among different eels were based solely on morphological data, failing to revealing the relationships among these three suborders [28, 29]. For example, all the currently identified suborders (Congroidei, Anguilloidei, and Muraenoidei) were recognized as polyphyletic groups, with two or three unnested monophyletic groups identified in each suborder [26]. Moreover, due to the lack of genetic resources, especially genome information, the phylogenetic relationships and evolutionary origins among different Anguilloidei species are still poorly understood [30], and classification at family level especially needs to be revised because many currently recognized genera and even families may be polyphyletic [8].

In this study, the complete mitochondrial genomes of 12 Gymnothorax spp and 2 Echidna spp species in the East China Sea and South China Sea, including G. pseudothyrsoideus, G. undulatus, G. chilospilus, G. favagineus, G. rueppelliae, G. javanicus, G. fimbriatus, G. meleagris, G. flavimarginatus, G. reticularis, G. niphostigmus, G. reevesi, Echidna polyzona and Echidna nebulosa were sequenced, assembled, and annotated. The relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) in protein-coding genes (PCGs) was analyzed to explore potential functional implications of these genes. Furthermore, phylogenetic studies on Anguilloidei and related species were carried out to explore their genetic relationships. To study the evolution rate of moray eels and their allies, the new phylogeny in six families and one outgroup were time-calibrated with 3 time points to propose new timescales for eel evolution. The evolutionary rates of 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp species were further calculated to assess the temperature adaptation. Sequencing and re-arranging of the mitogenome in the Anguilliformes not only supplements the genetic resources of eel genomes, but also enables in-depth genome comparisons of various eels.

Materials and methods

Sample collection, sequencing, and mitochondrial genome assembly

Samples of moray eels were collected from the southern waters of the South China Sea in China. The sampling was conducted from March to May 2020 over multiple occasions. The primary sampling locations were nearshore areas of the South China Sea, including Wenchang Puqian (20.0274° N, 110.5773° E), Qionghai Tanmen (19.2420° N, 110.5880° E), Haikou East Coast (spanning a range of coordinates along the eastern coastline of Haikou, approximately 20.0445° N, 110.1999° E), and Ledong Lingtou (18.6952° N, 108.7919° E). Samples were collected from returning fishing vessels operating in these nearshore areas to ensure comprehensive coverage of the target regions. After collecting tissue samples from their caudal fins, the moray eels were released back into their native habitat. The collected samples were preserved in 95% alcohol and stored at −20 °C for further use. The species identification was performed based on the morphological characteristics. After the sequencing of mitochondrial genome (Table S1), species identification was confirmed using the cox1 sequences and searched for the closely related taxa on the online identification platform of BOLD system (http://www.barcodinglife.org). Genomic DNA was extracted from muscle tissues using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Dusseldorf, Germany) following the provided protocol. Libraries of small fragments (350 bp) were constructed, and paired-end sequencing (PE 150 bp) was carried out on the Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform (San Diego, CA) by Shenzhen Huitong Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). The mitochondrial genome assembly was performed using the GetOrganelle tool [31], which detects and assembles organellar genomes from short-read data. Initially, Fastp was employed to discard unpaired, short, and low-quality reads, ensuring only high-quality data were retained for subsequent analysis. Then, the BLAST search was performed on the clean reads to identify the best match to a known Anguilliformes mitochondrial genome, providing the “seed” for GetOrganelle. Finally, SPAdes was used as the assembler within the GetOrganelle toolkit to generate the de novo mitochondrial genome assembly. Then, the clean reads were extracted and assembled to the de novo mitgenome using SPAdes software [32].

Mitochondrial genome annotation and characterization

Rough boundaries for each gene identified in this study were initially determined using the online tools of Mitos (http://mitos.bioinf.uni-leipzig.de/index.py). All the 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs) and 2 rRNA genes were identified and aligned with orthologs in the known Anguilliformes mitochondrial genome downloaded from GenBank. And the Geneious were used for the correction of preliminary annotation results [33].

The tRNA genes were identified using the MITOS WebServer and tRNAscan-SE 2.0, applying the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code [34, 35]. Secondary structures of the tRNA genes were predicted using tRNAscan-SE (http://lowelab.ucsc.edu/tRNAscan-SE/) [35]. Protein-coding regions were identified using the ORF Finder on the NCBI website (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/orffinder/) with the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code. Base composition, relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU), and amino acid content were calculated using MEGA v. 7.0 software in this study [36]. All protein-coding genes were annotated using the vertebrate mitochondrial genetic code. Special attention was given to incomplete stop codons, which are completed post-transcriptionally by polyadenylation. The assembled mitogenome sequences have their accession numbers listed in Table 1, whereas the raw sequencing data (NGS HiSeq Illumina data) accession numbers are provided separately in Supplementary Table S1.

Phylogenetic analysis

In this study, a dataset consist of mitogenomes from 12 Gymnothorax spp and 2 Echidna spp and 35 other species (downloaded from GenBank) was used for phylogenetic analysis, and 3 species in Congroidei were selected as outgroups (Table S1). First, sequences of 13 protein-coding genes were extracted using Geneious 9.0. MEGA v. 7.0 was used to remove stop codons from the extracted protein-coding genes, and MAFFT was used to perform multiple alignment of the 13 protein-coding genes [37], and Gblocks was used to eliminat ambiguous sequences [38]. Phylogenetic analysis utilized both Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Bayesian Inference (BI) approaches. The IQ-TREE in the ML method was used for tree construction [39, 40], 1000 strand bootstraps were selected to evaluate the reliability of branch nodes, and the bootstrap resampling (BP) of each branch was calculated. Each sample were performed every 1000 generations, the posterior probability (PP) is calculated after discarding the top 25% of trees as the aging stage. To ensure the stability of the phylogenetic tree, the average standard deviation of split frequencies was maintained below 0.01.

Divergence time estimates

BEAST divergence times are estimated in BEAST 1.10.4 [41]. The BEAUTI v1.10.4 was used to construct an XML file [42], with setting of the prior taxa clade A, clade B, and clade C respectively. General Time Reversible (GTR) was selected using the nucleotide replacement model [43]. The time of each clade was set according to a previous study [1]. For the Monte Carlo Markov chains, each chain was set as 100 million generations, and the state was recorded every 10,000 generations. Convergence was verified using Tracer v1.10.4, and the maximum clade credibility tree (MCC) with annotated branches was generated using TreeAnnotator v1.10.4, excluding 10% as burn-in.

Selection test

Nucleotide sequences of 13 mPCGs from 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp species were converted into amino acids using MEGA 7.0, and each gene’s amino acid sequence was aligned with Muscle using default settings. After verifying the alignments visually and saving them in FASTA format, DAMBE 6.4 was utilized to transform the data from FASTA to PML format [44]. For each gene sequence data set, the non-synonymous mutation rate (Ka), synonymous mutation rate (Ks), and their ratio (Ka/Ks or ω)—a measure of selection within a phylogenetic context—were calculated using DnaSP 5 [45].

Results

Sequence characteristics of complete mitogenomes from 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp. species

Our analyses of the mitochondrial genome showed that the length of the mitgenome from 14 species ranged from 16.5 k to 16.6 k, and the CG content varied from 41.1 to 46.1% (Fig. 1). The mitochondria genome in the 14 species are circular molecules encoding a total of 37 genes (Fig. 1), among which there are 13 protein-coding genes (PCGs) including 2 ATPase subunits encoding genes (atp6, atp8), 3 cytochrome oxidase subunits encoding genes (cox1, cox2, cox3), 1 cytochrome b encoding gene (ctyb), 7 NADH dehydrogenase subunits encoding genes (nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4, nad4L, nad5, nad6), small and large subunit rRNA encoding genes (rrnL and rrnS) (Table S2). We also identified 22 tRNA genes and a non-coding control region (CR) (Table S2). Among them, 7 tRNA genes trnA, trnN, trnC, trnY, trnE, trnP and trnQ and 1 protein coding gene nad6 are localized on the negative strand (-) (Table S2).

The codon usage patterns among 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp species

To characterize the codon usage pattern of the mitgenome, we analyzed the start and stop codons. The analysis revealed that the start codons for the 13 identified PCGs were ATG or GTG, while the stop codons were TAG or TAA (Fig. 2). Additionally, the relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) values indicated that six codon families are most frequently used in Gymnothorax. (CUA, UCA, ACA, CAA, AAA, UGA and CGA). Comparison of RSCU showed that many species use the stop codon TAA and some species use TAG as a stop codon, and stop codon usage varied among 14 species (Fig. 2). CGA exhibited the highest codon usage frequency (RSCU > 2.0) among the 14 species, followed by CUA (highly used in Gymnothorax flavimarginatus, Gymnothorax reticularis and Gymnothorax chilospilus), and thirdly UCA (highly used in Echidna polyzona) (Fig. 2). The least frequently used codons among the 14 species are UUG, ACG, GCG and AGU.

Ribosomal and transfer RNA genes analysis

Secondary structure prediction indicated that nearly all tRNA genes adopt a classic cloverleaf configuration, comprising four distinct domains and a short variable loop (Fig. 3). The secondary structures of tRNA genes include the amino acid or receptor (AA) stem, the dihydrouridine (DHU) arm (D stem + D loop), the anticodon (AC) arm (AC stem + AC loop), the thymidine (T) arm (T stem + T-loop), and the variable (V) loop (Fig. 3). The lengths of various stems are as follows: AA stem = 7–8 bp, AC stem = 4–5 bp, T stem = 4–5 bp, D stem = 3–4 bp, variable loop = 4–5 bp, T loop = 6–8 bp, D loop = 5–13 bp, V loop = 4–6 bp. Additionally, the DHU of tRNA-S1 forms only a small loop without a stem.

Mitochondrial gene rearrangement

Rhynchoconger ectenurus, Ariosoma meeki and Conger erebennus are thought to be the order of ancestral states (Fig. 4) [46–48]. Compared with the ancestral type nad5-ctyb-trnT-nad6-trnE, the gene sequence in the Xenocongridae, Moringuidae, Myrocongridae and Muraenidae belongs to type B, in which was rearranged to nad5-nad6-trnE-ctyb-trnT (Fig. 4). Among species analyzed in this study, Heterenchelyidae is independent of types B and D, the trnL1 in this species is inserted between trnL2 and nad1, which belongs to type C (Fig. 4). Types A, B, and D are arranged as trnL2-nad1-trnI, and trnL1 is located between trnS1 and nad5 (Fig. 4). The gene order in Anguillidae shows the highest similarity to type B. The difference is that the trnQ is not on the negative strand (-) but on the positive strand (+) of the mitgenome (Fig. 4).

Phylogenetic and divergence time analyses

The phylogenetic analysis were performed among 51 species from seven families (Congridae, Xenocongridae, Moringuidae, Anguillidae, Heterenchelyidae, Myrocongridae and Muraenidae) (Fig. 5). Phylogenetic trees generated using different methods exhibited the same topology and strong intermediate bootstrapping and post-probability values (Fig. 5). Our results showed that the Muraenidae species are clustered into a single branch (Fig. 5). This aligns with traditional morphological classification, suggesting a strong correlation between the morphological traits of different fish and their genetic backgrounds [49–52]. The phylogenetic tree further revealed that within Anguilloidei, Muraenidae shares the closest evolutionary relationship with Myrocongridae and the most distant with Heterenchelyidae (Fig. 5).

At the order level, suborder of the phylogenetic tree was divided into two lineages, the Lineage one was composed of the family Moringuidae, Anguillidae, and Xenocongridae, while another clade was consisted of the family Muraenidae, Myrocongridae, and Heterenchelyidae (Fig. 5). Our results showed that the family Muraenida, Anguillida, and Xenocongridae were all monophyletic. Xenocongridae and Moringuidae were sister groups, and form another sister group of Anguillidae. Muraenidae and Myrocongridae. What is more, the Anguilla in the family Anguillidae was a monophyletic group at the genus level, the genus Moringua in the family Moringuidae was also a monophyletic group, and the genus Gymnothorax in the family Muraenidae was a paraphyletic group.

Divergence time estimation

In order to uncover the evolution pattern of different Anguilliformes: Muraenidae, we calculated the specific divergence time of 14 species. Our results showed that the divergence time of Muraenidae is 61 million years, and that of Muraenidae and Myrocongridae is about 68 million years (Fig. 6).

Selection analyses

Subsequent selection analyses on the 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp mitogenomes revealed that the atp8 gene had the highest average Ka and Ka/Ks values among the 13 PCGs, suggesting it may have undergone more rapid evolution compared to other mitochondrial genes in Gymnothorax (Fig. 7). In contrast, the genes cox3 and cox1 exhibited the lowest mean Ka and Ka/Ks values, respectively (Fig. 7). Interestingly, when comparing the Ka/Ks ratios across different lineages for all 13 PCGs using the free ratio model, all Ka/Ks values were found to be below 1 in this study (Fig. 7).

Discussion

In this study, the mitochondrial genomes of 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp species were sequenced, assembled and annotated, greatly expanding our understanding of the evolution patterns of moray eels. A comprehensive phylogenetic tree was generated based on the mitochondrial gene sequences of all existing species in Anguilloidei (consist of 6 families) and Congroidei (Congridae), our results showed the phylogeny and divergence time of these 7 families. Analyses on the specific mitochondrial rearrangements in Muraenidae have provided a new basis of the evolutionary dynamics of the mitochondrial genome in moray eels and enhanced our knowledge of eel phylogeny at a new level.

Previous studies showed that species do not use the same frequency of the synonymous codons for specific genes, resulting in codon usage bias [53]. Several factors, including gene length, translation selection, protein amino acid composition, tRNA abundance, mutation bias, and GC composition, may lead to codon usage bias [54]. Given the essential role of temperature adaptation in biomolecular evolution, the unresolved nature of this phenomenon persists to this day [55]. Fishes living in the tropics and subtropics adapt to corresponding water temperatures through adaptive modifications of specific biomolecules. Previous research has shown that temperature adaptation of cold-water fishes and tropical fishes are closely related to the codon usage bias [55]. To investigate codon usage bias in warm-water fish, this study computed the codon usage patterns of various genes in Gymnothorax species. Among the 14 species of moray eels examined, CGA exhibited the highest codon usage frequency (RSCU > 2.0), which may be related to the adaptation of moray eels to warm-water environments.

Studies have previously documented genetic rearrangements in species with distinct habitats, such as cave-dwelling crayfish adapted to low-light cave environments and Notothenioids adapted to frigid climates [56, 57]. It has been argued that random genomic rearrangements are unlikely to benefit the maintenance of the structural stability as most rearrangements were considered deleterious [58]. Researchers have revealed that mitochondrial gene rearrangements are infrequent in the evolutionary lineage of vertebrates, yet they serve as dependable phylogenetic markers for specific vertebrate lineages [59]. However, in this study we found that there is an increasing number of mitochondrial gene rearrangements in the Anguilloidei lineage. After combining the mitochondrial genomes from latest sequenced 14 and published 34 eel (from 5 families) and 3 Congroidei (Congridae) and summarizing gene rearrangement types of 51 existing eel mitochondrial genomes from 7 families, we discovered three types of gene rearrangements patterns in Anguilloidei, providing extremely valuable genetic resources for in-depth research on the evolution and environmental adaptability of eels.

Within Anguilloidei, the phylogenetic relationships among different families remain largely unresolved [8, 60]. Besides, the classification of species within Muraenidae only relies on morphological observations without sufficient molecular biological evidence [49, 50, 61]. PCGs in mitochondria genome are considered as reliable molecular markers for phylogenetic analysis in various teleosts [62]. In this study, phylogenetic trees were constructed using 13 PCGs, employing both maximum likelihood (ML) and Bayesian inference (BI) methods. The resulting topologies were consistent. Among the six families of Anguilloidei (Anguillidae, Heterenchelyidae, Moringuidae, Xenocongridae, Muraenidae, Myrocongridae), Muraenidae is the closest to Myrocongridae, followed by Heterenchelyidae. Species from different genera including Echidna rhodochilus (Echidna), Strophidon sathete (Strophidon), Rhinomuraena quaesita (Rhinomuraena) and Enchelynassa canina (Enchelynassa) are grouped together forming the clade Muraenidae, and this classification is consistent with the morphological classification of eels. Individual clades contain only one representative species (such as Heterenchelyidae and Myrocongridae), and future studies need to analyze larger taxonomic samples and more taxonomic markers to fully understand the phylogenetic relationships in Anguilloidei.

All 13 mitochondrial PCGs are integral to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), a critical metabolic process influencing animal ecological adaptation. These genes are highly responsive to selection pressures related to energy metabolism [63, 64]. At the genomic level, organisms’ adaptation to their environment is primarily reflected in two factors: synonymous and non-synonymous substitutions [65, 66]. Research conducted previously indicates that non-synonymous substitutions can impair respiratory activity, leading to decreased metabolic efficiency and generally having detrimental effects on the organism [67]. The deleterious effects of non-synonymous mutations in the mitochondrial genome trigger efficient purification selection, thereby maintaining the fitness of the mitotic genome [68]. Mitochondrial genes are targets of natural selection to help organisms to adapt to different energy needs in different environment. For example, positive selection drives mitochondrial genes to increase metabolic efficiency in Atlantic salmon at low temperatures. Since purifying selection and genetic drift are major evolutionary forces [69], in our investigation, we observed that the Ka/Ks ratios for all PCGs were < 1, indicating that purifying selection predominantly governs the evolution of these functional genes. Notably, the atp8 gene displayed the highest average Ka and Ka/Ks values, indicating greater amino acid variation compared to other mitochondrial genes. These findings align with previously reported Ka/Ks ratios in other fish species [70, 71]. A higher Ka/Ks ratio suggests that the atp8 gene may have undergone less stringent selective pressures, resulting in more accumulated mutations and an increased likelihood of functional loss during evolution.

Positive selection can provide important adaptation details to new environments [72]. Fish living in different temperature environments undergo moderate evolutionary adaptations in their maximum mitochondrial respiration rates. For instance, Antarctic fish like Pagothenia borchgrevinki achieve their peak swimming speed at −0.8 °C but cannot swim above 2 °C [73]. The optimum temperature for the growth of Atlantic salmon is between 5 and 26 °C, with the optimal temperature around 16 °C [74]. Brown trout thrive within temperature ranges of approximately 4 to 19.5 °C, with optimal growth occurring around 14 °C [74]. Mortality in Atlantic salmon and brown trout is typically observed at chronic temperatures around 27 ± 5 °C and 24 ± 7 °C, respectively [75]. It has been reported that the COX super complex is a group of enzymes present in the inner mitochondrial membrane that participate in mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) [76]. COX3 plays a vital role in maintaining the stability and function of the COX super complex [77]. In this study, we found that cox3 exhibited the smallest non-synonymous mutation value among all the 13 protein-coding genes, indicating that cox3 is the most conserved gene that has experienced the strictest selection restriction by maintaining mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. In the evolution of moray eels, cox3 may maintain energy metabolism and thus facilitate the adaptation of moray eels to warm water environments.

Conclusion

In this study, we identified and characterized the complete mitochondrial genomes of 12 Gymnothorax spp. and 2 Echidna spp species in the families Anguilloidei and Muraenidae, and the mitogenomes contain 37 genes. In addition, rearrangements were identified between the two families. A comprehensive phylogenetic tree based on the complete mitogenome of Anguilloidei was meticulously constructed. Our molecular phylogenetic analysis showed that Anguilloidei was classified into six major monophyletic clades, with three Congridae species of Congroidei being outgrouped. The divergence time of Muraenidae is 61 million years and that of Muraenidae and Myrocongridae is about 68 million years. Based on Ka/Ks analysis, the patterns of molecular evolution for individual PCGs show notable consistency across the 13 species of moray eels. Analysis of mitochondrial genome sequences and gene arrangements provides new insights into the environment adaptation and phylogenetic relationships between the major lineages of the superfamily Anguilloidei and Muraenidae.
