Gambling disorder in late life: A systematic review of behavioral patterns, clinical correlates, and psychotherapeutic interventions
Authors: Graziella C Prezzavento, Maria C Quattropani, Vittorio Lenzo, Alberto Sardella, Chiara Franceschini, Giulia Galea, Pasquale Caponnetto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesGambling in older adults represents an emerging but still underrecognized mental health concern. Although gambling participation is increasingly observed in later life, the specific psychological, social, cognitive, and biological factors associated with gambling‐related problems in older adults remain insufficiently characterized.MethodsA systematic review was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Empirical studies involving adults aged ≥55 years with Gambling were identified through searches of Web of Science, PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, PsycNET, ClinicalTrials.gov, and WHO ICTRP from January 15 to June 15, 2025.ResultsFrom 40,263 identified records, 35 studies met the inclusion criteria. The included studies, published between 2000 and 2025, primarily investigated gambling behaviors, severity, psychosocial correlates, and associated mental health outcomes in older adults. Commonly identified correlates to gambling‐related problems included loneliness, reduced social support, financial difficulties, male gender, stressful life events, and early gambling initiation. Gambling was frequently reported as a recreational activity but could become maladaptive when used as a strategy to regulate negative emotions or cope with social isolation. Psychiatric comorbidity, particularly depression and anxiety, was frequently observed in clinical and high‐risk samples. Evidence regarding psychological interventions remains limited but suggests potential benefits.Discussion and ConclusionsCurrent evidence highlights substantial gaps in age‐specific assessment and targeted interventions. Future studies should prioritize tailored psychological interventions addressing loneliness, emotional regulation, cognitive distortions, and life transitions in later adulthood.

BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES

Gambling Disorder remains the only formally recognized non‐substance‐related addictive disorder, defined by the DSM‐5 as a pattern of persistent and recurrent problematic gambling behavior causing significant impairment or distress. 1 Diagnosis requires meeting at least four out of nine criteria within a 12‐month period, including preoccupation with gambling, failed attempts to cut back, use of gambling to cope with negative emotions, chasing losses, and continued gambling despite serious adverse consequences. Importantly, DSM‐5 diagnostic criteria are agnostic with respect to the precipitating factors or motivations underlying gambling behavior: if the criteria are met, the disorder is present regardless of whether gambling was initiated for social, emotional, or recreational reasons. This distinction is clinically relevant when assessing older adults, whose motivational pathways to gambling may differ from younger populations without this implying a lesser degree of psychopathological severity. In recent years, gambling participation is increasing significantly among the older people and becoming a particularly widespread and regular recreational behavior among this population. 2

Estimating the true prevalence of gambling disorder in later life, however, remains methodologically challenging. The wide range reported in the literature, from 0.01% to 10.6% for adults aged 60 and above, reflects substantial heterogeneity in measurement instruments, age thresholds, cultural contexts, and sampling strategies, and should therefore be interpreted with considerable caution rather than as a reliable epidemiological estimate. 3 Factors contributing to this vulnerability include social isolation, cognitive decline, and financial insecurity. These factors, however, are not exclusive to older adults; younger problem gamblers may present similar vulnerabilities. What distinguishes the older adult context is the convergence and co‐occurrence of multiple age‐specific transitions (retirement, bereavement, functional decline, reduced social networks, and increased unstructured leisure time) which together may create a particularly fertile environment for gambling escalation in this population, even when individual risk factors are shared with younger age groups.

A further biological vulnerability specific to older adults deserves explicit consideration. Neurobiological changes associated with aging, including dopaminergic system decline, reduced frontal inhibitory control, and altered reward processing, may increase susceptibility to reinforcement‐based behaviors such as gambling. 4 Moreover, dopamine agonist medications, including pramipexole and ropinirole, prescribed for Parkinson's disease and restless leg syndrome, have been well documented as pharmacological precipitants of impulse control disorders including gambling disorder in older adults. 5 Clinicians assessing gambling in older adults should routinely screen for current dopaminergic medication use as a potential etiological factor.

Several theoretical frameworks have been proposed to explain the emergence and maintenance of this behavioral addiction across the lifespan, and their application to older populations deserves explicit consideration. The Pathways Model of Problem Gambling proposed by Blaszczynski and Nower conceptualizes gambling disorder as the outcome of distinct vulnerability trajectories, including behaviorally conditioned, emotionally vulnerable, and impulsive–antisocial pathways. This model emphasizes heterogeneity in etiological mechanisms and may be particularly relevant for older adults, whose psychological profiles and life experiences differ substantially from younger populations. 6 From a lifespan perspective, Socioemotional Selectivity Theory posits that as individuals perceive their remaining time as limited, motivational priorities shift toward emotionally meaningful goals and regulation of affective states. 7 While boredom, social isolation, and emotional dysregulation may drive gambling engagement across all age groups, in older adults these motivations are amplified by the structural conditions of later life, including retirement‐related loss of routine and identity, bereavement, and reduced access to alternative social activities. Emotional vulnerability arising from these losses may drive older adults toward gambling as a strategy to escape negative emotions such as loneliness or depression, and to restore a sense of well‐being. 8 Tira et al. identify three pathways to gambling disorder: the grief pathway, driven by negative life events and emotional distress; the habit pathway, in which repeated exposure to gambling fosters erroneous beliefs (gambler's fallacy, near‐miss effects) and escalating behavior, with boredom shifting from a reason to start gambling to an excuse to continue; and the dormant pathway, rooted in predispositions to compulsive behavior, low self‐control, and strong comorbidities such as tobacco and alcohol dependence. 9 Stress–vulnerability models suggest that addictive behaviors may emerge from the interaction between predisposing individual characteristics and environmental stressors. 10 In older adults, life transitions such as retirement, bereavement, declining health, or financial insecurity may act as stressors that interact with individual vulnerabilities (e.g., impulsivity, emotional dysregulation), increasing the risk for maladaptive behavioral engagement.

Despite the growing prevalence of gambling in older adults and its significant impact on quality of life, psychological well‐being, and healthcare utilization, research in this population remains limited.

This systematic review aims to address this gap by pursuing three explicit objectives: (1) to characterize the prevalence and severity of gambling disorder and problematic gambling behaviors in adults aged 55 years and older; (2) to identify the psychological, cognitive, social, and biological correlates associated with gambling in this population, with particular attention to risk and protective factors; and (3) to evaluate the available evidence on psychological and psychotherapeutic interventions targeting this condition in later life. We hypothesize that gambling‐related problems would be associated with a combination of psychosocial vulnerabilities (e.g., loneliness, reduced social support, stressful life events), psychiatric symptoms, and biological contributors rather than with aging alone, and that the functional role of gambling as a coping strategy will emerge as a central mediating mechanism. These hypotheses guided both the extraction and the interpretation of the evidence.

METHODS

This systematic review was recorded on PROSPERO (CRD42025639009) and conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta‐Analyses (PRISMA 2020). The review focused on the following research question: “How do psychosocial vulnerabilities and coping‐related mechanisms contribute to Gambling Disorder in adults aged 55 years and older, and which evidence‐based psychological or psychotherapeutic interventions effectively address these mechanisms in prevention and treatment?”.

Population (P)

Inclusion: adults aged ≥55 years diagnosed with or presenting symptoms of Gambling Disorder or Problem Gambling (DSM‐5, ICD‐11, SOGS, PGSI, or equivalent); studies comparing older and younger adults are eligible if gambling outcomes are reported separately for the older cohort.

Although no consensus exists on the age threshold for “older adulthood,” with gambling studies ranging from 50 to 70 years, we adopted a cutoff of 55 years, consistent with prior gambling addiction literature and empirical evidence identifying this age as a meaningful transition point (onset of regular gambling in older women; beginning of age‐related decision‐making impairment). This threshold was established a priori to ensure adequate coverage of the available evidence. We acknowledge that the 55+ category encompasses heterogeneous subgroups (“young‐old” 55–69; “older‐old” 70+) that may differ in cognitive and functional status, which may affect the interpretability of findings.

Information sources

The following information sources were used to identify studies for inclusion in this review:

Databases

Web of Science (WOS), PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus, PsycNET.

Registers

ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform (ICTRP).

Reference lists

Screening of reference lists from included studies and relevant reviews.

Search strategy

A comprehensive search strategy was developed for each database and source (Supporting Information S1: Table 1) and conducted between January 15 and June 15, 2025. No separate update search was performed.

Selection process

The selection process followed these steps:

Screening of titles and abstracts

Three independent reviewers (G.C.P, G.G., C.F.) screened all records identified through database searches to exclude irrelevant studies.

Full‐text review

Full‐text articles of potentially eligible studies were retrieved and independently assessed by the same reviewers.

Consensus and resolution

Disagreements between reviewers were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, consultation with supervisors (M.C.Q., V.L., A.S., P.C.).

Automation tools

Rayyan was used for screening and deduplication.

Data collection process

Data were collected from eligible reports using a standardized data extraction form (Table 1). Three reviewers (G.C.P., C.F., G.G.) independently extracted data to ensure accuracy and completeness. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion or consultation with supervisors (M.C.Q., V.L., A.S., P.C.).

Study risk of bias assessment

In this systematic review, rigorous tools were employed to meticulously assess the Risk of Bias. Specifically, the Newcastle–Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale was utilized for Cross‐Sectional, Observational Cohort and Comparative Observational Studies (NOS); while the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist for Case Reports was used for a case report.

RESULTS

Study selection

The articles resulting from the research phase in the databases listed above produced a total of 40,263 reports. The duplicate manuscripts were deleted until a number of 35,116 unique reports were produced. These unique reports were examined by the title and abstract and 203 were selected. These reports were opened and analyzed in full‐text version. In conclusion following full‐text screening 35 studies were included in the systematic review. The PRISMA flow diagram in Figure 1 outlines the selection process in detail.

PRISMA flow diagram.

As shown in the flow diagram, several studies initially appeared to meet the inclusion criteria but were ultimately excluded for specific reasons: a total of 168 studies did not meet the inclusion criteria: 93 studies were excluded due to the unavailability of full text, 67 studies involved participants younger than the required age threshold (below 55 years), and 8 studies did not focus on psychological or psychotherapeutic interventions.

Study characteristics

The 35 included studies (Table 1) were published between 2000 and 2025 and were conducted mainly in North America and Europe. Study designs included primarily cross‐sectional studies, as well as longitudinal studies, secondary analyses, observational studies, comparative studies, and one case report. Sample sizes ranged from small case reports (n = 1) to large population‐based studies exceeding 10,000 participants. All studies examined gambling disorder among older adults, focusing on prevalence, severity, psychosocial factors, comorbidities, and associated risk factors. Outcome measures were heterogeneous and included assessments of gambling severity, psychological symptoms, cognition, quality of life, and social functioning.

Risk of bias in studies

The results of the risk of bias assessment are presented in Tables 2 and 3. Overall, the cross‐sectional studies demonstrated good to very good methodological quality, with most scoring between 7 and 10 points on the adapted Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Several studies achieved very high ratings (≥9 points), reflecting strong representativeness, use of validated assessment tools, and appropriate statistical analyses. 16 , 18 , 19 , 26 , 27 , 31 , 32 A smaller group of studies, such as Cousins and Witcher and Botterill et al., received satisfactory ratings (5–6 points) due to limitations in sampling procedures, incomplete reporting of non‑respondents, or reliance on ad hoc measures. 20 , 33 For case reports, the JBI Critical Appraisal Checklist was applied. The only included case report, Sauvaget et al., met nearly all methodological criteria except for the reporting of adverse events, and was therefore judged as “Include” with minor concerns. 35

Results of studies

The 35 studies identified meeting the inclusion criteria and providing a comprehensive, multi‐perspective overview of gambling behaviors, clinical correlates, and trajectories in older adults. The vast majority of these investigations (n = 28) utilized cross‐sectional designs, which precludes the establishment of temporal precedence or causal inference. Furthermore, the included literature exhibited substantial heterogeneity in how gambling behaviors were operationalized, spanning a wide spectrum from recreational participation to at‐risk and clinically significant gambling disorders.

Prevalence

Prevalence estimates for problem and pathological gambling varied markedly across studies, reflecting the diversity of assessment instruments and sampling strategies employed. Severe problem gambling was reported at 6.9% by Turner et al., while Clarke and Clarkson reported a 9% prevalence using the SOGS‐R. 28 , 38 Venuleo et al. observed a problem gambling prevalence of 11.5%, with an additional 26.7% of the sample classified as moderate‐risk. 41 In Canadian community samples, Wiebe and Cox found that 1.6% of participants were problem gamblers and 1.2% were probable pathological gamblers. 19 Similarly, Ladd et al. noted rates of 5.9% for problem gambling and 4.7% for pathological gambling, whereas Erickson et al. reported 6.4% and 3.8%, respectively. 13 , 18 A higher prevalence of 16% was identified by Parke et al. within a UK sample. 39 Finally, regarding past‐month behaviors in specific cohorts, Ng revealed that 27% of low‐income Singaporean older adults had engaged in gambling activities. 29

Sociodemographic and risk factors

Sociodemographic analyses consistently identified male gender as a significant predictor for being classified as a problem gambler. 26 , 40 , 42 However, Gorenko found that women exhibited higher mean PGSI scores, demonstrating that female gender, lower social support, and an accumulation of stressful life events were robust predictors of greater gambling severity. 45 Age‐stratified observations indicated internal variability within the geriatric cohort: younger segments of the older adult population (the “young‐old”) frequently exhibited higher gambling frequency and greater vulnerability to life stressors compared to older‐old subgroups. 13 , 38 Additional independent predictors of elevated problem gambling risk included lower income, being unmarried, and separated or divorced status. Environmental and contextual factors, such as participation in casino bus tours, were also positively associated with gambling. 13 , 37 , 38 Regarding specific gambling formats, slot machines and electronic gaming machines (EGMs) emerged as the predominant problematic form of gambling across multiple samples. 15 , 21 , 36 , 37 Other formats included bingo, which correlated with lower education levels and physical, as well as mahjong and various lotteries, which were strongly linked to lower life satisfaction or specific ethnic identity affiliations. 20 , 22 , 29 , 32 , 43

Motivations

Older adults endorsed a combination of monetary, social, and emotional motivations for gambling. McNeilly and Burke documented that primary drivers among casino patrons included relaxation/fun (68.1%), getting away for the day (43.7%), passing time (42.7%), and relieving boredom (30%). Similarly, Martin et al. (2011) identified entertainment (79.5%) and winning money (62.7%) as primary motivations, alongside boredom and loneliness relief (38.2%). 11 Problem gamblers were significantly more likely to report gambling alone, and regular gamblers scored higher on dimensions of curiosity and escape. 26 , 28 , 42 At a structural motivational level, Philippe and Vallerand found that obsessive passion was positively associated with gambling severity, whereas harmonious passion was negatively associated. 23 Furthermore, path modeling by Parke et al. identified loneliness and anxiety as the only direct predictors of problem gambling, with frailty and pain exerting indirect effects through depression and anxiety. 39 Loneliness was also found to completely mediate the relationship between marital status and problem gambling specifically among older men. 33

Cognitive correlates

Empirical evidence regarding cognitive correlates in older gamblers yielded mixed outcomes. Zhang et al. found that older recreational gamblers demonstrated better global cognition and episodic memory compared to nongamblers, but exhibited significantly lower performance in executive function and working memory. 43 In a clinical case study, Sauvaget et al. documented an older adult who developed severe online gambling disorder and accumulated massive debts despite exhibiting no global cognitive impairment, with the presentation instead characterized by severe social isolation and specific pre‐existing personality traits. 35

Comorbidity

Psychiatric comorbidity was highly prevalent, particularly within clinical, helpline, and treatment‐seeking samples. Kerber et al. reported that among recovering pathological gamblers, 82.5% presented with mood disorders (all meeting criteria for major depression), 47.5% with anxiety disorders, and 60% with personality disorders (predominantly obsessive‐compulsive personality disorder at 37.5%). 25 Potenza et al. noted that among older adults contacting a helpline, gambling‐related depression was endorsed by 74.4% and suicidality by 21.8%. 21

Early‐onset gamblers exhibited significantly worse physical and psychological health outcomes compared to late‐onset gamblers. 14 Phenotypic cluster analyses by Granero et al. identified a distinct high‐risk subgroup characterized by strategic gambling preferences, comorbid substance use, and poor overall psychological functioning. 40 Bazargan et al. found that heavy/pathological gamblers reported significantly more stressful life events alongside lower psychological well‐being, higher anxiety, and more obsessive‐compulsive symptoms. 12 Longitudinally, Pilver et al. demonstrated that at‐risk and problem gambling was independently associated with the subsequent development of incident generalized anxiety disorder (OR = 2.51) and any substance‐use disorder (OR = 2.61). 31

Longitudinal trajectories

Longitudinal data indicated that while gambling severity can fluctuate or decrease over time, it remains heavily anchored to stable individual characteristics. Gorenko found that PGSI scores declined only modestly over five assessment waves, with most of the variance attributable to stable individual traits rather than time‐based changes. 45 Black et al. observed no significant differences in problem gambling outcomes between older and younger pathological gamblers over a mean follow‐up of 2.6 years, with the odds of problem gambling decreasing by a factor of 0.65 per year for both age groups. 36 Lastly, Kausch found that among treatment‐seeking older adult gamblers, over 25% had developed a gambling problem within 5 years, and nearly 50% within 10 years prior to admission. 15

DISCUSSION AND CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review provides an overview of Gambling Disorder and problematic gambling behaviors among adults aged 55 years and older, focusing on prevalence, associated clinical correlates, and available psychological interventions. The findings indicate that gambling‐related problems represent an important but still insufficiently characterized clinical issue in later life. The current literature does not support a qualitatively distinct disorder in older adults; instead, common vulnerability mechanisms appear to interact with age‐related circumstances, influencing the onset, maintenance, and clinical expression of gambling problems. The first objective of this review was to characterize the prevalence and severity of gambling‐related problems in older adults. The included studies showed highly variable prevalence estimates, ranging from low levels of problem gambling to substantially higher rates in selected samples. This variability reflects differences in assessment tools, recruitment strategies, and definitions of gambling‐related impairment rather than providing a stable epidemiological estimate. In particular, the frequent use of screening instruments rather than DSM‐based diagnostic evaluations limits direct comparison between studies; prevalence findings should therefore be interpreted cautiously, and future epidemiological studies should prioritize standardized diagnostic approaches adapted to older populations. 2 A relevant implication emerging from these findings is that gambling problems in older adults should not be interpreted only through the lens of aging‐related difficulties. Although loneliness, financial strain, reduced social support, and stressful life events were frequently associated with gambling severity, these factors are also recognized correlates of gambling problems across age groups. 2 The distinctive feature of later life is instead the accumulation of multiple transitions, retirement, changes in social roles, bereavement, reduced opportunities for alternative reinforcement, so that aging functions less as an independent risk factor and more as a context that modifies existing vulnerabilities. Consistent with this interpretation, the motivational findings suggest that gambling in older adults occupies a complex position between recreational activity and maladaptive coping strategy. Several studies reported that older adults gamble for entertainment, social interaction, relaxation, and boredom reduction. 11 , 24 , 28 These motivations alone do not indicate psychopathology. However, when gambling becomes a primary mechanism for regulating distress, compensating for social isolation, or escaping negative emotional states, it may contribute to clinically significant impairment. This distinction has important clinical implications, as assessment should consider not only gambling frequency or financial consequences but also the psychological function that gambling serves. The findings also highlight substantial heterogeneity among older adults who experience gambling‐related problems. Some individuals appear to present gambling behaviors primarily associated with emotional vulnerability, while others show earlier gambling initiation, greater persistence, or broader psychiatric difficulties. 9 , 40 This variability supports the use of individualized clinical formulations rather than assuming a single pathway to Gambling Disorder in later life. An additional contribution of this review is the identification of biological and medical factors as an underexplored area in the current literature. Although most included studies focused on psychosocial correlates, clinicians should consider the possible contribution of neurological conditions, age‐related changes in reward processing, and medication exposure. In particular, dopamine agonists prescribed for Parkinson's disease and restless leg syndrome have been associated with impulse control disorders, including pathological gambling. 5 Although not the focus of this review, medication‐induced gambling warrants systematic assessment in future studies and clinical evaluations of older adults. Psychiatric comorbidity represented another consistent finding across the included studies. Older adults with gambling problems, particularly those recruited from clinical or support settings, showed elevated levels of depression, anxiety, and suicidality. 21 , 25 Longitudinal evidence further suggests that gambling problems may be associated with subsequent psychiatric difficulties, including anxiety disorders and substance‐related problems. 31 These findings reinforce the need to integrate gambling assessment within broader mental health evaluations rather than treating gambling behavior as an isolated symptom. The relationship between gambling and cognitive functioning remains unclear. While some studies reported associations between problematic gambling and poorer executive functioning, other findings suggested that recreational gambling may coexist with higher cognitive engagement or social activity. 43 These differences likely reflect the distinction between recreational and disordered gambling, emphasizing the importance of avoiding the assumption that gambling participation alone represents pathology. Taken together, the evidence partially confirms the hypotheses formulated in the Introduction. Psychosocial vulnerabilities and coping‑related mechanisms consistently emerged across studies, whereas biological contributors were less frequently examined but showed preliminary relevance. This hypothesis‑driven interpretation provides a coherent framework for understanding late‑life gambling and highlights priority areas for future research. Despite the clinical relevance of these findings, intervention research in older adults remains limited. Available evidence suggests that psychological approaches may reduce gambling severity; however, most interventions have not been specifically developed for later life. Future interventions should consider age‐related clinical needs, including social isolation, emotional regulation difficulties, financial consequences, and the role of gambling as a coping behavior. Several limitations of the current evidence base should be considered. First, most included studies used cross‐sectional designs, limiting conclusions regarding causality. Second, the broad age category of 55 years and older may mask important differences between younger‐old and older‐old adults, whose social conditions, health status, and cognitive profiles may differ substantially. 26 , 45 Third, the available literature is concentrated in Western countries, limiting generalizability across cultural contexts in which gambling practices and social meanings may differ. In conclusion, this systematic review indicates that Gambling Disorder in older adults is an understudied but clinically meaningful condition. The available evidence suggests that late‐life gambling problems emerge from the interaction between individual vulnerabilities, psychosocial circumstances, and biological factors rather than from aging alone. Clinical approaches should therefore balance recognition of gambling's potential social and recreational functions with careful assessment of situations in which gambling becomes a maladaptive coping strategy. Future research should prioritize longitudinal designs, age‐specific assessment tools, and targeted interventions to improve detection and treatment of Gambling Disorder in later life.

SCIENTIFIC SIGNIFICANCE

Gambling‐related problems, ranging from at‐risk patterns to diagnosable Gambling Disorder, represent an emerging but still underrecognized concern in older adults. This review highlights key risk factors including social isolation, financial hardship, and emotional dysregulation while emphasizing that these factors should be interpreted within a broader framework of individual vulnerability and age‐related transitions rather than as exclusive characteristics of later life. Although gambling has been more extensively studied in younger populations, evidence regarding older adults remains limited, particularly concerning clinical trajectories, age‐specific assessment, and targeted interventions. Despite preliminary indications that psychological interventions may hold promise, the current evidence base in older adults remains extremely limited and predominantly observational. There is a pressing need for rigorously designed intervention studies specifically targeting this age group. In clinical practice, systematic assessment of gambling‐related problems should be integrated into geriatric assessments, particularly among older adults presenting with loneliness, significant life transitions, financial vulnerability, or psychiatric symptoms. Rather than relying mechanically on standardized tools, clinicians should also consider a functional evaluation of the role that gambling plays in the patient's life, distinguishing, for instance, between a form of socialization and a compulsive escape strategy, since this distinction has direct implications for intervention planning. This approach is particularly important in older adults, in whom gambling may range from a socially embedded leisure activity to a maladaptive coping mechanism associated with significant impairment. Psychological interventions, even when adapted from protocols originally developed for younger adults, appear to hold promise and should be offered wherever available. However, future interventions should incorporate age‐specific targets, including loneliness, emotional regulation, cognitive distortions, and the consequences of retirement or social role changes. From a research perspective, the field urgently requires longitudinal and experimental designs, the development and validation of age‐specific screening instruments, and the systematic inclusion of participants from non‐Western and low‐income contexts, so as to overcome the geographic bias documented in the current literature. Future studies should also adopt age‐stratified approaches, distinguishing “young‐old” from “older‐old” adults, whose risk profiles and clinical presentations may differ substantially. Furthermore, future research should systematically consider biological and medical contributors to gambling vulnerability in later life, including neurological conditions and exposure to medications associated with impulse control disorders, such as dopamine agonists. Finally, at the policy level, gambling regulations should explicitly account for the specific vulnerabilities of older populations while social inclusion programs should be promoted as a structural protective factor. Investment in geriatric mental health infrastructure and in the training of healthcare providers to recognize and address Gambling Disorder in older adults is strongly warranted, particularly given the current underrecognition of this condition in clinical settings.
