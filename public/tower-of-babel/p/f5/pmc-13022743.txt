Expanding access to low‐barrier opioid use disorder treatment in non‐traditional settings: Washington's opioid treatment network
Authors: Shay M Daily, Sharon Reif, Elizabeth Speaker, Lee Panas, Grant T Ritter, Maureen T Stewart
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeIncreasing utilization of medications that treat opioid use disorders (MOUD) remains an essential strategy to curb the opioid crisis nationwide, especially among rural areas where access can present challenges. Washington State expanded access to MOUD through its opioid treatment networks (OTN), which provide low‐barrier access to MOUD in non‐traditional settings with an emphasis on buprenorphine and rural locations. We examined changes in buprenorphine utilization between Medicaid beneficiaries who initiated treatment at OTNs compared to individuals outside OTN facilities and by rural–urban residence.MethodsWe used comparative time‐series analyses to examine longitudinal patterns of buprenorphine utilization among a sample (n = 93,401) of age 18 to 64 Washington State Medicaid beneficiaries diagnosed with OUD between 2019 and 2022. Monthly aggregated rates of buprenorphine utilization, stratified by OTN versus non‐OTN and rural–urban residence, were calculated for analysis.FindingsBetween 2019 and 2022, rates of buprenorphine utilization were significantly higher among Medicaid beneficiaries initiating treatment at OTNs compared to beneficiaries treated at non‐OTN facilities regardless of rural–urban status. Furthermore, utilization was most prevalent among rural OTNs (β = 25.5, SE = 1.91, p < 0.001, 95% CI 21.7, 29.2). Despite increases in OUD prevalence, buprenorphine rates remained consistent during the study period, except for a decrease within micropolitan OTNs.ConclusionsWashington's OTN provides an example of one strategy that may support greater prescribing of buprenorphine and help mitigate the opioid crisis in small town/rural areas. Except among micropolitan areas, OTNs were able to keep pace in buprenorphine utilization despite demand from increased OUD prevalence.Keywords: buprenorphine use, buprenorphine utilization, Medicaid, MOUD, OUD

INTRODUCTION

Adverse opioid‐related problems continue to be a leading cause of preventable death and injury in the United States. 1 Despite a 4% decrease from 2022 to 2023, an estimated 105,000 people died of a drug overdose in 2023, with approximately 75% of those deaths related to opioid use. 2 , 3 Three FDA‐approved medications are considered the standard of care for treating opioid use disorder (MOUD): buprenorphine, methadone, and naltrexone. Research has shown buprenorphine and methadone reduce opioid use and are associated with lower risk of fatal overdose. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 Despite their established effectiveness, most people with a diagnosed opioid use disorder (OUD) do not receive treatment. Estimates range from 11% among the privately insured 9 to 19% among nationally representative samples, 10 and 25% among Medicaid beneficiaries. 11 Although the DEA waiver requirement for providers to prescribe buprenorphine was withdrawn in 2023, 12 several challenges including stigma, accessibility, and low rates of primary care integration remain widespread. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16

Opioid‐related research on overdose disparities between rural–urban residences remains relatively inconclusive. 17 Evidence suggests that small town/rural counties tend to exhibit lower rates of opioid overdose deaths than their metropolitan counterparts. 18 Yet, rates alone provide only a broad overview of the potential challenges individuals in small town/rural areas must navigate. 18 , 19 For example, populations in small town/rural areas tend to struggle with access to MOUD because of considerable barriers such as distance to treatment with some traveling hours to receive care. 20 Small town/rural populations tend to also have fewer medical providers available with an estimated 57% of rural counties without a buprenorphine prescriber. 11 , 21 Availability of different types of MOUD has been outlined as a notable disparity since under current federal regulations ongoing methadone treatment for OUD is only available from federally certified opioid treatment programs (OTPs). 22 As of 2022, there were approximately 2120 OTPs in the United States 23 yet an estimated 80% of counties and the entire state of Wyoming have no OTPs. 24 For these reasons, evidence supporting low‐barrier strategies to improve OUD treatment utilization, especially for more flexible options such as buprenorphine are needed.

Similar to the nation, Washington State has seen exponential increases in opioid‐related morbidity and mortality. 25 From 2020 to 2022, Washington reported nearly 7000 drug overdose deaths with an estimated 73% related to opioids. 26 In same the period, Washington experienced an estimated 15,000 drug‐related hospitalizations (17 per 100,000) with opioids responsible for the bulk of these occurrences. 27 However, the burden of opioid‐related issues is not equitably distributed with Washington's small town/rural areas (40 per 100,000) demonstrating a clear disparity compared to urban settings (21 per 100,000). 26 Furthermore, medical providers that serve rural communities must navigate the challenges presented by OUDs differently than their urban comparators. A 2020 study by Kriegel et al. 28 reported that rural Washington‐based providers received less opioid treatment training, had access to fewer ways to address co‐occurring disorders, and had fewer supports available for recovery resources. Barriers to treatment such as long driving time or distance are also seminal challenges in Washington with rural residences having an estimated 7–11 times more difficulty accessing MOUD services. 29 Despite the challenges, Washington has begun to accrue evidence on ways to improve access to MOUD, especially buprenorphine, with an emphasis on partnerships between rural and urban communities. 28 , 30

In 2018, as part of its State Opioid Response (SOR, formerly State Targeted Response), Washington implemented a program of low‐barrier opioid treatment networks (OTNs) with a particular focus on more rural and frontier regions such as Grays Harbor on the western coast, Okanogan county along the northern border, and Spokane county on the eastern border. 31 Washington's OTNs, like its earlier Hub and Spoke (HS) model, 30 were based on innovative programs (see Vermont hub and spoke model 32 , 33 , 34 , 35 and the Massachusetts Collaborative Care model 36 , 37 , 38 ) designed to increase access to MOUD in primary and specialty care settings. Washington's OTNs were also positioned to provide low‐barrier access to MOUD in non‐traditional settings with an emphasis on prescribing buprenorphine. These low‐barrier and non‐traditional settings include facilities such as walk‐in clinics, syringe service programs, emergency departments, jails, and other community assets (e.g., fire departments). 39 Washington initially selected 17 OTN sites that included eight medical facilities, four community facilities, and five jails as induction sites. OTNs are staffed with a medical provider, nurse care manager, and care navigator with a primary goal of helping individuals with their initial induction and referral to a local MOUD provider to promote treatment maintenance and maximized utilization. Several sites were positioned to support small town/rural areas with an emphasis on Washington's larger eastern counties where coordinated systems of care are sparser.

Washington State has improved utilization for all types of MOUD by an estimated 193% from 2010 to 2019 compared to a 106% increase nationally. 40 According to a 2025 report by the Medicaid and CHIP Payment and Access Commission (MACPAC), 41 an estimated 70%–79% of Washington's Medicaid beneficiaries in with an OUD receiving any form of MOUD treatment in 2022. Washington's progress may be due, in part, to the impacts from public investments like SOR and STR, which funded Washington's hub and spoke (HS) model beginning in 2017. From 2016 to 2019, Washington Medicaid beneficiaries treated in HS facilities demonstrated higher rates of buprenorphine utilization when compared to a non‐HS cohort. 42 Furthermore, Washington's HS model has provided some evidence as a strategy that may reduce racial and ethnic disparities by improving access to MOUD, especially for buprenorphine. 43 The same patterns may therefore be attributable to OTNs, which are, by design, a tailored extension of the HS model. Because OTNs emphasize improving buprenorphine access for small town/rural populations and for non‐treatment seeking individuals, analyses of changes in MOUD utilization by OTN participation and rural–urban residence may identify further insights and opportunities to improve access to low‐barrier MOUD.

This study sought to examine the overall longitudinal patterns of monthly buprenorphine utilization in small town/rural areas compared to micropolitan and metropolitan counterparts among Washington Medicaid beneficiaries with OUD receiving buprenorphine as part of the OTN (excluding jail induction sites) versus individuals outside this program. We hypothesized that during the study period, (1) buprenorphine utilization would be higher among OTN Medicaid beneficiaries located in small town/rural areas compared to their micropolitan and metropolitan counterparts (within OTN) and (2) buprenorphine utilization rates would be higher among OTN Medicaid beneficiaries compared to individuals outside of the OTN program.

METHODS

Data source and study population

We used Medicaid health care claims provided by the Washington State Health Care Authority, which included clinical diagnostic, procedural, and pharmacy data from 2019 to 2022. 44 The analytic sample of buprenorphine rates was based on Washington State Medicaid beneficiaries with an OUD diagnosis (n = 151,972) who were aged 18–64 years (n = 145,901), eligible for Medicaid in available data years (n = 132,209), not eligible for Medicare or Children's Health Insurance (n = 131,051), continuously enrolled for at least 6 months after diagnosis (n = 127,974), had no missing gender or urbanicity information (n = 127,640), initiated buprenorphine treatment between July 01, 2019 and June 30, 2022 (n = 108,900), and were not part of Washington's HS model (n = 93,401; see Table S1 for a detailed flowchart). We excluded Medicaid beneficiaries who were treated in the HS model to reduce the likelihood of contamination and focus exclusively on OTNs as alternative low‐barrier access locations to OUD treatment. We additionally reviewed potential impacts of COVID‐19 as Washington followed federal permission to prescribe buprenorphine through telemedicine. 45 Because we did not discriminate between in‐person and telemedicine visits, we found limited evidence of any dramatic shifts in the occurrence of OUD diagnosis or rates of buprenorphine utilization. Our study was approved by Brandeis University Institutional Review Board (IRB) and the Washington Department of Social and Health Services IRB.

Research design

We defined two cohorts based on where buprenorphine was initiated: (1) non‐jail OTN facility or (2) facility outside the OTN model (i.e., comparisons). Jail OTNs were excluded because: (1) access and initiation to MOUD may be different among an incarcerated institutionalized population and (2) possible data integrity issues, 46 based on recommendations from our Washington partner. The OTN cohort was identified using a directory provided by Washington State as a reporting requirement of the participating facilities. These data were linked with a unique research identifier to Medicaid claims data. The comparison cohort consisted of Medicaid beneficiaries with OUD who were not linked to the OTN program during the study period. Each cohort was additionally stratified by rural–urban residence, which was defined using the US Department of Agriculture's Rural–Urban Continuum Area codes (RUCA). 47 We collapsed the ten RUCA levels into three categories: metropolitan (codes 1–3; population > 49,000), micropolitan (codes 4–6; population 10,000–49,000), and small town/rural (codes 7–10; population < 10,000).

We used a quasi‐experimental comparative time‐series design to investigate buprenorphine utilization using the Medicaid claims data. Buprenorphine utilization was identified using National Drug Codes and Health Care Common Procedural codes (Table S3) within each month in which the beneficiaries had at least one buprenorphine claim. Within our identified sample, we aggregated monthly frequencies of people with an OUD diagnosis in the previous 6 months including current month (denominator) and overall utilization of buprenorphine per month (numerator). Then for each month, we calculated monthly utilization rates (per 100) for each cohort (OTN vs. non‐OTN) and by rural–urban residence. This enabled an analysis of medication use among those with clinically identified OUD who are likely to benefit from buprenorphine initiation. Three‐month rolling averages were then calculated and analyzed to smooth rates of buprenorphine utilization. Using our aggregated sample, we analyzed monthly rates of buprenorphine utilization across approximately 216 periods stratified by cohort and rural–urban residence (i.e., 36 months of data across 6 cohorts).

Statistical approach

Bivariate analyses were used to describe sample characteristics and buprenorphine use rates in the aggregated sample by cohort and rural–urban residence. Comparative time‐series models were used to explore changes in buprenorphine use over time, overall, and stratified by OTN or rural–urban residence status. Independent variables in these models included a sequential time measure identifying the observation's month (e.g., 1, 2, … 36) to estimate the overall time trend, a cohort measure to demonstrate the initial effect between cohorts (e.g., OTN small town/rural vs. OTN metropolitan), and a set of time × cohort measures, in which the reference cohort group was either OTN or metropolitan (or both) where the measure was coded as zero for all months for this cohort. These models assessed the longitudinal patterns of 1) buprenorphine use by time, reflecting the possible changes in treatment use during the analytic period, 2) the initial (one‐time) difference in buprenorphine use between the “intervention” and comparison cohorts, and 3) the difference in slope of buprenorphine use for the “intervention” group over time. The results are reported as standardized beta coefficients with standard errors and 95% confidence limits. For every unit change in an indicator (e.g., time), the beta coefficient was used to describe the respective change in buprenorphine use as a proportion of the variable's standard deviation. All analyses were completed by using SAS© 9.4. 48

RESULTS

For context, characteristics from the non‐aggregated sample (n = 93,401) suggest 47% of Medicaid beneficiaries were female, and the majority were less than 40 years of age (63%), White non‐Hispanic (70%), and resided in a metropolitan setting (86%). See Table S2 in the supplemental materials for additional details.

From July 2019 to June 2022, the 3‐month rolling average for the number of Medicaid beneficiaries with an OUD diagnosis at OTNs per month increased from 1409 to 1838 (30% increase) among metropolitan residents, 231 to 274 (19% increase) for micropolitan residents, and 162 to 258 (59%) increase for small town/rural residents. Comparatively, Medicaid beneficiaries not treated in OTNs saw more limited growth in OUD diagnoses across the study period with marginal growth among metropolitan residences (24,437 to 25,355, 3.8% increase) compared to micropolitan (2353 to 2525, 7.3% increase) and small town/rural (1260 to 1380, 9.5% increase) residences (Table 1). Rates of buprenorphine utilization were consistently higher among Medicaid beneficiaries in OTNs compared with those in the comparison cohort: 61% vs. 35% in small town/rural areas, 53% vs. 31% in micropolitan areas, and 45% vs. 28% in metropolitan areas. Across the study period, rates of buprenorphine use among Medicaid beneficiaries in OTNs ranged from 60.4% to 61.4% (1% increase) for small town/rural 58.8% to 48.1% (10.7% decrease) for micropolitan, and 45.5% to 44.1% (1.4% decrease) for metropolitan residences. In contrast, Medicaid beneficiaries treated outside OTNs showed increases among metropolitan (3%), micropolitan (4%), and small town/rural (2.2%) residences (Table 1).

Within the OTN cohort, individuals in small town/rural (β = 11.14, SE = 2.52, p < 0.001) and micropolitan (β = 9.58, SE = 1.94, p < 0.001) areas showed higher rates of buprenorphine utilization than metropolitan areas at the start of the study period. Furthermore, comparing small town/rural versus micropolitan areas showed each cohort began with similar rates of buprenorphine utilization (β = 0.34, SE = 2.25, p = 0.879). Among OTN individuals in small town/rural areas, buprenorphine rates increased over the study period when compared to micropolitan (β = 0.27, SE = 0.12, p = 0.033) and metropolitan (β = 0.32, SE = 0.11, p < 0.004) areas. This contrasted with micropolitan OTN individuals, which showed significant declines in buprenorphine utilization over the study period (Table 2 and Figure 1).

Time‐series analyses between OTN and non‐OTN cohorts by rural–urban residence suggest that rates of buprenorphine utilization were significantly higher among individuals treated in OTNs (β range 18.55 to 28.47, SE range 1.68 to 3.01, p < 0.001) at the start of the study period. Changes in buprenorphine utilization across the study period, however, remained consistent (i.e., flat) regardless of OTN or rural–urban residence. One exception was among individuals treated in OTNs in micropolitan areas, which indicated a significant decline in buprenorphine utilization over time (β range –0.31 to –0.29, SE range 0.08 to 0.09, p < 0.05).

DISCUSSION

Our study sought to investigate Medicaid beneficiaries’ rates of buprenorphine utilization within Washington State's opioid treatment networks and compare them to people with OUD who were outside of this program. Overall, our results suggest rates of buprenorphine were highest among small town/rural Medicaid beneficiaries within OTNs when compared to their metropolitan or micropolitan counterparts. Additionally, this pattern appeared to hold when beneficiaries at OTNs were compared to Medicaid beneficiaries outside of the program. Studies suggest access to OUD treatment is a seminal challenge for small town/rural areas, 49 , 50 , 51 thus low‐barrier interventions may be ideal. Mitchell and colleagues 52 performed a spatial analysis across all US census tracts to assess OUD treatment utilization. Their findings reveal the many “treatment deserts” across the nation and highlighted the extensive issue in small town/rural areas. Furthermore, their study provides detailed maps of the scarcity of OUD treatment by access to opioid treatment programs (OTPs) and buprenorphine. 52 Geographically, much of Washington qualifies as a treatment desert with much of its small town/rural and frontier areas providing limited to no access to OTPs. On the other hand, Washington's access to buprenorphine among small town/rural areas in their study is observably robust and supports the findings of our study. This is notable because a central goal of the OTNs was not only to improve access with a low‐barrier approach and expand access to treatment in non‐traditional treatment settings, but to also localize treatment in the community. This may be especially important to small town/rural communities where access to resources is often distal and requires travel to reach them. Hence, the OTN program's emphasis on buprenorphine may overcome the barriers related to OTPs and methadone administration.

Our findings are an example of a single low‐barrier OUD treatment program that was meant to bolster Washington's established hub and spoke model, which also improved access to MOUD. 30 , 39 , 53 , 54 According to a 2023 report by the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA), 55 22 states have used their SOR funds to implement hub and spoke like models with few described in the literature. Of those that are, the Vermont hub and spoke model identified increased use of OUD treatment following implementation. 32 , 34 California's hub and spoke also showed associations to OUD treatment uptake. 56 Furthermore, California identified barriers to support hub and spoke implementation, which included provider attitudes, stigma, and lack of training. 56 Our results build on previous studies about OUD treatment utilization as a result of initiatives to expand access and follow population patterns after Medicaid expansion. 57 Similarly, our rates were consistent with those observed by Lindner et al., 58 who reported 58% of Medicaid beneficiaries diagnosed with an OUD in Washington received MOUD, which was lower than MOUD utilization reported by MACPAC. 41

Although our findings are encouraging, some nuances to these patterns must also be acknowledged. First, prevalence of OUD diagnoses among Medicaid beneficiaries increased during study period, which was similar to national trends. 11 Increases in OUD prevalence were found to be most substantial among small town/rural areas with a 59% increase among OTNs compared to a 10% increase among non‐OTN. In addition, for every 100 Medicaid beneficiaries in small town/rural areas diagnosed with an OUD, an average of 61 received buprenorphine when accessing treatment among OTNs compared to 35 individuals outside the program. Furthermore, rates in metropolitan areas were lower with about 44 per 100 Medicaid beneficiaries with OUD in OTNs and 28 per 100 for beneficiaries outside the program. Given that the number of Medicaid beneficiaries being served in metropolitan areas is larger, our findings provide some evidence that OTNs supported higher rates of buprenorphine utilization with the greatest impact among small town/rural communities. Although relative to the need, rates of buprenorphine utilization over time remained relatively unchanged among OTN Medicaid beneficiaries except for micropolitan areas which showed a significant decline. Reasons for the decline among micropolitan areas are difficult to discern but may be influenced by improved availability of MOUD in adjoined urban areas. 59 Although continued improvement is desired, having consistent rates of buprenorphine utilization is important because as OUD prevalence grows, those increases are met with a readiness to provide access to MOUD.

Despite the higher rates among OTNs, underutilization of buprenorphine remains an issue. 60 Although shown to improve access to treatment and reduce mortality, research on low‐barrier MOUD models like OTNs or hub and spokes remains relatively scarce. A vital aspect to these public investments is the funding mechanisms that support facilities and program personnel. For example, Washington used a portion of its SAMHSA SOR grant funds to establish the OTNs and increase access to MOUD with a focus on underserved small town/rural communities. 61 Because of public investments like SOR, evidence has started to emerge that provide results on data driven approaches to address opioid‐related issues. 55 , 62 Our study adds to this literature and expands our understanding for approaches that may improve buprenorphine utilization among small town/rural populations. Reasons as to why OTNs demonstrated higher rates of buprenorphine utilization in small town/rural areas require further exploration. However, among small town/rural areas, factors such as social determinants of health like having enough food, a safe place to live, and a ride to the clinic have been outlined as essential facilitators to starting and staying in treatment. 21 Other factors such as social supports or having a choice when selecting which type of MOUD treatment may also influence initiating care. 21 Although the why is likely multifaceted, there is a clear socioecological paradigm (i.e., culture) that contributes to the behaviors exhibited among small town/rural populations. 63 Investigations that examine the usefulness of programs like Washington's OTN to address OUD‐related outcomes like treatment continuation are needed to help expand our understanding beyond simply improvements to access. This idea leans into the continued need for an appropriate political and financial response to the opioid crisis. Programs that are tailored to serve small town/rural communities, like the OTNs’ low‐barrier access to MOUD, could serve as a worthwhile strategy and public investment in the cadre of evidence‐based practice.

Limitations

OTNs were selected through a competitive application process with the state as part of expanding the hub and spoke network. These facilities may have already possessed a readiness and capacity to improve buprenorphine uptake. Therefore, findings may not be generalizable to other programs. Our analysis focused on Medicaid beneficiaries and may not be representative of people treated elsewhere, especially outside of public health care insurance systems. Addressing opioid related issues was a primary focus in Washington State, moreover the nation, during the implementation of OTNs. Therefore, we are unable to disentangle any potential exogenous factors (e.g., policy changes) that may have occurred during the study period. However, the likelihood that specific changes to policy meant only for the OTN cohort were likely low. Thus, the comparative time‐series design provided a robust framework for our analytic approach. We used Medicaid claims data, which may underestimate the number of people with OUD and do not include information on services not billed to insurance. Further, our approach to estimating OUD prevalence in the claims data was conservative and may underestimate the true prevalence compared to other approaches. However, our approach provided consistent prevalence estimates for the study period, which supported reliable trend estimation. Lastly, we did not differentiate between in‐person and telehealth visits, which may demonstrate varied patterns of buprenorphine utilization by rural and urban location, furthering valuable policy insights among small town/rural areas.

CONCLUSION

Low‐barrier systems of care are needed to improve access to MOUD, especially in small town/rural communities. With an emphasis on buprenorphine, Washington's OTN provides an example of one strategy that may facilitate and expand MOUD utilization. We found that among small town/rural areas, OTNs were associated with higher rates of buprenorphine utilization, which provides evidence for Washington's intended goal. Further research is needed to understand the potential usefulness of these strategies to support long‐term MOUD continuation or assess differences in Medicaid beneficiaries’ characteristics. Our findings provide support for importance and justification for federal and state efforts to facilitate expanding access to MOUD.
