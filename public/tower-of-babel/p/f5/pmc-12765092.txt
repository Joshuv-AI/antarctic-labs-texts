Cognition is associated with daily-life mobility in people with Parkinson’s disease
Authors: Pablo I. Burgos, Carla Silva-Batista, Anjanibhargavi Ragothaman, Vrutangkumar V. Shah, Patricia Carlson-Kuhta, Fay B. Horak, Martina Mancini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•People with PD with poor visuospatial abilities walked slower and with shorter strides.•People with PD with poor executive function had longer double support time.•Poor executive function was also associated with reduced variability in turning performance.•Disease duration and age had a stronger influence on the relationship between cognition and mobility than FoG status.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment six times more common in individuals with Parkinson’s disease (PD) than in healthy, age-matched individuals [1]. Multiple cognitive domains can be impacted, including memory, attention, visuospatial abilities, and executive functions, such as mental flexibility, set-switching, planning, and solving problems [2]. Early cognitive changes usually involve subtle executive and visuospatial impairments [3].

The ability to safely ambulate in daily life is essential to maintain independence and a good quality of life. However, gait and turning impairments are common in people with PD, even in early stages, increase as the disease progresses, and often disrupt daily life activities. The most reported gait and turning impairments in PD are slower gait speed, shorter step length, reduced trunk motion and arm motion while walking, increased turning duration, and decreased stability of gait, such as longer double-support time [4]. Levodopa can improve many gait features such as velocity and step length; however, other characteristics such as variability and stability aspects of gait may not change or worsen with levodopa [5], and episodic gait disorders, such as Freezing of Gait (FoG) can occur, even when individuals are taking levodopa [6].

FoG is an episodic symptom described as the feeling of “the feet getting glued to the floor” while walking, turning, or initiating walking [6]. People with PD and FoG (FoG + ) often present with worse cognitive function, including impaired executive function, global cognition, attention, language, memory, and visuospatial domains, than those without FoG (FoG-) [7]. It is unclear if worse cognitive function in people with PD and FOG + is due to involvement brain areas that are related to both cognitive function and or to severity of disease. People with PD and FoG + also have worse gait when not freezing (e.g., higher gait variability) in the home and community settings than FoG- [8]. However, the relationships between cognitive impairments and quality of real-world mobility (gait and turning) and the influence of FoG status have not been investigated.

In the laboratory, dual-task gait paradigms have been used to explore the simultaneous use of the motor and cognitive systems during walking. However, a more ecological way to capture this relationship is to record mobility in real-life situations. Our previous studies report the feasibility of mobility data collection in daily life for many days with inertial sensors on the feet and low back and report specific gait and turning variables sensitive to people with PD [9]. Real-world mobility measures capture both the quantity and quality of gait and turning that are sensitive to disease status [10].

The previous literature organizes gait deficits in domains that include: pace (step time, stride length, step velocity, cadence), variability (step velocity variability), asymmetry (swing time asymmetry), and postural stability (double-support time) [11]. Several studies have reported the relationships between cognition and gait domains in PD but not using real-world gait [12,13]. For example, executive function has been related to spatiotemporal step features (pace), dual-task performance, gait variability, asymmetry, and postural control [14,15]. Visuospatial function has been related to postural stability [3,14], pace, and gait variability [16,17]. On the other hand, there are fewer studies linking turning variables with cognition. Executive functions, such as set-shifting and inhibitory control may be related to the quality and variability of turning features, particularly in FOG+, because turning safely involves complex neural control of balance and gait that require a tight coupling between gait and higher-order executive processes that are affected in FOG+ [18].

Here, we hypothesize that specific, real-world gait and turning parameters would be associated with specific cognitive domains of executive and visuospatial functions and that FoG would affect these associations. Thus, this study aimed to investigate, for the first time, the association between cognition (visuospatial and executive functions) and daily-life mobility in people with PD and the influence of FoG status.

Methodology

Participants

Sixty individuals with idiopathic PD participated in the study. The inclusion criteria were: a) diagnosis of idiopathic PD from a movement disorders specialist with the United Kingdom Brain Bank criteria of bradykinesia with one or more of the following − rest tremor, rigidity, bradykinesia, and balance problems not from visual, vestibular, cerebellar or proprioceptive conditions; b) good response to levodopa (self-perceived); c) Hoehn & Yahr stages II-IV; d) ages 55–80 years old; e) cognitive ability sufficient to participate in study procedures (Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) Scores greater than 21) [19], and f) free of any medical conditions or medication that contraindicates participation in moderate intensity exercise. Participants were screened and enrolled between November 2020 and December 2022 in the Balance Disorder Laboratory, OHSU, Portland, Oregon, USA. Participants were assessed in the ON medication state, about one hour after intake of antiparkinsonian medication.

Criteria from the New Freezing of Gait Questionnaire (NFOG-Q), particularly the question “Did you experience freezing in the last month?” was used to determine whether FoG influenced relationships between cognition and daily life gait. If the participant did not know what FoG is, the examiner showed a NFOG-Q video explaining it. Clinical variables

The Movement Disorders Society-Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale, Motor (MDS-UPDRS Part III), quality of life scale, Parkinson's Disease Questionnaire – 39 (PDQ 39) and the Activities-specific Balance Confidence (ABC) Scale were assessed during a laboratory visit. A certified examiner administered the MDS-UPDRS Part III and MOCA.

Cognitive variables (Digital assessment)

The Tablet-based Cognitive Assessment Tool (TabCAT, UCSF) was used for cognitive assessment [20]. Executive functions were assessed by means of the Flanker and Set-Shifting tests (See Fig. 1). The Flanker test assesses attention and inhibition, asking the participants to choose the direction of a center arrow, surrounded by 2 other arrows on each side (5 in total) [21]. Participants must press with their thumbs on the right or left side of the iPad touchscreen to answer. Some trials are congruent, which means that the direction of the center arrow and the sides arrows point in the same direction. The incongruent trials show different arrow directions for the center and side arrows. TabCAT uses a summary value integrating the accuracy and reaction times called, “Flanker Score”. Set-Shifting evaluates cognitive flexibility by asking participants to classify objects by their shape or color [22]. The participants see a colored shape in the center of the screen and the words “Shape” or “Color” indicate the rule, but after some trials, this rule changes. At the bottom of the screen, there is a colored shape on the left side and another one on the right side. The participants touch the left or the right shape consistent with the rule and the central shape. We used the TabCAT variable called “Set Shift Score” that considered both the reaction time and accuracy. Higher scores indicate better cognitive flexibility.

Visuospatial function was assessed by the Judgement of Line Orientation test [23]. Similar to Set- Shifting, there is a central stimulus and 2 bottom stimuli (Left and Right). However, in this case, the stimulus is a line oriented at different angles with respect to the horizontal. The participant chooses which of the bottom lines is parallel the central line. The variable used is a composite metric of TabCAT called “LO Threshold Score 3–10”, which reflects the error during trials 3 to 10. Bigger values represent worse visuospatial function.

Gait and turning in daily life

Subjects wore 3 synchronized, body-worn inertial sensors (Opals by APDM Wearable Technologies, Clario) with one sensor on the lumbar level and 2 sensors in instrumented socks positioned on the dorsum of the feet. Each sensor contained a tri-axial accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer sampling at 128 Hz. The participants were asked to wear the sensors during the day (at least 8 h) and to remove and charge the sensors each night for 7 days. The sensor battery life is 12 h. The minimum acceptable use for the analysis was 5 days and 5 h per day.

The gait and turning parameters estimation from daily life data have been published [10]. Briefly, the algorithms follow these steps: First, it identifies potential walking bouts using time-domain analysis of inertial sensor data from the feet and detects turns based on the yaw rotational movement of the pelvis. Second, individual steps are grouped into possible walking bouts, provided the interval between consecutive steps does not exceed 2.5 s. Third, walking bouts containing at least three steps and lasting a minimum of 3 s are analyzed using the commercial gait analysis algorithms available in Mobility Lab V2 (APDM) designed for prescribed gait tests. For this study, gait metrics were calculated as the mean and variability across all strides recorded over a week, focusing exclusively on straight walking while excluding walking during turns. Turning measures were derived using a previously published algorithm that identifies and characterizes each turn [10]. In brief, turns were defined as trunk rotations around the vertical axis at a minimum angular velocity of 40°/s, with turn initiation and termination set at a threshold of 15°/s. Only turns lasting between 0.5 and 10 s and with a minimum angle of 40° were included in the analysis.

We extracted 12 mobility measures related to gait, turning, and activity. We use the coefficient of variation (standard deviation divided by the mean, CV) to calculate the variability of each metric. Gait variables were: Gait Speed, Stride Length, Pitch at initial contact (dorsiflexion foot angle), Stance time as proportion of gait cycle, Double-support proportion, and Swing time duration. Turning variables were: Turn Angle (Angle during turns, mean, and CV) and Turn Duration per step average (step duration during turning, mean, and CV). Activity variables were the number of gait Bouts per day and the number of Turns per hour.

Statistical analysis

We used average and standard deviation as descriptive statistics for all variables. For the normality test, we used the Shapiro-Wilk test. The correlations between cognitive and daily-life mobility metrics (in all participants or by FOG status) used the Sheperd’s Pi correlation coefficient. The Shepherd’s Pi correlation is a robust method that returns the Spearman correlation coefficient after removing bivariate outliers, using a bootstrapping of the Mahalanobis distance. The multiple correlations were corrected using False Discovery Rate (FDR).

Comparisons between FoG + and FoG- groups were performed using a t-test or Wilcoxon depending on the normal distribution. Group comparisons using covariates (FoG status, age, disease duration, MDS-UPDRS, and MoCA) were analyzed with ANCOVA, using eta squared as effect size variables. An eta squared value equal to 0.01 indicates a small effect, 0.06 indicates a medium effect and 0.14 indicates a large effect size.

All the estimations were performed in Python 3 (Python Software Foundation. Beaverton, OR, version 3.9) using the libraries statsmodels and pingouin.

Results

Demographics and sensor wear time

The 60 participants were 33 males and 27 females, and they had a mean and standard deviation age of 68.7 ± 6 years, PD duration of 7.9 ± 6 years, MDS-UPDRS-III score of 31.9 ± 9, MoCA score of 26.5 ± 2, ABC-Scale score of 86.6 ± 12, and a PDQ39 score of 20.4 ± 14. When we classified the participants by FoG status based on the NFOG-Q, 17 were defined as FoG + and 43 as FoG-. Most of the participants were in Hoehn & Yahr (HY) stage of 2 (n = 56 for HY2, n = 4 for HY3, without participants in HY4).

Participants wore the motion sensors for a minimum of 5 days with an average of 7.01 h per day. The maximum wear time recorded was 12 days and 12 h, while the overall average across the cohort was 7.12 days with 8.8 h of wear per day.

Worse visuospatial function in PD was related to slower gait pace in daily life

Worse Line Orientation was significantly related to slower gait speed (r = -0.46, p = 0.008) and shorter stride length (r = -0.40, p = 0.022), see Fig. 2. Worse executive function in PD was related to dynamic postural stability in daily life

Worse Set-Shifting scores were significantly related to longer stance times (r = -0.35, p = 0.046), and longer double-support times (r = -0.35, p = 0.046). Also, Set-Shifting scores were significantly related to a smaller variability of step durations during turning (r = 0.44, p = 0.016). However, none of these correlations were significant when we separated the participants by the FoG status (Fig. 3). Flanker scores did not correlate with daily life gait measures.

People with PD experiencing FoG had a shorter step duration during turning than nonfreezers, without differences in cognitive function

Several gait and turning measures during daily life showed differences between FoG + and FoG-. Specifically, people with FoG + had shorter stride length (p = 0.009), less ankle dorsiflexion at foot contact (Pitch at Initial Contact, p = 0.005), shorter swing durations (p = 0.019), smaller turn angles (p = 0.028) and shorter step duration during turning (p = 0.001). People with FoG + also had longer disease duration (p = 0.0002), worse cognition (MoCA, p = 0.016), worse balance confidence (ABC, p = 0.001), and worse quality of life (PDQ39, p = 0.030) compared to FoG- (Table 1). However, when correcting the analyses for disease duration, the group differences in gait, turning, balance confidence, quality of life, as well as executive and visuospatial cognitive function were no longer significant except for step duration during turning (Table 1).

FoG status was less important than disease duration or age in the cognitive-mobility associations

The ANCOVA effect sizes (eta squared) of the clinical variables for the Line-Orientation associations with gait metrics (gait speed and stride length), from higher to lower values, were 0.25 for disease duration, 0.16 for MDS-UPDRS, 0.12 for age, 0.08 for FOG status and 0.06 for MoCA. The effect sizes for the Set-Shifting associations with gait metrics (stance proportion, double support proportion and variability of the step durations during turning) were 0.34 for age, 0.32 for MoCA, 0.30 for disease duration, 0.19 for FOG status and 0.19 for MDS-UPDRS.

Discussion

Our findings show that specific characteristics of gait and turning in daily-life are associated with visuospatial versus executive function in people with PD, with or without FoG. Specifically, people with PD who have worse visuospatial ability walk more slowly, with shorter stride length than those with better visuospatial ability. In contrast, those with worse cognitive flexibility (Set Shifting) had longer stance times, longer double-support times, and smaller variability of step duration during turning than those with better cognitive flexibility. Disease duration and age had more influence than FoG status in these associations between cognition and daily gait quality. Although the FoG + group had shorter step duration during turning than the FoG- group, both groups had similar cognitive function. Therefore, gait and turning are differently associated with executive and visuospatial functions, consistent with our hypothesis, but FoG status only affects associations for visuospatial functions, unlike what we had hypothesized. However, FoG status was less important than disease duration or age in associations between cognition and gait impairments.

People with PD with worse visuospatial functions had slower gait speed and shorter stride length during daily life. Both gait metrics are included in the pace domain. Visuospatial function deficits in people with PD have been previously shown to be related to worse pace, rhythm, variability and postural control for prescribed gait tasks [16,17,24]. People with PD rely more on visual feedback to control balance and locomotion than healthy subjects [16]. Here we show that visuospatial function is also important for daily-life mobility in people with PD. In contrast to visuospatial function, executive functions have been strongly related to the gait domains of variability, asymmetry, and postural control during prescribed gait tasks [14,15,25]. Previous studies showed that PD more likely affects executive control (set-shifting) than inhibition (Flanker) which is consistent with our results [26]. Impaired set-shifting is associated with previous falls in older adults with and without PD, which is consistent with our result of longer double-support proportion in those with worse set-shifting [3]. These results suggest the important contribution of prefrontal cortical networks for daily life walking.

Interestingly, we found a significant association between worse set-shifting and reduced variability of step duration during turning. Although we did not find previous studies about turning variability and cognition in PD, our previous data in healthy older adults showed that worse visuospatial function was related to reduced variability of turn duration [27]. In contrast to turning, gait variability reports show that worse cognition is associated with greater gait variability when the patients are assessed in a laboratory setting [17]. Our results showed that when navigating in the real world, people with PD had less turning variability related to worse cognitive flexibility. Our previous study showed that increased variability of steps during daily-life turning may predict prospective falls in the elderly [27]. In this sense, people with PD may prioritize slower, less variable, and cautious turning strategies to try and avoid falls during daily life. Thus, less variability in stepping during daily-life turning for people with PD may be either a compensatory behavior to avoid falls or a loss of sensorimotor skills necessary to adapt gait/balance control in the real world due to worse executive resources.

A previous study investigated he association of real-world gait with cognition but used gait metrics only, not turning or activity metrics [28]. Our study is the first that related digital measures of turning and activity metrics (as well as gait) during real-world mobility to cognitive domains. Unlike turning quality, we did not observe any significant relationships between cognitive abilities and activity metrics in daily life, suggesting that executive and visuospatial function does not affect how much people with PD walk in a day or turn in an hour in daily life.

Interestingly, only the quality, and not quantity (number of turns and gait bouts) of mobility at home differed between freezers and non-freezers [29]. Our real-world gait data showed that freezers, compared to non-freezers, have smaller and greater variability of ankle angle at initial heel contact, as well as smaller turning angles. FoG + also show shorter step durations during turning, which may be related to their smaller turning angles. People with FoG + likely freeze more during turning than during straight walking, so they may try to avoid large angle turns in daily life.

Previous studies have identified cognitive differences between FoG- and FoG + groups. Cognitive impairments in people with PD and FoG + include global cognition, frontal lobe function, executive function, attention, and working memory [30]. In addition, better global cognition and visuospatial function were associated with a lower risk for FoG [31]. However, larger studies suggest that cognitive impairments in people with FoG are present but disappear after covariable corrections, such as disease duration and severity [32]. Despite some previous studies relating executive and visuospatial functions to FoG [33], this evidence was criticized because of the small sample size, the lack of control by disease severity, and no long-term follow-up [32]. Other factors such as disease duration, age, anxiety, depression, learning, sleepiness, and severity of motor symptoms are associated with FoG, suggesting it is a multidimensional and complex clinical problem [34].

Limitations

Cognitive assessment was collected during a laboratory study visit in the ON state, whereas gait data was collected over 7 days of daily life also in ON periods but with the typical fluctuations associated with Levodopa medication. Future designs might also collect more cognitive assessments in the home.

The different designs of people’s homes might create different environmental challenges for the participants that we cannot control. Participants agreed to wear the three sensors throughout the day and charge them overnight. This level of compliance may introduce selection bias, as it potentially reflects a subgroup of individuals with PD who are more motivated or physically capable. However, our previous studies in individuals with PD have shown that wearing these sensors imposes minimal burden [10,35,36], supporting the feasibility of this approach in real-world monitoring..

The smaller number of participants with FoG + than FoG- may limit the power of group comparisons and should be interpreted with caution. Our cohort did not include individuals under 50 years old with early-onset PD or those over 80 years old, in order to reduce the confounding effects of age on gait and cognitive function. As a result, our findings may not generalize to these age groups. We also excluded individuals with severe cognitive or motor impairments, which limits the applicability of our results to more advanced stages of disease. While we used robust statistical methods to explore cognition and mobility associations, alternative modeling approaches, such as linear regression or machine learning, may help to further quantify the contribution of individual factors. Finally, the cross-sectional and observational nature of the study precludes causal inferences regarding the relationship between cognition and mobility.

Conclusion

Daily-life gait and turning metrics are differently associated with visuospatial and executive functions in people with PD. People with PD with worse visuospatial functions had worse gait pace while participants with worse executive function (set-switching) had worse dynamic postural control while walking and turning. FoG affects the association between cognitive function and gait, although FoG status was less important than disease duration or age in the associations between cognition and daily life mobility.
