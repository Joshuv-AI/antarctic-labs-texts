Background

Wilson disease (WD) is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by pathogenic variants in ATP7B, encoding a copper-transporting P-type ATPase essential for biliary copper excretion and systemic copper homeostasis. Copper accumulation may lead to hepatic, neurological, or mixed presentations, and diagnosis requires integration of phenotype, copper-related biomarkers, and supportive clinical/imaging findings [1–3].

With increasing access to clinical genetic testing, ATP7B variants may be identified incidentally in individuals lacking typical WD manifestations. Interpreting such findings without a standardized diagnostic framework can lead to overdiagnosis and unnecessary evaluations, particularly when key biochemical evidence is absent [4].

Here, we describe a child with paroxysmal movement attacks in whom an incidental heterozygous ATP7B truncating variant and a single low serum copper measurement prompted concern for WD, but subsequent evaluation supported a non-WD interpretation.

Case presentation

A 13-year-old Han Chinese boy presented with recurrent brief paroxysmal attacks for > 1 year, with increased frequency in the month prior to admission. Episodes were consistently triggered by standing up after sitting, featuring transient postural instability accompanied by unilateral head deviation and ipsilateral involuntary movements described as dystonic/choreiform. Each episode lasted approximately 10 s and resolved spontaneously. He remained fully conscious throughout, without urinary/fecal incontinence, convulsive activity, or postictal drowsiness. In the month before presentation, attacks increased to dozens per day, impairing school performance. Appetite, sleep, bowel and bladder habits were normal, with no weight loss.

Past medical history was unremarkable. Family history included consanguinity in grandparents and an uncle with epilepsy. Vital signs were stable (T 36.5 °C, P 86/min, R 19/min, BP 129/79 mmHg); weight was 53 kg. There was no jaundice, no hepatosplenomegaly, no abdominal pain, and no bleeding tendency. Interictal neurological examination was normal. Slit-lamp examination showed no Kayser–Fleischer ring.

To evaluate the paroxysmal dystonic and choreiform movements, our initial differential diagnosis primarily included epileptic seizures (particularly frontal lobe or supplementary motor area seizures) and structural basal ganglia lesions. Ambulatory EEG successfully captured three typical clinical attacks without any concurrent epileptiform discharges or ictal EEG changes, effectively ruling out an electroclinical seizure. Brain MRI was completely normal, excluding structural etiologies. Consequently, primary paroxysmal movement disorders, such as paroxysmal kinesigenic dyskinesia (PKD), were heavily suspected. For symptom control, oxcarbazepine 0.15 g twice daily reduced attack frequency. Pharmacogenetic testing showed HLA-B*15:02 heterozygosity, implying increased risk of severe cutaneous adverse reactions associated with carbamazepine/oxcarbazepine; therefore, on 28 November, therapy was switched to levetiracetam 0.5 g twice daily for risk management. To investigate the exact genetic etiology of PKD, this clinical suspicion directly prompted the use of an XY Paroxysmal Movement Disorders panel based on targeted capture and next-generation sequencing (NGS). Importantly, screening of the genes included in this panel that are associated with primary paroxysmal dyskinesias and other early-onset pediatric movement disorders, including PRRT2, PNKD, SLC2A1, KCNMA1, and ADCY5, yielded no pathogenic or likely pathogenic variants matching the patient’s phenotype and inheritance pattern. This panel incidentally identified a heterozygous ATP7B nonsense variant (NM_000053.4:c.2851 C > T; p.Gln951Ter). According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines, this variant is classified as likely pathogenic (PVS1_very strong: predicted to cause premature protein truncation; PM2_supporting: absent from the gnomAD database). For genetic context, common ATP7B variants reported in Han Chinese patients with Wilson disease include missense hotspot variants such as p.Arg778Leu and p.Pro273Leu. In contrast, the c.2851 C > T (p.Gln951Ter) variant detected in this patient is a rare nonsense variant predicted to cause premature protein truncation and was therefore classified as likely pathogenic. However, the clinical significance of this finding required interpretation in the context of zygosity, inheritance pattern, and copper-metabolism evidence rather than by genotype alone. Subsequent targeted Sanger sequencing of both asymptomatic parents revealed that the father is a heterozygous carrier of the same variant, while the mother does not carry the mutation, confirming paternal inheritance and ruling out a de novo mutation.

Prompted strictly by this incidental genetic finding, a WD-focused work-up was initiated. A biochemical evaluation revealed low serum copper (9.43 µmol/L; reference 10.50–29.90, flame atomic absorption). However, normal ceruloplasmin (231.6 mg/L; reference 200.0–420.0 mg/L, immunoturbidimetry) and normal ceruloplasmin oxidase activity (0.372 OD; reference > 0.200 OD, p-phenylenediamine method) were observed. Liver biochemistry was normal (ALT 11 U/L, AST 16 U/L, direct bilirubin 4.5 µmol/L, indirect bilirubin 6.3 µmol/L, GGT 14 U/L). Abdominal ultrasound demonstrated a normal liver (normal size and contour, homogeneous echotexture, normal intrahepatic vasculature), no biliary dilatation (CBD ~ 4 mm), and an incidental gallbladder polypoid lesion (~ 4 × 3 mm). Crucially, a follow-up quantitative 24-hour urinary copper test yielded a result of 72.06 µg/24 h (reference range: <100.00 µg/24 h), with a recorded 24-hour urine volume of 1.40 L; the urinary copper excretion was within normal limits. At telephone follow-up 2 months later, attacks decreased to 0–1 per day, markedly improved from baseline.

Discussion and conclusions

Despite detection of a heterozygous truncating ATP7B variant and a single low serum copper measurement, this patient lacked supportive evidence for WD: ceruloplasmin oxidase activity, normal liver enzymes and bilirubin, no Kayser–Fleischer ring, and normal liver ultrasound findings [1, 2]. Ambulatory EEG recorded three habitual events without ictal EEG correlates, which reduced the likelihood of epileptic seizures and supported a paroxysmal movement disorder phenotype. By formally applying the Leipzig scoring system, the patient obtained a total score of 2: Kayser-Fleischer ring (0), neurological features (1 point for paroxysmal movement disorder), serum ceruloplasmin (0), quantitative 24-hour urinary copper (0), and ATP7B variant detection (1 point for a single heterozygous mutation). Using the Leipzig/Ferenci scoring system, the patient’s total score was 2, which is below the diagnostic threshold for Wilson disease and is not supportive of active Wilson disease in the context of normal ceruloplasmin, normal 24-hour urinary copper excretion, absence of Kayser–Fleischer rings, and normal hepatic evaluation [3]. Consequently, given the normal non-invasive biochemical profile, normal hepatic imaging, absence of Kayser–Fleischer rings, and low Leipzig score, an invasive liver biopsy for hepatic copper quantification was clinically unindicated in this pediatric patient.

Crucially, parental segregation analysis confirmed the paternal inheritance of the ATP7B variant. Given that WD strictly adheres to an autosomal recessive inheritance pattern requiring biallelic mutations for clinical expression, the presence of a single heterozygous variant—shared by a healthy, asymptomatic parent—supports a paternally inherited heterozygous state rather than active disease. Previous studies further support the interpretation that a single heterozygous ATP7B variant is generally insufficient to cause classical Wilson disease. Gromadzka et al. [5] reported that middle-aged heterozygous carriers of Wilson disease did not show significant phenotypic deviations related to copper metabolism. Similarly, population-based sequencing studies have demonstrated that heterozygous ATP7B carrier frequencies are substantially higher than the clinically observed prevalence of Wilson disease, emphasizing that isolated heterozygosity should not be equated with active disease. Therefore, in the absence of biallelic pathogenic variants and supportive biochemical evidence, the paternally inherited heterozygous ATP7B variant in our patient is best interpreted as a carrier finding rather than the cause of classical Wilson disease. This inherited heterozygous state is consistent with the patient’s normal ceruloplasmin and 24-hour urinary copper levels, further highlighting the danger of diagnostic anchoring when interpreting incidental genetic findings. Genetic information must be integrated with a standardized diagnostic pathway to mitigate anchoring bias—a well-documented cognitive heuristic in medical decision-making where clinicians rely disproportionately on the initial piece of information, such as an incidental ‘pathogenic’ genetic report [6]. The risk of genomic overdiagnosis is particularly high for WD. Recent population studies demonstrate that the heterozygous carrier frequency of ATP7B variants can be exceptionally high (e.g., approximately 1 in 19 in some cohorts) [7]. Consequently, incidental findings are statistically probable and must not override biochemical gold standards. Consistent with previous reports by Antos et al. [8] and Stattermayer et al. [4]. (summarized in Table 2), decoupling genetic results from the clinical phenotype frequently leads to severe diagnostic dilemmas. The increasing accessibility of NGS has inadvertently amplified the risk of diagnostic anchoring bias—a cognitive heuristic where clinicians rely too heavily on the first prominent piece of information acquired. In our patient, the incidental ATP7B nonsense variant served as a powerful diagnostic anchor that initially overshadowed the typical clinical phenotype of PKD and robust contradictory biochemical evidence. To mitigate this bias, genetic findings must remain strictly subordinate to a rigorous, phenotype-driven diagnostic framework.

This case also underscores the limited specificity of total serum copper as a standalone diagnostic marker. Total serum copper is strongly influenced by ceruloplasmin and can be low in non-WD contexts. When ceruloplasmin is normal and urinary copper is not elevated, a single low serum copper level should not drive a diagnosis of WD [1, 9]. Clinically, he had no liver-related symptoms or signs, including jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, abdominal pain, or bleeding tendency, further lowering the likelihood of WD.

The patient’s neurological presentation—brief, strictly kinesigenic attacks with preserved awareness, completely normal interictal examinations, and a dramatic initial therapeutic response to low-dose sodium channel blockers (oxcarbazepine)—was highly characteristic of PKD. Importantly, focal aware seizures—particularly frontal lobe seizures—were meticulously excluded. While frontal lobe seizures can present with preserved consciousness, hyperkinetic movements, and even normal ictal scalp EEGs due to deep cortical generators, they frequently occur during sleep or lack specific triggers; conversely, our patient’s attacks were strictly kinesigenic and exquisitely responsive to a very low dose of oxcarbazepine, definitively fulfilling the clinical diagnostic criteria for PKD. Although WD can present with a wide spectrum of movement disorders (e.g., tremor, dystonia, chorea), these are typically progressive, persistent, and accompanied by other systemic signs, rather than being strictly paroxysmal. The diagnostic workup for WD in this case was not driven by the clinical phenotype itself; rather, it was inappropriately triggered by the incidental ATP7B finding on the movement disorder panel and a single non-specific low serum copper level. Our report intentionally focuses on this genetic anchoring pitfall. Furthermore, the short-term clinical improvement following targeted therapy further supported the primary paroxysmal movement disorder diagnosis, although longer follow-up is needed to confirm sustained treatment response [10]. Pharmacogenetic information (HLA-B*15:02) appropriately informed risk management for oxcarbazepine/carbamazepine-associated severe cutaneous adverse reactions and supported switching to an alternative agent [11–14].

To explicitly translate these findings into clinical practice, we propose the following management recommendations: When an incidental heterozygous ATP7B variant is detected, clinicians should (1) strictly prioritize the clinical phenotype (e.g., distinguishing strictly paroxysmal from progressive continuous neurological signs); (2) calculate a formal Leipzig score; (3) mandate robust quantitative biochemical evaluations, such as 24-hour urinary copper testing, before considering WD-specific interventions; and (4) perform parental segregation analysis to clarify whether the variant represents an inherited heterozygous state rather than evidence of active disease or more complex pathogenicity.

Furthermore, our findings align with a growing body of literature highlighting the pitfalls of over-relying on genetic tests. As summarized in Table 2, several reported cases demonstrate how isolated ATP7B pathogenic variants can lead to diagnostic dilemmas when decoupled from mandatory biochemical evidence. Positioning our pediatric case within this context reinforces the absolute necessity of standardized biochemical confirmation.

Conclusion: Incidental detection of a heterozygous ATP7B nonsense variant, particularly alongside a single nonspecific biochemical abnormality, can misdirect clinicians toward WD. A structured diagnostic approach—centered on phenotype and standardized copper-metabolism evidence—helps prevent overdiagnosis and unnecessary interventions.

Limitations

First, the genetic testing was performed using a targeted NGS panel, which inherently cannot detect deep intronic variants, regulatory region mutations, or large structural variants/copy number variations. Although the targeted movement disorder panel included ADCY5, ATP1A3 was not included in the 67-gene panel; therefore, an ATP1A3-related paroxysmal movement disorder could not be genetically excluded, representing a limitation of the genetic screening. Furthermore, as this was a retrospective clinical evaluation, gene expression analysis (such as quantitative PCR) was not performed to assess ATP7B transcription levels. Therefore, we cannot absolutely exclude the presence of a second undetected cryptic ATP7B variant on the trans allele. However, even if a second variant were theoretically present, the normal quantitative 24-hour urinary copper and ceruloplasmin levels are not supportive of active Wilson disease. In addition, although the 24-hour urinary copper value was within the reference range and the total 24-hour urine volume was recorded as 1.40 L, formal documentation of collection completeness, such as 24-hour urinary creatinine excretion, was not available; this represents a minor limitation. Second, the follow-up period was limited to 2 months; longer clinical and biochemical monitoring would further strengthen diagnostic certainty and help exclude late-emerging copper metabolism abnormalities, although the current clinical, biochemical, ophthalmological, hepatic imaging, and Leipzig score evidence is not supportive of active Wilson disease.
