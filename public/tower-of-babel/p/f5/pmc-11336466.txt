I (don't) want to go home. Will young people return to the de-occupied territories of Ukraine?
Authors: Anastasiia Popova, Natalia Tsybuliak, Hanna Lopatina, Yana Suchikova, Sergii Kovachov, Ihor Bogdanov
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study focuses on understanding the intentions and perspectives of the youth in Ukraine regarding their return to their native cities post-de-occupation. In the context of sustainable regional development, the research aims to grasp the complexities of the youth's mindset, which is crucial for effective policy-making and strategic planning in the post-occupation period. The study utilized a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative surveys and qualitative focus group discussions. The quantitative phase involved a survey, targeting youth aged 14–35 and its district, to gather data on their willingness to return to de-occupied territories, trust in local institutions, and views on reconstruction efforts. This was followed by qualitative research through structured focus groups, segmented into participants with varying attitudes towards returning. The study highlights the importance of stability, economic growth, and the rebuilding of trust from the youth's viewpoint. It emphasizes the critical role of young people as key stakeholders in the reconstruction and planning processes.Keywords: De-occupation, Youth, Reconstruction, War, Ukraine

Introduction

The protracted war in Ukraine, enduring for over a year and a half, has precipitated a dramatic transformation of the country's political landscape, as evidenced by the shifts in geopolitical dynamics and socio-economic challenges [1,2]. Specifically, regions such as Kharkiv, Zaporizhia, Donetsk, and Luhansk have experienced varying degrees of occupation. At the same time, active confrontations have occurred in the territories of Kyiv, Chernihiv, Sumy, and other key provinces. The ramifications of this large-scale conflict touch upon every facet – the destruction of vital civil infrastructure, a sharp increase in humanitarian needs, and a mass exodus from the afflicted zones constitute merely the tip of the iceberg ([[3], [4], [5]]; Suchikova, 2023; Suchikova and Tsybuliak, 2023; [[6], [7], [8]]). As the global community grapples with the macroscopic environmental and economic consequences of the war, much discourse surrounding these themes is rife with debates and discussions [[9], [10], [11], [12], [13]]. Despite the plethora of studies and media reports detailing the global repercussions of this invasion, the literature offering insights into the occupied territories' unique challenges remains starkly limited. Within this niche, the primary focus has been on Russia's aggressive politics and actions, shedding light on the parallels between the contemporary occupation and the occupations during World War II (Malyarenko and Kormych, 2023, [14,15]; Suchikova et al., 2023a; Suchikova and Kovachov, 2023; [16]). These studies underscore the incessant human rights violations, establishment of filtration camps, and widespread torture reminiscent of past occupations [17].

Bukrieieva and Afanasieva [18] offer a poignant depiction of the occupation landscape in Melitopol, unveiling the horrific population stratification by Russian forces. This systematization categorizes individuals for varied levels of treatment, ranging from physical extermination to potential collaboration. Despite this meticulous stratification, the primary objective remains lucid: to suppress resistance and coerce Ukrainians to acquiesce to the Russian occupation. However, another paper by these same authors accentuates the indomitable spirit of the Ukrainians and their steadfast belief in triumph, portraying a citizenry imbued with hope and determination [19].

Today, almost a year has elapsed since the de-occupation of Kherson. However, due to the relentless shelling of this city, the population that had evacuated during the occupation has not returned. The city remains almost desolate. As the Armed Forces of Ukraine succeed in de-occupying some territories, attention inevitably shifts to the multifaceted challenges awaiting these regions in the aftermath, from infrastructure reconstruction to socio-economic rejuvenation. We posit that preparation for reconstruction and reintegration of territories should commence even before de-occupation, focusing on formulating policies for the population's return.

At the same time, the prolonged occupation influences the reintegration of de-occupied territories. For instance, Fadieiev [20] emphasizes the challenges of social reintegration in Ukraine, made pertinent during the Russo-Ukrainian war of 2014–2022. The author also notes that the prolonged occupation of portions of Ukraine's territory and its citizenry engendered new divisions of roles and opinions among Ukrainians, which will pose obstacles to successful reintegration post-de-occupation.

Research conducted by Kuzikova and colleagues [21] indicates that the longer the period of occupation, the more detrimental effects are observed on individuals' physical and psychological well-being. Traumatic loss positively correlates with post-traumatic stress disorder and adversely impacts the interpersonal relationships of inhabitants from de-occupied territories.

Müller-Funk and Fransen [22] analyze the formation and realization of desires to return among refugees living in prolonged displacement conditions. Their study demonstrates that broader life aspirations are pivotal in shaping the desire to return. They also emphasize that current and future desires to return are distinct concepts.

These studies underscore the necessity for a comprehensive approach to the reintegration of occupied territories, considering both social and psychological aspects. Our research aims to enhance the understanding of this problem, mainly focusing on the role of youth.

The question of how to involve the youth in this process becomes especially relevant. Young people are a resilient and adaptive segment of society, endowed with innovative thinking, energy, and a desire for change ([23]; Strielkowski et al., 2019). They can act as critical players in the reconstruction, offering fresh perspectives and new ideas and laying the foundation for a sustainable future (Vasyltsiv et al., 2020; [24]). However, the youth is also the group most frequently migrating in search of better opportunities [25], education, or simply a safe place to live, especially in times of conflict and instability [26,27].

Rhodes, Young, and Darrah-Okike [28] research focuses on young individuals' experiences of changing their living areas. They analyze youth reactions to relocating from high-poverty areas to more prosperous suburbs. The study underscores that residential areas do not impose culture on the youth; instead, they offer action strategies that young individuals may adopt.

Khymenko [29] concentrates on the theme of emigration and internal migration of Ukrainian youth in the conditions of the war in Ukraine since 2014 and up to February 24, 2022. Special attention is given to sociological studies of migration processes since the full-scale invasion by Russia, social aspects, causes, and consequences. The primary migration factors for the youth are psychological attachments to family, the health conditions of relatives, and a desire to be near them and care for their well-being. Most of those who migrated and returned to Ukraine affirm they do not plan to migrate again. The author outlines three potential explanations for this phenomenon: a sense of safety in their former residence, exhausted financial resources for living abroad, the desire to return to relatives left in Ukraine or the inability to adapt to a new environment due to cultural, social, or linguistic barriers. The article emphasizes the need for developing recommendations concerning effective migration policy to fill the labor market deficit and Ukraine's global development.

Stefansson [30] explores the relationship between property restitution and return patterns, and between the concept of the house and the sense of home following the war in Bosnia and Herzegovina. He posits that while the restoration of pre-war housing was viewed by displaced individuals as crucial for moral integrity, justice, and economic recovery, a sustainable return also necessitated access to employment and other sources of livelihood, often situated within a transnational context. Similarly, Raeymaekers [31] highlights the difficulties faced by youth in securing employment and housing in new host communities following migration due to the war in Congo. Additional research addresses the ethical, cultural, and political dimensions of youth returning to post-conflict regions [[32], [33], [34]]. Khasalamwa-Mwandha [35], drawing from interviews with displaced individuals and returnees in Northern Uganda, asserts that war and displacement extend beyond mere temporary disruptions, as the physical and social scars of war are deeply ingrained in individuals' lives. Consequently, recovery efforts must take these impacts into account to facilitate a new post-war future. Purdeková [36] further contends that return to post-war areas is frequently accompanied by challenges such as community conflict, social tension, and segregation issues.

Understanding the youth's motivations, perceptions, expectations, and fears regarding returning to de-occupied territories is crucial for formulating effective strategies and policies that foster their interest and activity. Given the significant impact of war on migration patterns, particularly among the youth, it is essential to understand the driving forces behind their decisions to return or remain displaced. A comprehensive examination of these factors can provide valuable insights into formulating effective policies for regional reconstruction and sustainable development. By identifying the key motivations, concerns, and barriers young people face, this research aims to inform strategies that can facilitate their return and active participation in rebuilding efforts. Such an understanding is critical for ensuring that the youth are willing and adequately supported to contribute to the socio-economic revival of de-occupied territories. Consequently, this study seeks to bridge the gap in the existing literature by offering a detailed analysis of the youth's perspectives, thereby laying the groundwork for targeted interventions that align with both immediate and long-term regional development goals.

Focusing on the case of the city, which remains under occupation, this study aims to draw broader conclusions and formulate substantiated political recommendations to facilitate the return and effective engagement of the youth in de-occupation reconstruction. The study utilized a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative surveys with qualitative focus group discussions to provide a holistic understanding of the youth's perspectives and intentions regarding returning to their native cities post-de-occupation.

Methodology

Research design

The study employed a mixed-methods approach, combining quantitative and qualitative research in a structured sequence to achieve a comprehensive understanding of the youth's perspectives on returning to de-occupied territories.

The initial phase, which involved a survey via Google Forms, was designed to gather broad demographic data and general sentiments regarding the willingness to return, trust in local institutions, and views on reconstruction efforts. This phase provided a cursory overview, ensuring a representative cross-section of the youth population. The findings from this survey informed the design and focus of the subsequent qualitative phase.

The next phase, consisting of focus group discussions, followed the survey. This sequential approach allowed the quantitative data to inform the qualitative inquiry, ensuring that the focus groups could explore specific themes and concerns identified in the survey. This allowed delving deeper into the nuanced opinions and attitudes of the participants, allowing for a richer, more detailed understanding of the underlying motivations, concerns, and aspirations of the participants.

This phased approach ensured that the qualitative data could provide context and depth to the quantitative findings, resulting in a holistic view of the research questions.

While this manuscript primarily presents qualitative data from surveys and focus group discussions, it is important to note that the quantitative methods employed were introductory. These quantitative approaches served to identify potential topics for discussion and target specific focus groups effectively. This initial quantitative phase provided a foundational understanding that informed the more detailed qualitative exploration of youth perspectives regarding the return to de-occupied territories. For readers interested in a more comprehensive quantitative analysis, we refer to the detailed study by Bohdanov et al [37].

Quantitative research

The initial phase, which involved a survey conducted via Google Forms, was designed to gather broad demographic data and general sentiments regarding the willingness to return, trust in local institutions, and views on reconstruction efforts. This phase provided a cursory overview, ensuring a representative cross-section of the youth population. The survey included questions about the respondents' socio-demographic situation, such as age, gender, having children, place of residence, and employment. Additionally, questions addressed their desire to return, their concerns about safety, economic stability, social dynamics, and the potential for reintegration into the community.

The sample was selected using a stratified approach to ensure a representative cross-section of the community. Respondents aged 18 to 35 were primarily selected through social networks, chat rooms of resettled communities, and universities, targeting students and young professionals. This method ensured that the sample included individuals who are actively engaged in their communities and likely to be affected by de-occupation policies. For respondents under 18, recruitment was conducted through schools, where prior consent was obtained from parents via school principals. This dual approach not only facilitated the inclusion of a diverse range of youth perspectives but also enhanced the representativeness of the sample by covering various socio-economic backgrounds and regions within the de-occupied territories. The inclusion criteria for participants were youth aged 14 to 35 currently residing in or displaced from de-occupied territories of Ukraine. Exclusion criteria included individuals outside the specified age range. The selection aimed to capture a diverse range of experiences and perspectives within the targeted demographic.

The degree of community representation was further ensured by surveying over two weeks in May 2023, capturing a broad temporal snapshot of youth attitudes and intentions. With 1180 respondents participating, the sample size is sufficiently large to allow for generalizable insights, reflecting the broader sentiments of the youth in de-occupied territories.

The findings from this survey informed the design and focus of the subsequent qualitative phase.

Research in focus groups

Following the quantitative survey, the next step was to delve into qualitative territory through focus group discussions. Three focus groups were conducted, each comprising 10 participants, ensuring an environment conducive to in-depth conversations while maintaining a manageable group size for moderation. The qualitative phase of this study involved conducting focus group discussions (FGDs) to gain a deeper understanding of the nuanced opinions and attitudes of young people regarding their potential return to de-occupied territories. The FGDs were divided into three thematic groups based on the respondents' expressed attitudes towards returning: intention to return, ambivalence, and reluctance.

Recruitment for the FGDs was conducted following the quantitative survey phase. Participants were selected based on their responses to specific survey questions that indicated their overall sentiment towards returning to their hometown post-de-occupation (Table 1).

All focus group sessions were audio-recorded (with prior consent) and subsequently transcribed. These transcriptions underwent thematic analysis. The data were coded and grouped into key themes and sub-themes reflecting the research questions. Themes were then cross-referenced with quantitative results to validate, compare, or deepen insights. Identified patterns, recurrent narratives, points of divergence, and areas of consensus were meticulously noted, forming the foundation for subsequent conclusions and the research discussion section. After the analysis, preliminary conclusions were presented to a subset of focus group participants for member-checking, ensuring interpretations align with their viewpoints and minimizing potential researcher bias.

In summation, merging quantitative and qualitative insights, especially the nuances garnered from the focus groups, allowed the study to construct a multidimensional panorama of the youth's stance, concerns, aspirations, and recommendations regarding the future of the de-occupied territories.

Sample characteristics and city portrait

The survey garnered participation from respondents aged 14 to 35. The age range of 14–35 years was chosen to encompass a broad spectrum of youth experiences and perspectives. This range captures individuals in various life stages, from adolescence through early adulthood, critical periods for personal development, educational pursuits, and career establishment. Including those as young as 14 allows the study to account for the formative years of high school students who may have strong attachments to their hometowns and are significantly impacted by educational disruptions due to the conflict. Extending the range to 35 ensures that the study also includes young adults who might be in higher education, early career stages, or starting families, providing a comprehensive view of the youth population. This wide age span aligns with international standards for youth categorization used by organizations such as the United Nations, which defines youth as individuals between 15 and 24 years old but often extends this range to 35 to include those in similar transitional phases. Given the large sample size, it was feasible to extrapolate the results to represent broader sentiments of the youth in de-occupied territories.

The majority of respondents (67.8 %) were women. The overwhelming majority (77.1 %) do not have children. 67.2 % are currently studying in secondary, vocational, or higher education institutions. Also, at the time of the survey, most respondents (61.7 %) were internally displaced persons residing in territories controlled by Ukraine, 24.2 % were external migrants (moved abroad), and 14.2 % lived in the occupied city.

The study focused on young people from Berdyansk and the Berdyansk territorial community, who either remained in Berdyansk during the occupation or migrated to other territories of Ukraine or abroad. This specific inclusion criterion was chosen to capture the diverse experiences and perspectives of youth directly affected by the occupation and their potential intentions to return post-de-occupation.

The selection of respondents from the Berdyansk community was deliberate. Berdyansk is a port and resort city located in the Zaporizhzhia region in southeastern Ukraine (Fig. 1).

The city of Berdyansk and the Berdyansk Territorial Community on the map of Ukraine.

The city is on the northern coast of the Sea of Azov, which connects to the Black Sea. Berdyansk is an essential industrial center in the Azov Sea region. The city has approximately 20 enterprises spanning various sectors, including metal processing, food, petrochemical industries, and machinery manufacturing. As of 2022, the city's population exceeded 100,000 inhabitants. From February 27, 2022, Berdyansk has been under Russian military occupation. Estimating the current population of Berdyansk is exceptionally challenging. According to various estimates, over 3/4 of the population has left the occupied territories of the Zaporizhzhia region.

The community serves as a critical case for studying migration and return motivations. We are particularly interested in exploring the hopes and prospects for return among the youth, uniquely positioned to influence the region's future socio-economic fabric. If the territories were already de-occupied, the emotional responses might include feelings of guilt, obligations, and other complex emotions. In contrast, the anticipation of de-occupation allows us to investigate young people's forward-looking perspectives and perceived prospects. The uncertainty surrounding the future of these territories adds another dimension to our research, providing valuable insights into how young people perceive their potential return to de-occupied areas.

Ethical considerations

Ethical considerations were paramount in this study, especially given the topic's sensitive nature and the minors' inclusion. For respondents under 18, consent was obtained from parents through school principals before participation. This process ensured that parents were fully informed about the study's objectives, procedures, and the voluntary nature of participation. For respondents aged 18 to 35, consent was obtained directly, with clear information provided about the study's purpose, confidentiality assurances, and the option to withdraw at any time without consequence.

The study received ethical approval from the Ethics Committee of Berdyansk State Pedagogical University (protocol № 07/2023), ensuring that all procedures adhered to rigorous ethical standards. This included maintaining the anonymity of participants, securing data storage, and ensuring that the findings would be reported in an aggregated manner to prevent any potential identification of individual respondents.

Limitations of the research

Despite our efforts, the study has several limitations. The reliance on Google Forms for survey distribution inherently limits the sample to those with internet access and familiarity with digital tools, potentially excluding segments of the population who are less digitally literate or lack internet access. Moreover, the self-reported nature of the survey data may introduce biases, such as social desirability bias, where respondents may provide answers they perceive as favorable rather than their genuine opinions.

Additionally, including participants from occupied Berdyansk without fully accounting for their unique circumstances may affect the comparability of the data. The experiences and perspectives of those who remained in the occupied territories could differ significantly from those who migrated, introducing potential variability in the findings. This variability underscores the need to carefully interpret the results and consider these distinct contexts in future research.

As researchers, we acknowledge our positionality and reflexivity in this study. Being part of Ukraine's academic and social fabric, we are deeply invested in the outcomes of this research. Our backgrounds, experiences, and expectations inevitably shape our interpretations and interactions with the participants. We strived to maintain objectivity and openness, ensuring that the voices of the youth were authentically represented without undue influence from our perspectives. Additionally, we were mindful of the power dynamics at play, especially when interacting with younger participants, and took measures to create a safe and respectful environment for open dialogue.

Results

Survey results

The heatmap vividly highlights heightened concerns from respondents about safety (85 %). They also fear that post-de-occupation, there would be economic instability (80 %). The reported reintegration issues by 70 % of the respondents highlight the intricacies of merging with a socio-cultural environment that has drastically changed. The indigenous population of the city massively emigrated due to the occupation. In contrast, during the occupation, inhabitants from depressive regions of Russia and representatives of minor ethnic groups actively moved to the city. Property and housing concerns (75 %) further attest to the apprehensions related to returning. Emotional and psychological stress account for 65 %. While not the primary concern, it underscores latent traumas and the emotional reverberations of war. Political instability and educational opportunities concern 60 % and 55 % of the respondents, respectively, pointing towards governance issues and the quest for quality education. 58 % of participants expressed that access to medical aid is a primary factor in resettlement decisions. This, coupled with concerns about cultural shifts (50 %) and infrastructural damages (72 %), complements the list of challenges that could potentially impede smooth reintegration. The survey results and qualitative data provide a rich tapestry of the challenges and hopes faced by the youth in Berdyansk. The reluctance to return is primarily driven by concerns over safety, economic instability, and social dynamics. These concerns highlight the need for targeted interventions that address both tangible and intangible barriers to return.

Conversely, the desire to return is fueled by strong emotional and cultural ties to the city, as well as a proactive vision for its future. This duality suggests that successful reintegration and reconstruction strategies must balance immediate needs for safety and economic stability with long-term investments in community development and cultural revitalization.

A comprehensive approach to addressing both tangible and intangible issues is paramount to creating a conducive environment for return and reintegration. Officials, local authorities, and international organizations must adapt their interventions according to these articulated fears and concerns.

A general aspiration for peace and stability in the city is evident across all groups. However, the nature of the connection and the desire to actively participate in its rejuvenation varies significantly.

The "Intention to Return" group exhibits a profound determination to be an integral part of the city's future, viewing the city not merely as their birthplace but as an indelible part of their identity. Their aspirations are aligned with the reconstruction and elevation of the city's pre-war status. This deep emotional connection suggests that these individuals see the city as a significant part of their personal and cultural heritage, reinforcing their commitment to its restoration.

The sentiments of the "Ambivalence" group pose both a challenge and a latent opportunity for policymakers. Their ambivalent feelings suggest that appropriate incentives and assurances can prompt a portion of this group to either return or contribute to rejuvenation initiatives. This group's perspective highlights the need for strategic interventions that address uncertainties and offer tangible benefits, such as economic opportunities and improved infrastructure.

The stances of the "Reluctance" group highlight the profound impact of war and occupation on the youth's psyche. Their hesitations are driven by infrastructural or financial challenges and deeply rooted emotional and psychological traumas. Overcoming these barriers is pivotal, and a holistic strategy focused on physical reconstruction and socio-emotional reconstruction and reconciliation is imperative.

Key stakeholders in potential return and sustainable development

The subsequent question discussed in the focus groups pertained to the youth's perceptions concerning the role of potential stakeholders in return and sustainable development. Their ratings of stakeholders provide detailed insights into whom they trust most when considering the possibility of returning to the de-occupied territories (Fig. 3).

Stakeholder ratings among three groups.

The "Intention to Return" group prefers local initiatives. They believe that the best solutions, considering regional nuances, will likely come from local administrations, primarily if they are supported by non-governmental organizations (NGOs). This preference suggests a strong belief in the capacity of local bodies to understand and address specific community needs effectively. Global organizations, in their view, play a secondary or tertiary role, likely due to their perceived distance from local issues and slower response times.

The "Ambivalence" group highly values field operations and immediate assistance, explaining their inclination towards NGOs. They appreciate the agility and on-the-ground presence of these organizations, which can provide swift support and tangible benefits. Although they recognize the pivotal role of local self-government bodies in broader schemes, such as infrastructure development, skepticism about their efficiency remains. This skepticism might stem from past experiences or perceived bureaucratic inefficiencies.

On the other hand, the "Reluctance" group, probably influenced by their apprehensions, trusts global organizations to manage the situation. They view global organizations as paragons of neutrality and arbiters of a safe environment, perhaps due to their established protocols and resources. Immediate needs, in their opinion, would be best addressed by NGOs, which they see as more capable of providing urgent and direct support. Local authorities, albeit acknowledged, are perceived with a hint of skepticism, possibly due to lingering past experiences or perceived biases.

Across the groups, there is a palpable sense that an integrated approach involving collaboration between local authorities, NGOs, and global organizations is deemed essential. However, as perceived by the groups, leadership in this tripartite cooperation fluctuates based on individual experience, trust levels, and the juxtaposition of immediate and long-term priorities.

Discussion

Exploring youth perspectives regarding the present and future state of territories currently under occupation and potentially to be liberated, such as the city, unveils a plethora of profound insights. The survey results and focus group discussions offer a multidimensional understanding, with each group presenting distinct perceptions and challenges. Nonetheless, a recurring theme is envisioning a rejuvenated, thriving, and harmonious region (Fig. 4). This aligns with the general aspirations of war-torn regions, where a return to peace and stability remains paramount (Zhuravka et al., 2023).

Word clouds constructed from the answers to the question "What words or phrases do you associate with your hopes and aspirations for the future of the city?" (a) "How do you see the future of the city" (b).

On the other hand, the disparities in views among the groups reflect broader socio-political dynamics signaling the necessity for a more intricate comprehension of de-occupation realities.

The "Intention to Return" group demonstrates a profound emotional and cultural connection to their city, perceiving it as an integral part of their identity. Supported by theoretical frameworks on place attachment and identity [41], these strong emotional bonds motivate these individuals to engage actively in the city's recovery and development [42]. Their perception is rooted in nostalgia, a longing for stability, and a deep desire to restore their hometown's peace and normalcy. Despite their strong desire to return, this group is concerned about the city's safety post-de-occupation, potential economic instability, and the challenges of social reintegration. Theoretical discussions on trauma and post-conflict recovery [43] underscore the necessity of addressing both material and psychological needs to facilitate effective reintegration and healing. Their worries about economic viability and social fabric changes underscore the complexity of rebuilding efforts in post-conflict settings [44].

The desire among the youth to contribute towards reconstruction, both remotely and on the ground, is inspiring. Despite challenges, it mirrors global trends where youth often emerge as change agents in post-conflict scenarios ([37]; Suchikova et al., 2023b; [21,[45], [46], [47]]). Simultaneously, one cannot overlook the economic dynamism introduced by the private sector. As articulated by young participants, the allure of employment opportunities, innovation, and infrastructure development largely hinges on the involvement of business, entrepreneurs, and industry. They can serve as the cornerstone of economic rejuvenation.

The "Ambivalence" group exhibits mixed feelings towards returning to their hometown. While they retain some emotional ties and curiosity about reintegration, their decision to return is heavily influenced by external factors such as visible progress in reconstruction and socioeconomic incentives. This group's perspective highlights the need for strategic interventions that address uncertainties and offer tangible benefits, such as economic opportunities and improved infrastructure. Place-based incentives [48] are particularly relevant here, as creating favorable conditions might tip the balance toward positive engagement with the city's future. Their curiosity and hope for reintegration are evident, contingent on specific plans and visible progress. The "Ambivalence" group's fears about infrastructure and psychological trauma align with the concept of Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) and its impact on migration decisions [49,50]. Addressing these concerns requires integrated policies that combine infrastructure development with mental health services to support individuals coping with war-induced trauma.

The "Reluctance" group feels detached from the city due to new beginnings elsewhere or deep psychological scars from the conflict. Their responses highlight significant barriers to return, driven by safety concerns, limited opportunities, and social judgment. Theoretical frameworks on trauma [51] emphasize the impact of psychological scars on migration decisions. They are primarily motivated by a need for psychological closure and may consider returning only if substantial safety guarantees and socio-emotional support are provided. Their reluctance underscores the need to address physical and emotional needs to encourage return migration. This group is primarily concerned with safety, the perception of limited opportunities in the city, and the potential for social judgment upon return. The Relative Deprivation Theory [52] posits that perceptions of relative disadvantage influence migration decisions, highlighting the need for policies that reduce perceived inequalities and foster a supportive community environment.

The factors compelling the youth to consider a return underline the intersection of socio-economic and emotional facets. Financial stability, security, and cultural ties emerge as reiterated themes, reinforcing previous studies that suggest that mere economic opportunities are not sufficient to incentivize reverse migration to post-conflict territories [[53], [54], [55], [56]]. Concerns predominantly revolve around security, uncertainty, and lack of clarity about prospects. This mirrors findings from other post-conflict regions where trauma and conflict-induced ambiguity linger even post-resolution, impacting return decisions [[57], [58], [59]].

A multifaceted approach is required to ensure the return of the youth and their involvement in the reconstruction of temporarily occupied territories. Financial incentives such as reconstruction grants and affordable housing schemes can encourage those with a strong emotional attachment to return and actively participate in rebuilding efforts [60]. Implementing robust safety measures and ensuring visible progress in infrastructure development can address the ambivalent group's concerns. Cultural revival programs can foster social cohesion and community identity [61]. Establishing transparent governance mechanisms and providing psychological support are crucial for addressing the deep-seated traumas of the reluctant group. Engaging the diaspora and leveraging their resources can also support the recovery process [49,62].

An integrated approach involving local authorities, NGOs, and global organizations is essential for the successful return and sustainable development of de-occupied territories. Multi-stakeholder partnerships [63] can harness the strengths of each group, ensuring a collaborative environment where local insights, agile responses, and global support are harmoniously integrated. Encouraging inclusive and transparent decision-making processes can foster active civic engagement. Providing platforms for community involvement and recognizing the diverse participation styles of youth will be critical for sustainable reconstruction [64]. By aligning these strategies with established theoretical frameworks, policymakers and stakeholders can develop comprehensive plans that address the multifaceted challenges faced by returning youth, ensuring a robust and sustainable recovery process.

In light of the findings of this research, a series of recommendations and strategies can be proposed to address the challenges associated with the return of young individuals to the de-occupied territories. For local government bodies, it is paramount to devise policies that cater to the specific needs and aspirations voiced by the youth. Their concerns about safety and infrastructure should be addressed with clear communication and tangible efforts to restore and ensure stability. Introducing a dedicated communication channel to inform young citizens of the milestones and initiatives concerning the de-occupation of territories and future reintegration can strengthen trust and clarity.

The importance of NGOs and international organizations in this ecosystem cannot be overstated. Joint projects, internships, and partnerships fostered by these entities can be a magnet for the youth, prompting them to envision their future in their hometown post-de-occupation, with prospects of employment and significant contribution. Lastly, perhaps most critically, establishing a state-funded mentorship program could be invaluable. Pairing young individuals with seasoned professionals and community leaders can provide guidance, instill confidence, and foster a sense of purpose in their decision to return.

Conclusions

This study offers invaluable insight into the intricate and multifaceted perceptions, aspirations, and challenges the youth face when returning to their hometown after the de-occupation of a region. Focusing on the perspectives and views of the youth from the city that still under occupation, we endeavored to discern an answer to the overarching question – are young individuals prepared to return home post-de-occupation?

A primary conclusion drawn is that the youth exemplify a potent blend of resilience, hope, skepticism, and determination. While they are acutely aware of the challenges associated with occupation and subsequent de-occupation, their aspirations for a better future remain unshaken. This duality, characterized by apprehension and optimism, underscores the importance of tailored policy and strategic intervention.

In conclusion, the efficacy of political strategies and recommendations hinges on adaptability and flexibility, accounting for the needs and views of the youth as pivotal stakeholders in rejuvenation. In examining the situation in regions awaiting de-occupation it is imperative to emphasize the necessity of forecasting and planning for the future in advance. A clear vision for their development must be established before the reclamation of de-occupied territories. Properly articulated policies, effective collaboration among stakeholders, and, primarily, the active role of the youth should be the essential components of this vision. This research accentuates that the youth should not merely be perceived as aid recipients but as equal partners in the planning and reconstruction. Their visions, aspirations, and concerns can serve as a reliable compass in shaping strategies for the future. Adopting such a proactive approach will invariably ensure a stable and productive reconstruction process post-de-occupation.
