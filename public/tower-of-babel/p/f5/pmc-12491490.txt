Contrastive learning uncovers cellular interactions and morphologies in the tumor microenvironment of lung adenocarcinoma linked to immunotherapy response
Authors: Hortense Le, Nicolas Coudray, Anna Yeaton, Sitharam Ramaswami, Afreen Karimkhan, Wei-Yi Cheng, James Cai, Tai-Hsien Ou Yang, Salman R Punekar, Luis Chiriboga, Vamsidhar Velcheti, Kwok-Kin Wong, Daniel H Sterman, Andre L Moreira, Harvey I Pass, Aristotelis Tsirigos
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) presents diverse histomorphological features within the tumor microenvironment (TME) that influence prognosis and response to immunotherapy. Leveraging contrastive learning, we developed an unbiased atlas of cell neighborhoods to systematically explore the LUAD microenvironment at the cellular scale and investigate how these neighborhoods combine to form histologic patterns. This multiscale approach enables a comprehensive understanding of both cell-specific interactions and broader histologic architecture in LUAD. Our analysis identified distinct histomorphological phenotype clusters of cellular neighborhoods (cn-HPCs) with prognostic significance. For example, cn-HPC 0 was associated with immune activation and favorable survival, while cn-HPC 23 was enriched in necrotic, immune-excluded regions and aligned with poorer outcomes. Through associations with immunophenotypes, co-expressed gene modules, and pathway enrichment, we found that cn-HPCs reflect underlying processes of immune modulation, cellular growth, and inflammation, offering insights into the functional landscape of LUAD. In an independent LUAD immunotherapy cohort, we found that cn-HPC 23 showed promising predictive value for treatment response. These findings suggest that fine-grained spatial profiling of the TME may offer complementary value for identifying patients more likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Taken together, our study highlights the potential of cn-HPCs to bridge histopathological patterns and clinical outcomes, supporting their relevance for prognosis and treatment stratification in LUAD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-17117-8.Subject terms: Non-small-cell lung cancer, Predictive markers

Introduction

Lung cancer is one of the deadliest cancers among both men and women in the United States. The risk of cancer recurrence for early-stage non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients remains over 30% following surgery1. Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) is the most prevalent subtype of NSCLC, accounting for 40% of all lung cancer cases.

Previous studies have demonstrated a strong link between prognosis in resected early-stage LUAD patients and various histopathological and histomorphological features, such as histological grading and predominant or high-grade patterns2–4. The gold standard used by lung pathologists is an interpretation of such patterns found in hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) stained tissue sections. However, H&E images constitute a large and detailed source of information at different resolutions from cell structures to tissue architecture and analyzing whole slide images (WSIs) is highly time-consuming and subjective to inter-observer variability.

Recent advances in machine learning, particularly deep learning, have paved the way for automating this process, but traditional methods rely heavily on large, annotated datasets5–8. Self-supervised learning (SSL) addresses these challenges and enables the extraction of relevant histomorphological phenotype features from whole slide images without extensive annotations9–11. By leveraging unlabeled data, SSL techniques can learn meaningful representations that enhance the accuracy and generalization of an extensive range of downstream tasks, such as tumor detection, subtyping, grading, staging, prognostic and therapeutic response prediction. SSL has been widely used for predicting clinical features in lung adenocarcinoma12,13. A recent study applied a SSL-based YOLOv5 model for predicting spread through air spaces (STAS) in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma from H&E images14. Their model achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.83, indicating its effectiveness in predicting tumor invasiveness.

Recently, foundational models using SSL techniques have emerged as a transformative shift in pathology, offering large, generalizable models that can be fine-tuned across various pathology tasks. Models like UNI15 and Prov-GigaPath16 have demonstrated strong capabilities in classification and subtyping. UNI, pretrained on over 100 million images from diverse H&E samples spanning 20 tissue types, excels in tissue classification, slide classification, and disease subtyping. Similarly, Prov-GigaPath, pretrained on 171,189 WSIs from 31 tissue types, surpasses state-of-the-art methods in predicting gene mutations and subtyping cancers. In contrast, phenotypic mapping models bring unique advantages by offering highly detailed insights into tissue microenvironments. For instance, the Histomorphological Phenotype Learning (HPL) framework17which leverages contrastive learning, is specifically designed to identify an array of histomorphological features. HPL organizes clusters that reflect histomorphological patterns across a spectrum of tissue states, including benign, reactive, and malignant phenotypes, and suggests potential transitions based on morphological similarity. Additionally, the HPL framework includes an annotated atlas of histomorphological phenotypes (HP-Atlas) in LUAD, created in collaboration with a lung pathologist. This atlas links distinct histological patterns to survival outcomes and immune phenotypes, providing essential interpretability that enhances our understanding of complex cancer phenotypes.

Parallel to these histomorphological advances, cell-level analyses using RNA sequencing have opened additional possibilities for characterizing the tumor microenvironment (TME). By studying the distribution and types of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes, researchers have identified relationships between immune cell presence and lung cancer prognosis18. Additionally, multicellular immune hubs within the TME have been associated with immunotherapy responses in LUAD19while specific gene signatures correlated with patient survival have been linked to mesothelial cell and monocyte abundance in tumor-adjacent tissue, along with heightened TNF-α and NF-κB signaling20.

LUAD is characterized by complex interactions within the TME, where cellular heterogeneity and spatial organization play crucial roles in tumor progression, and response to therapy21,22. As immunotherapy increasingly becomes a central strategy in the treatment of LUAD23, understanding the detailed cellular landscapes within tumors is essential for predicting treatment outcomes and identifying patients likely to benefit from immune-based interventions22. However, the traditional methods of characterizing LUAD have largely focused on tissue-level features, which can overlook the intricate cellular interactions that define the tumor’s microenvironment24. In this study, we introduce a novel, unbiased approach leveraging contrastive learning to systematically map the TME at the cellular scale. By developing an atlas of cell neighborhoods (cn-HPCs) that captures both cell-specific interactions and their organization into broader histologic patterns, we offer a multiscale perspective that provides deeper insights into the LUAD microenvironment. This approach enables a comprehensive understanding of cellular diversity and spatial relationships, which are crucial for deciphering the functional landscape of LUAD. Importantly, by incorporating molecular association analyses such as immunophenotype profiling, co-expressed gene modules, and pathway enrichment, we propose that cn-HPCs could serve as key tools for understanding the molecular underpinnings of LUAD and predicting patient outcomes. We also hypothesize that these cellular neighborhoods might have predictive power for immunotherapy response, offering a novel framework for patient stratification. This approach provides a more granular and precise way to explore tumor biology, which has the potential to enhance precision oncology strategies and guide the development of more personalized immunotherapy treatments.

Results

We apply contrastive learning models to cell neighborhoods to generate a comprehensive mapping of the LUAD microenvironment. We then assess how these cell neighborhood features associate with patient survival and response to immunotherapy.

Histopathological phenotype learning pipelines for tissue-level and cell-neighborhood analysis

Figure 1 outlines the original and cell-neighborhood HPL methodologies, which were trained on the LUNG dataset available from the TCGA database. The original HPL pipeline8 (Fig. 1a) takes in TCGA-LUAD and TCGA-LUSC WSIs and run multiple preprocessing steps where the WSIs are initially segmented into 224 × 224 pixels tiles without overlap at 5X magnification resulting in tiles of ~ 452 × 452 μm (see Methods for more details). Stain normalization is applied to each tile. 250,000 tiles are subsampled to train a Barlow Twins model25. Upon training, each tile is transformed into a 1 × 128 tile vector representation capturing distinct histomorphological phenotype patterns. Following the HPL pipeline, the TCGA-LUAD tile vector representations are organized into a nearest neighbor graph and Leiden community detection26 is applied to define a set of clusters regrouping tiles with similar features together (HPCs).

Framework overview of HP-Atlas of histomorphological phenotypes and cell neighborhoods. (a) Histomorphological Phenotype Learning (HPL) pipeline. Whole slide images (WSIs) are tiled at 5X magnification. A Barlow Twins model is trained on a subset of the tiles via redundancy reduction. Tiles are projected into the frozen backbone of the Barlow Twins model to produce embeddings. Histomorphological phenotype clusters (HPCs) are defined using Leiden community detection over a nearest neighbor graph of the embeddings. (b) Cell-centered HPL pipeline. Whole slide images (WSIs) are tiled at 20X magnification. Tiles are input into Hover-Net for cell segmentation and classification. A new set of tiles are created centered around the detected cells. A Barlow Twins model is trained on a subset of the cell-focused tiles via redundancy reduction. Tiles are projected into the frozen backbone of the Barlow Twins model to produce embeddings. Cell-focused histomorphological phenotype clusters (cn-HPCs) are defined using Leiden community detection over a nearest neighbor graph of the embeddings.

The cell-neighborhood pipeline (Fig. 1b, see Methods for details) creates tiles at 20X magnification with tile size of ~ 113 × 113 μm from the TCGA-LUAD cohort. The tiles are fed into the cell segmentation and classification model Hover-Net27 pretrained on the dataset PanNuke28. Using the cell segmentation results, the WSIs are retiled into 224 × 224 tiles centered around the cell centroids at 20X magnification (~ 113 × 113 μm). Stain normalization is applied. The rest of the pipeline follows the original pipeline, resulting in 27 clusters (cn-HPCs) with distinct cell features and which show cell-cell interactions between different cell types (examples of tiles from each cn-HPC can be found in Supplementary Fig. 1–2). For each patient, a cn-HPC compositional vector is defined.

cn-HPCs refine and enrich findings from existing LUAD HPCs

To investigate how different cellular neighborhoods contribute to the broader histologic patterns observed in LUAD, we followed a multiscale approach enabled by the self-supervised trained networks described previously. Broad histological patterns identified at 5x were obtained from the foundation model and atlas previously published in Claudio Quiros et al.17. To systematically explore the LUAD microenvironment at the cellular scale, we further developed an unbiased atlas of cell neighborhoods (cn-HPCs). By mapping 20x cn-HPC tiles to their corresponding 5x HPCs, we were able to compute the percentage of cn-HPC tiles belonging to each HPC (Fig. 2a). The overlap between distinct HPCs, such as HPC 14 (necrosis) and HPC 10 (hemorrhagic lung), with the same cn-HPC (e.g., cn-HPC 23, Fig. 2b), shows that these tissue regions share similar cellular features at higher magnification —such as immune cell presence or cellular breakdown—regardless of their low-magnification annotation.

On the other hand, our analysis revealed that certain HPCs, such as HPC 1 (sheets of inflammation with destruction, Fig. 2c), overlapped with multiple cn-HPCs, reflecting the presence of diverse cellular compositions within similar inflammatory regions.

To further explore these relationships, we constructed consensus graphs (Fig. 2d) and heatmaps showing interactions between cn-HPCs within different HPCs (Supplementary Fig. 3). In particular, we found that HPC 14 (necrosis) and HPC 10 (hemorrhagic lung)’s fingerprints (Supplementary Fig. 4a-b) revealed distinct network structures. Although both highly showing high cn-HPC23 interactions with other cn-HPCs, the rest of their cn-HPC interactions were very different (Supplementary Fig. 4c). The results show that individual cn-HPCs are distributed heterogeneously across traditional HPCs, reflecting added granularity in cellular composition.

Mapping cn-HPCs onto broader tissue-level annotations reveals that histopathological features such as necrosis, inflammation, or hemorrhage are composed of diverse cellular substructures shaped by distinct or overlapping cell interactions.

Cell-neighborhood HP-Atlas identifies cell-cell interactions and tumor grade in LUAD

We applied dimensionality reduction using Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) on the cell-neighborhood tile vector representations, overlaying the corresponding cn-HPCs (Fig. 3a) and analyzing cn-HPC cell density and cell composition (Fig. 3b and c). We then computed the number of interactions between each cell type within each tile of a cn-HPC (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 5) and asked a lung pathologist to review and annotate the clusters by identifying LUAD grades and cell types (Supplementary Tables 1, 2). Additionally, we performed survival analysis with Kaplan-Meier (Fig. 3e, Supplementary Fig. 6) and visualized the location of each cn-HPC with respect to the tumor and inflammatory cells classified by Hover-Net in representative WSIs from the TCGA-LUAD cohort (Fig. 3f).

(d) Visualization of cell-cell interactions for cn-HPC 0, 11, 23 and 24. Cell type labels were obtained from Hover-Net. Interestingly, cn-HPCs 0 and 23—despite their proximity in UMAP space—exhibited markedly different cell densities, compositions, and survival associations. cn-HPC 0, annotated as tumor cells admixed with lymphocytes (Supplementary Table 1), was associated with better survival. Visual overlays demonstrated clear colocalization of cn-HPC 0 tiles with inflammatory cells in tumor-rich regions (Fig. 3f), consistent with a spatial arrangement indicative of immune infiltration. This was quantitatively supported by spatial metrics: 86.8% of cn-HPC 0 tiles were located near immune cells, 75.2% of cn-HPC 0 tiles within tumor regions were immune-adjacent, over half were within tumor regions (55.4%), and the mean distance to tumor boundaries was 1.88 μm (Supplementary Fig. 7b). These results show that cn-HPC 0 frequently localizes within immune-infiltrated tumor zones, consistent with previously reported patterns of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs)-enriched regions29,30.

Conversely, cn-HPC 23 was associated with worse survival and exhibited several hallmarks of aggressive tumor regions. It showed a high density of necrotic–necrotic cell interactions, a predominance of necrotic cells, and frequent localization at the interface of tumor clusters. Although cn-HPC 23 tiles often overlapped visually with tumor cells along the tumor margin (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 7a), spatial overlays revealed limited immune cell colocalization within these regions. This observation was supported quantitatively in Supplementary Fig. 7b: cn-HPC 23 tiles demonstrated a lower probability of being proximal to immune cells, both globally and within tumor-defined areas, and were situated closer to tumor boundaries than other cn-HPCs. Spatial patterns show that cn-HPC 23 is enriched near tumor margins, characterized by high necrotic-necrotic interactions, low colocalization with immune cells, and consistent association with worse survival.

Beyond these patterns, we identified additional cn-HPCs with strong survival associations (Fig. 3e). cn-HPC 11, which was significantly associated with better survival, was characterized by predominant necrotic-inflammatory cell interactions (Fig. 3d) and had the lowest proportion of neoplastic cells among all clusters (Fig. 3c). Its spatial distribution resembled that of cn-HPC 0, showing colocalization with inflammatory cells adjacent within tumor regions (Fig. 3f). cn-HPC 11 had a high proportion of necrotic-inflammatory interactions and low neoplastic cell content, with spatial patterns resembling those of cn-HPC 0. On the other hand, cn-HPC 24, significantly associated with worse survival, was enriched in neoplastic cells (Fig. 3b-c) and predominantly exhibited neoplastic-neoplastic cell interactions (Fig. 3d). Unlike cn-HPC 23, which is located at the tumor edge, cn-HPC 24 tiles are found within the tumor bundle itself, yet they are invaded by inflammatory cells (Fig. 3f) while lacking necrotic cells (Fig. 3d), indicating a distinct spatial pattern compared to cn-HPC 23. These findings indicate that cn-HPC 24 forms near an inflamed yet resistant tumor niche, distinct from environments where immune infiltration leads to suppression or necrosis signals aggressive progression.

Differences in spatial localization, cell interactions, and survival associations across cn-HPCs reflect diverse tumor microenvironments.

Validation of prognostic cn-HPCs in independent LUAD cohorts

To further assess the robustness and clinical relevance of the prognostic cn-HPCs identified in TCGA-LUAD, we evaluated their presence and associations in two external LUAD datasets.

In the publicly available Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN)31,32 LUAD33 cohort (n = 20 slides from 8 patients), no cn-HPC 23 tiles were detected. All patients in this cohort were tumor-free at last follow-up. A lung pathologist confirmed the absence of necrotic regions typically associated with cn-HPC 23. This absence is consistent with our prior findings of cn-HPC 23 enrichment in aggressive tumors in TCGA-LUAD.

We next evaluated the Stage I LUAD cohort from NYU (n = 276 WSIs from 276 patients), previously described in Claudio Quiros et al.17. Consistent with results from the Stage I subset of TCGA-LUAD, we observed that cn-HPC 0 was associated with improved survival (p = 0.082), cn-HPC 24 was associated with significantly worse survival (p = 0.00049), cn-HPC 11 and cn-HPC 23 were not significantly associated with survival (Supplementary Fig. 8). The lack of significant associations for cn-HPC 11 and 23 in this early-stage cohort contrasts with their patterns in the full TCGA-LUAD dataset, where these clusters were more enriched in histologically aggressive or necrotic regions.

In this external cohort, cn-HPC 0 and cn-HPC 24 were again associated with improved and worse survival, respectively, consistent with observations in TCGA-LUAD.

cn-HPCs are associated with specific molecular signatures

We applied BayesPrism34 to deconvolute bulk RNA-seq data from TCGA-LUAD tumor samples, utilizing single-cell RNA-seq (scRNA-seq) data from metastatic lung adenocarcinoma patients as a reference35 (Supplementary Fig. 9a). We then computed Pearson correlations between patient cell proportions and patient cn-HPC composition to examine potential cn-HPC enrichment or depletion in specific cell types (Supplementary Fig. 9b). We observed distinct patterns of cell-type enrichment and depletion across cn-HPCs, reflecting variation in immune and stromal cell proportions. For example, cn-HPC 0 showed enrichment in Tip-like endothelial cells (ECs) and a depletion of tumor cells. cn-HPC 23 was enriched in ciliated cells, natural killer (NK) cells, and T lymphocytes. These patterns highlight distinct cell-type composition profiles across cn-HPCs.

Additionally, we performed UMAP on the genes from our bulk RNA-seq data, resulting in the creation of 20 gene modules (Fig. 4d). Each module was then labeled according to the major pathways identified through gene set enrichment analysis, providing insight into the predominant functional themes of each cluster (see Methods; Supplementary Figs. 10–14). We generated gene module scores by averaging the expression of the genes within each module and computed Pearson correlations between patient gene module scores and patient cn-HPC composition to examine pathway activations and suppressions in each cn-HPC (Fig. 4e). The gene module activation patterns differed across cn-HPC0, cn-HPC23, and cn-HPC24, and corresponded to their distinct cellular compositions. Specifically, cn-HPC0 correlated strongly with modules related to cellular growth (M12), inflammatory response (M5), extracellular matrix activity (M3), and inflammatory immune activation (M2). In contrast, cn-HPC23 was associated with activation of modules related to proliferation (M16), immunodeficiency (M8), and detoxification (M4), and with suppression of immune-related modules (M2, M5, M15). Similarly, cn-HPC24 was associated with suppression of metabolic immune dysregulation (M15), inflammatory response (M5), immune response (M10), and growth/motility modules (M12–M14).

Given the spatial heterogeneity of LUAD, we sought to validate and refine these associations using spatial proteomics. We leveraged our NYU LUAD Stage I cohort with matched H&E and NanoString GeoMx® Digital Spatial Profiler (DSP)36 data on tissue microarrays (TMAs). For each TMA core, we aligned H&E images with GeoMx fluorescent images (Fig. 4a), and correlated region of interest (ROI)-level cn-HPC composition with ROI-level protein expression in PanCK⁺ (tumor) and CD45⁺ (immune) compartments (Fig. 4b,c).

This spatial analysis revealed that cn-HPC0 is positively associated with T-cell and apoptosis markers in PanCK⁺ regions and shows a trend toward improved survival in this early-stage cohort (Kaplan–Meier p = 0.082). We also identified cn-HPC4, which correlates negatively with T-cell and apoptosis markers in CD45⁺ regions, and is significantly associated with worse survival in both the NYU Stage I cohort (p = 0.04) and TCGA-LUAD (p = 0.009).

Bulk transcriptomic deconvolution and spatial profiling revealed complementary associations between cn-HPC composition, immune marker localization, and survival. The integration of both approaches offers complementary insights into how histomorphological patterns interface with molecular states and patient outcomes—highlighting candidates such as cn-HPC0 (immune-active) and cn-HPC4 (immune-silent) that may have prognostic or therapeutic relevance.

Exploratory testing of cn-HPC predictive associations in an independent LUAD immunotherapy cohort

To explore the relevance of HPC and cn-HPC profiles in a clinical immunotherapy setting, we analyzed a cohort of LUAD patients treated with immune checkpoint inhibitors from New York University Langone Health (NYULH). After excluding post-treatment and non-primary (non-lung/non-pleural) samples, 148 pre-treatment WSIs were retained for analysis (Fig. 5a). Patient response was defined using best overall response (BOR), with partial or complete/stable response considered “responders”.

We applied the original and cell-neighborhood HPL pipelines to this cohort and projected tiles into pretrained models (see Methods), assigning HPCs and cn-HPCs accordingly. Tile composition differences between responders and non-responders were tested using Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, and AUCs were computed based on rank distributions (Supplementary Fig. 15).

Among HPCs, HPC 42 was significantly enriched in non-responders (p = 5.7e-05) and yielded a predictive AUC of 0.825 (Fig. 5b). While HPC 42 was previously annotated as cartilage in TCGA-LUAD, most NYULH cohort tiles assigned to this cluster resembled smooth muscle histology.

cn-HPC 23 was significantly enriched in non-responders (p = 0.018) and showed a predictive AUC of 0.725 (Fig. 5c). In contrast to HPC 42, cn-HPC 23 tiles were histologically and spatially consistent across both TCGA-LUAD and NYULH cohorts. Upon review by our expert pathologist, the tiles classified under cn-HPC 23 from both the TCGA-LUAD and NYULH cohorts consistently exhibited hallmark features of aggressive tumor regions. These included extensive tumor necrosis accompanied by ghost cells, hemorrhage with extravasated red blood cells, and dense sheets of atypical tumor cells characterized by high nuclear-to-cytoplasmic ratios. The tumor areas showed high cellularity with minimal preservation of normal alveolar architecture. In addition, many tiles displayed prominent infiltrates of neutrophils and/or chronic inflammatory cells. Tiles assigned to cn-HPC 23 consistently exhibited necrosis, ghost cells, hemorrhage, and atypical tumor morphology across both cohorts.

To contextualize these findings, we compared the predictive performance of cn-HPC 23 with LUAD mutation status. Among individual mutations, KRAS yielded the highest AUC (0.59), while a combined model of multiple LUAD mutations (ALK, EGFR, KRAS, STK11, PD-L1, etc.) reached an AUC of 0.657 (Supplementary Table 3, Supplementary Fig. 16). The AUC of cn-HPC 23 (0.725) was higher than that of KRAS alone (0.59) or the combined mutation model (0.657).

Spatial profiling of gene expression in tumor regions enriched for cn-HPC 23 in independent LUAD immunotherapy cohort

To further characterize the molecular features of cn-HPC 23, we performed spatial transcriptomic profiling on tumor regions from five NYULH patients using the NanoString GeoMx® DSP36 Only regions enriched in CK⁺ tumor cells and derived from lung or pleural tissue were included (Fig. 5d). Correlating gene expression with cn-HPC 23 abundance across regions revealed a significant enrichment of genes involved in TNF-α signaling via NF-κB (Fig. 5e).

We also examined this TNF-α/NF-κB signature in bulk RNA-seq data from the TCGA-LUAD cohort. No significant correlation was observed between the signature and the proportion of cn-HPC 23 tiles per slide. In contrast, stronger associations were seen with other cn-HPCs such as cn-HPC 0 and cn-HPC 12.

Discussion

Our study introduces an innovative approach to exploring the LUAD tumor microenvironment (TME) by constructing an unbiased atlas of cell neighborhoods using contrastive learning. This atlas enables systematic identification of cellular neighborhoods (cn-HPCs) and their integration into histologic patterns, providing a multiscale view of the spatial and cellular organization of LUAD. Through this framework, we elucidate how fine-grained cellular interactions contribute to histologic heterogeneity, immune evasion, and clinical outcomes.

A key contribution of our work is the demonstration that cn-HPCs refine and enrich conventional histopathological phenotypes. Unlike broader HPCs, cn-HPCs capture cellular-scale features such as immune infiltration, necrosis, and vascular organization. This granularity reveals distinct tumor-immune niches associated with differential patient prognosis. For example, cn-HPC 0, associated with TIL-enriched, immune-active environments, was linked to improved survival. Its enrichment in Tip-like endothelial cells and inflammatory gene modules suggests a vascularized, pro-inflammatory microenvironment that may promote immune trafficking and tumor containment.

In contrast, cn-HPC 23 consistently exhibited hallmark features of aggressive tumor regions, including extensive necrosis, hemorrhage, ghost cells, and atypical tumor morphology. These features were reviewed by a pathologist and matched across datasets, suggesting a reproducible histomorphological pattern. Biologically, necrotic regions within tumors are often associated with rapid cell proliferation exceeding vascular supply, resulting in hypoxia and cell death37–39. Such regions may also contribute to immune suppression by attracting immunosuppressive cells or releasing pro-tumorigenic factors40. These mechanisms may help explain the consistent association of cn-HPC 23 with poor prognosis and resistance to immunotherapy.

Beyond its morphological and transcriptomic features, cn-HPC 23 was also enriched in ciliated cells, NK cells, and T lymphocytes—suggesting a microenvironment characterized by immune cell infiltration. However, this immune presence may not reflect effective surveillance. Instead, it may represent a reactive but functionally exhausted immune landscape. The presence of NK and T lymphocytes in this context could indicate immune recognition of tumor antigens, but a failure to eliminate tumor cells due to T-cell exhaustion. This is supported by prior studies showing that exhausted T cells often overexpress inhibitory receptors (e.g., PD-1, TIM-3) and exhibit diminished cytokine production, thereby weakening anti-tumor immunity41. These immunological features may further explain the association between cn-HPC 23 and poor outcomes, and underscore the importance of spatial context when interpreting immune infiltration in LUAD.

We also identified other spatial phenotypes of interest. cn-HPC 11, which shared immune colocalization with cn-HPC 0, showed fewer neoplastic cells and more necrotic-inflammatory interactions, possibly representing an advanced immune clearance state. cn-HPC 24, while associated with worse survival, lacked necrosis and was infiltrated by inflammatory cells within tumor bundles—suggesting a tumor-immune standoff where immune presence fails to elicit cytotoxic effects. These distinctions underscore the value of spatial profiling in resolving microenvironments where immune infiltration does not equate to immune efficacy.

Importantly, not all survival-associated cn-HPCs were predictive of immunotherapy response. cn-HPCs such as 0, 11, and 24 were significantly associated with overall survival but did not show strong correlations with response to immune checkpoint blockade. This apparent disconnect likely reflects distinct underlying biological processes: while some cn-HPCs capture tumor-intrinsic features like necrosis or proliferation that influence long-term prognosis, others may directly modulate immune surveillance or therapeutic resistance. Notably, cn-HPC 23 was both prognostic and predictive, exhibiting an AUC of 0.725 for immunotherapy response. Its consistent morphology across cohorts and spatial correlation with immune-suppressive inflammation support its role as a robust marker of aggressive, therapy-resistant tumor niches.

The robustness of these spatial phenotypes was further supported by their validation across independent LUAD cohorts. In the Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN) LUAD dataset, cn-HPC 23 was entirely absent—consistent with the fact that all patients in this cohort were tumor-free at last follow-up and lacked necrotic histology upon expert review. This observation aligns with our interpretation of cn-HPC 23 as a marker of aggressive, therapy-resistant disease. In the NYU Stage I LUAD cohort, we observed significant associations between cn-HPC 0 and improved survival, and between cn-HPC 24 and worse survival—paralleling results from the Stage I TCGA-LUAD subset and reinforcing their relevance in early-stage disease. Interestingly, cn-HPCs 11 and 23 were not prognostic in the NYU cohort, contrasting with their associations in the full TCGA dataset. This may reflect their enrichment in necrotic or aggressive tumor states, which are less prevalent in early-stage tumors. These findings underscore the stage-specific relevance of certain spatial microenvironments and highlight the reproducibility of key cn-HPCs across datasets with varying clinical characteristics.

We also examined transcriptional signatures associated with cn-HPCs using both bulk RNA-seq and spatial transcriptomic profiling. In bulk data, gene module activation patterns aligned with expected cellular compositions—for instance, inflammatory and extracellular matrix modules in cn-HPC 0, and proliferation/immunodeficiency modules in cn-HPC 23. However, spatial profiling revealed signals undetectable in bulk: in tumor regions enriched for cn-HPC 23, we observed a significant enrichment of genes involved in TNF-α signaling via NF-κB (Fig. 5e), a pathway previously linked to chronic inflammation and immune resistance42. While this signature was detected in spatial data, it was not associated with cn-HPC 23 abundance in TCGA bulk RNA-seq, instead correlating with other cn-HPCs such as 0 and 12. This discrepancy underscores the added value of spatial transcriptomic profiling, which enables detection of region-specific inflammatory programs that may be diluted or obscured in whole-tissue RNA measurements. Notably, the presence of the TNF-α/NF-κB signature in tumor regions mirrors prior findings in tumor-adjacent lung tissue, where chronic inflammation was linked to immune evasion and adverse outcomes in LUAD20. These parallels suggest that localized inflammatory signaling within tumors—particularly in necrotic, immune-excluded niches like cn-HPC 23—may contribute to resistance against immune checkpoint therapy.

To further strengthen our spatial insights, we extended our analysis to the NYU Stage I LUAD cohort with matched H&E and GeoMx proteomic data. This analysis revealed that cn-HPC 0 colocalized with T-cell and apoptosis markers in tumor regions, supporting its role as an immune-accessible niche. Conversely, cn-HPC 4—marked by negative correlation with immune proteins in non-tumor regions—emerged as a consistent marker of immune exclusion and poor outcome across both early- and mixed-stage LUAD datasets. These results suggest that some cn-HPCs (e.g., cn-HPC 0 and 4) may represent conserved spatial phenotypes with consistent prognostic relevance across disease stages and cohorts.

In our NYULH immunotherapy cohort, we also identified HPC 42 as a strong predictor of non-response (AUC = 0.825), despite its original annotation as cartilage in the TCGA-LUAD dataset. Interestingly, most tiles assigned to HPC 42 in the NYULH cohort resembled smooth muscle histology upon review. This discrepancy may reflect differences in tissue processing, staining, or sampling between cohorts, which can influence histological appearance and cluster assignment. Nonetheless, the consistent association of HPC 42 with poor immunotherapy response highlights its potential clinical relevance, regardless of precise tissue annotation.

We further contextualized the predictive utility of cn-HPCs using LUAD mutation status. Logistic regression models trained on individual gene mutations (e.g., EGFR, KRAS, TP53) or combined panels achieved modest predictive performance (AUCs ~ 0.59–0.66), whereas cn-HPC 23 alone reached an AUC of 0.725. While these mutations are not validated predictors of immunotherapy response and are typically used for guiding treatment selection, our exploratory comparison suggests that spatially derived histological features may offer complementary information for identifying patients likely to benefit from immunotherapy. Rather than replacing molecular profiling, spatial tissue context could enhance patient stratification and capture tumor-immune dynamics not reflected in mutation status alone.

Overall, our study underscores the utility of fine-grained, multiscale mapping to dissect the LUAD TME. By bridging tissue-level phenotypes and localized cellular niches, we reveal how diverse tumor microenvironments shape disease trajectory and therapeutic response. Although our spatial transcriptomic findings are limited by the small number of profiled patients, they highlight the promise of localized gene–tile correlation frameworks in uncovering region-specific immune resistance mechanisms. Future studies with larger spatially resolved cohorts and multi-modal integration will be critical to validating these findings and translating them into clinically actionable biomarkers.

Ultimately, this cell-neighborhood atlas framework can serve as a foundation for precision oncology in LUAD and potentially other cancer types. Extending this methodology to pan-cancer settings may reveal conserved or divergent cellular ecosystems underlying tumor progression, immune escape, and treatment failure—informing new strategies for targeted therapy and patient stratification.

Methods

Dataset

This study was based on publicly available data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) lung adenocarcinoma study. We retrieved 541 H&E-stained formalin-fixed and paraffin-embedded (FFPE) WSIs from 478 patients via the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) Portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/) and transcriptomic data from 515 patients via the UCSC Xena platform43.

External validation datasets

HTAN LUAD cohort

To explore external generalizability, we analyzed data from the Human Tumor Atlas Network (HTAN) lung adenocarcinoma atlas31–33. For this study, we focused on a subset of 20 pre-treatment H&E-stained FFPE LUAD WSIs from 8 patients, sourced from the HTAN Data Coordinating Center (https://humantumoratlas.org/). All patients were tumor-free at last follow-up.

NYULH LUAD stage I cohort

To assess prognostic value in early-stage disease, we analyzed a large cohort of LUAD Stage I patients from NYU Langone Health. This dataset includes 276 diagnostic H&E-stained FFPE WSIs from 276 unique patients diagnosed with pathologic Stage I LUAD. TMAs were extracted from 98 of those patients’ tissue samples. From those TMAs, we acquired FFPE H&E-stained images and fluorescent images along with sequenced protein assays (70 proteins) targeting CK + tumor compartments (epithelial/tumor marker) and CD45 + immune compartments (leukocyte marker) for highly cellular ROIs using the nanoString GeoMx® DSP platform36. The cohort was used previously in Claudio Quiros et al.17 and includes long-term follow-up for survival analysis.

External exploratory immunotherapy dataset

The research conducted adheres to all relevant ethical regulations. All patients signed written informed consent.

To evaluate our findings and their predictive power, we used an independent external cohort from NYULH. All patients in this cohort were diagnosed with LUAD and were treated with either nivolumab or pembrolizumab. The classification of patients to responders and non-responders was based on their best overall response (BOR). Response was defined as either partial or complete/stable response. We collected 192 H&E-stained FFPE WSIs from 139 patients. Slides taken after immunotherapy, and slides from metastatic tissues except for the pleura were excluded. 148 slides matched our exclusion criteria. Additionally, we sequenced WTA assays for 23 samples targeting CK + tumor cells using the nanoString GeoMx® DSP platform36. We obtained readout expression levels for ~ 16,000 protein coding genes for several ROIs within each sample. ROIs were selected by specialists for their high cell content.

HP-atlas of histomorphological phenotype clusters (HPCs)

In this study, we utilized the Barlow Twins model pretrained on 452 × 452 μm tiles at 5X magnification on TCGA-LUNG H&Es by Claudio Quiros et al., as well as the cluster configurations for removal of background and artifact tiles, and the corresponding 46 annotated HPCs characterizing the landscape of histormophological phenotypes in LUAD that Claudio Quiros et al. provided in their publication17.

Following the identification of broader histomorphological phenotypes in LUAD, we wanted to examine cellular and subcellular details within these patterns. This higher resolution enables a deeper analysis of cell morphology and microenvironmental interactions, essential for validating and refining our previous HPCs.

Cell-neighborhood self-supervised learning

Whole slide image preprocessing

Using DeepPATH5we tiled each WSI into non-overlapping 113 × 113 μm tiles at 20X magnification (0.054 μm/pixel). Tiles with more than 10% background pixels were excluded. Background pixels were defined as pixels with a grey level above 230. We applied Reinhard’s method for stain normalization44.

Cell-neighborhood tiles

We used the pretrained Hover-Net23 model, initially trained on the publicly available PanNuke15 dataset, to perform cell segmentation and classification on our preprocessed tiles. We mapped all the centroids detected back to the WSIs. We generated a new set of tiles centered around each of the centroids at 20x magnification with tile size of 113 × 113 μm, representing large cell neighborhoods. We applied Reinhard’s method for stain normalization44 to the cell-neighborhood sets of tiles.

Histomorphological phenotype learning (HPL) training

We ran the HPL17 pipeline for our set of cell-neighborhood tiles. We split the TCGA-LUAD patients into training (80%) and validation (20%) sets. For the training set, we randomly selected ~ 50,000 tiles from each cell type (necrotic, neoplastic, non-neoplastic, inflammatory, connective). For the validation set, we randomly selected ~ 13,000 tiles from each cell type. We ran training of the self-supervised model Barlow Twins25 for 60 epochs and a batch size of 64.

We randomly sampled an additional ~ 137,000 tiles per cell type from all TCGA-LUAD patients and projected them as well as the training and validation sets into the frozen trained networks. We generated tile vector representations with 128 features.

HP-Atlas of cn-HPCs

Using k-nearest neighbors and Leiden community detection26 on the training set, we identified 27 cn-HPCs and assigned them to the validation and test sets.

Correlation between HPCs and cn-HPCs

Using the TCGA-LUAD H&E WSIs, we mapped the 20x cn-HPC tiles back to their corresponding 5x HPC tiles. We computed the percentage of tiles from each 20x cn-HPC overlapping with each 5x HPC, showing the correlation between different types of cell interactions and histomorphological and phenotype patterns.

To further characterize the relationships between tissue-level HPCs and their constituent cell neighborhoods, we computed two complementary spatial representations: HPC fingerprints and cn-HPC interaction graphs.

We constructed cn-HPC interaction graphs, where each node represents a cn-HPC and edges connect pairs of cn-HPCs co-occurring within the same tile. Edge weights were defined as the product of the instance counts between each pair of cn-HPCs within that tile, reflecting interaction frequency. Node attributes encoded spatial position (centroid) and relative abundance. To summarize cellular interactions across all 5× tiles within an HPC, we computed a consensus interaction graph by averaging adjacency matrices from individual tile-level graphs. The resulting graph preserved node connectivity and edge weight statistics across tiles, with node sizes indicating mean abundance and edge widths scaled by average co-occurrence frequency. Low-frequency interactions were filtered using an empirical threshold (80th percentile) to retain dominant interaction patterns.

In parallel, we generated HPC fingerprints—tile-level heatmaps that represent pairwise co-occurrence frequencies between cn-HPCs. We aggregated adjacency matrices for each HPC and visualized these fingerprints as clustered heatmaps. To compare interaction patterns between different HPCs, we aligned and contrasted their consensus adjacency matrices, computing difference matrices.

These spatial graph and fingerprint analyses provided insights into how diverse cellular neighborhoods organize within and across histological phenotypes, revealing the multiscale architecture of LUAD.

To systematically compare the fingerprint structure of all HPCs, we constructed a dendrogram based on the similarity of their cn-HPC interaction graphs. This dendrogram defined the display order for all HPC fingerprints. For each HPC, the fingerprint heatmap was normalized by its number of cn-HPC tiles. This integrated view of cn-HPC relationships across HPCs enabled multiscale comparison of LUAD histomorphological patterns.

Downstream cell analysis

For visualization, we applied dimensionality reduction using UMAP (where each point represents a tile) on the vector representations of all cell-neighborhood tiles. We defined patient cn-HPC representations with the number of cn-HPCs as dimensionality and each dimension defined as the percentage contribution of a HPC to the total number of tiles from that patient.

To characterize the cellular makeup and spatial organization of each cn-HPC, we leveraged Hover-Net cell segmentation and classification outputs. For each cell-centered tile, we aggregated the cell-type labels predicted by Hover-Net to obtain a per-tile count of each cell category (e.g., neoplastic, inflammatory, connective, necrotic). To compute the cellular composition of a cn-HPC, we pooled all tiles assigned to that cn-HPC and calculated the proportion of each cell type relative to the total number of classified cells across the tiles. This enabled comparison of cell-type enrichment patterns across cn-HPCs.

Additionally, we asked a lung specialist to review all cn-HPCs and annotate them.

Cell-cell interaction

To assess spatial organization, we constructed a cell–cell adjacency matrix for each tile. Cells were considered adjacent if their centroids were within ~ 15 μm pixels of each other. We then quantified the density of cell-cell interactions within each tile as the number of edges connecting two cells, normalized by the total number of interactions. These interaction scores were averaged across tiles in each cn-HPC to derive a cluster-level interaction metric. This spatial measure provides additional insight into histological organization, complementing compositional summaries by capturing how cell types are spatially arranged.

Survival analysis

For each cn-HPC, survival analysis was conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method to estimate and visualize survival probabilities over time. The log-rank test was employed to compare survival distributions between patients within the top quartile tile and patients within the bottom quartile of a cn-HPC, assessing whether the differences observed in the survival curves were statistically significant. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05.

Tumor region and immune proximity analysis

To identify and retain biologically meaningful tumor regions for downstream analyses, we implemented a spatial filtering approach based on epithelial cell density and spatial coherence. For each slide, we quantified the epithelial cell density in each tile by counting epithelial centroids normalized by tile size. Binary patch masks were generated by thresholding the density values. To reduce noise and exclude isolated tumor-like tiles, we applied connected component analysis using 8-connectivity and retained only regions with at least two connected tiles. The resulting tumor masks were saved per slide and used to restrict downstream analyses to contiguous tumor regions.

To assess the immune microenvironment across cellular neighborhoods, we computed the spatial proximity between immune cells and cn-HPC tiles across all slides. Using tile-level cluster assignments and the tumor masks, we distinguished tiles located within versus outside tumor regions. Immune cell locations were obtained from HoverNet-derived cell summaries. We constructed spatial k-d trees for each cn-HPC and quantified the fraction of immune cells within 100 microns of each neighborhood, both globally and conditional on tumor localization. We also computed the mean distance of each cn-HPC tile to the nearest tumor region. Finally, we recorded the fraction of each cn-HPC’s tiles that were located within the tumor mask. Together, these features provided a multiscale view of immune infiltration and tumor association across cellular neighborhoods.

Molecular landscape of cn-HPCs

Correlation of cn-HPCs with protein expression

In our NYU LUAD Stage 1 cohort, we aligned the TMA’s molecular and histomorphological data by registering H&E and fluorescent images using a predetermined rotation angle and additional image processing. Each ROI from the GeoMx platform was mapped onto the aligned H&E section to compute the cn-HPC composition, defined as the relative frequency of each histomorphological cluster within the ROI.

Protein expression values were background-corrected, normalized using NanoString DSP processing pipelines, and log2-transformed with a pseudocount of 1. Marker expression was retained separately for PanCK⁺ (tumor) and CD45⁺ (immune) compartments within each ROI.

To assess spatial associations between cn-HPCs and molecular signals, spearman correlations were computed between centered-log-ratio-normalized cn-HPC proportions and protein expression values across all ROIs. Analyses were stratified by compartment (PanCK⁺ vs. PanCK⁻) to distinguish tumor-intrinsic from tumor-adjacent immune patterns. We adjusted the p-values using the False Discovery Rate method (FDR) and statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p-value less than 0.05.

Correlation of cn-HPCs with cell types deconvoluted using bayesprism

In a previous study20we applied BayesPrism34 on the TCGA-LUAD bulk RNA-seq data in combination with a LUAD scRNA-seq dataset35 as a reference to deconvolve cell type proportions. We ran Pearson correlation between the resulting cell type percentages from that study and the cn-HPC tile percentages. We adjusted the p-values using the False Discovery Rate method (FDR) and statistical significance was defined as an adjusted p-value less than 0.05.

Co-expressed gene modules

We computed the variance for each gene across TCGA-LUAD patients and selected the top quartile genes. We applied z-score normalization, ran principal component analysis (PCA), and generated 20 clusters. We ran gene set enrichment analysis using clusterProfiler45 to label each gene module with their most relevant pathways based on Hallmarks of Cancer46 Gene Ontology terms47,48 and KEGG pathway annotations49–51 (no original KEGG pathway images were used). We created module scores for each TCGA-LUAD patient and ran Pearson correlation between the module scores and the cn-HPC tile percentages. We corrected all p-values using FDR. P-values were defined as significant if their adjusted self was lower or equal to 0.05.

Mapping external validation datasets to LUAD HPCs and cn-HPCs

LUAD HPCs correlation with response to immunotherapy

We used DeepPATH5 to tile each WSI of our external validation cohort into non-overlapping 452 × 452 μm tiles at 5X magnification (2.016 μm/pixel). Tiles with more than 10% background pixels were excluded. Background pixels were defined as pixels with a grey level above 230. We applied stain normalization to all tiles44.

All tiles were projected into a frozen Barlow Twins model pretrained on 5X tiles from the TCGA-LUNG H&Es17 to generate tile vector representations. Using k-nearest neighbors, we assigned the cluster configurat ions for removal of background and artifact tiles to our tile vector representations and removed the tiles corresponding to the predetermined background and artifact clusters. Applying k-nearest neighbors a second time on our filtered tile vector representations, we assigned the existing LUAD HPCs to each tile of our external dataset and defined HPC patient representations. For each HPC, we performed a Wilcoxon test comparing tile percentages between responders and non-responders and calculated rank-based AUCs for predicting immunotherapy response using patient HPC tile percentages.

cn-HPCs validation in external LUAD cohorts

Like our cell-centered HPL preprocessing pipeline, using DeepPATH5 and Hover-Net27 we generated cell-centered tiles with tile size 113 × 113 μm at 20X magnification from our three external datasets, and projected them into the pretrained cell-neighborhood network to generate tile vector representations. Using k-nearest neighbors, we assigned the cn-HPCs to each tile of our external datasets and defined cn-HPC patient representations.

For the NYULH Stage I cohort, we conducted the same survival analysis as we did for the TCGA-LUAD cohort. We also subset the TCGA-LUAD cohort into Stage I cases and repeated the survival analysis.

For the NYULH immunotherapy cohort, for each cn-HPC, we performed a Wilcoxon test comparing tile percentages between responders and non-responders and calculated rank-based AUCs for predicting immunotherapy response using patient cn-HPC tile percentages. To compare the predictive value of these spatial features with respect to common LUAD driver genes (EGFR, KRAS, TP53, STK11, KEAP1, SMARCA4, BRAF), we also built logistic regression models using binary mutation status as predictors of response.

Pathway analysis of enriched genes for each cn-HPC

After performing Q3 normalization on the WTA data from the NYULH cohort, we selected the WTA data for the 5 patients who had slides from the lung or pleura. For our cn-HPCs of interest, we calculated the correlation between each gene and the tile percentages. We then conducted gene set enrichment analysis on the significantly positively correlated genes, focusing on the hallmarks of cancer.

Software

The WSIs preprocessing code and HPL pipeline were developed in Python. The deep learning pipeline was implemented in TensorFlow, with Leiden clustering performed using ScanPy, following the original study. All downstream analyses were conducted in R. UMAPs were generated with the uwot package, cell-cell interaction plots were created using circlize, and gene set enrichment analysis was performed with clusterProfiler45.
