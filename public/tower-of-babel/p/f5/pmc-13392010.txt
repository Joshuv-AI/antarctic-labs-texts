A systematic review examining the association between parity and gravidity & hypertension and blood pressure
Authors: Nabilah Gulamhusein, Badal S. B. Pattar, Sandra M. Dumanski, Tyrone G. Harrison, Sonia Butalia, Magali Robert, Jane Schulz, Sofia B. Ahmed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Parity and gravidity have been shown to be associated with hypertension and changes in blood pressure, but results are conflicting. We aimed to estimate the association between gravidity and parity with hypertension and blood pressure. A systematic search of multiple databases was performed. Studies were included if they reported on parity or gravidity and hypertension or blood pressure. Study characteristics were extracted and estimates were pooled using random-effects meta-analysis. A meta-analysis of 18 studies demonstrated that parity was associated with greater odds of hypertension (pooled OR = 1.31;95%CI:1.14,1.50;I2 = 90.4%) when compared to nulliparity. There was no significant difference in odds of hypertension at a parity of 1 or 2; at a parity of 3 and 4, odds of hypertension were significantly higher when compared to nulliparity. Results suggest that parity is associated with hypertension risk in a graded manner, however, there was high heterogeneity. The mechanism remains unknown but is likely multifactorial.

Introduction

Globally, hypertension is a leading risk factor for cardiovascular disease and premature death in women1. Although there are recognized differences in blood pressure (BP) and hypertension between female and male individuals2,3, there is minimal consideration of sex and gender in hypertension management guidelines4. A joint Presidential Advisory from the American Heart Association and American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists has highlighted the importance of recognizing connections between female reproductive health factors and cardiovascular outcomes5. However, how female-specific risk factors contribute to the development of hypertension remains under-studied1,6, underscoring the need to identify and understand how female reproductive factors may contribute to hypertension and changes in BP.

Pregnancy is common during the female reproductive lifespan with global fertility rates of 2.2 births per woman7. While complications of pregnancy, such as hypertensive disorders of pregnancy and gestational diabetes, are associated with hypertension in later life8–10, the relationship between gravidity and parity with blood pressure and risk of hypertension are less clear. Although a greater risk of hypertension with parity when compared to nulliparity has been described11, other studies have reported an inverse relationship, showing a significant decrease in BP and risk of hypertension following pregnancy12,13.

Given the relative paucity of evidence in this area, the objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was to evaluate the association between gravidity and parity with hypertension and BP.

Eight studies15,18,22,24,26,35,41,43 reported the number of post-menopausal participants, ranging from 32% to 100% of the study population. Seven studies reported on menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) use14,18,20,22,24,26,42, in which MHT users made up 13% to 57% of the study population. Use of oral contraception was reported in 6 studies and ranged from 10% to 57%17,24,26,27,33,42. No studies reported on pregnancy-related complications or use of assisted reproductive technologies (ART).

Seventeen studies reported the prevalence of diabetes14–16,19,21,24–27,32–34,38,40,42–44, and 22 reported the prevalence of hypertension11,14–16,18–21,23–27,29,30,32,33,37,38,42–44. Only 6 studies described other baseline cardiovascular conditions (e.g., myocardial infarction, heart failure) in study population15,16,21,24,28,43.

Study appraisal demonstrated that 30 were fair quality, while three were poor quality (Tables S1, S2). No cohort or cross-sectional study provided a power calculation or sample size justification. Few studies accounted for important confounders, including physical activity and menopause, in their analyses (n = 2). However, majority of studies clearly stated the objective or research question (n = 32) and clearly defined the study population (n = 29).

Multiple definitions of parity were used across studies (Table S3), the most common being “number of live births” (n = 10)11,22,24,33–36,40,41,43. Three studies did not provide a definition of parity25,38,39. The definition of gravidity used in all studies was “number of pregnancies” (n = 3)13,20,27.

Definitions of parity are displayed in Table S3. Five studies compared any parity to nulliparity15,21,26,38,43. Thirteen of the studies could be categorized into the following parity groups: 0, 1, 2, 3, ≥418,24,25,29–31,33–36,39,40,42.

Definitions of hypertension are listed in Table S3. The most common definition of hypertension was a “SBP ≥140 mmHg and/or a DBP of ≥90 mmHg or self-reported use of antihypertensive medications” (n = 5)11,28,32,38,42. One study defined hypertension as ≥140 or ≥90 mmHg33, while two studies defined hypertension as ≥160/95 mmHg19,20.

Data on the risk of hypertension with any parity compared to nulliparity are summarized in Fig. 2. Eighteen studies were included in the meta-analysis examining the odds of hypertension with parity compared to nulliparity. The pooled odds of hypertension were 1.31 (95% CI: 1.14 to 1.50) with parity when compared to nulliparity (Fig. 2); however, significant heterogeneity was noted (I2 = 90.4%, p < 0.001). In a sensitivity analysis of studies using the most common definition of parity (e.g., number of live births), the odds of hypertension were significantly higher with parity compared to nulliparity (pooled OR = 1.26; 95%CI: 1.17 to 1.37; I2: 0%; n = 3) (Fig. S4a). Removal of studies where cutoffs defining hypertension included SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg or studies deemed to be of poor quality did not significantly change results (Figs. S5a, S6a). Univariable meta-regression revealed that year of publication accounted for some heterogeneity, but not sample size, BMI or age (Table S7).Fig. 2Odds of hypertension for parity compared to nulliparity.

Stratifying by parity number (e.g., 1, 2, 3, ≥4) compared to nulliparity showed that greater parity was associated with increasingly higher odds of developing hypertension (Fig. 3). There was no significant difference in odds of hypertension at parity of 1 (pooled OR = 1.05; 95%CI: 0.80 to 1.36; I2: 84.8%; n = 6) or 2 (pooled OR = 1.18; 95%CI: 0.83 to 1.67; I2: 93.7%; n = 6) compared to nulliparity (Fig. 3a, b). However, with parity of 3 (pooled OR = 1.44; 95%CI: 1.01 to 2.05; I2: 93.4%; n = 6) and 4 (pooled OR = 1.88; 95%CI: 1.13 to 3.13; I2: 96.9%; n = 6), the odds of hypertension were significantly higher compared to nulliparity (Fig. 3c, d). When stratifying studies by country income, HIC (pooled OR = 1.14; 95%CI: 1.05 to 1.24; I2: 67.1%; n = 13), upper-MIC (pooled OR = 1.48; 95%CI: 1.13 to 1.94; I2: 0%; n = 2) and LIC (pooled OR = 2.25; 95%CI: 1.50 to 3.38; I2: 0%; n = 1) showed any parity was associated with increasingly higher odds of hypertension; no association was observed in studies in LMIC (pooled OR = 2.08; 95%CI: 0.69 to 6.26; I2: 96.3%; n = 2) (Fig. 4). Meta-analysis (n = 16 studies) examining the weighted mean difference in SBP showed no significant difference with any type of parity compared to nulliparity (SBP: 0.54 mmHg, 95%CI: -0.40 mmHg to 1.48 mmHg; I2 = 70.1%, p < 0.001) with parity (Fig. S10a). No significant differences in weighted mean difference of SBP were observed (Fig. S11) with stratification by parity number. In a sensitivity analysis of only studies using the most common definition of parity (e.g., number of live births), parity was associated with a greater SBP (1.22 mmHg; 95%CI: 0.23 mmHg to 2.21 mmHg; I2:36.3%; n = 9) (Fig. S4b).

Meta-analysis (n = 16 studies) examining the weighted mean difference in DBP with any type of parity compared to nulliparity showed no differences (95%CI: −0.04 to 0.96; I2 = 77.3%) (Fig. S10b). No significant differences in weighted mean difference of DBP were observed (Fig. S12) with stratification by parity number. In a sensitivity analysis of only studies using the most common definition of parity (e.g., number of live births), no differences in DBP were observed (1.63 mmHg; 95%CI: 0.00 mmHg to 1.26 mmHg) (Fig. S4c).

A prospective cohort study of 45 individuals demonstrated that parity was associated with decreased MAP21.

In a retrospective-cohort study (n = 289,573), a J-shaped association was observed between gravidity and hypertension; the lowest prevalence of hypertension occurred at gravidity of 127.

Two prospective cohort studies (n = 1093, n = 2357) reported no association between gravidity and SBP13,20. In a retrospective-cohort study (n = 289,573), a J-shaped association between gravidity and SBP was reported, with the lowest measures occurring at gravidity of 1 (Table S13)27.

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, we assessed the association between parity and gravidity with hypertension and blood pressure. Our key findings were as follows: (1) parity was associated with greater odds of hypertension compared to nulliparity; (2) parity of three or more was associated with increasingly higher odds of hypertension; (3) the odds of hypertension associated with parity may be greater in lower compared to higher income countries. Our findings suggest that parity may impact future maternal hypertension risk, with potential variation of relationship strength by country of residence playing a role in mediating this risk. Given the increasing global prevalence of hypertension45, these findings may have important implications for prenatal, peripartum, and postpartum care, as well as risk factor screening and stratification.

Globally, an estimated 600 million women are hypertensive1, highlighting the urgent need to examine female sex-specific factors contributing to hypertension risk. While pregnancy complications46,47 are associated with future hypertension risk, less is known about how parity and gravidity may affect blood pressure. Normal pregnancy is characterized by changes in lipid metabolism with maternal health implications48,49, lipid derangements are associated with adverse pregnancy outcomes and possible residual vascular damage, which increase future hypertension risk. Similarly, insulin resistance is characteristic of healthy pregnancies50. Number of children is associated with increased diabetes prevalence in women but not men, and grand multiparity is associated with the highest Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance scores34, suggesting biological explanations for associations between increased parity and hypertension51. Moreover, multiparity is associated with obesity28, which in turn can contribute to hypertension52. In the 2019–2021 India National Family and Health Survey, higher parity and pregnancy losses were each associated with at least one high-risk cardiometabolic cluster53. Finally, animal studies suggest that multiparity may lead to enhanced reactive oxygen species production and decreased expression of endothelial nitric oxide synthase54 resulting in reduced production of nitric oxide, ultimately contributing to endothelial dysfunction55.

Although biology may contribute, social factors could play a role in associations between parity and hypertension56. In a study of 2851 Iranian couples, increased parity was associated with hypertension in not only mothers but also their male partners11. In a British cross-sectional study of 4286 women and 4252 men, number of children was positively associated with BMI and waist-hip ratio in all participants. While similar “J” shaped associations between number of children and coronary heart disease were observed between women and men, greater risk was observed in women even after adjustment for obesity and metabolic risk factors34. The prevalence of stroke for both women and men is higher for those with more children57. With increasing parity comes more responsibilities, and potentially greater physical and mental stress, which could contribute to increased risk of hypertension58. Socioeconomic position (SEP) may influence the relationship between parity and hypertension, with disadvantaged families being at the greatest risk for hypertension58,59. Of note, an inverse relationship between SEP and family size exists, which may contribute to our observed associations60.

The increasing odds of hypertension with parity compared to nulliparity in lower income countries when compared to higher income countries deserves mention. Globally, two-thirds of hypertension cases are in low- and middle- income countries, largely due to increased risk factor prevalence in recent decades53,61,62. Populations in low- and middle-income countries have greater tobacco and alcohol use, as well as exposure to different dietary patterns, heat stress and air pollution63, while hypertension prevalence has decreased in high-income countries due to improvements in detection, treatment and control61.

This study has limitations. First, there was a lack of consistent definitions for parity and hypertension with significant heterogeneity in meta-analyses. However, sensitivity analyses using only the most common definitions of parity and hypertension did not significantly change results. Next, hypertension and blood pressure were rarely study primary outcomes, and few studies reported coexisting chronic conditions. Furthermore, because the risk of hypertension increases with age, it was not possible to determine the independent contribution of age to hypertension or the timeframe over which hypertension developed after pregnancy. Finally, this review did not include pregnancy-related factors (e.g., adverse pregnancy outcomes64) or (ART use65 as these data were not available in the included studies; however, these represent important areas of future research. This would likely have minimal impact on the observed associations as majority of pregnancies are uncomplicated66 and ART accounts for 0.5% of births worldwide67.

Because pregnancy is common and hypertension is a leading cause of morbidity and mortality, pregnancy history and other female sex-specific factors should be routinely incorporated into research6. Despite the limitations of this systematic review, we observed a clear association between parity and subsequent risk of hypertension. Large, prospective studies using standardized definitions of parity and hypertension, as well as routine incorporation of female-specific factors6,54, are required to fully assess the observed associations to appropriately inform clinical practice and shared decision-making. As pregnancy can have long term health implications5, healthcare professionals should encourage not only post-natal follow-up68,69, but also ongoing blood pressure surveillance across the life course. Educating patients about their individual risk factors for hypertension, potentially including pregnancy, would support informed decision-making. Given the substantial global burden of hypertension in women, continued surveillance and monitoring remain essential.

Methods

This systematic review was reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) checklist70 (Table S14) and Meta-Analysis of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (MOOSE) checklist71 (Table S15). The protocol for this work has been previously published72 and is registered with the Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO; CRD42024560535). This study was guided by a population, exposure, control, outcome and design (PECOD) framework. The population was human females. The primary exposure was parity, and the secondary exposure was gravidity (as defined by study authors), with nulliparity and nulligravidity as controls, respectively. The primary outcome was hypertension (as defined by the study authors) and the secondary outcome was BP (e.g., systolic, diastolic, mean arterial pressure). Observational study designs were considered for inclusion.

An electronic search from inception to January 2025 was completed in Ovid MEDLINE, Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), Excerpta Medica Database (EMBASE), Web of Science and Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) without language or geographic restrictions. The search used the “AND” term to combine medical subject headings and key words to identify the exposures and outcomes. The MEDLINE search strategy (Table S16) was developed with the assistance of a medical librarian and was peer-reviewed by another librarian using the Peer-Review of Electronic Search Strategies (PRESS) guidelines73. The MEDLINE search strategy was then translated to the appropriate terms for the remaining databases (Tables S17–20). The reference lists of included articles were also hand searched. Study authors were contacted for any missing data.

All studies identified through the search strategies were uploaded onto the Covidence platform (Covidence systematic review software, Veritas Health Innovation, Melbourne, Australia) and duplicates were removed. To capture relevant studies, the title and abstract screening was intentionally broad. Any studies reporting on the exposure of parity or gravidity and contained data from an original study were eligible for full-text review. A calibration exercise was completed using the first 100 studies to identify any discrepancies in the screening process between two independent reviewers (NG, BSBP). The remaining titles and abstracts of all uploaded studies were assessed for eligibility independently and in duplicate by the two reviewers. Full texts were then reviewed independently by each reviewer and were eligible for inclusion if they met inclusion criteria. Any conflicts were resolved through discussion or with the involvement of a third reviewer (SBA). If two studies were included but used the same study sample, the study with the largest sample size and/or longest follow-up time was included.

Study quality was assessed in duplicate through critical appraisal using the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Quality Assessment Tool for Observational Cohort and Cross-Sectional Studies and Case-Control Studies74. Any conflicts were resolved through discussion or with involvement of a third reviewer (SBA). Both quality assessment tools were modified to aggregate the question into groups to maintain consistency in reporting of quality assessment75.

Data extraction was completed independently and in duplicate using an Excel spreadsheet (Microsoft Excel, Redmond, WA, Version 16.94), with conflicts resolved through discussion or with the involvement of a third reviewer (SBA). The following variables were extracted: study identifiers (e.g., title, author, year of publication and country), study characteristics (e.g., sample size, study design), sample characteristics (e.g., age, smoking status, body mass index [BMI], comorbid conditions, menopausal status, history of complications of pregnancy), reproductive characteristics (e.g., singleton pregnancy, pre-eclampsia), chronic health conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypertension, kidney disease), exposure variables (parity, gravidity), outcome variables (hypertension, BP) and measures of associations (e.g., odds ratios [ORs], risk ratios). When measures of association were not available, the means and standard deviations of BP or the number of participants with hypertension by parity or gravidity categories were extracted.

For studies reporting dichotomous outcomes, such as diagnosis of hypertension, pooled odds ratios were calculated. When continuous outcomes (e.g., BP) were reported, weighted mean differences were calculated. We used the DerSimonian-Laird random effects model to pool the calculated crude ORs76. Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic and Cochrane Q statistic. A variety of analyses were planned a priori and can be found in detail elsewhere72. Stratified analyses were completed by the following variables: parity number, country income (e.g., low, lower-middle, upper-middle, high)77, geographic location (by continent) and study design. A sensitivity analysis was conducted with the most common definition of parity reported in included studies. Sensitivity analyses were completed by excluding studies that were deemed to be poor study quality and had definitions of hypertension systolic BP (SBP) and diastolic BP (DBP) measures that differ from current definitions ( ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP of ≥90 mmHg)78,79. Univariable meta-regression was completed for the following variables: age, sample size, BMI and year of publication. A two-sided p-value of <0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were conducted using StataBE 17.0 (StataCorp LLC, Texas, USA).
