Association between primary Sjögren’s disease and temporomandibular disorder in Taiwan: a 15-year nationwide cohort, with an exploratory analysis of hydroxychloroquine
Authors: Hsiao Yu, Tsung-Fu Chang, Wei-Zhi Huang, Da-Yo Yuh, Chi-Hsiang Chung, Feng-Cheng Liu, Dun-Yu Hsu, Tsu-Hsuan Weng, Gunng-Shinng Chen, Ling-Yu Kung, Wu-Chien Chien

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe relationship between primary Sjögren’s disease (pSjD) and temporomandibular disorder (TMD) has received little attention in previous studies. Therefore, we investigated this association using data from a large, population-based cohort.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study analyzed data from Taiwan’s Longitudinal Generation Tracking Database for the period 2000–2015. Background

Primary Sjögren’s disease(pSjD), formerly referred to as primary Sjögren’s syndrome, is a chronic autoimmune condition with systemic involvement and lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands. The disease affects approximately 0.09% of the adult population and primarily involves the salivary and lacrimal glands, leading to classic sicca symptoms such as xerostomia (dry mouth) and keratoconjunctivitis sicca (dry eyes) [1–3]. Beyond glandular involvement, pSjD exhibits a wide range of extraglandular manifestations, including neurological abnormalities, renal dysfunction, psychological disorders, and musculoskeletal pain, which substantially impact patients’ quality of life [4–7].

Traditionally, Sjögren’s syndrome has been classified into primary and secondary forms. Primary occurs independently, whereas secondary develops in association with other autoimmune disorders, such as rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), which may complicate diagnosis and clinical management. However, recent expert commentaries have suggested that the traditional distinction between primary and secondary forms may not fully capture the disease’s heterogeneity. Contemporary frameworks propose describing the condition as “primary Sjögren’s disease (pSjD)” occurring alone or “Sjögren’s disease associated with another autoimmune disease,” in line with the 2016 ACR–EULAR criteria, which no longer employ the term “secondary” [8].

The management of pSjD is predominantly supportive and is escalated according to disease severity. It ranges from symptomatic treatments to systemic therapies such as nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs and systemic glucocorticoids (e.g., prednisone). Previous studies involving RA populations have suggested that systemic glucocorticoid therapy does not significantly reduce the risk of temporomandibular disorder (TMD) [9]. In patients with more severe pSjD manifestations, disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs), particularly hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), are commonly prescribed to alleviate fatigue, glandular symptoms, and musculoskeletal discomfort [10, 11]. Given its widespread use in pSjD management, HCQ exposure was recorded for secondary exploratory analysis; however, it was not the primary focus of this investigation.

Temporomandibular disorder represents a diverse set of conditions affecting the temporomandibular joint (TMJ), masticatory muscles, and associated anatomical structures [12, 13]. Clinically, TMD is characterized by symptoms such as jaw discomfort, joint noise, and limited movement of the mandible [14–21]. Although many cases are self-limiting, chronic forms of TMD may lead to persistent orofacial pain and functional impairment. Several contributory factors—including emotional stress, disrupted sleep patterns, and systemic inflammatory disorders such as RA and SLE —are associated with the development of TMD [22].

Because pSjD shares immunopathological characteristics with other autoimmune diseases, including RA, and SLE, it is plausible that pSjD may also predispose individuals to TMD. However, current evidence exploring this association remains limited and inconclusive. Previous studies in RA populations showed that long-term DMARD use was not significantly associated with TMD development. Nevertheless, interpretation of these findings was limited by the absence of TMD cases in some cohorts and by potential confounding related to concurrent therapies [9, 23–26].

To our knowledge, the relationship between pSjD and TMD has not been specifically examined in previous research. Therefore, this study sought to explore the potential association between pSjD and the onset of TMD using data from a large, population-based cohort. Additionally, a secondary analysis was conducted to explore whether HCQ exposure was associated with differences in observed TMD incidence.

Methods

Study design and data source

This retrospective cohort study, based on a population-level dataset, drew on information from Taiwan’s Longitudinal Health Insurance Database (LHID), which is a subset of the broader National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD). The NHIRD encompasses healthcare claims from Taiwan’s single-payer National Health Insurance (NHI) program, which has covered > 99.6% of the population since 1995 and contracts with 97% of healthcare institutions nationwide [27]. The LHID, derived from the Longitudinal Generation Tracking Database (LGTD), comprises longitudinal medical records for two million individuals—a random 10% sample of all NHI enrollees—stratified by sex, age, and location to ensure population representativeness [28–30]. Because the NHIRD provides diagnoses exclusively through the International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-9-CM), all diagnostic information must be coded using this system [31].

While the database does not include lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking and alcohol use) or detailed imaging/laboratory results, its diagnostic accuracy has been repeatedly validated through systematic chart reviews by the NHI Administration, demonstrating > 85% concordance between coded diagnoses and clinical documentation for chronic diseases [32–34]. Given its large-scale and longitudinal design, the database has been widely validated as a credible source of data for analyzing disease associations and therapeutic impacts. The LGTD’s robustness is further supported by its extensive use in peer-reviewed epidemiological studies, including investigations of autoimmune disorders and orofacial pain conditions, and both pSjD and TMD have been the focus of separate database-driven analyses in previous research, consistent with all nationwide epidemiological studies from Taiwan examining orofacial pain or TMJ-related diseases. with the results being published [9, 35–42].

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in full compliance with the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Tri-Service General Hospital (TSGHIRB No.: E202416037). Informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board of Tri-Service General Hospital because the NHIRD provides anonymized secondary data, precluding the identification of individual patients.

Study population

Case identification and validation

Following the contemporary terminology, our study specifically focuses on patients meeting criteria consistent with pSjD, while excluding all other autoimmune diseases to avoid misclassification as “associated Sjögren’s disease” [8]. Individuals newly diagnosed with pSjD between January 1, 2000, and December 31, 2015, were identified based on the ICD-9-CM diagnostic code 710.2. Diagnosis adhered to the American–European Consensus Group (2002) criteria, requiring fulfillment of ≥ 4 of 6 domains: ocular/oral symptoms; objective sicca signs (e.g., Schirmer’s test ≤ 5 mm/5 min); histopathological confirmation (focus score of ≥ 1); salivary dysfunction; and anti-Ro/SSA or anti-La/SSB seropositivity [35, 43, 44].

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if (1) they had overlapping autoimmune diseases, i.e., RA (714.0), SLE (710.0), systemic sclerosis (710.1), polymyositis (710.4), dermatomyositis (714.3), or sarcoidosis (135) [9, 23–26]; (2) they were diagnosed with pSjD before 2000 (n = 31); (3) those with TMD were diagnosed before tracking (ICD-9-CM 524.60–524.69; n = 9); (4) they did not have tracking data (< 1 year; n = 267); (5) they were below 18 years of age (n = 110); or (6) they had missing demographic data (sex unknown; n = 1).

Comparison cohort selection

A control cohort without pSjD was selected and matched to the pSjD group at a 1:4 ratio using propensity score matching. The matching was based on variables including age, sex, index year, urbanization level, and income quintile, following methods previously reported in the literature [30, 45, 46]. In addition, a subgroup analysis was subsequently conducted within the pSjD cohort to examine the potential influence of HCQ use on TMD risk. All cohorts were followed up until a diagnosis of TMD, death, or the end of the observation period on December 31, 2015.

Outcome definition and TMD identification

Primary outcome ascertainment

The primary outcome, incident TMD, was identified using the ICD-9-CM codes 524.60 (unspecified TMD), 524.61–524.64 (specific TMD subtypes: arthralgia, articular disc disorders, and joint sounds), and 524.69 (other dentofacial anomalies). These codes correspond to clinically diagnosed TMD manifestations, including pain, joint dysfunction, and radiographic abnormalities [9, 41, 42].

Diagnosis validation protocol

In Taiwan’s NHI system, TMD diagnoses require confirmation by dentists or oral surgeons within dental departments. This protocol ensures diagnostic reliability by mandating clinical evaluations (e.g., palpation for joint tenderness and assessment of mandibular mobility) and excluding nonspecific orofacial pain diagnoses [9, 41, 42, 47].

Follow-up and censoring

The index date was designated as the date of the first pSjD diagnosis for the case group or the corresponding matched date for the control group. Follow-up continued until the earliest occurrence of any of the following events: (1) first TMD diagnosis; (2) death; (3) NHI program withdrawal; and (4) study endpoint (December 31, 2015).

Censoring at NHI withdrawal accounted for potential loss to follow-up, while temporal alignment between cohorts minimized immortal time bias. This revision clarified TMD subtypes covered by ICD-9-CM codes, emphasized diagnostic rigor through dental specialist involvement, and aligned censoring criteria with cohort study reporting standards (e.g., STROBE). The framework is in line with the outcome definitions found in recent studies on orofacial disorders using NHIRD data.

Covariates and patient characteristics

Demographic and socioeconomic variables

Baseline covariates (based on previous studies) included (1) age, categorized as 18–44, 45–64, and ≥ 65 years [48, 49]; (2) sex, male or female; (3) monthly insured income, stratified as < NT18,000–34,999, and ≥ NT$35,000 [49, 50]; (4) location, categorized as northern, central, southern, eastern Taiwan, and offshore islands [49–51]; (5) urbanization level, classified as levels 1 (most urbanized) to 4 (least urbanized) based on population density and infrastructure [49, 50, 52, 53]; (6) level of care, classified as that provided by local clinics, regional hospitals, or medical centers, reflecting Taiwan’s hierarchical healthcare system [49, 51, 54].

Comorbidity assessment

Comorbidities were identified using ICD-9-CM codes during the 12-month pre-index period. The chronic conditions identified were diabetes mellitus (250.x), hypertension (401.x–405.x), depression (296.2–296.3, 300.4, 311), insomnia (307.4, 780.5), stroke (430.x–438.x), dementia (290.x), chronic kidney disease (585.x), lipid profile abnormalities (272), anxiety (300.0) and cardiovascular disease (410–414). The validation criteria for these comorbidities were ≥ 2 outpatient diagnoses or 1 inpatient admission for each condition [27–29].

Statistical analysis

We conducted a time-to-event analysis with incident TMD as the outcome. For unadjusted comparisons, we plotted Kaplan–Meier curves and compared groups using the log-rank test. For adjusted associations, we fitted Cox proportional hazards models to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). The time scale was days from the index date to the earliest of: first TMD diagnosis, death, NHI withdrawal, or December 31, 2015. Censoring was applied accordingly. Model covariates were prespecified from prior literature and included age group (18–44, 45–64, ≥ 65 years), sex, monthly insured income (< NT18,000–34,999; ≥NT$35,000), region (north/central/south/east/offshore), urbanization level (1–4), level of care (clinic/regional hospital/medical center), and comorbidities (diabetes, hypertension, depression, insomnia, stroke, dementia, chronic kidney disease, lipid profile abnormalities, anxiety, and cardiovascular disease.) defined via ICD-9-CM codes within the 12-month pre-index window (≥ 2 outpatient or 1 inpatient record per condition). HCQ exposure was treated as a fixed medication variable based on prescription records prior to outcome occurrence. Proportional-hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residual tests (global and covariate-specific) and by inspecting log(–log) survival plots; multicollinearity was assessed with variance inflation factors, and influence diagnostics were examined to identify high-leverage observations. To address multiplicity in stratified and multi-group contrasts, Bonferroni correction was applied (e.g., α/3 = 0.0167 for three-group comparisons). All tests were two-sided with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05 (equivalently, α = 0.05). Analyses were performed in SPSS v22.0 (SPSS, Chicago, IL).

Results

Cohort selection and baseline characteristics

The study population was drawn from Taiwan’s LGTD, which includes comprehensive outpatient and inpatient records from 2000 to 2015, starting with an initial cohort of 1,936,512 individuals. The inclusion criteria identified 36,613 individuals diagnosed with pSjD.

After applying the exclusion criteria (individuals diagnosed with Sjögren’s syndrome before 2000 [n = 31]; those with TMD diagnosed before tracking [n = 9]; individuals without tracking data [n = 267]; individuals below 18 years of age [n = 110]; and those with unknown sex [n = 1]), 418 individuals were omitted, resulting in a final cohort of 36,195 individuals with pSjD (Fig. 1).

Flowchart depicting the process of selecting the study population from Taiwan’s Longitudinal Generation Tracking Database. Abbreviations: pSjD: primary Sjögren’s disease, TMD: temporomandibular disorder

A comparison cohort, established using the same exclusion criteria, consisted of individuals without pSjD during the study period. This cohort was matched with the study cohort using a 4-fold propensity score based on sex, age, comorbidities, and inclusion date, resulting in 144,780 individuals. The tracking endpoint was set at December 31, 2015. TMD incidence was recorded, with 41 cases in the study cohort and 58 cases in the comparison cohort (Fig. 1).

The analysis of the demographic characteristics and medical comorbidities of the study and comparison cohorts showed that there were no significant differences between the two cohorts in age, sex, and most comorbid conditions, including diabetes mellitus, hypertension, depression, insomnia, stroke, dementia, and chronic kidney disease, lipid profile abnormalities, anxiety, and cardiovascular disease. The average age across both groups was around 55 years, and females accounted for 80.15% of the participants in each cohort (Table 1). However, a significant disparity was found in the distribution of insured premium levels (p < 0.001), with 70.18% of individuals in the pSjD group classified in the lowest income bracket (< NT$18,000). Location also differed significantly (p = 0.017), with the largest proportion of pSjD patients residing in northern Taiwan (33.33%). Additionally, disparities were noted in urbanization level (p < 0.001), with 23.58% of pSjD patients living in the most urbanized areas, and in the level of care received (p < 0.001), with 38.12% of pSjD patients seeking care at medical centers. These significant differences were adjusted for in the multivariate analyses to minimize potential confounding (Table 1).

Multivariable Cox regression analysis

The Cox proportional hazards analysis indicated a significant association between pSjD and an elevated risk of developing TMD. After adjusting for demographic and clinical variables, the aHR was 1.921 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.39–2.58; p < 0.001) (Table 2). Age stratification revealed progressively higher risks in older cohorts, with individuals aged 45–64 years (aHR = 1.34, 95% CI = 1.00–1.56; p = 0.038) and those ≥ 65 years (aHR = 1.54, 95% CI = 1.32–1.77; p < 0.001) showing a significantly elevated TMD incidence compared to the 18–44-year reference group. While male sex initially appeared marginally associated with TMD risk in the crude analysis (HR = 1.32, 95% CI = 1.00–1.58; p = 0.048), this association lost statistical significance in the adjusted model (p = 0.074) (Table 2).

Comorbid conditions exerted significant effects on TMD risk, with depression (aHR = 2.31, 95% CI = 1.66–3.00; p < 0.001), diabetes mellitus (aHR = 2.00, 95% CI = 1.39–2.50; p < 0.001), and hypertension (aHR = 1.83, 95% CI = 1.40–2.08; p < 0.001) emerging as the strongest predictors (Table 2). The level-of-care stratification revealed an elevated risk of TMD incidence in medical centers (aHR = 2.44, 95% CI = 1.74–2.96; p < 0.001) and regional hospitals (aHR = 1.71, 95% CI = 1.29–2.38; p < 0.001) compared to local clinics. Urbanization level 1 residents exhibited nearly double the TMD risk of their rural counterparts (aHR = 1.96, 95% CI = 1.36–2.80; p < 0.001) (Table 2). Location demonstrated collinearity with healthcare access variables and was excluded from the final model.

Analysis stratified by HCQ exposure

In exploratory stratified analyses, differences in observed incidence were noted across HCQ exposure groups, with the highest incidence observed in pSjD patients receiving HCQ (13.67 per 100,000 person-years), followed by pSjD non-users (11.12 per 100,000 person-years) and non- pSjD controls (4.51 per 100,000 person-years) (Table 3). After adjusting for potential confounders, pSjD patients without HCQ exposure still exhibited a 68% elevated TMD risk compared to controls (aHR = 1.66, 95% CI = 1.20–2.24; p < 0.001), while HCQ-treated pSjD patients showed a higher observed risk (aHR = 2.05, 95% CI = 1.48–2.76; p < 0.001) (Table 3). These differences remained statistically significant after Bonferroni correction (p < 0.0167). Additional analysis revealed that the rate of TMD incidence was significantly greater in the pSjD group, at 12.81 per 100,000 person-years, compared to the non-pSjD control cohort. In the pSjD cohort, HCQ-treated patients exhibited the highest risk, while untreated patients had an elevated but comparatively lower risk (Table 3). The elevated risk associated with HCQ exposure likely reflects confounding by indication, given the drug’s preferential use in more severe pSjD cases.

Cumulative incidence of TMD in pSjD cohorts

Patients with pSjD exhibited a significantly higher cumulative incidence of TMD (log-rank p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). Over the 15-year follow-up period, the crude cumulative incidence was approximately 0.11% in the pSjD cohort (41/36,195) and 0.04% in the control cohort (58/144,780). Although the absolute incidence remained low in both groups, the Kaplan–Meier curves showed a clear divergence over time, indicating a higher risk of TMD among patients with pSjD. These findings were consistent with the hazard ratios derived from the Cox proportional hazards models (Table 2).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first large-scale, nationwide population-based investigation using rigorous diagnostic criteria to demonstrate a significant positive link between pSjD and TMD. Patients with pSjD exhibited nearly twice the risk of developing TMD compared to individuals without pSjD. The crude cumulative incidence was 0.11% in the pSjD cohort and 0.04% in controls, corresponding to an adjusted hazard ratio of 1.921 (Table 2). This association may be attributable to the autoimmune nature of pSjD, which promotes chronic inflammation and joint involvement, thereby contributing to the pathogenesis of TMD (Table 3). Notably, these findings remained statistically significant throughout the 15-year follow-up period (Fig. 2).

Earlier studies yielded inconsistent results for the pSjD –TMD relationship. In a 1999 study, List et al. found no significant difference in TMD symptoms between individuals with pSjD and healthy controls. However, the study’s cross-sectional design, small sample size, restricted imaging techniques, and absence of longitudinal follow-up limited the strength of its conclusions [55]. In contrast, subsequent research, including a cross-sectional study by Yıldızer Keriş et al. [56] and a systematic review by Hysa et al. [57], provided emerging evidence supporting a possible association, although inconsistencies remained. Unlike these earlier studies, the present large-scale, longitudinal cohort with 15 years of follow-up provides stronger epidemiological evidence of a significant association between pSjD and TMD (Fig. 1), with the divergence in incidence becoming statistically significant after the fourth year of observation (Fig. 2).

Hypothesized mechanisms linking pSjD to TMD remain incompletely understood, although several plausible pathophysiological pathways may explain the increased TMD risk in pSjD [55, 57]. First, the widespread systemic effects of autoimmunity in pSjD are mainly driven by lymphocytic infiltration of exocrine glands, resulting in reduced salivary flow, extensive dental caries, loss of periodontal attachment, and increased susceptibility to oral candidiasis [58–62]. Like other autoimmune conditions, including RA and SLE, pSjD may contribute to the development of TMD via persistent systemic inflammation [57]. A Mendelian randomization study further supports a genetic predisposition linking autoimmunity and TMD risk [63]. Notably, our finding that HCQ exposure might be associated with a higher observed TMD incidence may suggest that more complex, tissue-specific mechanisms beyond generalized immune modulation may be involved.

Second, environmental, psychological, and autonomic factors may interact to influence TMD development. Patients with pSjD exhibit higher rates of anxiety, depression, and sleep disturbances [6, 64]. In our study, depression, anxiety, and insomnia significantly increased TMD risk, with a higher aHR for depression among pSjD patients, suggesting a synergistic effect that may amplify autoimmune-driven joint dysfunction (Table 2). This finding aligns with recent evidence demonstrating that anxiety, depression, and stress are major risk factors for TMD, and that TMDs frequently coexist with mental health disorders in a self-reinforcing cycle [65, 66]. Moreover, autonomic dysfunction, frequently noted in both pSjD and TMD, including features such as decreased heart rate variability and impaired sympathetic activity [7, 20, 67–76], may play a role in the onset and maintenance of TMD.

Third, systemic comorbidities may substantially contribute to TMD development. In our study, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, stroke, lipid profile abnormalities, chronic kidney disease, and cardiovascular disease were all independently associated with increased TMD risk, with diabetes and hypertension exhibiting the highest adjusted hazard ratios (aHR > 1.8) (Table 2). These consistent positive associations align with the cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome proposed by the American Heart Association in 2023, which underscores the interconnected pathophysiology among metabolic, renal, and cardiovascular systems [77, 78]. Our findings further support the premise that shared inflammatory and oxidative stress pathways may also contribute to musculoskeletal manifestations such as TMJ dysfunction [7, 19, 70–73].

Fourth, subtle musculoskeletal manifestations may contribute to TMD incidence. Musculoskeletal symptoms, including muscle pain, fatigue, and fibromyalgia-like features, are common in pSjD [4, 58]. Articular involvement, detected through musculoskeletal ultrasound and disease activity indices such as Sjögren’s Systemic Clinical Activity Index (SCAI) [79, 80], further supports the role of underlying musculoskeletal dysfunction as a factor contributing to TMD [22, 81–85].

Finally, anticipated age-related degenerative processes may further exacerbate TMD risk. Aging is a well-established risk factor of TMD, with individuals aged ≥ 65 years demonstrating the highest risk in our cohort. TMJ degeneration associated with aging, including dementia (which demonstrated the same association in our study), cartilage thinning, proteoglycan loss, and increased proteolysis [86–88], combined with chronic autoimmune burden, may amplify the susceptibility to TMD of elderly patients with pSjD (Table 2).

In exploratory analyses, pSjD patients receiving HCQ demonstrated a higher observed incidence of TMD. However, HCQ is preferentially prescribed for patients with more active disease, and the database lacks information on disease severity, dosage, and treatment adherence. Therefore, this observation is likely influenced by confounding by indication and should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only. Among the key strengths of this study are its use of an extensive, population-based dataset and a 15-year longitudinal follow-up period, which helped reduce selection bias and strengthen the reliability of the results (Figs. 1 and 2). Time-to-onset analyses provided novel insights into the temporal dynamics of TMD development following pSjD diagnosis (Tables S2-1 and S2-2). Additionally, the concordance between our results and those of previous observations on comorbidities, such as psychological stress, insomnia, and stroke, reinforces the validity of our findings (Table 2). These methodological strengths enhance the external validity and generalizability of our study. Clinicians, particularly dentists and rheumatologists, should consider integrated screening protocols for orofacial pain in pSjD patients, especially those with co-existing psychological or metabolic conditions.

The limitations of this study must also be noted. First, causality cannot be inferred because the analysis is observational and based on claims data. Second, important confounders—including smoking status, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and nutritional factors—were not available in Taiwan’s NHIRD database. Third, diagnoses in the NHIRD rely exclusively on the ICD-9-CM system, making its use mandatory. Mild cases that do not prompt medical visits are likely under-ascertained, contributing to misclassification. Moreover, ICD-9-CM–based definitions of TMD lack the granularity needed to differentiate muscle-related disorders, joint disorders, disc displacement, arthralgia, or degenerative joint disease, and therefore cannot fully align with contemporary diagnostic frameworks such as the DC/TMD, which represents the current gold standard incorporating both Axis I (clinical/structural) and Axis II (psychosocial) domains. However, our study period (2000–2015) predates the adoption of DC/TMD in clinical practice; therefore, applying DC/TMD criteria for case identification was not temporally or operationally feasible [89]. Although TMD diagnoses recorded in the NHIRD were made by licensed dentists or oral surgeons using clinical assessments available at the time, detailed diagnostic data—such as imaging, palpation findings, mandibular range of motion, functional limitation, or pain intensity—were not accessible in this retrospective analysis. The absence of these data prevented more granular evaluation of TMD severity, symptom fluctuations, functional impairment, or disease course. Consequently, our analysis of HCQ was restricted to incident TMD events and cannot be interpreted in terms of individual symptom severity, disease trajectory, or treatment response. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed clinical assessments and lifestyle information are needed to validate our findings and elucidate the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms linking pSjD and TMD. Therefore, our results should be interpreted as population-level associations rather than patient-level clinical predictions.

Conclusions

This large-scale, population-based cohort study demonstrated a significant association between pSjD and an increased risk of TMD over a 15-year follow-up period. The findings suggest that patients with pSjD demonstrate a higher clinical risk of subsequent TMD diagnosis, particularly in the presence of metabolic and psychological comorbidities. Future prospective studies incorporating detailed clinical assessments are warranted to clarify causal mechanisms and to better characterize TMD subtypes in this population.
