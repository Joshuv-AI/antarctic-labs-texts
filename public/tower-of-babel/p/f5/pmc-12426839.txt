Thanks for reading this CASE editorial. By doing so, you have demonstrated a dedication to echocardiography, education, and the written word owned by only a few.

In this month's issue of CASE, there are multiple excellent reports that focus on uncommon presentations of common pathologies, common presentations of uncommon pathologies, and innovative approaches aimed at expanding our use of echocardiography and multimodal CV imaging. Each CASE report represents many hours of effort from many individuals to create this final product for your benefit. They are the culmination of the authors' initial written approach and subsequent submission to the CASE editorial team. On occasion, CASE is not the first destination and the report may have already spent many hours at another journal's editorial peer review process prior to making it to CASE. Once it arrives with us, our incredible managerial editors will read the report to ensure the submission adheres to CASE's author instructions. Depending on whether it fulfils these requirements, it may be returned to the authors for modification or it may move on to the editor-in-chief's (EIC) in-box.

Once arriving in my in-box, I am immediately alerted of this pending review. Then, I read the report (typically within 24-48 hours depending on my day job [Figure 1]), as well as the author's cover letter, and I list my immediate thoughts supported by extensive notes (from inherent knowledge or targeted research). At this point, it is ready to move on to the next stage of the editorial review process. It may be returned back to the authors (if it is unilaterally rejected), or most often, forwarded to selected members of the esteemed editorial board. The manuscript is anonymized in our system so our peer reviewers do not see either the authors or their institutions or geographic locations.

Proving that the job of the EIC never stops, this is me on the bridge of the National Geographic Explorer working on CASE in Antarctica.

The next component of the editorial peer review process, the subsection editors, have been carefully selected to provide the journal with a diverse background of content expertise and have demonstrated both a willingness and ability to complete quality scientific reviews within a short timeline. Their task is to either return the CASE report to the EIC with their high-level, detailed review or, more typically, to reach out and request additional peer review from our global pool of peer reviewers, many of whom make up our editorial board. For those of you reading this who are peer reviewers for CASE, thank you for volunteering your time and expertise to this important process. Thank you for your quality, thoughtful input. Thank you for your quick refusal when you know you simply do not have sufficient time to complete a high quality review within our requested time frame.

Having now prepared their own review and compared their insights with other peer reviewers, these subsection editors create a summative evaluation of the manuscript and return all input back to the EIC. The EIC then matches their own notes with the subsection experts' notes and reads each of the peer reviewers' notes. Once this initial series of reviews is complete, the EIC returns a decision letter to the authors. The decision will either be accept, reject, or revise (either a minor or major revision). Since CASE is an image-focused journal and ASE is an educational-focused society, image and educational quality is paramount. When images or their captions require editing or the educational perspectives warrant revision, this is usually considered a ‘major’ revision.

Now, the authors have guidance on the preparation of their manuscript. They may choose to withdraw their report and submit it to another journal (this rarely happens). They may accept some or all of the suggestions for structured revisions and return a revised manuscript. At this point, the managerial editors and the EIC once again review the revised manuscript in great detail. The external subsection editors and peer reviewers are usually involved in new submissions (undergoing their own initial reviews) and are rarely re-invited to perform subsequent reviews on the manuscript. However, on occasion (for example, if the authors disagree with requested changes and choose to not revise sections of their original manuscript), the entire initial review process will occur again and the same content experts and possibly the peer reviewers will be asked to look at a revised CASE report.

There is extensive written dialogue between the authors, editors and reviewers that never makes it to the final report. Although I have greatly enjoyed the privilege to write 37 editorial musings since starting as the EIC, I believe that change is important to maintain reader engagement. From this editorial forward, I will be focusing on the unwritten word as a means to enhance the full understanding of each published CASE report. I will compress some of the authors' own input regarding how they perceive the value of their CASE. Sometimes, the authors’ reasons for not making recommended changes during the editorial review process are incredibly insightful, but there is not a mechanism to let the readers fully appreciate those perspectives. My goal is to use this space to help provide that avenue of additional education.

In this month's issue, there is an excellent report on the Multimodal Assessments of Cardiac Sarcoidosis (CS) with Right Ventricular Involvement. In the authors' own words, their case was unique in that they were able to “document the TTE-based clinical course of a dramatic decrease in RV contractility in CS with RV involvement.” They emphasized that the “temporal change may be key.” For our CMR and nuclear PET/CCT readers, the authors point out that their case “highlights the utility of STIR imaging (for) CS” and it may “serve as a practical and cost-effective alternative” to PET imaging. During the peer review process, the authors were asked to address the severe TR, and they reiterated that there were “no studies that specifically evaluated TR severity in CS” and believed that this “reflects (the) advanced disease state in CS patients with RV involvement.”

The authors of Double Trouble: A Bifid Left Atrial Appendage Requiring a Double Lobe-and-Disc Device Approach recognized, through the peer review process, that their report was limited by not having longer term follow up investigations. Based upon the reviewers’ input, the authors were also able to provide their comparison and contrast with an additional published report, commenting that their CASE report “adds broader applicability to this technique across multiple device designs.” Initially, the authors had not detailed the reasons for requiring this approach, but were able to clarify that the conventional approach was abandoned since “it was not well-seated and the disc protruded into the LA cavity.” Their graphical abstract nicely demonstrates their final innovative approach.

In Unique, Early Prenatal Presentation of Generalized Arterial Calcification of Infancy, the authors and reviewers discussed the non-specific nature of 'echo-brightness' on echocardiography and the limitations dependent upon technical settings (e.g. gain). This resulted in greater clarification in “the steps taken during acquisition to minimize errors.” Although not included in the final report, there was discussion surrounding the lack of acoustic shadowing and whether this might represent 'early' or 'less-dense' pathology. The authors agreed with that possibility and added “especially as the patient was undergoing abnormal calcium deposition throughout fetal life, rather than at one fixed time point.”

In another fetal echocardiography report, the authors of Clues on the Three-Vessel View for Fetal Diagnosis of Supracardiac Total Anomalous Pulmonary Venous Connection emphasized that their CASE “is significant because despite the unexpected and rare combination of situs solitus, AVSD and TAPVD, we [made the antenatal diagnosis] because we noticed important clues in 3 vessel and trachea view.” They pointed out that “antenatal detection is very poor” and “our [CASE emphasizes the] importance of recognizing subtle signs when performing fetal echocardiography. Our clue to discovering the abnormal pulmonary venous drainage in this rare combination of normal organ arrangement and AVSD was distinguishing between the left SVC and ascending vein in 3 vessel view guided by a few very important clues.”

In Acute Myocardial Infarction Mimicking Apical Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy, the authors were asked to reiterate the transient finding of an imaging pattern on TTE and ECG that might be misinterpreted as an apical variant HCM during the post-infarct recovery stage (e.g. diffuse TWI and apical wall thickening). For example, had their patient been seen at another institution during that transient (post-infarct, remodeling/recovery) stage (without knowledge of the preceding AMI event; or possibly after an AMI with spontaneous reperfusion, but without PCI), the TTE and 12 lead ECG may have been interpreted as apical variant HCM, with some degree of confidence.

In Role of Intracardiac Echocardiography in Ventricular Tachycardia Ablation: Where is the Scar? the authors provided additional thoughtful discussions on the ability of echo to visualize myocardial fibrosis using ICE. The revised report provided their expert insights regarding the current and future value of ICE for this purpose. “The echo bright regions are visualized due to acoustic mismatch. These areas are seen as the echo bright regions of ICE and TTE. Future advancement in imaging techniques may improve acoustic interface and delineation of cardiac structures and may become more automatic. This would lend echocardiography to have an increasing role in substrate for EP procedures, particularly VT ablations.”

If you allow me to quote Paul McCartney from Paperback Writer one more time: “If you must return it, you can send it here; But I need a break.”

Remember, every echo you see today has a teaching point and every teaching point is a potential new CASE report!
