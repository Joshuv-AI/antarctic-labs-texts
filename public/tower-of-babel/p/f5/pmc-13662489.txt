Comparison of Bruker Biotyper Sirius, VITEK MS PRIME, and ASTA MicroIDSys MALDI-TOF MS Systems for Cryptococcus Species Identification Across Protein Extraction Protocols
Authors: Bosung Park, Eunsang Suh, Eun Jeong Won, Tae Yeul Kim, Hee Jae Huh, Heungsup Sung, Mi-Na Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAccurate and rapid identification of Cryptococcus spp. is critical for patient care. Matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS) is increasingly used for yeast identification; however, its performance varies with instrument and database coverage. We compared the performance of three MALDI-TOF MS systems—Bruker Biotyper Sirius (Sirius; Bruker Daltonics), bioMérieux VITEK MS PRIME (PRIME; bioMérieux), and ASTA MicroIDSys (ASTA; ASTA Inc.)—for detecting Cryptococcus spp. across protein-extraction protocols.MethodsWe analyzed 51 C. neoformans, two C. deuterogattii, five Vanrija humicola, and one Naganishia liquefaciens clinical isolates. Each isolate was tested after on-plate formic acid extraction; isolates failing identification were retested after repeat on-plate and in-tube extractions. The results were categorized as correct, incomplete, incorrect, or no identification against ribosomal DNA sequencing results.ResultsAfter initial on-plate extraction, correct identification rates were 86.4%, 88.1%, and 69.5% for Sirius, PRIME, and ASTA, respectively. After repeat on-plate extraction, ASTA accuracy increased to 81.4% (P=0.016). Additional in-tube extraction did not significantly improve identification across platforms, although PRIME outperformed ASTA (P=0.012). All platforms reliably classified C. neoformans (correct identification rates: 94%–96%) after additional on-plate extraction; discrepancies occurred mainly in uncommon species, possibly owing to gaps in database coverage.ConclusionsSirius and PRIME demonstrated robust performance for C. neoformans identification and C. gattii complex differentiation, outperforming ASTA for relatively rare species. Single on-plate extraction is sufficient in clinical practice. However, continued database expansion is essential for maximizing MALDI-TOF MS performance and accurately identifying Cryptococcus spp., thereby informing antifungal therapy and improving patient outcomes.

INTRODUCTION

Cryptococcus, a genus of encapsulated yeasts, includes clinically relevant human pathogens. C. neoformans and C. gattii complexes cause cryptococcosis, primarily manifesting as meningitis or pulmonary infection, especially in immunocompromised individuals [1–3]. Rapid and accurate species-level identification of Cryptococcus is important because species complexes differ in terms of epidemiology, clinical management, and antifungal susceptibility [3–6].

Before the adoption of matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS), yeast identification in clinical laboratories primarily relied on commercial biochemical kits. However, these assays are often limited by prolonged incubation or culture durations and insufficient discriminatory power for closely related species, making accurate species-level identification challenging [7, 8]. MALDI-TOF MS has transformed microbial identification in clinical laboratories [9–11]. It enables rapid, cost-effective identification of yeasts and bacteria based on spectral matching to reference databases [12–14] and can accurately identify Cryptococcus spp., differentiating between C. neoformans and C. gattii [15, 16]. Globally, the Bruker Biotyper (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany; hereafter referred to as “Sirius”) and bioMérieux VITEK MS (bioMérieux, Marcy-l’Étoile, France; hereafter, “PRIME”) platforms dominate clinical use [17, 18]. The ASTA MicroIDSys (ASTA, Suwon, Korea; hereafter, “ASTA”) platform was recently introduced [19]. These systems differ in terms of fungal database coverage and require different sample-preparation methods for optimal results [20–22]. For yeasts, on-plate formic acid extraction or in-tube extraction improves protein spectral quality and identification rates, when compared with direct smear methods, by exposing intracellular proteins, particularly in heavily encapsulated yeasts such as Cryptococcus [23, 24]. Although comparative studies have demonstrated a high overall accuracy of MALDI-TOF MS for common yeast species [25, 26], its performance for Cryptococcus spp., particularly uncommon species, has not been comprehensively assessed across platforms. In a study by Lee et al. [24], VITEK MS identified all 23 C. neoformans isolates after initial on-plate extraction, whereas Biotyper identified only two isolates initially and all isolates only after in-tube extraction. These procedures are critical for ensuring optimal spectral quality and are tailored to database and sample type of each platform [21]. A few studies have evaluated different MALDI-TOF MS platforms for Cryptococcus identification. Herein, we evaluated the performance of the Bruker Biotyper Sirius, VITEK MS PRIME, and ASTA MicroIDSys systems using on-plate and in-tube sample-preparation methods.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Isolates and identification of a reference standard

We analyzed 59 non-duplicate, consecutive clinical yeast isolates belonging to Cryptococcus and related genera, collected at two university hospitals—Asan Medical Center and Samsung Medical Center (approximately 5,000 beds in total)—in Seoul, Korea, between January 2020 and January 2024. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of Asan Medical Center (approval No. 2023-0467) and Samsung Medical Center (approval number 2024-05-027-001). Requirement of informed consent was waived, considering the use of de-identified stored clinical isolates. All isolates were identified by sequencing the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region and D1/D2 domain of 26S rRNA at Asan Medical Center. Species assignment required concordant ITS and D1/D2 top-hit species with ≥99% identity and ≥98% coverage. For isolates belonging to the C. neoformans complex, intergenic spacer sequencing was additionally performed to ensure accurate discrimination between C. neoformans (formerly C. neoformans var. grubii) and C. deneoformans (formerly C. neoformans var. neoformans) [27]. Of the 59 isolates, 51 were C. neoformans; none corresponded to C. deneoformans. The remaining eight isolates comprised two C. deuterogattii (formerly C. gattii VGII), five Vanrija humicola (syn. Cryptococcus humicola), and one Naganishia liquefaciens (syn. Cryptococcus liquefaciens).

MALDI-TOF MS

The three MALDI-TOF MS platforms were evaluated at Samsung Medical Center between May 1 and 8, 2024. Each platform was operated in conjunction with its most current in vitro diagnostic (IVD) database: Sirius with the MBT IVD Library 2023, VITEK MS PRIME with KB v3.3, and ASTA with CoreDB v1.28.01. As per the manufacturers’ specifications, the Sirius library covers 4,239 species, including 19 Cryptococcus-related taxa; the PRIME library covers 1,585 species, including 10 Cryptococcus-related taxa; and the ASTA library covers 2,566 species, including 32 Cryptococcus-related taxa. Cryptococcus-related taxa included those currently classified as Cryptococcus spp., as well as taxa reclassified from Cryptococcus, such as Vanrija humicola and Naganishia liquefaciens. Research use only libraries were not used in this study. All instruments were operated using the standard IVD acquisition parameters recommended by each manufacturer, including the default laser intensity and number of laser shots, and were calibrated according to the manufacturers’ instructions. Cryptococcus spp. coverage for each database is summarized in Supplemental Data Fig. S1.

Sample preparation

All isolates were analyzed using on-plate formic acid extraction as the primary approach. For isolates that could not be reliably identified, the on-plate formic acid procedure was repeated (hereafter, “repeat on-plate”), and in-tube extraction (hereafter, “additional in-tube”) was performed concurrently. To newly prepare sample/matrix spots for both repeat on-plate and additional in-tube extractions, we obtained fresh colonies from the culture plate used for initial testing. All sample preparations were conducted per the manufacturers’ recommendations under aseptic conditions.

On-plate preparation

Colonies grown on Sabouraud dextrose agar for 48–72 h at 35°C and 5% CO2 were smeared onto a MALDI target plate and air-dried. For Sirius and ASTA, 1.0–1.5 µL of 70% formic acid was overlaid on the dried smear, followed by an equal volume of α-cyano-4-hydroxycinnamic acid (CHCA) matrix. For PRIME, 0.5 µL of 25% formic acid and 1 µL of CHCA matrix were sequentially applied.

In-tube extraction

For Sirius, several colonies were suspended in 300 µL of distilled water and mixed with 900 µL of absolute ethanol at room temperature. The mixture was centrifuged at 13,000×g for 2 min. The pellet was washed in ethanol, centrifuged at 13,000×g for 2 min, and air-dried for ≥5 min. Subsequently, 25 µL of 70% formic acid was added and mixed thoroughly to resuspend the pellet, followed by the addition of 25 µL acetonitrile. After pipetting the mixture, the sample was centrifuged at 13,000×g for 2 min, and 1 µL of the supernatant was spotted onto a MALDI plate and air-dried before matrix application.

For PRIME, fungal biomass was collected using a swab moistened with API suspension medium or deionized water and suspended in 900 µL of 70% ethanol in a 2-mL tube at room temperature. After vortexing, the suspension was centrifuged at 13,000×g for 2 min. The pellet was re-dissolved in 40 µL of 70% formic acid, and then 40 µL of acetonitrile was added. Following centrifugation at 13,000×g for 2 min, 1 µL of the supernatant was spotted and air-dried before applying 1 µL of CHCA matrix.

For ASTA, approximately 20 µL of colony biomass was transferred into a tube containing 1 mL of 70% ethanol and vortexed for 5 min at room temperature. After centrifugation at 13,000×g for 5 min, the pellet was air-dried for 5 min, 50 µL of 70% formic acid was added, and the sample was vortexed for 30 s. Subsequently, 50 µL of acetonitrile was added, and the sample was vortexed briefly. After a final centrifugation at 13,000×g for 5 min, 1.5 µL of supernatant was spotted onto a target plate, air-dried, and overlaid with 1.5 µL of CHCA matrix.

Interpretation of identification categories

For each isolate and platform, we recorded the top identification and its confidence metric and then classified the outcome into one of four mutually exclusive categories. Platform-specific acceptance thresholds were Sirius log (score) ≥1.7, PRIME confidence ≥80%, and ASTA score ≥140/300. Results meeting or exceeding the relevant threshold were adjudicated as follows:

Correct identification: concordance with the final sequencing-based species assignment. Recognized synonyms were counted as correct (e.g., C. humicola for V. humicola and C. liquefaciens for N. liquefaciens) [28].

Incomplete identification: assignment to a different species belonging to the same phylogenetic cluster (e.g., C. neoformans and C. deneoformans were considered part of the C. neoformans complex, and C. gattii and C. deuterogattii were grouped under the C. gattii complex).

Incorrect identification: any confident species call outside the correct complex or to a non-synonymous species.

No identification: a result falling below the system-specific confidence threshold or a report lacking species-level resolution.

Statistical analysis

Exact 95% confidence intervals for proportions were calculated. Unless otherwise specified, all tests were two-sided, and significance was set to P<0.05. Within each platform, three related proportions (on-plate, repeat on-plate, and additional in-tube) were compared using Cochran’s Q test, and pairwise comparisons were made using two-sided McNemar exact tests. Across platforms, under the same preparation method, the same Q/McNemar framework was applied. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS v21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

RESULTS

Table 1 summarizes the overall identification results from the three platforms in combination with the indicated extraction methods. Following initial on-plate extraction, Sirius (86.4%) and PRIME (88.1%) showed comparable correct identification rates; ASTA exhibited a lower rate (69.5%). Sirius incompletely identified one C. deuterogattii isolate, misidentified two isolates (one C. deuterogattii and one N. liquefaciens), and initially failed to identify five isolates of C. neoformans. PRIME incompletely identified one C. deuterogattii isolate and failed to initially identify five isolates (four C. neoformans and one N. liquefaciens). In contrast, ASTA MicroIDSys did not identify 18 isolates (10 C. neoformans, five C. humicola, two C. deuterogattii, and one N. liquefaciens) at the initial stage.

We compared species-level identification accuracy among the preparation protocols for each MALDI-TOF MS platform. Table 2 (panel A) summarizes the pairwise comparisons. Differences among initial on-plate formic acid extraction, repeat on-plate extraction, and additional in-tube extraction were assessed using McNemar’s test for paired proportions (N=59 isolates per platform). For Sirius and PRIME, repeat on-plate extraction yielded modest but statistically insignificant improvements in identification rates; the Sirius species-level identification rate increased from 86.4% (51/59) to 91.5% (54/59), representing three additional correct identifications (P=0.250), whereas the PRIME identification rate improved from 88.1% to 89.8%, with only one additional correct identification (P=1.000). In contrast, the correct identification rate for ASTA significantly increased from 69.5% (41/59) to 81.4% (48/59) (seven additional correct identifications; P=0.016) after repeat on-plate extraction. Additional in-tube extraction offered little or no benefit across all three platforms, with no significant differences in the results from those obtained after initial extraction (P>0.2 in all cases). As for ASTA, the in-tube extraction yield (74.6%) was slightly lower than that after repeat on-plate extraction (81.4%); however, the difference was not significant (P=0.289).

Table 2 (panel B) enlists pairwise comparisons between Sirius, PRIME, and ASTA for each preparation condition. After initial on-plate formic acid extraction, performance varied significantly among the three platforms (Cochran’s Q, P=0.001), primarily due to the lower identification rate of ASTA. The species-level identification rate of ASTA was significantly lower than that of Sirius (P=0.013) and PRIME (P=0.001), whereas no significant difference was noted between Sirius and PRIME (P=1.000), indicating comparable accuracy. Following repeat on-plate extraction, the gap between platforms narrowed: Sirius, PRIME, and ASTA achieved 91.5%, 89.8%, and 81.4% correct identification rates, respectively. However, the overall heterogeneity across platforms remained significant (Cochran’s Q, P=0.032). Pairwise McNemar tests revealed that differences between Sirius and ASTA (P=0.070) and PRIME and ASTA (P=0.125) were not significant, indicating that the initial deficit of ASTA was largely mitigated by the repeat extraction, whereas that of Sirius and PRIME was indistinguishable (P=1.000). With in-tube extraction, Cochran’s Q test revealed a significant difference across the three platforms (P=0.008). Pairwise comparisons showed that PRIME outperformed ASTA (P=0.012), whereas ASTA did not differ significantly from Sirius (P=0.092); Sirius and PRIME also remained comparable (P=0.625). Overall, ASTA consistently yielded fewer correct species identifications than did Sirius and PRIME, with the most pronounced disparity found with the initial on-plate protocol, whereas Sirius and PRIME showed equivalent accuracy in all scenarios.

Results for the eight non-C. neoformans isolates—C. deuterogattii (N=2), V. humicola (N=5), and N. liquefaciens (N=1)—are listed in Table 3; these results reflect both library coverage and predefined error classifications. Sirius accurately identified all five V. humicola isolates at the species level. Among both C. deuterogattii isolates, one was reported as C. gattii (classified as an incomplete identification) and the other as C. neoformans (counted as an incorrect identification). N. liquefaciens, despite being present in the Sirius database, was misidentified as N. diffluens. PRIME showed a similar pattern: all five V. humicola isolates were identified at the species level, whereas both C. deuterogattii isolates were reported either as C. gattii (incomplete identification) or as C. neoformans (incorrect identification). The N. liquefaciens isolate yielded “no identification” across all preparations. ASTA failed to generate any valid identification for these eight isolates.

DISCUSSION

MS is increasingly used in clinical laboratories across a wide range of applications with advances in analytical technologies [29–31]. In particular, MALDI-TOF MS has become widely used for the rapid identification of microorganisms, including fungi [12–14]. In this study, we performed a comparative evaluation of three MALDI-TOF MS platforms and found that all systems performed well overall, particularly for the most common pathogen, C. neoformans. With optimized sample preparation, Sirius and PRIME each achieved approximately 90% identification accuracy, whereas ASTA achieved approximately 81%, possibly owing to its narrower database coverage. All platforms correctly identified most C. neoformans isolates. When encountering a Cryptococcus sp. not present in the database, these platforms either identified the correct complex or returned “no identification,” rather than assigning an unrelated taxon; no high-confidence misidentifications outside the genus were observed.

Improved identification rates were driven by retesting, rather than by switching to a different extraction protocol. When isolates that failed the initial run were re-spotted using the same on-plate method, correct identification rates increased markedly for ASTA (approximately 70%–81%) and modestly for Sirius and PRIME. Both organism- and operator-related factors might have contributed to the improved identification rate upon repeat on-plate extraction. Cryptococcus spp. possess a thick, heterogeneous capsule that impedes efficient protein extraction and diminishes spectral quality. Reducing capsule thickness can enhance MALDI-TOF MS performance [32]. On repeat extraction, Cryptococcus capsules may have become smaller, potentially improving identification. Although prolonging the incubation time may have further enhanced identification by influencing organism-related factors such as the capsule size, we performed repeat testing immediately after the initial test and therefore could not assess this potential effect.

Additionally, MALDI-TOF MS performance can be affected by operator-related factors, such as the biomass amount applied, smear thickness, and uniformity of matrix co-crystallization [17]. Repeat spotting can modify these factors, leading to enhanced identification. The results of a previous Korean multicenter isolate collection study demonstrated that repeat on-plate extraction substantially improved C. neoformans identification, corroborating its utility [24]. We performed on-plate and in-tube extractions similarly for isolates that initially failed, consistent with reports that both methods offer comparable accuracy for most clinically encountered yeasts, with limited benefit from in-tube extraction in routine practice [23, 32, 33]. Optimizing factors that affect MALDI-TOF MS performance (such as sample preparation, matrix application, or instrument calibration) may further enhance identification accuracy and reduce the need for retesting [34]; however, Cryptococcus-specific optimal conditions have not yet been established.

Sirius and PRIME were highly reliable for C. neoformans identification and identifying other species present in their databases. The lower performance of ASTA for identifying C. deuterogattii, V. humicola, and N. liquefaciens possibly reflects the absence of these species from its current IVD database, despite including the largest number of Cryptococcus-related taxa (N=32). In contrast, the Sirius and PRIME databases contain entries for C. gattii and C. humicola, which plausibly enabled their successful identification at least to the genus level. Although N. liquefaciens is represented in the Sirius MBT IVD Library 2023 (Supplemental Data Fig. S1), Sirius reported Naganishia diffluens with a log (score) of 1.84 across preparations, likely due to nearest-neighbor effects within Naganishia, whereby the query spectrum correlated more strongly with the available N. diffluens spectral profiles than with the (potentially fewer or less diverse) N. liquefaciens entries. Alternatively, preparation or culture conditions may have shifted the protein profile away from the reference library for N. liquefaciens. This observation highlights that database inclusion alone does not guarantee accurate identification unless the reference set captures sufficient intraspecies diversity. In contrast, PRIME (KB v3.3) and ASTA (CoreDB v1.28.01) returned “no identification” for this isolate, due to database coverage gaps. Unexpected Naganishia/Vanrija calls, even with adequate scores, require sequencing confirmation. Moreover, morphological characteristics should be not ignored; in the case of V. humicola, yellow, dry, cerebriform colonies on Sabouraud dextrose or blood agar can help with identification [35].

To clarify the analytical performance independent of differences in database coverage, we recalculated identification rates for species included in all three IVD databases (Supplemental Data Fig. S1). Correct identification rates subsequently increased across all systems; Sirius (initial on-plate: 89.5%; repeat on-plate: 94.7%; in-tube: 89.5%), PRIME (92.9%, 94.6%, and 94.6%, respectively), and ASTA (80.4%, 94.1%, and 86.3%, respectively). Given that the correct identification rates after additional on-plate extraction were comparable across all platforms, their analytical performance for species represented in the IVD databases appears largely similar. However, Sirius and PRIME outperformed ASTA in the initial run, reducing the frequency of retesting and associated cost and labor, making them more suitable for routine clinical practice.

Sirius and PRIME distinguished one of both C. deuterogattii isolates from C. neoformans, reporting it as C. gattii (showing a complex-level accuracy). However, both systems identified the other isolate as C. neoformans (classified as “incorrect identification,” as these species belong to different complexes). This is not surprising: C. deuterogattii and C. neoformans share highly similar protein spectra, and multilocus sequence data may be required for definitive identification. ASTA returned “no identification” for both C. deuterogattii isolates due to absent database entries. The results of studies involving custom libraries have shown that MALDI-TOF can resolve major molecular types within the C. neoformans/C. gattii lineage; however, current commercial databases lack separate entries for several newly recognized species (e.g., C. deuterogattii and C. bacillisporus) [4, 5]. Our findings reflect the present state of database coverage and not intrinsic technological shortcomings. Nonetheless, laboratories in regions where the C. gattii complex is prevalent should consider supplementing MALDI-TOF results with molecular typing results for finer species-level resolution, especially during outbreak investigations or for surveillance. Routine reporting often groups C. deuterogattii under the C. gattii complex, which aligns with current practice and MALDI databases constraints.

Our findings provide insights into the appropriate handling of uncommon Cryptococcus-related species, such as V. humicola and N. liquefaciens. Although no longer classified within Cryptococcus, some libraries still list these species under the former Cryptococcus genus. Accurate identification and differentiation of these Cryptococcus spp. is clinically important. Sirius and PRIME readily identified V. humicola, presumably because their databases include this organism (likely as C. humicola). In contrast, ASTA failed to recognize V. humicola, highlighting that certain uncommon yeasts may be absent from its library, leading to identification failure despite correct spectral acquisition. For N. liquefaciens, Sirius reported N. diffluens, whereas PRIME returned “no identification.” Ensuring that clinically relevant uncommon yeasts are represented in MALDI-TOF databases is important for comprehensive coverage. Both ASTA and Sirius have previously achieved >91% overall accuracy in broad yeast identification [19]; however, our focused evaluation of Cryptococcus revealed that differences in database completeness become much more evident when uncommon or taxonomically challenging species are included.

Our findings highlight database coverage as a limiting factor for species identification; however, database coverage is a dynamic issue as manufacturers update their libraries. Previous comparisons have revealed similar gaps in databases in other systems, such as VITEK MS, which yielded the highest number of “unidentified” organisms because many species were absent from its library [26], aligning with our observation that PRIME did not identify N. liquefaciens owing to absence from the database, whereas Sirius’s more comprehensive library enabled identification of the isolate at the genus level. Clinically, a “no identification” result is safer than a high-confidence incorrect call, as it prevents therapeutic misdirection and triggers confirmatory testing. None of the platforms generated high-confidence misidentifications outside the Cryptococcus genus in our study, highlighting their practical safety.

This study has a couple of limitations. First, the sample size and composition were constrained (59 clinical isolates, 51 of which were C. neoformans). This heavy weighting toward C. neoformans reflects clinical reality (the vast majority of cryptococcal infections are caused by C. neoformans); however, it meant that only a small number of C. gattii complex isolates were included. Nonetheless, because nearly all Cryptococcus clinical isolates in a recent epidemiological study in Korea were found to be C. neoformans [36], we believe that this coverage is sufficient. Second, we evaluated each MALDI-TOF platform with a single version of its database (i.e., the latest versions at the time of testing). As databases improve continually, our performance results represent a snapshot in time. Possibly, some gaps identified in this study will be filled with newer releases, underscoring the need for dynamic library updates to fill the database gaps.

For clinical laboratories, understanding the MS system’s database coverage is essential for interpreting “no identification” results and deciding when to use alternative methods. Additional on-plate extraction instead of in-tube extraction sufficed to readily improve identification rates. Our data indicate that commercial MALDI-TOF MS databases need to be updated to help accurately identify Cryptococcus spp., which could reduce delays in antifungal therapy and enhance patient outcomes.
