Effectiveness of various exercise interventions on peak VO2 and quality of life in patients with heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF): a network meta-analysis
Authors: Yanzhi Lan, Meng Wu, Sa Zhang, Zhengling Xie, Jinju Wang, Jiantao Guo, Wei Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a prevalent and challenging subtype of heart failure, with limited effective treatment options, especially for improving functional capacity and quality of life. Exercise interventions have shown potential benefits for patients with HFpEF; however, the relative efficacy of different exercise modalities remains unclear.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate and compare the effects of various exercise interventions on peak oxygen uptake (peak VO2) and quality of life in patients with HFpEF using a network meta-analysis approach.MethodsA systematic search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating exercise interventions in HFpEF patients. Eligible studies included those comparing exercise modalities such as high-intensity interval training (HIIT), moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT), inspiratory muscle training (IMT), usual care (UC), and no-exercise control. Data on peak VO2 (mL/kg/min) and Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLWHF) scores were extracted. A network meta-analysis was performed to assess relative efficacy, and surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) values were calculated for each intervention.ResultsA total of 14 RCTs comprising 810 participants were included. For peak VO2, compared to no exercise (medical treatment without structured exercise), IMT showed the largest numerical improvement (mean difference [MD] 3.29 mL/kg/min; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.76 to 5.82), followed by HIIT (MD 2.77 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: 0.85 to 4.69) and MICT (MD 1.90 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: 0.63 to 3.17). Usual care showed no significant difference from no exercise (MD 0.36; 95% CI: -1.77 to 2.49). Head-to-head comparisons among HIIT, MICT, and IMT did not reach statistical significance. Regarding quality of life, IMT demonstrated a significant reduction in MLWHF scores compared to UC (MD -10.20; 95% CI: -19.13 to -1.28). MICT and HIIT showed numerical improvements that did not reach statistical significance (MICT vs UC: MD -6.00, 95% CI: -16.28 to 4.28; HIIT vs UC: MD -6.00, 95% CI: -22.46 to 10.46). SUCRA rankings indicated that IMT had the highest probability of being the best intervention for peak VO2 (SUCRA = 88.5%) and quality of life (SUCRA = 80.7%), while HIIT ranked second for peak VO2 (SUCRA = 88.2%).ConclusionAmong the exercise modalities analyzed, IMT and HIIT demonstrated the most substantial improvements in peak VO2 for patients with HFpEF, whereas IMT showed the greatest benefit for enhancing quality of life. However, head-to-head differences among exercise modalities were not statistically significant, and peak VO2 is a composite outcome reflecting multiple physiological systems. These findings suggest that exercise intervention selection should be individualized based on patient-specific limitations and therapeutic goals in HFpEF management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-026-05787-9.Keywords: HFpEF, Exercise intervention, Network meta-analysis, Peak VO2, Quality of life

Introduction

Heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) is a common and increasingly recognized subtype of heart failure, representing nearly half of all heart failure cases [1]. HFpEF is especially prevalent among older adults, with incidence rates rising as the population ages. Unlike heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF), which is more extensively studied and treated, HFpEF poses unique clinical challenges due to its complex pathophysiology, involving systemic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and ventricular stiffness [2]. Patients with HFpEF often experience debilitating symptoms, including exercise intolerance, fatigue, and dyspnea, which significantly impact their functional capacity and quality of life [3]. Beyond the personal toll on patients, HFpEF is associated with high morbidity, frequent hospitalizations, and substantial healthcare costs. Current pharmacological options for HFpEF are limited and have not demonstrated the same survival benefits as treatments for HFrEF [4]. This has led to growing interest in non-pharmacological interventions, with exercise therapy emerging as a promising approach to improve functional outcomes and reduce symptoms in HFpEF patients.

Exercise interventions offer several physiological benefits for patients with HFpEF, addressing key limitations in both cardiovascular and skeletal muscle function that contribute to the disease’s symptom burden. Regular physical activity has been shown to improve vascular endothelial function, reduce ventricular stiffness, and enhance the efficiency of oxygen utilization by skeletal muscles, which are often impaired in HFpEF patients [4]. These adaptations can lead to improvements in peak oxygen uptake (VO2), a critical marker of aerobic capacity and exercise tolerance [5]. Furthermore, exercise can help alleviate symptoms such as fatigue and dyspnea, ultimately enhancing quality of life and reducing daily functional limitations. Previous studies have investigated various exercise modalities, including high-intensity interval training (HIIT), moderate-intensity continuous training (MICT), and inspiratory muscle training (IMT), each showing promise in improving specific clinical outcomes in HFpEF patients. However, the optimal exercise modality for maximizing benefits in HFpEF remains unclear, as studies have reported mixed results, and no single approach has emerged as the standard recommendation.

The objective of this study is to evaluate and compare the effects of different exercise interventions on peak VO2 and quality of life in patients with HFpEF. To achieve this, we employed a network meta-analysis, a robust methodological approach that enables the synthesis of data from multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This approach not only allows for direct comparisons between exercise interventions but also facilitates indirect comparisons across different exercise modalities, even when they have not been directly compared within individual studies. By leveraging the power of network meta-analysis, this study provides a comprehensive assessment of the relative effectiveness of various exercise types, including HIIT, MICT, IMT, and others, on key outcomes in HFpEF.

Methods

Study design and search strategy

This study was conducted as a systematic review and network meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) extension statement for network meta-analyses [6]. The aim was to evaluate and compare the effects of various exercise interventions on peak VO2 and quality of life in patients with HFpEF. A comprehensive literature search was performed across major electronic databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library. The search strategy combined keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms related to four domains: (1) population—“heart failure with preserved ejection fraction” OR “HFpEF” OR “diastolic heart failure”; (2) intervention—“exercise” OR “exercise training” OR “high-intensity interval training” OR “HIIT” OR “moderate-intensity continuous training” OR “MICT” OR “inspiratory muscle training” OR “IMT” OR “aerobic exercise” OR “physical activity” OR “cardiac rehabilitation”; (3) outcome—“peak oxygen uptake” OR “peak VO2 ” OR “VO2 max” OR “exercise capacity” OR “quality of life” OR “MLWHF”; and (4) study design—“randomized controlled trial” OR “RCT.” Boolean operators (AND, OR) were used to combine search terms across domains. The search covered studies published from the inception of each database through January 2026. Reference lists of included studies and relevant review articles were manually screened to identify any additional eligible studies.

Network meta-analysis framework and control group considerations

This network meta-analysis integrated studies with varying control conditions, including usual care, no exercise, and active exercise comparators (e.g., MICT as the comparator for HIIT). In network meta-analysis, the designation of a particular arm as “control” is less critical than in pairwise meta-analysis; the essential requirement is that each study contributes direct comparisons between treatment nodes (HIIT, MICT, IMT, UC, or no exercise), thereby forming a connected evidence network. Studies employing active comparators provide valuable head-to-head evidence that strengthens network connectivity and enables more precise estimation of relative treatment effects across all intervention types.

Two types of non-exercise control conditions were included and analyzed as separate nodes in the network:

No-Exercise Control: Participants received standard medical treatment for HFpEF but were not enrolled in any structured exercise program and received no specific physical activity counseling.

No-Exercise Control: Participants received standard medical treatment for HFpEF but were not enrolled in any structured exercise program and received no specific physical activity counseling.

Usual Care (UC): Participants received standard medical treatment plus general lifestyle counseling, which may have included non-specific recommendations to remain physically active, but without any supervised or structured exercise training program.

Usual Care (UC): Participants received standard medical treatment plus general lifestyle counseling, which may have included non-specific recommendations to remain physically active, but without any supervised or structured exercise training program.

These two control types represent different levels of non-intervention. The rationale for using different reference comparators for different outcomes was as follows: for peak VO2, “no exercise” was used as the reference to isolate the physiological effects attributable specifically to structured exercise training; for quality of life (MLWHF scores), “usual care” was used as the reference to provide a more pragmatically relevant comparison against routine clinical practice, where patients typically receive general activity advice. While IMT targets different physiological mechanisms (respiratory muscle strengthening) compared with systemic aerobic exercise interventions (HIIT and MICT), it was included in the network because all three modalities aim to improve the same clinical outcomes (exercise capacity and quality of life) in the same patient population. In HFpEF, both cardiovascular dysfunction and respiratory muscle weakness contribute to exercise intolerance, making IMT a clinically relevant comparator. Furthermore, clinicians must choose among these modalities in practice, and comparative effectiveness evidence—even across mechanistically different interventions—is valuable for informing individualized treatment decisions.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria

(1) Study design: Only RCTs were included to maintain methodological rigor and minimize bias. (2) Population: Studies enrolling adult patients (aged ≥ 18 years) diagnosed with HFpEF based on clinical diagnostic criteria, including preserved left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) with signs and symptoms of heart failure. Studies using varying LVEF thresholds (e.g., ≥ 40%, ≥ 45%, or ≥ 50%) were included, and this heterogeneity was addressed through sensitivity analysis. (3) Intervention: Trials assessing one or more structured exercise interventions, such as HIIT, MICT, or IMT. Usual care or no-exercise control arms were also eligible as comparator conditions. (4) Outcomes: Studies reporting at least one of the following: peak oxygen uptake (peak VO2, mL/kg/min) as a marker of exercise capacity, or quality of life measured by the Minnesota Living with Heart Failure Questionnaire (MLWHF).

Exclusion criteria

(1) Studies focused exclusively on patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF). (2) Non-randomized study designs, including observational studies, cohort studies, and case–control studies. (3) Studies that did not report data on either peak VO2 or quality of life as defined above. (4) Studies not published in English, due to resource limitations for translation. (5) Conference abstracts, dissertations, letters, editorials, and unpublished studies without full peer review. (6) Secondary analyses of previously included trials to avoid double-counting of the same patient population.

Data extraction and outcome measures

Data extraction was conducted independently by two reviewers (Y.L. and M.W.) using a standardized data extraction form. Discrepancies were resolved by consensus or through consultation with a third reviewer (J.G.). The following data were extracted from each included study: Study Characteristics: first author, publication year, country/region, sample size, mean age of participants, diagnostic criteria for HFpEF (including LVEF threshold), and exercise intervention duration. Intervention Details: type of exercise intervention (HIIT, MICT, IMT, or other), intensity, frequency, session duration, total program duration, and details of the control condition (usual care or no exercise).

Outcome measures

Primary outcome: peak oxygen uptake (peak VO2, mL/kg/min), a validated and prognostically important measure of exercise capacity in heart failure patients.

Secondary outcome: quality of life, assessed by the MLWHF questionnaire. Only studies using the MLWHF were included in the quality of life analysis to ensure outcome homogeneity and avoid methodological complications arising from combining different assessment instruments.

For studies with multiple intervention arms (e.g., Study 10: IMT, FES, and IMT + FES), only arms corresponding to pre-specified network nodes (HIIT, MICT, IMT, UC, or no exercise) were included. Specifically, functional electrical stimulation (FES) and combined IMT + FES arms were excluded from the network analysis, as these interventions were not represented in other included studies and could not form connected nodes within the evidence network [7].

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in each included study was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool (RoB 1.0), a standardized and widely recognized instrument for evaluating the methodological quality of RCTs. The following domains were evaluated: (1) random sequence generation (selection bias); (2) allocation concealment (selection bias); (3) blinding of participants and personnel (performance bias); (4) blinding of outcome assessment (detection bias); (5) incomplete outcome data (attrition bias); (6) selective reporting (reporting bias); and (7) other sources of bias. Each domain was rated as low risk, unclear risk, or high risk. Risk of bias assessment was performed independently by two reviewers (Y.L. and S.Z.), with disagreements resolved by discussion or consultation with a third reviewer (J.G.).

Sensitivity analysis and publication bias

Several sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the primary findings: (1) Exclusion of studies using LVEF thresholds below 50% (i.e., restricting to studies with LVEF ≥ 50%) to address potential inclusion of patients with heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF). (2) Exclusion of IMT from the network to examine the robustness of findings among aerobic exercise modalities only and to assess the impact of including mechanistically distinct interventions. Publication bias was assessed by visual inspection of comparison-adjusted funnel plots for asymmetry, with symmetrical distributions suggesting a low likelihood of publication bias or selective reporting.

Statistical analysis

The network meta-analysis was conducted using a frequentist framework implemented in Stata software with the “network” and “mvmeta” packages. This approach integrates both direct evidence (from studies directly comparing two interventions) and indirect evidence (derived through a common comparator) within a single analytical framework, enabling simultaneous comparison of all exercise modalities. Effect sizes for continuous outcomes were calculated as mean differences (MD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For the primary outcome, peak VO2 (mL/kg/min) was evaluated as a measure of aerobic exercise capacity. For the secondary outcome, the mean difference in MLWHF questionnaire scores was used, where lower scores indicate better quality of life. The consistency assumption (i.e., agreement between direct and indirect evidence) was evaluated using node-splitting analysis, which compares direct and indirect estimates for each pairwise comparison. A p-value < 0.05 was considered indicative of significant inconsistency. Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using the I2 statistic and the between-study variance (τ2). An I2 value of 0–25% was considered low, 25–50% moderate, 50–75% substantial, and > 75% considerable heterogeneity. Interventions were ranked using surface under the cumulative ranking (SUCRA) values, which represent the probability of each intervention being the most effective for each outcome. SUCRA values range from 0% (worst) to 100% (best), with higher values indicating a greater likelihood that the intervention is among the most effective. Rankograms (ranking probability plots) were generated to visualize the posterior ranking distribution for each intervention.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A total of 1,668 records were identified through database searches, with no additional records sourced from other sources. After removing 124 duplicates, 1,544 records remained for screening. Of these, 1,514 records were excluded based on title and abstract review, as they did not meet the inclusion criteria due to factors such as non-HFpEF populations, lack of exercise interventions, or non-RCT study designs. The remaining 30 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 16 were excluded at this stage for the following reasons: review articles (n = 13), articles that could not be retrieved (n = 2), and secondary analysis of an already included trial (n = 1). Ultimately, 14 studies met all inclusion criteria and were included in both the qualitative synthesis and the quantitative synthesis (network meta-analysis) [8–18]. The study selection process is outlined in the PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1). The 14 included RCTs were published between 2004 and 2022 and conducted across multiple countries, including the United States (n = 6), Germany (n = 2), Japan (n = 2), Spain (n = 2), Australia (n = 1), and Brazil (n = 1). The total number of participants across all studies was 810. Sample sizes for exercise intervention groups ranged from 8 to 120 participants, with corresponding control groups ranging from 6 to 60 participants. Exercise intervention durations varied from 4 weeks to 12 months, with the majority of studies employing programs of 12 to 16 weeks. The studies investigated several exercise modalities, including HIIT, MICT, IMT, and structured walking programs. LVEF thresholds used to define HFpEF varied across studies: nine studies used LVEF ≥ 50% (or > 50%), three studies used LVEF ≥ 45% (or > 45%), and two studies used broader criteria. The primary outcome across studies was peak VO2 (mL/kg/min), and quality of life was assessed using the MLWHF questionnaire in the subset of studies reporting this outcome. The mean age of participants generally ranged from 53 to 76 years in the exercise groups. Detailed study characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment for the 14 included studies is summarized in Fig. 2. The majority of studies demonstrated low risk of bias for random sequence generation (approximately 85% rated as low risk) and allocation concealment. However, due to the inherent difficulty of blinding participants and personnel in exercise intervention trials, performance bias was rated as high or unclear risk in most studies. Blinding of outcome assessment was adequately reported in approximately half of the trials, with the remainder rated as unclear risk. Attrition bias was rated as low risk in most studies, indicating acceptable rates of participant retention and complete outcome reporting. Selective reporting bias was low across nearly all studies. Overall, the included studies were judged to have acceptable methodological quality, with the primary source of potential bias being the lack of participant blinding, which is an unavoidable limitation of exercise intervention research.

Network structure and treatment comparisons

The evidence network comprised five intervention nodes: HIIT, MICT, IMT, UC, and no exercise. These were analyzed as separate nodes within the network. The network structure for the two outcomes is illustrated in Fig. 3. For peak VO2 (Fig. 3A), the network included comparisons across all five nodes. MICT was the most extensively studied intervention (8 studies, n = 186), followed by usual care (6 studies, n = 125), HIIT (4 studies, n = 102), no exercise (4 studies, n = 86), and IMT (3 studies, n = 37). The most common direct comparisons were between MICT and usual care, and between MICT and no exercise. For quality of life (Fig. 3B), the network included fewer studies. MICT was again the most represented node (5 studies, n = 116), followed by usual care (3 studies, n = 44), no exercise (3 studies, n = 73), IMT (2 studies, n = 29), and HIIT (1 study, n = 10). Direct comparisons were available primarily between MICT and no exercise, MICT and usual care, and IMT and usual care. For peak VO2 outcomes, effects are reported relative to “no exercise” to isolate the physiological effects of structured exercise training. For quality of life outcomes, effects are reported relative to “usual care” to provide a pragmatically relevant comparison against routine clinical practice.

Primary outcome: peak VO₂

The network meta-analysis results for peak VO2 are presented in Figs. 4 and 8A. Compared to no exercise, all three structured exercise interventions demonstrated improvements in peak VO2. IMT showed the largest numerical improvement (MD 3.29 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: 0.76 to 5.82), followed by HIIT (MD 2.77 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: 0.85 to 4.69) and MICT (MD 1.90 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: 0.63 to 3.17). All three comparisons reached statistical significance, as the 95% confidence intervals excluded zero. Usual care did not differ significantly from no exercise (MD 0.36 mL/kg/min; 95% CI: −1.77 to 2.49). The complete pairwise comparison matrix is shown in the league table (Fig. 8A). Notably, head-to-head comparisons among the three exercise modalities did not reveal statistically significant differences: IMT versus HIIT (MD 0.52; 95% CI: −1.86 to 2.90), IMT versus MICT (MD 1.39; 95% CI: −0.80 to 3.59), and HIIT versus MICT (MD 0.87; 95% CI: −0.57 to 2.31). All three exercise interventions were significantly superior to usual care: IMT versus UC (MD 3.65; 95% CI: 2.28 to 5.02), HIIT versus UC (MD 3.13; 95% CI: 1.18 to 5.08), and MICT versus UC (MD 2.26; 95% CI: 0.54 to 3.97) (Figs. 4 and 8A).

Secondary outcome: quality of life

The quality of life outcomes, assessed using the MLWHF questionnaire, are presented in Figs. 5 and 8B. Lower MLWHF scores indicate better quality of life; therefore, negative mean differences favor the row intervention. Compared to usual care, IMT demonstrated the largest improvement in quality of life with a statistically significant reduction in MLWHF scores (MD −10.20; 95% CI: −19.13 to −1.28). MICT and HIIT also showed numerical improvements compared to usual care, but these did not reach statistical significance (MICT vs UC: MD −6.00; 95% CI: −16.28 to 4.28; HIIT vs UC: MD −6.00; 95% CI: −22.46 to 10.46). No exercise did not differ significantly from usual care (MD −1.00; 95% CI: −13.57 to 11.56). The complete pairwise comparison matrix (Fig. 8B) showed that none of the head-to-head comparisons among the three exercise modalities reached statistical significance: IMT versus MICT (MD −4.20; 95% CI: −17.82 to 9.41), IMT versus HIIT (MD −4.20; 95% CI: −22.92 to 14.52), and MICT versus HIIT (MD −0.00; 95% CI: −12.85 to 12.85). IMT also showed a numerical but non-significant advantage over no exercise (MD −11.21; 95% CI: −26.62 to 4.21).

Ranking and probability of efficacy

The SUCRA values and rankograms for both outcomes are presented in Figs. 6 and 7. For peak VO2, SUCRA values ranked IMT highest (SUCRA = 88.5%), closely followed by HIIT (SUCRA = 88.2%) and MICT (SUCRA = 66.5%). Usual care (SUCRA = 44.9%) and no exercise (SUCRA = 12.2%) ranked lowest. The rankograms (Fig. 7A) confirmed that both IMT and HIIT had the highest probability of being ranked as the best or second-best intervention for improving peak VO2, while no exercise had the highest probability of being ranked last. For quality of life, SUCRA values ranked IMT highest (SUCRA = 80.7%), followed by MICT (SUCRA = 60.5%) and HIIT (SUCRA = 60.5%). No exercise ranked fourth (SUCRA = 19.0%), and usual care ranked lowest (SUCRA = 22.6%). The rankograms (Fig. 7B) confirmed that IMT had the highest probability of being the most effective intervention for improving quality of life. These rankings indicate that IMT had the highest overall probability of being the best intervention for both peak VO2 and quality of life, although the differences between IMT and HIIT were marginal for peak VO2. Importantly, the SUCRA ranking of IMT should be interpreted with particular caution, as it is derived from only three trials contributing to the peak VO2 network and two trials contributing to the quality of life network, with a combined sample size of fewer than 40 participants per node. SUCRA values are sensitive to the number and precision of contributing studies, and rankings derived from a limited evidence base may be unstable and subject to change as additional data become available. These rankings therefore reflect relative probabilities within the current evidence network and should not be interpreted as establishing definitive clinical superiority of IMT over HIIT or MICT. However, given that peak VO2 is a composite outcome influenced by multiple physiological systems, and that head-to-head comparisons among active interventions were not statistically significant, these rankings should be interpreted as reflecting relative probabilities rather than definitive clinical superiority.

Publication bias assessment

Publication bias was assessed using comparison-adjusted funnel plots for both outcomes (Fig. 9). For peak VO2 (Fig. 9A), the funnel plot showed a generally symmetrical distribution of study-specific effect sizes around the comparison-specific pooled effect, with most data points falling within the pseudo 95% confidence limits. For MLWHF scores (Fig. 9B), the funnel plot also displayed a largely symmetrical distribution, although the smaller number of studies contributing to the quality of life network limits the interpretability of this assessment. Overall, the funnel plot patterns suggest a low likelihood of substantial publication bias for both outcomes.

Discussion

This network meta-analysis, encompassing 14 RCTs and 810 participants, provides a comprehensive comparative assessment of exercise interventions for patients with HFpEF. The principal findings are as follows. First, for peak VO2, all three structured exercise interventions (IMT, HIIT, and MICT) significantly improved exercise capacity compared to no exercise. IMT and HIIT demonstrated similar and the highest SUCRA rankings (88.5% and 88.2%, respectively), followed by MICT (66.5%). However, head-to-head comparisons among the three modalities did not reach statistical significance, indicating that while all are beneficial, definitive superiority of one modality over another cannot be established from the available evidence. Second, for quality of life, IMT was the only intervention that demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in MLWHF scores compared to usual care (MD −10.20; 95% CI: −19.13 to −1.28), and ranked highest among all interventions (SUCRA = 80.7%). MICT and HIIT showed numerical but non-significant improvements. These findings carry important implications for HFpEF management, a condition characterized by limited pharmacological treatment options and persistent symptoms that impair functional capacity and quality of life. The evidence supports exercise as a valuable non-pharmacological intervention and highlights the potential for individualized exercise prescriptions tailored to patient-specific therapeutic goals.

Comparison with previous research

The findings of this network meta-analysis align with previous studies suggesting that exercise interventions can significantly benefit patients with HFpEF, though our results provide a clearer hierarchy of efficacy across different exercise modalities. Prior research indicates that both HIIT and MICT can improve exercise capacity in HFpEF. Importantly, the multicentre OptimEx trial directly compared HIIT with MICT in a large HFpEF cohort of 180 patients, providing key head-to-head evidence that informed our network analysis. However, the overall number of direct comparisons across all available trials remains limited, and the relative differences between training modalities remain uncertain—consistent with the overlapping confidence intervals observed in our network estimates. Our results indicate that IMT and HIIT achieved similarly high SUCRA rankings for peak VO2 (88.5% and 88.2%, respectively), with IMT showing a marginally larger point estimate (MD 3.29 vs. 2.77 mL/kg/min compared to no exercise). This finding warrants cautious interpretation given the small number of IMT trials (n = 3 for peak VO2; n = 2 for quality of life) and the limited total sample size (fewer than 40 participants per IMT node). SUCRA rankings are inherently sensitive to the precision and volume of contributing evidence; when a treatment node is informed by few small trials, its ranking probability may be disproportionately influenced by sampling variability rather than true treatment effect. The non-significant head-to-head comparison between IMT and HIIT (MD 0.52; 95% CI: −1.86 to 2.90) further underscores that the apparent superiority of IMT in probabilistic rankings should not be equated with demonstrated clinical superiority. Future trials with larger IMT cohorts are needed to confirm or refute these preliminary rankings. The comparable ranking of HIIT is consistent with evidence from other cardiovascular populations indicating that high-intensity intervals may drive substantial cardiovascular and metabolic adaptations.

The observed benefits of IMT on quality of life also support previous research demonstrating the role of respiratory training in enhancing dyspnea and overall functional status in HFpEF patients. IMT has been shown to improve inspiratory muscle strength, which may alleviate symptoms related to respiratory muscle weakness, a common issue in HFpEF [19]. However, few prior meta-analyses have distinguished IMT as a primary quality-of-life intervention, making this a unique finding in our study that underscores the importance of IMT for patients who prioritize symptom relief and daily function.

Clinical implications

These findings have practical implications for the selection and personalization of exercise interventions in HFpEF management: For patients whose primary goal is to improve exercise capacity and cardiorespiratory fitness, both IMT and HIIT represent effective options. Given the similar SUCRA rankings, the choice between them may depend on patient-specific factors: HIIT may be preferred for patients with predominantly cardiovascular limitations and adequate baseline fitness to tolerate high-intensity exercise, whereas IMT may be particularly suitable for patients with significant dyspnea or documented respiratory muscle weakness, as it specifically addresses ventilatory limitations. For patients who prioritize symptom relief and quality of life improvement, IMT demonstrated the most robust evidence, being the only modality to achieve a statistically significant improvement in MLWHF scores compared to usual care. IMT offers the additional advantage of being a low-risk, home-based intervention that can be implemented independently, making it accessible for frail or deconditioned patients who may not tolerate supervised aerobic training programs initially. A combined or sequential approach may also be considered. Patients could begin with IMT to alleviate respiratory symptoms and improve exercise tolerance, then gradually incorporate HIIT or MICT to further enhance cardiovascular fitness. Such a strategy would address multiple physiological domains contributing to exercise intolerance in HFpEF. It is essential to recognize that HIIT and MICT exert their primary effects through systemic cardiovascular and skeletal muscle adaptations—including improvements in cardiac output, peripheral oxygen extraction, and mitochondrial efficiency—whereas IMT specifically targets respiratory muscle strength and endurance, thereby reducing the ventilatory limitation that contributes substantially to dyspnea and exercise intolerance in many HFpEF patients. These mechanistically distinct pathways mean that the SUCRA-based treatment rankings in this network should not be interpreted as establishing a universal hierarchy of therapeutic superiority. Rather, they represent probabilistic estimates of relative effectiveness within a connected evidence network that includes interventions targeting different physiological domains. The clinical implication is not that IMT is universally superior to HIIT or MICT, but rather that the optimal exercise modality should be selected based on the patient’s predominant physiological limitation: aerobic training modalities for patients with primarily cardiovascular constraints, and IMT for those with significant dyspnea or documented inspiratory muscle weakness. A personalized, mechanism-informed approach to exercise prescription is therefore recommended over a rankings-based universal prescription.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This study has several methodological strengths. A comprehensive search strategy across four major databases minimized the risk of missing relevant studies. The network meta-analysis framework enabled both direct and indirect comparisons among exercise modalities, providing a hierarchical assessment of relative efficacy that would not be possible through conventional pairwise meta-analysis. Sensitivity analyses, including restriction to studies using LVEF ≥ 50% and exclusion of IMT from the network, confirmed the robustness of the primary findings. Funnel plots showed generally symmetrical distributions, suggesting a low likelihood of substantial publication bias.

However, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the definition of HFpEF varied across included studies. While the majority of studies (nine of fourteen) used LVEF ≥ 50%, others employed lower thresholds (≥ 45% or ≥ 40%), potentially including patients who would currently be classified as having heart failure with mildly reduced ejection fraction (HFmrEF). HFmrEF may represent a distinct pathophysiological phenotype with different exercise training responses. Our sensitivity analysis restricted to studies with LVEF ≥ 50% showed consistent results, but the possibility that inclusion of HFmrEF patients influenced the primary estimates cannot be definitively excluded. Second, several included studies did not require objective diagnostic criteria beyond LVEF, such as natriuretic peptide elevation or echocardiographic evidence of diastolic dysfunction or structural abnormalities. This diagnostic heterogeneity may limit external validity and generalizability to broader HFpEF populations defined by contemporary guideline criteria. Third, due to the nature of exercise interventions, blinding of participants and personnel was not feasible in most studies, introducing potential performance bias. While some studies employed blinded outcome assessors, the lack of full blinding may have influenced the assessment of subjective outcomes such as quality of life. Fourth, the inclusion of interventions with heterogeneous mechanisms (respiratory training versus systemic aerobic exercise) within the same network may partially violate the transitivity assumption underlying network meta-analysis. Although we addressed this through sensitivity analysis excluding IMT—which confirmed the stability of aerobic exercise rankings—the rankings comparing IMT against HIIT and MICT should be interpreted within the context of different therapeutic targets rather than as a strict universal hierarchy. Fifth, the quality of life network was informed by fewer studies (particularly for HIIT, with only one study contributing data), resulting in wider confidence intervals and reduced statistical power for this outcome. The quality of life rankings are therefore less precise than those for peak VO2 and should be interpreted with appropriate caution. Sixth, variability in exercise intervention protocols (including intensity, duration, frequency, and supervision) and exercise intervention durations (ranging from 4 weeks to 12 months) may introduce heterogeneity. Although network meta-analysis can partially account for such variability, these differences may still influence the consistency and comparability of results across studies. Seventh, most included trials enrolled older adults with a substantial comorbidity burden, which is characteristic of the typical HFpEF population referred to cardiac rehabilitation and therefore supports the clinical relevance of our findings. However, generalizability may be more limited for underrepresented subgroups, such as very frail individuals, those with severe non-cardiac exercise limitations, or specific HFpEF phenotypes that were not consistently captured across trials. Eighth, an important limitation is the reliance on peak VO2 as the primary outcome for intervention ranking. While peak VO2 is a well-validated prognostic marker in heart failure, it is a composite physiological measure that does not fully capture the clinical heterogeneity or patient-centered benefits of different interventions. Future research should incorporate multidimensional outcome assessments, including submaximal exercise performance, daily physical activity levels, and patient-reported outcomes, to provide a more comprehensive evaluation of intervention effectiveness. Lastly, potential confounders such as baseline fitness levels, adherence rates, and concurrent medication changes were not uniformly reported across studies, which may have influenced individual responsiveness to different exercise modalities.

Recommendations for future research

Several priorities for future research emerge from the present findings. First, larger RCTs with extended follow-up periods are needed to assess the long-term sustainability of exercise-induced improvements in peak VO2 and quality of life, as well as the potential for exercise to modify disease progression, reduce hospitalization rates, and improve survival in HFpEF. Second, future trials should adopt uniform, contemporary diagnostic criteria for HFpEF—incorporating LVEF ≥ 50%, natriuretic peptide levels, and diastolic dysfunction indices—to enhance external validity and enable more precise subgroup analyses based on diagnostic certainty. Third, head-to-head trials directly comparing IMT, HIIT, and MICT— ideally with adequate statistical power and standardized protocols—would help resolve the uncertainty regarding the relative superiority of these modalities that persists despite our network analysis. Fourth, combination interventions (e.g., HIIT combined with IMT) represent a particularly promising research avenue. Such hybrid programs could simultaneously address both cardiovascular and respiratory limitations, potentially yielding synergistic benefits beyond what single-modality interventions can achieve. Fifth, stratified analyses examining the influence of age, sex, baseline fitness, comorbidity burden, and HFpEF phenotype on treatment response would support more personalized exercise recommendations. Sixth, cost-effectiveness analyses of structured exercise programs in HFpEF are critically needed. Understanding the economic viability and implementation feasibility of these interventions would provide healthcare systems and policymakers with evidence to inform resource allocation and clinical guideline development.

Conclusion

This network meta-analysis of 14 RCTs involving 810 patients with HFpEF provides a comprehensive comparison of exercise interventions for improving exercise capacity and quality of life. IMT and HIIT demonstrated the largest improvements in peak VO₂ compared to no exercise, with closely matched SUCRA rankings, followed by MICT. For quality of life, IMT was the only intervention to achieve a statistically significant improvement in MLWHF scores compared to usual care, and ranked highest among all modalities. Notably, head-to-head comparisons among the three exercise modalities did not reach statistical significance for either outcome, and SUCRA rankings should be interpreted as reflecting relative probabilities rather than definitive clinical superiority. Furthermore, as peak VO₂ is a composite outcome influenced by multiple physiological systems, and different modalities may improve this measure through distinct mechanisms, clinical selection should be individualized rather than based solely on treatment rankings. These findings support the integration of structured exercise as a core component of HFpEF management and highlight the value of personalized exercise prescriptions tailored to patient-specific limitations and therapeutic goals. Future large-scale trials with standardized HFpEF diagnostic criteria and direct head-to-head comparisons are needed to further refine these recommendations.
