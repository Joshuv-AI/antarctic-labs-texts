High-frequency energy fusion (HFEF) for nuclei segmentation with boundary-aware loss
Authors: Wenyang Yin, Wei He, Bing Shang, Bing Zhao, Xuening Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate nuclei segmentation in histopathological images is essential for quantitative pathology analysis but remains challenging due to nuclei clustering, morphological heterogeneity and weak or discontinuous boundaries. Although transformer-based models improve global context modeling, explicit enhancement of boundary-related features and effective suppression of false negatives (FNs) along nuclei edges are still limited. This study aimed to develop a boundary-enhanced nuclei segmentation framework by integrating high-frequency energy fusion (HFEF) with a sensitivity-aware loss.MethodsWe propose an HFEF framework that enhances nuclei boundary representation by integrating high-frequency components derived from the stationary wavelet transform (SWT) with Laplacian filtering. The resulting high-frequency energy map is fused as an additional input channel to enrich edge-related features in transformer-based segmentation models. In addition, a sensitivity-tuned loss (ST-Loss) is introduced to dynamically penalize low-confidence FNs, particularly in boundary regions. The proposed method was implemented in two edge-aware Swin Transformer-based segmentation models and evaluated with three publicly available histopathology and microscopy datasets. Performance was assessed using region-based metrics [Dice similarity coefficient (DSC) and intersection over union (IoU)] and boundary-aware metrics [Hausdorff distance (HDF) and boundary F1 score (BF1)]. Statistical significance was evaluated using paired one-tailed t-tests.ResultsAcross three datasets and two model architectures, HFEF consistently improved nuclei segmentation performance compared with baseline configurations. DSC and IoU increased by 1.07% and 2.20%, respectively (P<0.05). Boundary delineation was substantially improved, with a 27% reduction in average HDF and a 0.89% increase in BF1. Incorporation of ST-Loss further increased recall by 2.48% (P=0.005), indicating effective suppression of boundary-related FNs. Visual inspection confirmed sharper nuclei boundaries and fewer missed detections, particularly in clustered and overlapping nuclei.ConclusionsThe proposed HFEF framework, combined with sensitivity-aware loss optimization, enhances nuclei boundary representation and reduces false-negative errors, leading to improved segmentation accuracy and boundary precision. This approach is model-agnostic and has potential applicability to medical image segmentation tasks where accurate boundary delineation is critical. Introduction

Accurate nuclei segmentation is a cornerstone of histology image analysis, supporting critical applications such as disease diagnosis (1), treatment planning (2), and cellularphenotype classification (3). However, precise delineation remains challenging due to frequent clustering of nuclei, diverse morphologies, and complex edge structures (4). These challenges are exacerbated in histology images, where overlapping nuclei and intricate patterns often cause segmentation errors (5), potentially compromising diagnostic accuracy.

Deep learning has advanced nuclei segmentation substantially (6), with Transformer-based architectures (7,8) excelling at capturing global spatial relationships through self-attention. Many state-of-the-art methods employ multitask learning (MTL) frameworks that use edge detection as an auxiliary task (7,9). Yet, most lack explicit mechanisms to enhance edge-specific representations—critical for accurate boundary delineation—and rely on loss functions such as cross-entropy (CE) or dice loss (10) that treat all misclassifications equally, neglecting the greater impact of false negatives (FNs) in boundary regions.

Wavelet-based methods have shown promise in preserving high-frequency features important for edge representation (11,12). Discrete wavelet transform (DWT) has been integrated into convolutional neural network (CNN)-based nuclei segmentation (13-15), but its down sampling leads to spatial resolution loss and shift sensitivity (16), limiting its utility for fine boundary detection. In contrast, stationary wavelet transform (SWT) (12,17) avoids down sampling, preserves high-frequency detail, and offers shift invariance, making it well suited for Transformer-based segmentation—yet its integration in this context remains underexplored.

Loss design is another key factor for edge preservation. Standard CE loss (18) and variants like focal loss (19) and Tversky loss (20,21) can address class imbalance or tune false positive (FP)-FN tradeoffs but often require manual parameter tuning or lack dynamic FN suppression (22-24). This underscores the need for adaptive, sensitivity-oriented losses that automatically adjust to boundary uncertainty.

To address these gaps, we propose high-frequency energy fusion (HFEF), which integrates SWT-derived high-frequency components with Laplacian filtering to enrich edge features in Transformer-based nuclei segmentation. Unlike prior wavelet methods, HFEF selectively amplifies boundary-relevant details. We further introduce a sensitivity-tuned loss (ST-Loss) that dynamically penalizes FNs in edge regions, improving weak and discontinuous boundary detection. Evaluations on three benchmark datasets using Swin Transformer-based models demonstrate consistent improvements across Dice similarity coefficient (DSC), intersection over union (IoU), recall, and F1-score (Figure 1), highlighting the potential of our approach not only for nuclei segmentation but also for other biomedical applications requiring precise boundary delineation.

Methods

This study introduces a novel framework for nuclei segmentation that addresses the challenges of edge detection and optimization through two key innovations: HFEF and ST-Loss. HFEF combines the SWT with Laplacian filtering to extract and enhance high-frequency edge details, improving the model’s ability to delineate fine nuclei boundaries. ST-Loss complements this by dynamically adjusting penalties for FNs based on predicted probabilities, thereby prioritizing sensitivity while preserving F1 stability. Together, these techniques are designed to enhance edge segmentation and improve overall segmentation accuracy, particularly in MTL frameworks that leverage edge detection as an auxiliary task. Normalization was omitted because, in our experiments, it consistently reduced segmentation performance when applied to the SWT-derived high-frequency components. Empirically, we observed that normalization compressed the dynamic range of the LH/HL/HH coefficients, weakening local contrast that is critical for boundary enhancement. Although this observation was initially experimental, it is consistent with prior findings that certain normalization operations can attenuate wavelet coefficients associated with fine-scale edge structures (11). Based on this behaviour, we retained the unnormalized high-frequency maps to preserve edge-related intensity variation.

The specific design choices in HFEF were guided by both signal-processing principles and empirical stability considerations. SWT was adopted instead of decimated wavelet transforms due to its shift-invariance, which is critical for preserving spatial alignment of boundary structures. Multi-directional high-frequency sub-bands (LH, HL, and HH) were jointly fused to capture complementary edge information across orientations, rather than relying on a single directional component. Laplacian sharpening was applied with moderate kernel strength to enhance boundary contrast while avoiding amplification of high-frequency noise. These settings were selected based on consistent performance observed across datasets and were kept fixed throughout experiments to ensure fair comparison.

ST-Loss

Nuclei segmentation accuracy suffers when FNs occur, as missing boundaries lead to segmentation gaps and degraded performance. ST-Loss is designed to prioritize FN reduction over FPs by dynamically adjusting penalties based on the predicted probability p, applying exponentially larger penalties as p→0 (Figure 4). This focuses learning on hard-to-detect nuclei edges, commonly misclassified as background, improving recall while maintaining stable F1 scores.

Data preprocessing

All input images are resized to a consistent resolution of 512×512 to standardize dimensions across datasets. Pixel values are normalized to binary levels, where 0 represents the background and 1 represents the foreground, including nuclei or edges. HFEF, which combines SWT-based high-frequency map extraction and Laplacian filtering, is applied to generate an additional high-frequency channel. This pre-processing step produces a four-channel input (RGB + HFEF) to enhance the model’s edge detection capabilities during training and evaluation. Dataset-specific preprocessing is managed through dedicated data loaders, which handle tasks such as annotation parsing, pixel value normalization, and augmentation, tailored to the requirements of each dataset.

Implementation detail

Model configuration

The architectures utilized in this study include MoE-NuSeg (9) and TransNuSeg (7), both of which are designed to improve nuclei segmentation by leveraging edge information within an MTL framework based on a U-shaped Swin Transformer. TransNuSeg (7) features a single encoder and three decoders: a nuclei decoder, an edge decoder, and a cluster edge decoder. The latter two decoders perform auxiliary tasks that enhance the primary nuclei segmentation. In contrast, MoE-NuSeg (9) incorporates a mixture of experts (MoE) in each decoder layer, effectively unifying the three decoders of TransNuSeg into a single structure, benefiting from parameter sharing. The MoE mechanism utilizes an attention-based gating network to dynamically adjust the contributions of the three experts.

Training protocol

The datasets were split into training, validation, and testing sets in an 8:1:1 ratio. For patch-based datasets, this split was performed at the image (or WSI) level rather than at the patch level; all patches extracted from the same image or WSI were assigned exclusively to one split to avoid data leakage. Early stopping was employed, terminating training if the validation loss did not improve after 50 consecutive epochs. The segmentation loss was calculated using a weighted combination of DSC loss and CE loss, expressed as λ·DSC + (1 − λ)·CE for nuclei segmentation. For edge segmentation, CE loss was substituted with either focal loss or ST-Loss. Training was performed using the AdamW optimizer, with an initial learning rate and weight decay set to 1.0×10−4.

All experiments were performed on a single NVIDIA RTX 4090D GPU (24 GB VRAM), using Python 3.8.10 and PyTorch 1.11.0+cu113, running on an 18-core AMD EPYC 9754 processor with 64 GB of RAM.

Hyper-parameter selection

The γ parameter of ST-Loss was determined through a grid search over the range [0, 1] with a step size of 0.25, ensuring a systematic exploration of sensitivity tuning. The λ parameter, which balances the contributions of Dice loss and CE loss, was fixed at 0.6, following its effectiveness in previous studies (7,9,10). The tolerance of the Laplacian filter was set to 1 based on experimental comparisons, where a tolerance value of 1 provided slightly better boundary sharpness and segmentation accuracy compared to a value of 2.

Evaluation metrics

Segmentation performance was assessed using standard metrics—DSC, IoU, F1-score, precision, recall, and accuracy—consistent with prior work (7,9). These are widely used in segmentation and not redefined here for brevity. Accuracy is included as a high-level indicator, though it is less sensitive to small segmentation differences due to the large background pixel count.

Statistical analysis

Statistical significance was evaluated using a paired one-tailed t-test on per-image performance metrics, reflecting our directional hypothesis that the proposed enhancements (HFEF, HFEF + ST) improve segmentation performance relative to their baselines. A significance threshold of P<0.05 was used.

Discussion

Our results demonstrate that HFEF and ST-Loss address complementary aspects of nuclei segmentation. HFEF improves boundary localization by fusing SWT-derived high-frequency components with Laplacian-based sharpening, enhancing edge contrast without losing spatial context. ST-Loss focuses the optimization process on FNs by introducing a probability-adaptive penalty, leading to higher recall in edge-sensitive regions where low-confidence FNs are most prevalent. Compared with commonly used imbalance-aware or false-negative-aware loss functions, such as class-weighted CE or focal loss, ST-Loss differs in that it introduces a probability-adaptive penalty specifically targeting low-confidence FNs. While class-weighted losses apply fixed reweighting at the class level and focal loss emphasizes hard-to-classify samples globally, ST-Loss dynamically increases the penalty for FNs based on prediction confidence. This design is particularly effective for boundary regions, where FNs often occur with intermediate confidence and are not explicitly emphasized by standard loss formulations.

Compared with recent transformer-based methods such as TransNuSeg (7) and our prior MoE-NuSeg (9), this framework adds an explicit frequency-domain edge channel and a sensitivity-aware loss, providing consistent improvements across histology, fluorescence, and Lizard datasets. While other works have explored wavelet transforms or class weighting, our approach uniquely combines multi-directional frequency fusion with dynamic probability-based penalty scaling. Preliminary experiments on the LIDC-PM lung nodule dataset (29,30) (Table S2) further demonstrate that ST-Loss can substantially improve sensitivity and F1-score in a modality outside histology, highlighting its potential for cross-domain generalization.

Importantly, both HFEF and ST-Loss are designed to be model-agnostic. HFEF operates at the input or early feature level by enhancing high-frequency structural information and does not rely on architecture-specific components such as attention mechanisms or hierarchical decoders. As a result, it can be readily integrated into both convolutional frameworks and transformer-based architectures. Similarly, ST-Loss is defined purely at the optimization level and depends only on predicted probabilities and ground-truth labels, allowing it to replace or complement standard loss functions without modifying network structure. This architectural independence makes the proposed method complementary to a wide range of existing segmentation models rather than tied to a specific backbone design. This design philosophy is complementary to recent advances in hierarchical and scale-aware architectures that emphasize multi-scale feature aggregation or consistency learning (31,32), as HFEF and ST-Loss operate at the frequency-enhancement and optimization levels rather than modifying backbone topology.

Recent years have witnessed rapid progress in deep learning–driven pathology image analysis, particularly with the emergence of large-scale and foundation models trained on diverse histopathology data. Representative examples include whole-cell segmentation frameworks enabled by extensive annotation efforts (33), pathology foundation models for cancer diagnosis and prognosis prediction (34), and prompt-based segmentation models such as SAM-Med2D (35). These approaches reflect a broader trend toward general-purpose, transferable representations that can be adapted across tasks and datasets.

Within this evolving landscape, SAM-based extensions that incorporate task-specific priors, such as edge-guided segmentation approaches [e.g., EDGE-SAN (36)], further demonstrate that foundation models can benefit from additional structural guidance when applied to densely clustered cellular structures. Rather than replacing these foundation models, HFEF and ST-Loss are designed to complement them by explicitly enhancing high-frequency boundary information and suppressing FNs near object boundaries—challenges that remain critical in pathology segmentation. This perspective is also consistent with recent architectural trends emphasizing hierarchical and scale-aware feature learning for dense medical image prediction (31,32).

From an application perspective, improved boundary delineation has direct clinical relevance, as accurate segmentation underpins downstream quantitative pathology tasks such as cellular morphology analysis, tumor margin assessment, and spatially resolved biomarker evaluation. Recent studies have highlighted the importance of reliable image-to-phenotype transformation and integrative analysis for diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making (37,38). By reducing false-negative errors at object boundaries, the proposed framework may support more robust quantitative measurements in such clinically relevant workflows.

Limitations

First, HFEF preprocessing introduces computational overhead, mainly from data loading rather than from SWT or Laplacian computation. Integrating these operations more tightly into the model pipeline could further reduce runtime. Second, our experiments focus on binary nuclei segmentation; adapting HFEF and ST-Loss to multi-class or instance-level segmentation tasks requires further validation. Third, clinical datasets often contain staining variations and artifacts that are not fully represented in current benchmarks.

We also acknowledge that an exhaustive ablation analysis over all possible wavelet bases, fusion stages, and Laplacian parameter settings was not conducted in this study. Such a comprehensive sensitivity analysis would involve a large combinatorial design space and substantially increased computational cost. Nevertheless, the proposed HFEF framework is not dependent on a single handcrafted configuration, but is designed as a modular frequency-enhancement strategy in which alternative wavelet families, fusion locations, or sharpening strengths can be readily substituted without modifying the overall architecture. A systematic sensitivity analysis of these components therefore represents an important direction for future work, particularly in conjunction with larger-scale or task-specific datasets.

Future directions

Extending HFEF and ST-Loss to three-dimensional (3D) (39) medical imaging [e.g., volumetric magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) or computed tomography (CT)] could further improve boundary delineation in tumor or organ segmentation. In particular, HFEF can be adapted through 3D wavelet filtering to enhance volumetric high-frequency structures, while ST-Loss can be reformulated using 3D neighborhood context to more effectively penalize false-negative boundary voxels. In addition, integrating our method into emerging segmentation foundation models presents another promising direction. Recent large-scale models trained with extensive annotation or weak supervision have demonstrated strong generalization across pathology domains, including whole-cell segmentation and cancer diagnosis (33,34), as well as prompt-driven medical image segmentation frameworks such as SAM-based models (40). HFEF features could serve as auxiliary prompts or conditioning signals to guide SAM toward sharper boundaries, while ST-Loss could refine SAM-generated masks during fine-tuning or post-processing. We also recognize that stronger generalization assessment through leave-one-dataset-out validation and WSI-level inference constitutes an important next step. Although these directions are beyond the scope of this paper, we now provide concrete implementation considerations to clarify feasibility and anticipated challenges. Beyond imaging, the core principle of ST-Loss—emphasizing low-confidence FNs—may generalize to biomedical signal analysis tasks where sensitivity is critical, such as electrocardiogram (ECG) arrhythmia detection or EEG seizure monitoring. A more comprehensive ablation study comparing ST-Loss with additional contemporary FN-aware loss functions (e.g., asymmetric or boundary-aware formulations) is an important direction for future work. More broadly, accurate and boundary-preserving segmentation serves as a critical foundation for downstream clinical tasks such as phenotype quantification, diagnostic decision support, and integrative histology-omics analysis, as demonstrated in recent generative and multimodal clinical artificial intelligence (AI) studies (36,38).

Conclusions

This study presented a novel framework for enhancing nuclei segmentation through two complementary innovations: HFEF and ST-Loss. HFEF integrates SWT with Laplacian filtering to enrich high-frequency edge features and sharpen boundary delineation, effectively addressing challenges such as clustered and overlapping nuclei. ST-Loss complements this by adaptively penalizing FNs, improving recall and sensitivity in boundary-sensitive regions.

By bridging frequency-domain signal processing with deep learning, the proposed framework strengthens edge representation while preserving overall segmentation accuracy. The results underscore the importance of high-frequency feature extraction and sensitivity-aware loss functions in advancing biomedical image segmentation, with promising potential for broader applications in tasks requiring precise boundary delineation beyond histology.
