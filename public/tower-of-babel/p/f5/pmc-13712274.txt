Molecular mechanisms, crosstalk and therapeutic potential of novel acylation modifications in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases
Authors: Wanchun Li, Weile Ye, Jiaojiao Wang, Zhiping Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metabolic reprogramming, the fundamental process by which cells adapt to fluctuations in energy demands, is pivotal in the pathophysiology of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (CCVDs). Aberrant reprogramming of glucose, lipid, and amino acid metabolism is intricately associated with pathological processes in CCVDs such as cardiac hypertrophy, atherosclerosis, and ischemia-reperfusion injury. This metabolic remodeling disrupts the dynamic balance of metabolites (cc, acetyl-CoA and lactate) in the cardio-cerebrovascular systems, thereby inducing abnormal post-translational modifications (PTMs) on histones and non-histone proteins. These PTMs drive disease progression by modulating gene transcription or the activity of key proteins, which subsequently influences downstream signaling pathways. Here, we systematically examine the regulatory enzymes and functional roles of nine recently characterized acylation modifications: crotonylation, β-hydroxybutyrylation, 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation, succinylation, malonylation, glutarylation, lactylation, propionylation, and butyrylation. Furthermore, this review elaborates on the molecular mechanisms, crosstalk and the latest preclinical advancements regarding these acylations in CCVDs, emphasizing the influence of distinct acylation patterns on various pathological processes and highlighting the clinical translational prospects of targeting these novel modifications for therapeutic intervention.

Introduction

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases (CCVDs), characterized by vascular lesions in the heart and brain, exhibit high mortality and disability rates, with multi-system complications, posing a major challenge to global public health1, 2, 3. According to the American Heart Association, approximately 523 million people worldwide were affected by CCVDs in 2021, resulting in more than 18.6 million deaths4. It is alarming that the Poisson regression model study indicates that the number of cardiovascular fatalities is predicted to reach 35.6 million in 2050, and the prevalence of cardiovascular disease may rise by 90% between 2025 and 20505. The high incidence of CCVDs globally is attributed to their intricate pathogenesis. Aging is the primary risk factor for CCVDs; as individuals age, the decline in metabolic capacity and the deterioration of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular systems significantly accelerate the onset of conditions such as atherosclerosis and stroke6, 7, 8. In addition, environmental exposure and lifestyle choices jointly disrupt metabolic homeostasis by inducing oxidative stress and epigenetic alterations9, 10, 11. These determinants collectively affect disease progression via metabolic regulatory pathways and gene expression modulation, a mechanistic cascade that contributes to the complexity of pathogenic mechanisms and poses significant challenges for therapeutic interventions.

Protein post-translational modifications (PTMs) are chemical modifications after protein biosynthesis that affect the structure and function of the protein without altering the DNA sequence. A key mechanism by which PTMs exert their influence is the addition of groups such as methyl, acyl or glycosyl, induction of oxidative modifications, to amino acid residues. This process primarily governs protein activity, localization, and interactions with other cellular molecules, ultimately playing a crucial role in cell signal transduction, gene expression regulation and cell cycle control12, 13, 14. Based on the target protein, PTMs are broadly categorized into histone modifications and non-histone modifications. In histone modifications, amino acid residues on histones, mainly lysine residues, affect the accessibility of chromatin and influence the enrichment of other proteins on chromatin to regulate gene transcription15, 16, 17, whereas non-histone modifications impact disease processes through modulating the functional activities, subcellular localization, and interaction networks of key proteins18,19. Currently, the types of PTMs being studied include acylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, among others. It has been confirmed that PTMs participate in regulating tumors, neurological diseases, and immunity, as well as CCVDs20. Among these types, several innovative acylation modifications, such as crotonylation21, succinylation22, lactylation23, β-hydroxybutyrylation24, 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation25, malonylation26, glutarylation27, propionylation28 and butyrylation28 have gained increasing attention in contemporary research, facilitated by advancements in anti-acylated antibodies and ultrasensitive mass spectrometry29. Since these new acylation modifications were discovered, the accumulating evidence associating them with CCVDs underscores their significance and the rapid progress in this area of CCVDs research, pointing toward new strategies for clinical drug discovery.

In light of the significantly increased interest in novel acylation modifications and their potential role in CCVDs, this review examines nine such modifications, including crotonylation (cr), β-hydroxybutyrylation (bhb), 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (hib), succinylation (succ), malonylation (mal), glutarylation (glu), lactylation (la), propionylation (pr) and butyrylation (bu), on both histone and non-histone proteins. We discuss their metabolic sources, regulatory enzymes, and biological functions, emphasizing how these acylation pathways connect cellular metabolism to the regulation of CCVDs. Most critically, we also summarize the current research progress of each novel acylation modification in cardio-cerebrovascular pathologies, and the clinical application prospects of these acylation modifications, providing a new direction for future research and therapeutic strategies of CCVDs.

Sources, principles, and regulatory mechanisms of ten novel PTMs

Metabolites, such as metabolic intermediates of glucose, lipids, and proteins, play a crucial role in cell growth under physiological and pathological conditions. A large number of studies have confirmed that the occurrence and development of various diseases are accompanied by significant metabolic reprogramming. For instance, the activation of glycolysis or fatty acid oxidation (FAO) helps meet cellular energy demands and sustain biological activity in disease states30,31. Notably, such metabolic reprogramming disrupts the homeostasis of multiple metabolic intermediates during the disease progression and may further drive and exacerbate the disease through mechanisms such as PTMs30,32,33. Through PTMs, metabolites can indirectly participate in gene transcription or alter protein localization and stability.

Acylation is an important class of PTM whose acyl donors primarily originate from active acyl-CoA molecules generated during metabolism. The most well-known example is histone acetylation mediated by acetyl-CoA derived from glycolysis34. In addition to acetylation, recent studies have demonstrated that a series of novel acylation modifications are closely associated with metabolic processes. The donors for these novel acylation modifications are likewise predominantly derived from metabolic processes. Specifically, butyrylation, propionylation, crotonylation, 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation and glutarylation are mainly derived from fatty acid and amino acid metabolism, whereas β-hydroxybutyrylation, succinylation, lactylation and malonylation are primarily originated from glucose metabolism35.

The dynamic regulation of protein acylation modification depends on three enzymes: the writer enzyme, the reader enzyme, and the eraser enzyme. Generally, except for some non-enzymatic mitochondrial acylation events that occur in a substrate-dependent manner36, 37, 38, most nuclear acylation reactions are catalyzed by writer enzymes that modify specific residues. Conversely, the removal of acyl groups is mediated by eraser enzymes, underscoring the reversible nature of these modifications. Reader proteins recognize specific acyl marks and recruit downstream effectors to execute regulatory functions. This coordinated writer-eraser-reader network constitutes a dynamic equilibrium system for acylation, which plays significant roles in both physiological and pathological processes in CCVDs. Crotonylation

Crotonylation is an evolutionarily conserved histone post-translational modification that differs structurally and functionally from acetylation due to its more rigid chemical configuration21. The levels of histone crotonylation are directly influenced by global cellular concentrations of crotonyl-CoA (Cr-CoA), which is derived from endogenous crotonate metabolism, as well as from mitochondrial and peroxisomal fatty acid oxidation (FAO)39, 40, 41, 42. While acyl-CoA oxidase 2, an enzyme involved in peroxisomal bile acid metabolism, has been implicated in regulating non-histone crotonylation through its interaction with methylcrotonyl-CoA carboxylase in liver metabolism43, its precise regulatory mechanisms for both histone and non-histone crotonylation remain to be fully elucidated. Conversely, chromodomain Y-like (CDYL) protein, a Cr-CoA hydratase, negatively regulates histone crotonylation by converting crotonyl donors into β-hydroxybutyryl-CoA44.

Similar to other histone modifications, the establishment and removal of crotonyl marks are regulated by specialized enzymes, often classified as “writers”, “erasers”, and “readers”45, 46, 47. Intriguingly, several well-characterized histone acetyltransferases, which are “writers” of histone acetylation, have also been found to possess histone crotonyltransferase activity, including members of the GNAT, MYST, and p300/CBP families. Specifically, the p300/CBP family, MYST family members such as lysine acetyltransferase 8 (KAT8, also called MOF), lysine acetyltransferase 7 (KAT7, also called HBO1), and lysine acetyltransferase 5 (KAT5, also called TIP60 or ESA1), and the GNAT family member lysine acetyltransferase 2A (KAT2A, also called GCN5), have all demonstrated this “writing” capability for crotonylation39,41,48, 49, 50, 51. Notably, p300 displays greater activity and efficiency in catalyzing crotonylation compared to acetylation, and this crotonylation activity can occur independently of its acetylation activity41.

The primary “erasers” of histone crotonylation are class I histone deacetylases (HDACs), which exhibit robust histone decrotonylase activity52, 53, 54. In contrast, sirtuins (SIRTs), another major class of deacetylases, generally lack significant decrotonylase activity, further underscoring the biochemical and functional distinction between lysine acetylation and crotonylation (Kcr). Beyond the enzymes that add and remove crotonyl marks, specific protein domains act as “readers” to recognize and interpret these modifications, thereby influencing downstream cellular processes. They are mainly proteins containing double PHD finger (DPF) domains and YEATS domain55, 56, 57, 58, 59. In addition, CDYL has been also identified as a reader for crotonylation60.

According to the initial report, P300-mediated histone crotonylation promoted gene expression39,41, and HDAC mutants with histone decrotonylation activity were also found to inhibit gene expression54, suggesting crotonylation as an active modification of histone. These regulatory effects are often mediated by the influence of histone crotonylation on the binding of transcription regulatory proteins to chromatin. Studies have shown that the recruitment of CDYL1 into double-stranded broken DNA facilitates gene transcriptional silencing and later studies have found that this effect is associated with histone crotonylation on the double-stranded broken DNA. Specifically, Cr-CoA hydratase activity of CDYL1 counteracts crotonylation at the double-strand breaks site, triggering the expulsion of histone acetylation reader elongation factor ENL to lead to gene silencing61. This regulatory effect has also been extended to non-histone proteins, where Kcr of single-chain binding protein in eukaryotes has been found to enhance its interaction with single-stranded DNA to exert single-strand stability and play an important role in camptothecin-mediated DNA damage repair62. Intriguingly, recent work has identified H3K27 crotonylation as a transcriptionally repressive chromatin mark, revealing a previously unrecognized functional state for this modification55.

As for non-histone, crotonylation serves in multiple aspects, including protein interaction63, conformation64, thermodynamic stability64, subcellular localization65, and enzyme activity. Crucially, the activation and function of a series of enzymes in glycolysis seem to be tightly associated with crotonylation. In hippocampal neurons, crotonylation of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex E1 subunit α at K39 activates PDH to increase ATP production and promotes pyruvate metabolism66. Similarly, crotonylation of lactate dehydrogenase A (LDHA) facilitates its tetramerization and activation, driving metabolic reprogramming to support proliferative vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) growth67. Likewise, crotonylation of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) contributes to dimerization and nuclear translocation of PKM2, which facilitate the expression of glucose transporter type 1 and LDHA, ultimately accelerating glycolysis and phenotypic switching of VSMCs68.

Although metabolic reprogramming-mediated phenotypic transformation of VSMCs has been confirmed as a key link in vascular remodeling and the development of related cardiovascular diseases, the upstream signaling mechanisms driving this transformation, especially the crotonylation modification of key glycolytic enzymes, and how they precisely regulate metabolic flux and influence the chronic progression of diseases remain a blind spot in current research. Elucidating these mechanisms will integrate epigenetic modifications with metabolic remodeling and cell fate determination, ultimately establishing a novel theoretical framework for understanding cardiovascular pathogenesis.

β-Hydroxybutyrylation

β-Hydroxybutyrylation (Kbhb) is a recently characterized and evolutionarily conserved acylation modification in eukaryotic cells. Its key distinction from crotonylation lies in the source of the acyl donor: Kbhb utilizes β-hydroxybutyrate (BHB), a ketone body produced under conditions such as starvation, prolonged exercise, or ketosis24. This characteristic enables P300-mediated β-hydroxybutyrylation to sensitively perceive metabolic fluctuations in response to hunger, thereby directly converting physiological stress signals into epigenetic regulatory instructions, allowing cells to rapidly adjust transcriptional programs in response to changes in overall metabolic levels69.

Enzyme-catalyzed acylation plays a primary role in regulating Kbhb levels. A recent study has pointed out that ACSS2 and HBO1 cooperatively catalyze β-hydroxybutyrylation70. HDAC1–3 are perceived as erasers of β-hydroxybutyrylation which exert synergistic effects71,72. Interestingly, the sirtuin family deacetylase SIRT3 also exhibits site-selective histone de-β-hydroxybutyrylase activity, with a preference for specific histone H3 sites (K4, K9, K18, K23, and K27) over H4 sites (except H4K16). This distinct substrate specificity differentiates SIRT3 from zinc-dependent HDACs and suggests a hierarchical regulatory role in Kbhb removal73. Additionally, the YEATS domain protein ENL has recently been identified as a reader of H3K9bhb, displaying stronger binding affinity for H3K9bhb than for H3K9ac, thereby addressing a prior gap in our understanding of Kbhb recognition74.

Ketogenic diets modulate the epigenome partly through β-hydroxybutyrylation. Among the known Kbhb marks, H3K9bhb is the most extensively studied. It is induced by prolonged fasting and marks transcriptionally active genes; this function overlaps with, but is non-redundant to, those of H3K4me3 and H3K9ac24. This active chromatin state also plays a protective role in ischemic cardiomyopathy. Specifically, BHB-induced H3K9bhb enhances chromatin accessibility and activates transcription of angiogenesis-related genes, including carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1a75. To date, accumulating evidence indicates that ketone bodies participate in chromatin remodeling and cellular signaling via histone and non-histone β-hydroxybutyrylation, thereby exerting anti-inflammatory effects and promoting mitochondrial homeostasis. These findings open new avenues for exploring the role of ketone bodies in cardiovascular protection.

2-Hydroxyisobutyrylation

2-Hydroxyisobutyrylation is a novel histone modification characterized by its special structure. Research proposed lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib) may derive from 2-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA, a metabolic intermediate in relation to lactic acidosis in humans25,76. As the generation of 2-hydroxyisobutyryl-CoA is highly dependent on the oxidative decomposition of glucose, functional glycolysis is considered the material basis for maintaining the normal modification level of histones (such as H4K8hib), which reveals the potential connection between Khib and metabolic disorders such as lactic acidosis in humans77. P300 and TIP60 regulate Khib in humans78,79, while in E. coli, TmcA, an RNA acetyltransferase, functions as a lysine 2-hydroxyisobutyryltransferase and promotes transcription80. HDACs are the main enzymes to remove Khib in eukaryotes79, and CobB catalyzes lysine de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation in prokaryotes81. Furthermore, it is necessary for Rpd3 (class Ⅰ HDAC) and Hos3p (class Ⅱ HDAC) to cooperate in de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation in H4K877.

Pathway enrichment shows that Khib possibly correlates with protein synthesis and degradation, cellular signaling and DNA repair79. In addition, several Khib modification sites in enzyme active sites and substrates or cofactor binding sites have been identified79, suggesting that Khib takes part in maintaining enzyme activity and interaction. In atrial fibrillation, numerous proteins, particularly those in energy metabolism pathways, exhibit significant changes in Khib. Among them, de-2-hydroxyisobutyrylation of hexokinase 1 impairs its binding to the substrate glucose and reduces ATP production, which is associated with the onset of atrial fibrillation82. Notably, although enzymes specifically governing Khib have yet to be identified, 63 Khib sites were detected in previous studies. Among these, 27 sites have not been reported in either acetylation (Kac) or crotonylation (Kcr) datasets25. This divergence suggests that Khib is regulated through distinct mechanisms and implies the existence of specific “writers” for Khib that warrant further investigation.

Succinylation

Lysine succinylation (Ksucc) is an evolutionarily conserved lysine modification that induces substantial changes in protein chemical properties and structure. Compared with acetylation and other fine-tuning modification, succinylation causes more drastic changes in the physicochemical properties of proteins: its succinyl group is larger (100 Da), and it can reverse the charge state of lysine residues from +1 to −122. Such drastic structural perturbations typically exert a decisive influence on protein function.

At the metabolic level, succinyl-CoA (Succ-CoA), an important metabolic intermediate in the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, serves as an endogenous provider of succinyl groups for Ksucc. The level of Succ-CoA is considered the main regulator of non-enzymatic Ksucc38, while enzymatic Ksucc might also be induced by succinyltransferases. In terms of “writers”, in addition to the classic histone acetyltransferases such as histone acetyltransferase 183, GCN584, 85, 86 and p300/CBP87 that were found to have succinyltransferase activity, specific succinyltransferases such as 3-oxoacid CoA-transferase 188 and carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1A (CPT1A)89,90 have also been identified. These enzymes play a regulatory role in modifying cytoplasmic, mitochondrial and nuclear proteins by transferring succinyl groups to lysine residues of target proteins. Among them, CPT1A-mediated succinylation in cardiomyocytes has been demonstrated to relieving the inflammatory response of cells under hypoxic conditions91.

In terms of “erasers”, sirtuin family members (SIRT587,92, 93, 94, SIRT795) participate in non-histone desuccinylation. In particular, SIRT5 has been shown to exert protective effects on critical parenchymal cells of cardio-cerebrovascular system by mediating protein desuccinylation, such as cardiomyocytes and neurons, thereby reducing the expression levels of damage-associated proteins or promotes their degradation96, 97, 98. These desuccinylation enzymes affect protein function by dynamically regulating succinylation levels. As for “readers”, only the YEATS domain of Glioma-Amplified Sequence-41 protein has been found to recognize the succinylation of histone H3K122 in a pH-dependent manner99, and other succinylation recognition proteins are yet to be discovered. A recent study found that increased histone succinylation inhibits the interaction between chromatin and bromodomain-containing protein BRD4, suggesting that bromodomains may not function as readers for histone succinyaltion100. To date, research on targeting succinylation has primarily focused on the desuccinylase SIRT5 and SIRT7. Notably, Succ-CoA ligase, a complex multimeric enzyme composed of SUCLG1, SUCLA1, SUCLG2, has been shown to regulate succinylation via Succ-CoA synthesis or degradation pathways in tumor and liver injury progression contexts101,102. However, the effect of Succ-CoA ligase targeting succinylation in CCVDs remains unexplored.

In terms of histone modification, the regulation of histone lysine succinylation is mainly mediated by class I HDAC1/2/3 (rather than SIRT family proteins), and this HDAC1/2/3-mediated desuccinylation regulation directly affects nucleosome stability103. Succinylation introduces negative charges at histone lysine residues, which induces looser combination with DNA and increases transcriptional output under physiological conditions. It has been demonstrated that H2BK34succ104, H3K122succ87, H4K77succ105 destabilized nucleosomes by affecting DNA-histone interactions.

Due to the substantial steric hindrance and hydrophobicity introduced by Succ-CoA, succinylated proteins often exhibit significant alterations in activity, protein interactions and subcellular localization. For instance, in cardiomyocytes, succinylation of the N1-methyladenosine methyltransferases TRMT10C promotes mitochondrial redistribution, which mitigates nuclear accumulation-induced cellular injury91. This finding inspires the development of CPT1A agonists to modulate succinylation specifically, thereby offering a potential therapeutic strategy for coronary microembolization. The succinyl group is not recognized by the proteasome. Therefore, changes in protein stability induced by succinylation are often linked to the ubiquitin–proteasome pathway and the lysosomal pathway, demonstrating the complex regulatory functions of succinylation106,107. Based on these functions, proteins involved in several metabolic pathways and biochemical processes can activate downstream signaling pathways by succinylation or desuccinylation.

Malonylation

Lysine malonylation (Kmal) is a recently identified protein acylation modification that shares characteristics with Ksucc and is evolutionarily conserved across mammalian and bacterial cells26. Its acyl donor, malonyl-CoA, is an intermediate in the de novo fatty acid synthesis pathway. Studies indicate that the acyl-coenzyme A synthetase family member 3 (ACSF3) is required for mitochondrial protein malonylation by converting malonate to malonyl-CoA108. Similar to succinylation, malonylation introduces a significant mass increase (∼86 Da) and reverses the charge of lysine residues from +1 to −1.

To date, SIRT5 is the most extensively validated deacylase with demalonylase activity26,109,110, whereas GCN5 has been shown to function as a malonyltransferase110. Notably, while SIRT5 regulates both succinylation and malonylation, approximately 47% of malonylation sites on mitochondrial proteins do not overlap with succinylation or acetylation sites. This divergence suggests that malonylation (Kmal) may be governed by non-enzymatic mechanisms or specific, yet-to-be-identified acyltransferases, representing a critical avenue for future investigation111. Importantly, activation of SIRT5, which inhibit malonylation, has been identified as a novel cardioprotective strategy, rescuing cardiomyocyte viability following myocardial infarction (MI)112. In addition, recent evidence indicated that in cardiomyocytes, the SIRT3-mediated demalonylation participates in the regulation of mitochondrial function, which has not been observed in other cells, suggesting a unique role of SIRT3-mediated demalonylation in cardiac protection113.

Based on the current research findings, it has been reported that both histone and non-histone Kmal can change the functions of chromatin and then affect gene expression and downstream signal transduction. H2AK119mal inhibits the interaction between BUB1 mitotic checkpoint serine/threonine kinase and H2A, inducing a decrease in shugoshin protein in chromatin. As a result, mis-segregation of the chromosomes might happen in mitosis and meiosis. This process also involves H2AS121 phosphorylation114. In addition, malonylation on lysine 213 of glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase leads to its dissociation from tumor necrosis factor-α mRNA and promotes translation in macrophages115.

Current research suggests that mitochondrial malonylation actively participates in mitochondrial homeostasis in cardiomyocytes. Despite these insights, the site-specific patterns and functional consequences of malonylation in different pathological contexts and cellular microenvironments, particularly in the metabolic dysregulation characteristic of other CCVDs, remain poorly understood. Moreover, the prospective regulatory role of histone malonylation remains unexplored in CCVDs, despite being a crucial element of epigenetics in the modulation of pathogenic processes.

Glutarylation

Lysine glutarylation (Kglu) is a recently discovered acylation modification enriched in mitochondrial proteins and histones27. Its acyl donor, glutaryl-CoA, is a key metabolite derived from lysine and tryptophan catabolism116. Kglu regulates gene transcription and metabolic activity through epigenetic marking and PTM of metabolic enzymes27. The primary “erasers” of Kglu are the NAD+-dependent deacylases SIRT5 and SIRT727,117, whereas the addition of glutaryl groups is mainly catalyzed by KAT2A117. Intriguingly, the serine hydrolase ABHD11 was recently identified as a deglutarylase independent of deacetylase activity in T cell. Loss of ABHD11 increases glutarylation of the 2-oxoglutarate dehydrogenase complex, which attenuates essential lipoylation and impairs oxidative phosphorylation118. However, the expression pattern of ABHD11 in parenchymal cells of the CCVDs system remains unclear.

Initial studies revealed that Kglu widely regulates physiological processes, modifying key enzymes involved in redox reactions, fatty acid and amino acid metabolism, coenzyme metabolism, and aerobic respiration. Subsequent work demonstrated that SIRT5-mediated deglutarylation activates glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase, promoting NADPH production and antioxidant defense93. A feedback loop involving glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase in the lysine/tryptophan oxidation pathway also regulates glutarylation of key enzymes116.

Histone and non-histone Kglu can modulate transcription by altering protein–protein and protein–DNA interactions. For example, glutarylation of the transcription factor Nrf2 enhances its stability and DNA-binding affinity at antioxidant response elements119. In chromatin, H4K91glu destabilizes nucleosomes by disrupting H2A/H2B and H3/H4 interactions117. Besides, in glial cells, glutamate dehydrogenase glutarylation may interfere with protein-protein interaction120.

Although direct evidence for the regulatory role of glutarylation in the cerebro-cardiovascular disease (CCVD) microenvironment remains limited, growing data characterizing glutarylation as an inhibitory modification of key metabolic enzymes support the hypothesis that it significantly influences disease onset and progression. Specifically, glutarylation may act by reshaping metabolic networks under pathological conditions. Therefore, elucidating the mechanisms of metabolic enzyme glutarylation and its role in metabolic reprogramming represents a scientifically significant and innovative research frontier.

Lactylation

Lactylation is a newly identified PTM that tightly connects with glycolysis. In cancer, the Warburg effect describes a significant increase in lactate production, which indicates that the cell's energy metabolism shifts from oxidative phosphorylation to aerobic glycolysis, thereby providing energy for disease progression. l-Lactate derived from glycolysis triggers lysine lactylation by lactyltransferases. This not only indicates that lactate is an important source of lactylation but also provides a new perspective on the possible mechanism by which lactate promotes cancer progression in the Warburg effect, suggesting that histone lactylation (Kla) may play a key role in the occurrence and development of the disease23,121. It is worth noting that in addition to the lactyltransferase-dependent pathway, a study also described an enzyme-independent lactate group transfer method. To be specific, methylglyoxal, a byproduct of glycolysis, is coupled with glutathione to form S-d-lactoylglutathione through the glyoxalase cycle, thus achieving the non-enzymatic transfer of lactate directly from S-d-lactoylglutathione to protein lysine residues122,123.

Current studies indicate that l-lactate-mediated enzymatic histone lactylation (rather than d-lactate) is the key consequence of glycolysis and the Warburg effect. Unlike non-enzymatic reactions, this form of histone lactylation depends on specific enzymes to convert lactate to lactoyl-CoA (La-CoA), which can be used as a high-energy intermediate for lysine lactylation23,121. Recent studies identified GTP-specific succinyl-CoA synthetase (GTPSCS) as a nuclear La-CoA synthetase that interacts with p300 to promote histone lactylation without affecting succinylation, suggesting a specific regulatory role124. Acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2) also converts lactate to La-CoA and cooperates with KAT2A to facilitate histone lactylation125. However, the precise enzymes responsible for La-CoA production remain controversial.

As a breakthrough discovery in epigenetic modifications, Kla could orchestrate complex enzymatic processes to regulate protein function. Up to now, a variety of acetyltransferases involved in the enzymatic process of lysine Kla have been identified in eukaryotic and prokaryotic cells, including p30023, TIP60126, HBO1127, YiaC128, MOF 129, and others. These enzymes generally require additional factors to generate La-CoA. Among them, HBO1 preferentially catalyzes H3K9la127. Strikingly, recent studies have identified alanyl-tRNA synthetases (AARS1 and AARS2) as authentic lactyltransferases that mediate lysine lactylation independently of Lactyl-CoA (La-CoA) in cardiomyocytes, endothelial cells (ECs), neurons, and macrophages130, 131, 132, 133, 134. Furthermore, acetyl-CoA acetyltransferase 1 (ACAT1) and dihydrolipoyllysine-residue acetyltransferase (DLAT) have been shown to exhibit lactyltransferase activity in cardiac tissues and neutrophils. These enzymes directly facilitate non-histone lactylation, thereby modulating cardiac function following myocardial infarction135,136. In addition to acetyltransferases, lysine deacetylases also play a role in the removal of lactylation modifications. Enzymes such as HDAC1–3 and SIRT1–3 are able to remove a variety of acylation modifications in eukaryotes, including Kla137,138.

Lactylation is a key responder to metabolic reprogramming. In macrophages, glycolysis-driven Kla directly activates M2-associated homeostatic genes under M1-polarizing conditions23. During somatic cell reprogramming, the transcription factor Dux recruits p300 to promote H3K18la, which is recognized by Brg1 to activate mesenchymal-epithelial transition genes139. In cerebral ischemia, glycolysis forms a positive feedback loop with LDHA and HIF-1α via H3K9la in microglia140. Non-histone lactylation also reinforces glycolysis; for example, lactylation of polypyrimidine tract-binding protein 1 enhances its RNA-binding ability and stabilizes PFKFB4 mRNA, further promoting glycolytic flux141.

Kla also regulates non-histone protein function. For example, Kla of the transcription factor EB (TFEB) at K91 weakens its interaction with the E3 ubiquitin ligase WWP2. TFEB regulates the expression of genes related to lysosomal biogenesis142. This reduction inhibits ubiquitination and proteasome-mediated degradation of TFEB, thereby enhancing autophagic flux142. Besides influence the protein–protein and protein–nucleic acid interactions, lactylated nucleolin is enriched in the primary transcript of mitogen-activated protein kinase-activating death domain protein (MADD), which contains a premature termination codon, leading to alternative splicing that effectively translates MADD and promotes tumorigenesis143. Collectively, these studies demonstrate that Kla regulates disease progression through diverse mechanisms.

Propionylation and butyrylation

Propionylation (Kpr) and butyrylation (Kbu) are prevalent short-chain acylation modifications on protein lysine residues28,144. Propionyl-CoA and butyryl-CoA (Bu-CoA) are the essential acyl donors of histone propionylation (Kpr) and histone butyrylation (Kbu), of which the former is derived from odd-chain FAO and branched-chain amino acid degradation, and the latter is the product of FAO and the substrate for fatty acid synthesis145, 146, 147. Due to structural similarity to acetyl-CoA, several histone acetyltransferases can catalyze Kpr and Kbu under elevated acyl-CoA conditions144. p300/CBP28,148 and HBO151 exhibit both propionyltransferase and butyryltransferase activities in vivo and in vitro. Although MYST family member MOZ and GNAT family members KAT2B (PCAF) and GCN5 also catalyze propionylation, and exhibit the same enzymatic activity as acetylation, their activity toward butyrylation remains unclear, like due to steric hindrance in the enzyme active site149, 150, 151, 152. Currently, the identified “erasers” for Kpr and Kbu are limited to classical deacetylase such as SIRT1–328,144,148. In addition, the bromine domain is regarded as the main domain for recognizing Kpr and Kbu153, which further reveals the recognition and regulatory mechanisms of these two modifications in cellular signaling pathways. In summary, due to the structural resemblance between long-chain acylation modifications and acetylation, contemporary research on the enzymes governing propionylation and butyrylation has predominantly focus on traditional acetylation regulators. This narrow focus has hindered a comprehensive understanding and potential application of these distinct acyl modifications.

As regular intermediates of cellular metabolism, propionyl-CoA and Bu-CoA can reach relatively high intracellular concentrations28, underscoring the potential significance of their corresponding modifications. Similar to acetylation, histone propionylation and butyrylation are generally associated with transcriptionally active chromatin154, 155, 156. Recent studies have linked branched-chain amino acid (BCAA) consumption to cardiac stress, a process mediated by the BCAA-induced enrichment of the active chromatin marker H3K23pr157. Furthermore, propionylation appears to function as an activating modification on non-histone proteins; specifically, it has been shown to facilitate thrombosis and drive aberrant cardiac energy metabolism158,159.

The impact of butyrate, a potent HDAC inhibitor, on gene transcription has been extensively studied. Butyrate markedly upregulates antioxidant genes and suppresses pro-inflammatory genes in rat myotubes and ECs, effects likely mediated by HDAC inhibition160. In VSMCs, butyrate alters histone post-translational modifications, which occurs not through butyrylation, resulting in changes of cell cycle regulators expression and inhibition of cell proliferation161. Although this evidence connects the effect of butyrate in cardiovascular regulation with epigenetics, the underlying regulatory mechanism mainly involves in the inhibition of deacetylases. A recent study elucidated that the butyrylation-specific inhibitor LTK-14A, rather than targeting acetylation, significantly attenuated body weight increase in mice subjected to a high-fat diet, which is associated with less H4K5bu in adipocytes and liver162. Similarly, butyrate-induced histone butyrylation in the kidney is involved in regulating the expression of hypertension-related genes163. Intriguingly, butyrate-mediated butyrylation inhibits glycolytic enzyme activity by competing with lactylation at the K263 residue, thereby ameliorating calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD) in a glycolysis-dependent manner164. These findings highlight the promise of investigating butyrylation rather than acetylation in the regulation of CCVDs.

Novel acylation modification in CCVDs

The acute and chronic CCVDs involve complicated pathological processes, which poses a great challenge to the development of effective therapeutic drugs. However, it is encouraging that novel acylation modifications have shown great potential in regulating various pathophysiological processes of CCVDs. Furthermore, the study of the relationship between novel acylation modifications and the growing burden of CCVDs has opened up a new perspective for addressing this global challenge. Heart failure

Heart failure represents the end-stage of multiple cardiovascular diseases, and its occurrence and development are closely related to profound cardiac metabolic remodeling and energy metabolism disorders214. Recent studies have shown that a variety of novel acylation modifications play a central role in this pathological process by directly regulating key metabolic enzymes, contractile proteins, and epigenetic programs.

Primarily, cardiac energy homeostasis is highly dependent on mitochondrial metabolism. Under physiological conditions, Kbhb serves as a dynamic regulatory marker for the energy adaptation of the heart. In the neonatal heart, the activity of mitochondrial metabolic enzymes, including those associated with the TCA cycle, is stimulated by Kbhb to support elevated energy production215,216. In contrast, the aged heart exhibits a significant decrease in mitochondrial Kbhb, which is mostly concentrated on proteins associated with energy metabolic pathways, including fatty acid oxidation and the TCA cycle217. These findings indicate that Kbhb is a key modulator for maintaining the physiological energy homeostasis. Furthermore, succinylation and crotonylation also serve as direct contributors to mitochondrial dysfunction under pathological conditions. For instance, research has found that SIRT1 deficiency promotes the crotonylation of sarcoplasmic/endoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA2a) at lysine residue 120, diminishing the binding energy between SERCA2a and ATP, which may cause abnormal cardiac energy metabolism and electrophysiology; in SIRT1 gene knockout mice, this phenomenon is often manifested as cardiac hypertrophy, especially in the left ventricular wall and septal hypertrophy212. Similarly, mice lacking SIRT5 also exhibit cardiac dysfunction and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy. Mechanistically, SIRT5 activates another major cardiac regulatory enzyme, enoyl-CoA hydratase α-subunit, through desuccinylation, thereby enhancing FAO and stabilizing ATP levels in cardiac tissues172. These two modifications illustrate how acylation precisely modulates cardiac energy homeostasis by targeting energy production and utilization pathways, respectively.

Beyond interfering with mitochondrial energy metabolism, recent studies have found that acylation modifications are also involved in regulating myocardial contractility and adverse cytoskeleton remodeling. NEDD8-activating enzyme E1 regulatory subunit (NAE1), which mediates neddylation, is a crucial target for protecting cardiomyocytes from injury and maintaining postnatal cardiac development218,219. Notably, NAE1 K238 crotonylation (K238cr) promotes gelsolin neddylation, thereby enhancing the stability and expression of gelsolin. As an actin-binding protein, gelsolin augments actin-severing activity, ultimately contributing to cytoskeletal remodeling and the development of cardiac hypertrophy206. Furthermore, propionate-induced histone propionylation and acetylation facilitate the upregulation of Pde9a and Mme, which are associated with the downregulation of cyclic guanosine monophosphate (cGMP) signaling and cardiac systolic dysfunction220. Additionally, a report indicated that a BCAA-free diet reduces H3K23pr enrichment at the promoters of collagen isoforms, extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, and fibroblast markers in pressure-overloaded hearts. This diet also suppresses electron-transport-chain (ETC) genes in a propionylation-dependent manner, thereby preventing cardiac collagen deposition and fibrosis157. However, the expression of ETC genes does not appear to be directly influenced by histone propionylation, a finding that necessitates further investigation. Similarly, H3K18cr and H2BK12cr in cardiomyocytes act as upstream drivers of hypertrophic cardiomyopathy by promoting the fetal gene Nppb through the enhanced transcriptional activation and enrichment of pivotal transcription factors174.

Notably, lactylation exhibits contrasting effects on myocardial hypertrophy and heart failure. A clinical trial has validated the cardioprotective effect of lactate in heart failure221. Excessive lactate efflux and a reduction in α-myosin heavy chain K1897la in cardiomyocytes inhibit the interaction between α-MHC and titin, thereby impairing cardiac geometry and function. Consequently, administering sodium lactate or modulating lactate transporters has been shown to attenuate heart failure in experimental models165. Furthermore, the activation of histone lactylation appears to improve cardiac function during the advanced phases of remodeling222. Conversely, histone lactylation has also been identified as a driver of cardiac hypertrophy. Specifically, elevated H3K18la in cardiomyocytes acts as an active chromatin state that that directly promotes the expression of BCL2 interacting protein 3 (BNIP3), GATA binding protein 4 (GATA4) and transforming growth factor-β2 (TGF-β2). Increased BNIP3 expression leads to dysfunctional mitophagy, while the upregulation of GATA4 and TGF-β2 triggers the expression of hypertrophic markers183, 184, 185, 186.

In conclusion, these findings underscore the pivotal role of metabolic pathways and their associated PTMs in maintaining myocardial homeostasis. These insights provide a robust theoretical framework for developing therapeutic strategies targeting metabolic modifications. Looking ahead, advancements in high-throughput proteomics and modification-specific antibodies will likely facilitate the design of targeted interventions, offering significant potential for the precise treatment of heart failure.

Myocardial infarction and cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury

Myocardial infarction (MI) results from prolonged ischemia culminating in cardiomyocyte death. Although timely restoration of blood flow is essential, reperfusion can paradoxically exacerbate tissue damage, a phenomenon termed ischemia–reperfusion injury (IRI). IRI involves multiple programmed cell death pathways (e.g., pyroptosis, ferroptosis, necroptosis)223 and triggers a vicious cycle of inflammation, oxidative stress, adverse remodeling, and fibrosis224, 225, 226, ultimately leading to heart failure227, 228, 229. Consequently, therapeutic interventions targeting these inflammatory and fibrotic pathways represent critical strategies to alleviate myocardial ischemic injury. Notably, diverse acylation modifications play a pivotal role in modulating these pathological processes.

In IRI, mitochondrial dysfunction is considered the central event throughout the pathophysiological process. Early studies established that when succinate accumulates during ischemia, subsequent reperfusion triggers succinate dehydrogenase (SDH) to rapidly re-oxidize the succinate pool. This drives extensive ROS generation via the electron transport chain230. Conversely, SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of SDH-α subunit (SDHA) represses SDH activation, which may temper myocardial IRI.211. Similarly, malonate serves as an SDH inhibitor that prevents IRI when administered at the onset of reperfusion231,232. In addition, the succinylation and crotonylation of anti-oxidative enzymes, including thioredoxin, glyoxalase 1 and glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase, decrease their enzymatic activity, thereby impairing oxidative stress resistance and cardioprotection213,233. Recent discoveries indicate that neutrophil migration and excessive neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) activation are intricately linked to mitochondrial damage in MIRI, a process governed by lactylation. During MIRI, lactate directly enhances H3K18la at the promoter of the mitochondrial Ca2+ uptake-related gene MICU3 and triggers the lactylation of MICU3. The former amplifies MICU3 expression, while the latter augments its stability; collectively, they induce excessive Ca2+ uptake and mitochondrial dysfunction, ultimately resulting in abnormal NETs activation and microvascular endothelial cell damage190. Additionally, another study highlighted that S100A9 translocates to the neutrophil nucleus following lactylation at K26. As a coactivator, lactylated S100A9 facilitates neutrophil migration and cardiac recruitment by binding to the promoters of migration-related genes. This process subsequently disrupts cardiomyocyte mitochondrial function via the release of lactylated S100A9 during NETs formation136.

Lactylation and succinylation further modulate ischemia-reperfusion injury (IRI) by regulating protein stability and inflammatory gene expression. For instance, in cardiac IRI, SERPINA3 protects cardiomyocytes from reperfusion-induced apoptosis in a paracrine manner by activating the reperfusion injury salvage kinase (RISK) and survivor activating factor enhancement (SAFE) pathways. Specifically, lactylation of SERPINA3 at K351 enhances its stability and prolongs its half-life, thereby exerting cardioprotective effects210. Conversely, LDHA-mediated NLRP3 K245 lactylation (K245la) contributes to NLRP3 stabilization and triggers cardiomyocyte pyroptosis, which exacerbates myocardial injury207. Recent research also suggests that HSPA12A maintains histone lactylation levels under IR conditions, which is essential for cardiomyocyte survival. This protective effect may be mediated by the increased expression and stability of the glycolytic transcription factor HIF-1α234. Furthermore, in macrophages, H3K23 succinylation (H3K23succ) is enriched at the promoters of pro-inflammatory genes, facilitating macrophage recruitment to the infarcted region and driving the expression of inflammatory cytokines195.

Compensatory angiogenesis is essential for myocardial repair following myocardial infarction (MI). Recent evidence suggests that lactylation and Kbhb are closely linked to the activation of angiogenic genes and endothelial protective factors post-MI. Specifically, the activity of eNOS is regulated by its interaction with Cav1. It has been demonstrated that the lactylation of isocitrate dehydrogenase 2 (IDH2) at K272 activates eNOS, thereby enhancing vascular protection and angiogenesis in diabetic MI models. Mechanistically, lactylated IDH2 exhibits increased binding affinity for Cav1, which competitively diminishes the inhibitory Cav1-eNOS interaction135. Furthermore, a study focusing on histone modifications revealed that glycolysis and monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1)-mediated H3K18la regulation are pivotal for post-MI recovery. Uniquely, H3K18la serves as an upstream regulator for reparative genes, including Lrg1, Vegf-α, Il-10188. Similarly, β-OHB promotes neovascularization by inducing the β-hydroxybutyrylation of HIF-prolyl hydroxylase at K239/K385. This modification blocks HIF-1α degradation and boosts VEGF production, ultimately improving cardiac function post-MI208. Moreover, H3K9bhb acts as an upstream driver for angiogenic genes such as Cpt1a. This BHB-facilitated process highlights the clinical potential of BHB nutritional supplementation in managing ischemic cardiomyopathy75.

Fibrosis, a primary driver of adverse post-MI remodeling235, is initiated by chronic inflammation that transforms fibroblasts into activated myofibroblasts. These cells secrete extracellular matrix (ECM) components in an abnormal and sustained manner, ultimately disrupting tissue architecture and impairing cardiac function236,237. The aberrant activation of myofibroblasts, often triggered by the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT), is a hallmark of fibrotic progression238. Lactylation has been implicated in driving EndMT following MI. Specifically, lactate enhances the lactylation of Snail1 in ECs, facilitating its nuclear translocation. This modification increases the binding affinity of Snail1 for the TGF-β promoter, subsequently activating the TGF-β/Smad2 signaling pathway18. Similarly, PKM2-mediated lactate accumulation and H3K18la act as upstream regulators of TGFB1 transcription, promoting fibroblast activation and atrial fibrosis191. In contrast to the pro-fibrotic effects of lactylation, crotonylation appears to exert a protective role in MI/IRI. For instance, crotonylation of the cytoskeletal protein tropomyosin alpha-1 chain (TPM1) at K28/K29 regulates cytoskeletal organization and Ca2+ handling, thereby preserving cardiomyocyte contractility and attenuating fibrosis. Additionally, crotonylation of the mitochondrial protein isocitrate dehydrogenase 3α (IDH3α) at K199 inhibits BNIP3-induced mitophagy and mitochondrial depolarization, effectively shielding cardiomyocytes from isoproterenol-induced apoptosis and subsequent fibrotic remodeling201.

Stroke

Stroke, encompassing both ischemic and hemorrhagic subtypes, results in blood–brain barrier disruption, neuronal apoptosis, and neuroinflammation239, 240, 241. As the primary immune cells involved in neuroinflammation, microglia possess a functional state that is intricately linked to their metabolic profile; persistent metabolic alterations can profoundly exacerbate cellular dysfunction242, 243, 244, 245. Recent research has demonstrated that increased lactylation, driven by enhanced glycolysis, mediates the inflammatory activation of microglia post-stroke. During M1 polarization, the accumulation of lactate triggers the TNF-α signaling pathway via H3K9la176. Additionally, in ischemic stroke, histone lactylation is enriched at the promoters of immuno-metabolic genes, thereby driving metabolic reprogramming and inflammatory responses140,196. Similarly, at the non-histone level, lactate buildup facilitates the lactylation of MECP2 at K210. This modification upregulates hexokinase 2 (HK2) expression, subsequently inducing mitochondrial ROS generation and increasing glycolytic flux, which worsens mitochondrial dysfunction and neuroinflammation following stroke202. However, this regulatory landscape is nuanced: evidence also suggests an anti-inflammatory role for H3K18la in microglia, where it initiates the transcription of Plxnb2. These findings highlight the complex, context-dependent role of lactylation in modulating neuroinflammation193.

Reducing neuronal degeneration and mortality is crucial for improving neurological outcomes after intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH). Evidence indicates that MeCP2 lactylation is essential for limiting neuronal death and preventing infarct expansion. Mechanistically, lactylation of MeCP2 at K210/K249 enhances its binding affinity for the promoters of apoptosis-related genes. This modification allows MeCP2 to act as a transcriptional repressor of Pdcd4 (programmed cell death 4) and Pla2g6 (phospholipase A2 group VI), thereby reducing neuronal apoptosis and providing neuroprotection post-stroke203. Ferroptosis is actively involved in neural damage and the poor prognosis following both hemorrhagic and ischemic stroke246,247. Interestingly, the transcription and chromatin accessibility of the iron transporter gene Zip14 are regulated by H4K12la, which activates Zip14 expression and initiates ferroptosis following cerebral ischemia-reperfusion injury (CIRI)200. Furthermore, lactate-induced H3K14la is enriched at the promoters of plasma membrane Ca2+ ATPases, pumps responsible for extruding intracellular Ca2+ from cells. This enrichment leads to a transcriptional repression state, resulting in neuronal Ca2+ overload. Conversely, inhibiting H3K14la prevents excessive intracellular Ca2+ accumulation and attenuates neuronal ferroptosis180.

Astrocytes, like microglia, can polarize into either a pro-inflammatory A1 phenotype or a neuroprotective A2 phenotype upon stimulation, significantly influencing functional recovery following brain injury248,249. The phenotypic transition of astrocytes—the primary cells providing structural and trophic support in the brain—warrants close investigation during cerebral hypoxia-ischemia and reperfusion. A recent study indicated that subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH)-induced upregulation of bromodomain-containing protein 4 (BRD4) enhances H4K18la, which inhibits A1 polarization and promotes the recovery of neural supportive functions197. This SAH-triggered H4K18la appears to be an intrinsic protective mechanism of astrocytes in response to pathological stimuli. Furthermore, lactylation mediates astrocyte-neuron crosstalk during CIRI, thereby modulating the repair of damaged neurons. Specifically, low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) suppresses glycolysis and lactate production, subsequently reducing the lactylation of ADP-ribosylation factor 1 (ARF1) at K73. This reduction is beneficial, as ARF1 lactylation has been shown to impair the mitochondrial transfer from astrocytes to damaged neurons, a process essential for neuronal survival169.

Beyond lactylation, the regulatory roles of other acylation modifications, including succinylation and Kbhb, have been extensively documented in the context of stroke. The accumulation of succinate in neural stem cells leads to increased succinylation of cell division control protein 42 (CDC42), a protein essential for regulating cell proliferation and differentiation. This modification suppresses its enzymatic activity, thereby hindering neurogenesis following ischemic stroke170. In microglia, SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of annexin A1 (ANXA1) inhibits its SUMOylation and reduces its membrane localization and secretion, which contributes to microglial hyperactivation and neuroinflammation167. Additionally, succinylation of acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4) at K661 stabilizes the protein and promotes ferroptosis following ischemia–reperfusion166. During acute cerebral ischemia, adaptive lipolysis increases ketone body levels in brain EC. This elevates H3K9bhb at the Tjp1 (ZO-1) promoter, upregulating its expression and thereby preserving blood–brain barrier (BBB) integrity and alleviating neuroinflammation179.

In conclusion, the study of acylation modifications in stroke underscores the emergence of a critical “metabolo–epigenetic” axis, where metabolites like lactate, β-hydroxybutyrate, and succinate directly reprogram glial cell function and neuronal survival, offering novel targets for maintaining brain homeostasis post-stroke.

Atherosclerosis

Atherosclerosis is a chronic immuno-inflammatory process driven by lipid accumulation, which primarily leads to adverse clinical events such as arterial plaque rupture and thrombosis, potentially resulting in life-threatening conditions like myocardial infarction250. The pathogenesis of atherosclerosis is highly complex, involving a cascade of interrelated processes: from initial endothelial dysfunction and lipid deposition to the recruitment of macrophages, their polarization into foam cells, and the formation of fatty streaks (early atherosclerotic lesions). Notably, chronic inflammation persists throughout all stages of this progression251. This persistent inflammatory environment not only hastens fibrous cap development but also facilitates extracellular matrix degradation, thereby undermining plaque structural integrity and increasing plaque vulnerability to rupture and subsequent thrombosis251,252.

As the primary mediators of the immune response, macrophages exhibit either pro-inflammatory or anti-inflammatory properties by activating diverse signaling pathways within the disease microenvironment253. Therefore, modulating macrophage polarization and function is considered an effective strategy to mitigate atherosclerosis254. Methyl-CpG-binding protein 2 (MeCP2), a selective DNA-binding protein, modulates gene expression through transcriptional regulation and the reorganization of chromatin architecture255,256. In macrophages, exercise has been shown to enhance the K271 lactylation (K271la) of MeCP2, facilitating their transition into a reparative phenotype204. Mechanistically, H3K36me2 regulates MeCP2 chromatin binding, while K271la promotes the interaction between MeCP2 and H3K36me3. This interaction suppresses Runx1 transcription, thereby driving plaque macrophages toward a pro-repair M2 phenotype204. Similarly, in ECs, exercise-induced MeCP2 K271la inhibits epidermal growth factor expression, thereby attenuating epidermal growth factor receptor phosphorylation and downstream MAPK signaling. This reduces the expression of pro-inflammatory molecules such as VCAM-1, ICAM-1, MCP-1, IL-1β, and IL-6, hence facilitating atherosclerotic regression205. Furthermore, inhibition of monocarboxylate transporter 4 (MCT4), which is highly expressed in atherosclerotic plaque macrophages, promotes inflammation resolution by enhancing histone lactylation181. MCT4 deficiency increases p300-mediated H3K18la, initiating local repair programs and driving the stage-specific transition from M1 to M2 macrophages. Collectively, these findings indicate that lactylation in macrophages and ECs represents a promising therapeutic target for combating inflammation in atherosclerosis.

However, in different chromatin landscapes, histone lactylation may also act as a risk factor for atherosclerosis. Endothelial dysfunction is a hallmark of various vascular diseases, including atherosclerosis257. Recent studies have demonstrated that H3K9la is markedly increased in ECs during atherosclerotic progression, leading to the activation of stearoyl-CoA desaturase 1 (SCD1). Importantly, pharmacological suppression of H3K9la significantly improves endothelial function and attenuates plaque development177. In addition, lipid peroxidation has been shown to drive EndMT-mediated atherosclerosis via H3K18la modification182. Specifically, lipid peroxidation increases lactate levels, which—in coordination with the histone chaperone ASF1A and the acetyltransferase p300—elevates H3K18la. This epigenetic alteration enhances the transcriptional activity of mesenchymal genes, thereby promoting EndMT and atherosclerotic progression182. Beyond macrophages and ECs, emerging evidence indicates that VSMC senescence, fueled by H4K12la, accelerates atherosclerosis198. Mechanistically, abnormal tumor necrosis factor receptor-associated protein 1 (TRAP1) deficiency dramatically increases aerobic glycolysis. This metabolic reprogramming facilitates H4K12la enrichment at the promoters of senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP) genes, thereby exacerbating VSMC senescence198. These findings imply that targeting the TRAP1/H4K12la axis may alleviate VSMC senescence and stabilize atherosclerotic plaques.

Although the multifaceted roles of lactylation in cardiovascular diseases are increasingly recognized, research focusing on ECs and VSMCs remains insufficient. This gap exists partly because current studies predominantly focus on inflammation and metabolism, whereas the molecular mechanisms underlying other pathological processes, such as how lactylation regulates epigenetic plasticity or lipid homeostasis, have not been systematically elucidated. Therefore, in-depth exploration of the cell-specific roles of lactylation may reveal novel pathogenic mechanisms and provide promising directions for targeted therapeutic interventions.

In general, histone and non-histone lactylation seem to have the dual effect of alleviating and worsening atherosclerosis, which is worthy of further study. Furthermore, accumulating evidence indicates that fatty acid metabolites (e.g., butyrate and propionate) and intermediates of the TCA cycle, such as succinate, are actively involved in the regulation of key pathological processes of atherosclerosis including cholesterol metabolism258, endothelial dysfunction259,260, and inflammatory responses261,262. These regulatory effects contribute to the mitigation of atherosclerotic progression. These findings suggest that the aforementioned metabolites may exert their biological effects through PTMs, such as butyrylation, propionylation, and succinylation. However, a comprehensive understanding of how these acylation modifications specifically modulate protein structure and function in the context of atherosclerosis remains lacking, representing a critical frontier for future research.

Diabetic cardiomyopathy

Diabetic cardiomyopathy (DbCM) is a cardiac dysfunction secondary to chronic hyperglycemia. Its progression is characterized by myocardial fibrosis, increased stiffness, ventricular hypertrophy, and systolic dysfunction, ultimately culminating in heart failure263. In DbCM, metabolic remodeling is a prominent feature; within this dysregulated environment, metabolite-mediated acylation alterations significantly drive disease progression.

Studies indicate that in an environment of enhanced ketone body metabolism, the levels of level of Kbhb on mitochondrial proteins are significantly elevated. This suggests that Kbhb may serve as a metabolic indicator of ketone body availability during mitochondrial oxidative remodeling in DbCM168,264. Similarly, while SIRT5 expression is slightly increased in diabetic hearts, its deficiency has been shown to promote the succinylation of carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 (CPT2) at K424. This modification inhibits CPT2 enzymatic activity and impairs fatty acid oxidation (FAO), although fatty acid uptake remains unaffected. This metabolic imbalance leads to the abnormal accumulation of fatty acylcarnitines and lipotoxic species within myocardial tissue, ultimately driving the pathogenesis of DbCM171.

Mitochondrial dysfunction and energy stress are pivotal mechanisms inducing cell death in cardiomyocytes, ECs, and other cell types in DbCM265,266. Recent investigations reveal that oxidative stress-related proteins undergo acylation alterations during metabolic remodeling in DbCM, potentially impairing their antioxidant and detoxification capacities. Specifically, the stability of glutathione S-transferase P1 (GSTP1) is compromised by K121 malonylation (K121mal), which impairs the antioxidant capacity of cardiomyocytes and increases mitochondrial ROS production. These changes further promote the expression of pyroptosis-associated proteins, including NLRP3, Caspase-1, and the N-terminal domain of Gasdermin D (GSDMD-N), as well as ECM-related proteins, ultimately leading to cardiomyocyte pyroptosis and fibroblast activation173. Furthermore, malonylation of Argonaute 2 (AGO2) at K440 in diabetic hearts impairs its binding to the mitochondrial transporter TIMM17B, reducing its mitochondrial localization. Mitochondrial AGO2 normally supports the translation of electron transport chain (ETC) components (e.g., cytochrome B) by recruiting the mitochondrial elongation factor Tu (TUFM), thereby suppressing ROS generation; consequently, its loss exacerbates cardiac dysfunction113. Conversely, the antioxidant activity of mitochondrial thioredoxin reductase 2 (TrxR2) is enhanced by K340 lactylation (K340la), which maintains TUFM expression to bolster ferroptosis resistance133.

While ketone bodies such as β-hydroxybutyrate (β-OHB) have traditionally been viewed as cardioprotective in diabetes267, 268, 269, recent evidence reveals a more complex role, particularly regarding its epigenetic impact. Specifically, β-OHB-mediated H3K9bhb enrichment at the lipocalin-2 (Lcn2) promoter promotes Lcn2 transcription and subsequent NF-κB-driven inflammation, thereby exacerbating DbCM. Conversely, overexpression of β-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase 1 (BDH1) reprograms ketone metabolism, reducing β-OHB levels and H3K9bhb, thereby attenuating the inflammatory response, which highlights the distinct regulatory role of β-OHB, rather than other ketone bodies, in cardiac function178.

The discovery of novel acylation modifications provides a mechanistic explanation for the dysregulation of metabolism and inflammation in DbCM. Based on existing evidence, succinylation and malonylation are detrimental PTMs that impair the activity of metabolic enzymes and aggravate inflammation. Consequently, strategies targeting deacetylases are expected to improve DbCM outcomes by restoring metabolic homeostasis and alleviating inflammation through multiple pathways.

Cardiovascular calcification

Calcific cardiovascular disease is a complex chronic disorder involving multiple contributing factors, including atherosclerotic, aortic valve, and vascular calcification. The primary pathological hallmark is the osteogenic differentiation of cells, resulting in tissue sclerosis. VSMCs are the principal components of the arterial media; consequently, the phenotypic transition of VSMCs into osteoblast-like cells is a key pathological manifestation of calcific vascular disease270. Similarly, valve interstitial cells (VICs) undergo osteogenic differentiation when exposed to diverse stimuli271.

Recent studies have demonstrated that various histone lactylation modifications, serving as markers of active chromatin, are enriched at the promoters of calcification-related genes. Under inflammatory conditions and increased glycolysis, H3K9la and H3K14la promote the expression of Runt-related transcription factor 2 (Runx2) and bone morphogenetic protein 2 (BMP2), driving the osteogenic differentiation of VICs175,272. In VSMCs, NR4A3 facilitates glycolysis and the enrichment of H3K18la at the promoters of calcification-associated genes, particularly Phospho1, thereby enhancing its expression and leading to the loss of the contractile VSMC phenotype187. In VICs, GAPDH K263la also serves as an upstream regulator of Runx2 and BMP2. Notably, this glycolysis-dependent aortic valve calcification can be attenuated by butyrate-mediated butyrylation164. Furthermore, H3K18la has been shown to upregulate CHI3L1 via the IL 13-IL–13Ra2–JAK1–STAT3 pathway, contributing to diabetic arterial calcification189.

Beyond lactylation, research indicates that branched-chain amino acid transaminase 2 (BCAT2) deficiency leads to decreased propionyl-CoA levels. The resulting inhibition of H3K23pr reduces Runx2 transcription, thereby mitigating VSMC osteogenic differentiation and diabetic atherosclerotic calcification. This suggests that histone propionylation signifies an active chromatin state for calcification-related genes194. At the non-histone level, succinylation of high-mobility group Box 1 (HMGB1) at K90 directly enhances its protein stability and promotes its cytoplasmic accumulation, which in turn upregulates Runx2 expression90.

In conclusion, the pathological progression of cardiovascular calcification is no longer viewed as a passive mineral deposition, but rather as a regulated phenotypic transition driven by cellular metabolic reprogramming. Targeting these pivotal drivers—particularly those involving lactate signaling, orphan nuclear receptors, and HMGB1—represents a promising strategy for the therapeutic management of calcific cardiovascular disorders.

Crosstalk between novel acylation modifications and other protein modifications

The preceding sections detail how novel acylation modifications regulate various diseases and their pathological processes. Alongside well-established PTMs (e.g., acetylation, phosphorylation, ubiquitination, SUMOylation) that critically control protein expression, activation, stability, and degradation in cancers, CCVDs, metabolic disorders, and autoimmune diseases, recently discovered acylations (e.g., crotonylation, lactylation, succinylation) have been shown to influence these classical pathways. Crosstalk with acetylation

As previously mentioned, the majority of novel acylation modifications rely on the same acetyltransferases and deacetylases as acetylation, suggesting a competitive landscape among these modifications. Research indicates that crotonylation can competitively inhibit acetylation at identical lysine residues. For instance, crotonylation of KAT7 obstructs its own acetylation, thereby diminishing its histone acetyltransferase (HAT) activity and contributing to colorectal tumorigenesis273. During white adipocyte browning, the RAS/ERK pathway and mitochondrial function are regulated by the reciprocal inhibition between crotonylation and acetylation of dihydrolipoamide dehydrogenase (DLD)274. Such competitive relationships may be dictated by the relative concentrations of acyl-CoA substrates in tissues and the binding affinity of enzymes for these substrates. In cardiomyocytes, for example, mitochondrial malonylation is promoted via the acetylation-dependent activation of SIRT5, rather than direct regulation by SIRT3275. Furthermore, Kbhb and acetylation co-modulate the activity of metabolic enzymes in the neonatal heart. The metabolic maturation of cardiomyocytes is facilitated by increased mitochondrial Kbhb levels in response to ketone body supplementation216.

Lactylation appears particularly intertwined with acetylation, likely because acetyl-CoA and lactate represent two major metabolic fates of pyruvate during glucose metabolism. Acetylation participates in lactylation regulation indirectly by altering the activity of key enzymes in glycolysis, which mainly affects the production of lactate and subsequently La-CoA. A recent study showed that the interaction between GTPSCS and p300 depends on acetylation at K73, which regulates the nuclear translocation of GTPSCS and H3K18la124. Similarly, K488ac of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex component X blocks its interaction with dihydrolipoyl transacetylase, destroying assembly and activation of pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, which indirectly facilitates the conversion of pyruvate to lactate and the recruitment of oncogene expression regulator H3K56la to promote tumor progression276. During chemotherapy, low intracellular glucose contributes to the interaction between acetyl-coenzyme A acetyltransferase 1 and malic enzyme 2 (ME2), leading to ME2 acetylation at K156. This alternation catalyzes the metabolism of malate to pyruvate in TCA, promoting lactylation of proteins involved in homologous recombination repair to acquire chemoresistance in cancer cells277. Furthermore, studies have found that lactate and short-chain fatty acid metabolites may also be involved in acetylation regulation220,278,279. Therefore, many studies that focus on lactylation also need to exclude the interference of acetylation. A study points out that besides promoting the expression of anti-inflammation genes directly by lactylation, the anti-inflammation effect of lactate also reflects in the regulation of histone acetylation. To be specific, lactate can serve as a fuel source for the TCA cycle to produce acetyl-CoA, which in turn regulates H3K27ac. On this basis, immunosuppressive programs including NR4A1 are initiated and suppress the immune inflammatory response278. However, the result that macrophages do not undergo histone lactylation activation during this process seems to contradict with previous studies, suggesting the activation sequence of lactylation and acetylation during inflammation response.

Crosstalk with phosphorylation

Kinase- and phosphatase-mediated phosphorylation and dephosphorylation regulate the activation or deactivation of enzymes, triggering diverse downstream signaling cascades280. It has been shown that acylation modifications can function as upstream regulators of phosphorylation by recruiting or facilitating the transcriptional activation of specific kinases. For instance, in obstructive sleep apnea, histone lactylation upstream of Pde4b (phosphodiesterase 4B) promotes the phosphorylation and nuclear translocation of FUS, thereby driving pulmonary artery smooth muscle cell proliferation and migration281. Another study demonstrated that cAMP response element-binding protein lactylation at K136 is required for S133 phosphorylation, in which the recruitment of protein kinases is related to the change of spatial steric hindrance after lactylation282. The stimulation of protein phosphorylation induced by lactylation has also been found in Alzheimer's disease and in skin fibrosis resulting from EndMT283,284. In contrast, Kbhb, crotonylation, and malonylation have been shown to antagonize phosphorylation at neighboring threonine/serine residues114,285, 286, 287. Specifically, Kbhb of STAT1 at K679 inhibits its phosphorylation and transcriptional activity, contributing to the anti-inflammatory effects of BHB in macrophages285. This suggests that Kbhb may serve as an upstream modulator of phosphorylation-dependent signaling in cardiac anti-inflammatory responses. Similarly, crotonylation of protein kinase B (PKB/Akt) interferes with its binding to pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 1 (PDK1), suppressing its autophosphorylation; conversely, reduced crotonylation in muscle injury models correlates with elevated Akt phosphorylation286. Collectively, these findings elucidate a critical mechanism by which non-histone acylation modifications control protein activity through their interplay with phosphorylation.

Crosstalk with ubiquitylation

Ubiquitination plays a central role in protein degradation and signaling288, primarily mediated by E3 ligases such as the NEDD4 and HERC families, STUB1289. Novel acylations can influence ubiquitination by altering protein stability or E3 ligase activity. Lactylation upregulates the expression of E3 ligases (e.g., NEDD4 and HECTD2) and the deubiquitinase PSMD14290, 291, 292. In brain-injured neurons, H3K18la activates the expression of PSMD14, which deubiquitinates PKM and maintains its stability292. At the non-histone level, lactylation of NEDD4 at K33 impairs its interaction with caspase-11, reducing caspase-11 ubiquitination and degradation and thereby accelerating non-canonical pyroptosis293. In rapidly proliferating cancer cells, TFEB K91la diminishes the interaction of TFEB with E3 ubiquitin ligase WWP2, thereby repressing TFEB ubiquitination142.

It is worth noting that succinylation plays a significant role in regulating CCVDs by promoting ubiquitin-dependent degradation. In sepsis-induced cardiac dysfunction, succinylation-induced changes in the steric hindrance of SERCA2a are hypothesized to expose ubiquitin-prone residues, potentially altering its protein turnover294. Furthermore, LDHA-mediated metabolic reprogramming promotes the succinylation of thioredoxin reductase 1 (TrxR1) in cardiomyocytes during heart development, which in turn inhibits its succinylation-dependent ubiquitination. Similarly, the ubiquitin-mediated degradation of MG53, a key target for cardioprotection in acute myocardial infarction, depends on succinylation at the same lysine residue295. These observations suggest that succinylation can remodel protein architecture, thereby exposing target residues for ubiquitination and modulating E3 ligase binding or catalytic activity. Nonetheless, determining whether this interplay depends on the specific spatial orientation of succinylation and ubiquitination sites warrants further structural biology investigations. Such insights will be essential for elucidating the universal principles governing succinylation–ubiquitination crosstalk in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular pathologies.

Crosstalk with SUMOylation

SUMOylation is a PTM with a similar enzymatic cascade to ubiquitination, which regulates DNA damage repair, inflammation and mitochondrial function by influencing protein function, localization, stability and interaction296, 297, 298. Ubiquitination mainly mediates proteasome degradation, while SUMOylation can either promote protein degradation or enhance protein stability through synergistic or competitive effects with ubiquitination299,300. It has been reported recently that the effect of histone lactylation on enhancing CTP synthase 1 (CTPS1) stability is associated with antagonism of SUMOylation and ubiquitination. In particular, similar to ubiquitin ligases, RASD2, a SUMO E3 ligase, is activated by H3K18la, thereby promoting CTPS1 SUMOylation but inhibiting CTPS1 ubiquitination, aggravating endometriosis lesion growth301.

Succinylation also engages with SUMOylation. In cerebral ischemia, SIRT5-mediated desuccinylation of ANXA1 reduces its interaction with the SUMO-protease SENP6, leading to decreased ANXA1 SUMOylation. This alteration promotes ANXA1 nuclear translocation, microglial hyperactivation, and neuroinflammation167. During DNA damage repair, phosphorylation-dependent succinylation of flap endonuclease-1 (FEN1) at K200 stimulates its SUMOylation, enhancing FEN1 interaction with the Rad9-Rad1-Hus1 repair complex and facilitating DNA replication repair302.

In summary, the intricate crosstalk between novel acylations and canonical PTMs reveals that cellular signaling is governed by a dynamic and interconnected “modification network” rather than by linear pathways. This “modification network” poses challenges for targeting novel acylation modifications to regulate disease progression. Therefore, it is of great significance for the clinical translation of PTMs to focus on how to precisely utilize the cascade effects among various acylation modifications or circumvent their potential risks, and this area requires further exploration.

Therapeutic potential and clinical translation of targeting novel acylation modifications

Potential biomarkers related to lactylation in the diagnosis and prognosis of CCVDs

Previous studies have identified significant changes of histone and non-histone protein modifications, including lactylation, succinylation, β-hydroxybutyrylation, and malonylation, during the progression of CCVDs. Nonetheless, direct clinical evidence substantiating the efficacy of these novel acylations as biomarkers for the diagnosis and prognosis of atherosclerosis, heart failure, and stroke remains limited. Encouragingly, a burgeoning corpus of research has begun to elucidate the clinical significance of lactylation within this diagnostic framework. Specifically, analyses utilizing transcriptomic and single-cell sequencing databases have identified markedly differential expression of lactylation-related genes, including proteins essential for glucose and lactate metabolism, in human patients and mouse models of dilated cardiomyopathy, acute ischemic stroke, atherosclerosis, and aortic dissection177,303, 304, 305, 306. One study indicated that a metabolic shift toward glycolysis in stroke patients is associated with post-stroke neurological impairment and the risk of cerebral edema, particularly in the elderly. This suggests that serum lactate levels and the lactate/pyruvate ratio may serve as potential prognostic biomarkers for stroke outcomes192. Similarly, a comprehensive clinical analysis found a notable association between circulating lactate levels and markers of myocardial injury in patients with type 2 diabetes199. Notably, a retrospective study investigating cardiac mortality following myocardial infarction (MI) revealed that S100A9 K26la levels were negatively correlated with cardiac function indices and patient survival rates at 3, 6, and 12 months. The absence of such a correlation for total (unmodified) S100A9 implies that S100A9 K26la specifically represents a promising biomarker for predicting mortality risk following acute MI136.

Overall, in addition to lactylation, research on biomarkers associated with other novel acylation modifications remains scarce. Although initial progress has been made in the clinical application of lactylation-related markers (see Table 3), most studies are still in the preclinical omics screening stage and exhibit relatively weak specificity. Furthermore, in clinical-stage research, the lack of extensive prospective cohort studies remains a primary obstacle to the clinical translation of fundamental findings. Drugs targeting novel acylation modifications

PTMs have been demonstrated to function as critical molecular mechanisms linking cellular metabolism to epigenetic and functional protein regulation. Dysregulation of acylation patterns, frequently driven by metabolic alterations, is increasingly associated with pathological states, indicating that targeted intervention in these processes may offer a promising therapeutic approach for various diseases. Currently, clinical research on medications specifically targeting novel acylation modifications is stalled. Most of the writing and erasing processes of acylation modifications are enzyme-dependent. Consequently, targeting protein acylation by modulating acetyltransferases and deacetylases has emerged as a promising therapeutic approach for CCVDs. In calcific aortic valve disease (CAVD), andrographolide inhibits LDHA-dependent lactate production and antagonizes p300 activity to reduce H3K18la, thereby attenuating Runx1-driven osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells272. However, the therapeutic potential of andrographolide is hindered by its poor oral bioavailability and safety profile. To address this, a recent study proposed a strategy using carrier polymers with high solubility and biocompatibility to develop an andrographolide solid dispersion, which enhances its pharmacological efficacy by improving drug solubility318. Similarly, jatrorrhizine has been demonstrated to interact with KAT5, suppressing lactylation of ALDOA and inhibiting expression of Runx2314. In cardiac hypertrophy, p300-mediated 2-hydroxyisobutyrylation (Khib) of DLD reduces pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) activity and impairs cardiac energetics. Ginsenoside Rg3 inhibits this modification, restoring PDH function and ATP production in injured hearts312. Furthermore, gastrodin has demonstrated cardioprotective effects against sepsis-induced myocardial injury by promoting the ubiquitin-dependent degradation of KAT2A and suppressing H3K14la311. A Phase I clinical trial (NCT07422142) assessing the safety and pharmacokinetics of gastrodin injection in healthy individuals is currently registered, potentially providing compelling evidence for its clinical translation. A separate study indicated that nobiletin improves heart failure through the dual regulation of the desuccinylase SIRT5 and the acetyltransferase p300. Nobiletin specifically stimulates SIRT5, which facilitates p300 desuccinylation and inhibits its enzymatic activity316. Despite its limited bioavailability attributed to inadequate stability and pH sensitivity, new techniques for manufacturing stable Pickering high-internal-phase emulsions—utilizing soy protein isolate—have been developed to improve both the digestive stability and bioaccessibility of nobiletin319,320.

Various acylation modifications are driven by metabolic reprogramming, and emerging research indicates that pharmacological interventions targeting metabolic pathways can modulate these modifications. Specifically, dexmedetomidine promotes the nuclear receptor NR3C1 phosphorylation-dependent activation of pyruvate dehydrogenase kinase 4 (PDK4). This, in turn, facilitates PDH phosphorylation, thereby inhibiting the flux of pyruvate into the lactate production pathway. Consequently, this reduces MDH2 K241la levels, alleviating mitochondrial damage and oxidative stress associated with MDH2 dysfunction310. Excitingly, results from clinical trials indicate that dexmedetomidine, administered at appropriate doses, exhibits acceptable safety in newborns undergoing cardiopulmonary bypass surgery. A pharmacokinetic model has been developed to optimize stable plasma concentrations, thereby facilitating the precise therapeutic application of dexmedetomidine for treating cardiac ischemia–reperfusion injury321. Additionally, narciclasine suppresses glycolytic activity and the expression of associated enzymes, such as GLUT1 and PFKFB3, consequently reducing H3K18la-mediated NF-κB activation and endothelial inflammation in atherosclerosis315. Furthermore, an adenosine-rich extract of Ganoderma lucidum has been shown to regulate lipid metabolism by modulating protein acetylation and crotonylation within the PPAR signaling pathway. The minimal hepatotoxicity and nephrotoxicity of this extract in mouse models suggest its potential as a safer alternative to pure adenosine, which possesses lipid-regulating properties but is limited by significant adverse effects307.

For the myocardial injury caused by mitochondrial dysfunction, researchers are exploring new therapeutic strategies. Among them, a promising approach is to target the malonylation modification of the mitochondrial outer membrane channel protein voltage-dependent anion channel 2 (VDAC2). Research has demonstrated that the malonylation modification of VDAC2 induces structural alterations, resulting in mitochondrial dysfunction. To address this issue, a mitochondrial-targeted nanomaterial named TPP-AAV has been developed. This material can directly target VDAC2 and effectively ameliorate mitochondrial dysfunction by inhibiting its malonylation modification. Currently, the successful synthesis of TPP-AAV and its favorable membrane permeability have been verified. Furthermore, histological and cytotoxicity assessments have provided additional confirmation of its safety. These findings collectively suggest that TPP-AAV possesses extensive potential for applicability in the treatment of sepsis-induced myocardial dysfunction317.

The pharmacological targeting of new acylation marks represents a promising avenue in cardiovascular therapy; yet, it faces a fundamental challenge of achieving precision. First of all, current strategies, which range from broad-spectrum enzyme inhibitors such as p300 blockers to metabolite-modulating drugs, generally demonstrate “proof-of-concept” efficacy but frequently lack mechanistic specificity and pose concerns about off-target effects. A critical bottleneck is the scarcity of truly modification-specific regulators; most “writers” and “erasers” exhibit promiscuity across different acyl groups. Future drug development must therefore pivot towards strategies that enhance specificity, such as designing molecules that disrupt the interaction between a unique acylation mark and its dedicated “reader” protein. Secondly, the clinical translation of several pharmaceuticals encounters obstacles pertaining to insufficient bioavailability and inadequate formulation stability. The development of highly biocompatible materials or application of nano-delivery systems represents an effective approach to addressing this issue. Ultimately, the majority of current preclinical research is confined to efficacy and safety assessments utilizing rodent models, which unavoidably intensifies the translational challenge presented by intrinsic interspecies variations. Developing large animal models of CCVD injuries to further explore the pharmacodynamic mechanisms and biosafety of these medications is one strategy to address this dilemma.

Conclusions and future perspectives

Metabolic reprogramming occurs during the progression of CCVDs, and there appears to be a bidirectional causal relationship between this reprogramming and the onset or progression of these diseases. To adapt to hypoxia or nutrient deprivation during the disease process, cells have to change metabolic pathways to maintain energy needs, and this adaptive change is mainly reflected in the key enzymes in glucose, lipid or amino acid metabolism322. Although this adaptive response may be protective early in the disease, more importantly, continuous metabolic reprogramming may lead to more severe pathological changes caused by metabolite-induced protein acylation modifications323. Histone acylation directly modulates chromatin accessibility, thereby activating or repressing specific genomic programs (see Fig. 2). In non-histone proteins, acylation exerts diverse effects: it can enhance the transcriptional activity of transcription factors, alter the enzymatic function or stability of signaling proteins, and influence downstream pathways. A variety of acylation modifications can be catalyzed by almost the same enzymes, which are mainly classical acetyltransferases and deacetylases. Due to the intricate regulatory network of organisms, this non-selective acylation poses a considerable obstacle for comprehensive exploration of the specific regulatory mechanisms governing modifications such as lactylation, crotonylation, succinylation, and β-hydroxybutyrylation in CCVDs, as well as for the clinical relevance of therapeutic targets linked to novel acylation modifications. Although current data indicates that acylation changes are often regulated through overlapping mechanisms, certain deacetylases demonstrate distinct substrate specificity, with the SIRT family being the most prominent. Specifically, SIRT5 possesses a catalytic pocket containing conserved tyrosine and threonine residues, which facilitate robust demalonylase and desuccinylase activities92. In contrast, SIRT1–3 are characterized by hydrophobic pockets, exhibiting a strong propensity for binding substrates with hydrophobic acyl groups324. Furthermore, one thing is certain: these acylation or deacylation modifications do not occur simultaneously, indicating that alongside the possible participation of distinct regulatory enzymes in acylation modulation, disparities in metabolic conditions that induce alterations of metabolite concentrations or cytokines present in particular pathological microenvironments may also influence the selection of acylation types. For instance, in atherosclerotic ECs, the upregulation of SLC22A6 facilitates increased glycolysis, leading to H3K9la-associated endothelial dysfunction177. While in the presence of elevated serum BHB levels, ECs after myocardial infarction activate H3K9bhb to enhance cardiac collateral circulation75. As discussed, acylation modifications, functioning as downstream targets of metabolic reprogramming, have shown significant promise in regulating the pathogenic course of CCVDs. Consequently, it is crucial to discover particular regulatory enzymes that modulate acylation modifications via omics-based screening and biochemical interaction studies, or to examine alterations in protein interactions under various clinical situations.

The regulatory function of epigenetics is intimately linked to the cell-type-specific epigenome and the complex network of protein interactions that support unique physiological functions325, 326, 327, 328. In ECs, subjected to a chronic inflammatory milieu, the H3K18la-mediated transcriptional activation of Snail1 is meticulously regulated by ASF1A (a p300 cofactor), thereby influencing atherosclerotic susceptibility182. In contrast, in macrophages—pivotal regulators of cardiovascular homeostasis—H3K18la is enriched at the promoters of TCA cycle and anti-inflammatory genes, initiating oxidative phosphorylation-dependent M2 polarization181. Similarly, increased lactate following stroke induces MeCP2 lactylation at K210 in both microglia and neurons. In microglia, this modification activates cellular processes by enhancing hexokinase 2 (HK2) transcription; whereas in neurons, it modulates pro-apoptotic gene expression to maintain an anti-apoptotic state202,203. Acylation modifications can cross-regulate other PTMs, such as phosphorylation, acetylation, and ubiquitination. Although these interactions are often indirect-typically mediated by changes in enzyme activity or stability rather than direct chemical competition-they represent a complex layer of functional regulation. Despite the immense potential of acylation modifications in CCVDs, the underlying upstream and downstream mechanisms remain in the preliminary stages of investigation. Specifically, the mechanistic evidence for conditions such as diabetic cardiomyopathy and calcific atherosclerosis requires further strengthening. Consequently, it is essential to perform extensive multi-omics profiling of cardiovascular epigenomes within their pathological microenvironments. Identifying specific cofactors and downstream effectors that regulate acylation will be crucial for elucidating these intricate regulatory networks and for advancing the therapeutic application of acylation-based interventions.

In summary, this review comprehensively integrates the rapidly expanding field of novel acylation modifications, emphasizing the emergence of a pivotal “metabolo–epigenetic” axis in CCVDs. We highlight that metabolites serve as more than mere energy substrates; they act as direct, context-dependent regulators of a complex “acyl-code” that orchestrates protein function, gene expression, and intercellular crosstalk—particularly within pathways governing inflammation, endothelial integrity, and mitochondrial homeostasis. While current research has successfully identified multiple acylation modifications implicated in disease pathogenesis, offering promising avenues for modulating biological functions under pathological states, these findings have yet to reach the threshold for clinical translation. This gap underscores an urgent need for substantial advancements to expand clinical applicability, necessitating future efforts to prioritize the identification of modification-specific regulators, the elucidation of precise crosstalk mechanisms, and the development of selective pharmacological tools. Ultimately, the goal is to engineer precision molecular interventions—such as designer “readers” or site-specific editing tools—capable of selectively reprogramming pathogenic modification networks to arrest disease progression and promote functional tissue repair, thereby unlocking the full clinical potential of this novel regulatory axis in cardiovascular and cerebrovascular medicine.
