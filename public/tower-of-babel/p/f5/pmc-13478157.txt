Action video game playing impacts occupational screening for high-stakes professionals
Authors: Aaron Cochrane, Ciara Sibley, Rebecca NeSmith, C Shawn Green, Daphne Bavelier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAction video game playing has been linked with enhanced cognitive and perceptual abilities measured via a variety of basic psychological lab tests. However, much less work has examined such relations in the context of measures with direct real-world workplace relevance. Here we examined the performance of male United States military personnel on a psychomotor task battery, part of an official selection test designed to assess aptitude for military aviation. We found that action video game playing was significantly and positively associated with performance on this battery. More specifically, increased action video game experience among Naval Flight Students was linked to both better initial performance as well as more rapid learning within the psychomotor task. In contrast, when examining the same associations in a control group for whom the psychomotor task was not occupationally relevant (i.e., not Naval Flight Students), action video game playing was not reliably associated with psychomotor task learning. Time spent playing recreational video games with mechanics more similar to the screening task, flight simulator games, was not related to psychomotor task learning speed in either group. This pattern suggests that recreational action video game playing may work in concert with explicit occupational training to support effective learning and successful workplace outcomes in high-stakes professions.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Learning and memory

Introduction

The demands of the 21st-century workforce necessitate constant learning and expertise acquisition on the part of individuals. Indeed, it is estimated that the average US worker today will change jobs between 12 and 13 times in their lifetime, with about 1 in 4 of those job changes being part of a broader full career change (unmuld.com, 2022; zippia.com, 2023). Each of these job changes will, in turn, necessitate a sizable amount of new learning—including everything from new terminology, to new technology, to entirely new skill sets. This constant pressure for adaptability calls for a better understanding of the factors that may facilitate such flexibility.

To date, most basic science research examining predictors of this type of adaptability has focused on individual-level traits or constructs. This includes, for instance, the construct of intelligence, which has at its core the ability to learn from experience and efficiently adapt to new contexts1–3. Perhaps not surprisingly, then, measures of intelligence, and in particular fluid intelligence, have been shown to be excellent predictors of success in the workplace4–6. A host of other constructs, many of which are related to or are thought to partially underpin intelligence, have similarly been shown to be associated with successful careers. These include working memory and various executive functions7,8.

There is considerably less work, though, on specific life experiences (outside of formal education) that might be related to the ability to learn and develop new abilities. One such experience that has been put forward is playing first or third-person commercially available shooter games (together referred to as “action video games”). In correlational studies, playing action video games has been associated with better performance in a wide range of cognitive functions, including perception, top-down attention, and task-switching9–14. All told, meta-analytic work has shown that individuals who, as part of their regular daily life, have played 3 or more hours per week of action video games for at least 6 months outperform individuals who report playing fewer than 3 h of video games by around 2/3rd of a standard deviation across all measures of cognitive function combined15,16.

Importantly, intervention studies have further established that the relationship between action video game play and enhanced cognitive function is causal. That is, it is not simply the case that people who are born with better cognitive functions choose to play action video games disproportionately. Across a number of studies, individuals (who had rarely or never played video games previously) were asked to play either action video games (experimental group) or non-action video games (control group) for 30–50 h. While both groups showed improvements on the games they played, those in the action video game group showed disproportionate improvements in basic vision (e.g., contrast sensitivity) and visual attention (e.g., as measured by visual crowding, visual search, or multiple object tracking paradigms), more agile task-switching and multitasking abilities, and better decision-making, amongst other skills, as compared to the control group that played non-action video games12,13,17–20. Meta-analytic work has again confirmed the beneficial, causal impact of action video game training on cognition, with estimates of the benefit being around 1/3rd of a standard deviation15,16 (noting that this magnitude depends highly on factors such as the particular cognitive domain, the amount of training, etc, although see ref. 21 for an opposing view of the field).

Interestingly, the finding that action video game experience produces benefits not just on the games themselves, but on basic measures of cognitive function that do not bear direct resemblance to action video games, runs counter to much of the literature on human learning22. This state of affairs is most tellingly illustrated by the case of perceptual learning, where the benefits of training are typically reasonably tied to the specifics of the trained task23. For instance, individuals trained to judge offsets of vertically aligned boxes will improve in that ability (i.e., will be able to do the task with smaller and smaller offsets), but if the boxes are subsequently changed to be aligned horizontally, they’ll frequently show no advantage of the previous training24. In other words, the benefits of training are specific to the trained orientation, failing to generalize to new orientations. What makes action video game training unique, then, is that individuals show improvements not just on the action video games themselves, but also on tasks that are far more dissimilar to action video games than are, for instance, horizontally aligned boxes and vertically aligned boxes (e.g., psychology lab tasks using letters, dots, lines, gratings, etc.). Indeed, most theories of learning assume that near transfer or transfer to tasks closely related to the trained one is much easier to induce than far transfer or transfer to tasks that share fewer characteristics with the trained one25,26. As such, it is the degree of far transfer induced by action video game experience that has made it of interest both from a theoretical and a practical perspective.

Two possibilities for how action video game-related enhancements manifest have been considered27. The first possibility is that the rich array of skills challenged by action video game play taps certain shared constructs with laboratory-designed tasks and thus directly transfers to different aspects of cognition. This predicts that the benefits of previous action video game play will be seen right away, from the very moment an individual encounters a new task. Alternatively, it could be that action video game play facilitates adaptability or the learning of novel tasks or contingencies—in other words, produces “learning to learn.” In this case, the benefits of action video game play would be expected to manifest as faster learning or greater learning gains on new cognitive tasks. Of note, these two mechanisms are certainly not mutually exclusive and could be simultaneously at play.

While most of the early results in the action video game field were interpreted as direct transfer, it was also the case that tasks and data analyses were not designed to adjudicate between these alternatives. As researchers have, in recent years, started to design tasks to more clearly differentiate direct transfer and learning to learn, evidence is accruing suggesting that the learning to learn mechanism accounts for at least part of the benefits that arise from action video game play. For example, in one recent action video game intervention, participants were compared on two learning tasks, one a perceptual learning task and the other a cognitive learning task, after 45 h of training on either action video games or on control video games28. The two groups showed reasonably similar initial performance on the two learning tasks. However, those individuals who had trained with action video games showed significantly faster improvements in performance through time. Additional support for the idea that action video games foster learning to learn has been found in laboratory settings testing perceptual, visuo-motor control and working memory learning29–31.

Yet, despite the fact that the effect sizes produced by action video games are of a magnitude that they would be predicted to have real-world value, the literature focused on real-world-relevant outcomes is limited. A few studies point to the potential value of video game play for surgeons, in particular, laparoscopic surgeons32–34. Yet these studies remain quite scarce and of high heterogeneity. Other studies point to video game play, and especially action video game play, as a tool to facilitate learning to read in children35–37. But, virtually none of the more real-world focused research has examined whether any observed benefits are better described as direct transfer, learning to learn or a mix of both.

There is thus a clear need for additional work examining the extent to which action video game experience is associated with direct transfer, learning to learn, or both, in measures of more real-world relevance. The present work does so by leveraging an experiment performed with active duty military personnel who repeatedly performed a subset of the Aviation Selection Test Battery (ASTB) and also completed a survey regarding their video game experience. The ASTB is a screening tool used by the United States Navy, Marine Corps, and Coast Guard to identify prospective candidates for officer aviation training programs. A component of the ASTB is the Performance Based Measures (PBM) battery, which was introduced in 2013 to assess specific skills and abilities identified as relevant to aviation aptitude38. The purpose of the PBM is to evaluate candidates’ “physical dexterity, eye-hand coordination, and ability to divide attention among different tasks” through the administration of increasingly challenging tracking subtasks performed concurrently with other cognitively demanding tasks39. That military-centered occupations could be an interesting early testbed to examine potential real-world positive applications of video game training (and/or the use of video game expertise as a predictor of performance) has been suggested by a host of research groups, noting, for instance, various shared demands between video gaming and those occupations (e.g., need for speeded decision-making40,41).

While the individual subtests of the PBM battery are strong predictors of future aviation training performance42, conventional analyses that only rely on aggregated performance metrics may miss valuable insights regarding within-task and across-task learning. We therefore sought to investigate performance on the PBM as a task that not only measures present abilities, but also captures learning capabilities. To this end, we leverage recent work43–45 that has shown that most tasks where participants’ experience unfolds through time (e.g., 64 trials of a working memory task or 100 trials of an executive function task) can be thought of, and effectively modeled, as learning tasks. This, combined with information concerning video game habits, allowed for the examination of whether previous action video game experience was associated with either immediately better performance and/or faster learning on the PBM battery. Of note, our dataset included two distinct participant populations: the first group comprised Naval Flight Students, who were selected for aviation training based, in part, on their performance on the ASTB. Therefore, this group was expected to demonstrate higher baseline proficiency in the skills and abilities assessed by the PBM battery. In contrast, the other group was enlisted service members pursuing aviation-related roles, who were likely less familiar with the type of skills the PBM battery taps.

In summary, our aim here was to test the extent to which action video game play may facilitate direct transfer and/or learning to learn in an ecologically valid context. By modeling initial performance and score progression through these series of tests, we assessed how these aspects of performance correlated with participants’ action video game history. If greater action video game play history was found to be associated with better performance at the very start of the task, this would indicate direct transfer; conversely, if greater action video game familiarity was found to be linked to a faster progression rate as the task proceeds, this would support learning to learn (as above, it is important to note that the two effects are not mutually exclusive; both could be present simultaneously).

Of further interest was the contrast between the effect of action video game familiarity and that of flight simulator game familiarity. At stake here is the issue of transfer of training. On the one hand, one may expect flight simulator game familiarity to be more aligned with the skills tested by the PBM battery than action video game play. At its core, the ASTB was designed to select individuals predicted to be successful in military aviation school. This would, in turn, result in the prediction that performance on the PBM battery should be more related to flight simulator game experience than to action video game experience. On the other hand, the well-documented fact that training gains tend to be highly specific suggests that flight familiarity may not necessarily easily transfer to this test. In contrast, the benefits of action video game play have been noted to transfer to other novel tasks, especially in the perceptual, cognitive and motor domains27,46. If so, this would predict that action video game experience may be more related to PBM battery performance than flight simulator game experience.

Methods

Participants

Active-duty military personnel (N = 461) stationed at Naval Air Station Pensacola consented to participate in this study that took place over two days. Participants were not compensated in any way for their participation, which was voluntary. Due to scheduling constraints, 183 participants were unable to return for the second session, which occurred exactly one week after the first. An additional 13 participants were removed for poor performance. This left 265 participants, of whom 25 were female. Sex was determined by self-reported survey responses, in which participants were provided the option of male or female, or to skip responding. Although an original goal was to examine variations between males and females in learning with relation to video game playing, the highly skewed proportions led us to be concerned about biased inferences. As such, we focused our analysis only on the male participants, resulting in a final sample of 228 participants (Age M = 23.81 years, SD = 3.38). The data we report here is a subset of that reported in Draheim et al. (2025), in which the authors did not address links between action video game playing and rates of learning within the PBM. Naval Research Laboratory’s Institutional Review Board approved this protocol: NRL.2021.0003. This work was not preregistered.

These 228 participants were, in turn, categorized as belonging to one of two groups. The first group (n = 128) comprised Naval Flight Students (referred to below as flight students) who had been selected for flight training, and had therefore taken the ASTB at least once. The flight students had not yet started training. The second group (n = 100) comprised enlisted U.S. Navy and Marine Corps personnel in training for roles as Aviation Ordinancemen, Aviation Structural Mechanics, and Naval Air Crewmen (referred to below as naive). Overall, 75% of the sample reported some college experience. Data were available for 116 of the 128 flight students regarding the time interval between their most recent official ASTB attempt and participation in this laboratory study. The median time interval was 665 days (SD = 415 days) with a range of 157–2491 days. For the sample of naive participants, this was their first exposure to the PBM battery.

Procedure

Data were collected during two sessions, separated by one week, within a controlled laboratory environment at U.S. Naval Air Station Pensacola, Florida. The laboratory was equipped with 20 standardized computer stations, each comprising a 24-inch monitor, a Logitech X52 flight control system (consisting of a left-handed throttle and right-handed stick controller), and stereo headphones (noting here that this system is quite different from a standard video game controller or keyboard). Each session lasted approximately three hours, for a total of six hours over two sessions. Sessions commenced with eye-tracking calibration and two brief eye-tracking tasks, not pertinent to this project. Subsequently, participants were instructed on how to log in to the Navy’s official ASTB testing platform. Participants were then informed that they would complete three consecutive rounds, or “blocks,” of a modified version of the ASTB’s PBM battery, described in detail below.

Upon completion of each PBM block, participants received a score and were instructed to write it down and wait for the rest of the group to finish that block. Participants then engaged in a group discussion facilitated by the experimenter, focusing on that block’s scores, with the intent of fostering engagement and motivation. Following the third PBM block, participants completed a video game survey (see Supplementary Materials) and a battery of cognitive tests not relevant to this project. The second session largely replicated the general task sequence of the first session, beginning with the eye tracker setup, followed by three blocks of the PBM, and commencing with additional cognitive assessments, also outside the scope of this project.

Tasks

The Iterative PBM

The Naval Research Laboratory and Naval Aerospace Medical Institute collaborated to build an experimental, repeatable version of the Navy’s PBM battery, henceforth referred to as the Iterative PBM. The intention of this modified version was to investigate within-task learning across repeated engagement with the psychomotor tracking subtasks. Given this focus, we excluded the PBM’s direction orientation subtest, but all other subtasks within the Iterative PBM were identical to those experienced by official ASTB test takers. The original PBM subtest instructions were adapted to enhance participant comprehension and to explicitly address the iterative nature of the task.

The Iterative PBM encompasses six subtests administered sequentially during each block: dichotic listening test (DLT), vertical tracking test (VTT), 2-dimensional aviation tracking subtask (ATT), two-handed tracking subtask (ATTVTT; see Fig. 1), two-handed tracking with dichotic listening subtask (ATTVTTDLT), and two-handed tracking with emergency scenarios subtask (ATTVTTSCN). In the initial block of each session (i.e., blocks 1 and 4), participants receive self-paced instructions and a 30-second unscored practice period for each subtest, with the exception of the ATTVTTSCN, which mirrors the official test procedure by omitting practice. Abbreviated instructions, with no practice, are provided in subsequent blocks (blocks 2 and 3) of each day. The duration of the scored portion of each subtask varies, as shown in Table 1. The following section provides details about each subtask within the Iterative PBM. Further information regarding the official PBM tasks and their validation can be found in Phillips et al. (2011) and Walker et al.38.

Dichotic listening (DLT)

During the dichotic listening subtask (DLT), participants wear stereo headphones and listen to auditory stimuli consisting of serially presented letters and numbers. During the task, participants keep their left hand on the throttle and their right hand on the stick and are instructed to selectively attend to only one ear for 30 second blocks of time. They respond to odd numbers by pressing the throttle button and even numbers by pressing the stick button. Within this Iterative PBM, the DLT is administered initially for 120 s to familiarize participants with the subtask, because it is performed later in the Iterative PBM with a tracking subtask. DLT performance data were collected, but fall outside the scope of this analysis.

Vertical tracking (VTT)

The vertical tracking subtask (VTT) requires participants to track a yellow airplane moving along the Y-axis on the left-hand side of the display (see the leftmost section of Fig. 1). During the instruction phase, participants are directed to place their left hand on the throttle, which controls the movement of the red crosshair, and to attempt to precisely track the movement of the yellow airplane. The yellow airplane’s trajectory involves bouncing off the top and bottom boundaries of the display and changing vertical direction after being tracked closely for a specified duration (note that specific information is withheld to maintain the integrity of the operational test). Following self-paced instructions and a 30-second unscored practice period, participants complete 60 s of tracking. During this period, the speed of the yellow airplane (i.e., task difficulty level) increases at the 20 and 40-s marks.

2-dimensional airplane tracking (ATT)

In the 2-dimensional airplane tracking subtask (ATT), participants are tasked with tracking a yellow airplane moving in straight lines, across two dimensions (horizontally, vertically, and diagonally), in the main part of the display (see Fig. 1). Participants are instructed to position their right hand on the control stick, which controls the movement of the red crosshair. The stick’s Y-axis controls are inverted, such that moving the stick forward moves the crosshair upwards on the display, while pulling the stick back moves the crosshair downwards on the display. The yellow airplane moves in straight lines and reflects off the display boundaries, and it also changes direction after being tracked closely for a specified duration. Following self-paced instructions and a 30-second unscored practice period, participants complete 60 s of tracking. During this period, the speed of the yellow airplane increases at the 20 and 40-s marks.

Two-handed Tracking (ATTVTT)

The two-handed tracking subtask (ATTVTT) requires participants to simultaneously perform the VTT and ATT, as shown in Fig. 1, with the left hand on the throttle and right on the stick. Participants are given instructions, followed by a 30-second practice period, and 120-s scored phase where the speed of the airplanes increase at the 40 and 80-second marks.

Two-handed tracking with dichotic listening (ATTVTTDLT)

The two-handed tracking with dichotic listening subtask (ATTVTTDLT) requires participants to do the ATTVTT while simultaneously performing the DLT. Participants are given instructions, followed by a 30-second practice period, and 180-s scored phase where the airplane speeds increase at the 60 and 120-s marks.

Two-handed tracking with emergency scenarios (ATTVTTSCN)

The two-handed tracking with emergency scenarios subtask (ATTVTTSCN) requires participants to perform the ATTVTT while simultaneously responding to three randomly presented emergency scenarios. These scenarios require rapid execution of specific response sequences, involving a combination of button presses and knob adjustments, as explained during the instructional phase. Participants are given instructions, followed by a 120-s scored phase where the airplane speeds increase at the 40 and 80-s marks.

The DLT and VTT subtasks are not included in the model presented in the results, as they were simple enough that most participants performed near asymptotic performance, leaving little room for learning to be observed.

The performance measure used for each of the Iterative PBM’s tracking subtasks was the Euclidean distance between the target and the crosshair. This distance, which we will hereafter refer to as tracking error, was recorded at a rate of 5 samples per second. These errors were downsampled by dividing each of the subtasks’ difficulty levels’ recording durations into 10 equally-spaced time windows, thereby leading each subtask for each block to be reflected by 30 tracking error values. Across all difficulties, subtasks, and blocks, each participant thus had 900 tracking error values (i.e., 10 tracking errors for each of 3 difficulties, on 5 subtasks with 6 blocks each).

Surveys

The video game survey was modeled after the Bavelier lab video game questionnaire47 and used to capture experience with specific genres of video games during adulthood and childhood. As such, the survey included two overarching questions: the first asked participants to respond based on their gaming activities during the last 12 months, while the second question directed participants to respond based on their gaming activities from ages 3–13. For each question, participants were told “for each type of game, specify the number of hours played per week when you played the most” and listed nine independent genres of video games, including the same eight game genres as Bavelier’s (First/Third-Person Shooters, Action-RPG/Adventure, Sports/Driving, Real-Time Strategy/MOBA, Turn-Based/ Non-Action Role-Playing/Fantasy, Turn-Based Strategy/Life Simulation/Puzzle, Others) in addition to Flight Simulator Games. Participants were given six ordinal options to choose among for responses: “Never,” “Less than 1 h”, “Between 1 and 3 h,” “Between 3 and 5 h,” “Between 5 and 10 h,” and “More than 10 h,” which we coded as a 0 to 5 scale. See the Supplementary materials for a copy of the survey. To calculate a continuous score of action video game experience, we summed the coded scores from the following genres for both the childhood and adult questions: First/Third-Person Shooters; Action-RPG/Adventure; Sports/Driving; Real-Time Strategy/MOBA. This resulted in one score per participant that could range from 0 to 40.

Participants also responded to a flight experience survey that included questions related to any actual logged flight hours and flight simulator experience (both professional and recreational). We were interested in the relative links between actual flight experience and Iterative PBM tracking error; however, the overwhelming majority of naive participants had zero flight experience and no experience with professional-level flight simulators. Therefore, in order to compare groups, we used the summed score of flight simulator game experience from the video game survey, across childhood and adulthood; this led to a score that could range from 0 to 10.

Modeling

Changes in tracking error through time were modeled as an exponential function of experience duration on the task48–52. We estimated these trajectories of change using Bayesian generalized nonlinear mixed-effects regression43,53,54 using the brms package in R. Priors were minimally informative and had no influence whatsoever on participant-level estimates of parameters, which were the primary values of interest (see Supplement for model specification). A 3-parameter saturating exponential function was specified by modeling starting tracking error, asymptotic tracking error, and the shape of the exponential curve (parameterized as a time constant defining the amount of time taken to proceed 50% of the way from the start to the asymptote; we refer to this as the time-to-learn parameter). Asymptotic tracking error represents the error level approached with an infinite amount of practice, or in other words, how well a participant would perform if we were to allow them an arbitrarily large amount of time on the task.

We note that several features of the data led us to make certain choices in model specification. First, starting tracking error and asymptotic tracking error were defined according to a linear model structure including a fixed effect contrast between flight students and naïve participants as well as a fixed effect of subtask and difficulty (i.e, speed of the target) within subtask, thereby accounting for the broader structural differences in start and end performance assumed with subtasks of varying difficulties. In contrast, time-to-learn was specified according to a linear mixed-effects structure including a fixed contrast between flight student and naive participants, but not subtask and difficulty. In doing so, we assume a common time-to-learn across the four subtasks of interest for each participant. Second, given our interest in individual-level learning estimates, a by-participant random intercept was also added to the starting tracking error and time-to-learn. Because learning unfolded slowly relative to the measured timescale55, we conservatively did not estimate by-participant values for asymptotic performance. In doing so, we assume that participants, for a given subtask, difficulty level and group, arrived at the same asymptotic performance. While unlikely to be true, strictly speaking, we believe that this was a worthwhile simplification for the sake of maintaining model estimability and robustness of our other by-participant parameters. In sum, nested within the exponentially-decaying nonlinear generalized model54, the following linear mixed-effects models were used: (i) starting tracking error = group (2 level fixed effect) + subtask (4-level fixed effect) * difficulty (3-level fixed effect) + participant-level random intercept; (ii) time-to-learn = group (2 level fixed effect) + participant-level random intercept; (iii) asymptotic performance = group (2 level fixed effect) + subtask (4-level fixed effect) * difficulty (3-level fixed effect).

We modeled the median values of tracking error as a function of time on task (i.e., using the asymmetric Laplace distribution and likelihood function in brms with the estimated quantile fixed to be .5, due to our lack of knowledge of the true generative distributional properties of the tracking error measure). In addition, although the median was being estimated, it remained necessary to estimate a dispersion parameter; this dispersion parameter was modeled as a log-linear function of time.

For full model specification and fixed-effects posterior distributions, see Supplement. Tests of parameter reliability (e.g., comparing naïve to flight student) were conducted by identifying whether the relevant parameter’s posterior distribution 95% credible interval (CI) included zero. Subsequent associations with video game playing measures used participant-level estimates and were tested with bootstrapped rank correlations, wherein we assessed whether the 95% confidence intervals included zero, or with linear regression models. All tests were two-sided, and all tests were designed such that the data would meet the assumptions of each test.

We also estimated a model with the same distributional assumptions as our model of learning but with no change estimated over time; in this non-learning model, the unchanging median tracking error was estimated using the same mixed-effects formula as the starting value described above ; (i) tracking_error = is_NFS + subtask * difficulty + (1 | ID). This non-learning model acts as a baseline model against which the learning model can be quantitatively compared in order to assess goodness-of-fit.

Evidence for learning on the subtasks

Tracking error was seen to decrease over time in each of the 4 key tracking subtasks. The change between block 1 and block 6, broken down by group and subtasks, can be seen in Table 2.

Our Bayesian modeling confirms and qualifies such learning (see Fig. 3). The Bayesian nonlinear mixed-effects model converged (R-hat < 1.05 for all parameters). When comparing the model that included learning over time to the model with no learning, the model with learning performed much better. The Leave-One-Out Information Criterion (LOOIC) indicated a large improvement in predictive power ΔLOOIC = −35481 ± 8.9; values less than -4 are conventionally considered to indicate meaningful differences in model predictiveness;56. A heuristic effect size can be calculated by observing the rank correlation between the null model fitted values and true distances (rho = 0.66) and the learning model rank correlation (rho = 0.72). Note that the null model already captures much of the variation in errors due to the large differences between participants, subtasks, and difficulty levels. A naive pseudo-ΔR2, found by squaring the above rho values and then taking the difference, would indicate an additional 7.6% of variance explained by adding a learning component to the model.

Learning characteristics per group and subtask

Discussion

Here we examined the extent to which learning on an occupationally-relevant visuo-motor tracking task was related to action video game play. More specifically, we examined both whether action video game play was associated with immediate differences in performance (i.e., from the very earliest moments) and/or with differences in learning through time. This is critical, as analyses of such tasks typically do not consider learning. Instead, it is more typical for single dependent variables for each subtask to be calculated by aggregating over measurements. We have recently argued that for many (if not most) tasks used in perceptual, cognitive, and motor psychology, such aggregation-based analyses are not appropriate44,45,49,52,54. Indeed, at the theoretical level, aggregated analyses inherently assume that performance across all timepoints is identically and independently distributed; in other words, that early behavior in the task is generated via the exact same process as late behavior in the task. This is unlikely to be true given how effectively human beings learn. Furthermore, empirical results show that modeling even very short “measurement” tasks as changing through time provides both a better fit to the data and permits deeper inferences about the given behaviors43–45,49,52,54,57. Although many have argued that the best way to characterize behavior is through the lens of learning, it is only recently, thanks to greater computational power, that trial-by-trial analyses of how performance progresses over time have become more common.

By including both a flight student group with some familiarity with the task as well as a naive group, our data further speaks to various stages of learning and how these may be affected by everyday activities such as video game training. According to the theory of Fitts and Posner58, for example, one would expect little impact of action video game experience on the very early cognitive stage of learning, where the rules of the task have to be cognitively mastered. In contrast, one would expect a greater impact of action video game experience once in the associative stage of learning, during which participants become more at ease with the task27.

Our results demonstrated, first and foremost, clear learning in each of our groups of participants and in each of the four tracking subtasks considered. Learning was notable despite quite large variability in learning curves across groups as well as within groups (see Fig. 2). Consistent with their lesser familiarity with the task, our naive group showed greater starting tracking error than the flight student group. The two groups did not show a significant difference in learning rate (with, on average, each group getting halfway from start to asymptote in about 3.2 blocks). Finally, the flight student group was estimated to achieve better asymptotic performance than the student group. This latter finding is not surprising, given that the flight students represented a selected sample of individuals expected to have an aptitude for the skills and abilities represented in the subtasks of the PBM battery.

When it came to the relationship between action video game experience and learning, both starting tracking error and time-to-learn correlated significantly with action video game play experience in flight students (rho = −0.31, rho = −0.35, respectively). Surprisingly, the learning indices showed less marked correlations with flight simulator game play experience. Starting tracking error did correlate with flight simulator game play (rho = −0.21), but time-to-learn did not (rho = −0.09). This finding warrants further exploration, especially given the fast pace of flight training that requires flight students to learn quickly, as well as the Navy’s recommendation that it may be useful to “practice using flight simulator software with a stick-and-throttle set” in preparing for the PBM subtest (NAMI, 2025). Perhaps encouraging action video game play may also be warranted, given that this finding supports the view that action video game play fosters the future learning of tasks that are not directly related to such game play. Finally, and crucially given our initial question, both starting tracking error and time-to-learn are related to action video game play in flight students, which suggests the possibility of both direct transfer and faster learning rate.

In contrast to the flight student population, neither action video game play nor flight simulator game play correlated with the initial tracking performance or learning measures in naive participants. This was the case despite the two groups showing very similar distributions of time-to-learn. On the one hand, this may signal that action video game play may only relate to learning in its intermediate stages after sufficient familiarity has been gained with the task. On the other hand, it may also result from greater sources of variability affecting naive participants’ performance as compared to the flight students. Such variability may, for example, arise due to lower motivation among the naive (enlisted) sample compared to the flight students, for whom the PBM battery was relevant to their military occupation and used for selection. Naive participants may also be more variable in their performance, as suggested by Fig. 2, especially in their starting tracking error. This may be due to various idiosyncratic differences in experience more heavily contributing to their performance on the task as compared to flight students, who had all already been exposed to previous official selection testing and unofficial practice materials obtained prior to testing. This would place flight students at an intermediate level of learning where performance is seen to be less noisy, while placing naive participants at an earlier, and noisier, level of performance.

The lack of correlation between our learning outcomes and flight simulator game play in the naive participants is expected, given the very low level of reported flight simulator play in the naive group. Indeed, both correlations were quite low and clearly centered on zero. This was not the case for the action video game correlations, which, despite being non-significant, showed the same basic patterns (i.e., toward better starting performance and faster learning as action video game experience grows) as was seen in the flight student group.

Limitations and future directions

The methodological constraints and limitations that inherently arise from performing work at the intersection of basic and applied science, in turn, leave a host of open questions for future research. One such limitation is with regard to our participant sample, which consisted of 240 male participants and only 25 female participants. This imbalance, along with the overall low number of female participants, required that we analyze only male participants in the current work. While some previous literature suggests that females benefit as much (or in some cases more) than male participants from video game experience17,59, other research suggests the opposite60. As such, whether the same pattern of results we found here in male participants would be found in female participants requires future examination. Furthermore, the naive sample comprised military personnel who were not selected through the same stringent screening process as the flight students, which may introduce confounds related to baseline cognitive ability. Therefore, future research should examine how action gaming experience relates to cognitive ability and repeated PBM performance, or more generally, complex skill acquisition.

Next, there are a host of additional measures of individual backgrounds and experiences that could both speak to underlying mechanisms as well as potentially improve predictive models. For example, the video game survey that was utilized focused only on hours played by genre, leaving information about factors such as skill unmeasured. Because skill and experience are necessarily partially confounded, separate measures are necessary to pin down the nature of the effects61,62. There are also a host of other types of experience that could be useful predictors of performance on the task utilized here, from general computer experience (which could vary more widely in this population than, for instance, the university populations that make up the samples in much of the literature) through other cognitively and motorically demanding real-world experiences like certain sports63,64.

Similarly, here we measured performance on a reasonably short task that is known to be predictive of real-world behavior, but is not the real-world behavior itself. As such, it would be useful to know how long various effects last; that is, if one were to use video games to attempt to train performance on this task, how long would the effects remain (noting that the small amount of previous literature on this topic has seen benefits on the timescale of months to years17,65). Additionally important would be investigating whether non-gamers eventually “catch up” and if so, how long that process takes. Furthermore, although there are known links between performance on the tracking task utilized here and flight training performance, and our work here shows links between action gaming and performance on the tracking task, this does not necessarily mean that a link would be found between action gaming and flight training performance (i.e., action gamers here could be good at the tracking task for a reason that does not underpin the task’s relation with flight training). As such, establishing the full causal chain will be necessary before any full applications can be considered.

Next, although the pattern of results seen here is more consistent with learning to learn than direct transfer, unpacking the exact mechanistic underpinnings of this learning-to-learn effect will require future research. Indeed, such learning-to-learn can arise via a number of different mechanisms, including improvements in cognitive skills that underpin successful learning (e.g., attentional control, cognitive and/or motor flexibility27) or various types of structure learning45,57. Given the nature of the tracking task, gaining a deeper understanding of the exact improvements that are resulting in augmented learning rates will be important.

Finally, while we have focused here on flight aptitude, as noted in the introduction, learning flexibility is at a premium in many 21st-century occupations. In particular, while links between video game play and a host of other real-world tasks/occupations have been examined to those far afield from flight, such as laparoscopic surgery33,34, or those more related to flight, such as unmanned aerial vehicles66,67, none have been differentiated between direct transfer and learning to learn. In particular, although here we have focused on the benefits of action video game playing, knowing whether any occupations might be harmed by such play would be of foremost importance68.

Conclusion

In all, this study provides an additional demonstration that action video game play experience is linked with enhanced performance on tasks that, on the surface, are not directly related to action video games (or at a minimum are far more different than tasks between which no such generalization is seen in the perceptual and cognitive literature; i.e., vertical lines and horizontal lines). We see that it may do so both through direct transfer (better initial performance), as well as through learning-to-learn (faster learning rate). That this effect was only detected in flight students but not naive participants, despite both groups showing similar action video game play distributions (Cohen’s d = 0.23, CI95 = [−0.02, 0.47]), points to other sources of variability being a greater contributor to learning behavior. More or less experience on the very task we measure learning on, early in the learning phase, would indeed be expected to overshadow any contribution of action video game play. It will be important in future studies to further evaluate when action video game play experience may best predict learning.
