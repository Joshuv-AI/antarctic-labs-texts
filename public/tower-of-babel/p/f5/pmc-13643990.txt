Toward tailored implementation of an artificial intelligence–based healthy dietary chatbot: differences in technology acceptance pathways by health consciousness and user characteristics
Authors: Chiu-Liang Liu, Chien-Ta Ho, Tzu-Chi Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArtificial intelligence (AI) health chatbots are increasingly used for health education and health promotion, yet the mechanisms underlying their adoption and the heterogeneity across user groups remain insufficiently understood.MethodsDrawing on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), this study examined behavioral intention to use an artificial intelligence–based healthy dietary chatbot, incorporating interactivity and entertainment as key system features, and testing whether health consciousness and sociodemographic subgroups moderate technology acceptance pathways. An anonymous questionnaire survey was conducted after participants interacted with a LINE-based AI dietary chatbot. Structural equation modeling and subgroup analysis were applied to evaluate TAM relationships, moderation by health consciousness, and boundary conditions across demographic groups.ResultsPerceived ease of use and perceived usefulness significantly predicted behavioral intention. Interactivity was primarily associated with intention indirectly through perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness, whereas entertainment showed a stronger direct association with intention. Health consciousness moderated the interactivity–usefulness and entertainment–intention relationships. Multi-group analyses further indicated pathway heterogeneity: interaction-related effects were stronger among younger, female, and more highly educated users, whereas older users placed greater weight on enjoyment and ease of use, and males exhibited a stronger usefulness–intention link.ConclusionsOverall, the findings support the applicability of TAM to artificial intelligence–based health chatbot adoption and suggest the coexistence of cognitive, interactivity-driven and affective, entertainment-driven acceptance routes. The moderating roles of health consciousness and user characteristics underscore the importance of segment-specific design and dissemination strategies to enhance adoption.

Introduction

Artificial intelligence (AI) based chatbots have increasingly been applied in the healthcare domain as scalable tools for health promotion and behavior change [1]. With strong scalability, these systems can be accessed through multiple channels, including smartphones and other convenient digital devices, enabling broad and continuous use in daily life. By incorporating high levels of interactivity and personalization, AI-based chatbots are able to deliver tailored guidance and dynamically respond to user inputs, thereby exerting varying degrees of influence on users’ behaviors [1]. To date, AI chatbot interventions have been implemented across multiple lifestyle behavior domains, including physical activity, smoking cessation, sleep quality, stress management, and diet [2–4].

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), originally proposed by Davis, is one of the most widely applied theoretical frameworks for explaining users’ acceptance of information technologies. TAM posits that perceived usefulness (PU), defined as the extent to which users believe that using a system will improve their performance, and perceived ease of use (PEOU), defined as the extent to which users believe that using a system will be free of effort, are the two core beliefs shaping users’ behavioral intention to use a technology [5, 6]. These core constructs provide a useful theoretical basis for examining how users evaluate and form intentions toward emerging digital technologies [7]. In the healthcare and digital health domains, TAM has also been repeatedly validated and used to explain users’ behavioral intention and its key determinants across a range of applications, including health information systems and digital health services such as electronic health records and clinical information systems, mobile health applications, telecare services, and AI-related solutions [8–12]. Across these contexts, perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use are typically treated as the core explanatory constructs underlying technology acceptance pathways. Across these contexts, PU and PEOU are typically treated as the core explanatory constructs underlying technology acceptance pathways. However, a traditional TAM-only specification may not fully capture context-specific influences, particularly in health-related settings where adoption can be shaped by system characteristics and usage conditions. Accordingly, TAM conceptualizes external variables as upstream determinants that affect system use primarily through their influence on users’ acceptance beliefs and attitudes [13].

In the context of artificial intelligence–based chatbots, commonly discussed external factors include system and interaction characteristics such as conversational responsiveness [14], personalization, social presence [15], transparency [16], privacy assurance [17], and interface design [18–21]. Given that chatbots rely on ongoing, two-way exchanges rather than one-time interactions, this study focuses on two salient system features—interactivity and entertainment—as key external variables that may condition users’ acceptance pathways.

Interactivity refers to the extent to which a chatbot enables responsive and personalized two-way communication, including tailoring conversations and addressing users’ inquiries, and is regarded as an important determinant of user acceptance [22–24]. Prior evidence suggests that user expectations for interactivity and rapid responsiveness may vary across user characteristics, with younger and more self-confident users tending to place greater emphasis on interactive experiences when engaging with AI-based chatbots [25]. Interactive communication also helps build human–machine relationships and enhance user satisfaction. Moreover, research on AI-based conversational digital assistants has shown that interaction style can influence users’ functional evaluations, including PEOU and PU, while AI chatbot studies suggest that functional and form design features may enhance PU, PEOU, and enjoyment, thereby facilitating behavioral intention [26]. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H1

Interactivity positively influences perceived ease of use (PEOU).

H2

Interactivity positively influences perceived usefulness (PU).

Entertainment is a hedonic means of delivering useful and valuable information, referring to the pleasure, fun, and enjoyment experienced by users during interactions with chatbots, as AI technologies evoke playfulness and enhance the overall interaction experience by creating impressions of human-like interaction, thereby increasing perceived enjoyment [21, 23, 27, 28]. Prior AI-related research suggests that hedonic motivation, such as enjoyment and entertainment, may enhance users’ perceived value of the system and promote behavioral intention [29]. In chatbot environments, entertainment has also been shown to improve customer experience and satisfaction, ultimately promoting intention [30]. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H3

Entertainment positively influences perceived usefulness (PU).

H4

Entertainment positively influences behavioral intention (BI).

Based on the core assumptions of TAM and prior empirical findings, perceived ease of use, perceived usefulness, and behavioral intention are expected to be closely related in the technology acceptance process [31]. Therefore, we propose the following hypotheses:

H5

Perceived ease of use (PEOU) positively influences perceived usefulness (PU).

H6

Perceived ease of use (PEOU) positively influences behavioral intention (BI).

H7

Perceived usefulness (PU) positively influences behavioral intention (BI).

Health consciousness (HC) refers to an individual’s tendency to focus attention on one’s own health. Prior research has shown that HC predicts a wide range of health-related attitudes and behaviors and is closely associated with how individuals seek, process, and respond to health information [32]. Individuals with higher levels of HC are more attentive to health messages, more engaged in health discussions, and more likely to adopt healthier lifestyles [32, 33]. Furthermore, studies on the adoption of health information in mass media indicate that responses to health information are influenced not only by health consciousness but also by demographic characteristics such as sex and education level [34] Accordingly, considering individuals’ health consciousness is important when designing health promotion strategies, health technology dissemination, and segmenting target populations.

Because target users may differ in sociodemographic background and health consciousness, tailored system design and subgroup-specific evaluation are essential for effective implementation [35]. Nevertheless, in the context of AI–based chatbot adoption, it remains insufficiently understood how system features translate into technology acceptance pathways and how these pathways vary across different user groups. To address this gap, the study examines variations in technology acceptance pathways across user groups and interaction features, aiming to inform the design and targeted deployment of future artificial intelligence–based chatbot interventions and to enhance their effectiveness and scalability in real-world health promotion contexts. Therefore, this study pursued three objectives aligned with a tailored implementation perspective: [1] to validate a TAM-based adoption model for an AI healthy dietary chatbot and quantify how interactivity and entertainment influence behavioral intention; [2] to examine whether health consciousness moderates the proposed adoption pathways by comparing path coefficients between high and low health consciousness groups; and [3] to assess whether these pathways differ across user subgroups (sex, age, education level, and prior usage experience) through multi-group comparisons, thereby informing segment-specific design and dissemination strategies.

Methods

Instrument development

The online questionnaire comprised two sections. The first section contained measurement scales assessing health consciousness, entertainment, interactivity, and the constructs of the TAM. The second section collected respondents’ demographic characteristics, including gender, age, experience, and educational level. The survey was conducted anonymously and did not collect any personal health information. All multi-item constructs employed in this study were adapted from previously validated and used scales in prior empirical research (Table 1). To ensure consistency with the original instruments, the item wording was retained, with only minor contextual adaptations made when necessary. In addition, two master’s-level reviewers in business and management independently examined the items to confirm semantic clarity and appropriateness in the study context. Each item was measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree”) to 5 (“strongly agree”).

Ethical considerations

We carried out a noninterventional, online questionnaire-based study applying standard social science survey methods to examine participants’ perceptions. Under Article 5 of Taiwan’s Human Research Act, studies conducted in public settings with nonspecific individuals that involve no intervention, collect data anonymously, and do not allow identification of any individual are exempt from ethics review [44]. Before completing the questionnaire, participants received an information statement describing the study aims and were told that participation was voluntary and anonymous; no personal identity or sensitive private information was collected. The study procedures adhered to the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

The target population comprised current users and potential users of artificial intelligence–based chatbot in Taiwan. Participants were recruited in public exhibition venues and similar open settings, where the chatbot was made available for free trial use. The chatbot could be accessed via smartphones through an interactive LINE-based interface. After completing the trial interaction, individuals were invited to complete a self-administered questionnaire assessing their perceptions and intended use of the chatbot.

Data management

First, respondents’ demographic characteristics were collected. Preliminary analyses, including means, standard deviations, and bivariate correlations, were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics. Subsequent analyses, including assessment of the measurement model, estimation of the structural model, and hypothesis testing of the proposed paths, were performed using AMOS 21.0. Covariance-based structural equation modeling (CB-SEM) was performed to test the proposed measurement and structural model. SEM was estimated using maximum likelihood estimation based on the covariance matrix of the observed variables [45]. It is a widely used multivariate statistical approach in social, behavioral, and health-related research because it enables the simultaneous assessment of latent constructs, measurement validity, and hypothesized causal pathways among theoretical variables [46]. To assess potential multicollinearity and common method bias (CMB), variance inflation factors (VIF) were calculated by regressing each endogenous construct on its predictors. If all VIF values were below 3.3, indicating that multicollinearity and common method bias were unlikely to affect the results [47]. The sample size was determined based on general recommendations for structural equation modeling, with a minimum of 100 participants and a preferable target of at least 150 to ensure stable parameter estimation and adequate model evaluation [48].

The measurement instrument was subsequently assessed for reliability and validity, with internal consistency examined using Cronbach’s alpha; coefficients of 0.70 or above were regarded as acceptable [49]. Next, convergent and discriminant validity were assessed to evaluate the measurement model. Convergent validity indicates the extent to which indicators of the same construct share variance; it was evaluated using the Average Variance Extracted (AVE), which measures the proportion of variance explained by the construct relative to measurement error [50]. Convergent validity was considered satisfactory when each construct’s AVE exceeded 0.50 and composite reliability (CR) reached or exceeded 0.70 [51]. Discriminant validity was evaluated using the Fornell–Larcker criterion: the square root of each construct’s AVE (diagonal) exceeded its correlations with other constructs (off-diagonal elements), indicating that constructs were empirically distinct [50]. Model fit was evaluated using multiple goodness-of-fit indices, including the chi-square statistic (χ²), the normed chi-square (χ²/df), the Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), the Goodness-of-Fit Index (GFI), Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Adjusted goodness-of-fit index(AGFI), Tucker–Lewis index (TLI), Normed fit index (NFI), and Root Mean Square Residual (RMR) [52, 53].

Finally, the hypothesized relationships among the constructs were examined using the structural model. The hypotheses were evaluated based on the estimated path coefficients and their corresponding significance levels. The significance of the structural paths was assessed using the bootstrapping resampling procedure, with p values < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Moreover, respondents’ demographic characteristics were collected. To examine the effects of demographic factors on HC, this study investigated the associations between HC and sex, education level, age, and usage experience. Because the original categories of education and age were relatively detailed and the sample size within some subgroups was limited, resulting in potentially uneven group distributions, these variables were recategorized to enhance statistical power and facilitate interpretation. Education level was reclassified into two groups: “high school or below” and “university or above”. Age was dichotomized using a 30-year cutoff. This decision was guided by the observed distribution, in which the two largest adjacent age bands were 20–29 and 30–39 years, as well as by prior literature defining emerging adulthood and “young adults” as ages 18–30 [42, 54–57].

Independent-samples t-tests were conducted to examine differences in HC across groups defined by sex, age, and experience. HC was treated as a continuous variable for the purpose of group comparison. Prior to conducting the t-tests, Levene’s test for equality of variances was performed to assess the homogeneity of variance assumption. When Levene’s test was not significant (p > 0.05), the equal-variance t-test results were interpreted; when the test was significant (p < 0.05), the results not assuming equal variances were reported. All statistical tests were two-tailed, with the level of significance set at α = 0.05.

Multigroup structural equation modeling was employed to examine potential differences in structural paths across subgroups. Based on the validated structural model, subgroup analyses were conducted to compare whether the hypothesized paths differed across groups defined by education level, age, sex, health consciousness and usage experience. Path differences were assessed by comparing critical ratios (z values), with absolute z values greater than 1.96 indicating statistical significance at the 95% confidence level [46].

Results

This study collected data at several public events, including technology exhibitions, between October 2025 and January 2026. A total of 280 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding responses with missing data or incorrect answers to reverse-coded items, 208 valid questionnaires remained, yielding a final response rate of 74.3%.

Among the respondents, 56.7% were female and 43.3% were male. The largest age group was 30–39 years (27.4%), followed by 20–29 years (26.9%), 40–49 years (21.6%), 50–59 years (12.5%), under 20 years (8.7%), and 60 years or older (2.9%). Regarding educational attainment, most respondents held a bachelor’s degree (57.7%), followed by a master’s degree (23.1%), a high school diploma (17.3%), and a doctoral degree (1.9%). Additionally, 79.3% of participants reported prior experience using AI chatbots (Table 2.).

Result of hypotheses

All seven hypotheses were supported with statistical significance. The results of the hypothesis testing are presented in Fig. 3, and the direct, indirect, and total effects are summarized in Table 6. Overall, the influence of entertainment on behavioral intention was greater than that of interactivity. Entertainment exerted a direct effect on behavioral intention, whereas the effect of interactivity on behavioral intention was primarily indirect, mediated through perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness. Both perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness positively influenced behavioral intention, with the direct effect of perceived ease of use being particularly pronounced (β = 0.392, p < 0.001).

Subgroup comparisons of structural paths indicated that, among participants with higher health consciousness (Table 8), the effects of interactivity and entertainment on perceived usefulness and behavioral intention were stronger than in the lower health consciousness group (z = − 2.851 and − 3.229, respectively; both p < 0.01). Gender differences were also observed (Table 9): females showed a stronger pathway from interactivity to perceived ease of use (z = − 1.98, p < 0.05), whereas males demonstrated a stronger effect of perceived usefulness on behavioral intention (z = 4.409, p < 0.01). Regarding age (Table 10), younger participants exhibited a stronger influence of interactivity on perceived usefulness (z = − 3.46, p < 0.01), while older participants showed greater effects of entertainment and perceived ease of use on behavioral intention (z = 2.64 and 2.695, respectively; both p < 0.01). For educational level (Table 11), respondents with a bachelor’s degree or above demonstrated a stronger effect of interactivity on perceived usefulness (z = 2.861, p < 0.01). Discussion

Existing evidence also indicates that chatbots are no longer merely information-delivery tools, but may serve as effective platforms for health behavior change interventions, with chatbot interventions shown to improve lifestyle-related behaviors [3]. However, compared with evidence on their effectiveness, less is known about how such platforms can be designed to feel more approachable, engaging, and acceptable to users, particularly because design needs may differ across user groups [58]. In this regard, human-centered design and iterative refinement may offer a practical solution by helping developers identify engagement barriers, adapt interaction styles, and optimize chatbot features according to users’ needs and preferences [59].

The findings confirm the applicability of the TAM in the context of AI-chatbot adoption. Both PEOU and PU positively influenced behavioral intention, with PEOU demonstrating the strongest direct effect. In addition, the role of system features in technology adoption was supported: both entertainment and interactivity were positively associated with behavioral intention; however, entertainment exerted a direct effect and showed a larger influence than interactivity. This pattern suggests the presence of two distinct acceptance routes in the AI-chatbot context. Interactivity primarily functions as a cognitive facilitation mechanism: by enabling users to obtain information according to their individual needs, responsive and reciprocal communication reduces the effort required to understand system operations, thereby enhancing perceived ease of use and subsequently perceived usefulness and behavioral intention [5, 60]. In contrast, entertainment operates as an affective motivational mechanism that directly increases users’ willingness to engage with the system [61]. These results indicate that users evaluate chatbots not only as instrumental information systems but also as experiential interaction media.

Although TAM provides a useful framework for explaining users’ acceptance of Artificial Intelligence–Based Healthy Dietary Chatbots, its explanatory scope may be limited in voluntary healthcare contexts. In such contexts, acceptance may be shaped by various external factors, including users’ intrinsic health motivation, perceived personal relevance, and social influences from family or peers [62]. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting technology acceptance pathways rather than a complete explanation of health-related behavioral adoption.

Interactivity and entertainment are distinctive design features of AI chatbots and represent actionable intervention points that developers can immediately adjust [24]. Prior AI chatbot research has shown that interactivity is an important system characteristic that can enhance users’ trust and may also be linked to continuance intention through increased social presence [20, 24]. In addition, a study examining customer experience and purchase intention in chatbot environments operationalized entertainment through both visual elements, such as website style and design, and textual elements, such as expressions commonly used or preferred by the target audience, thereby creating a greater sense of closeness with the chatbot. These entertainment-oriented features may improve user experience and intention and are positively associated with usage intention. This finding suggests that, beyond gamification through games, stories, or attractive designs, entertainment can also be enhanced by optimizing multiple design elements within the chatbot environment [24, 63].

The results further indicate that health consciousness varies across demographic groups, with higher levels observed among individuals with higher educational attainment and older age, consistent with prior research [64, 65]. More importantly, health consciousness plays a key moderating role in the AI-chatbot acceptance mechanism. Specifically, significant differences were observed between high- and low-health-consciousness groups in the pathways from interactivity to perceived usefulness and from entertainment to behavioral intention. Health consciousness can be regarded as a form of intrinsic motivation. When individuals possess stronger health awareness and self-care motivation, the system’s functions and experiential features are more readily translated into actual adoption intention [66]. In this context, the chatbot is perceived as a tool supporting health management, and its interactive feedback and content are more likely to be interpreted as providing practical value, thereby enhancing perceived usefulness.

In the context of technology adoption, including telemedicine, variations in socioeconomic status—such as sex, age, education level, and socioeconomic background—have been shown to shape structural pathways differently, resulting in heterogeneous behavioral intentions [67]. Consistent with this perspective, subgroup analyses indicated that the acceptance mechanism of the AI chatbot varied according to user characteristics, suggesting the presence of boundary conditions within the proposed model. Interactivity exerted stronger effects among younger and more highly educated users, implying greater responsiveness to interactive features that facilitate information exploration and user control. These users were more likely to engage in exploratory interactions and learn system functionality through trial-and-feedback processes, thereby enhancing perceived ease of use and subsequent evaluations. A qualitative study on a web-based healthy eating chatbot for young adults similarly found that tailored chatbot style, language, and interaction patterns—particularly positive, gain-framed, and professional expressions—were important [58]. Conversely, aging-related cognitive and physiological decline may increase the difficulty of perceiving, learning, and managing eHealth-related technologies, thereby making PEOU increasingly important for older users’ acceptance of digital health tools [68]. Moreover, user competence is a key antecedent of information system usage behavior. Its development may be influenced by age and educational level, as differences in these factors can lead to variations in information processing speed, technological learning ability, and knowledge absorption, thereby shaping distinct psychological pathways in system adoption [69].

Gender differences were also identified. Females demonstrated stronger pathways from interactivity and perceived ease of use to behavioral intention, indicating greater sensitivity to usability and communication experience. This pattern is consistent with prior technology acceptance research showing that men’s technology usage decisions tend to be more strongly influenced by perceived usefulness, whereas women are more influenced by perceived ease of use and subjective norms [6, 70].

Practical implications

The findings provide several practical implications for the future development of health- and diet-related chatbots. Interactivity and entertainment are important external factors and should be considered essential design components during the initial implementation stage. In addition, future chatbots should adopt a precision design approach by moving beyond simple nutritional information delivery and tailoring the balance between interactivity and entertainment to the needs of different user subgroups. For users younger than 30 years, developers should strengthen interactive features, such as responsive dialogue, real-time feedback, and personalized guidance. For users aged 30 years or older, greater emphasis should be placed on entertainment-based engagement, such as gamified goals, progress visualization, and motivational messages. Among users with higher health consciousness, both interactivity and entertainment should be considered essential design components. Educational background should also guide chatbot development: entertainment-oriented features may be more suitable for users with a high school education or below, whereas users with a college degree or above may benefit more from interactive functions that enhance perceived usefulness, such as tailored explanations and evidence-based dietary feedback.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited from a single geographic region, which may limit generalizability to populations with different cultural or digital health contexts. Second, the findings were derived from a specific AI health chatbot; therefore, the identified results may not be directly transferable to systems with different designs or functionalities. Third, the cross-sectional design assessed behavioral intention rather than actual long-term usage, limiting the interpretation of long-term real-world adoption. Fourth, although TAM provides a parsimonious baseline for explaining technology acceptance, it may not fully capture all determinants of adoption in health-related settings; therefore, the applicability of the TAM-based pathways should be interpreted under clearly defined boundary conditions. Finally, only two system features—interactivity and entertainment—were examined; additional system characteristics should be incorporated in future research to provide a more comprehensive understanding of technology acceptance. Future studies should examine actual usage behavior, retention, and long-term engagement, rather than relying solely on behavioral intention. In addition, future research should experimentally test whether subgroup-tailored chatbot designs can improve acceptance and sustained use and further assess whether improved chatbot acceptance translates into meaningful dietary behavior changes.

Conclusion

The findings support the applicability of the Technology Acceptance Model to artificial intelligence–based health chatbots, with perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness significantly predicting behavioral intention. Interactivity and entertainment operated through distinct mechanisms: interactivity influenced intention mainly via ease-of-use–related perceptions, whereas entertainment showed a more direct association with intention. Importantly, acceptance pathways were not uniform across users. Interaction-related effects were stronger among younger, female, and more highly educated users, whereas older users placed greater weight on enjoyment and ease of use, and males exhibited a stronger usefulness–intention relationship. Together, these results suggest that segment-specific design and dissemination strategies may be more effective than a one-size-fits-all approach for implementing artificial intelligence–based health chatbots across diverse user groups.
