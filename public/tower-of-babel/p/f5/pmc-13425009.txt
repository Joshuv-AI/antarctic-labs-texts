Preoperative Esophageal Physiologic Testing in Bariatric Surgery: How Far Should We Go?
Authors: Tae Hee Lee
Published: 2026

TO THE EDITOR: We read with great interest the article by Leelakiatpaiboon et al,1 who compared high-resolution esophageal manometry (HREM) with the upper gastrointestinal (UGI) series for diagnosing sliding hiatal hernia (HH) in morbidly obese patients undergoing bariatric surgery. Their paired design, the use of intraoperative findings as the reference standard, and the focus on clinically relevant hernias (≥ 2 cm) provide valuable data for preoperative assessment.1 The authors report excellent diagnostic performance of HREM (sensitivity 92.3%, specificity 93.7%) and substantially better inter-observer agreement than the UGI series. These results are consistent with previous reports in which HREM more accurately detects and grades sliding HH than endoscopy or radiology when surgical findings are used as the gold standard.2 We agree that HREM can refine risk stratification in high-volume bariatric centers with access to experienced motility specialists.

We would, however, like to place these findings in the context of current guideline recommendations. For classic antireflux surgery, consensus statements such as ICARUS and other expert panels regard preoperative esophageal manometry and reflux monitoring as mandatory to document gastroesophageal reflux disease and exclude major motility disorders.3,4 In contrast, bariatric surgery guidelines from major societies do not mandate routine manometry or pH-impedance testing in all bariatric candidates; rather, objective reflux testing is generally reserved for selected patients with significant reflux symptoms or diagnostic uncertainty, and many programs still rely mainly on symptoms, UGI series, and esophagogastroduodenoscopy for preoperative evaluation.5,6

Against this background, the algorithm proposed by Leelakiatpaiboon et al,1 which incorporates systematic HREM and 24-hour multichannel intraluminal impedance–pH monitoring for all morbidly obese patients being considered for bariatric surgery, may be difficult to implement in centers with limited resources or motility expertise.1 Comparative studies that evaluate stepwise strategies—reserving HREM and reflux monitoring for patients with troublesome reflux symptoms, discordant UGI or endoscopic findings, or planned concomitant antireflux procedures—would help determine the most cost-effective and pragmatic approach.

Nevertheless, the authors are to be commended for providing robust prospective data on HREM for HH detection in the bariatric setting. Their work highlights the potential role of HREM in clarifying esophagogastric anatomy and reflux burden and will stimulate further discussion on how best to integrate advanced physiologic testing into bariatric surgery pathways.
