Genomic characterization and application on food samples of bacteriophage ETEC-S3 to control enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli
Authors: Jessica Maria Kurniani Widjaja, Diana Elizabeth Waturangi, Adi Yulandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) contamination remains a major global concern, including in Indonesia, where efforts to develop an ETEC vaccine to prevent foodborne diseases are currently underway. This study aimed to perform genomic characterization of bacteriophage φETEC-S3 and evaluate the efficacy of its application in food samples targeting Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli. The phage exhibited a high lytic activity, reaching a titer of 3.8 ± 0.50 × 1010 PFU/mL and remained stable at both 4 °C and 37 °C based on the results of the thermal stability assays. Whole-genome bioinformatics analysis identified φETEC-S3 as a virulent double-stranded DNA phage belonging to the genus Phapecoctavirus within the class Caudoviricites. This genome contained structural and lytic genes, including those encoding the major capsid protein, tail fiber, and Rz-like spanin. Application studies demonstrated that phage φETEC-S3 significantly reduced the enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli population in all food samples from the initial day until the seventh day of incubation. The highest reduction percentages were for φETEC-S3 in soy milk on the initial day (93%) and in mashed potatoes on the seventh day (94%). Thus, φ ETEC-S3 is shown to be a promising candidate for a biocontrol agent against bacteria responsible for food spoilage. Introduction

Foodborne pathogens have been a long-standing issue for human health; however, their impacts have become more visible in recent decades due to improved reporting. These agents, represented by bacteria, viruses, parasites, and toxins, are responsible for numerous food spoilage and foodborne diseases globally. Among these pathogens, Escherichia coli is frequently associated with contaminated food, with Enterotoxigenic Escherichia coli (ETEC) being a well-recognized cause of diarrheal disease in humans and transmitted primarily through contaminated food or water. In 2020, Indonesia reported a 4.55% mortality rate from diarrhea among toddlers aged 12–59 months, prompting ongoing research on vaccine against ETEC to prevent foodborne disease [1]. ETEC produces heat-labile (LT) and/or heat-stable (ST) enterotoxins, which disrupt intestinal fluid regulation and lead to acute watery diarrhea [2].

Diarrheal diseases account for hundreds of millions of cases annually worldwide, with ETEC responsible for a substantial proportion, particularly in regions with inadequate sanitation. Conventional ETEC control in food systems relies on hygienic processing, thermal treatments, and chemical preservatives; however, these methods may negatively affect food quality or prove insufficient against persistent contamination. Moreover, the increasing prevalence of antimicrobial-resistant E.coli strains has further limited the effectiveness of antibiotic-based interventions and underscores the urgent need for alternative biocontrol strategies.

Bacteriophages have garnered interest as an alternative approach to fighting foodborne pathogens. These agents are “precision” antibacterials that can selectively eliminate their bacterial target without affecting the commensal microflora [3]. They are present in nearly all locations where living bacteria thrive, such as soil, providing opportunities for isolation for therapeutic uses [4, 5]. Importantly, several phage-based products have already been approved for use in food systems. Commercial preparations such as Listex™ P100 (targeting Listeria monocytogenes), SalmoFresh™ (targeting Salmonella spp.), and EcoShield™ (targeting E. coli O157:H7) have received regulatory approval in the United States and other countries, demonstrating the feasibility and safety of phage application in food biocontrol.

Although bacteriophages targeting ETEC have been reported, data regarding their genomic characterization and evaluation for food-related applications are still limited. Genomic characterization of candidate phages is essential to assess their lytic potential, host specificity, and safety application in food environments. Based on these gaps, this study aimed to conduct genomic characterization of bacteriophage φETEC-S3 and assess its effectiveness against ETEC in food-sample applications.

Methods

Bacteriophage purification and enrichment

The host bacteria used in this study was Escherichia coli, specifically ETEC US Namru-1, while the bacteriophage was φETEC-S3; both were were obtained from earlier research [4]. ETEC was cultured in LB broth medium and incubated overnight using a water bath shaker at 120 rpm and 37 °C. Next, 150 µL of phage lysate and 300 µL of its host bacteria (OD600 = 0,132) were inoculated into new LB broth media. Following overnight incubation at 120 rpm and 37 °C in a water bath shaker, the suspension was centrifuged at 6,000 x g for 15 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm microfilter, and the purified bacteriophage stock was preserved at 4 °C by Ringer Solution (OXOID) in a 1:1 v/v ratio [6].

Bacteriophage titer determination

The double-layer agar method was used to determine the titer of bacteriophage φETEC-S3. The host bacteria were prepared and diluted to an OD600 of 0.132 after overnight incubation. The isolated bacteriophage was diluted tenfold in triplicate using SM buffer, supplemented with 100 µL 10 mM CaCl2, 100 µL 10 mM MgSO4, and 100 µL host bacteria. MgSO₄ and CaCl₂ were added to the buffer because the intrinsic nature of divalent cations such as Mg²⁺ and Ca²⁺ allows them to stabilize phage particles, protect them from inactivation, and facilitate adsorption and infection of host cells; therefore, they are commonly included in phage media such as SM buffer to enhance phage activity [7]. The mixture was plated onto 2% LB agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Plates containing 30–300 plaques were selected, and plaques were counted to calculate the plaque-forming units per milliliter (PFU/mL) based on the dilution factor [4].

Thermal stability assay

For the thermal stability test, bacteriophage lysate was incubated at various temperatures (4, 37, 60, and 75 °C) for an hour. The titer was then determined using the double-layer agar method. Phage φ ETEC-S3 was diluted eightfold in triplicate using SM buffer, mixed with 4 mL of soft LB agar containing 100 µL of the incubated bacteriophage dilution, host bacteria, 10 mM CaCl2, and 10 mM MgSO4. The mixture was then plated onto a thin layer of 2% LA agar and incubated overnight at 37 °C [8].

Bacteriophage DNA extraction and NGS

Bacteriophage DNA was extracted and purified by following the protocol of the Wizard® Genomic DNA Purification Kit from Promega. The isolated phage DNA was analyzed and whole-genome sequenced by Genetika Science for NGS using an Illumina NextSeq 2000 platform, generating 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were uploaded and analyzed using UseGalaxy (https://usegalaxy.org.au) between July and August 2025. Quality assessment of raw reads was conducted using FastQC v0.74. Genome assembly was performed using Shovill v1.1.0, which internally uses SPAdes v4.2.0 as the assembler. Assembly polishing was carried out using Pilon v1.20.1 to improve consensus accuracy. The quality of the assembled genome was subsequently evaluated using QUAST v5.3.0 to assess assembly metrics, including contig number, N50 value, and genome length.

Bacteriophage annotation, antibiotic resistance gene, life cycle determination, and tail fiber protein analysis

PhageScope [9] and UniprotKB 2025_3 (accessed August 2025) [10] were used to annotate the bacteriophage, as the outputs of these two platforms complement one another. UniprotKB was also used for analysis of tail fiber protein. Additionally, PhageAI generated the phylogenetic tree used for preliminary classification and identification of closely related phages. Selected reference genomes were aligned, and the phylogenetic tree was constructed based on whole-genome sequences. The presence of antibiotic-resistance genes within the bacteriophage genome was identified using the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD) v4.0.1 (accessed August 2025) [11]. Similarly, PhageScope and PhageAI v1.0.0 (accessed September 2025) [12], which are designed for the classification of phage taxonomy and lifecycle, were used for further characterization.

Bacteriophages application on food samples

Pieces of fish fillet, beef, pork, and cabbage were prepared at1 cm x 1 cm. Mashed potatoes and minced chicken were measured at 1 g, while up to 1 mL UHT milk and UHT soymilk were used. The samples were sterilized in an autoclave for 15 min at 121 °C, except for cabbage, which was sterilized by rinsing it with clean water, swabbing with 96% alcohol swab, and exposing to UV light in a laminar airflow cabinet for approximately 45 min.

After sterilization, 100 µL of mid-log phase bacterial host strain suspensions were added to each sample and incubated for 15 min. Subsequently, 100 µL isolated bacteriophage at an MOI (Multiplicity of Infection) of 0.001 following Artawinata et al. [4] was added to each sample. All samples were incubated at 28 °C for seven days. ETEC counts were assessed after one day (D1) and seven days (D7) of incubation at 28 °C.

After incubation, 9 mL SM buffer was added to every sample and vortexed. Each sample was then subjected to eightfold serial dilutions in triplicate, and then spread onto an LB agar plate and incubated overnight at 37 °C. For positive control, only the host strain was inoculated. For the negative control, only the isolated bacteriophage lysate was inoculated.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to compare continuous variables. Tukey’s B test and paired samples T-test were then used to compare the differences between control and treatment groups and between day 1 and day 7 groups. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05.

Results

Titer determination and thermal stability assay

The bacteriophage titer of φETEC-S3 (GenBank accession number PZ027503) was 3.8 ± 0.50 × 1010 PFU/mL. Visible clear zone forms (plaques) were observed in this study (Supplementary Fig. 1). In the thermal stability assay, four temperature treatments were applied: 4 °C, 37 °C, 60 °C, and 75 °C. Figure 1 shows no significant differences between 4 °C and 37 °C, suggesting that φETEC-S3 can be stored at these temperatures because of their high titers. In contrast, at 60 °C and 75 °C, a significant difference was observed compared to other temperatures due to their low titers. Differences in titer determination and thermal stability assay at 4 °C were also observed.

Bacteriophage NGS, annotation, antibiotic resistance gene, life cycle determination, and tail fiber protein analysis

The φETEC-S3 sequence was 150,931 bp in length and had a GC content of 39.01%. The assembled genome annotated by PhageScope and UniprotKB was associated with infection, host recognition, replication, assembly, bacteriophage structure, and DNA packaging (Supplementary Tables 1 and 2). Genome annotation of bacteriophage φETEC-S3 revealed genes encoding structural, replication, and lysis-related proteins typical of lytic Escherichia phages. Several predicted proteins, including DNA polymerase, primase/helicase, ribonucleotide reductase, and terminase large subunit, showed high sequence identity (≥99.7–100%) to homologs from other Escherichia phages. Structural components associated with host recognition and virion assembly were detected, including tail fiber, tail sheath, tape measure protein, baseplate J-like domain-containing protein, portal protein, and major capsid protein E. In addition, a putative colanic acid-degrading protein was identified. Genes involved in host cell lysis were also present, including N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidase, Rz-like spanin, holin-like proteins, and additional peptidases and hydrolases. No genes associated with lysogeny or known virulence factors were detected.

No antibiotic resistance genes were detected in the φETEC-S3 genome after CARD analysis. PhageScope was used to determine the predicted taxonomy and life cycle status of the bacteriophage, as shown in Supplementary Tables 1 and 2. Also, PhageScope can generate a graphical representation of predicted open reading frames and functional annotations across the phage genome (Supplementary Fig. 2). In the result, φETEC-S3 was predicted to be a virulent bacteriophage, classified within the class Caudoviricites and genus Phapecoctavirus. Based on similarity predictions from PhageAI, closely related reference phages were selected. A phylogenetic tree constructed from whole-genome alignment showed that phage φETEC-S3 clustered closely with Escherichia phage vB_EcoM_3A1_SA_NWU and Escherichia phage ECO340P1.

The bacteriophage’s ability to eliminate bacteria in food samples is shown in Table 1. Statistical analysis indicated that in all food matrices, φETEC-S3 significantly reduced pathogen counts (p < 0.05) compared with the control. Following artificial contamination with phage, the highest percentage bacterial reductions in φETEC-S3 phage were observed in soy milk on Day 1 and mashed potatoes on Day 7, with values of 93% and 94%, respectively. Cabbage had the lowest percentage of 26.5% as listed in the table. At 28 °C, φETEC-S3 was able to significantly reduce the population of its host bacteria, although the magnitude of reduction varied, which might be influence by the food sample matrices.

Discussions

A high titer value is crucial for effective biocontrol of foodborne pathogens, as it increases the phage effectiveness in eliminating the targeted bacteria [8, 13]. In this study, φETEC-S3 was propagated to a titer of 1010 PFU/mL, which falls within the range reported for lytic phages intended for applications. Luzon-Hidalgo et al. [14] classified titers of 106 – 109 PFU/mL as high. Ács et al. [15] also reported that sample concentrations between 107 and 109 PFU/mL are high, showing that φETEC-S3 has higher efficacy when compared to the previously mentioned standards.

In the thermal stability assay, φETEC-S3 remained stable at 4 °C and 37 °C for 1 h, whereas a decline in phage viability was observed at 60 °C and 75 °C. This is because low temperatures halt host bacterial metabolism, allowing free phage particles to remain stable during long-term storage [16]. At 37 °C, the host bacterial conditions are favorable; therefore, when bacterial growth occurs and sufficient phage are present, replication can take place upon phage–host contact. At 60 °C and 75 °C, the capsid proteins of phage begin to denature, leading to the loss of tail structure and receptor-binding proteins, ultimately abolishing infectivity [17]. However, the observed reduction in titer determination and thermal stability assay at 4 °C reflects residual infectivity after incubation and handling rather than instability of the stock lysate. Transfer to test containers and sampling at certain time intervals can introduce variations, which may happen even at low temperatures and are not captured during immediate stock titration.

The NGS analysis of φETEC-S3 showed a GC-content of 39.01% and a total length of 150,931 bp. Annotation results from UniprotKB and PhageScope identified up to 284 protein-coding genes based on sequence similarity searches; however, no genes associated with the lysogenic cycle, such as integrase and excisionase, were detected. φETEC-S3 was classified within class Caudoviricites and genus Phapecoctavirus based on genome-based taxonomic prediction, consistent with the presence of icosahedral capsids and contractile or non-contractile tails that enable effective adsorption and DNA injection into bacterial hosts [18].

Phylogenetic analysis on Fig. 2 placed bacteriophage φETEC-S3 on a distinct branch separate from the clade containing Escherichia phage vB_EcoM_3A1_SA_NWU and Escherichia phage ECO340P, indicating that it does not belong to the same immediate phylogenetic lineage. Nevertheless, φETEC-S3 clustered within a broader group of lytic Escherichia phages and shared conserved genomic features, suggesting functional congruence despite phylogenetic separation. It should be emphasized that the phylogenetic study described here is limited by the use of a single tree and lacks bootstrap support, reducing confidence in branching order and evolutionary relationships. Future studies incorporating different phylogenetic approaches, bootstrap validation, and larger genomic datasets will be required to refine φETEC-S3’s taxonomic position.

Genome annotation using UniProtKB and PhageScope identified genes associated with phage replication, morphogenesis, and host cell lysis. Most of these genes, including those encoding DNA polymerase, terminase large subunit, major capsid protein, and portal protein, are conserved among tailed bacteriophages and are consistent with the typical genomic organization of lytic E.coli phages. Particular attention was given to genes involved in host recognition and adsorption, as these determine phage host range and specificity. The genome of bacteriophage φETEC-S3 encodes baseplate-associated proteins that are known to mediate binding to specific bacterial surface receptors [19]. Comparative sequence analysis revealed that the predicted tail fiber protein of φETEC-S3 shares similarity with tail fiber proteins from other E.coli infecting phages (Supplementary Table 1), suggesting a conserved mechanism of host attachment [20]. However, no unique domains or clearly divergent receptor-binding motifs were identified that would distinguish φETEC-S3 from previously described ETEC or E. coli phages. In addition, a putative colanic acid-degrading protein was identified, which may facilitate penetration of the bacterial capsule and enhance adsorption efficiency [21]. Similar proteins have been reported in other E.coli phages and are associated with infection of encapsulated strains. The host range determination for φETEC-S3 was previously conducted by Artawinata et al. [4].

The identification of tRNA genes suggests a potential role in optimizing translation efficiency during infection. Phage-encoded tRNAs identified in the φETEC-S3 genome may function to supplement the host’s translational machinery by compensating for differences in codon usage or supporting translation under conditions of high phage protein demand [22]. In the present study, no clear genomic features distinguishing φETEC-S3 from other related phages were identified.

In the results, important protein genes for bacteriophage lytic activity were also identified, including N-acetylmuramoyl-L-alanine amidase, which functions as an endolysin [23]; Rz-like spanins which mediate fusion between the inner and outer membranes in Gram-negative bacteria [24]; hol-like chemotaxis likely encodes proteins that help the phage by influencing the host bacteria’s chemotaxis mechanism [25]and anaerobic ribonucleoside-triphosphate reductase that is involved in converting ribonucleotides into deoxyribonucleotides in the absence of oxygen [26].

The efficacy of bacteriophage treatment is also influenced by MOI, which determines the initial ratio between phage particles and bacterial cells. A previous study by Artawinata et al. have demonstrated that an MOI of as low as 0.001 can still be effective for bacterial reduction, supporting the use of this value in the present study [4]. Bacteriophages have a higher “mobility” in the presence of moisture; therefore, phage particles are more effective at reducing bacterial contamination on moist food surfaces and in liquid matrices than on dry food matrices. Bacteriophage and bacteria typically make their first encounter by diffusion and Brownian motion [4, 27].

The higher reduction observed in food matrices such as soy milk and mashed potatoes may be influenced by the physicochemical properties of the food environment that affect phage-host interactions. The other factors, such as nutrient availability, viscosity, and surface accessibility, can modulate bacterial growth and phage diffusion, thereby impacting infection efficiency [28]. Semi-liquid or homogenized matrices such as soy milk may facilitate closer contact between phages and bacterial cells compared to more solid or structured foods, enhancing adsorption and subsequent lytic activity.

After one day of treatment, cabbage showed a reduction of 0.13 log CFU/g (10⁻⁵ dilution), which is indeed the lowest reduction log. Although statistically significant compared to the untreated control, this decrease is considered microbiologically limited. In food microbiology, antimicrobial interventions typically require reductions of at least 1–3 log CFU to be regarded as microbiologically meaningful, as stated by the FDA [29], and it can be seen that soy milk on Day 1 and mashed potatoes on Day 7 have a value of 1. Therefore, the observed decrease indicates a modest inhibitory effect of phage φETEC-S3 rather than a substantial microbiological reduction.

Table 1 shows significant differences between treatments on Days 1 and 7, although not across all food matrices. Significant differences were observed in beef, minced chicken (percentage reduction decreased), and cabbage (percentage reduction increased). These effects on beef and minced chicken could be explained by a deficiency of free water in the food matrices after a few days, and due to uneven surface conditions [30]. In contrast, the increase observed in cabbage may be due to the structural breakdown caused by longer storage time and higher humidity levels, which enhances the interaction between bacteria and phages.

Conclusions

This study provides a genomic and preliminary functional framework for bacteriophage φETEC-S3 as a potential biocontrol agent against ETEC in food systems. The absence of lysogeny and antibiotic resistance-associated genes, together with stability under relevant storage temperatures, supports its suitability for further development. Future works should focus on expanding host range testing across diverse ETEC strains, evaluating efficacy at different phage titers, and assessing performance under industrial food-processing and storage conditions to enable its application under real food conditions.

Limitations

Characterization was only done for temperature stability, further characterization related to environmental pressure needs to be observed. The phage also need to be continued for further host range assays with more pathogenic E. coli strains to tested its capacity.
