Reexamining gender inequality in accessing resources and education stance in reducing masculine gender hegemony in Nigeria
Authors: Olajumoke O Alabi, Mmaduabuchukwu C Mkpado, Abiodun O Ajala, Chika A Ifejirika, Arinze Ngwube, Ken Amaechi Egbo, Sunday C Aniobi, Ndidiamaka S Mkpado, Chika Mmaduakor, Tanko P Sani, Sunday I Ogunjimi, Olayiwola Fasoranti, Saifullahi Idris
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe role of education in abolishing cultural practices that encourage gender inequality needs consistent evaluation for inclusive, responsive policy development and implementation in a dynamic society. Eliminating masculine gender hegemony may result in attainment of international commitments like gender equality and poverty eradication. Reexamining the role of education in reducing gender inequality in patriarchal societies can highlight critical issues needing policy actions for attainment of sustainable development goals. The objectives of the study were to examine effects of education on marital and family life, income and access to land resources by male and female, as well as the effects of education on girl child education. The hypotheses that guided the study were: parents' education attainment increases girl child education opportunity; education attainment reduces male-female income gap and education attainment increases women access to land. The study was conducted in Southwest Nigeria. Multi-stage sampling procedure was used to collect primary data from 720 households using a well-structured questionnaire. Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, Chi-square and Pearson Correlation tests. Results showed significant disparity among men, women, girls and boys education attainments. Significantly, male have more access to land and other resources than female. The work discussed implications of the existing gender disparity despite efforts to bridge the gap and noted effects of education in reducing the gaps and ensuring gender equality. The paper is optimistic that terminating gender inequality as education policy can significantly improve girl child education, reduce girls' marriages, improve marital life, increase females’ income and access to information as well as land resources.Keywords: Family life, Certificates, Income, Land access, Information, Equality

Introduction

Masculine gender dominance globally, all over Africa and Nigeria in particular is attributable to the patriarchy and its associated patrilineal inheritance structure. These had placed a huge premium on male children as heirs, lineage successors and custodians. Major traditional craftsmanship and occupation for survival are often linked to the masculine gender. Consider words like blacksmith, goldsmith, tailor, hunter, huntsman, fishermen, merchant and middle men just to mention a few. This trend dominated establishments and development of educational institutions in Nigeria such that gender disparity was evident in Nigeria, even education system for decades. For instance, prior to 1920, religious organizations mainly owned and managed primary and secondary schools in Nigeria. Only about 12 % of secondary schools were established for girls. This represented 3 out of 25 secondary schools at that time [1,2]. It was noted that by 1949, the proportion of girls' secondary schools had increased to 14 percent of the number of boys' secondary schools. This was because the number of girls' secondary schools had increased to 8; while the number of boys' secondary schools increased to 57 [2]. The years 1950–1960 witnessed the establishment of more secondary schools. During that decade, there were 14 girls' secondary schools, 10 mixed sex schools and 61 boys’ schools [3].

The emergence of mixed sex schools may have helped to gradually shift emphasis from gender based educational institutions to enrollment and completion rates of boys and girls. For instance, statistics form [4] showed that girls appear to compete better with boys in Junior Secondary Schools (JSS) than Senior Secondary Schools (SSS). It was because girls' enrolment rates in JSS in Nigeria were 48.31 % in 2018, 50.07 % in 2019 and 40.80 % in 2020 while statistics for boys were 51.69 %, 49.93 % and 50.20 % for the same periods respectively. The completion rates for girls in JSS were 51.55 % and 49.78 % in 2018 and 2019 respectively and for the boys, it were 48.45 % and 50.22 % for the same periods respectively. On the other hand, girls' enrolments SSS in 2018 and 2019 were 47.13 % and 47.41 % while boys’ enrolments were 52.87 % and 52.59 % respectively for the same periods.

It appears that girls do not have more educational opportunities or parents tend to give preference to the boys schooling. Expensive training and education of the girl child were often viewed as investments in another man's family due to the fact that the girl will most likely get married to another family either before she was a spinster or afterwards. Girls' education suffered with its attendant social ills that further increased disdain on feminine gender. A significant push for children education irrespective of gender globally and in developing countries like Nigeria was in May 1961; when the Universal Declaration of Human Rights by United Nations and United Nations Education, Science and Cultural Organization's educational agenda was made. This plan targeted achieving 100 % universal primary education in member countries which include Nigeria by the year 1980 [5]. The compulsory primary education policy was aimed at helping to remove gender bias in education of boys and girls. Also, many of the educated females have proved to be illustrious daughters and wives to their parents' families and husband homes respectively.

Despite the advances made by United Nations' programmes and educational institutions, patriarchal societies in African countries like Nigeria are still faced with masculine gender dominance in most aspects of life. This has perpetuated a dominant trend of males’ superiority over females [6]; and possible dependence of females on males. It is because access to crucial means of livelihood and assets are largely determined by patrilineal inheritance structure which tends to maintain that property and titles are passed from generation to generation through the male offspring [7]. Thus, women access to land in Nigeria had largely been through marriage as they can share land with their husbands [8,9]. Males have dominated the commercial and education system as they had more access to and control of resources such as credits, livestock, land, education and even extension services [10] as well as preference in choosing a mate and also dominated the decision-making organs, institutions and processes.

It has been noted that females were poorer when compared with their male counterparts in terms of income or wealth indicators [11], and per capita consumption expenditure [12]. There was disparity in income between men and women as income of widows and unmarried women were less than those of men [13]. This is against the theory of gender equality which proposes that men and women as well as boys and girls enjoy the same protections, opportunities, resources and rights without discrimination [14]. Thus, the attainment of a number of international commitments may depend on reducing masculine gender hegemony especially in patriarchal societies.

Statement of research Problem

Africa and Nigeria in particular did not attain the Millennium Development Goals (MDGs) in poverty reduction. This may largely be due to limited access to resources by the women as they consist of a very important part of the population. For instance, in Nigeria which is the most populous country in Africa, females represent about half (49.99 %) of the population [15], and females constitute 50.14 % of the African population [16]. It has been noted that in Tanzania materially poor women had very limited access to land and received very poor wages [17] and women involvement in productive activities like agriculture are limited by their access to land [18]. Fear exists because of persistence gender inequality. Can gender inequality mar the attainment of the SDGs especially on gender equality, zero hunger and poverty eradication? Similar question can be posed with respect to the Africa we want 2063 initiative. Documenting the outcomes of education on gender inequality indicators can help shape policy and projects for the desired goals.

Education has been reported to help women gain employment and income as salaries [19,20]. Has education also helped women gain title to land? Africa and Nigeria especially are rich in land resources. For instance, in 2010; 70 % of world uncultivated land existed in Africa [21]. Limiting any gender the right to these resources could mean reducing the persons’ welfare because access to land had been reported to increase income and food security [22].

Education has been a major tool of the United Nations in advocating for gender equality. A number of efforts have been made by United Nations and women to promote gender equality. These include international women conferences in Mexico, Copenhagen and Beijing China in 1975, 1980 and 1991 respectively. The African women under the umbrella ‘Kilimanjaro initiative’ have been advocating implementation of gender equality in Africa. They can quote their chatter and the African Union Protocol/African Charter on Human and Peoples' Rights and the Rights of women in Africa which was adopted in 2003 as their strength. Are many women aware of legal documents supporting gender equality? Information is power, the awareness of such chatter and African Union protocol can give impetus to the individual struggle for gender parity in Nigeria.

Women education and access to resources had been reported to help household food security. For instance, a positive relationship between women access to resources and child welfare was reported by Ref. [23]. Also [24], noted that women's production and contribution to development are hampered by limited education attainment and inequality with men in access and control of resources. Positive effects of education on prevention of premarital sex among adolescents has been reported [25]. Have these reports on positive impact of education helped to change the disposition toward girls' education? The education as means of instructing and training people to understand negative trends in life, overcome bottle necks to development initiatives has become forces for social justice and gender equality. For instance, Mary Slessor through educational programmes fought the ugly practice of killing twins in many parts of Africa especially in Nigeria and eventually it was abolished many years after her death [26]. Examining the effects of education on the pathway of gender equality can help inform relevant stakeholders to stay on the pathway of progress till the whole success is achieved.

Women appear to do less labour-intensive work in agriculture and other industries [22]. This has contributed to keeping their income lower than that of their men counterparts. In fact, a number of researchers have calculated women wages as a fraction of men wages. It was reported that women wages were lower than that of men by 51 %, 45 %, and 23 % in Nigeria, Mozambique and Burkina Faso respectively [27]. Similarly, a wage difference for male and female workers on agricultural enterprises in Tanzania was reported by Ref. [17]. The good news is that human drudgery in agriculture and industries are been speedily replaced with artificial intelligence and mechanization [28]. Improving the capacity of women to earn money with modern technology is important as artificial intelligence and mechanization are used to handle labour intensive aspects in industries thus removing the drudgery from human. Development often includes transition of communities and industrial process from use of crude implements to machineries. Education of female in this technical line is needed. Feminine gender digital skills were reported to be low when compared with males in low- and middle-income countries [29]. Recently, a ratio of about 13:22 for females' and males' usage of computer was reported for Nigeria (15). A good performance in higher technical education requires robust secondary education which in turn requires a good primary education. It will be informative to examine the dynamics of females’ education and their preparedness to attain gender equality in education. The feminine gender continued relevance in development processes and outcomes will depend largely on the quality and quantity of education possessed. This will enable females to serve certain niches in economic and industrial world with the advancement in technology.

The paper complements existing literature because it assesses the broader economic and social returns of education beyond income or child education such as marital and family life and implications on females' higher/technical education which has received less attention. It touches on how education can affect family life which has implications for reducing gender-based violence. It gives insight on how education can affect daughters' access to certain parents and community resources. It deepens understanding of a developing economy's experience in the role of education over decades in overcoming certain gender-based prejudices based on traditions. It also gives insight on how education is limiting traditional practices that tend to accelerate gender inequality that may mar efforts made for attainment of SGDs in a patriarchal society.

Research objectives

The general objective of the study was to examine the effects of education on men and women family life experiences and access to resources. Specifically, the study examined along gender line, the effects of education on: (i) income, (ii) family life and marital status, and (iii) girl child education. Also, (iv) effects of education on females’ access to resources like information, parents and community land properties.

Hypotheses: the null hypotheses tested were

There is no significant relationship between parents' educational attainment and girl-child improved educational opportunities.

There is no significant relationship between education attainment and reduction in male/female income gap.

There is no significant relationship between parents' educational attainment and willingness to share property with their daughters.

There is no significant relationship between females' educational attainment and access to land properties.

Methodology

The Study Area and Population: The study was conducted in Southwest Nigeria. The Southwest Nigeria is one of the six geo-political zones of the country. It consists of 6 states and made up of people with different educational and social backgrounds. The population of the study area is 34,739,663 [30].

Sampling procedure

Primary data were collected through structured questionnaire. Multi-stage sampling procedure was used. First was a purposive selection of three out of the 6 states in the geopolitical zone, based on their accessibility to the researchers. Second, was a random selection of 5 percent of Local Government Areas (LGAs) from each state afore selected based on budget constraints of the researchers. This gave 12 LGAs, and third was a random selection of three communities from each of the selected LGAs while the fourth procedure aimed at gender balance by stratified random selection of ten men and ten women, (12∗3∗20 = 720).

Data collection

The questionnaire was developed by the researchers. The validation of research instrument was done by conducting a pretest with 50 respondents in a village called ‘Itaji’ in Oye-Ekiti Local government Area on the 3rd of June 2023. The data collected were subjected to reliability test using Cronbach's Alpha. The coefficients for almost all the categories were acceptable as most were above 0.76. Lower Alpha score (0.65) was obtained in a section that combined actions parents can take to protect their daughters' property and possible obligations parents expect from daughters. When the responses for the items in this section were separated into different sections and reanalysed the score was 0.82 which is acceptable. Thus, the questionnaire was considered satisfactory for the survey.

The researchers used the validated instrument to train research assistants selected from each of the community to be surveyed; the research assistants also served as local guides. A combination of questionnaire administration and interview schedules where applicable with interpretation of the questionnaire into local language were used in data collection. This helped to accommodate those who have little or nor formal education. A total of 36 research assistants/local guides worked with the researchers for the data collection. They received a token to cover transportation and other incidentals but the respondents were not paid. The researchers and their assistants received overwhelming acceptance apparently not only because the local people were involved in the data collection but the theme of the research involved gender and access to resources which has become an intriguing issue in Nigeria.

Measurement of variables

Age was measured in years.

Sex was measured with nominal scale as female, 0 and male, 1.

Gender was measured with nominal scale for the role in society as a boy 1 or girl 0; man 1 or woman 0.

Marital status was measured with a nominal scale as: Yet to marry 0, Married 1, Separated 2, Divorced 3 and Widowhood 4.

Educational qualification was measurable using a nominal scale rated as follows: possession of no primary school certificate scored 0; Primary school completed/certificate scored 1; Secondary school completed scored 2; OND/NCE certificate scored 3; HND/BSC or its equivalents scored 4; Postgraduate certificate scored 5 and any other qualification scored 7. Also, educational qualification was measurable using number of years spent in formal education.

Knowledge of feminine land request measured with 4 points ordered scale as: I have not heard it 0; I have heard it 1; I have seen it 3; I have experienced it 4.

Income of respondents was measured in Naira while size of land was measured in hectares.

The measurement of variables helped in determining the types of analytical tools employed for the objectives and hypotheses. For instance, when variables like income and educational qualifications (years of schooling) were measured in continuous numerical values, Pearson Correlation and t-test were used; but when variables were measured in nominal or ordered scale, Chi-Square test was used. Thus, appropriate variable measurement was used for each analytical tool.

Method of data analysis

Descriptive statistics and t-test were useful in presentation of results while Pearson Chi-square and Correlation tests were used in achieving the objectives and testing of hypotheses. Objective (i) involved the use of both tests, but objectives (ii), (iii) and (iv) involved the use of Chi-square test. Similarly, hypothesis, Ho1 involved the use of both tests while hypothesis Ho2 involved the use of correlation analysis, and hypotheses Ho3 and Ho4 involved the use Chi-Square test. Data were analyzed using Microsoft Excel and International Business Machine Statistical Package for Social Sciences (IBM SPSS).

Ethical considerations

The research was conducted in line with the declaration of Helsinki for experiments involving humans because appropriate ethics were adopted. The researchers and their assistants had appropriate scientific education and training for the research. Stratified random selection of respondents was adapted to ensure equal representation of male and female.

The consents of participants were obtained; no one was coarse to get involved or give a response. The questionnaire used for the study had an introductory section which explained the aim of the research and how the data collected would be handled anonymously. The research team members endeavoured to explain these procedures used to conduct the research that met the University ethical approval and the researchers adhered to these procedures.

Theoretical Framework

The theories of radical feminism and gender equality were pertinent to this work as presented.

The theory of radical feminism

This theory opposes patriarchal system not just men [2,31]. Radical feminists are of the view that patriarchy tend to oppress females depriving them of the opportunity to have equal right to resources, training, decision making and opportunities [32]. Radical feminists use cultural change, education and re-socialization to pursue gender equality. The feminists believe that patriarchal structure is made up of norms, rules and institutions that give undue advantages to males [33]. The radical feminists are of the view that uprooting these structures will help females enjoy equality with males. Formal education system is one of the main tools of radical feminists. Formal education promotes a culture of gender parity [34]. It gives the same training and opportunities to individuals irrespective of their gender.

Gender equality theory

Proposes fairness, parity and non-biasness in dealing with people irrespective of their gender. Gender equality also deals with social justice in accessing resources, education, child care and protection [2,35]. The applications of gender equality theory in ‘collective ownership, production and consumption’ among others was outlined by Ref. [36]. The work noted that “equal control over resources'’ is the hallmark of gender equality in Israeli kibbutz. The building of individuals' capacities to function shoulder to shoulder irrespective of their gender is one of the main aims of modern education. Gender equality in line with social justice also, advocates for removal of all segregation by gender in functions of the society. This theory has been the major basis for examination of gender dominance in carrier. Hence [33] was able to examine women's representation and legislative committee appointments. Literature has reported similar work in education [34], health care [37] and security outfits among others, [38].

These theories help us to understand the masculine gender hegemony and efforts females are dissipating to reduce the inequality. They help to show the value of education as an important tool in attaining gender equality. Most of the educated households and societies do not adhere to cultural barriers that limit females’ opportunities and potentials. The women are utilizing every avenue in gradual liberation process and the quest for gender equality requires all hands of relevant stakeholders on the desk.

Limitations of the study

The study as a cross-section survey does not establish direct causality. However, it identifies associations between the variables which are necessary in understanding current trend when compared with older literature involving cross section data as well.

Results of marital status shows that out of the 8.5 % of the respondents that did not have access to basic education, the largest proportion (5.1 %) of them belong to the married, followed by the widowed with 2.9 %, while yet-to-marry, separated and divorced had 0.14 % each. Among 24.6 % that had OND/NCE, married respondents again had the highest percentage which was 21.9 % and was distantly followed by widowed accounting for 1.1 % while 0.7 % were yet-to-marry, also 0.7 % were separated and 0.1 % were divorced. It means that marital status differs with respect to education qualifications. In this instance, married individuals that possess at least secondary school certificate accounted for over 50 % of the respondents. It is possible that married respondents supported each other to access formal education and educated individuals often tend to marry fellow educated persons. Thus, educated female can attract prospective suitors more than others that were not educated. This data gives credence to the role of education in marriage which is important in human empowerment and development.

The highest percentage (50.7 %) of females' children that did not complete secondary school was in households where parents did not attend primary school. Thus, it can be inferred that parents who did not attend primary school do not value education. Out of the 50.7 %, 26.1 % were from households where women were interviewed while 24.6 % were from households where men were interviewed. An indication of men's push for girls' education can be seen from the fact that households where men were interviewed had less proportion of girls that did not complete secondary school.

A further breakdown shows that 28.1 % of female children that did not complete secondary school were in household where parents completed primary school. Out of this 28.1 %, 14.3 % were from households where men were interviewed while 13.8 % were from households where women were interviewed. The 12.5 % of female children that did not complete secondary school occurred in households where parents completed secondary school. Out of this 12.5 %, 6.7 % were in households where men were interviewed while 5.8 % were in households where women were interviewed.

On the contrary, 6.1 % of female that did not complete secondary school were in households where parents possessed OND/NCE; out of the 6.1 %, 3.2 % were from households where women were interviewed while 2.9 % were from households where men were interviewed. This is in line with the findings in Table 2, where it was inferred that priority for boys’ education may have resulted in poor educational attainment of girls leading to poor reward for middle manpower which may be responsible for domination of OND/NCE by the female gender.

Implications of findings

Parents' education attainment increases boy and girl child's education opportunities: The percentage of children that did not complete secondary school comprised of 36.34 % male and 38.89 % female. It was noted that increasing years parents schooled reduces the number of children in households (birth rate) and number that did not complete secondary school. National data shows that 36 % and 27 % of female and male respectively have no formal education in Nigeria. The percentage of males of about 6 years in urban areas with no formal education was 13 % compared to 37 % in rural areas, while the percentage of female with no education in urban and rural areas was 20 % and 49 %, respectively [47]. It can be instructive to state that the analysis of percentage of school dropouts was statistically significant enough to give the government serious concern. The dropouts can invariably represent uninformed army of youths that can pose as threat to national security; due to social ills associated with dropouts or untrained individuals. It is possible that these school dropouts can constitute households that would be known for gender discrimination. All of these can have negative implications for attainment of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). A good antidote to this is education.

Education encourages better family life and ensures prospects for gender equality: The fact that majority (81.4 %) was married can be an indication for companionship and prospects of enjoying the values and privileges as well as shouldering the responsibilities that are associated with marriage. Majority of married respondents were more educated than their counterparts who were less educated. According to Ref. [48], there are a number of legal, financial and social benefits of marriage. Also [49], concluded that marriage confers more protective benefits through pooled income than other marital statuses. Similarly [50,58], aptly reported that marriage helped women cope in times of crisis and educated females as well as married women and females possessing secured land tenure coped better in times of economic crisis.

Family life as a couple is best for training responsible gender balanced individuals. A number of traits form social, economic, financial and health risk that can cause death of a child are more pronounced in single parents’ families than households where both parents are striving shoulder to shoulder [[51], [52], [53]]. Heavy financial burden on men may lead to sudden death of men or other serious health risks can jeopardize the family future. It is because females often had little or no access to economic resources such as land to support their families thereby leaving males with the job of shouldering the responsibilities of the family. Gender equality in resource allocation can be a good antidote for this type of risk.

Education can help reduce early child marriage or child brides: Early child marriage was rampant as this facilitated woman assuming very early in life child bearing functions with attendant health issues. Early girl marriage can deny females the opportunity to develop and contribute more to development initiatives. A reducing trend of prevalence of early child marriage has been reported by Ref. [54] but the overall proportion is still high because many girls are dropped out of school. UNICEF [57] reported six West African countries that had an average of over 50 % of early child brides. Notable among them were Niger 76 %, Central African Republic 68 %, Chad 67 % and Nigeria; as the most populous country in West Africa had the highest number of child brides up to 40 % of the sub region. Education can play crucial roles in terminating early child brides and associated early birth.

Education can grant power and access to resource for female: Education is one of the primary determinants of an individual's earning potentials. Hence, those with higher levels of education tend to earn more over their lifetimes compared to those with lower levels of education. Proper education can empower individuals, particularly women, to understand and assert their legal right. In addition, respondents who have access to more land also had more income and income had positive relationship with education of respondents. Males' income leads females' income because most female work in informal sector; increasing, opportunities for female such as education can help them gain more income as men. Higher levels of education typically open up more career opportunities and access to better-paying jobs. This can lead to improved socio-economic outcomes for individuals and their families. Adedeji, Afolabi and Akinlolu [55] noted that education is a significant way to increase monetary literacy among women and reduce the gender gap in access to human rights such as owning houses.

The female tends to dominate the male counterpart in informal sector; for instance, in the National Open Apprenticeship Scheme in 2017 and 2018 female had 64.50 % and 58.50 % respectively, for a survey in 2017. Female employment in small and medium scale enterprises was 43.43 % but most of these females were in small scale enterprises and accounted for 42.02 % of the industrialists [38]. In 2017, female accounted for 15.43 % of professors in Nigerian Universities but higher in Federal Civil Service as up to 43.5 % (38). Women and girls were often left to do domestic work and carrying baby. Few years ago, Ayo-Aderele [56] reported a president of an African country saying ‘my wife belongs to my kitchen and the other room’ while office of the first lady can be a robust arm of governance. Good education will correct the ugly trend.

Conclusion and recommendations

Education has proved to be critical element in development paradigm for shaping peoples’ behaviours. It has been informative to reexamine gender inequality and the stance of education in reducing the male dominance occasioned by patriarchy in Africa. The study has demonstrated the role of education in attaining gender equality. The paper is of the view that education of both genders is imperative in achieving proper understanding and use of gender in development. It emphasises that education of the girl child is a principal way to allow her enjoy life despite the persistent gender inequality. Consequently, the following recommendations were made:

Government and non-government organizations can educate parents on the need to value the female children and reject early marriage for the female children as this is necessary for the welfare of girls and enshrining inclusiveness as well as quality education and achieving women empowerment.

Parents can encourage gender equality through access to resources as this can help reduce the financial burden men bear and possibly reduce the number of widows.

Men need to increase managing household affairs with more humane approaches as this can also reduce the number of women living as single parents.

Parents and governments should continue encouraging education of the girl child as this gives her the prospects to enjoy family life and gain lifelong access to productive resources like land.

African women need to create more awareness of the African Union and African women charter and Protocols on land and inheritance as this can help to achieve the needed change in the society with respect to land access by females.
