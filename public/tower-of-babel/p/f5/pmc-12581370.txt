Gender differences in the predictive value of serum uric acid for major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with acute coronary syndromes: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Cuimei Guo, Nan Lin, Ailin Cao, Chaoxin Han, Qifeng Jin, Yan Liu, Xinxin Bian, Fengyi Shan, Zhongxu Sun, Shaohui Zhang, Xueying Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSerum uric acid(SUA) is proinflammatory and increases the risk of cardiovascular events in patients with coronary artery disease. SUA levels differ between men and women, and it is unclear whether there are gender differences in the correlation between SUA levels and major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with acute coronary syndromes (ACS). In this paper, we analyze the correlation between SUA and MACE in men and women with ACS respectively, to achieve a more accurate prognosis prediction and to determine the optimal level of SUA control in ACS patients of different genders.MethodsThis was a retrospective cohort study that ultimately selected 2799 ACS patients who were hospitalized in the Department of Cardiology of the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical College from May 2013 to October 2015, and grouped the patients according to the gender-specific criteria for hyperuricemia(HUA). With a mean follow-up of 4.42 years after discharge, MACE were collected, and the correlation between SUA and MACE risk in patients of different genders was investigated using the Cox proportional risk regression model. Smooth curve fitting was used to analyze the nonlinear relationship between SUA and MACE, and sensitivity and subgroup analyses were performed to investigate further the correlation between SUA and MACE in different populations.ResultsA total of 309 patients developed MACE during the follow-up period. Kaplan-Meier curves showed that patients with HUA had a higher risk of MACE (p < 0.01). Multivariate Cox proportional risk regression modeling analysis revealed a significant correlation between SUA and MACE risk. In crude Cox analyses, SUA levels were associated with higher risk of MACE (total: HR 1.22,95% CI 1.13, 1.31, p < 0.0001, men: HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.14, 1.35, p < 0.0001, women: HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.00, 1.34, p = 0.0477). However, after adjusting for various covariates including SYNTAX scores(SS), the prediction of MACE risk by SUA was clinically significant only in men (HR 1.21, 95% CI 1.03, 1.42, p = 0.0191), and the incidence of MACE events was elevated with elevated uric acid in women but was not clinically significant (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.82, 1.38, p = 0.6633). In addition, when results were analyzed according to SUA tertiles and gender, men with higher SUA tertiles had a higher risk of MACE (p < 0.0001). After adjusting for variables, smoothed-fit curve analysis similarly showed a trend toward higher MACE occurrence with elevated uric acid in a variety of populations. The inflection points present in the threshold effect analysis in the whole population, men and women curve fits were 7.11 mg/dL, 7.13 mg/dL, and 6.31 mg/dL, respectively, and for every 1-unit increase in uric acid to the right of the inflection point, the risk of MACE increased by 1.48-fold, 1.24-fold, and 1.48-fold, respectively. (( HR (95%) CI: 2.48 (1.57, 3.90) p < 0.0001, in total; 2.24 (1.37, 3.65) p = 0.0013, in men; 2.48 (1.02, 6.01) p = 0.0442, in women)). Subgroup analyses and interaction tests showed that the association between uric acid and MACE was more significant in men in the high triglyceride and high LDL population, whereas in women it was more significant in patients with high BMI, mild coronary artery stenosis, high creatinine, and normoglycemia (interaction p-value < 0.05).ConclusionsThere is a gender difference in the risk of MACE and uric acid levels in patients with ACS, with elevated uric acid levels being more likely to lead to cardiovascular events in men patients, whereas there was no significant difference in women patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12872-025-05262-x.

Introduction

Coronary artery disease (CAD) occurs mainly due to the narrowing or blockage of blood vessels caused by coronary atherosclerosis, which ultimately leads to myocardial hypoxia, ischemia or necrosis. Its incidence is increasing year by year with a trend towards younger age, and it has become one of the most serious diseases threatening human health and causing human death, constituting an increasing public health burden globally [1, 2]. Acute coronary syndrome (ACS) is one of the most critical and serious conditions in cardiovascular diseases. Its harm is not only reflected in the extremely high mortality in the acute phase, but also in the long-term and serious consequences, even if the patients survive [3]. Despite advances in coronary revascularization treatment techniques such as coronary stent implantation and coronary artery bypass grafting, the long-term prognosis of ACS patients remains poor due to the complexity of coronary artery lesions. Therefore, it is crucial to clarify the factors affecting the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) in patients with ACS, which is significant in reducing the occurrence of MACE.

Uric acid, the end product of purine metabolism, is an antioxidant that accounts for 50% of the total antioxidant capacity of human biological fluids, and higher serum uric acid (SUA) concentrations may be a compensatory mechanism for counteracting oxidative damage associated with atherosclerosis and aging in humans, which would make it beneficial for patients with cardiovascular disease [4]. However, when SUA is present in the cytoplasm of cells or the acidic/hydrophobic environment of atherosclerotic plaques, SUA is converted into a pro-oxidant [5], which has been associated with coronary artery disease through mechanisms that increase oxidative stress, decrease nitric oxide, impair endothelial function, promote local and systemic inflammatory responses, and enable the proliferation of vascular smooth muscle cells, insulin resistance, and metabolic derangements [6, 7]. In 2015 A Mendelian randomization analyzing SUA in 3315 patients showed that high UA increases the risk of MACE, especially sudden cardiac death [8].

However, there is still uncertainty about whether there are gender differences in the predictive effect of SUA levels on MACE in patients with ACS and their specific normal value ranges. The aim of this study was to investigate the prognostic value of SUA levels in patients with ACS by gender and to find an appropriate level of SUA control for them, in order to clarify how gender differences affect the prognostic value of SUA for MACE in ACS.

Methods

Study population

This was a retrospective cohort study that included 3792 patients who were diagnosed with ACS and underwent coronary angiography in the Department of Cardiology of the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical College from May 2013 to October 2015. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) meeting the relevant diagnostic criteria for ACS; (2) consenting to coronary angiography; (3) patients between the ages of 26–88 years. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with combined cardiovascular shock and severe cardiac arrhythmia (third-degree atrioventricular block, atrioventricular tachycardia, etc.); (2) patients with severe hepatic or renal insufficiency, infections, tumors, and anemia; (3) patients with combined severe pulmonary disease (bronchodilatation, pulmonary embolism, etc.); (4) patients with a history of major surgery or traumatic injuries within 3 months; (5) patients with history of cerebral hemorrhage or cerebral infarction within 1 month.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University. The requirement for informed consent was formally waived by the Affiliated Hospital of Jining Medical University due to the anonymized retrospective analysis of existing clinical data.

Coronary angiography

ACS mainly includes unstable angina pectoris, non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI). All patients underwent coronary angiography to observe coronary artery disease. Coronary angiography (CAG) was performed by three interventionalists with more than 10 years of experience in CAG procedures, which were done in the catheterization laboratory under X-ray fluoroscopy. After puncturing the patient’s radial artery, an arterial sheath is placed to guide the catheter, which is guided by a guidewire, into the opening of the coronary artery. Coronary contrast is then injected, and the need for balloon dilatation or stenting implantation is determined on the basis of the CAG results. At the end of the procedure, the catheter and sheath are removed and the puncture site is kept closed by compression.

Data collection

Data on demographic characteristics (gender, age, weight, height), past medical history, personal history, and medication use were collected through the electronic medical record system. Biochemical tests were performed to measure: fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), creatinine (Cr) levels. Left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was measured by cardiac ultrasound. SUA was measured by colorimetric method and values were reported as mg/dL units.

SYNTAX score testing

The SYNTAX Score (synergy between percutaneous coronary intervention with Taxus and Cardiac Surgery score, SS) is calculated based on CAG results. To determine whether the right or left coronary artery was dominant, each vessel and each lesion was examined individually, and vascular lesions with stenosis of more than 50% and a diameter of more than 1.5 mm were included in the calculation. In addition, factors such as whether the lesion was completely occluded, whether it was terminally obtuse, and whether there was antegrade collateral flow were considered in the scoring. Calculations were performed using a publicly accessible Web-based scoring calculator (http://www.syntaxscore.com). The core laboratory analysts who calculated the SS did not know baseline demographic data, treatment assignment, or clinical outcomes. Interoperator variability in SS measurements has been previously described in detail [9].

Follow-up

Enrolled patients were followed up until May 30, 2019. The primary endpoint of this study was the occurrence of MACE. Patient follow-up was obtained through routine clinic reviews, telephone interviews, and case reviews. First occurrence of MACE, including recurrent angina, sudden cardiac death, NYHA classification elevation of one grade or more, nonfatal myocardial infarction (MI), repeat coronary revascularization, and out-of-hospital death.

Treatment of missing data

The Supplementary Table shows the number of participants with missing data for each variable. To avoid bias caused by the direct exclusion of missing data and improve the test’s efficiency, we used multiple interpolation analyses to estimate missing values. Body mass index (BMI), SS, Heart Rate (HR), Systolic Blood Pressure (SBP), Diastolic Blood Pressure (DBP), Cr, TGs, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C, FBG, D-dimer, and EF were used to estimate the missing values based on five models.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range) or mean (SD: standard deviation), while categorical variables were expressed using percentages and frequencies. To assess differences based on the distribution and categorization of variables and data, t-tests or χ2 tests were used. Cumulative event curves were generated using the Kaplan-Meier method and stratified by the critical value of hyperuricemia(HUA), respectively. Differences in survival between groups were assessed by log-rank test. Sex differences in SUA and HUA about MACE risk were assessed using univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses. Three models were used: model I (not adjusted for any covariates), model II (adjusted for age), and model III (screened for age + covariates(SS, Smoking, Cr, D-dimer, EF, Clopidogrel, Spironolactone)). To assess the independent association of SUA with the primary endpoint. To explore potential nonlinear associations between UA and MACE risk, we used Cox proportional risk models with smooth curve fitting. Where nonlinearity emerged, recursive algorithms pinpointed inflection points. In addition, subgroup analyses and interaction tests were performed, using stratified Cox proportional risk regression models for different subgroups (age, BMI, smoking status, hypertension, etc.). In addition to the stratification factors, we adjusted for Age; SS; Smoking; Cr; D-dimer; EF; Clopidogrel, and Spironolactone.

All analyses were performed using the Easy Statistical Software (http://www.empowerstats.com, X&Y Solutions Inc., Boston, MA, USA) and R (version 4.2.0, http://www.R-project.org). Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

Baseline characteristics

The present study included 2799 patients out of which 1182 (42.23%) were women and 1617 (57.77%) were men. Tables 1 and 2 demonstrate the baseline demographic, clinical, and biochemical characteristics of men and women patients for the presence of HUA. For demographic characteristics, patients with HUA had high BMI and a higher percentage of hypertensive patients in both men and women. Men patients with HUA had an increased proportion of patients with a history of myocardial infarction (p = 0.038) and heart failure (p < 0.001). In contrast, women had a higher proportion of elderly patients with HUA (p = 0.013) and an increased proportion of patients with a previous history of diabetes mellitus (p = 0.024) and hyperlipidemia (p = 0.003), and gender differences were also observed in laboratory tests. Men with HUA had an increased proportion of patients with high total cholesterol (p < 0.001), high LDL (p = 0.032), and low ejection fraction (p = 0.020), whereas women did not. Regardless of gender, the proportion of patients with high creatinine, elevated triglycerides, and low HDL was higher in patients with HUA. For medications, ARBs were prescribed at a higher rate for men and statins and spironolactone for women in hyperuricemic patients compared with non-hypericemic patients. In addition, the incidence of MACE was higher in hyperuricemic patients regardless of gender.

Analysing the independent effect of SUA levels on MACE risk by gender

Univariate analyses showed (Supplementary Table 1) that in the full population and both genders, the risk of MACE was associated with SS, HDL, ejection fraction, and administration of drugs such as hydroclopidogrel, nitrates, and spironolactone. Smoking was only observed in men (p = 0.0185), and in the full population and men, MACE was shown to be associated with smoking, history of previous infarction, history of heart failure, creatinine, BNP, D-dimer, Beta blockers, and ACEI. In addition, in the full population and women, MACE was associated with age, history of previous PCI, total cholesterol, statins, and insulin.

To further explore the association between SUA levels and MACE risk by sex, we first performed a covariate screen, and we selected these confounders based on their association with the outcome of interest or a change of more than 10% in the effect estimate. Supplementary Tables S2-S5 show the association of each confounder with the outcome of interest. Three Cox proportional risk regression models (Model I unadjusted, Model II adjusted for age, and Model III adjusted for age and covariates screened for metrics) were developed with this in mind, as detailed in Table 4. In crude Cox analyses, SUA levels were associated with a greater than 1% change in effect estimates for the entire population (HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.13, 1.31, p < 0.0001) and both sexes (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.14, 1.31, p < 0.0001). 95% CI 1.14, 1.35, p < 0.0001, in men; HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.00, 1.34, p = 0.0477, in women) were significantly associated with MACE risk. However, after adjustment by multivariate Cox regression analysis via covariates, SUA (1 mg/dL) was a significant predictor of the risk of MACE only in men (HR 1.21,95% CI 1.03, 1.42, p = 0.0191), and in women, although it was shown to increase by 6% in the occurrence of a MACE event with each one-unit increase in UA, the p-value did not reach statistical significance (HR 1.06,95% CI 0.82, 1.38, p = 0.6633). When the results were analyzed according to SUA tertiles and gender, patients with higher SUA tertiles in both sexes had a higher risk of MACE (men, p < 0.0001; women, p = 0.0009). However, after adjusting for covariates, a positive association between SUA and MACE was only observed in men (p = 0.0355) and not in women (p = 0.5355). The different model analyses were based on 5 replicates of multiple interpolations detailed in Supplementary Tables S6- S12.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the association between SUA levels and the risk of MACE in a population with ACS. We found a significant sex-specific association between SUA and long-term prognosis in a population with ACS, independent of women after adjustment for covariates. The impact of gender differences on the role of SUA in the identification of long-term prognosis in patients with ACS populations has not been fully elucidated. The present study provides a comprehensive description of the correlation between SUA levels and the occurrence of coronary MACE in patients with ACS across gender.

HUA has become more prevalent worldwide in recent decades [10–12]. Although SUA is usually discussed in the context of gout, SUA may have multiple pro-inflammatory, pro-oxidative, and vasoconstrictor effects and is an important pathogenic risk factor for many cardiovascular diseases [13]. In addition, elevated intracellular SUA levels induce excessive accumulation of triglycerides in adipocytes and new lipogenesis in hepatocytes, which promotes insulin resistance-mediated visceral fat accumulation [14]. Previous literature suggests that HUA or elevated SUA levels are associated with a variety of disorders including hypertension [15, 16], chronic kidney disease [17], CAD [18], heart failure [19], metabolic syndrome [20] and diabetes mellitus [21]. Whether SUA represents an independent risk factor for the development of these diseases remains controversial [22]. Although, a meta-analysis did not find an independent predictive value of SUA in coronary heart disease [23]. Study in patients with chronic coronary syndrome (CCS) indicate that SUA levels show no significant association with the severity of CAD (including the number of involved vessels, lesion type, and Gensini scores and SS) or left ventricular diastolic dysfunction [24]. The role of SUA in acute versus chronic coronary syndromes also appears to differ [25]. However, recent studies have shown that HUA increases the risk of plaque formation and endothelial dysfunction assessed by ultrasonography [26]. A multicenter retrospective cohort study demonstrated that SUA levels were associated with MACE in patients after CABG, which was independent of traditional cardiovascular risk factors [27]. SUA in patients with acute myocardial infarction complicated by decreased left ventricular function and or heart failure was significantly associated with increased risk of hospitalization and death [28]. SUA concentration is an independent predictor of multivariable-adjusted cardiovascular mortality and hospitalization in patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction [29]. In addition, study showed that diuretic-related HUA carry a similar risk of cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality when compared with individuals that present HUA in absence of diuretic therapy [30]. Consistent with previous findings, we found that SUA was associated with an increased risk of MACE (HR 1.22, 95% CI 1.13, 1.31, p < 0.0001) in patients with ACS. Moreover, we adjusted for the SYNTAX score, which represents the degree of coronary stenosis and was first proposed by Sianos et al. in 2005 to assess the severity of coronary artery lesions and risk stratification, thus providing an important guideline for selecting appropriate revascularization strategies in patients with complex coronary artery disease. The score was also shown to be an independent predictor of major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with complex coronary artery disease, which had never been mentioned in previously analyzed studies. This was true even after potential confounders (HR 1.16, 95% CI 1.01, 1.33, p = 0.0321). The low percentage of patients with a history of hyperlipidemia in our study may be due in part to a lack of prior diagnosis. Given the critical role of lipids in coronary artery disease, all patients underwent systematic and repeated lipid measurements after admission to accurately assess dyslipidemia. NYHA classification [31] is a simple and practical assessment tool for cardiac function and risk in patients with heart failure (HF), which has been used for nearly a century. This grading system is recognized as an important clinical marker of worsening cardiac function and is widely used to determine eligibility for clinical trials and drug and device candidates. The results of this study suggest that SUA can be used as an important indicator to identify individuals at high risk for early cardiac function deterioration after ACS. Overall, SUA is an important risk and prognostic biomarker in patients with ACS.

Gender differences have been reported in the association between SUA and cardiovascular outcomes. For atrial fibrillation, SUA is more pathogenic in women patients than in men patients [32]. The prevalence of moderate-to-severe sleep apnea disorders increased with increasing SUA levels, and these were not found in men [33]. In patients with type 2 diabetes, SUA levels were independently associated with a high 10-year risk of developing atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease in women, but not in men [34]. In addition, elevated SUA levels were more strongly associated with MACE in women than in men undergoing percutaneous coronary intervention for acute coronary artery disease [35]. It seems that the correlation between SUA and cardiovascular outcomes is more significantly different in women patients. Several studies have shown that higher SUA levels correlate more strongly with hypertension and cardiovascular disease in women compared to men [36, 37]. However, in elderly patients with nonvalvular atrial fibrillation, SUA was independently associated with left ventricular hypertrophy in men [38]. For patients with heart failure, SUA was independently associated with all-cause and cardiovascular mortality in men but not women patients in the United States [39]. Furthermore, studies have shown that during a 25-year follow-up period, both SUA levels and the incidence of HUA in the general population exhibited a gradual increasing trend. Baseline SUA level was the most strongly associated predictive factor for these changes, and female sex was also associated with the future development of HUA [40]. Our present study also demonstrates that SUA levels can be used to predict the occurrence of major adverse cardiovascular events in men patients with ACS after adjusting for confounders, with no significant difference in women patients. This difference between genders may arise from several sources, including but not limited to the characteristics of the population we studied, the variables adjusted for in the statistical analysis, and sample size considerations. In addition, we cannot rule out the possibility that the women sample size may have contributed to this sex-specific difference. Further large sample-size studies are still needed to validate our findings and to clarify the underlying mechanisms behind the gender differences in SUA levels and their dynamic changes in patients with coronary heart disease and their impact on prognosis.

Studies have shown a close correlation between SUA levels and obesity-related indices such as glucose triglycerides [41]. SUA induces triglyceride accumulation by inducing endoplasmic reticulum stress in hepatocytes [42]. In addition, elevated levels of SUA promote oxidative stress, which in turn exacerbates insulin resistance, adipose tissue expression in obese patients, increased xanthine oxidase activity, and insulin resistance can elevate SUA levels by increasing tubular reabsorption of SUA [43]. A retrospective cohort study of 6476 healthy Japanese adults showed that increased SUA over 5 years was also an independent risk for the development of high LDL and hypertriglyceridemia [44]. This is consistent with the fact that in our subgroup analysis, increased SUA levels put the risk of MACE at a higher level in the total population and men patients with high triglycerides and men patients with elevated LDL. HUA is common in patients with chronic kidney disease, which may be due to decreased SUA excretion as a result of deteriorating renal function. Epidemiologic studies have also shown that HUA is an independent risk factor for adverse renal outcomes [45]. In our subgroup analyses, we also found that the elevated SUA levels exhibited by patients with high creatinine levels had a more significant effect on the development of MACE, both in all and in women patients.

Uric acid-lowering therapy has also been found to improve the prognosis of cardiovascular disease in some studies. Although the uric acid-lowering effect of weight loss appears to be weaker than that of uric acid-lowering drugs, other associated health benefits would make weight loss important [46]. In addition, sacubitril/valsartan reduced SUA by 0.24 (0.17–0.32) mg/dL (P < 0.0001) over 12 months compared with enalapril and improved outcomes [29]. Compared to the conventional threshold for SUA, multiple cardiovascular disease-related studies have proposed differing cut-off values, yet al.l indicate that the SUA level associated with a significantly elevated cardiovascular risk is generally lower than this conventional threshold [47, 48]. In conclusion, SUA is emerging as a potentially treatable risk factor in patients with cardiovascular disease, and the potential benefits of SUA still require further study.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective cohort study, bias may exist. Therefore, we used potential confounding variables as covariates in the regression model to control for referral bias and possible confounders. Second, we analyzed only baseline clinical data, which did not allow us to control for intraindividual variability and did not allow us to measure the prognostic impact of SUA changes over time. Third, we did not document dietary habits or other behaviors that might influence SUA levels. Fourth, we lacked data on coronary anatomy and the extent of revascularization, preventing an analysis of the potential prognostic factor of complete versus incomplete revascularization. Future prospective studies should address this. Fifth, stroke was not included in our primary MACE endpoint, limiting comprehensive cardiovascular risk assessment. Finally, because the participants in our study were only Chinese, the current results may not apply to different ethnic groups. Future large-sample, multicenter, and well-designed prospective studies are needed to strengthen our conclusions.

Conclusion

Our study shows that there is a gender difference in MACE risk and SUA levels in patients with ACS. Men patients with ACS were more strongly associated with the risk of MACE. In addition, increased SUA levels with elevated triglycerides, LDL, and an ejection fraction of 54–59% put the risk of MACE higher. In contrast, there was no significant difference in women patients. The control of normal values of SUA in patients with ACS in clinical practice should take into account gender differences to reduce the occurrence of MACE events in patients with ACS brought about by elevated SUA.
