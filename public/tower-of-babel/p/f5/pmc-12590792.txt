Triglyceride glucose-waist circumference dynamics and cardiovascular risk: a national longitudinal study
Authors: Jiana Zhong, Dixing Liu, Xinyi Huang, Jiajing Yuan, Yue Hong, Wenting Xuan, Weikun Chen, Xiuwei Zhang, Lingjie He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular disease (CVD) has been documented to be related with the triglyceride glucose (TyG) index. However, limited research has explored the change in the triglyceride glucose-waist circumference (TyG-WC) index in relation to CVD, particularly among individuals with prediabetes and diabetes. Thus, this longitudinal analysis aimed to investigate the association between changes in the TyG-WC and CVD in patients with glucose dysregulation.MethodsThis study comprised 2428 individuals with prediabetes and diabetes, sourced from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database. Using K-means clustering, three subgroups were identified based on their TyG-WC index trajectories. Additionally, the cumulative TyG-WC index was calculated and divided into tertiles (T1, T2, T3). Proportional hazards models were employed to examine the correlations between TyG-WC trajectories, cumulative TyG-WC tertiles, and the incidence of CVD during the follow-up period. Subgroup analyses were carried out to validate the robustness of the results.ResultsThe incidence rates of CVD, cardiac events, and stroke were highest among patients with a high-stable TyG-WC, significantly reduced in the moderate-stable TyG-WC group, and lowest among participants with low-stable TyG-WC (all P for trend < 0.001). After adjusting for confounders, the risk of CVD was significantly associated with the TyG-WC trajectories and the cumulative TyG-WC tertiles. Compared to the low-stable group, the CVD risk for the moderate and high-stable groups were 1.266 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.009–1.589) and 1.639 (95% CI: 1.269–2.117), respectively (both P < 0.05). Furthermore, the risk of CVD, stroke, and cardiac events in T2 group and T3 group was significantly higher than that in T1 group (all P < 0.05). ROC curve analysis showed that compared with baseline waist circumference, TyG-WC and TyG, the cumulative TyG-WC demonstrated a relatively higher discriminative ability in assessing CVD.ConclusionThe changes of TyG-WC are closely related to CVD among individuals with prediabetes and diabetes, which may serve as a novel indicator for the early detection of CVD risk in these populations.Keywords: Diabetes, Prediabetes, Cardiovascular disease, CHARLS, Triglyceride glucose-waist circumference, Long‑term changes, K‑means clustering

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major contributor to global mortality, presenting significant public health challenges and incurring substantial medical costs [1, 2]. Over the past two decades, the global prevalence of CVD has nearly doubled. It is reckoned that if current CVD risk factors remain unmitigated, approximately 7.8 million individuals will experience premature mortality due to CVD by 2025 [2]. Notably, individuals with prediabetes and diabetes are at a significantly heightened risk of developing CVD, which accounts for more than 50% of mortality within these populations [3, 4]. Thus, the identification and management of CVD risk factors in individuals with prediabetes and diabetes are essential for reducing mortality, particularly from cardiovascular-related causes [5].

Insulin resistance is a key factor contributing to the heightened incidence of fibrotic and sclerosing vascular alterations observed in metabolic disorders [6]. A growing body of research highlights the significant impact of insulin resistance on both the onset and progression of CVD in individuals with diabetes, establishing it as a pathogenic factor that adversely affects prognosis in this population [7]. The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index, calculated from fasting blood glucose (FBG) and triglyceride levels, serves as a validated surrogate indicator for evaluating insulin resistance in clinical practice. Statistical analyses reveal a significant association between the TyG index and CVD [8]. Recent studies have introduced novel indices, termed TyG-related indices, which integrate obesity-related metrics with the TyG index. This integration may enhance the capacity of TyG-related indices to detect insulin resistance more effectively than other surrogate markers. Comparative analyses indicate that TyG-related indices provide superior risk stratification for cardiometabolic mortality outcomes compared to the TyG index alone [9, 10]. Among these indices, the triglyceride glucose-waist circumference (TyG-WC) index has been identified as being associated with diabetes, prediabetes, stroke, and CVD in the general population [11–14]. In diabetic patients, the TyG-WC index has demonstrated superior predictive performance for coronary heart disease and myocardial infarction, whereas the TyG-BMI index has not shown significant superiority [15]. However, it is crucial to recognize that waist circumference, the TyG index, and the TyG-WC index are dynamic variables that exhibit temporal fluctuations. Current evidence is predominantly based on analyses of static indicators and lacks support from studies involving dynamic monitoring. To date, no research has explored the relationship between changes in the TyG-WC index and the incidence of CVD, particularly in diabetic and prediabetic populations.

Therefore, the primary objective of this study is to investigate the association between variations in the TyG-WC index and the development of CVD in individuals with prediabetes and diabetes in China. It is hypothesized that both the trajectories of TyG-WC and the cumulative TyG-WC are potentially associated with CVD. The cumulative TyG-WC measure may provide a more robust prediction of CVD risk compared to baseline TyG-WC, waist circumference, or the TyG index alone. This study pioneers the exploration of the largely unexamined domain of the changes in the TyG-WC and their association with CVD in populations with impaired glucose metabolism.

Methods

Data availability

The dataset utilized in this research was sourced from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a publicly accessible resource with ethical approval for research purposes. This dataset can be downloaded from the official CHARLS website: http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en.

Study participants

CHARLS is an ongoing longitudinal study that collects data on individuals aged 45 and older in China. The study encompasses 150 administrative regions and 450 rural communities, with an approximate sample size of 17,000 participants. Initial data collection commenced in 2011, with biennial follow-up waves continuing until the study’s conclusion. Each survey round has received ethical approval. The dataset includes demographic, clinical variables, lifestyle factors, and biochemical indicators such as HbA1c, FBG, high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), creatinine, urea nitrogen, and lipid profile.

Initially, the study recruited a cohort of 16,528 individuals for analysis. Among these, 12,069 participants who met the criteria for prediabetes or diabetes underwent further screening. Participants were subsequently excluded based on the following criteria: (1) lack of waist circumference data or extreme waist circumference values (waist circumference < 50 cm) during both Wave 1 and Wave 3; (2) missing FBG data during Wave 1 and Wave 3; (3) absence of triglyceride information at Wave 3; (4) missing age information; (5) presence of CVD at baseline or development of CVD by 2015; and (6) unclear CVD status throughout the follow-up period. Ultimately, the study included 2428 participants (Fig. 1).

Flowchart of patient enrollment

Measurement of TyG-WC and cumulative TyG-WC

TyG-WC = ln [(glucose (mg/dL) ×triglycerides (mg/dL))/2] ×waist circumference (cm).

The methodology for calculating cumulative TyG-WC involves determining the average of two measurements (Wave 1 and Wave 3) for each participant, followed by multiplying this average by the total duration of the observation period, which spans three years. This approach provides an estimate of the individual’s total cumulative exposure to TyG-WC over the observation period. That is, the cumulative TyG-WC= (TyG-WC at Wave 1 + TyG-WC at Wave 3)/2 × 3.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was the incidence of CVD, which included both stroke and cardiac events. Cardiac events were further categorized into heart attack, angina, heart failure, coronary heart disease, and other heart-related conditions. Participants who reported a physician-diagnosed stroke or heart disease were defined as having CVD. In this study, the secondary outcomes involved the separate examination of cardiac events and stroke.

Statistical analysis

In this analysis, categorical variables were reported as percentages. Quantitative variables with normal distribution characteristics were expressed as mean ± standard deviations, whereas those with non-parametric distributions were described using median and interquartile ranges. Trajectories were utilized to depict the evolving patterns of the investigated variables over different time points. Longitudinal trajectory modeling was employed to identify distinct variation pathways of the TyG-WC index through group-based analytical methods. The model was fitted using the K-means clustering algorithm, an unsupervised learning technique based on Euclidean distance. Subsequently, individuals were classified according to similar change patterns observed from wave 1 to wave 3. The process involved three steps: initially, the desired number of clusters (k) was determined using R (version 4.4.1) by integrating the Elbow Method and silhouette analysis. The Elbow Method revealed a distinct inflection point at k = 3, indicating optimal cluster compactness, while silhouette analysis confirmed adequate cluster separation at this value. Although k = 2 exhibited a slightly higher silhouette score, the three-cluster solution was preferred due to its capacity to identify a clinically meaningful intermediate-risk group and its consistency with established metabolic risk patterns [16]; next, individuals were randomly selected to serve as the initial cluster centers; finally, each individual was assigned to the nearest centroid, and the cluster centroids were updated iteratively. This procedure continued until the sum of squares within clusters reached its minimum value [16]. Each patient was assigned to a specific cluster based on the Euclidean distance from the cluster centers. The clustering results were visually represented. Notably, the algorithm operated independently of outcome variables throughout the clustering process, ensuring an unbiased analysis. The TyG-WC transition data, spanning from wave 1 to wave 3, were analyzed and sorted into three groups using the K-means method. The trajectories were classified as “Low-stable,” “High-stable,” and “Moderate-stable.” In Class 1 (n = 921), the TyG-WC values ranged from 657.10 in wave 1 to 651.99 in wave 3, indicating a low-stable TyG-WC. In Class 2 (n = 1019), it ranged from 787.88 in wave 1 to 791.62 in wave 3, indicating a moderate-stable TyG-WC. In Class 3 (n = 488), it ranged from 932.12 in wave 1 to 937.54 in wave 3, representing a high-stable TyG-WC (Fig. 2). The distribution of TyG-WC in each group was normal, and the average TyG-WC levels across these groups showed statistically significant difference.

Trajectories of TyG-WC among the study participants. From wave 1 to wave 3, the changes in TyG-WC were analyzed and sorted into three groups: "Low-stable" (n=921), "Moderate-stable" (n=1019), and "High-stable" (n=488) through K-means clustering

To compare the demographic and clinical characteristics of patients across different TyG-WC trajectories groups, various statistical methodologies were utilized. Continuous variables were analyzed using one-way ANOVA, whereas categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test. Multivariate Cox regression analyses were also carried out to calculate the adjusted hazard ratio (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for cardiovascular endpoints. In Model 1, adjustments were made for the confounding variables of age and sex. Model 2 included further adjustments for drinking habits, smoking status, and marital status. Model 3 further incorporated factors such as hs-CRP, creatinine, total cholesterol, and LDL-C. To investigate potential associations between cumulative TyG-WC and CVD, restricted cubic spline (RCS) regression analysis was employed. To assess diagnostic accuracy, receiver-operating characteristic (ROC) curves were constructed, and the corresponding areas under the curve (AUC) were calculated to assess the predictive potential of the baseline TyG index, waist circumference, cumulative TyG-WC, and baseline TyG-WC in relation to cardiovascular outcomes. Furthermore, to evaluate the overall performance of these cox regression models and ROC curves, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) and Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) were calculated for each model using the extract function in R 4.4.1 (survival package). The Harrell’s concordance index (C-index) was computed via the concordance function to assess the predictive accuracy of survival outcomes, with values approaching 1 indicating superior discriminatory ability.

Besides, an additional subgroup analysis was conducted to assess the relationship between TyG-WC trajectories and CVD, categorized by age (< 65 years, ≥ 65 years), gender, current drinking status, smoking status (current smokers, never smokers, former smokers), marital status (married and others), and disease stage (prediabetes, diabetes). In each model group, besides utilizing these variables as subgroup variables, other variables including age, gender, drinking habits, smoking status, marital status, hs-CRP, total cholesterol, creatinine and LDL-C were incorporated as confounders in multivariable Cox regression models. Data processing and statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.4.1 (survival package), with statistical significance set at P-values < 0.05.

Baseline characteristics

In this study, a total of 2,428 participants diagnosed with either prediabetes or diabetes was included. The cohort comprised 1096 men (45.1%) and 1332 women (54.9%). Table 1 presents the baseline sociodemographic and medical characteristics of the study population. Participants were categorized into three groups based on TyG-WC trajectories: “Low-stable,” “High-stable,” and “Moderate-stable.” It is noteworthy that female participants were more prevalent in the high-stable TyG-WC trajectory group (P < 0.001). Furthermore, this group exhibited the lowest prevalence of current drinking, yet had a higher proportion of former smokers. Compared to the low-stable group, participants in the other groups generally exhibited increased waist circumference, elevated diastolic and systolic blood pressure, and higher levels of FPG, HbA1c, Hs-CRP, triglycerides, and total cholesterol, along with reduced levels of HDL-C (all P < 0.05).

Clinical outcome

As shown in Fig. 3, patients with elevated TyG-WC trajectories demonstrated a significantly increased risk of CVD, cardiac events, and stroke (all P for trend < 0.001). The incidence of CVD was 14.0% in the low-stable group, 18.4% in the moderate-stable group, and 26.6% in the high-stable group (Fig. 3A). Furthermore, the occurrence of cardiac events was 10.2% in the low-stable group, 13.2% in the moderate-stable group, and 17.8% in the high-stable group (Fig. 3B). Regarding stroke, the incidence rates in high-stable, low-stable, and moderate-stable group were 11.3%, 5.3%, and 6.5%, respectively (Fig. 3C). Consistently, the risk of CVD, cardiac events, and stroke was all also elevated among patients with higher cumulative TyG-WC tertiles (Fig. 3D and F).

The incidence of CVD (D), cardiac events (E), and stroke (F) also increased with the elevation of the cumulative TyG-WC tertiles (all P for trend<0.001)

Relationship between variations in TyG-WC and CVD

Table 2 summarizes the association between TyG-WC trajectories and the incidence of CVD. In model 1, compared to the low-stable group, the risk of developing CVD during follow-up elevated by 31.8% (HR = 1.318; 95% CI: 1.052–1.651) for the moderate-stable group and by 75.4% (HR = 1.754; 95% CI: 1.371–2.244) for the high-stability group. After adjusting for demographic variables, including drinking habits, smoking status, age, gender, and marital status, significant differences persisted among the three groups (all P < 0.05). Further adjustment for confounding factors including hs-CRP, creatinine, total cholesterol, and LDL-C showed that, compared with the low-stable group, the CVD risk for the moderate and high-stable groups were 1.266 (95% CI: 1.009–1.589) and 1.639 (95% CI: 1.269–2.117), respectively (both P < 0.05). As the TyG-WC trajectories increased, there was a significant rise in the risks of stroke and cardiac events. After controlling for confounders in Model 3, compared with the low-stable group, the multivariate-adjusted HR for stroke incidence in the high-stable group was 1.899 (95% CI: 1.265–2.850), while the HR for cardiac events was 1.532 (95% CI: 1.128–2.080). Notably, Model 3 exhibited enhanced predictive accuracy, as indicated by Harrell’s C-index values of 0.811 for CVD, 0.908 for stroke, and 0.834 for cardiac events, suggesting strong discrimination of survival outcomes. Furthermore, when cumulative TyG-WC was divided into three groups based on the tertiles (T1-T3), the risk of CVD in T2 group (HR 1.436, 95%CI: 1.113–1.851) and T3 group (HR 1.675, 95%CI: 1.304–2.150) was significantly higher than that in T1 group (Table 3). RCS analysis showed that the risk of CVD, cardiac events, and stroke gradually increased with the increase of cumulative TyG-WC levels (all P < 0.05) (Fig. 4).

ROC curve analysis

ROC analysis revealed that the cumulative TyG-WC index exhibits a relatively higher discriminative ability in assessing CVD (AUC = 0.590, 95% CI: 0.561–0.619). This value was marginally higher than that of its baseline measurement (AUC = 0.586, 95% CI: 0.557–0.614) and its individual components, including waist circumference (AUC = 0.587, 95% CI: 0.558–0.616) and the TyG index (AUC = 0.552, 95% CI: 0.523–0.581) (Fig. 5A). Additionally, the cumulative TyG-WC showed the highest AUC values for both stroke and cardiac events (Fig. 5B). Complementing the ROC analysis, we further evaluated model fitness and predictive accuracy through Akaike/Bayesian Information Criteria (AIC/BIC) and Harrell’s C-index. The fully adjusted models (Model 3) showed a slightly improved fit compared to simpler models (AIC: 6759.195 vs. 6403.955; BIC: 8670.987 vs. 6416.263), suggesting that the inclusion of additional covariates may have provided a modest enhancement in explanatory power without overfitting. The C-index values ranged from 0.606 to 0.908 across TyG-WC trajectories, which was consistent with the AUC results and indicated a moderate discrimination ability for CVD risk stratification. These metrics suggest that TyG-WC dynamics may have potential utility as a predictive tool in clinical settings.

Subgroup analysis

The relationship between TyG-WC trajectories and the incidence of CVD, stratified by potential risk factors, was shown in Table 4. After adjusting for potential confounding factors, no significant interactions were observed between TyG-WC trajectories and subgroup variables such as age, smoking habits, current drinking status, and marital status (all P for interaction > 0.05). However, age was found to moderate the connection between TyG-WC trajectories and CVD (P for interaction = 0.015). The association between TyG-WC trajectory and CVD was more pronounced in individuals under 65 years of age. Compared to the low-stable group, the HR was 1.469 (95% CI: 1.124–1.922) for the moderate-stable group and 1.812 (95% CI: 1.345–2.442) for the high-stable group among individuals younger than 65 years. Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on disease stage (prediabetes vs. diabetes). The interaction was not significant (P = 0.679), suggesting that different disease stage had no significant impact on the relationship between TyG-WC trajectories and CVD. In the prediabetes group, compared to the low-stable group, both moderate-stable and high-stable trajectories significantly increased the risk of CVD, with HRs of 1.300 (95% CI: 1.005–1.681) and 1.547 (95% CI: 1.130–2.118), respectively. In the diabetes group, this relationship was slightly weaker, possibly due to the smaller sample size of the diabetes population (n = 677).

Discussion

This study investigated the connection between TyG-WC trajectories and CVD in a cohort of Chinese individuals diagnosed with prediabetes or diabetes. It was found that the longitudinal TyG-WC trajectories were significantly correlated with the risk of CVD. Additionally, a higher cumulative TyG-WC levels were linked to a higher incidence of CVD. The cumulative TyG-WC showed moderately higher discriminative ability for CVD compared to baseline TyG, waist circumference, and TyG-WC. Consequently, monitoring dynamic changes in TyG-WC is a crucial instrument for the early evaluation of CVD risk in diabetes and prediabetes populations.

Numerous prior studies across various populations have demonstrated a strong association between the TyG-WC index and CVD, with TyG-WC outperforming the TyG index alone and other TyG-related indices [17–22]. The mechanism linking TyG-WC levels to CVD is likely multifactorial, mainly originating from the pathophysiological consequences of atherosclerotic dyslipidemia, insulin resistance and the accumulation of visceral fat. However, given the dynamic nature of the TyG index and waist circumference over time, recent studies have increasingly focused on evaluating the impact of their longitudinal changes on the incidence of CVD events. In the CHARLS cohort, Wu et al. found that participants with a “high baseline and slow-rising” TyG-BMI pattern had a 62% higher risk of stroke compared to those with a consistently low trajectory [16]. Similarly, studies have shown that changes in TyG-WHtR are also associated with CVD, with “moderate-increasing” and “high-stable” TyG-WHtR patterns serving as independent predictors of increased CVD risk. Moreover, each 1-SD increase in cumulative TyG-WHtR was associated with a 27% increased risk of CVD [23]. Nonetheless, there is limited research investigating the relationship between changes in TyG-WC and CVD. In this study, we aim to investigate the relationship between dynamic changes in TyG-WC, including longitudinal trajectories and cumulative exposure, and CVD. Our findings indicate that participants with moderate-stable and high-stable TyG-WC trajectories have a significantly higher risk of developing CVD compared to those in the low-stable trajectory group. The observed graded, dose-response relationship-where risk progressively increases from low-stable to moderate-stable to high-stable trajectories-suggests that TyG-WC reflects a continuum of metabolic deterioration. This pattern implies that even subclinical, yet persistent elevations in this index may be pathogenic, and that any upward trajectory, irrespective of the initial level, requires clinical attention. Cox regression analysis based on tertiles of cumulative TyG-WC revealed that individuals in the highest tertile had a statistically significant increase in CVD risk. The fact that cumulative TyG-WC exposure showed a stronger correlation than the baseline value further reinforces the concept of “metabolic memory”, that is, the long-term burden of insulin resistance and visceral obesity contributes to disease progression.

Moreover, our findings also revealed a significant age-dependent pattern in the relationship between TyG-WC and CVD. The subgroup analysis demonstrated a significant interaction between age and TyG-WC trajectories, with a markedly stronger association observed in individuals under 65 years of age compared to those aged 65 years and older. This differential risk profile may reflect distinct pathophysiological stages throughout the lifespan. In younger individuals (< 65 years), insulin resistance and visceral adiposity—captured by TyG-WC—are likely primary contributors to early vascular damage, making TyG-WC dynamics a particularly sensitive early-warning biomarker. The attenuated association observed in older adults (≥ 65 years) may be attributed to competing risks from advanced vascular aging, a higher prevalence of multimorbidity, or potential masking effects of intensive cardiovascular medications (e.g., statins, antihypertensives) commonly used in this group [24, 25]. Our findings are consistent with previous studies that demonstrate obesity-related indices have a stronger predictive value for CVD in middle age [25]. This trajectory-based methodology quantifies the clinical implications of longitudinal patterns, representing a significant advancement over previous studies that relied on single-timepoint assessments.

Different trajectories of TyG-WC may represent the continuum of progressive metabolic disorders, delineating three distinct pathological phenotypes. Low-stable trajectories are indicative of an insulin-sensitive phenotype; moderate-stable patterns signify a transitional, metabolically unstable state characterized by early dysregulation and modifiable risk factors; whereas high-stable trajectories define a metabolically dysregulated phenotype with established lipotoxicity and visceral adiposity. Notably, despite the elevated risk of CVD, a considerable proportion of individuals within the high-stable subtype evade the conventional diagnostic criteria for metabolic syndrome, highlighting the clinical application value of trajectory stratification. In exploring the biological mechanisms underlying the relationship linking TyG-WC trajectories to CVD, we propose that two principal processes are predominantly involved. Firstly, insulin resistance may promote atherosclerosis and plaque formation, which can precipitate CVD [26, 27]. Multivariable mediation analysis demonstrates that insulin resistance increases cardiovascular risk factors through mechanisms such as renal sodium retention, adrenergic hyperactivation, and disruption of cellular ionic homeostasis [28]. Furthermore, it impairs the vasodilatory effects of nitric oxide released by endothelial cells on skeletal muscle arterioles [29]. In the context of insulin resistance, which is prevalent during the pre-diabetic stage, the functionality of intimal cells involved in atherosclerosis is compromised. Specifically, in vascular endothelial cells, macrophages, and vascular smooth muscle cells, there is a significant reduction in the insulin receptor and its associated signaling pathways [30]. Besides, insulin resistance is also closely related to inflammatory markers that aggravate the progression of atherosclerosis [31]. The adipocyte pathophenotype triad, consisting of chronic low-grade inflammation, lipid metabolite dysregulation, and hypoxic stress, constitutes a synergistic risk continuum that contributes to cardiovascular pathogenesis through pathways of endothelial dysfunction [24, 32–34]. In general, persistent obesity and over-nutrition are likely to trigger insulin resistance and mild systemic inflammation as a result of lipotoxicity [35].

Strengths and limitations

The following are the advantages of this study: Firstly, there is a notable scarcity of research on the role of TyG-WC in predicting CVD among individuals with diabetes and prediabetes in China. Additionally, previous investigations into the association between TyG-WC and CVD have predominantly relied on assessments at a single time point. This approach may not adequately reflect the dynamic changes in TyG-WC that can occur over time. To address this limitation, the current study employed group-based trajectory modeling to identify distinct temporal progression patterns in TyG-WC trajectories. By categorizing individuals with similar TyG-WC trajectories into groups, this method provides a robust framework for elucidating the potential impact of TyG-WC on CVD incidence. The application of dynamic trajectory modeling to TyG-WC is helpful for systematically understanding its overall change characteristics. This study further highlights the critical importance of longitudinal monitoring of TyG-WC among the Chinese population. Despite the development of a robust analytical framework, this data-centric research contains certain limitations. Firstly, since the CHARLS dataset is derived exclusively from Chinese population, subsequent studies should evaluate the generalizability of these findings to populations with diverse ethnic compositions and cultural contexts. Secondly, the absence of specific causes of death in the CHARLS database restricts our analysis to the CVD mortality as the study outcome. Thirdly, the lack of detailed data on antihypertensive, hypoglycemic, and lipid-lowering medications may result in unmeasured confounding. Finally, the limitations inherent in the CHARLS dataset should be acknowledged, particularly the inability to conduct classified comparisons of ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes and specific cardiac events, and thus it is unable to corroborate with the results of previous more detailed studies [11, 36].

Conclusion

Changes in TyG-WC are associated with CVD among individuals with diabetes and prediabetes, highlighting the clinical significance of obesity and insulin resistance in CVD. The longitudinal variations of TyG-WC values may become a novel biomarker, providing early CVD risk stratification and personalized intervention guidance for individuals with abnormal glucose metabolism.
