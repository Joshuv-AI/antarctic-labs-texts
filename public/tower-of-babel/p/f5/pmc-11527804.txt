Associations between income/employment status and diabetes care processes, health behaviors, and health outcomes in Japan: A cross‐sectional study using claims data linked to a questionnaire survey
Authors: Takuya Yamaoka, Takehiro Sugiyama, Noriko Ihana‐Sugiyama, Akiko Kimura, Kouko Yamamoto, Kenjiro Imai, Naoaki Kuroda, Mitsuru Ohsugi, Kohjiro Ueki, Toshimasa Yamauchi, Nanako Tamiya
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAims/IntroductionWe aimed to explore the associations between income/employment status and diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes.Materials and MethodsThis cross‐sectional study used health insurance claims data between April 2021 and March 2022, and a questionnaire survey between December 2022 and January 2023 in Tsukuba City. The study analyzed the participants with diabetes (other than type 1) from those selected by stratified random sampling. We evaluated diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes by calculating weighted proportions among the groups. We also assessed the associations between income/employment status and these variables using multivariable modified Poisson regression models.ResultsOf the 264 identified participants, 161 (64.2%) were men and 168 (72.8%) were aged ≥65 years old. Compared with the low‐income groups, the high‐income group had a higher proportion of sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors or glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists use (adjusted risk ratio [aRR] 1.70, 95% confidence interval (CI) 1.07–2.72), a higher proportion of attendance to annual health checkups for ≥2 years (aRR 1.68, 95% CI 1.07–2.64) and a lower proportion of all‐cause hospitalization (aRR 0.15, 95% CI 0.04–0.48); additionally, the middle‐income group had a lower proportion of high total outpatient medical expenses (aRR 0.57, 95% CI 0.35–0.92). Compared to the no work time group, the full‐time work group had a lower proportion of exercise habits (aRR 0.59, 95% CI 0.35–0.99) and a higher proportion of good self‐reported health (aRR 2.08, 95% CI 1.22–3.55).ConclusionsSeveral variables were associated with income/employment status. Policy intervention should focus on high‐risk groups identified by considering these associations.

INTRODUCTION

Increasing diabetes prevalence and diabetes‐related health expenditures impact the health of everyone 1 . The vulnerability to diabetes is socially determined 2 , and understanding the influence of social determinants of health is a priority. Socioeconomic status (SES) is a social determinant of health, and includes educational attainment, income and employment status 3 . In general, SES is associated with the onset and progression of diseases and mortality 4 . In diabetes care, specifically, SES is associated with mortality 5 , 6 and glycemic control 7 , 8 . Studies have proposed conceptual frameworks and explored mechanisms in the USA and Bangladesh 9 , 10 , 11 . The findings show that health inequities due to SES among people with diabetes are a significant public health issue.

In Japan, studies have reported the associations between SES and the onset of diabetes 12 , irregular physician visits 13 , glycemic control 13 and diabetes‐related complications 14 . However, evidence regarding SES and diabetes care in Japan is limited compared with that in other countries. Therefore, evidence must be generated based on the social and political background of the country using the relevant databases.

The quality of diabetes care has been reported using nationwide claims data 15 . Although the data availability in Japan increases, claims data cannot easily provide SES information. Integrating SES information with medical claims data would help produce significant findings.

The present study comprehensively investigated the relationship between SES and diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes using a database that linked claims data and questionnaire surveys in Japan.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Design, settings and participants

This cross‐sectional study secondarily used a database that matched the claims data of the National Health Insurance in Tsukuba City in fiscal year 2021 (FY2021; April 2021–March 2022) with a questionnaire survey covering the same city in FY2022. In the Japanese health insurance system, the National Health Insurance, which each municipality insures, covers individuals aged <75 years who receive neither employment‐based insurance nor public assistance. Consequently, it includes many self‐employed people or those with no paid work time (including those who have retired) 16 . Located in the suburbs of Tokyo, Tsukuba City had a population of approximately 240,000 in FY2022, with 170,000 aged 20–74 years. Approximately 40,000 (24%) are the beneficiaries of the National Health Insurance in Tsukuba City 17 .

For administrative purposes, Tsukuba City carried out a questionnaire survey from mid‐December 2022 to January‐end 2023, which we used as secondary data. The survey was distributed to 20–74‐year‐olds with diabetes. Having diabetes was defined as having an antidiabetic drug prescription (the anatomical therapeutic chemical classification code A10) in FY2021 claims data or glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) ≥6.5% in the FY2021 health checkup. The survey comprised 50 questions, including income, employment status, and information related to diabetes and health behavior (Figure S1).

The source population, 3,450 people who received only the National Health Insurance, was stratified by information from claims data, including antidiabetic drug prescription, type of diabetes (whether the disease name is type 1 diabetes) and age. The survey distributed the questionnaires to 1,000 randomly selected from each population stratum, with an oversampling of participants from strata with small sample sizes. This study included those who responded to the survey, and excluded those who met the following criteria: (1) those diagnosed with type 1 diabetes in the FY2021 claims data, because people with type 1 diabetes should differ considerably from those with type 2 diabetes in terms of lifestyle and diabetes treatment, and the study size was too small for statistical analysis; and (2) those who provided invalid responses in the questionnaire items. We used the listwise deletion criteria while partially considering the missingness using the inverse probability weighting method. Furthermore, we used additional exclusion criteria when calculating proportions regarding several variables (see Statistical analysis).

This study was approved by University of Tsukuba Medical Ethics Committee (Approval No: 1820‐1). The questionnaire format explained the secondary use of the survey data as follows: “participants' return of completed questionnaire forms was considered as their agreement to participate in the survey and research use, as explained in the questionnaire.” As the linked database was anonymized, we could not identify participants, nor obtain individual consent.

Measurements

To explore the associations between SES and diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes, we selected the variables regarding the conceptual model proposed by Brown 9 . The Data S1 describes details of the definitions.

SES

The present study defined household income and employment status using the self‐answered information. The questionnaire asked, “What was your total household income, or the combined income of you and your spouse/partner with whom you share a living arrangement, over the last year? Please give the amount in monetary terms before taxes and social security contributions”, with six response options: “<2,000,000 yen,” “2,000,000–4,000,000 yen,” “4,000,000–7,000,000 yen,” “≥7,000,000 yen,” “Unsure” and “No comment.” “Unsure” or “No comment” were treated as missing values; subsequently, we categorized the other answers into three groups: <2 million yen, 2–4 million yen and ≥4 million yen (lower‐/middle‐/higher‐income group). The questionnaire about employment status asked, “What is your job?” with nine response options: “unemployed,” “student,” “homemaker,” “part‐timer,” “others,” “full‐time worker,” “researcher,” “self‐employed” and “full‐time farmer.” We categorized them into three groups based on the assumed time as paid workers: unemployed/student/homemaker, part‐timer/others and full‐time worker/researcher/self‐employed/full‐time farmer (no paid work time/part‐time/full‐time group).

Diabetes care processes

Based on the Japanese clinical guideline and the Standards of Care released by the American Diabetes Association 18 , 19 , we defined the variables as follows: (1) statin use, (2) angiotensin‐converting‐enzyme inhibitors or angiotensin II receptor blockers use, and (3) the following tests carried out in outpatient care at least once a year: HbA1c or glycoalbumin test, eye examinations, general urine test and urine protein quantitative test or urine albumin quantitative test, based on claims data. We also defined relatively expensive antidiabetic medications as prescriptions of sodium–glucose cotransporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors or glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists on claims data for which no generic medications were available. We did not include dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors in the list of relatively expensive antidiabetic medications. Those with type 2 diabetes in Japan tend to receive them as first‐line medication 20 . When exploring the association between relatively expensive antidiabetic medications and SES among those who received antidiabetic treatments, it might not be reasonable to deal with dipeptidyl peptidase‐4 inhibitors in the same way as SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 receptor agonists. Therefore, we considered it necessary to separate them from relatively expensive antidiabetic medications. Additionally, we defined dropout as the absence of diabetes‐related medical treatment (HbA1c or glycoalbumin test or prescription of antidiabetic medications) for ≥4 months on claims data.

Health behaviors

Based on the self‐answered information, we defined the variables as follows: exercise habits – whether the respondent has been exercising for ≥30 min per session at least twice a week for at least 1 year continuously 21 , 22 ; smoking – whether the respondent currently smokes or used to smoke, but has not smoked in the past month; and attendance to annual health checkups carried out by Tsukuba City, school and workplace health checkups, and out‐of‐pocket physical examinations, excluding cancer screenings, for ≥2 years.

Health outcomes

We defined the variables as follows: (1) obesity: determined by whether body mass index was ≥25; (2) self‐reported health: determined by whether the respondent answered “excellent” or “very good” to the question “How is your current health condition?” from among five response options, including “good,” “fair” and “poor” 23 , 24 , 25 ; (3) all‐cause hospitalization; and (4) high total outpatient medical expenses. We defined (1) and (2) based on the self‐answered information, and (3) and (4) based on claims data.

Covariates

We selected covariates in the regression models based on the conceptual model (Figure S2) 9 , and treated all the following variables as categorical variables: sex (men or women, based on claims data); age (20–64, 65–69 and 70–74 years, based on the beneficiary register as of October 2021); family members (alone or ≥2, based on the following survey response options: “Alone,” “Living with family,” “Living with non‐family.”); comorbidity (Charlson Comorbidity Index ≥3 or not, using International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision codes based on claims data 26 ).

Statistical analysis

The survey selected the study participants by stratified random sampling. Therefore, we carried out all statistical analyses using the survey weight calculated from the probability of sampling and response proportion for eight strata by diabetes drug prescription status, type of diabetes (type 1 diabetes disease or not), and age at the survey (Table S1). We used Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp, College Station, TX, USA).

First, we described participant characteristics (sex, age group, SES, family members, comorbidities, number of hospital beds where the participants received antidiabetic medications and whether they received them in FY2021). All variables were categorical and presented in numbers (weighted proportion).

Second, we described diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes. All variables were categorical, and we presented them in numbers (weighted proportion). We used χ2‐tests to compare the proportions within the three groups, respectively. When calculating proportions regarding several variables, we excluded the following populations: for general urine test and urine protein quantitative test or urine albumin quantitative test, we excluded those who received antidiabetic medications at medical facilities with ≥200 beds, because the facilities could underreport urine tests due to a bundled payment rule; for SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 receptor agonists use, we excluded those who did not receive antidiabetic medications in FY2021; for dropout and high total outpatient expenses, we excluded those who experienced hospitalization in FY2021, because it might overestimate dropout, and we could not evaluate the same period for total outpatient expenses.

Third, we used a modified Poisson regression analysis to evaluate the associations between each variable and household income/employment status using the following covariates 27 : sex, age group, family members, comorbidities and employment status. Furthermore, we carried out subgroup analyses based on sex and age group to evaluate the heterogeneity in these associations. Finally, we carried out a sensitivity analysis limiting the participants to those who answered “Yes” to the question, “Have you ever been told by a doctor that you have diabetes?”, and used a modified Poisson regression analysis with a crude model. Although Tsukuba City selected 1,000 people in the survey based on antidiabetic medication use or HbA1c, the questionnaire survey did not inform participants that it targeted only those with diabetes. The awareness of having diabetes might affect some of the associations examined in this study. Therefore, we investigated the robustness of the main results against limiting them to those aware of having diabetes.

Diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes by household income/employment status

Among the three annual household income groups, the higher income group was more likely to use SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 receptor agonists (29.7%, 38.6%, 57.2%, low‐, middle‐, high‐income group, P = 0.030) and attended annual health checkups for ≥2 years (34.4%, 45.1%, 60.0%, P = 0.045), and had a lower likelihood of being hospitalized (20.5%, 15.0%, 3.3%, P = 0.035). The middle‐income group also had the lowest proportion of high total outpatient medical expenses (54.3%, 27.1%, 41.7%, P = 0.009). Regarding the three employment status groups, the full‐time group had the lowest proportion of statin prescriptions (32.8%) and regular exercise (30.9%), and the highest proportion of respondents reporting excellent or very good health (41.9%; Table 2), all of which showed significant differences. Associations of household income/employment status with diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes

Figure 2 shows the results of the bivariate and multivariable regression models. The modified Poisson regression analysis showed significant associations between annual household income and the following variables: SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 receptor agonists use (high‐income vs low‐income: adjusted risk ratio [aRR] 1.77, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.10–2.85), attendance to annual health check‐up for ≥2 years (high‐income in comparison with low‐income: aRR 1.67, 95% CI 1.05–2.64) and all‐cause hospitalization (high‐income in comparison with low‐income: aRR 0.15, 95% CI 0.04–0.51). The low‐income group was more likely to pay high outpatient medical expenses than the middle‐income group (aRR 0.57, 95% CI 0.35–0.92), whereas the difference was insignificant compared with the high‐income group. The following variables were significantly associated with employment status: exercise habits (full‐time in comparison with no paid work time: aRR 0.59, 95% CI 0.35–0.99), good self‐reported health (part‐time in comparison with no paid work time: aRR 1.94, 95% CI 1.14–3.32; full‐time in comparison with no paid work time: aRR 2.08, 95% CI 1.22–3.5). Tables S4 and S5 show the results of the subgroup analyses by sex or age group. The variables that showed significant associations in the overall analysis showed no evidence of association heterogeneity by sex or age group.

In the sensitivity analysis, which restricted the sample to those who were aware of having diabetes, the associations were similar to those in the main analysis (Table S6).

DISCUSSION

The present study comprehensively explored the relationship between SES and diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes using the claims data linked with a questionnaire survey. Consequently, we found several variables on annual household income/employment status. High household income was associated with a high proportion of using SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 receptor agonists, a high proportion of having attendance to annual health checkups, and a low proportion of all‐cause hospitalization; by contrast, the middle‐income group was the least likely to pay high total outpatient medical expenses. The full‐time group had the lowest proportion of exercising at least twice a week for ≥30 min per session for at least 1 year, and the highest proportion of participants who reported good health. These results were consistent in the modified Poisson regression and sensitivity analyses. Conversely, we did not find associations between SES and the other variables, including regular tests, such as eye examinations. To the best our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate associations between annual household income/employment status and diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes by questionnaire surveys linked with claims data in Japan.

In the associations between SES and diabetes care processes, the highest‐income group was more likely to use SGLT2 inhibitors or GLP‐1 receptor agonists. These treatments also positively affect cardiovascular and renal issues 19 , whereas they are expensive and do not contain generic types. When co‐insurance rates of 30% are applied, these treatments cost approximately 40–150 yen per tablet or approximately 450–3,000 yen per injection kit, whereas generic antidiabetic medications cost approximately 2–10 yen per tablet or approximately 250–420 yen per injection kit 28 . Furthermore, in Japan, those who receive GLP‐1 receptor agonists need to pay the home injection fee (approximately 2,000 yen) and an additional fee for self‐monitoring of blood glucose if applicable 29 ; they might be more financially burdened. Previous studies in the USA have reported that lower‐income groups were less likely to use these medications 30 , 31 , which was consistent with the present study. Some people with low incomes might have wanted to use these drugs to prevent chronic renal or heart failure, but were unable to due to cost issues. A previous study reported that people with higher incomes and diabetes were more likely to have better knowledge about chronic kidney disease 32 . The relationship between income and the use of these drugs might be influenced by the knowledge level about diabetes‐related complications. Further study is important to explore the mechanism. This study also showed a positive association between annual household income and attendance at annual health checkups. The previous study reported the income‐based gap for attendance to annual health checkups among social insurance dependents 33 . The present findings imply that a gap in adherence to regular health checkups might affect the timing of detecting complications and comorbidities, resulting in a health gap 34 . In the associations between SES and health behaviors, the full‐time group was the least likely to exercise at least twice a week for ≥30 min per session for at least 1 year. Previous studies in Japan have reported that those with strongly perceived barriers were more likely to be employed 35 . Compared with the no paid work time, the employed might have more difficulty creating exercise habits due to a lack of time.

We also found that SES was related to several health outcomes. Higher income was associated with a lower incidence of all‐cause hospitalization. In Taiwan, socioeconomic gaps, including income, have been reported to play a role in preventable hospitalizations among adults with diabetes 36 . Those with lower incomes might have a more vulnerable health status, including worse glycemic control. However, the severity of the disease with hospitalizations might negatively affect income. Thus, we might not be able to resolve the reverse causality.

The present study also showed that the middle‐income group was the least likely to pay high total outpatient medical expenses. A previous study reported that lower‐income individuals have more diabetes‐related complications 14 and, subsequently, might have higher medical costs for the care of their disease, including complications. Previous reports showing that higher income groups are more likely to be treated with SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP‐1 receptor agonists 30 , 31 might also show that they are spending more on medical care due to their use of relatively expensive medical services.

The full‐time group had the highest proportion of those who reported excellent or very good self‐reported health. Previous studies have reported the association between being employed and good subjective health perception 37 , in line with our finding of an association in participants with diabetes. We could not address the possibility of reverse causality because of the cross‐sectional design. Therefore, further research with longitudinal design is needed to explore a causal relationship.

The present study did not observe significant associations between household income/employment status, regular diabetes‐related tests and dropout. Given that Japan has a universal health insurance system, the results of this study suggest that this system should have reduced the gaps in healthcare access caused by socioeconomic status. Nevertheless, the implementation of a universal health insurance system alone would not be enough to address some of the inequities identified in this study; supplementary measures would be required alongside it. However, these relationships might change with events that could affect society, such as pandemics, including the coronavirus disease 2019, and the revision of medical fees and guidelines. Follow‐up studies are needed to assess whether the currently observed equity will last in the future.

The present study showed associations between SES and several variables, and the variables associated with income differed from those associated with employment status. To close the health inequities due to SES differences among people with diabetes, we need policy interventions focused on high‐risk groups, which should be identified based on evidence as to which indicators of SES are associated with which health factors in those with diabetes. Future studies should carry out a nationwide, longitudinal questionnaire survey using a uniform format. This survey would enable comparisons among cities and prefectures, and assessment of the trend of the gaps over time. It would also consider variables, such as diabetes‐related hospitalizations and onsets of complications, thereby helping build better health policies.

The strength of the present study was the use of a database that merged the questionnaire survey with claims data – in Japanese claims data, not including SES, although medical treatment at multiple medical facilities can be tracked. This study highlighted important and novel findings using the linked database. We believe this is a meaningful step toward improving the health inequities among people with diabetes by SES in Japan.

The present study had several limitations. First, this study was cross‐sectional; thus, we could not adequately examine causal relationships, and found it difficult to evaluate some effect directions between SES and the variables. A previous study reported that cardiovascular events resulted in substantial losses in employment and earnings 38 . Therefore, the effects of some variables in this study on SES might be as important as those of SES on these variables. Furthermore, longitudinal studies need to investigate the effect amalgam and evaluate each impact. Second, the survey period was in FY2022, and the claims data used in this study were from FY2021 (Figure S1). Annual income and employment status might differ between FY2021 (when this study assessed the variables) and the survey period. In the age‐related subgroup analyses, including those aged 65 years, the retirement age, when annual income and employment status might dramatically change, the results were similar to those for all ages (Tables S4 and S5). Third, the variables (including health checkups, exercise and self‐reported health) based on self‐reported questionnaires might include measurement errors, whereas we confirmed the consistency of our findings with previous studies 33 , 35 , 37 . We did not have information on the details of health checkups, which precluded us from identifying the effect of SES on the type of checkup carried out. However, people with diabetes should attend regular health checkups, regardless of type. The variable of household income might be misclassified, because it did not refer to pensions. However, we confirmed the consistency of the results between people aged ≥65 years (who were susceptible to this misclassification) and those who were not. Fourth, the data used in this study did not include educational history, a confounding variable in this study. Fifth, we could not calculate equivalized income, because we could obtain the number of family members. Sixth, although we evaluated the relationships considering comorbidities using the Charlson Comorbidity Index, some residual confounding might exist; we did not control for glycemic control either. Seventh, the questionnaire survey did not receive responses from all participants. Additionally, the survey's weighting might not have sufficiently considered the differences between the group that responded and the group that did not. They could introduce selection bias. For example, there was a difference in the response rate by sex. Future studies should include larger sample sizes and more precise stratification. Eighth, due to the small sample size, this study could not investigate the details of hospitalizations. Furthermore, the results of the individual analyses of GLP‐1 receptor agonists use presented in Tables S2 and S3, as well as the subgroup analyses presented in Tables S4 and S5, should be interpreted with particular caution due to the small sample size. Finally, our source population was from Tsukuba City. The present findings might not be generalizable to other communities.

In conclusion, the present study showed that several variables in diabetes care processes, health behaviors and health outcomes were significantly associated with household income/employment status, whereas other variables were not. The use of the linked database could help alleviate some inequities. Additional aids should be provided to the groups based on these associations and further research carried out, including nationwide and longitudinal studies.
