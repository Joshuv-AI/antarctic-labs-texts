AI‐Based Analysis of Tumor‐Infiltrating Lymphocytes and Homologous Recombination in Ovarian Cancer: JGOG3025‐A1 Study
Authors: Kohei Hamada, Junzo Hamanishi, Noriomi Matsumura, Akihiko Ueda, Shiro Takamatsu, Kosuke Yoshihara, Takayuki Nagasawa, Toshiyuki Seki, Akira Kikuchi, Etsuko Fujimoto, Mana Taki, Koji Yamanoi, Ryusuke Murakami, Hisaaki Kudo, Katsutoshi Oda, Muneaki Shimada, Aikou Okamoto, Masaki Mandai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTUnderstanding the tumor immune microenvironment, especially tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs), remains crucial in ovarian cancer. However, the distribution and prognostic significance of TILs across histological subtypes and genetic backgrounds remain unclear. As part of the JGOG3025‐A1 study, diagnostic slides from 400 cases were collected. Two artificial intelligence‐based cell classification models were used to evaluate the spatial distribution of TILs. The TIL score was calculated as the number of TILs divided by the analyzed area, and the immune‐inflamed group was defined based on TIL scores. Among histological subtypes, high‐grade serous carcinoma (HGSC) exhibited the highest TIL scores, clear cell carcinoma the lowest, and endometrioid carcinoma intermediate values. In HGSC, TIL scores did not significantly differ according to BRCA alteration or homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) status. The HRD/immune‐inflamed group had the most favorable prognosis. In the homologous recombination‐proficient population, the immune‐inflamed group had better progression‐free survival, whereas this trend did not appear for overall survival. In analyzes of the relationships between TIL levels and genomic structure, whole‐genome doubling was associated with lower TIL infiltration in HRD tumors but showed no association in HRP tumors. In conclusion, HGSC showed the highest amount of TIL infiltration among subtypes, and prognostic stratification can be achieved by integrating pathology‐based immunophenotypes and HRD status.Keywords: artificial intelligence, deep learning, digital pathology, ovarian cancer, tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes

Introduction

Ovarian cancer remains one of the most lethal gynecological malignancies worldwide [1, 2], with heterogeneous outcomes influenced significantly by tumor microenvironment characteristics, particularly tumor‐infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [3, 4]. TILs are recognized as a key determinant of antitumor immunity and have been associated with prognosis across multiple cancer types [5, 6]. However, the precise role and prognostic implications of TILs in ovarian cancer, particularly across different histological subtypes and genetic backgrounds, remain incompletely understood [7, 8].

High‐grade serous carcinoma (HGSC), the most prevalent histologic subtype, has homologous recombination deficiency (HRD) in more than half of cases and harbors germline or somatic BRCA1/2 mutations in approximately 20% of cases [9]. HGSC also demonstrates marked intratumoral heterogeneity and chromosomal instability [10, 11], along with the highest levels of TIL infiltration among histologic subtypes [8, 12, 13]. Numerous studies have established intraepithelial CD8+ TILs as a favorable prognostic factor in HGSC [5, 6], which our previous artificial intelligence (AI)‐based quantification pipeline also supported [14, 15]. Additionally, the association between genetic alterations—such as BRCA mutations or HRD—and TIL infiltration has been widely investigated. Although several studies have reported increased TIL infiltration in tumors with BRCA1 mutations [7, 16], the relationship between TILs and BRCA2 mutations or HRD driven by non‐BRCA homologous recombination pathway aberrations remains controversial [17, 18, 19]. Furthermore, only a limited number of studies have explored the prognostic significance of TILs in conjunction with BRCA or HRD status [20, 21].

Conventional histopathologic assessment remains vulnerable to inter‐observer and site‐selection bias, whereas AI‐based quantification offers improved reproducibility and accuracy. Recent advances in digital pathology have enabled joint nuclear segmentation and phenotypic classification on whole‐slide images, facilitating cell‐level quantification of TILs directly from hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) sections [22, 23, 24]. Applying these high‐resolution approaches to large cohorts may help resolve previously reported inconsistencies.

The JGOG3025 study (NCT03159572) prospectively collected clinical data from 701 patients with epithelial ovarian cancer [25]. As part of the JGOG3025‐A1 study, 400 cases of digitized H&E‐stained whole‐slide images (WSIs) were newly collected to enable a comprehensive evaluation of TIL distribution across histological subtypes, using two state‐of‐the‐art AI‐based cell classification models [22, 26]. For HGSC, the association between TILs and genetic background was further assessed using a previously established large multiomics dataset (JGOG3025‐TR2 dataset) [27, 28]. Building on our previous AI‐based TIL studies, the present work further refined AI‐based TIL assessment at single‐cell resolution and integrated AI‐derived immune phenotypes with HRD‐related genomic features, including BRCA‐related alterations, copy number variation (CNV) signature‐based clustering, and whole‐genome doubling (WGD) status.

Materials and Methods

Study Cohort and Datasets

The JGOG3025 study, conducted by the Japanese Gynecologic Oncology Group (JGOG), included patients with epithelial ovarian cancer whose treatment commenced between March 2017 and March 2019 across 55 affiliated institutions [25]. A total of 701 cases were included in the JGOG3025‐whole cohort following central pathological review. In the JGOG3025 study, targeted sequencing of 51 genes (Table S1), including DNA repair‐related genes and genes frequently mutated in ovarian cancer, was performed using frozen tumor samples. The mutational heterogeneity index (mutant‐allele tumor heterogeneity [MATH] score), tumor mutational burden (TMB) status, and microsatellite instability (MSI) status were retrieved from a previous study. Briefly, MSI status was estimated from targeted DNA sequencing using MSIsensor2, and tumors classified as TMB‐high were defined as those exhibiting an equivalent number of mutations as MSI‐high tumors.

As a correlative study, the JGOG3025‐A1 study involved 28 centers and acquired H&E‐stained slides from 400 cases. The slides were digitally scanned with a NanoZoomer Digital Pathology System (Hamamatsu Photonics, Hamamatsu, Japan) at 20× magnification (resolution of 0.5 μm per pixel). After excluding two cases with metastatic lymph node samples and 17 cases with insufficient tumor tissue (< 10 mm2), 381 cases were included in the final JGOG3025‐A1 cohort. As previous reports demonstrated distinct TIL infiltration patterns between primary and metastatic samples [14, 29], this analysis focused on primary tumor samples (n = 314). Clinical characteristics of the JGOG3025‐A1 study cohort are summarized in Table 1. In parallel, the JGOG3025‐TR2 study involved 274 and 15 cases of International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) stage IIB–IV HGSC and high‐grade endometrioid carcinoma (HGEC), respectively, which underwent single‐nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) array profiling, total RNA sequencing, and DNA methylation profiling [27, 28]. Among these cases, 151 overlapped with the JGOG3025‐A1 cohort and were incorporated into the present analysis.

The TCGA‐OV dataset consisted of 106 WSIs. After excluding four cases without TP53 mutations (not classifiable as HGSC) [14, 30], one case not derived from formalin‐fixed, paraffin‐embedded (FFPE) tissue, and four cases with insufficient tumor tissue (< 10 mm2), 97 cases were included in the TCGA cohort. SNP array, total RNA sequencing, and DNA methylation array data were also obtained from the TCGA‐OV archive. The Kyoto immunohistochemistry (IHC) cohort consists of 20 HGSC samples that include both H&E and CD8 IHC WSIs, as previously described [14]. The Kyoto evaluation cohort consists of nine patients with HGSC from Kyoto University Hospital and includes five WSIs derived from three different FFPE blocks of primary samples.

AI‐Based Nuclei Segmentation and Classification

Two state‐of‐the‐art AI models were employed for nuclear detection and cell‐type classification (Figure 1A). The Cell Vision Transformer (CellViT) is a vision transformer–based model with a convolutional backbone that was pretrained on large collections of histopathology tiles and subsequently trained on the PanNuke dataset, performing five‐way classification into neoplastic, inflammatory, connective/soft tissue, dead/necrotic, and epithelial cell types [22, 23]. The HoVer‐NeXt model is a convolutional neural network–based architecture that jointly predicts horizontal–vertical (HoVer) maps and semantic labels, and was trained on a modified version of the Lizard dataset for multiclass nuclear segmentation, providing seven‐class output comprising epithelial cells, connective cells, lymphocytes, plasma cells, neutrophils, eosinophils, and mitotic figures [26]. In this study, we used the publicly released pretrained weights and applied both models with their default configurations. No additional training or fine‐tuning was performed in the present study. No parameter tuning, case‐wise resampling, or cross‐validation was performed on the present dataset. CellViT inference was performed using the pretrained CellViT‐SAM‐H‐x20 model at 20× magnification with a patch size of 1024 pixels, 6.25% patch overlap, Macenko stain normalization, and a minimum tissue intersection ratio of 0.05. For HoVer‐NeXt, we used the publicly available pretrained lizard_convnextv2_large weights and performed inference with the default settings of the official inference repository, including test‐time augmentation (TTA = 4), a tile size of 256 pixels, and tile overlap of 0.96875.

Preliminary evaluation revealed a consistent pattern in which CellViT misclassified stromal fibroblasts as neoplastic cells across the dataset (Figure 1B). To avoid potential overcalling of tumor regions, tumor cells were defined only when CellViT predicted a cell as “neoplastic” and HoVer‐NeXt simultaneously predicted the same cell as “epithelial.” Tumor regions were then delineated based on the spatial coordinates of these jointly identified tumor cells (Figure 1C). Quantitatively, the tumor area after HoVer‐NeXt correction was 83.7% of the CellViT‐derived tumor area on average in primary HGSC tumors, indicating that 16.3% of the initially labeled tumor region was excluded (range, 0.4%–73.5%). To provide pragmatic validation of tumor‐region delineation, a gynecologic pathologist (Dr. Tomoyuki Otani) manually annotated representative regions from five selected HGSC cases, and the agreement with the AI‐derived tumor regions was high on average (Dice score, 0.961; intersection over union [IoU], 0.926), supporting the plausibility of the consensus tumor‐cell definition (Figure S1A).

Lymphocytes predicted by HoVer‐NeXt underwent further spatial classification. Lymphocytes residing inside tumor regions were categorized as intratumoral TILs (iTILs), and lymphocytes located within 1000 μm of the tumor boundary were categorized as stromal TILs (sTILs), in accordance with previous studies [14, 31]. Specifically, the 1000‐μm (1‐mm) peritumoral distance is recommended for ovarian cancer in a standardized TIL assessment framework, and was also adopted in our previous studies [14, 32]; therefore we applied the same threshold for consistency and comparability. TIL scores were calculated as the number of lymphocytes divided by the corresponding regional area. Patients were stratified into an immune‐inflamed group or a noninflamed group based on the upper tertile of the iTIL score, as previously described [14].

Immunohistochemistry and CD8 + T‐Cell Quantification in the Kyoto IHC Cohort

CD8 IHC staining was performed on 20 slides from the Kyoto IHC cohort as previously described [3, 14]. The HALO digital pathology analysis platform (Indica Labs) was utilized to quantify CD8+ T cells using the “Indica Labs‐CytoNuclear v2.0.9” module.

Analysis of HGSC Cases

HRD scores were computed from SNP array data using the scarHRD algorithm and were defined as the sum of large‐scale state transitions (LST), telomeric allelic imbalance (TAI), and loss of heterozygosity (LOH) scores [27, 28, 33]. HRD scores derived from SNP array have been reported to show strong correlation with scores obtained from the United States Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved HRD test, while tending to produce higher values [34]. Accordingly, a cutoff of 67 was adopted for the HRD score to distinguish between HRD and homologous recombination–proficient (HRP), as previously validated [27, 28]. Segmented allele‐specific copy number variations, estimated tumor ploidy, and tumor purity were calculated using ASCAT from the SNP array data, and WGD status was retrieved from the output of ascat.metrics function [35]. Gene mutation profiles, BRCA1 promoter methylation status, and LOH information for genes involved in the homologous recombination repair pathway were obtained from previous work [27, 28]. Additionally, tumor subtypes based on CNV signatures were also obtained from previous study results [27]. Single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) was performed using immune cell signatures described previously [14]. The “consensusOV” R package was used to classify each tumor into four molecular subtypes: immune‐reactive (IMR), mesenchymal (MES), differentiated (DIF), and proliferative (PRO) [36].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyzes were performed using Python 3.10. The correlation of continuous variables was assessed by Spearman's rank correlation coefficient. Medians of continuous variables were compared between groups by the Mann–Whitney U test or the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, without formal adjustment for multiple testing, as most comparisons were prespecified and biologically motivated. Cumulative survival probabilities were calculated using the Kaplan–Meier method. Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis and the log‐rank test were used to calculate p values, hazard ratios (HRs), and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Overall survival (OS) and progression‐free survival (PFS) were defined as the duration from initial treatment to death and recurrence or last follow‐up, respectively. Two‐sided p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant in all analyzes.

Results

Validation of Models' Performance

Validation of AI‐based TIL detection accuracy involved comparing AI‐derived TIL counts with CD8+ cell counts using 20 matched H&E and CD8 IHC slides from the Kyoto IHC cohort, which had been used in a previous report [14]. AI‐derived TIL counts showed a significant correlation with CD8+ cell counts (R = 0.57, p = 0.008), supporting the validity of AI predictions (Figure S1B). The number of slides required per case for reliable quantification was assessed using nine HGSC cases from the Kyoto evaluation cohort, using slides created from three different FFPE blocks of adnexal lesions. For one FFPE block, three sequential sections were prepared. Sequential slides yielded nearly identical TIL quantification results. Moreover, inter‐block comparisons showed that intrapatient TIL trends remained preserved (Figure S1C). Based on these findings, the slide with the largest tumor area was selected as the representative slide when multiple slides were available.

TIL Distribution Among Subtypes

Assessment of TIL distribution across histological subtypes in the overall JGOG3025‐A1 cohort is shown in Figures 2A and S2A. For both iTIL and sTIL scores, HGSC showed the highest values, followed by endometrioid carcinoma (EC), whereas clear cell carcinoma (CCC) showed the lowest scores (HGSC vs. EC: iTIL p = 0.006; sTIL p < 0.001; HGSC vs. CCC: both p < 0.001) (Figure 2B). Limited numbers of MSI‐high tumors showed higher TIL scores, and TMB‐high tumors exhibited significantly elevated TIL scores (Figure 2C). HGSC features pronounced intratumoral heterogeneity and chromosomal instability, which has been reported to activate antitumor immunity through the cGAS–STING pathway [37, 38]. Consistent with this biology, MATH score was highest in HGSC—mirroring the TIL patterns—when compared with EC and CCC (HGSC vs. EC, p < 0.001; HGSC vs. CCC, p < 0.001) (Figure 2D).

TIL Distribution According to HRD Status

The analysis next focused on HGSC, with the incorporation of TCGA cohort to enhance statistical power. Prior work classified HGSC into four CNV signature–based clusters (C1–C4), with C4 representing a molecularly defined HGEC‐like subtype that remained pathologically indistinguishable from HGSC [27]. Because C4 cases are biologically distinct from “pure” HGSC and are uniformly HRP, we considered them a potential source of confounding in analyzes of the relationship between HRD status and TIL infiltration. When TIL scores were compared across clusters, both iTIL and sTIL scores were lowest in the C4 cluster (p = 0.046 and 0.014, respectively) (Figure 3A). Examining the relationship between TIL and HRD scores across all cases—including C4—revealed weak but significant correlations (p = 0.046 and 0.035) (Figure 3B). However, in a “pure” HGSC set excluding the C4 cluster, these associations were no longer significant (p = 0.14 and 0.15), indicating no relationship between TIL infiltration and HRD score or HRD status (Figures 3B and S3A). Further assessment of C4‐excluded cases explored associations with BRCA and other HRD‐related genes (Figure 3C). We examined BRCA‐related categories in detail, including BRCA1/2 mutation with LOH of the wild‐type allele, BRCA1 promoter methylation, BRCA1/2 alterations without LOH of the wild‐type allele, and cases without detectable BRCA alteration. No significant association was observed between BRCA mutation status and TIL scores (Figure 3D). At the transcriptomic level, both iTIL and sTIL correlated well with immune‐related pathway expression (Figure S3B). For gene expression–based molecular subtypes defined by consensus‐OV, the IMR subtype showed significantly higher TIL scores (p = 0.003 and 0.014) (Figure S3C). To assess the robustness of the spatial definition of stromal TILs, we repeated key analyzes using an alternative 500‐μm cutoff. The overall results were largely unchanged (Figure S3D–F).

Prognostic Impact of TILs on HGSC

The analysis next examined the prognostic implications of TIL infiltration in advanced‐stage HGSC. Spatial assessment of TIL distribution relative to the tumor margin demonstrated an association between iTILs and favorable outcomes, whereas sTILs located farther from the tumor edge tended to correspond with shorter PFS (Figure 4A). An immunophenotypic classification that defined the top tertile of iTIL scores as the immune‐inflamed subtype produced a favorable trend in PFS for the immune‐inflamed group, whereas OS did not differ significantly (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.46–1.06; p = 0.09 for PFS; HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.61–1.63; p = 0.99 for OS) (Figure 4B). Subgroup analyzes consistently demonstrated better PFS outcomes for immune‐inflamed tumors (Figure S4A).

A stratified analysis based on HRD status and immune subtype revealed that the HRD/immune‐inflamed group exhibited the most favorable prognosis for both PFS and OS. An opposite directional pattern between OS and PFS emerged for immune‐inflamed tumors within the HRP population (Figure 4C). Multivariate analysis including stage IV disease, residual tumor, age, HRD status, immune‐inflamed phenotype, and bevacizumab use showed that the immune‐inflamed subtype remained a significant prognostic factor for PFS, whereas OS did not show a statistically significant difference (Figure 4D). Inclusion of the small number of advanced‐stage C4 cases did not materially alter the overall prognostic pattern (Figure S4B,C).

Additional analysis explored relationships between TIL levels and genomic structure in separate HRD and HRP groups. Although chromosomal instability can activate antitumor immunity via the cGAS–STING pathway, high levels of WGD in HGSC have been reported to suppress cGAS–STING signaling and antitumor immunity [37, 39]. When stratified by HRD status, WGD was significantly associated with lower TIL levels in the HRD group (p = 0.006 and 0.02 for iTIL and sTIL, respectively), whereas no association was observed in the HRP group (Figure 4E).

Discussion

Utilizing single‐cell–resolution cell classification models, the spatial distribution and prognostic relevance of TILs across different histological subtypes of ovarian cancer were analyzed. In HGSC, AI‐based TIL quantification supported the prognostic relevance of the immune‐inflamed phenotype and revealed distinct survival patterns when interpreted together with HRD‐related genomic context. The present analysis also enabled a more refined evaluation of genomic heterogeneity, including BRCA‐related alterations, CNV signature–defined subgroups, and WGD status.

Previous studies have reported an association between BRCA mutations—particularly BRCA1—and high levels of TIL infiltration, which contrasts with the present findings [7, 16]. Most previous assessments have relied on semiquantitative evaluation in selected hotspots [7, 16]. In contrast, this study quantitatively evaluated immune cells across WSIs. Additionally, this study included more detailed BRCA alteration status, including LOH of the wild‐type allele. Despite this level of detail, statistically significant differences in TIL levels were not observed. Similarly, the relationship between TIL abundance and HRD status has been controversial [16, 17]. Discrepancies in the methods used to assess HRD and in the cutoff values applied across studies and trials may partly explain these inconsistencies. Moreover, HGSC includes a C4 cluster that is pathologically HGSC and molecularly more HGEC‐like; these cases are universally HRP and exhibit markedly low TIL levels [27]. In a “pure HGSC” cohort excluding the C4 cluster, the correlation between HRD and TIL disappears (Figure 3B). This finding suggests that previously reported associations may have been confounded by inclusion of this distinct subtype. These findings suggest that immune cell distribution may not represent a characteristic of BRCA‐mutated or HRD tumors.

The favorable prognostic impact of the immune‐inflamed phenotype in HGSC observed in this study aligns with previous findings [14, 40]. Additional stratification achieved by incorporating HRD status also aligns with earlier reports [21]. Notably, these associations were validated in larger cohorts using a high‐resolution, AI‐based approach for quantitative immune profiling. Interestingly, within the HRD population, the inflamed group consistently showed better prognosis, whereas within the HRP population, the inflamed group was associated with improved PFS and worse OS. Moreover, although excessive WGD has been reported to suppress antitumor immunity [37], the present data demonstrated reduced TILs associated with WGD only in the HRD subset. This pattern may partly explain why TILs were associated with favorable prognostic implications for both PFS and OS in HRD tumors. In contrast, because WGD did not correlate with TIL levels in HRP tumors, alternative regulatory pathways may influence the immune environment in HRP disease, which may contribute to the attenuated or discordant prognostic signal of TILs in this subgroup. Nevertheless, the discordant associations of the immune‐inflamed phenotype with PFS and OS in HRP tumors should be interpreted cautiously. Because this was an exploratory subgroup analysis with a limited number of events, the observed pattern may reflect statistical instability. In addition, OS is more strongly influenced than PFS by postprogression duration, subsequent lines of therapy, and other clinical factors not fully captured in the present dataset.

This study has the strength of quantitatively assessing TILs using H&E‐stained slides from a large, prospectively collected, and relatively recent ovarian cancer cohort with detailed clinical information. A key limitation arises from the cohort composition, which included patients treated before poly(ADP‐ribose) polymerase inhibitors (PARPi) became widely available as first‐line maintenance therapy. As such, no patients received PARPi during initial treatment. Consequently, the prognostic implications of immune phenotype and HRD status in the context of PARPi maintenance warrant further investigation. An additional limitation is that both CellViT and HoVer‐NeXt were pretrained on nonovarian‐specific datasets, and domain mismatch may therefore have affected cell‐type classification and tumor‐region delineation in ovarian cancer tissues. Another limitation relates to the lack of mechanistic clarification regarding how TILs exhibit divergent prognostic effects for PFS and OS in the HRP population. Future work incorporating spatial immune profiling in PARPi‐treated cohorts may help clarify these mechanisms and define clinical utility.

In conclusion, the present findings suggest that pathology‐based immune phenotypes, interpreted together with HRD‐related context, may provide additional prognostic insight in HGSC. Although these findings should be regarded as prognostic and hypothesis‐generating rather than immediately practice‐changing, they provide new insight into the tumor immune microenvironment in ovarian cancer and highlight the importance of integrating histological features and genetic background into immune profiling.
