Microstructured chitosan mesh scaffold for efficient production of cell-cultured meat
Authors: Jiacheng Xu, Takeshi Hori, Kennedy Omondi Okeyo, Buntaro Tsurumi, Shotaro Yoshida, Yuji Nashimoto, Hirokazu Kaji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe rising global demand for sustainable food solutions has been a key driver of significant advancements in cell-cultured meat technology. However, challenges remain in efficiently inducing muscle cell maturation and constructing layered structures that closely resemble natural meat. In this study, we developed a novel microstructured chitosan mesh scaffold to address these challenges, with a focus on promoting cell alignment and minimizing non-cellular components in the final product. Chitosan, a biocompatible and edible polymer derived from chitin, was processed into customizable micromesh sheets to support three-dimensional cell culture. Incorporating gelatin into the chitosan matrix significantly enhanced the adhesion of C2C12 mouse myoblasts to the mesh sheets. Cell culture experiments demonstrated that the chitosan-gelatin mesh scaffold supports robust cell proliferation, leading to the formation of uniform cell sheets. Notably, the rhombus-shaped mesh openings effectively promoted cell alignment, a critical factor for inducing muscle cell maturation and replicating the anisotropic structure of natural muscle fibers. This approach has the potential to not only reduce the scaffolding material-to-cell ratio but also facilitate the creation of muscle fibers with enhanced structural and textural properties.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46287-2.Keywords: Chitosan, 3D cell culture, Cell sheet, Cell cultured meatSubject terms: Cell adhesion, Cell growth

Introduction

According to the United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), the global population is projected to reach approximately 9 billion by 2050, requiring a 70% increase in food production to meet future demand1. However, achieving this goal is increasingly challenging due to limited arable land, resource depletion, and the adverse effects of climate change2. Hunger remains a persistent global issue. In 2023, an estimated 733 million people were undernourished, highlighting the inability of traditional agricultural systems to meet global food demand effectively3. This urgent situation emphasizes the need for innovative solutions to ensure global food security. Among these, cell cultured, meat also known as lab-grown or cell-based meat is attracting attention as a sustainable food supply system4.

Cell cultured meat is produced by artificially culturing cells in vitro5. This technology has the potential to not only meet the demand for proteins, but also to mitigate the adverse environmental impacts of current food systems5–7. Cell cultured meat can also address the growing global call for animal welfare. In December 2020, Singapore became the first country in the world to approve the sale of cultured chicken meat (cultured chicken nuggets)8,9. Indeed, the number of companies focused on the business of cultured meat development or production has grown significantly, rising from approximately 10 startups in 2016 to 98 businesses by December 202210, and products such as chicken, beef, and fish meat are in development for future commercialization11–13.

However, although the development of cultured meat has progressed globally with notable advancements in the production of minced meat, research efforts directed toward improving the tissue structure of cultured meat to more closely resemble real muscle are still lacking. Clearly there is need to develop artificial meat that mimics the texture and properties of conventional meat, since currently available cell-cultured meat are lacking the structure of real meat, especially the muscle architecture. Given that native tissues, such as skeletal muscles, tendons, and ligaments, exhibit distinct alignments that are essential for their mechanical properties and biological functions, replicating these anisotropic (aligned) structures is vital to the development of high quality cell cultured meat.

For this reason, researchers have employed various techniques including temperature-responsive polymers combined with traditional cell alignment methods such as micropatterning and micro/nano grooves to achieve this feat14,15. Furuhashi et al. made strides in this field by developing cell-cultured meat composed of bundled cylindrical gel sheets16. These gel sheets can be layered to produce cultured meat, enabling real-time observation of cells under a microscope during cultivation16. This method promotes the alignment of cells within the rod-shaped gel, facilitating the formation of muscle fibers that closely mimic the texture of real meat. However, the high proportion of gels used in this approach results in a low cell-to-material ratio, potentially reducing the nutritional value of the final product due to the low cellular composition. An overly large scaffold volume can cause physical isolation between cells, hindering the formation of dense, continuous tissue and leading to a lack of cells in the construct. To successfully mimic the structure of natural muscle, the proportion of scaffold material must be minimized to promote cell-cell contact and fusion, ensuring that the final construct is primarily composed of cells rather than synthetic materials. Scaffolds with a high material volume fraction inherently exhibit a dense architecture and low porosity. This physically impedes crucial cellular processes such as spreading, migration, and proliferation, while severely limiting the diffusion of nutrients and the removal of metabolic waste, thereby compromising cell viability17. Beyond these biophysical constraints, such dense scaffolds often present an abnormally high matrix stiffness. This can generate aberrant mechanotransductive cues that misdirect cellular differentiation, particularly for stem cells, precluding the formation of the desired tissue type18. Furthermore, a high material incorporation rate can create a statically dense microenvironment that inhibits endogenous tissue remodeling. Cells are unable to effectively reorganize or enzymatically degrade the scaffold to create space for new extracellular matrix (ECM) deposition, leading to the stagnation of tissue morphogenesis19.

Therefore, a critical challenge in scaffold design is to develop platforms that minimize the material incorporation rate. The objective is to create a highly porous architecture that provides a permissive microenvironment, ensuring robust cell viability and function while facilitating dynamic tissue integration, thus avoiding the well-documented drawbacks associated with excessive scaffold material. Similarly, Kang et al. employed a 3D bioprinter to create muscle fibers12. While this technique allows for precise fiber placement, it is labor-intensive, as each fiber must be manually arranged. Furthermore, the mixing of cells with 3D printer materials leads to a high scaffolding material-to-cell ratio, resulting in excess of non-cellular materials in the final product. Both methods face significant challenges due to the substantial presence of scaffold material. High scaffolding material-to-cell ratio complicates the production process and limits the proportion of cellular components, highlighting the need for further innovations to overcome these issues.

We previously developed a novel cell culture technique called the micromesh cultures20–23, which utilizes a microstructured mesh sheet with a high opening ratio as a scaffold for cell culture. In this method, cells are cultured on a suspended mesh sheet, where they attach to the fine mesh lines. As the cells proliferate, they fill the mesh openings, forming a uniform cell sheet. A distinctive feature of this technique is that the cells within the mesh opening primarily rely on intercellular adhesion, closely mimicking the natural state of cells in the body. This contrasts with traditional cell culture on plastic substrates, where cell attachment depends on the surface adhesion. Due to this unique property, the mesh culture method enables cells to adopt a three-dimensional configuration, facilitating the production of thick cell sheets with relative ease. The mesh sheets developed to date feature line widths of approximately 5–20 microns, with mesh openings measuring 100–200 microns in diameter. The shape of the openings, such as rhombus or rectangular designs, can be freely customized, allowing precise control over cell alignment. This ability to align cells is particularly important for creating muscle fibers in cultured meat. Another key advantage of the mesh culture method is its ability to reduce the amount of scaffold material in the final cell sheet. This is especially important for applications like cultured meat, where reducing non-cellular components is essential to improve the product’s nutritional value and texture. However, the mesh sheets we have developed thus far are made from materials such as polyethylene terephthalate (PET), SU-8, nickel, or polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), which are not edible and therefore unsuitable for use in cultured meat production20–26.

In this study, we utilized chitosan to develop a micromesh scaffold, leveraging its ease of processing, strength, biocompatibility, and non-cytotoxic properties. Chitosan, derived from chitin, the primary structural component of crab and shrimp shells, is an edible and widely used biomass material. It offers unique attributes such as biocompatibility, biodegradability, non-toxicity, antimicrobial activity, hemostatic properties, and non-antigenicity, making it highly versatile for various applications, including dietary supplements27–30. The composite fibers were fabricated using a blend of chitosan and gelatin to leverage their complementary properties. While chitosan provides the necessary mechanical integrity and biodegradability, it lacks specific cell recognition sites. Gelatin addresses this limitation by introducing Arginine-Glycine-Aspartic acid (RGD) sequences, which are essential ligands for integrin binding, thereby significantly enhancing cell adhesion and proliferation. We successfully cultured mouse myoblast C2C12 cells on the chitosan mesh scaffolds, enabling us to produce cell sheets while reducing the proportion of scaffold material in the final cell sheet. Moreover, by designing the mesh openings in a rhombic shape, we achieved the alignment of C2C12 cells, facilitating the efficient formation of oriented muscle sheets. This innovation simplifies the creation of aligned muscle tissue, making the mesh culture technique a valuable tool for applications requiring structured muscle fibers. This chitosan-based mesh culture technique not only marks a significant step forward in the development of cultured meat but also holds significant potential for broader applications, including regenerative medicine and artificial organ engineering.

Materials and methods

Fabrication of SU-8 concave molds

Concave molds with multiple pillars were fabricated using SU-8, a chemically amplified, epoxy-based negative photoresist conventionally employed for producing ultrathick structures during device manufacturing in the semiconductor industry. It was fabricated using conventional photolithography to produce a convex mold of polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) (Fig. 1A-1). The height of the SU-8 concave patterns was set to 50 μm on the wafer. For silanization to strengthen the mold, the SU-8 concave mold was placed in an RVD-300 molding vacuum desiccator along with a container containing a small amount of Trichloro(1 H,1 H,2 H,2 H-perfluorooctyl) silane (Sigma-Aldrich). The desiccator was evacuated and left under vacuum for 4 h to allow the silane to coat the surface of the concave mold.

Fabrication of PDMS convex molds

Using the silanized SU-8 concave mold, a convex PDMS mold was fabricated via soft lithography (Fig. 1A(ii),B(ii)). To prepare the PDMS solution (Silpot 184; Toray-Dow Corning, Tokyo, Japan), the silpot 184 silicone elastomer base and curing agent were mixed at a 10:1 ratio. The prepared PDMS solution was poured into the silanized SU-8 concave mold and degassed to remove air bubbles (Fig. 1A-2). The PDMS was then heat-cured overnight at 60–70 °C. Once cured, the PDMS convex mold was carefully cut out using a scalpel (Fig. 1A-3).

Fabrication of 8 w/w% chitosan mesh sheets

Chitosan powder (#LLP, Kimica Co.) was used to prepare the chitosan solution. The chitosan had a deacetylation degree of 70% or higher and a particle size of 250 μm. To prepare an approximately 9 v/v% acetic acid solution, 10 mL of deionized water was mixed with 1 mL of acetic acid solution (Wako). One gram of chitosan powder was then added to the prepared acetic acid solution and mixed. Using a conditioning mixer (Thinky Co., Ltd., Model: AR-100), the mixture was stirred until the chitosan powder was fully dissolved, followed by degassing for more than 10 min. The final chitosan solution contained approximately 8 w/w% chitosan.

To coat the chitosan solution on the PDMS convex molds, the hydrophilicity of the PDMS mold surface was enhanced by treatment with oxygen plasma for 30 s using a plasma irradiator (PM100, Yamato Scientific) (Fig. 1A-4). The prepared chitosan solution was then spin-coated onto the PDMS mold using a spin coater (Active Co., Ltd., Model: ACT-220 A II). The spin-coating parameters were set at a rotation speed of 2500 rpm for 20 s (Fig. 1A-5). After spin coating, the mold was dried overnight at 50–60 °C. The spin-coated PDMS mold was immersed in 1 M sodium hydroxide solution in a dish for more than 5 min for neutralization of excess acetic acid and coagulation31 (Fig. 1A-6). The chitosan mesh sheet was then carefully peeled off from the PDMS mold using tweezers. Subsequently, it was washed twice with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS)(-) and more than three times with deionized water, and then air-dried at 70 °C (Fig. 1A-7). Finally, chitosan mesh sheets were peeled off from the dish using tweezers (Fig. 1A-8). Through these procedures, chitosan mesh sheets with rhombus-shaped or square-shaped apertures—hereafter referred to as rhombus-mesh or square-mesh, respectively—were fabricated. The acute angle of the rhombus-shaped apertures was designed to be 50°, with a line width of 50 μm and a porosity of 79%, featuring a minor diagonal of 200 μm (Fig. 2A(i)). The chitosan mesh sheets were imaged using a phase-contrast microscope (CKX53, Olympus). Thickness measurements were performed using either a digimatic indicator (Mitutoyo ID-C112XBS) or a measuring microscope (HISOMET, Union Optical).

Fabrication of 8 w/w% chitosan/6 w/v% gelatin mesh sheets

To enhance the cell adhesiveness of the chitosan mesh sheet, gelatin was added to a chitosan solution before the step of spin coating of chitosan onto the PDMS convex molds. To make it possible, first 20 v/v% acetic acid solution was prepared by mixing 2.5 mL of acetic acid solution and 10 mL of deionized water in a 100 mL beaker. Gradually, 2.5 g of chitosan powder was added to the mixture under continuous stirring. The solution was thoroughly mixed using a conditioning mixer until the chitosan was fully dissolved, resulting in a 16 w/w% chitosan solution. Next, to prepare a 12 w/v% gelatin solution, 6 g of gelatin powder was gradually added to deionized water, and the total volume was adjusted to 50 mL. The solution was stirred continuously using a magnetic stirrer while being heated to 60 °C to ensure complete dissolution. Afterward, the gelatin solution was maintained at 30 °C by using a hot plate to prevent premature gelation.

Equal volumes of the chitosan and gelatin solutions were combined in a 1:1 ratio and thoroughly mixed using a conditioning mixer to create a uniform, transparent blend. The PDMS substrate underwent plasma treatment for 2.5 min before being spin-coated with the mixture at 4,000 rpm for 20 s. The coated substrate was then left to dry at room temperature for 1 h. The mesh was detached by immersing the coated substrate in 1 M NaOH solution for 3 min in a dish. After detachment, the mesh was rinsed three times with deionized water to remove residual NaOH. Finally, the mesh was dried on a hot plate at 70 °C for 10 min before being carefully removed from the dish.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis

Chemical structure analysis of the samples was performed using a Fourier Transform Infrared Spectrophotometer (FT/IR-4700 type A, JASCO Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). Spectra were acquired using the Attenuated Total Reflection (ATR) method with a single-reflection ATR accessory (ATR PRO ONE) at an incident angle of 45°. A TGS detector was employed, and measurements were conducted in the wavenumber range of 4000–500 cm−1 with a resolution of 4 cm−1 and 32 scans per sample. A Cosine apodization function was applied for data processing.

Attachment of chitosan mesh sheet onto a frame for cell culture

A tape of 0.069 mm thick Kapton tape 52 (Nitto Denko, P-221 AMB) was cut using biopsy punches with diameters of 8 and 5 mm. The mesh was then cut into a circular shape using a 6 mm diameter biopsy punch. The cut chitosan mesh sheet was attached to a frame set of Meshtable® (MIZUTA Seisakusho, Inc., Japan). This process was essential to conduct mesh cultures using a suspended chitosan mesh sheet.

Cell cultures of C2C12 cells on micromesh sheets

C2C12 cells, a mouse myoblasts cell line, were purchased from RIKEN cell bank (# RCB0987, Japan). These cells were maintained on T75 flasks in high glucose Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) containing 10% FBS (fetal bovine serum, Gibco) and 100 units/mL penicillin and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Fujifilm Wako).

Before seeding of C2C12 cells onto the chitosan or chitosan/gelatin mesh sheet, the mesh sheet on the Meshtable® frame was coated with 40 µL of 0.05 mg/mL fibronectin (FC010, Sigma) to increase cell attachment to the mesh sheet. After coating for more than 1 h in an incubator at 37 °C, any unbound fibronectin solution was removed by aspiration immediately prior to cell seeding and 40 µL of C2C12 cell suspension (5 × 105 cells/mL) was placed on the mesh sheet and started to culture at 5% CO₂ and 37 °C in the incubator.

After more than 12 h, 3 mL/well of culture medium was then added to the well, and the culture was continued. After the cells reached confluence within the mesh structure, differentiation medium which is consisted of high-glucose DMEM supplemented with 2% horse serum (HS, Gibco), 100 units/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin was added to the well.

Staining cells with DiI

Cells on mesh sheets were stained with DiI (1,1’-dioctadecyl‐3,3,3’,3’tetramethylindocarboxyamine perchlorate) (C318, Wako, Tokyo, Japan) at a concentration of 12.5 µg/mL at 37 °C for 20 min in 12-well plates. After incubation, the cells were gently washed three times with PBS (-) to remove any excess dye. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) (163-20145, Wako, Tokyo, Japan) for 20 min for further analysis.

Scanning electron microscopy

The mesh sheet samples were sputter-coated with an 8 nm layer of platinum-palladium (Pt-Pd) using an Ion Sputter Coater (E102, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan). Subsequently, the surface morphology was examined using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; JSM-7900 F, JEOL, Tokyo, Japan) operated at an accelerating voltage of 5.0 kV.

Contact angle measurement

The contact angle was measured by placing a 2 µL droplet of deionized water onto the micromesh surface. Images were captured directly using a Hirox Digital Microscope KH-1300 to ensure accurate determination of the contact angle. The images were then analyzed using the contact angle plugin in ImageJ version 1.54 g32, which detects the droplet’s edges and fits the contour to either a circle or an ellipse. The tangent at the three-phase contact point was calculated within ImageJ, allowing for precise measurement of the contact angle.

Analysis of cell alignment

Cell alignment was analyzed using the publicly available ImageJ plugin “Orientation J” (NIH, USA)35. The distribution of orientation data was processed to identify the peak alignment orientation (orientation in degrees), which was used as the central reference for further calculations. Based on our previous analytical method with minor modification23, an orientation range of ± 15° around the peak alignment was defined. Alignment intensity within this range was computed as the sum of all vector elements falling into the defined range and expressed as a percentage of the total vector sum across the image.

Results

Novel chitosan mesh sheets for cell culture

We developed a strategy to fabricate chitosan mesh sheets by spin-coating a thin layer of chitosan solution onto a SU-8 mold with numerous concave patterns, followed by drying and peeling the chitosan sheet off the mold. This approach enabled us to achieve free customization of the shape and size of the mesh openings. However, peeling the dried chitosan sheet off the mold proved to be challenging. To overcome this issue, we explored a process in which the chitosan was neutralized and coagulated using NaOH solution, enabling detachment of chitosan mesh sheets from the mold while submerged in an aqueous solution. Since the SU-8 mold could not withstand treatment with NaOH solution, we first fabricated a concave mold using SU-8 (Fig. 1A(i) and 1B(i)) and then used it to create a convex mold made of PDMS (Fig. 1A(ii) and 1B (ii)). The PDMS mold demonstrated resistance to 1 M NaOH solution, making it possible to successfully detach the chitosan mesh sheet from the mold while immersed in the NaOH solution. The coagulation effect of chitosan induced by NaOH treatment was essential for the fabrication of chitosan mesh sheets. Before this treatment, the chitosan-based structure remained water-soluble due to the protonation of amino groups (-NH3+) in the acidic environment. Immersion in the NaOH solution induced deprotonation (-NH2) and neutralized the residual acetic acid, triggering a neutralization-induced coagulation that transformed the mesh into a stable, water-insoluble structure capable of retaining its geometry upon demolding30. FTIR analysis demonstrated that chitosan and gelatin were well-mixed within the composite scaffold, indicating high miscibility at the molecular level (Fig. S1). Specifically, the spectra of the composites (CS-G and CS-G-NaOH) exhibited both the characteristic glycosidic linkage (C-O-C) peaks of chitosan (1000–1150 cm−1) and the Amide I and II bands characteristic of gelatin (around 1633 cm−1 and 1540 cm−1, respectively). Furthermore, the broad peak observed in the 3200–3450 cm−1 region suggests the existence of extensive hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between the chitosan and gelatin polymer chains, contributing to the enhanced compatibility of the blend system. Regarding the impact of the NaOH treatment, a comparison of the spectra before and after treatment revealed that the positions and relative intensities of the major characteristic peaks remained largely consistent. No significant spectral changes indicating chemical bond cleavage or the formation of new functional groups were observed. This indicates that while the NaOH treatment effectively neutralized the protonated chitosan to induce the sol-gel transition and structural fixation, it did not degrade the polymer backbone. Consequently, the NaOH neutralization process employed in this study was confirmed to be chemically mild, preserving the structural integrity of the material components. Additionally, the PDMS mold could be reused multiple times, enhancing the efficiency of the fabrication process. Using this method, we successfully produced chitosan mesh sheets with rhombus-shaped or square-shaped openings (Fig. 2A and B). These chitosan mesh sheets were strong enough to be picked up with tweezers without breaking (Fig. 2A-iv and 2B-iv), and SEM observations revealed their surfaces were very smooth (Fig. 2A-vi and 2B-vi).

Reducing the amount of chitosan in the mesh is essential for minimizing the chitosan content in the final cell sheet product. To address this, we investigated the minimum concentration of chitosan solution required to fabricate chitosan mesh sheets. Chitosan concentrations ranging from 4% to 12w/w% were tested. Our results indicated that chitosan mesh sheets with sufficient handling strength could not be produced at 4 or 6 w/w% concentrations, this defect is attributed to the solution’s excessively low viscosity, which prevents the polymer from being adequately retained within the mold grooves. Consequently, the dried filament structure becomes too thin to withstand the mechanical forces during demolding, ultimately leading to the disintegration of the mesh sheet. The satisfactory results were achieved at approximately 8 w/w% (Fig. 2C). When chitosan solutions with concentrations between 8 and 12 w/w/% were used, the resulting chitosan mesh sheets had a thickness of approximately 25 μm after drying (Fig. 2C). Next, we examined the relationship between spin-coating speed and the thickness of chitosan mesh sheets. Fabricating thin but strong chitosan mesh sheets is critical for reducing the chitosan content in the final cell sheet product. Interestingly, no significant changes in the thickness of the chitosan mesh sheets were observed across the tested spin-coating speeds ranging from 1,500 to 3,500 rpm (Fig. 2D). This result suggests that the interaction between the convex patterns and chitosan solution may play a key role in determining sheet thickness. We also evaluated the water absorption properties of the dried chitosan sheets (8 w/w%). The results showed that the mesh sheets absorbed approximately 200% of their weight in water (Fig. 2E). Compared to previous reports on chitosan films treated with sodium hydroxide30, our results show a higher swelling ratio. This is likely due to the higher specific surface area of the mesh. This high-water absorption capacity indicates that chitosan mesh sheets are well-suited for applications requiring the absorption of nutrient-rich solutions, further supporting their potential for use in cultured meat production and other cell culture applications.

Degradation characteristics of chitosan-gelatin meshes

The stability of the scaffolds was evaluated by monitoring the weight loss over a 7-day period in both PBS (-) and DMEM (Figure S2). All experimental groups exhibited a similar degradation profile, characterized by an initial mass loss of approximately 8–10% within the first 24 h, followed by a gradual stabilization. By Day 7, the cumulative weight loss plateaued at approximately 17%, indicating that the scaffolds retained over 80% of their initial mass. No significant difference was observed between the mesh geometries or the incubation media. The initial weight loss is likely attributed to the leaching of unbound gelatin molecules. These results confirm that the NaOH neutralization process imparts sufficient hydrolytic stability to the chitosan-gelatin scaffold to withstand the 7-day cell culture protocol without structural disintegration. Supplementary video shows that the mesh exhibits rapid degradation under simulated gastric fluid conditions. Upon exposure to simulated gastric fluid (SGF), the chitosan-gelatin mesh visibly disintegrates within about 10 s. This confirms that the scaffold is biodegradable and digestible, meeting the safety requirements for edible cultured meat products.

Cell cultures on chitosan mesh sheets

We previously developed a cell culture device called Meshtable®20, designed for cell culture on microstructured mesh sheets suspended above the bottom of a well. The original Meshtable® is fitted with a polyethyleneterephthalate (PET) mesh. In this study, we replaced the PET mesh in the Meshtable® with a newly developed dried chitosan mesh sheet, which was attached to the Meshtable® frame using Kapton tape (Fig. 3A).

Since surface tension is necessary to retain the cell suspension on the mesh sheets, hydrophobicity of the mesh sheets is a crucial factor to successfully culture cells on the mesh sheets. Hence, we evaluated the hydrophobicity of the chitosan mesh sheets by placing a 2 µL water droplet on their surface and determining the contact angle which was found to be approximately 65.5°.

Next, the surface of the chitosan mesh sheets was coated with a fibronectin solution, and 40 µL of a cell suspension of C2C12 cells was seeded onto the mesh sheets. On the following day, culture medium was added to fill the wells of a 12-well plate (Fig. 3B). Initial observations confirmed that the cells adhered to the chitosan mesh sheets (Fig. 3C). However, by day 2 post cell seeding, the cells detached from the mesh and formed aggregates, suggesting that the adhesive force between the cells and the mesh sheet was insufficient compared to the cell-cell adhesion (Fig. 3C). These findings underscored the necessity for further optimization to enhance the adhesion between C2C12 cells and the chitosan mesh, thereby improving its effectiveness for cell culture applications.

More adhesive chitosan mesh sheets containing gelatin

To improve the adhesion of C2C12 cells to the mesh sheet and prevent cell aggregation, we incorporated gelatin into the chitosan mesh sheets. A 12% gelatin solution was chosen as it represents highly concentrated yet practical solution for handling. By mixing the 12% gelatin solution with a 16% chitosan solution at a 1:1 volume ratio and following the same fabrication processes used for pure chitosan mesh sheet, we successfully produced chitosan-gelatin mesh sheets containing 6% gelatin and 8% chitosan (Fig. 4A). The chitosan-gelatin mesh sheet exhibited a contact angle of approximately 90° (Fig. 4B) and a thickness of approximately 17 μm (Fig. 4C). Interestingly, the contact angle of the chitosan-gelatin film without a mesh structure was approximately 60°, suggesting that the mesh-like microstructure contributed to the increased contact angle (Fig. 4B). Furthermore, the chitosan-gelatin mesh sheets were thinner than the pure chitosan mesh sheet, thereby further reducing the proportion of scaffold material in the final cell sheet product (Figs. 2D and 4C). One hypothesis is that the viscosity of the 8% chitosan / 6% gelatin blend solution (prepared by mixing 16% w/w chitosan and 12% w/v gelatin solutions at a 1:1 ratio) may differ from that of the 8 w/w% chitosan solution. Variations in solution viscosity can significantly influence the film thickness achieved during spin coating, especially considering the different spin speeds used (4000 rpm for Chitosan-Gelatin vs. 2500 rpm for Chitosan-only in Fig. 2C, although Chitosan-only thickness was relatively stable across 1500–3500 rpm as shown in Fig. 2D).Additionally, interactions between chitosan and gelatin, such as the formation of polyelectrolyte complexes or hydrogen bonding, could alter the solution’s rheological properties and its behavior during the spin coating and drying processes. Such interactions might lead to a more compact film structure upon solvent evaporation. This improvement indicates that the chitosan-gelatin mesh sheets may offer enhanced performance for cell culture applications.

Using the same method as for the chitosan mesh sheet, we seeded cells onto the chitosan-gelatin mesh sheet and initiated cell culture. Notably, cells were uniformly distributed seeded across the entire surface of the chitosan-gelatin mesh sheet (Fig. 5). This result suggests that, compared to the chitosan mesh sheet—where cell adhesion was strongly influenced by the efficiency of coating solution contact and subsequent washing—the chitosan-gelatin mesh sheet achieved a significantly higher cell adhesion rate. The enhanced adhesion of cells to the chitosan-gelatin mesh sheets allowed for stable cell proliferation parallel to the mesh sheet, with cells gradually filling the mesh openings. By day 2 after seeding, most of the mesh openings were covered, leading to the formation of a robust cell sheet (Fig. 5). It is worth noting that the fibronectin coating proved crucial for the long-term maintenance of the cell layers. In the absence of fibronectin, the cell layers were unable to maintain stable attachment to the mesh surface. Although the degree of detachment was less severe compared to pure chitosan meshes (as shown in Fig. S3), this observation confirms that fibronectin coating is still important for long-term cell stability.

Analysis of cell alignment

Our previous studies demonstrated that a rhombic micromesh with an acute angle of 50° effectively guides cell alignment intensity23. Consistent with these findings, C2C12 cells cultured on rhombus-mesh sheets exhibited pronounced alignment along the major axes of the rhombic grid. In contrast, cells cultured on square mesh sheets or flat films displayed a random orientation. To quantify cell alignment, we stained the cells with the fluorescent dye DiI and analyzed the alignment with ImageJ (Fig. 6A). The results demonstrated that cells cultured on the rhombus-mesh sheets exhibited significantly higher alignment intensity, averaging nearly 75% (Fig. 6B), compared to those cultured on the square meshes, which showed markedly lower alignment intensities, averaging around 25%. These findings underscore the critical influence of mesh geometry on cell alignment, with the rhombus-mesh sheets promoting enhanced directional alignment due to its distinct structural characteristics.

Discussion

Chitosan-based scaffolds have exhibited excellent biocompatibility and antibacterial properties and have demonstrated the ability to support the 3D culture of myocytes and adipocytes36. Chitosan is degraded in vivo by lysozyme, with its degradation rate adjustable through the degree of deacetylation37. These characteristics make chitosan a promising scaffold material for cultured meat development. However, chitosan-based scaffolds have low cell adhesion properties, which can hinder effective cellular attachment and proliferation. To improve cell attachment to chitosan scaffolds, researchers have used gelatin in tissue engineering due to their excellent biocompatibility, biodegradability, and tunable mechanical properties38–44. Leveraging these materials, here we succeeded in creating microstructured chitosan-gelatin mesh sheets that have adhesive properties and can control cell alignment by changing the shape of mesh openings. The synergistic effect of the gelatin-blended matrix and the surface fibronectin coating significantly enhanced cell-material interactions. Qualitative observations indicated robust cell adhesion, with negligible cell detachment found in the culture medium post-seeding. This high affinity ensures that the cells are effectively retained on the mesh surface to respond to the topological cues for alignment, validating the suitability of the surface seeding approach over bulk encapsulation. This mesh culture technique should play a crucial role in mimicking the texture of natural meat (Table 1).

The method of cell cultures on mesh sheets offers several advantages, including efficient nutrient and oxygen delivery to cells from both upper and lower sides of the mesh, promoting differentiation through three-dimensional cell culture, and enabling straightforward fabrication of cell sheets with high cell to material ratio20–26. Moreover, cell sheets constructed on mesh scaffolds demonstrated the potential to increase the cell-to-scaffold ratio, suggesting that this technology could facilitate the production of cell-rich cultured meat. Considering the partially layered structure of muscle tissue45, future studies will aim to utilize chitosan-gelatin mesh sheets to layer muscle cell sheets, adipocyte sheets, and stromal cell sheets. This approach holds promise for creating cultured meat that more closely resembles the structure and composition of natural muscle tissue.

A key advantage of using mesh sheets is their ability to precisely control cell alignment through the geometry of the mesh. In tissues such as skeletal muscle, endothelial tissue46, tendons47, neurons48, and cardiac tissue cells naturally align in specific directions, contributing to their unique structural and functional properties. This alignment is essential for promoting the functionality and differentiation of muscle cells, making it a critical factor in muscle cell culture. Although 3D bioprinting has emerged as a powerful tool for constructing aligned tissues, it still faces several challenges, including the requirement for complex bio-ink rheology, potential nozzle clogging, and the exposure of cells to high shear stress during extrusion, which can compromise cell viability49. In contrast, the chitosan-gelatin micromesh developed in this study offers a facile and cost-effective alternative. By utilizing the micromesh as a pre-fabricated template for contact guidance, our approach achieves precise cell alignment without the need for sophisticated instrumentation or subjecting cells to mechanical stress during fabrication. This simplicity and high reproducibility make it highly advantageous for high-throughput tissue engineering applications. In this study, we cultured cells on square mesh sheets, rhombus-mesh sheets with sharp 50° angles, and traditional flat dishes. Our findings confirmed that the rhombus-mesh sheet with 50° sharp angles significantly enhanced cell alignment, consistent with previous observations23. The enhanced alignment is likely due to the ability of C2C12 cells to sense and respond to the geometric structure of the mesh apertures23,50. It is well established that cells sense and respond to their mechanical environment through cell-matrix and cell-cell adhesion, mediated by extracellular integrins and cadherins51. This process, known as mechanotransduction, is facilitated by the actin cytoskeleton, which enables cells to adapt to geometric and physical boundary conditions52.

In addition to cultured meat, the novel chitosan cell sheets developed in this study hold significant potential for applications in regenerative medicine. In this field, transplanting cells as a sheet is widely regarded as more efficient and effective than transplanting dissociated single cells or spheroids 20,53, highlighting the benefit of cell sheet fabrication techniques such as ours. Moreover, since chitosan is a biodegradable and biocompatible material in vivo54.

In conclusion, we successfully developed chitosan mesh sheets with controllable microscale openings and achieved enhanced cell adhesion by incorporating gelatin to chitosan mesh sheets. Thus, our approach achieves innovative scaffolds for cell culture, presenting a technological advancement that will broaden the application of mesh-based culture to cultured meat production.
