Comparative physiological and transcriptomic analysis of rapid post-midday photosynthetic recovery in Saccharum spontaneum reveals candidate pathways for sugarcane improvement
Authors: Maoyong Ran, Ruiqian Zhou, Lixuan Ge, Junjie Zhao, Jiahui Yang, Bo Yu, Weitong Zhao, Yue Wang, Zhiwei Yang, Guoqiang Huang, Liangnian Xu, Chaohua Huang, Jiayong Liu, Zuhu Deng, Xinwang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlobal climate warming has increasingly exacerbated the adverse effects of high temperature on crop yield and quality. Saccharum spontaneum, a wild resource that serves as a crucial genetic reservoir for sugarcane, represents a valuable genetic asset for improving heat tolerance in sugarcane breeding. Recent studies have shown that S. spontaneum contributes to modern sugarcane breeding not only as a source of resistance genes but also to sugar accumulation in contemporary cultivars. However, the molecular basis underlying its photosynthetic recovery following combined heat and high light stress remains unclear.Materials and methodsIn this study, 20 S. spontaneum accessions were evaluated under natural high-temperature and high-light conditions by measuring photosynthetic parameters at 09:00, 14:00, and 17:00. Two accessions showing contrasting post-noon recovery of net photosynthetic rate were identified: the fast-recovering line 82–114 (L114) and the slow-recovering line 2021-17 (L17).ResultsTranscriptome sequencing and differential expression analyses revealed that the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were predominantly enriched in pathways related to plant hormone signal transduction, antioxidant enzyme systems, photosynthesis, and carbon metabolism. Further weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) identified co-expression modules highly associated with the afternoon samples of L114, along with key regulatory factors including WRKY, NF-YC, CAMTA, C2H2, and RLK family members. Physiological and molecular data together indicate that L114 orchestrates a dynamic response under heat and high light stress by remodeling ethylene, jasmonic acid, and auxin signaling networks; coordinately enhancing peroxisome-associated antioxidant systems; transiently downregulating and subsequently restoring the expression of light-harvesting and electron transport genes in the photosystems; and finely tuning glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, and the pentose phosphate pathway to achieve a temporal response characterized by “damage suppression–rapid repair–recovery of high photosynthesis.” This study is the first to investigate candidate pathways underlying the rapid recovery of photosynthesis following midday depression in S. spontaneum, and to identify germplasm with high photosynthetic capacity and rapid post-midday recovery. These mechanistic insights provide novel candidate targets for functional validation and genetic improvement in sugarcane breeding.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12870-026-09161-x.

Introduction

Sugarcane (Saccharum spp.), one of the world’s most important economic crops, accounts for more than 80% of global sugar production and holds substantial economic and ecological value [1].

Climate change is reshaping global environments by elevating mean temperatures that threaten agricultural productivity, while concomitant shifts in soil moisture and fertility further intensify stress-induced limitations on photosynthesis [2]. Extensive research has shown that midday depression induced by high temperature and high light severely affects crop yield and quality [3, 4]. High temperature and high light reduce photosynthetic efficiency in plants [3, 5], leading to substantial declines in the yields of major staple crops worldwide [6], Although adjusting sowing dates to modify crop growth cycles can partially mitigate these losses [7], the development of cultivars with enhanced tolerance to combined heat and high-light stress remains crucial. Excessive light and high temperatures impact photosynthetic efficiency and the stability of photosynthetic proteins, thereby reducing yield in sunflower [8], mulberry enhances its tolerance by upregulating genes involved in energy management, water conservation, and photosynthesis [9].

High-temperature stress is often accompanied by intense light and drought, and under elevated temperatures, photochemical reactions and carbon metabolism are particularly vulnerable [10]. To mitigate photodamage caused by excess excitation energy, plants employ photoprotective mechanisms such as non-photochemical quenching (NPQ), which dissipates surplus energy as heat to avert PSII photoinhibition [11–13]. Plants also enhance antioxidant defenses—including superoxide dismutase (SOD), catalase (CAT), and peroxidase (POD)—to remove excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) and thereby reduce oxidative damage [14–16]. Phytohormones including abscisic acid (ABA), jasmonic acid (JA), and salicylic acid (SA) also play critical regulatory roles under high-temperature and high-light conditions. ABA modulates stomatal aperture and photoprotective responses, while JA and SA activate defense-related gene expression, enhancing stress resilience [17–19].

Photosynthesis plays a central role in plant growth, development, and yield formation, and it profoundly influences energy flow and material cycling within ecosystems [20, 21]. Variations in photosynthetic capacity among genotypes reflect the importance of genetic diversity in regulating photosynthetic efficiency [22–25]. The molecular mechanisms underlying such variation encompass processes related to light harvesting, energy conversion, and carbon fixation. These involve coordinated actions of pigment–protein complexes, the photosynthetic electron transport chain, and key enzymes such as ribulose-1,5-bisphosphate carboxylase/oxygenase (RuBisCO) and phosphoenolpyruvate carboxylase (PEPC) [26–29]. Photosystem II (PSII) and photosystem I (PSI) function as core components for light capture and photochemical energy conversion, while RuBisCO and PEPC are crucial for CO₂ fixation and the C₄ carbon-concentrating mechanism [27, 30–32]. Their activities and expression levels directly shape photosynthetic efficiency. In addition, photosynthesis is highly sensitive to environmental variables—including water availability, temperature, light intensity, and atmospheric CO₂ concentration—which influence pigment composition, enzyme activity, and electron transport performance [33–36].

Although these molecular mechanisms are largely conserved across crops, adaptive responses can vary markedly among species. In tomato, enhanced expression of SlHSFB2b suppresses jasmonic acid (JA) catabolism, promoting endogenous JA accumulation and thereby increasing tolerance to combined high-light and heat stress (HL + HS) [37], In Arabidopsis thaliana, JA has also been shown to be essential for adaptation to HL + HS, where it contributes to the recovery of photosynthesis through activation of PSII repair and accumulation of JA and JA-Ile [38], In rice, high light significantly enhances thermotolerance and mitigates the strong negative effects of heat stress on root sugar content. However, the mechanisms by which sugarcane maintains photosystem stability under high temperature and high light stress and rapidly recovers photosynthesis after midday depression remain unclear. S. spontaneum L. (S. spontaneum), one of the most successfully utilized wild species in sugarcane breeding, has long been recognized as a key genetic resource for overcoming major breeding bottlenecks [39]. Although S. spontaneum is broadly distributed in nature with abundant genetic diversity, only a limited number of accessions have been effectively incorporated into breeding programs [40, 41]. Historically, breeding efforts have emphasized its role as a reservoir of resistance genes [41], QTL mapping provided evidence that rust-resistance loci in sugarcane originated from S. spontaneum [42], while genome-wide analysis of NBS-LRR genes further supported a substantial contribution of S. spontaneum to disease resistance in modern sugarcane cultivars [43]. While largely overlooking its contribution to sugar accumulation [44–46]. Zhao et al. demonstrated, based on full-length transcriptome analysis, that the S. spontaneum subgenome contributes to sucrose accumulation in sugarcane [44]. The S. spontaneum sucrose phosphate synthase (SPS) gene family, together with sugar transporters including SUT and TST, also participates in sucrose accumulation in modern sugarcane cultivars [47, 48]. Given that photosynthesis is the primary source of energy and fundamental to carbohydrate biosynthesis, emerging evidence showing higher net photosynthetic rates in S. spontaneum compared with tropical cultivars suggests that its value extends beyond stress resistance [49, 50]. Maintaining photosynthesis during hot, high-irradiance afternoons is critical for sugarcane productivity. Because S. spontaneum is a major wild donor in sugarcane breeding, dissecting recovery-associated traits in this species may reveal candidate mechanisms for crop improvement.

As climate change increasingly threatens crop productivity through rising temperatures, more frequent extreme weather events, and the loss of arable land, understanding the intrinsic regulatory mechanisms that crops have evolved in natural environments is of critical importance. Elucidating how different species protect and rapidly restore the photosynthetic machinery under high-temperature and high-light stress—through differences in gene expression, enzyme activity, antioxidant capacity, and photoprotective efficiency—will be indispensable for breeding resilient varieties capable of sustaining yield under future climate scenarios. In this study, we identified key genes in S. spontaneum that limit photosynthetic damage and enable the rapid recovery of photosynthesis under natural high-temperature and high-light conditions. We further identified recovery-associated mechanisms in S. spontaneum that represent promising targets for functional validation and improvement in cultivated sugarcane, thereby providing a theoretical basis for the precise utilization of S. spontaneum germplasm in sugarcane breeding. Although a comprehensive understanding will require the integration of physiological measurements (e.g., leaf temperature and chlorophyll fluorescence) and hormone quantification, transcriptomics provides an efficient strategy for hypothesis generation under field conditions involving multiple accessions. Integrating physiological and molecular insights into breeding frameworks remains essential for translating innovation into global food security outcomes [51].

Measurement of net photosynthetic rate

Net photosynthetic rate was measured in 20 S. spontaneum accessions using a LI-COR 6800 XT portable photosynthesis system (LI-COR Biosciences, Lincoln, NE, USA). Measurements were conducted at three time points during the day: 09:00, 14:00, and 17:00. Relative humidity was set to 50%, with a CO2 flow rate of 400 µmol·mol⁻¹ and CO2 injection at 17%. For each plant, measurements were taken on the youngest fully expanded leaf, with three repetitions per plant, and three plants per material. RNA extraction, library construction, and sequencing

Based on the photosynthetic measurements, two accessions were selected for transcriptome analysis: L114, which showed the highest rate and the most rapid post-noon recovery; and L17, which displayed a comparatively slower recovery. Each accession included three biological replicates.

Total RNA was isolated from leaf tissues using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Burlington, ON, Canada). After quality assessment, RNA samples were used for library construction. First-strand cDNA was synthesized using random hexamer primers, followed by second-strand cDNA synthesis with buffer, dNTPs, RNase H, and DNA polymerase I. Double-stranded cDNA was purified using AMPure XP beads, end-repaired, A-tailed, and ligated to sequencing adapters. Fragment size selection was also performed with AMPure XP beads. The libraries were enriched by PCR and subsequently sequenced on an Illumina PE150 platform. Sequencing was conducted by Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China).

Read processing and differential expression analysis

Raw reads were filtered to obtain high-quality clean reads and then aligned to the S. spontaneum NP-X reference genome using HISAT2. Transcript assembly was performed with StringTie (v1.3.1), and gene expression levels were normalized using fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads. All downstream analyses were based on clean, high-quality data.

Differential expression analysis between experimental and control groups was conducted using DESeq2 (v1.6.3). Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were defined as those with a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 and an absolute fold change ≥ 2. GO enrichment analysis of annotated DEGs was performed using topGO (v2.8), with significance defined as FDR < 0.05. KEGG pathway enrichment was conducted using KOBAS 2.0, and enrichment significance was determined by corrected p-values (KS < 0.05).

POD、CAT and SOD enzyme activities

The activities of SOD, POD, and CAT were measured using commercial assay kits (Solarbio, Beijing, China) following the manufacturer’s protocols. All physiological measurements included three biological replicates.

RT–qPCR validation

cDNA synthesized from samples collected at the three time points was used as the template for quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR). Primer sequences are provided in Table S1. The Sugarcane Cullin (CUL) gene was used as the internal reference. qRT–PCR reactions were performed using an ABI PRISM 7500 system (Applied Biosystems, Waltham, MA, USA) and a commercial fluorescent quantification kit (Biomarker Technologies Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Reaction procedures followed the manufacturer’s guidelines. Melting curve analysis was conducted after amplification, and relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCT method.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA v1.69; Langfelder & Horvath, 2008) was applied to infer co-expression modules from 36,209 genes. Low-expression genes (FPKM < 0.5) were filtered out prior to analysis. Module–trait associations were determined based on correlations between module eigengenes and phenotypic traits. Trait-associated genes from significantly correlated modules were subjected to KEGG enrichment analysis as described above. Hub genes within key modules were identified using the CytoHubba plugin in Cytoscape.

Results

Net photosynthetic rate analysis of twenty S. spontaneum accessions

We evaluated the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) of twenty S. spontaneum germplasm accessions with breeding potential, Measurements were conducted on 12 August 2024 at 09:00, 14:00, and 17:00 in the sugarcane germplasm nursery of Fujian Agriculture and Forestry University, Fuzhou, Fujian Province, China (119.18°E, 26.05°N). On the sampling day, the maximum air temperature reached 37 °C, the minimum temperature was 29 °C, and the photoperiod lasted 13 h 20 min. Across all accessions, Pn exhibited a characteristic diurnal pattern, declining from morning to midday and subsequently recovering in the late afternoon, with the lowest values observed at 14:00, consistent with the midday depression of photosynthesis.

During gas exchange measurements, the LI-COR 6800 XT chamber light source was used to provide photosynthetic photon flux density (PPFD) at the leaf surface. The PPFD was set according to the values recorded by the LI-6800 system, with setpoints of 1200 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at 09:00, 1800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at 14:00, and 800 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ at 17:00. In contrast, leaf temperature, chamber air temperature, leaf-to-air vapor pressure deficit (VPD), and relative humidity showed clear diurnal variation. At 09:00, leaf temperature and chamber air temperature ranged from 35.36 to 35.98 °C and from 35.31 to 35.81 °C, respectively. Leaf-to-air VPD ranged from 2.10 to 2.55 kPa, while relative humidity ranged from 63.8%–65.8% (Table 2). By 14:00, both leaf and chamber air temperatures increased markedly, with leaf temperature reaching 38.26–42.48 °C and chamber air temperature reaching 38.66–43.12 °C. During this period, leaf-to-air VPD increased to 2.90–4.29 kPa, whereas relative humidity decreased to 47.6–55.9%, indicating that leaves were exposed to greater atmospheric evaporative demand and a drier chamber microclimate during midday to early afternoon. By 17:00, leaf temperature and chamber air temperature declined to 35.96–36.56 °C and 36.26–37.18 °C, respectively. Leaf-to-air VPD decreased to 2.36–2.61 kPa, while relative humidity increased to 55.6–58.0%. The higher and more variable leaf-to-air VPD observed during the midday/afternoon period was mainly associated with elevated and fluctuating leaf and chamber air temperatures. Because VPD depends on the difference between the saturation vapor pressure at leaf temperature and the actual vapor pressure of the surrounding air, the increase in leaf temperature from 38.26 to 42.48 °C would have amplified the leaf-to-air vapor pressure gradient. The lower and more variable relative humidity during this period further contributed to the elevated VPD. By contrast, the more stable leaf temperature and chamber humidity in the late afternoon resulted in a narrower VPD range (Table 2).

Among the evaluated accessions, two materials—82–114 and 2021-17—displayed the highest Pn at 09:00 and were therefore selected for further comparison. At 09:00, Pn was 25.73 and 26.06 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ for 82–114 and 2021-17, respectively. By 14:00, these values decreased to 15.36 and 15.64 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹, indicating a comparable degree of midday inhibition under elevated temperature and high irradiance. Notably, by 17:00, 82–114 exhibited a substantially greater recovery of Pn (21.85 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹) than 2021-17 (17.46 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹), a divergence that motivated further investigation (Fig. 1E).

After comparative analyses of transpiration rate (E), intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci), stomatal conductance (gs), and chlorophyll content across the 20 germplasm accessions, we found that gs and E decreased progressively from morning to late afternoon, reaching their highest values at 09:00 and lowest values at 17:00. This pattern indicates that, at the population level, plants adopt a conservative, water-saving stomatal strategy to mitigate risk under stressful daytime conditions (Fig. 1A, B, C, D). In contrast, the net photosynthetic rate (Pn) exhibited a decline followed by a subsequent increase (Fig. 1E). The monotonic decreases in gs and E suggest that stomatal limitation contributes to midday depression; however, the late-afternoon recovery of Pn did not depend on a rebound in gs or E, implying that the release of non-stomatal constraints determines the magnitude of recovery (Fig. 1B, D, E). Thus, midday depression likely reflects the superimposition of non-stomatal limitations (e.g., photoinhibition and/or biochemical restrictions), which are alleviated toward late afternoon as temperature and irradiance decline, allowing Pn to recover even while stomatal behavior remains relatively conservative.

Across the three time points, intercellular CO₂ concentration (Ci) showed no pronounced differences between 82 and 114 and 2021-17, suggesting that the variation in Pn between the two accessions was more strongly governed by non-stomatal limitations (i.e., photochemical and/or biochemical constraints) (Fig. 1A). In this context, CO₂ supply was presumably not limiting; rather, impaired carbon assimilation capacity and/or inhibition of electron transport likely constituted the primary causes of the observed reductions in Pn. Notably, chlorophyll content in 82–114 exhibited a decline–recovery pattern that closely paralleled the temporal dynamics of Pn, with chlorophyll recovery occurring concomitantly with the restoration of net photosynthesis. This synchrony implies that damage to the photosynthetic apparatus and photosynthetic pigments in 82–114 was largely reversible and that the photosynthetic state recovered more rapidly(Fig. 1C, E). In contrast, chlorophyll content in 2021-17 decreased continuously throughout the day (Fig. 1C), indicating more persistent photoinhibition and/or accelerated pigment degradation, thereby further supporting a dominant role of non-stomatal limitations in constraining Pn.

To quantify midday inhibition and recovery capacity, we calculated the midday depression index (MDI), recovery index (RI), and recovery amplitude (AR) for all accessions. MDI ranged from 0.268 to 0.556, indicating that midday depression was prevalent across the population. In contrast, recovery capacity varied more widely, with RI and AR ranging from 0.670 to 0.926 and from 0.91 to 8.68, respectively. Accession 82–114 exhibited a strong post-midday recovery, with an RI of 0.849 (i.e., 17:00 Pn recovered to 84.9% of the morning level) and an AR of 6.49 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹ (Pn 15.36→21.85), ranking second in AR among all accessions. By comparison, 2021-17 showed the lowest RI (0.670) and a much smaller AR (1.82 µmol m⁻² s⁻¹; Pn 15.64→17.46). Notably, accession A1A1 displayed an RI of 0.87 and an AR of 8.68, both exceeding those of 82–114; however, its overall Pn remained relatively low (Table 3).

Given that 82–114 and 2021-17 exhibited comparable midday depression yet contrasting late-afternoon recovery, we conducted transcriptome profiling of these two accessions to elucidate the molecular determinants underlying post-stress photosynthetic recovery.

Transcriptomic differences between samples

To investigate the temporal transcriptional differences between genotypes 82–114 and 2021-17 across three time points within a single day, we performed RNA-seq on leaf samples collected simultaneously with net photosynthetic rate measurements, using three biological replicates per condition. A total of 202.14 Gb of clean data was generated, with each sample yielding no less than 7.49 Gb. The proportion of bases with a Q30 quality score exceeded 90.28% across all samples. Alignment of clean reads to the sugarcane reference genome resulted in mapping efficiencies ranging from 73.01% to 88.86%. For subsequent analyses, samples collected at noon from Yunnan genotype 82–114 were designated as L114, and those from genotype 2021-17 were designated as L17.

Principal component analysis (PCA) of the transcriptomic profiles revealed high consistency among biological replicates and clear separation between sample groups. Specifically, samples L114-14 and L114-17 clustered closely together, whereas L114-9 was more distantly positioned relative to the other two. A similar pattern was observed for the three L17 samples (Fig. 2A).

To characterize transcriptional dynamics across diurnal time points, we performed pairwise differential expression analyses for L114 and L17 separately at three time intervals (09:00 vs. 14:00 and 14:00 vs. 17:00). In parallel, we conducted cross-genotype comparisons at each time point to identify transcriptional differences between L114 and L17 under identical environmental conditions. In total, 64,184 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified. Specifically, in L114, 5,326 genes were up-regulated and 5,772 were down-regulated at 09:00 vs. 14:00, whereas 146 genes were up-regulated and 592 down-regulated at 14:00 vs. 17:00. In L17, 3,955 genes were up-regulated and 4,924 down-regulated at 09:00 vs. 14:00, while 1,888 genes were up-regulated and 3,065 down-regulated at 14:00 vs. 17:00 (Fig. 3). Notably, the cross-genotype comparisons at each time point yielded a substantial number of DEGs, indicating pronounced transcriptional divergence between L114 and L17 throughout the day (Fig. 2B).

Subsequently, we performed functional analyses of the DEGs identified in each comparison. KEGG enrichment revealed that DEGs in L114-9 vs. L114-14 were primarily associated with plant hormone signal transduction, biosynthesis of amino acids, carbon metabolism, starch and sucrose metabolism, and plant–pathogen interaction. The concurrent enrichment of these pathways indicates an activated immune state accompanied by physiological reallocation of energy and carbon–nitrogen resources. DEGs in L114-14 vs. L114-17 were enriched in endocytosis, Protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, spliceosome, starch and sucrose metabolism, carbon metabolism, plant–pathogen interaction, circadian rhythm – plant (Fig S1).

For L17, DEGs in L17-9 vs. L17-14 were mainly enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and starch and sucrose metabolism, as well as plant–pathogen interaction. Similarly, DEGs in L17-14 vs. L17-17 were enriched in plant hormone signaling, protein processing in endoplasmic reticulum, starch and sucrose metabolism, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and plant–pathogen interaction (Fig S2). Collectively, the transcriptional profiles of L114 and L17 indicate fundamentally distinct regulatory strategies: L17 maintains a constitutively activated “defense–metabolism–protein quality control” program, whereas L114 exhibits a dynamic, time-structured response—strongly activating defense pathways and resource reallocation before noon, followed by a transition toward receptor recycling, UPR/splicing, and circadian alignment in the evening. This flexible transcriptional adjustment allows L114 to allocate and utilize limited resources more efficiently in response to environmental fluctuations, thereby maximizing net photosynthetic rate. Between genotypes, DEGs in L17-9 vs L114-9 were enriched in plant hormone signal transduction, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and plant–pathogen interaction. In L17-14 vs. L114-14, enriched pathways included plant hormone signaling, ribosome, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and plant–pathogen interaction. DEGs in L17-17 vs. L114-17 were enriched in plant hormone signaling, MAPK signaling pathway-plant, ribosome, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, and plant–pathogen interaction (Fig S3).

GO enrichment analyses further refined these observations. DEGs in L114-9 vs. L114-14 were predominantly associated with RNA modification, chloroplast organization, rRNA processing, and photosynthesis. DEGs in L114-14 vs. L114-17 were mainly enriched in cellular response to heat, protein folding and refolding, protein stabilization and chlorophyll biosynthetic process (Fig S4). In L17-9 vs. L17-14, DEGs were enriched in photosynthesis, spermidine biosynthetic process, circadian rhythm, chloroplast organization and pyruvate metabolic process. For L17-14 vs. L17-17, enriched GO terms included protein–chromophore linkage, photosynthesis, light harvesting in photosystem I, cellular response to heat, and protein refolding (Fig S5).

These results suggest that L114 initially reinforces RNA modification, chloroplast biogenesis, and polyamine-related pathways, thereby establishing the structural and metabolic foundation for subsequent photosystem assembly and efficient photosynthesis, while simultaneously preparing for potential heat or high-light stress. By 17:00, L114 shows increased activation of heat-responsive and proteostasis-related pathways. In contrast, L17 elevates photosynthesis and primary metabolism earlier (at 14:00) to support rapid growth and assimilation, and later (17:00) relies on PSI reassembly and chaperone-mediated stabilization to maintain performance under photo-thermal stress. Between genotypes, GO terms enriched in L17-9 vs. L114-9 included defense response, double-strand break repair involved in meiotic recombination, protein import into peroxisome matrix, docking, negative regulation of brassinosteroid mediated signaling pathway and transposition, DNA-mediated. Enriched terms in L17-14 vs. L114-14 involved defense response, protein import into peroxisome matrix, docking and pentose-phosphate shunt. In L17-17 vs. L114-17, the dominant GO category was defense response (Fig S6).

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

To investigate the relationships among diurnal fluctuations in light and temperature, photosynthetic performance, and gene expression—and to further elucidate key regulators underlying photosynthetic regulation—we performed a weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) using all differentially expressed genes (DEGs). WGCNA partitioned the DEGs into five co-expression modules. Modules showing correlation coefficients ≥ 0.65 with p ≤ 0.05 were defined as sample-specific modules. The strongest module–sample associations were observed in L114-9 (MEturquoise, MEcyan), L114-14 (MEblue), L114-17 (MEblue), L17-9 (MEturquoise), L17-14 (MEblack, MEgreenyellow), and L17-17 (MEblack, MEgreenyellow). Genes within these modules are likely to play pivotal roles in modulating photosynthesis in response to diurnal changes in light intensity and temperature (Fig. 3).

Among these modules, MEblue exhibited the highest correlations with L114-14 and L114-17 and contained 7,313 DEGs. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that genes in this module were predominantly associated with plant hormone signal transduction, MAPK signaling pathway - plant, carbon metabolism, biosynthesis of amino acids, starch and sucrose metabolism, spliceosome and plant-pathogen interaction. GO enrichment indicated significant overrepresentation of biological processes such as histone H3-K36 demethylation, cellulose biosynthetic process, and box C/D snoRNP assembly. These DEGs are presumed to contribute to the reconfiguration of amino-acid metabolic networks in response to environmental cues, enhancement of stress tolerance and repair capacity, and maintenance of metabolic homeostasis and energy allocation through translational regulation (Fig S7).

To identify key transcriptional regulators, we screened genes based on gene significance (GS > 0.2), module membership (MM > 0.9), and module eigengene connectivity (kME > 0.9). A total of 97 transcription factors were identified, spanning families such as WRKY, NAC, MYB, C3H, C2H2, and bHLH. From these, seven transcription factors were prioritized as core regulators: WRKY, SWI/SNF-SWI3, RLK-Pelle_LRR-XII-1, NF-YC, CAMTA, RLK-Pelle_L-LEC, and C2H2 (Table S1).

Complex hormonal signaling pathways triggered by environmental fluctuations

Plant hormones play pivotal roles in orchestrating defense responses, particularly under abiotic stress conditions. They fine-tune a broad spectrum of physiological and biochemical processes that enable plants to adapt to unfavorable environments. Different hormones act through distinct but interconnected signaling pathways to coordinate stress perception and downstream responses. Consistent with this notion, KEGG enrichment analysis revealed that up-regulated DEGs in L114 were repeatedly enriched in the plant hormone signal transduction pathway.

Abscisic acid (ABA) is a central regulator of abiotic stress tolerance. By promoting stomatal closure, reducing transpirational water loss, and enhancing osmotic adjustment, ABA contributes to maintaining photosynthetic efficiency under stress. ABA also activates antioxidant systems to mitigate reactive oxygen species (ROS) damage. We identified NCED1, a key enzyme in ABA biosynthesis, which exhibited various levels of up-regulation in the 9:00 vs. 14:00 comparisons, with the strongest induction observed in L17. Its expression decreased in the 14:00 vs. 17:00 comparison. Notably, when L17-14 was compared with L114-14, the expression difference was no longer significant. Under stress, ABA binds to PYR/PYL receptors to form a stable ABA–receptor complex that inhibits type 2 C protein phosphatases (PP2Cs), thereby activating SNF1-related protein kinases (SnRK2s). Activated SnRK2s phosphorylate downstream targets such as ABA-responsive element–binding factors (ABFs), ultimately initiating ABA-dependent transcriptional responses regulating stomatal movement and stress adaptation. In our dataset, PYR-related genes displayed expression patterns consistent with NCED1—up-regulated during early stress and subsequently down-regulated—while expression differences between L17 and L114 were not significant. Unexpectedly, SnRK2 and ABF genes were generally down-regulated across comparisons, although both were up-regulated to varying extents in L17 vs. L114 at 14:00 and 17:00 (Fig. 4A).

Ethylene also contributes to abiotic stress acclimation by modulating stomatal dynamics, protecting photosynthetic machinery, and reducing photooxidative damage. The ethylene biosynthesis gene ACO was down-regulated in L17 but remained slightly up-regulated in L114. In the 9:00 versus 14:00 comparison, ACO expression remained largely unchanged in both genotypes, whereas the negative regulators CTR1 and the ethylene receptor ETR were significantly up‑regulated, indicating that although ethylene signaling was strongly activated, concomitant negative feedback regulation was also enhanced to prevent over‑activation, a pattern that was particularly pronounced in L114. EBF1 (EIN3‑BINDING F‑BOX 1), a negative modulator of ethylene signaling, exhibited sustained down‑regulation in L114, whereas in L17 it showed an initial up‑regulation followed by down‑regulation. This dynamic resulted in attenuation of the ethylene signaling pathway in L17 at 14:00. To visually illustrate these regulatory differences, we constructed a model of ethylene signal transduction (Fig. 4C). From 14:00 to 17:00, ethylene signaling genes exhibited a coordinated decline, indicating a transient, rhythm-like activation pattern that subsides during the late afternoon. Across all time points, ACO, ETR, and CTR1 consistently showed higher expression in L114 than in L17 (Fig. 4A).

Jasmonic acid (JA) is indispensable for plant acclimation to combined high-temperature and high-light stress. In the canonical JA pathway, JAR1 converts JA into the active JA-Ile conjugate, which is recognized by the COI1 receptor, triggering the degradation of JAZ repressors and releasing MYC transcription factors to activate downstream responses. In this study, JAR1 was broadly up‑regulated, with the most pronounced induction observed in the 14:00 vs. 17:00 comparison at L17. At all examined time points, transcript abundance of JAR1 was consistently higher in L17 than in L114. In contrast, COI1 expression was induced only in the 9:00 vs. 14:00 comparison and was significantly up‑regulated exclusively in L114. To visually illustrate this regulatory divergence, we constructed a simplified schematic of the jasmonate signaling pathway (Fig. 4E). This expression pattern likely reduced JAZ activity, thereby enhancing MYC2-mediated transcriptional activation and strengthening JA-dependent stress responses in S. spontaneum (Fig. 4A).

Auxin signaling is known to modulate leaf and stem morphology under high-temperature and high-light conditions, adjusting leaf angle to reduce excess light interception. The classical auxin receptor TIR1 was strongly up-regulated in both L114 and L17, indicating elevated auxin sensitivity under stress. Intriguingly, multiple auxin-related genes—including AUX/IAA, ARF and TAR2—were consistently down-regulated. In the two genotypes examined, members of the auxin early response gene families GH3 (Gretchen Hagen 3) and SAUR (Small Auxin Up RNA) exhibited distinct transcriptional dynamics. GH3 genes were significantly downregulated in L114, whereas their expression remained largely unchanged in L17. Conversely, SAUR genes showed pronounced upregulation during the recovery phase in L114, but their induction in L17 was not statistically significant. To visually illustrate these genotype‑specific regulatory differences, we constructed a simplified model of auxin signal transduction (Fig. 4D). Other auxin‑related genes displayed comparable expression patterns between the two genotypes. These results suggest that heat stress triggers a transcriptional reprogramming of the auxin signaling network: while TIR1 up-regulation maintains heightened sensitivity at the perception level, the concurrent suppression of ARFs and their downstream targets restricts energy-consuming elongation growth. This pattern reflects a stress-adaptive “high-sensitivity, low-flux” auxin mode that prioritizes cellular homeostasis over rapid biomass accumulation (Fig. 4B).

Previous studies have established that salicylic acid (SA) modulates ROS signaling and protects photosynthetic systems under stress, whereas gibberellin (GA) pathways are often repressed to conserve energy. NPR1, the central SA co-activator, and its partner transcription factor TGA form the core of the SA-dependent defense module. Typically, high temperature suppresses SA signaling, whereas enhanced light promotes SA accumulation and NPR1 activation. Consistent with this, NPR1 was down-regulated in both genotypes from 9:00 to 14:00. However, from 14:00 to 17:00, NPR1 showed slight up-regulation in L114 but continuous down-regulation in L17, suggesting that L114 rapidly reactivated SA signaling to protect photosystems and strengthen antioxidant defenses. Across all time points, L114 exhibited stronger SA pathway activity than L17 (Fig. 4B).

The GA receptor GID1 and the growth-repressing DELLA proteins were down-regulated initially and up-regulated later, consistent with the GA pathway’s dynamic role in balancing growth and stress adaptation (Fig. 4B).

Antioxidant protection under high-temperature and high-light stress

Under high-temperature and high-light stress, plants experience severe photo-oxidative pressure, which often leads to decreased photosynthetic efficiency, excessive accumulation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and disruption of cellular metabolic homeostasis. To mitigate these deleterious effects, plants activate a sophisticated antioxidant defense system that safeguards cellular metabolism and structural integrity.

In our study, we quantified the activities of peroxidase (POD), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT). At 14:00, CAT activity increased markedly in all materials, with L114 exhibiting the highest enzymatic activity. By 17:00, CAT activity in L17 had decreased substantially, whereas L114 maintained a comparatively high CAT activity. CAT scavenges H₂O₂, preventing cellular damage; SOD is critical for eliminating superoxide radicals (O₂⁻); and ACOX catalyzes the oxidation of long-chain acyl-CoA, thereby reducing lipid peroxides and enhancing stress tolerance. POD activity exhibited a significant increase in L114, whereas no significant changes were observed at any of the three time points in L17. SOD activity did not differ significantly between the two genotypes (Fig. 4A–C). We further examined the expression dynamics of CAT‑associated genes. In L114, CAT expression was significantly upregulated at 14:00, with no significant change observed at 17:00. In contrast, transcript levels in L17 remained statistically unchanged across all three sampling points, a pattern that diverges from the observed elevation in CAT enzyme activity at 14:00. Notably, although CAT activity in L114 was higher than in L17 at both 14:00 and 17:00, transcript abundance in L114 was consistently lower than in L17 at these time points. This discrepancy may reflect influences from post‑transcriptional regulation, cofactor availability, inhibitory factors, enzyme stability, or other regulatory mechanisms (Fig. 5A–D). We additionally profiled the expression changes of other genes associated with antioxidant enzyme activities.

Photosynthetic responses under high temperature and high light

High light intensity is one of the primary factors driving the midday depression of photosynthesis. Under excessive irradiance, the photosynthetic apparatus—particularly photosystem II (PSII)—undergoes photochemical damage, resulting in decreased photosynthetic efficiency. To cope with such stress, plants modulate the expression of photosynthesis-related genes, thereby protecting their photosystems and adjusting to fluctuating environments.

Lhca1–5 encode the peripheral light-harvesting antenna proteins of photosystem I (PSI), belonging to the chlorophyll a/b-binding LHC family. These proteins channel absorbed excitation energy directionally to the PSI reaction center P700, enabling efficient electron excitation and downstream electron transfer. Lhcb1–7 encode the PSII antenna proteins (LHCII), also chlorophyll a/b-binding complexes rich in chlorophyll b, which largely determine the characteristic green coloration of leaves. We examined the expression changes of genes associated with these proteins and found differential expression of Lhca1, Lhca3, Lhca4, Lhcb2, and Lhcb4 between the two genotypes. In L114, Lhca1, Lhca3, Lhca4, Lhcb2, and Lhcb4 exhibited a sustained downregulation, whereas in L17 these genes were upregulated at 14 h, which may have enhanced light harvesting under high light and thereby exacerbated photodamage and oxidative stress (Fig. 6). Additionally, Lhca2, Lhca5, Lhcb1, Lhcb3, Lhcb5, Lhcb6, and Lhcb7 were consistently downregulated in both genotypes (Fig. 6).

Carbon metabolism

Under high-temperature and high-light conditions, carbon metabolism regulates the assimilation rate of the Calvin cycle, sucrose/starch biosynthesis and transport, and its coordination with photorespiration. These processes collectively determine the efficiency of ATP and NADPH regeneration and utilization, thereby influencing the chloroplast’s capacity to dissipate excess excitation energy and reducing power. Consequently, carbon metabolic flux plays a central role in determining the extent of PSII photoinhibition and overall photosynthetic performance. In this study, differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in carbon-metabolism-related pathways.

Hexokinase 1/3 (HK1 and HK3), which catalyze the phosphorylation of glucose to generate glucose-6-phosphate (G6P), represent the entry point of both glycolysis and the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway (OPPP). We identified HK1- and HK3-associated genes and found that their transcript levels remained largely unchanged in L114 at 14:00, some genes were significantly up-regulated at 17 : 00. By contrast, L17 exhibited pronounced up- or down-regulation at 14:00, and displayed one markedly down-regulated gene at 17:00, while most others showed no significant changes. Compared with L17, L114 maintained relatively stable HK1/HK3 expression under midday stress conditions, suggesting a stronger buffering capacity in maintaining sugar-metabolic homeostasis. Glucose-6-phosphate isomerase (GPI), which catalyzes the reversible interconversion between G6P and fructose-6-phosphate (F6P), represents the second step of glycolysis. We observed that GPI expression was broadly repressed in L114, The expression was up-regulated in L17 and then down-regulated. Nevertheless, L114 consistently exhibited higher GPI transcript abundance than L17 at the same time points. This pattern indicates that although L114 down-regulates GPI activity, it maintains a relatively elevated and less fluctuating expression level, reflecting a more conservative and stable metabolic strategy (Fig. 7A).

Discussion

With ongoing global climate warming, extreme heat events have become increasingly frequent, and the area affected by high temperature continues to expand [52, 53]. In field environments, heat stress is often accompanied by high light, and their combined effects can severely compromise crop yield and quality [54, 55]. Understanding transcriptional responses to combined high-temperature and high-light stress is therefore important for elucidating plant acclimation mechanisms. Under natural conditions, photosynthesis usually peaks in the morning and then declines around midday when temperature and irradiance increase, a phenomenon known as the midday depression of photosynthesis [56]. This response is regulated by multiple physiological and molecular factors, including transpiration, intercellular CO₂ concentration, stomatal conductance, chlorophyll content, and gene transcription [56, 57]. In this study, we compared two S. spontaneum genotypes with similar net photosynthetic rates but contrasting capacities to restore photosynthesis after midday depression. The results suggest that L114 activates a more coordinated stress-response program than L17, involving phytohormone signaling, antioxidant defense, photosynthetic regulation, and carbon metabolism.

Phytohormones integrate growth, development, and defense responses under environmental stress [58]. ABA is a key abiotic-stress hormone and can reduce water loss by promoting stomatal closure [59]. In the present study, ABA biosynthetic and receptor genes were upregulated during stress and downregulated after stress relief, consistent with typical ABA regulation under stress conditions [60, 61]. Ethylene is also induced by high temperature and high light and remodels transcriptional networks through the EIN2–EIN3/EIL1–ERF pathway. Impaired ethylene signaling reduces basal thermotolerance, whereas enhanced ethylene signaling improves thermotolerance, survival, and recovery capacity [62–64]. Ethylene also regulates non-photochemical quenching, the xanthophyll cycle, ROS metabolism, and, under prolonged stress, chlorophyll degradation and senescence [18, 65–67]. In rice and Arabidopsis, EBF1-related modules are involved in heat responses and temperature acclimation [68, 69]. Thus, the genotype-dependent expression of EBF1 observed here may partly contribute to the contrasting stress responses of L114 and L17 by modulating ethylene-signaling output [70]. JA signaling may also participate in recovery from combined heat and high-light stress. High-light-induced ROS production can promote JA biosynthesis, and JA signaling during recovery activates genes associated with repair and antioxidant processes [71]. Exogenous methyl jasmonate enhances heat tolerance in perennial ryegrass, and accumulation of JA and JA-Ile has been reported under combined high-temperature and high-light stress [38, 72]. COI1 perceives bioactive jasmonate signals and promotes degradation of JA repressors, thereby activating JA-responsive gene expression [73]. Studies in citrus, maize, and Arabidopsis have linked COI1-mediated signaling to defense metabolism, growth regulation, photosynthetic efficiency, and recovery after high-light or heat stress [71, 74–76]. Thus, the dynamic expression of COI1 in L114 may reflect activation of COI1-dependent JA signaling and contribute to adaptation to combined stress. Auxin-related genes further indicate a shift between growth and stress acclimation. GH3 family members reduce the pool of bioactive auxin and participate in auxin, JA, and SA homeostasis [77]. Under combined temperature and light cues, accumulation of IAA conjugates is often accompanied by altered GH3 expression [78]. In this study, GH3 expression paralleled that of EBF1, suggesting that L114 may transiently restrict growth and favor homeostatic maintenance during stress. SAUR genes are also modulated under heat and high-light conditions to balance photostress tolerance with elongation-mediated heat dissipation [79]. The stronger dynamic changes in SAUR expression in L114 may therefore be associated with earlier release of growth inhibition and post-stress recovery.

ROS, including H₂O₂, can cause oxidative damage but also act as signaling molecules involved in defense, heat-stress responses, and hormone regulation [80, 81]. Under combined high-temperature and high-light stress, excessive ROS must be rapidly removed to prevent oxidative injury [82]. Consistently, CAT and POD activities generally increase under these conditions to eliminate H₂O₂ [83, 84]. Although SOD gene expression was significantly upregulated, SOD enzyme activity did not change significantly. This may reflect inherently high basal SOD activity in both genotypes, reducing the need for further post-translational adjustment of superoxide dismutation. Elevated ROS can also induce membrane lipid peroxidation and accumulation of lipid oxidation derivatives [85]. Epoxide hydrolase 2 (EPHX2) contributes to epoxide detoxification and cellular homeostasis [86, 87]. Its significant upregulation may therefore facilitate detoxification of reactive epoxides and adjustment of cuticular lipid metabolism, potentially strengthening barrier properties and reducing water loss [87, 88].

Photosystems are major targets of combined heat and high-light stress. PSII captures light energy and catalyzes water splitting, whereas PSI drives NADP⁺ reduction using electrons derived from PSII [89, 90]. Under high temperature and irradiance, PSII is vulnerable to photoinhibition because excess excitation energy and impaired electron transport promote ROS generation and damage to reaction-center proteins [91]. PsbD encodes the PSII reaction-center D2 protein and is involved in PSII stability and repair [92]. Its upregulation has been associated with enhanced thermotolerance of photosynthesis in wheat and high-light acclimation in Arabidopsis [93, 94]. Therefore, the significant upregulation of PsbD in L114 is consistent with stronger PSII maintenance under combined stress. In contrast, Lhca and Lhcb genes, including Lhca1, Lhca3, Lhca4, Lhcb2, and Lhcb4, were persistently downregulated in L114. Because these proteins are involved in PSI/PSII antenna function, excitation energy distribution, non-photochemical quenching, and high-light acclimation [95–98]. their downregulation may represent a photoprotective strategy that reduces effective antenna size, limits excess excitation pressure, and suppresses ROS generation.

Carbon metabolism was also transcriptionally adjusted under combined stress. When excitation energy exceeds carbon-assimilation capacity, plants often prioritize photoprotection and maintenance of the photosynthetic apparatus [99] PGK2 links photosynthetic carbon assimilation with plastidial energy metabolism and carbon allocation [100]. Sustained PGK2 upregulation may support chloroplast carbon metabolism, energy balance, and stress-related metabolic reprogramming, whereas PGK2 downregulation may indicate energy conservation, defense prioritization, or reduced chloroplast carbon assimilation [101, 102]. GPI/PGI interconverts G6P and F6P and regulates carbon partitioning among Calvin-cycle product export, starch and sucrose biosynthesis, glycolysis, and the oxidative pentose phosphate pathway. Its downregulation under high-temperature and high-light stress may attenuate metabolic flux and help prioritize stress survival over growth [103].

Although this study provides transcriptomic insights into the response of S. spontaneum to combined high-temperature and high-light stress, several limitations remain. First, the absence of parallel measurements of leaf temperature and chlorophyll fluorescence limits direct links between transcriptional changes and physiological processes. Second, although the transcriptomic analysis revealed clear differences between the two genotypes, the limited sample size restricts the generalizability of the conclusions. Future studies should include a larger number of genotypes to validate these findings, hormone-related inferences from transcriptomic data require validation through direct hormone quantification. Third, future studies should promptly perform functional validation of the transcription factors identified via WGCNA. Finally, the relevance of these responses to cultivated sugarcane remains to be experimentally verified.

Conclusions

This study represents the first combined physiological and transcriptomic analysis of post-midday recovery in S. spontaneum, providing novel targets for its utilization in sugarcane breeding and identifying the germplasm 82–114 with high net photosynthetic rate and rapid recovery capacity after midday depression. By integrating physiological measurements, transcriptome profiling, and weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA), we identified candidate pathways and key candidate genes associated with high photosynthetic efficiency and rapid recovery after midday cessation. Comparative transcriptomic analysis between the strongly recovering accession 82–114 and the weakly recovering accession 2021-17 revealed that 82–114 reorganizes through modulation of hormonal signaling, antioxidant defense, photosynthetic machinery, and energy metabolism to mitigate photodamage and facilitate rapid recovery, these responses establish a temporal regulatory framework of “flux restriction protection — rapid repair — restoration of high photosynthetic efficiency”. These candidate pathways in S. spontaneum not only sustain photosynthetic homeostasis under combined high-temperature and high-light stress but also represent promising targets to enhance resilience and productivity in cultivated sugarcane under increasingly extreme climatic conditions. Moreover, the identified wild germplasm and key regulatory genes provide a foundation for molecular-assisted breeding and the development of sugarcane varieties with improved stress recovery and yield stability under heat and high-light environments. We identify recovery-associated pathways in a major wild donor of sugarcane and prioritize candidate regulators for validation in cultivated sugarcane.
