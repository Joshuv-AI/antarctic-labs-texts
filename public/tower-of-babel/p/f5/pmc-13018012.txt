Moyamoya syndrome associated with hereditary spherocytosis: pathogenesis, management, and an illustrative case with rapid radiological progression
Authors: Lin Yan, Xia Lu, Bin Yang, Yan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe association between moyamoya syndrome (MMS) and hereditary spherocytosis (HS) is exceedingly rare, with only eight pediatric cases reported to date. This review explores the clinical intersection, pathophysiological theories, and management challenges of this dual pathology.Case descriptionWe present a 13-year-old boy with known HS who presented with acute left limb weakness. Imaging confirmed MMS. Over a 3-year follow-up, serial magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and digital subtraction angiography (DSA) demonstrated significant progression of cerebrovascular steno-occlusive disease. Management included chronic anemia correction, aspirin therapy, subsequent laparoscopic splenectomy, and ultimately, unilateral encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis (EDAS) revascularization surgery for symptomatic progression.ConclusionThis case, the ninth reported, suggests that chronic hemolysis and anemia in HS may potentiate rapid progression of MMS. It indicates the necessity of vigilant neurological monitoring in HS patients, advocates for maintaining hemoglobin levels > 110 g/L, and discusses the integrated roles of splenectomy and revascularization surgery. Early recognition and a multidisciplinary treatment strategy are crucial to mitigating stroke risk in this unique patient population.Keywords: Hereditary spherocytosis, Moyamoya syndrome, Stroke, Splenectomy, Cerebral revascularization

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a rare, progressive, non-atherosclerotic, non-inflammatory cerebrovasculopathy characterized by bilateral stenosis or occlusion of the terminal internal carotid arteries (ICAs) and their proximal branches, accompanied by the formation of a compensatory collateral network (“moyamoya” vessels) at the base of the brain. It is a significant cause of childhood stroke. It represents a significant cause of childhood stroke, accounting for approximately 6% of cases. Moyamoya syndrome (MMS) refers to the moyamoya vasculopathy occurring in association with underlying conditions such as hematologic disorders, neurofibromatosis type 1, or prior cranial irradiation [1, 2].

Hereditary spherocytosis (HS) is the most common inherited hemolytic anemia in individuals of Northern European descent, with a prevalence of 1 in 2000. It results from mutations in genes encoding red blood cell (RBC) membrane skeleton proteins (e.g., ANK1, SPTA1, SPTB, SLC4A1, EPB42), leading to loss of membrane surface area, formation of spheroidal and less deformable erythrocytes, chronic extravascular hemolysis, and anemia [3, 4].

While MMS is a well-established complication of sickle cell disease, driven by recurrent vaso-occlusive crises and endothelial damage, its association with HS is remarkably scarce [5]. To date, only eight pediatric cases have been described in the literature [6–13]. The pathophysiological link remains speculative, but chronic hemolytic stress, anemia-induced cerebral hemodynamic changes, and potential endothelial dysfunction are implicated [14]. This dual diagnosis poses significant challenges in timely recognition and optimal management. This mini-review, anchored by a detailed case presentation, aims to synthesize existing knowledge on the pathogenesis, clinical presentation, diagnostic approach, management strategies, and outcomes of MMS associated with HS, emphasizing lessons learned from a case with documented rapid radiological progression.

Historical background

The seminal description of MMD by Takeuchi and Shimizu in 1957 laid the foundation for understanding this unique vasculopathy. The association between moyamoya vasculopathy and systemic conditions evolved over subsequent decades. The link with hemolytic anemias was firmly established through extensive literature on sickle cell disease (SCD), where the prevalence of MMS is significantly elevated [15].

The first reported case linking HS and MMS was published in 1998, describing a 5-year-old boy [6]. Over the next three decades, sporadic case reports emerged, with the most recent prior to this report being published in 2024 [13]. This scant literature, totaling eight cases before the present one, is consistent with the association but leaves many questions unanswered regarding natural history and optimal intervention. The prevailing hypothesis has shifted from coincidence to a plausible causal relationship, where the chronic hematological perturbations of HS create a permissive environment for the development or acceleration of intracranial arteriopathy.

Clinical presentation

Patients with HS-associated MMS consistently present in childhood, with a mean age at neurological onset of approximately 6–7 years across reported cases. The clinical presentation is primarily ischemic, mirroring the typical pediatric MMD phenotype.

Neurological symptoms

Transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), often manifesting as reversible limb weakness (hemiparesis) or numbness, are the most common heralding events. These episodes are frequently precipitated by factors that exacerbate the underlying anemia or increase cerebral metabolic demand, such as fever, infection, dehydration, or acute hemolytic crises. Headache, seizures, and involuntary movements (chorea) are less frequent but reported presentations. A critical diagnostic clue is the temporal correlation between neurological symptoms and periods of worsened anemia or physiological stress [2].

Systemic signs of HS

Concurrent systemic findings are invariably present and should raise suspicion. These include pallor, jaundice (due to unconjugated hyperbilirubinemia), and splenomegaly, which is present in virtually all untreated patients. A history of gallstones, even in childhood, is common due to chronic bilirubin overproduction [16].

The index of suspicion for MMS must be high in any child with HS who presents with any acute neurological symptom, however, transient.

Diagnosis

A definitive diagnosis requires conclusive evidence for both HS and MMS, with neuroimaging confirming the vasculopathy.

Diagnosis of hereditary spherocytosis

This relies on a combination of the following: (1) positive family history (autosomal dominant pattern is common); (2) peripheral blood smear demonstrating spherocytes (small, dense RBCs lacking central pallor); (3) laboratory evidence of hemolysis: increased reticulocyte count, elevated indirect bilirubin and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH), and low haptoglobin; (4) confirmatory tests: increased osmotic fragility or, more specifically, a reduced eosin-5′-maleimide (EMA) binding flow cytometry test. Molecular genetic testing can identify the causative mutation and is particularly useful in atypical or recessive cases [3].

Diagnosis of moyamoya syndrome

Neuroimaging is the cornerstone. Brain MRI may show acute or chronic infarcts, often in watershed or frontal regions. Time-of-flight MR angiography (TOF-MRA) typically reveals steno-occlusion of the distal ICAs and proximal anterior/middle cerebral arteries with basal collateral vessels. The definitive diagnostic standard remains digital subtraction angiography (DSA), which precisely delineates the steno-occlusive pattern and the “puff of smoke” collaterals, while also assessing hemodynamics. In patients with HS presenting with any neurological symptom, a high index of suspicion for MMS is warranted, prompting timely vascular imaging [1].

Management

Management is dual-pronged, targeting both the hematological driver (HS) and the cerebrovascular consequence (MMS). A multidisciplinary team involving hematology, neurology, and neurosurgery is an important consideration.

Hematological management

The goal is to mitigate the chronic hemolytic and anemic stress hypothesized to fuel the vasculopathy.

Medical therapy

Folate supplementation is standard. Maintaining a hemoglobin level > 110 g/L is often recommended to prevent anemic hypoxia that could precipitate TIAs. The role of chronic transfusion therapy, as used in SCD, is not established in HS-MMS but may be considered transiently in high-risk settings [17].

Splenectomy

This is the definitive treatment for moderate to severe HS. It markedly reduces hemolysis, corrects anemia, and eliminates transfusion dependence [16]. In the context of MMS, its timing is critical. It can be performed prior to revascularization to stabilize the patient hematologically or may be necessitated by symptomatic HS. Laparoscopic splenectomy is preferred to minimize surgical trauma and postoperative pain. The long-term risks of post-splenectomy sepsis (necessitating vaccinations and antibiotic prophylaxis) and possible increased thrombotic risk must be managed.

Neurological and cerebrovascular management

Antiplatelet therapy

Low-dose aspirin (3–5 mg/kg/day) is widely used to prevent thromboembolic events in the stenotic or collateral vessels and is considered standard medical therapy for MMD/MMS.

Revascularization surgery

This is the mainstay for preventing recurrent strokes in patients with symptomatic progression or evidence of compromised CVR [18]. Surgical options include:

Direct bypass: Superficial temporal artery-to-middle cerebral artery (STA-MCA) anastomosis. Provides immediate blood flow augmentation but is technically challenging in young children with small vessels.

The decision for surgery is based on recurrent TIAs or strokes despite medical therapy, radiographic progression, and objectively impaired CVR.

Importantly, once the decision for surgical intervention is made—particularly on the side corresponding to the patient’s symptoms or hemodynamic compromise—delaying the procedure is not advisable. The symptomatic hemisphere is at imminent risk of recurrent ischemic events. Furthermore, the pathophysiological rationale for early intervention extends beyond immediate ischemia prevention. The fragile “moyamoya” collateral vessels, while compensatory, are prone to progressive and abnormal dilatation due to chronic hemodynamic stress. These vessels are inherently weak and are the primary source of intracranial hemorrhage in adult MMD patients. Timely revascularization surgery not only alleviates ischemia but also reduces the long-term hemodynamic demand on these abnormal collaterals, thereby potentially slowing their progression and mitigating the future risk of hemorrhagic stroke. This dual benefit—preventing both ischemic and hemorrhagic complications—suggests the importance of prompt surgical intervention in progressive MMS.

Prognosis and outcomes

The natural history of HS-associated MMS is not fully defined due to its rarity. Untreated, it carries a high risk of recurrent ischemic strokes, leading to cumulative neurological deficits, cognitive impairment, and reduced quality of life. Data from the nine reported cases (including ours) provide preliminary insights:

Infarct patterns: Frontal lobe infarcts are most common, followed by basal ganglia and watershed territories, consistent with ACA/MCA borderzone ischemia.

Exemplary case description

We present the case of a 13-year-old boy, which constitutes the ninth reported instance of HS-associated MMS and provides the longest documented serial imaging follow-up demonstrating rapid progression.

Initial presentation (age 7)

The patient, with a known family history of HS (mother and brother affected), presented with fever followed in 3 days by acute left-sided weakness and numbness. MRI revealed an acute infarct in the right frontal lobe. MR angiography suggested intracranial vasculopathy. He was diagnosed with cerebral infarction and possible vasculitis in the setting of HS. He received packed RBC transfusions for anemia (Hb 108 g/L) and was discharged with residual left hemiplegia.

Diagnostic confirmation at our center (age 10)

He presented with recurrent headaches. DSA confirmed the diagnosis of MMS (Suzuki Stage III), showing stenosis of the terminal ICAs and prominent moyamoya collaterals. Genetic testing identified a heterozygous pathogenic variant in SPTB (c.4291C > T; p.Arg1431*), confirming HS type 2. Hemoglobin was 95–108 g/L with ongoing hemolysis (elevated reticulocytes, LDH, bilirubin). Abdominal ultrasound confirmed splenomegaly and gallstones. He was started on aspirin (5 mg/kg/day) (Fig. 1).

Hematological intervention

Due to symptomatic HS, he underwent an uncomplicated laparoscopic splenectomy at a local children’s hospital 1 year later.

Neurological progression and surgical intervention

One year post-splenectomy (age 12), he experienced new-onset right limb TIAs (Fig. 2). Repeat DSA showed significant progression of the steno-occlusive disease, particularly on the left side. Given symptomatic progression and the recognition that further delay could expose him to irreversible ischemic injury and promote the ongoing development of fragile collaterals, he underwent a left-sided encephaloduroarteriosynangiosis (EDAS) procedure (Fig. 3). His postoperative course was uneventful.

Follow-up

Surveillance DSA 1 year post-EDAS (age 13) demonstrated excellent development of collateral vessels from the external carotid circulation on the operated side, with complete resolution of his TIAs (Fig. 4). The contralateral side remained stable. He remains neurologically intact on aspirin at his latest follow-up (age 13). Limitations

This study has several limitations inherent to its design as a single-case report combined with a mini-review of the sparse existing literature. First, as a single case observation, the findings and the proposed treatment algorithm cannot be generalized to all patients with HS-associated MMS. The rapid radiological progression observed in our patient may not represent the natural history for all individuals with this rare association. Second, the retrospective nature of the case report and the literature review is subject to publication bias, as cases with positive outcomes or striking features are more likely to be reported. Third, the pathophysiological link between HS and MMS remains speculative, based on a “second-hit” hypothesis, as we lack direct molecular or histopathological evidence from the affected cerebral vessels. Fourth, the follow-up period in our case, while providing valuable longitudinal data, is still relatively short (3 years) to assess long-term outcomes, particularly the risk of de novo or hemorrhagic strokes in adulthood. Finally, the optimal sequence and timing of splenectomy versus revascularization surgery remain undefined and require further investigation through collaborative, multi-institutional registries.

Conclusions and future perspectives

The association between HS and MMS, though rare, is clinically significant and likely under-recognized. The chronic hemolysis, intermittent anemic hypoxia, and potential endothelial injury inherent to HS are hypothesized to create a “second-hit” environment that can unmask or accelerate a predisposition to moyamoya vasculopathy. Our case, with its objective evidence of rapid progression, strengthens this causal hypothesis.

Key management principles emerge from this review:

High index of suspicion: Any neurological symptom in a child with HS should prompt urgent cerebrovascular imaging (MRI/MRA).

Multidisciplinary strategy: Care must be coordinated between hematology and neurosurgery. Control of the hematological disease (goal Hb > 110 g/L, timely splenectomy) is foundational.

Individualized revascularization: Revascularization surgery remains the definitive treatment for preventing both ischemic and, potentially, long-term hemorrhagic complications in progressive MMS. The decision should be guided by symptoms and objective hemodynamic assessment, and when indicated, should be performed promptly to maximize benefit.

Future efforts should be directed towards establishing an international registry for such rare associations to better define natural history and optimize treatment protocols. Basic science research exploring the precise molecular links between RBC membrane defects, hemolytic byproducts (e.g., cell-free heme, arginase-1 release), and intracranial arteriopathy is warranted [20, 21]. For now, awareness, early diagnosis, and an integrated treatment approach offer the best hope for preserving neurological function in these vulnerable patients.
