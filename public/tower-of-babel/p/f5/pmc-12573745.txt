Ion Size Effects on the Thermodynamic, Kinetic, and Mechanical Properties during Ion Exchange in Solid-State Electrolytes
Authors: Harsh D. Jagad, Stephen J. Harris, Brian W. Sheldon, Yue Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ion exchange offers a pathway to impose residual compressive stresses to mitigate the electro-chemo-mechanical cracking of solid-state electrolytes such as lithium lanthanum zirconium oxide. This study uses a coupled multiscale framework (integrating density functional theory (DFT), molecular dynamics (MD), and continuum modeling) to examine how exchange ion size influences stress, diffusion, fracture toughness, and electronic properties. Larger isovalent ions (Na+, Ag+, K+) were exchanged with Li+, with DFT confirming their preference for octahedral 96h sites and a linear relationship between ion size and chemical free expansion coefficient. MD simulations reveal stress and concentration effects on exchange ion diffusivity at elevated temperatures, with Na+ and Ag+ maintaining favorable mobility while K+ showing concentration-dependent clustering. Continuum modeling predicts the range of fracture strength improvements and the required ion exchange concentration profile. It was shown that a 5% surface exchange concentration can induce ∼0.6 GPa of surface compressive stress using Na+ and ∼1.0 GPa of surface compressive stress using Ag+. On the other hand, larger ion exchange species may penalize Li+ diffusivity by increasing the activation volume and activation energy. Interestingly, Na+ has a negligible penalty on Li-ion diffusivity. The room temperature Li+ ion diffusivity is reduced by ∼40% with Ag+ ion exchange. Electronic band structure analysis shows no size-dependent change in the bandgap, though Ag+ introduces localized defect states near the valence band maximum. This study highlights ion size as a key factor in optimizing LLZO properties, offering a framework to improve the solid-state battery performance.

Introduction

Oxide-based Li7La3Zr2O12 (LLZO) is a unique solid-state electrolyte material for solid-state batteries, with high ionic conductivity, , stability against Li metal, , and a wide electronic bandgap. , Yet, when LLZO is paired with a Li metal anode, Li metal filaments penetrate and mechanically crack the LLZO. − It has been demonstrated by various experiments and modeling that cracking at the LLZO/lithium metal anode is electro-chemo-mechanically coupled − by several factors that can accelerate the Li filament growth. Electronic conductivity is provided by electron density on preexisting surface-type defects, including surface pores, grain boundaries, surface flaws, and newly formed crack surfaces. , This electron density is trapped on La-ions in these defects, which can reduce Li+ ions to metallic lithium, causing slow metallic lithium filament formation inside LLZO. The current focus can lead to enhanced lithium flux into these filaments, causing compressive stress in the lithium metal. This will, in turn, create tensile stresses near the tips of crack-like flaws in the LLZO and lead to fast lithium filament propagation via fracture of the ceramic. ,,,

In previous works, we proposed inducing surface residual compressive in-plane stresses as an amelioration strategy to resist surface crack propagation and tackle this coupled degradation mechanism. , (Residual stresses are stresses that remain in a material when all outside loadings are removed.) The level of macroscopic stress to mitigate crack propagation is likely to be on the order of 200 MPa, as demonstrated by Fincher et al., who engineered biaxial mechanical loads in a Li6.6La3Zr1.6Ta0.4O12 (LLZTO) sample, although those loading conditions are different from the proposed surface residual compressive stress. Recently Xin et al. have shown that nanoscale doping of Ag on LLZTO caused subsurface Li/Ag ion exchange up to 50 nm and resisted nanoindentation forces, suggesting the effectiveness of surface modification on crack resistance.

Several methods, including ion implantation and ion exchange, were discussed by Qi et al. to induce surface residual compressive stress. Recently, ion-implantation-induced stress in LLZTO was reported by several experimental groups. Yao et al. used Xe implantation up to a depth of 60–70 nm, in which they claimed that compressive stress of ∼3–20 GPa was generated, depending on the dosage of Xe. Flatshcer et al. implanted Ag in LLZTO up to a depth of 1 μm and generated a compressive stress of ∼700 MPa. However, the ion implantation created an amorphization depth ∼650–700 nm. Thus, the ion-implantation method can generate substantial surface compressive stress but may induce surface amorphization due to high implantation energies and/or fluxes. In general, submicron surface compression layers will not significantly impact fracture that initiates from micron-scale surface flaws, which include the intrinsic grain boundaries with a length scale of typical grain size in LLZO. The amorphization in the ion-implanted region due to the bombardment of high-energy foreign ions on the lattice may also hinder the ionic conductivity of Li+, which manifests as a large increase in impedance. ,

Unlike ion implantation, ion exchange may provide residual compressive stress without (or with minimal) amorphization. In previous work, we modeled the use of K+ to induce residual compression in LLZO using ion exchange with micron-level depth. Although the discussion of the depth level of ion exchange in solid-state battery electrolytes is limited, this process has been explored extensively in glasses. In commercial glasses, typically a high-temperature (600 K) molten salt precursor generates diffusion-controlled concentration (and hence stress) profiles of the ion exchange, which can be tens to hundreds of microns thick. For example, Belostotsky ion-exchanged Na+ with K+ using a molten KNO3 salt for a depth of up to 35 μm. Gy reported an exchange depth from 40 to 100 μm depending on the process time and temperature, with a peak compressive stress of 600 MPa. Tyagi et al. showed a Li+/Na+ exchange in mixed aluminosilicate glass, resulting in an exchange depth of 200 μm with a peak compressive stress of 1200 MPa. The ion exchange concentration profiles typically follow an error function, erf (x), in these thermal diffusion processes. Electric fields can be used to enhance the ion exchange process in addition to thermal diffusion (called field-assisted ion exchange) and to develop nearly uniform concentration profiles. − An accurate understanding of the macroscopic concentration profile of the ion exchange is necessary as it dictates the stress profile in the solid electrolyte. Our previous work assumed a shallow depth of ion exchange with no concentration gradient. We show later in this article that while the uniform concentration model can predict reasonable concentration and stress profiles, it does not accurately capture the gain in the apparent fracture toughness.

We expect two distinct differences between ion exchange in glass versus in crystalline LLZO. One is related to ion-exchange-induced stress, and the other to Li+ conductivity. The expectation that larger ions deliver larger residual compressive stress will be limited in glassy material , due to viscous stress relaxation of amorphous networks. − Ion exchange in crystalline LLZO is not limited by this type of relaxation mechanism. LLZO can also tolerate much higher temperatures than glasses; thus, it may be possible to capitalize on more extensive diffusion at high temperatures for the ion exchange process. Crystalline oxide structures generally exhibit linear elastic mechanical responses at the elevated temperatures needed for ion exchange in these types of materials. Thus, it may be possible to engineer large stresses in LLZO, via ions with larger sizes or higher concentrations. However, the ion exchange scheme in LLZO may not be easily transferred to other solid electrolytes exhibiting viscous flows. The diffusion of ions being exchanged in glasses has been well-studied and exhibits the “mixed alkali effect”, , where the introduced ions exhibit concentration-dependent diffusivities, and the native /host ions show decreased diffusivity due to increased activation energy. These effects generally do not have a significant effect on materials that are not expected to have fast ion conductivity. However, it can have a profound impact on solid electrolytes whose merit lies in the fast conduction of Li+.

The goal of ion exchange is to “stuff” a larger ion into the subsurface region of the solid-state electrolyte at the electrolyte–electrode interface to provide residual in-plane biaxial compression (as opposed to a stack pressure, which leads to an out-of-plane stress). Considering only isovalent cations that are larger than Li+, and excluding radioactive, toxic, , and expensive cations, we focus on ion exchange with Na+, Ag+, and K+. Li+ can occupy both tetrahedral and octahedral sites in LLZO. The Shannon radii for Li+, Na+, Ag+, and K+ are 0.59, 0.99, 1.00, and 1.37 Å, respectively, in the tetrahedral sites; and they are 0.76, 1.02, 1.15, and 1.38 Å, respectively, in the octahedral sites. This suggests that Na+, Ag+, and K+ are 68, 69, and 132% larger than Li+ if occupying the tetrahedral site and 34, 51, and 81% larger in the octahedral site. Therefore, several key questions arise with respect to the impact of ionic size effects: the site occupancy; the variation of chemical strain with ion size; the appropriate ion exchange concentration gradient to induce compressive stress on the order of hundreds of MPa; and whether they hinder Li+ ion diffusion at room temperature.

This multifaceted investigation is necessary to holistically understand the thermo-kinetic–mechanical behavior of ion exchange to optimize residual stresses without penalizing Li+ transport. In the absence of experimental evidence, we relied on a fully self-consistent multiscale model framework to address these key questions about ion exchange in LLZO when Li+ is exchanged with the larger isovalent ions Na+, Ag+, and K+. The model for macroscopic stress induced by the ion exchange concentration gradient is similar to the diffusion-induced stress (DIS) models for electrode materials − and ion-exchanged glasses. Traditionally, these continuum models reply on fitting the diffusion coefficients and chemical strains to experimental measurements. , Qi et al. predicted Li concentration-dependent elastic constants and chemical strain from density functional theory (DFT) calculations for the continuum DIS modeling and showed better agreement with the in situ strain maps in graphite electrodes and single crystalline Ni-rich electrode particles. These efforts motivated this work to develop a fully self-consistent multiscale ion exchange model coupling the chemical strain/stress with the diffusion coefficient, which can then be used to predict the ion exchange concentration profile and fracture toughness increase.

Computational Methods

Figure shows the flowchart of this fully self-consistent multiscale modeling framework, which integrates different modeling approaches. At the atomic scale, DFT calculations (purple box in Figure ) were performed to evaluate the site selectivity of the ion exchange and determine the most stable configurations. The most stable configurations were then used to evaluate the ion exchange formation energy, E f, which is combined with other DFT results to screen various salt precursors to predict the feasible ion exchange salts/precursors. The effect of ion exchange on the electronic properties and the chemical free expansion coefficients, γ, were also evaluated at the DFT level. Defect sampling also provided a data set to train and finetune the forcefield used for our molecular dynamics (MD) calculations. MD simulations (red outline in Figure ) were used to determine the diffusion coefficients of Li+-ions, D Li, and ion exchange, D IX. Both can be a function of temperature (T), stress (σ c ), and concentration of ion exchange (C IX). These predicted stress–concentration-coupled diffusion coefficients D IX (T, σ c , C IX) and the chemical free expansion coefficient, γ, were used to develop a continuum-level diffusion-induced-stress model for ion exchange at microscale (golden outline in Figure ). The continuum-level model was then used to understand the improvement in the apparent fracture strength of LLZO and evaluate the risks of spontaneous fracture in LLZO.

Density Functional Theory Calculations

The cubic I-3m structure for Li7La3Zr2O12 was used with a lattice parameter of 13.003 Å. The simulation box of LLZO has 8 formula units, thus Li56La24Zr16O96. The Li+ distribution was based on rules provided in refs ,− . All site sampling calculations were done using density functional theory (DFT) in the Vienna Ab Initio Simulation Package (VASP). A plane wave basis set was chosen with the energy cutoff of 600 eV. Electronic minimization was performed under the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the exchange correlational functional of Perdew, Burke, and Ezenhorf (PBE) using a Gaussian smearing of 0.1 eV. The electronic convergence criterion was set to 10–5 eV, and the force criterion was set to 0.03 eV/Å per atom. A k-point density of 1 × 1 × 1 was used. For the electronic density of states (DOS) calculation, the electronic occupancy was based on the tetrahedron method with a 2 × 2 × 2 Monkhorst Pack k-point mesh for higher accuracy.

Site sampling was conducted by exchanging 1 Li+ with 1 [Na+, Ag+, K+], equivalent to a concentration of C IX = 1.7% of ion exchange. The ionic positions were first relaxed with fixed cell shape and size. To obtain the structure at higher concentrations, the minimum energy structure with 1.7% ion exchange was used as the basis for further sampling at higher concentration (C IX = 3.4% for 2 exchanges in the simulation cell). Similarly, for 5.1% concentration sampling, 3.4% concentration was taken as the basis. Then, the constrained DFT cells are fully relaxed in size and shape, as well as the ionic positions at various concentrations of the ion exchange species (C IX = 0, 1.7, 3.4%).

The defect formation energy of ion exchange when exchanging n Li+ with n cations is written in the dilute limit as 1 Ef=(EIX−Ebulk)n+ΔμLi−ΔμIX where E IX and E bulk refer to the 0 K DFT energy of LLZO with ion exchange (IX) and bulk LLZO, respectively, and Δμ i refers to the change in the chemical potential of the species, i = Li or IX, depending on the anionic reference state. For a similar change in the anionic reference states (for example, Li2O vs Na2O/Ag2O/K2O), the difference in the change in chemical potential between Li+ and the ion exchange was approximated as ΔμLi – ΔμIX ∼ E Li DFT – E IX DFT, where E i DFT is the energy per alkali species obtained from the Materials Project database. Additional corrections necessary to obtain the temperature-dependent “defect formation free energy” are provided in the Supporting Information (SI).

Although each point defect induces a chemical-strain tensor, which can be calculated from DFT, , the average volume change due to the randomly distributed point defects can still be described by a scalar with isotropic chemical strains (εc). Therefore, the cube root of the volume of the freely expanded cell corresponds to the freely expanded lattice constant “a”, which depends on the concentration C IX (in terms of percentage of Li+). Molecular Dynamics Simulations

Molecular dynamics simulations were conducted to predict well-averaged diffusion coefficients of both Li+ and the ion exchange. To obtain a statistically accurate description for the low concentration of the ion exchange, a larger 2 × 3 × 3 supercell with 3456 atoms was used. Thus, the structure has 18 and 36 exchanged ions for C IX = 1.7 and 3.4%, respectively. All classical MD simulations were conducted in the package DL_POLY 4.10. The polarizable Bush forcefield included the long-range Coulombic interaction and the short-range Buckingham interaction with a core–shell model for oxygen atoms. The forcefield parameters for Li–O, Zr–O, and La–O were obtained from refs , while initial Na–O, Ag–O, and K–O parameters were obtained from ref and refitted using DFT sampling results. The parameters and the goodness of the fit between MD and DFT calculations are provided in Table SI.1 and Figure SI.1, respectively.

The MD protocol used an NVT ensemble with a Nosé thermostat set to 0.1 ps, an equilibration run for 1 ps, and a time step of 0.75 ps. MD trajectories were generated for 1.5 ns for temperatures in the range of 1200–900 K at 100 K intervals. Our previous results following this approach led to the activation energy of Li-ion diffusivity in pristine LLZO compared well with experiments. − To evaluate the stress–diffusion coupling, the cell parameters were varied such that the hydrostatic strains close to +1, 0, −1, and −2% were applied. At each strain state, NVT MD simulations were performed to obtain the diffusivity as a function of stress. These results were then fitted to stress–temperature-dependent diffusion coefficients, following a two-step fitting procedure established before.

Continuum Modeling of Concentration and Biaxial Residual Stress Profiles

Ion exchange can be performed in two distinct configurations: single-sided and two-sided. In principle, the single-sided ion exchange may be sufficient to arrest the growth of surface cracks of LLZO from the Li metal (anode) electrode side. However, a single-sided exchange may cause bending in the solid electrolyte, which may be used to characterize the ion-exchange-induced stress or may cause concerns when the solid electrolyte thickness is low. On the other hand, two-sided ion exchange does not face such restrictions as the net bending can cancel out. In addition, the two-sided exchange is easier for material handling during manufacturing. As two-sided exchange offers more benefits, we restrict ourselves to a two-sided ion exchange in this work, and a future work will discuss the mechanics of single-sided ion exchange. However, the two-sided exchange would generate larger tensile stress in the bulk of the electrolyte, as the net tension is contributed by two sides. Thus, special emphasis is placed on the analysis of the tensile stress in the bulk.

Results and Discussion

Site Selectivity of Ion Exchange

Li+ ions in LLZO prefer tetrahedral (T) sites over octahedral (O) sites by ∼0.2 eV. , This is because the Li–O bond distance in the tetrahedral site (1.94 Å) is closer to the native Li–O bond distance in Li2O (2.02 Å in the Fm3̅m structure), compared with the Li–O bond distance in the octahedral site (2.27 Å). As a result, the pristine LLZO simulation cell has 56 Li atoms occupying both the 24d tetrahedral sites (occupancy 54%) and 96h (occupancy 45%) octahedral sites, well-aligned with experiments.

To understand the site selectivity of ion exchange, we performed the following DFT calculations. One Li+ was exchanged with one Na+ or Ag+ or K+ in the DFT simulation box. Twenty-one different Li+ were chosen; 14 in the octahedral site and 7 in the tetrahedral site. For a particular ion exchange species, one of the 21 cases has the minimum energy. This structure was used as a reference state to compare the site occupancy energies, defined as ΔE f. Figure shows the distribution of ΔE f for the three ion exchanging species: Na+ exchange is shown in green triangles, Ag+ in orange squares, and K+ in navy circles. On the horizontal axis, the exchange with Li+ originally in an octahedral site is shown as O while the exchange with Li+ originally in the tetrahedral site as T. The * marked T configurations (*T1, *T2, *T3) in which the ion exchange was initially set in a tetrahedral site, however upon ionic relaxation, hopped into an octahedral site. Figure reveals that for all three ion exchange species, the lowest energy is an octahedral configuration (O13). Ion exchange stuck in tetrahedral configurations (T4–T7) has much higher energy compared to the octahedral cases and thus can lower their energies by hopping into a neighboring empty octahedron (*T1–*T3). This behavior can be understood by comparing the exchanged ion–O bond lengths in the native oxides. Na–O bond length in Fm3̅m Na2O (2.42 Å), K–O bond length in Fm3̅m K2O (2.80 Å), and Ag–O bond length in Pn3̅m Ag2O (2.09 Å) are larger than the allowable bond length in the tetrahedral site (1.94 Å).

Site selectivity of the larger ion exchange showing a preference for octahedral sites. The configurations in which the Li+ was exchanged out of an octahedral site are marked as ″O”; tetrahedral are marked with a “T”. The * marked configurations indicate that the ion-exchanged hopping from a tetrahedral to an octahedral site upon ionic relaxation.

Li-ions diffuse in LLZO via a connected network with alternating octahedral and tetrahedral sites. The octahedral site (48g/96h) is oppositely face-shared with two tetrahedral sites, which connect to four octahedral sites via face sharing. The ion exchange on the octahedral sites exhibits lower energy when the connected tetrahedral sites remain empty (O13). Thus, the ion exchange effectively blocks Li+-ion hopping through the occupied octahedral site, the two connected tetrahedral sites, and nearby six octahedral sites that connect to the tetrahedron. To ensure a percolated diffusion network for Li+-ions, the ion exchange concentration must be kept low, for example, < 5% of the Li+-ion concentration in this work.

Within the dilute defect limit, the concentration of ion exchange may be approximated as CIX∼e−Ef/RT . Assuming the ion exchange occurs at 1200 K for sufficient ion exchange mobility (discussed in Section ), it can be estimated that E f ∼ 0.3 eV would provide a concentration of ∼5%, which is required to obtain biaxial residual stress of 1 GPa (discussed in Section ). Negative E f, suggesting a corrosive (spontaneous) ion exchange reaction, is not recommended. These criteria allow us to screen precursors based on quick DFT calculations. For the ion exchange using Na+, Na3PO4 (E f = 0.29 eV) or NaF (E f = 0.45 eV) may be used. We add Na2O (E f = −0.03 eV) to the list as E f is only mildly negative. For the ion exchange using Ag+, AgNO3 (E f = 0.27 eV) or Ag3PO4 (E f = 0.22 eV) may be used. For the ion exchange with K+, the E f for all salts is higher than 1 eV and hence the thermal driving force may not yield sufficient K+ concentration, requiring additional driving forces. One such driving force could be oxygen gas release by using KO2 as a precursor to yield Li2O + O2(g) after ion exchange.

Next, we investigate if the dilute assumption is still valid upto 5.1% by evaluating E f at 3 different C IX values as shown in Figure SI.2. For simplicity, the reference state chosen was the native metal. The E f varies less than 0.025 eV per percentage change of C IX for Na+, Ag+, and K+, respectively, which we take as low. Thus, a dilute approximation can be assumed for ion exchange formation energy, at least up to C IX = 5%.

Free Expansion Coefficient, γ

Assuming the ion exchange concentration is dilute, the chemical strain should increase linearly with the concentration. This was confirmed by analyzing the freely expanded lattice constant (a) of ion-exchanged LLZO with all three cations, shown in Figure a. Note that the volume expansion due to ion exchange is not the elastic strain due to tensile stress. If the ion-exchanged region cannot fully expand due to the constraints, it will experience mechanical stress, which is modeled in Section by the continuum model. The continuum model requires the chemical-strain-derived free expansion coefficient (γ), computed according to eq . The comparison of γ against the size of three ion exchange species and Li+ in a 6-fold coordination is shown in Figure b. The dashed line is a linear fit between the ion size and the γ, showing a good fit (R 2 = 0.995). Thus, larger ion exchange species cause larger chemical strain in LLZO and the magnitude of the strain increases linearly. As a reference, we provide the strain at 3.4% of each exchanged ion, as Na+ ∼ 2.38 × 10–3, Ag+ ∼ 3.8 × 10–3, and K+ ∼ 5.94 × 10–3. High-Temperature Mobility of Ion Exchange Species in LLZO

To generate residual in-plane compressive stresses in subsurface LLZO, the ion exchange species need to diffuse into the LLZO. Experimentally, the ion exchange process would be carried out at an elevated temperature such that the high temperature promotes the exchange and diffusion of ions to set up the concentration profile. Once the desired profile is set, the system can be cooled to freeze this profile as the ion exchanging species are immobile at room temperature. To investigate the behavior of the ion exchange species at such temperatures, MD simulations were conducted at temperatures from 900 to 1250 K. Higher temperatures would enter the sintering regime of LLZO − and hence were avoided; lower temperatures cause insufficient hopping of the ion-exchanging species (see Figure SI.4a,b) within the time of our simulations.

We first discuss the behavior of K+ diffusion in LLZO at an elevated temperature. DFT simulations at 0 K suggest that the exchanging K+ defects are dilute until they reach a concentration of 3.4%. This was verified in MD simulations at 1.7% concentration, where the MSD for K+ increases linearly with time (Figures SI.4c and 5a). However, at 3.4%, the MSD shows a plateau in the diffusion after ∼600 ps, where the K+ ions hop aggressively in the beginning and end up clustered around each other as shown in Figure SI.5b. The clustering of K+ at 3.4% depends on the temperature and stress in the MD simulations. This was not captured at 0 K DFT calculations but is consistent with the large IX formation energy leading to a low solubility limit and phase segregation beyond the solubility limit. Therefore, K+ is no longer considered in the continuum models.

The trajectories of Na+ and Ag+ did not show clustering (within the simulation time). The MSD for Na+ and Ag+ in LLZO shows stress and C IX dependence (Figure SI.6a,b for Na+ and Figure SI.6c,d for Ag+). Although the defect formation energy and chemical strain follow the dilute limit at 0 K based on DFT calculations, high-temperature diffusion of ion exchange species may be more sensitive to the ion exchange concentration. First, we comment on the need to implement concentration gradients to accurately estimate the increased fracture toughness. Previously, we assumed a uniform concentration in a shallow exchange depth to estimate the compressive stress. A comparison of this uniform concentration model with the concentration gradient model is shown in Figure SI.7. Both cases have identical total dopant amounts and identical surface concentrations, so the concentration gradient model has a deeper ion exchange depth than the uniform concentration model. It is seen in Figure SI.7c that the uniform concentration model can predict the magnitude of the stress profiles reasonably well but underestimates the exchange depth. Furthermore, as shown in Figure SI.7d, it provides inaccurate gains in the fracture strength by overestimating the improvements with respect to the bulk at shallow exchange depths and underestimating them beyond the shallow depth because of abrupt truncation. Hence, we use only the concentration gradient model for further discussion.

Within the concentration gradient model, the exact magnitude of the compressive and resulting tensile stress depends on the temperature (T), time for diffusion (t), and surface concentration boundary condition (C s). t can be made dimensionless by scaling it to the time that the ion exchange species takes to diffuse across the electrolyte (characteristic time τ) to achieve a constant concentration. For a 1-mm-thick electrolyte pellet with two-sided exchange, the estimated τ = 2 × 105 s (∼2.3 days) was based on MD predicted diffusivity. The dimensionless time (t/τ) can be used along with dimensionless spatial coordinates (x/L) to develop dimensionless maps for Na+ and Ag+ as shown in Figure SI.8a,b. The maps provide the concentration profile (right panel) and the resulting dimensionless compressive stress in the left panel (scaled to the intrinsic fracture strength of LLZO) with various surface concentrations and dimensionless time (t/τ) at 1200K. The MD predicted diffusivity values may be faster or slower than experiments and ignore the microstructure effects; thus, the dimensionless analysis was used to extend to experimental values in future works.

We believe desirable ion exchange should be controlled by two factors, shown in red in the dimensionless maps. The lower bound of ion exchange is determined by the exchange depth. It is noted that the compressive stress profile goes to zero (no improvement) before the concentration profile goes to zero due to finite stress relaxation by the tensile stress in the bulk. Thus, the exchange depth is quantified based on the compressive stress profile and not the concentration profile of the ion exchange. Any exchange depth that does not entirely encapsulate the tip of the crack does not provide any benefit to crack resistance since growth of a sharp crack (at the continuum level) depends on the magnitude of the external stress at the tip of the crack (schematic illustrating this is shown in Figure a). In the case of LLZO, the crack sizes, α, are dictated by preexisting flaws (surface damage, pores etc.), which tend to be within the limit of 2–20 μm , for the presently thicker electrolyte pellets (∼1 mm). However, future designs would have to be thinner, which may have smaller flaws. Hence, for generalization, we discuss the flaw length in dimensionless units (α/L) by scaling it to the thickness of the pellet. Thus, in the dimensionless maps, the lower bound of exchange profiles is determined by a limiting αL=0.05 , shown as a red triangular notch in the left panels of Figure SI.8a. Effective ion exchange concentration profiles should exist beyond this red notch.

The upper bound of ion exchange is determined by the resulting tensile stress in the bulk, as exceeding the critical fracture strength σf ∼ 150 MPa causes the fracture toughness (K 1) to reach the critical value of K 1C (1 MPa·m1/2 for LLZO , ), leading to spontaneous shattering. This line of spontaneous bulk fracture is shown as the horizontal red line in the left panel of the dimensionless stress map (Figures b,c, SI. 8a,b) with a value of +1. The resulting maximum tensile stress in the bulk should be lower than σf.

Within the two bounds of ion exchange, the dimensionless maps show the different profiles for stress and concentration depending on the C s. The C s can be estimated using two strategies: the first method relies on thermodynamic arguments. The thermodynamic argument is as follows. At the ion exchange temperature ∼1200 K, we find the precursor such that E f ∼ 0.3 eV, which corresponds to a C s∼5% within the dilute defect approximation. The second method relies on back-calculating the C s from the desired stress on the surface (σ c 0) by a simple approximation Cs∼−σC0(1−ν)Eγ . Experimentally, the thermodynamic method is more likely to be applicable as the equilibrium defect energy cost is dictated by the precursor. For C s ∼ 5%, Na2O may be used as a precursor for Na+ exchange, while AgNO3 may be used as a precursor for Ag+ exchange at 1200K. The modeling in this work is performed strictly on the surface cracks and flaws of the solid electrolyte, without incorporating the behavior of lithium filament growth. The residual compressive stress will increase the nucleation overpotential for lithium due to the additional work required to expand against the internal pressure in the filament. , Thus, the ion-exchange-induced compressive stress becomes more beneficial to prevent lithium filament growth.

Ion exchange, on the one hand, can induce residual compressive stress in the exchange region; however, on the other hand, direct insertion of ion exchange species in the Li+ conduction pathways can have adverse effects on Li+ mobility and eventually reduce diffusivity. T and σ c are the fitting temperature and stress and R refers to the gas constant. The two-step fitting procedure for Li+, to obtain activation volume and then activation energy, was conducted akin to our previously reported approach in refs , . The ion exchange concentration (substitution-type) remains low compared to the total Li-ion concentration; thus, D Li is not explicitly sensitive to Li+ concentrations nor ion exchange concentration as the effect is implicitly captured in the D 0, V*, and E a.

Figure a shows the activation volume of Li+ in ion-exchanged LLZO. The positive activation volume means that compressive stress will slow Li+ diffusion. The general observation is that the activation volume of Li+ increases with the size of the ion exchange species. This is because larger exchanged ions exert a larger local compressive stress on the nearby mobile Li+, which needs to overcome a larger local active volume for the same hop observed in native LLZO. We also draw attention to the magnitude of the activation volume of Li+ being about 4 times less compared to the ion exchange species (shown in Table ); this is intuitive as Li+ would require less volume compared to the larger ions. However, the ratio is not proportional to the size of the ion exchange species since the bottlenecks of diffusion pathways in LLZO (the triangular face-shared between the octahedral and tetrahedral Li+ sites) are optimized for Li+ and not the larger ion exchanging species.

Figure b shows that the activation energy of Li+ diffusion increases almost linearly with the size of the ion exchange species. We first note that ion exchange with Ag+ and K+ increases the activation energy of Li+. As indicated by the DFT calculations in Section , the larger exchange ions prefer the octahedral site and disallow the Li+ to access the closest tetrahedral sites around them. This causes an enlarged blockage in the diffusion pathway of Li+. In the case of Ag+, concentration seems to have no effect on the Li+ diffusivity as both activation energies are similar, ∼0.28 eV (on top of each other). On the other hand, exchange with Na+ does not affect the diffusivity of Li+ since the difference in the activation energy from the native LLZO is ∼0.01 eV. This may be because of the smaller mismatch between the size of Li+ and Na+.

Figure c shows the natural logarithm of the diffusion prefactor increasing linearly with the exchanged ion size. This can be understood by the simple Meyer–Neldel rule, as the activation energy scales linearly with the size of the ion exchange species.

Figure d shows the extrapolated room temperature and zero stress diffusivity of Li+ in LLZO with ion exchange. The Li+ diffusivity decreases linearly with the ion exchange species size. For the case of Na+, there is a negligible drop in diffusivity, for Ag+ the diffusivity drops by 40% and with K+ the drop is ∼90%. However, we note that the drop is less than 1 order of magnitude with respect to the bulk for Na+ and Ag+. Even under the maximum compressive stress shown in Figure c of ∼1 GPa, the diffusivity drops by 24%, thus still staying within the order of magnitude. Thus, ion exchange is suitable, as it does not penalize the diffusivity of Li+ significantly.

In the context of the mixed alkali effect, we do see an ion size-dependent decrease in the diffusivity of the Li+ with the larger ion exchanging species causing a larger decrease. We attribute this decrease primarily to the increase in the activation energy due to the creation of blockage in the diffusional network of Li+ caused by the larger ion exchanging species.

Effect on Electronic Properties from Ion Exchange

Solid-state batteries require the electrolyte to be an insulator, which is true for the case of native LLZO (electronic bandgap for bulk LLZO ∼4 eV). Ideally, ion exchange must not alter the electronic band structure of these wide bandgap materials. This was confirmed by analyzing the electronic density of states occupation with ion exchange for the three species at 1.7% concentration from DFT calculations shown in Figure SI.9. The total electronic density of states is shown as a dashed navy curve representing the K+ exchange, dashed orange for Ag+, dashed green for Na+, and solid black for bulk LLZO. No changes were observed for the K+ and Na+ exchange with respect to the LLZO. This is unsurprising as alkali-metal ions do not have any unpaired electrons, and the bonds formed by the alkali–oxygen are of the alkali s– and oxygen p orbitals, which are similar for Li+, Na+. and K+. On the other hand, the case of Ag+ exchange is interesting as it introduces isolated defect states close to the valence band maximum (VBM) as discussed in ref . Thus, the size of the ion exchanging species has no effect on the electronic band structure, but the effects arise due to the nature of the transition-metal electrons versus the alkali-metal electrons. Since Ag+ (or Na+) has a higher reduction potential than Li+, we do not rule out the possibility that surface Ag+ (Na+) will be reduced first. However, the subsurface Ag+(Na+), which provides the compressive residual stress, will not diffuse out at room temperature. Thus, they are not expected to be reduced.

Conclusions

The electro-chemo-mechanical failure of an all-solid-state battery at the lithium metal anode–solid-state electrolyte interface may be ameliorated by inducing residual compressive stress. Ion-exchange-induced surface residual compressive stresses in LLZO were modeled by a fully integrated multiscale modeling approach. The size effects of isovalent ions Na+, Ag+, and K+ exchanging at Li+ sites in LLZO were investigated. Using DFT calculations, it was confirmed that larger ions show a preference to inhabit larger octahedral sites. Based on defect formation energies, promising precursors for driving Na+ ion exchange can be Na3PO4, NaF, or possibly Na2O, while Ag3PO4 and AgNO3 are identified as Ag+ sources. DFT calculations were also used to calculate the free expansion coefficient due to ion exchange and were found to increase linearly with the size of the ion exchange species.

Molecular dynamics with the Bush polarizable forcefield was used to sample the ion exchange due to dilute concentrations. High-temperature MD simulations reveal that K+ ions cluster at 3.4% concentration; thus, K+ may be unsuitable for ion exchange. Na+ and Ag+ diffusion behavior at 1200 K was simulated and fit to a stress–concentration relationship, serving as input for continuum models. In contrast, Li+ diffusion is less dependent on the ion exchange concentration. The activation volume and activation energy increase linearly with the size of the ion exchange species.

A microscale two-sided ion exchange process in LLZO was simulated by continuum models with DFT and MD inputs. First, the ion exchange concentration profiles were simulated by solving Fick’s law with the coupled stress and concentration-dependent diffusion coefficient. Then, the stress profile and fracture toughness as a function of surface flaw size were predicted. The value of this approach was highlighted by comparing the increase in fracture strength against a previously assumed shallow doping and uniform concentration model. For the concentration gradient model, dimensionless maps exploring the concentration and stress profiles with diffusion time were provided for the process of ion exchange. The lower bound and upper bounds of improvement in the fracture strength were discussed. Assuming ion exchange precursor bath dictated surface concentration to be 5%, the corresponding stress using Na+ is −674 MPa and that while using Ag+ is −1081 MPa. The residual compressive stress field ensuring depth of exchange of 5% of the thickness of the electrolyte was superimposed on a linear elastic Griffith-type crack model indicating a gain of a factor of 3.26 using Ag+ and 2.13 in the fracture strength with respect to bulk crack of dimensionless length of 1%.

The stress–concentration-coupled diffusivity of Li+ was extrapolated to room temperature at 0 and 1 GPa compressive stress. The drop in the Li+ diffusivity is less than 1 order of magnitude with respect to the bulk of LLZO. In addition, investigation of the electronic density of states yields no change in the case of Na+ and K+ exchange with respect to bulk. To the contrary, ion exchange with Ag+ introduces isolated defects near the VBM that are localized to the Ag–O octahedral cage. Thus, ion exchange using Na+ and Ag+ is recommended as an effective strategy to induce residual compressive stress to ameliorate the electro-chemo-mechanically coupled cracking of the LLZO–lithium metal anode interface.
