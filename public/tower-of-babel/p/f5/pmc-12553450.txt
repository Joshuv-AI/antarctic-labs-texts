Development and Internal Validation of a Clinical Imaging–Based Stroke Prediction Model in a Community Cohort
Authors: Yan‐Yan Wang, Ding‐Ding Zhang, Fei‐Fei Zhai, Zi‐Yue Liu, Ning Su, Li‐Xin Zhou, Jun Ni, Ming Yao, Li‐Ying Cui, Bin Peng, Ming‐Li Li, Zheng‐Yu Jin, Shu‐Yang Zhang, Fei Han, Yi‐Cheng Zhu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExisting stroke prediction models tend to overestimate contemporary stroke risk and inadequately incorporate neuroimaging parameters. This study aimed to develop a novel and accurate stroke prediction model for a community‐based population by integrating comprehensive neuroimaging data.MethodsA prospective cohort study was conducted involving 1586 eligible participants from northern rural China. Baseline clinical and neuroimaging data were collected, with annual follow‐ups to assess incident stroke. Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was used to identify key predictors, which were subsequently incorporated into a Cox proportional hazards model to develop the final predictive model. Internal validation was performed via 500 times bootstrap resampling. Model performance was evaluated using time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic curves, Brier scores, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis.ResultsDuring a mean follow‐up of 8.0 years, 54 incident strokes occurred among 1173 participants (4.6%). The final model incorporating least absolute shrinkage and selection operator–selected predictors (current smoking, diabetes, high cerebral small‐vessel disease burden, and severe intracranial artery stenosis) showed strong discrimination, with area under the curve values of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.92) for 5‐year and 0.84 (95% CI, 0.79–0.90) for 7‐year prediction. Bootstrap validation confirmed model robustness (area under the curve values, 0.87 and 0.83, respectively). The model was well calibrated (Brier scores, 0.03 at 5 years and 0.04 at 7 years), and decision curve analysis indicated favorable net clinical benefit. A clinically applicable nomogram was developed for individualized risk assessment.ConclusionsThe newly developed predictive model, combining clinical and neuroimaging features, provides accurate prediction of 5‐year and 7‐year stroke risk. Targeted management of diabetes, smoking, silent cerebral small‐vessel disease, and intracranial artery stenosis is essential for primary stroke prevention in community populations.

Stroke is the second leading cause of death globally and the leading cause of death in China, posing a major public health threat. 1 , 2 Early prevention is crucial, emphasizing the importance of stroke risk prediction models to identify high‐risk individuals and guide appropriate interventions to manage risk factors. 3

Since the early 1990s, cardiovascular risk prediction models have been developed, beginning with the original Framingham Stroke Risk Profile. 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 However, these models have shown limitations in clinical practice. For instance, the Framingham Stroke Risk Profile, a model based on data from the 1960s to 1980s, 4 overestimated the 6‐year absolute risk of stroke by 3.7‐fold in men and 4.4‐fold in women in a French cohort from the early 2000s. 9 Similarly, the Prediction for Atherosclerotic Cardiovascular Disease Risk in China (China‐PAR), which was developed using data from 1994 to the early 2000s, 8 overestimated the 10‐year risk by 2.6‐fold in a low‐risk group when applied to a more contemporary cohort from the 2010s to 2020s. 10 A potential explanation for these overestimations is the poor control of cardiovascular risk factors in earlier cohorts. 11 However, awareness and control rates of these risk factors have improved significantly in recent years. 12 This highlights the need for updated risk equations that incorporate indicators of target organ injury to better reflect the impact of changes in risk factor management over time.

The assessment of brain parenchyma and arteries through brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and magnetic resonance angiography (MRA) may enhance the accuracy of identifying individuals at high risk of stroke. For instance, in the population‐based Rotterdam study, with a mean follow‐up of 4.9 years, the presence of microbleeds on baseline brain MRI was associated with a 0.9‐fold increased risk of all strokes. 13 Moreover, the growing use of brain MRI during routine physical examinations or in patients presenting with nonspecific symptoms has facilitated the collection of neuroimaging biomarkers for primary stroke prevention. This underscores the need for more precise stroke risk assessment in these individuals.

In this prospective cohort with complete brain MRI and MRA data, we aimed to develop and internally validate a personalized stroke prediction model leveraging detailed assessments of brain parenchyma and intracranial arteries.

Methods

This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the Transparent Reporting of a Multivariable Prediction Model for Individual Prognosis or Diagnosis guidelines for reporting clinical prediction models. 14 The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Participants

The Shunyi study design has been previously published. 15 , 16 Briefly, it is a prospective, population‐based cohort study that investigated the risk factors and consequences of cardiovascular and age‐related diseases. Between June 2013 and May 2016, 1586 individuals aged ≥35 years were recruited from 5 villages in Shunyi, a rural district of Beijing, China. Of these, 329 lacked baseline brain MRI data. In the present analysis, 84 individuals with a history of stroke (63 ischemic strokes, 12 hemorrhagic strokes, and 9 both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes) were excluded. Thus, the final analysis was based on 1173 stroke‐free individuals (Figure 1). The Shunyi study was approved by the Ethical Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (reference number: B‐160), with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

Baseline Data Collection

Based on the influential factors of stroke reported in the previous literature, this study determined alternative predictors based on the data characteristics. 5 , 8 , 15 At baseline, trained researchers conducted face‐to‐face interviews using standard structured questionnaires to collect demographic information, physical measurements, and medical history. Smoking status was classified as “current” (at least within the previous month) or “noncurrent.” Body mass index was calculated using weight and height as follows: body mass index=weight (kg)/height2 (m2). Definitions of hypertension, diabetes, and hyperlipidemia were consistent with those previously published in literature. 17 Hypertension was defined as a systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg, a diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg, current use of antihypertensive medications, or a self‐reported history of hypertension. Diabetes was diagnosed on the basis of a self‐reported history of diabetes, current use of oral hypoglycemic agents or insulin therapy, or fasting plasma glucose levels ≥7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL). Hyperlipidemia was defined as either a self‐reported history of hyperlipidemia, ongoing treatment with lipid‐lowering medications, total cholesterol levels exceeding 5.2 mmol/L (200 mg/dL), or low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol levels >3.36 mmol/L (130 mg/dL). Atrial fibrillation was defined as self‐reported atrial fibrillation, with ECG findings showing the disappearance of the p‐wave, replacement by a small f‐wave, and a completely irregular ventricular rate. Venous blood samples, routinely collected after overnight fasting, were analyzed for total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, and triglyceride.

Brain MRI and MRA were performed using a single 3‐T Siemens Skyra scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany). The magnetic resonance parameters and definitions of the imaging markers for cerebral small‐vessel disease (CSVD), including lacunes, microbleeds, dilated perivascular space, and white matter hyperintensity, were reported in published criteria. 18 , 19 The total CSVD score was calculated on an ordinal scale ranging from 0 to 4, 20 with 1 point assigned to the following markers: lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, perivascular space in the basal ganglia (>10), periventricular hyperintensity Fazekas 3, or deep white matter hyperintensity Fazekas 2 to 3. In this study, the CSVD burden was dichotomized into low burden (total CSVD score, 0–1 point) and high burden (total CSVD score, 2–4 points). Intracranial artery stenosis (ICAS) was assessed using a standardized method, 21 with ICAS defined as any degree of stenosis in at least 1 of the following arteries: the intracranial segment of the vertebral artery, internal carotid, middle cerebral, anterior cerebral, basilar, or posterior cerebral arteries. 15 In this study, ICAS was treated as a dichotomous variable with a 50% stenosis cutoff value. Stenosis <50% was classified as nonsevere ICAS, while stenosis ≥50% was defined as severe ICAS. Neuroimaging data were collected by trained physicians blinded to the clinical data. The intrarater agreement has been described in previous studies. 22 , 23

Outcome Definition and Follow‐Up

Event ascertainment procedures and adjudication processes for the Shunyi cohort have also been previously published. 15 Participants without stroke at baseline were followed up annually for stroke occurrence through on‐site interviews using a structured questionnaire. Additionally, we continuously monitored the medical records of all the participants at a community‐based health service station. When a stroke event was suspected, relevant details, such as brain computed tomography and MRI, were obtained and recorded from the hospital where the participant had been treated. An experienced neurologist confirmed the diagnosis of stroke, which was defined as the acute onset of focal (or global) neurological deficits lasting >24 hours without an apparent nonvascular origin. Based on the brain computed tomography or MRI findings, stroke was classified as either ischemic or hemorrhagic.

Brain MRI was performed at baseline for all 1173 participants. Follow‐up commenced after MRI completion and continued until the earliest of the following events: (1) stroke (fatal or nonfatal), (2) death from any cause, or (3) the prespecified study end point (October 1, 2023). Crucially, complete follow‐up (0% attrition) was achieved for all enrolled participants.

Sample Size Assessment

Based on events of the variable rule, we assessed whether the available sample size was sufficient for the prediction model. Earlier simulation studies suggested a Cox proportional hazards model (Cox model) with a minimum of 10 events of the variable. 24 , 25

Statistical Analysis

Baseline data were presented as mean±SD for continuous variables and number (percentile) for categorical variables. Continuous variables that did not conform to a normal distribution first underwent a natural logarithm transformation. Continuous variables were compared using the t test, and categorical variables were compared using the χ2 test. Differences were considered statistically significant at a bilateral P value <0.05. The data analysis of this part was performed using the SPSS statistical software version 25 (IBM, Chicago, IL).

We implemented a 2‐stage modeling approach for stroke risk prediction. First, the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was performed for variable selection through 10‐fold cross‐validation using the λ1se criterion. This approach effectively mitigated multicollinearity while retaining covariates with independent predictive significance. The selected predictors were subsequently incorporated into a Cox model. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed globally and for each covariate using Schoenfeld residuals (with significance threshold at P<0.05 for violation). Linearity of covariates was examined via Martingale residual plots. Models satisfied all assumptions unless otherwise noted. Missing data (frequency detailed in Table S1) were handled via multiple imputation using the R mice package (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Internal validation was conducted using bootstrap resampling with 500 iterations to estimate model optimism and evaluate stability of the predictors' effects. The validated core predictors were then visualized via a clinically applicable nomogram. The performance of the model was systematically evaluated using 3 complementary metrics: discrimination (assessed via Harrell's C‐index, a measure analogous to the area under the curve [AUC] with 95% CI), calibration (visually examined through calibration curves and numerically quantified via the Brier score), and clinical utility (decision curve analysis against treat‐all and treat‐none reference strategies). In addition, comparative analysis against established models (revised Framingham Stroke Risk Profile [R‐FSRP] and China‐PAR) was conducted using standardized metrics. The implementation process for comparing the 3 models is detailed in Table S2. All analyses were performed in R Studio version 4.4.0 with regression assumptions systematically verified.

To evaluate potential bias from nonstroke death acting as competing risks, we conducted sensitivity analyses using a Fine–Gray proportional subdistribution hazards model, which treated death from other causes as a competing event while estimating cumulative stroke incidence. Covariates identical to those in the primary Cox model were included to ensure direct comparability of estimates.

Results

Baseline Characteristics

This study included 435 male participants (37.1%), with a mean age of 55.5±9.3 years. A total of 78.6% of the study population had a CSVD score of 0 to 1, while 21.4% scored 2 to 4. During a mean follow‐up duration of 8.0±1.5 years, 54 participants (4.6%) experienced an incident stroke, of which 47 (4.0%) were ischemic and 7 (0.6%) were hemorrhagic. Additionally, 59 nonstroke deaths (5.03% of the cohort) occurred during follow‐up. The leading causes were malignant tumors (n=22), cardiac diseases (n=14), injury and poisoning (n=7), and deaths from unknown causes due to inadequate medical documentation (n=16). Compared with the nonstroke group, participants in the stroke group were older (P<0.001) and had higher rates of current smoking (P<0.001), hypertension (P=0.025), diabetes (P<0.001), CSVD burden (P<0.001), and ICAS (P<0.001). Prediction Model Built on the Basis of Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator–Cox Regression

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression was used to screen baseline variables, including age, sex, body mass index, current smoking, hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, natural logarithm triglyceride, CSVD burden, and ICAS. The variation characteristics of the coefficient of these variables are shown in Figure S1A. The 10‐fold cross‐validation method was applied to the iterative analysis, and a model with excellent performance but minimum number of variables was obtained when λ was 0.023 (logλ=−3.77) (Figure S1B). As presented in Table 2, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator regression analysis identified 4 independent predictors of stroke (all P<0.001): current smoking (hazard ratio [HR], 2.60 [95% CI, 1.52–4.44]), diabetes (HR, 2.87 [95% CI, 1.66–4.95]), CSVD burden (HR, 5.19 [95% CI, 2.97–9.06]), and ICAS (HR, 4.63 [95% CI, 2.47–8.68]). These variables were subsequently incorporated into a multivariable Cox model to establish the final stroke risk prediction model. The final Cox model demonstrated excellent discrimination, with a C‐index of 0.83 (SE=0.026). The likelihood ratio test confirmed significant improvement over the null model (χ2=90.05, P<2e‐16). As shown in Figure S2, all covariates met the proportional hazards assumption (Schoenfeld test P>0.05). No significant nonlinearity was detected in covariates (the results of Martingale residuals are shown in Figure S3).

Model Performance Evaluation and Validation

Given the minimum follow‐up period of 7.4 years for stroke‐free individuals in this cohort, we selected 5‐ and 7‐year intervals as clinically relevant time points for stroke risk prediction. The model demonstrated excellent discrimination, with AUC values of 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.92) for 5‐year prediction and 0.84 (95% CI, 0.79–0.90) for 7‐year prediction. Internal validation via 500 bootstrap resamples maintained robust performance, yielding AUCs of 0.87 (95% CI, 0.80–0.93) and 0.83 (95% CI, 0.75–0.91) for 5‐ and 7‐year predictions, respectively (Figure 2A).

Calibration analysis revealed strong agreement between predicted and observed stroke risk. Bootstrap‐corrected calibration curves closely followed the 45‐degree reference line, with slightly better fit for the 5‐year prediction (Figure 2B) compared with the 7‐year prediction (Figure 2C). The Brier scores further confirmed model accuracy, with values of 0.03 (95% CI, 0.02–0.04) for 5‐year and 0.04 (95% CI, 0.03–0.05) for 7‐year predictions, both before and after bootstrap validation (Figure S4).

Decision curve analysis (Figure 3) demonstrated the model's clinical utility, showing net benefit over “treat‐all” or “treat‐none” strategies across threshold probabilities of 0% to 40% for 5‐year risk and 0% to 30% for 7‐year risk. These results support the model's potential to guide personalized clinical decision making based on individualized stroke risk estimation. Additionally, Kaplan–Meier analysis was performed in the overall cohort to visually assess stroke‐free survival probability (Figure S5).

Model Visualization

A nomogram was created to visualize the model (Figure 4). In the nomogram, longer lines represent predictors with greater weight. The ranking of feature importance, consistent with Table 2, was as follows: CSVD burden>ICAS>diabetes>current smoking. Each predictor was assigned a “points” value ranging from 0 to 100, displayed at the top of the nomogram. The sum of these points yielded a “total points” score, which was directly correlated with the predicted risk of stroke. For example, a participant who is a noncurrent smoker (points=0) but has diabetes (points=63), a high CSVD burden (points=100), and severe ICAS (points=92.5) would have a “Total Point” score of 255.5. The score corresponds to a 5‐year stroke risk of 42% and a 7‐year stroke risk of 51%.

Comparison With Previous Models

As shown in Table 3, the observed 5‐year stroke incidence in the overall population was 3.4% (40/1173). In comparison, the mean 5‐year stroke probabilities predicted by the new model, R‐FSRP and China‐PAR, were 3.5%, 11.1%, and 7.6%, respectively. The 5‐year AUC values were 0.88 (95% CI, 0.83–0.92) for the new model, 0.72 (95% CI, 0.64–0.80) for R‐FSRP, and 0.77 (95% CI, 0.69–0.86) for China‐PAR. The 5‐year Brier score were 0.03 (95% CI, 0.02–0.04) for the new model, 0.05 (95% CI, 0.04–0.06) for R‐FSRP, and 0.03 (95% CI, 0.02–0.04) for China‐PAR. Figures S6 and S7 compare the performance of 3 stroke prediction models. Time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic analysis (Figure S6) revealed that the Shunyi model achieves superior discriminative ability (AUC=0.88) compared with both R‐FSRP (AUC=0.72, P<0.001) and China‐PAR (AUC=0.77, P=0.010) in the overall population. This advantage remained consistent across subgroups: in men, the AUC was 0.86 versus 0.63 for R‐FSRP (P<0.001); in women, the Shunyi model outperformed both R‐FSRP (0.85 versus 0.67, P=0.017) and China‐PAR (0.85 versus 0.64, P=0.020). The calibration curves (Figure S7) further confirm the Shunyi model's better alignment between predicted and observed risks across all examined populations. Additionally, decision curve analysis (Figure S8) demonstrated greater net clinical benefit for the Shunyi model across most threshold probabilities, establishing its superiority over existing models in both statistical performance and clinical utility.

Sensitivity Analysis for Competing Risks

Fine–Gray competing risk regression yielded subdistribution HRs for stroke risk factors that demonstrated substantial concordance with HRs from primary Cox regression analysis (Table S3). Both models produced consistent results: All risk factors exhibited statistically significant associations (P<0.001), with identical risk hierarchies (CSVD burden>ICAS>diabetes>current smoking). Critically, no significant differences emerged between models for any HR estimates (P‐difference>0.7). These findings indicate that competing events introduced minimal bias in the primary Cox model analysis.

Discussion

In this community‐based cohort involving 1173 stroke‐free participants with a mean follow‐up of 8.0 years, 54 participants experienced an incident stroke, of which 47 were ischemic and 7 were hemorrhagic. We identified current smoking, diabetes, high CSVD burden, and severe ICAS on MRA as the most important stroke risk predictors. The Cox model constructed using these factors demonstrated high accuracy in personalized stroke risk prediction, surpassing the predictive performance of both the R‐FSRP and China‐PAR in this population. These findings emphasize the importance of early clinical intervention for individuals at high risk of stroke.

A cross‐sectional study based on a nationally representative survey published in 2020 reported that the incidence rate of stroke was 589.4 per 100 000 person‐years in northern China. 2 In terms of stroke subtypes, 86.8% were ischemic strokes, 11.9% intracerebral hemorrhage, and 1.3% subarachnoid hemorrhage. 2 After standardizing the incidence in our cohort, based in rural areas of Beijing, China, the stroke incidence rate was 575.0 per 100 000 person‐years, which is comparable to the rate reported for northern China. Our slightly lower rate may be partly explained by the inclusion of participants aged ≥35 years, whereas the national survey included individuals aged ≥40 years. Regarding stroke subtypes, our proportion of ischemic strokes (87.0%) and hemorrhagic strokes (13.0%) was consistent with the national data.

When imaging parameters were incorporated into the variable screening process, age, hyperlipidemia, and hypertension were excluded from the final model. This may be attributed to the traditional risk factors' effects on stroke being partially accounted for or substituted by the imaging parameters. The relatively younger age of the included participants (≥35 years) may have contributed to the lower number of stroke events and influenced the statistical selection of age in the predictive model. Furthermore, improvements in awareness and treatment rates have led to better control of hypertension and hyperlipidemia, 26 potentially reducing their relative impact on future stroke events. In this rural population, basic blood pressure monitoring is widely accessible and convenient, while professional blood glucose adjustment management remains challenging. This disparity has resulted in a significantly lower diabetes control rate compared with hypertension. 27 Poorly controlled diabetes exerts a substantial impact on future stroke risk, which could explain why diabetes was retained in the final model. Additionally, the higher proportion of smokers in this population may account for the independent predictive value of current smoking for future stroke risk. Previous studies have shown that China is the world's largest producer and consumer of tobacco, with smoking prevalence notably higher in rural areas compared with urban areas. 28 , 29 These findings suggest the critical importance of closely managing diabetes and smoking in rural Chinese populations to reduce stroke risk effectively.

Intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis, 30 individual CSVD marker such as lacunes, 31 white matter hyperintensities, 32 , 33 cerebral microbleeds, 13 , 34 and enlarged perivascular space, 35 as well as the total CSVD score 36 , 37 have all been shown to increase the risk of stroke. Notably, prior data from the Shunyi cohort identified that the CSVD burden (7.72%) accounted for a larger proportion of the variation in stroke risk compared with the severity of large‐artery stenosis (3.49%) in multivariate models. 15 In line with these findings, we observed that individuals with a high CSVD burden had more than a 5‐fold increased risk of stroke, while those with severe ICAS on MRA faced a more than 4‐fold higher risk, suggesting that CSVD burden had a higher predictive value for incident stroke. However, CSVD markers are often overlooked in clinical practice. Due to the lack of typical clinical symptoms, they are generally regarded as incidental findings of unclear or minor significance, particularly by nonneurologists. 38 Additionally, current guidelines do not recommend routine brain and neck vascular screening for the asymptomatic population. 39 This study highlights the critical need for greater attention and management of asymptomatic individuals with severe ICAS and high CSVD burden to prevent stroke, particularly for individuals who undergo MRI and MRA during routine physical examination or other medical purposes.

Although previous studies have demonstrated the incremental value of brain MRI parameters in predicting future stroke, no prediction model has yet incorporated these parameters for the primary prevention of stroke. In this prospective cohort study of a community‐based, stroke‐free population, we developed a novel model for predicting 5‐ and 7‐year new strokes, using current smoking status, diabetes, CSVD burden, and ICAS as key predictors. To enhance usability, we transformed the complex Cox model into a straightforward visual nomogram, allowing clinicians to easily interpret the results. This nomogram provides accurate digital stroke risk estimates for individual patients, aiding in clinical decision making regarding intervention intensity and individualized treatment strategies. When compared with models based solely on clinical data, such as R‐FSRP and China‐PAR, our new model demonstrated superior performance in predicting 5‐year stroke risk in the overall population as well as in sex‐specific subgroups. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating brain MRI and MRA parameters into stroke risk prediction. From a cost‐effectiveness perspective, it is impractical to implement brain imaging across the entire population. However, in specific clinical settings such as physical examination centers and stroke high‐risk outpatient clinics, for individuals identified with a high vascular risk profile, brain MRI and MRA may facilitate the early detection of occult cerebral lesions and provide a more precise risk assessment.

Although imaging parameters play a vital role in stroke prediction, they often need to be combined with other clinical information to achieve optimal prediction accuracy. Additionally, the applicability and performance of different models may vary, highlighting the importance of selecting the most appropriate prediction tool based on the specific context and needs of practical applications.

The near‐perfect concordance between Cox and Fine–Gray estimates reflected 2 key aspects. First, the low proportion of competing events (5.03%) inherently minimized bias. Second, it confirmed that the identified stroke risk factors predominantly influence the biological hazard of stroke rather than acting indirectly through competing mortality pathways. Aligning our methods with established tools such as China‐PAR enhances the model's clinical applicability. Moreover, the added validation through competing risk analysis increases confidence in applying these predictors to populations with comparable mortality profiles.

Strengths and Limitations

The primary advantage of this study is its prospective cohort design, which enhances the validity of the pathogenetic inferences. Additionally, we developed a novel stroke prediction model by integrating clinical features and imaging parameters. The model demonstrated strong predictive value, particularly in a population with relatively low educational levels in rural northern China.

However, this study has several limitations. First, the relatively low number of stroke events in our cohort restricted our ability to conduct an in‐depth analysis of stroke types (ischemic versus hemorrhagic) and ischemic stroke subtypes. Second, the applicability of the new stroke prediction model is largely limited to individuals with access to complete brain MRI and MRA information, which may restrict its use in settings without neuroimaging data. Third, the study population, derived from a rural community in northern China, may differ in geography, lifestyle, and education levels, which could limit the generalizability of the findings to nonrural populations. Finally, while we addressed competing risks through sensitivity analyses, the low proportion of nonstroke deaths (5.03%) suggests minimal impact on risk estimates.

Conclusions

In community‐based populations, intensive management of diabetes, smoking, high CSVD burden, and severe ICAS is essential for stroke prevention. This study developed a novel stroke prediction model that integrates clinical features with imaging parameters to estimate 5‐ and 7‐year stroke risks, thereby improving clinical decision making. The model demonstrated strong performance in both discrimination and calibration. However, further external validation through prospective, multicenter, large‐scale studies is necessary to confirm its robustness and generalizability.
