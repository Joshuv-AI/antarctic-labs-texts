PTBP1 knockdown reprograms glioma stem cells into neuronal-like cells and suppresses tumorigenesis via the DUSP5-ERK1/2 signaling pathway
Authors: Cunyu Li, Manyu Chen, Songyi Guo, Yuhao Ma, Suli Zhang, Guanwei Li, Jianqi Wu, Tianqi Liu, Yueyi Jin, Xing Liu, Hu Zhao, Hongmei Liu, Sijin Wu, Hangjin Jiang, Wen Cheng, Anhua Wu, Shuang Hao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundGlioblastoma (GBM), the most prevalent and aggressive primary brain tumor in adults, has a median survival of merely 14 months. Current therapeutic approaches, including maximal safe resection, radiotherapy, and temozolomide-based chemotherapy, have limited efficacy owing to resistance and the high rate of recurrence.MethodsWe analyzed H&E-stained specimens from 65 patients with glioma using deep learning-based morphological classification and analyzed a mouse model through tissue clearing and 3D imaging. Integrated transcriptomic and single-cell RNA-seq analyses identified PTBP1 as a morphology regulator. We validated the function of PTBP1 through lentiviral knockdown in vitro and in orthotopic models and performed structure-based drug screening against PTBP1 with experimental validation.ResultsWe detected a clinically significant association between glioma cell morphology and patient survival times. Mechanistically, PTBP1, an RNA-binding protein abundantly expressed in glioma cells, regulated dual-specificity phosphatase 5 (DUSP5) expression post-transcriptionally and modulate ERK1/2 phosphorylation dynamics, thus reducing glioma stem cell proliferation and enhancing differentiation into neuronal-like cells to suppress tumor growth. Importantly, we developed a nanotherapeutic strategy using A2-PLGA/venetoclax; this strategy repurposes venetoclax, a known clinical drug for leukemia, as a PTBP1-targeting agent that effectively suppresses glioma progression in mouse models.ConclusionOur findings establish a novel PTBP1/DUSP5/ERK1/2 axis governing glioma stem cell proliferation and differentiation and identify the A2-PLGA/venetoclax nanoparticle as a mechanistically justified therapeutic candidate for glioblastoma.

Importance of the Study

High-grade gliomas (HGGs) represent aggressive, treatment-resistant brain tumors with poor prognosis. Through an integrated analysis of 65 patient specimens and 3D tumor-clearing in murine models, we identified 2 distinct glioma cell phenotypes: sphere-like and non-sphere-like subpopulations. Clinically, the frequency of the sphere-like subpopulation was inversely correlated with patient survival, establishing tumor cell morphology as a potential independent prognostic factor in glioma progression. Mechanistically, multi-omics analyses revealed that PTBP1 is preferentially expressed in sphere-like cells. Genetic ablation of PTBP1 induced neuronal differentiation and suppressed tumorigenicity in glioma stem cells and xenograft models. Through structure-based virtual screening, we identified venetoclax, an FDA-approved BCL2 inhibitor, as a PTBP1-targeting therapeutic. To overcome delivery limitations, we developed A2-PLGA/venetoclax nanoparticles. In preclinical studies, this nanoformulation significantly reduced the tumor burden and extended median survival. Our findings elucidate a PTBP1-mediated mechanism governing glioma cell differentiation and demonstrate the potential of the A2-PLGA/venetoclax nanotherapeutic for HGG.

Gliomas are the most common primary intracranial tumors, accounting for approximately 80% of malignant brain tumors.1 According to the WHO classification, gliomas are assigned to grades 1-4 based on a combination of histological features, including the presence and degree of invasion, cellular atypia, mitotic activity, microvascular proliferation, and necrosis.1 Glioblastoma, a WHO grade 4 glioma, is the most aggressive form of brain tumor,2 with a median survival time of approximately 14 months3 and 5-year survival rate of less than 5%.4 Current clinical treatments, including surgery, radiotherapy, and alkylating chemotherapy, fail to achieve expected goals owing to drug resistance and recurrence. Novel therapies are urgently needed to improve patient quality of life and prolong overall survival.

Glioma stem cells (GSCs) display different morphologies, contributing to histological heterogeneity, due to their self-renewal and differentiation ability.5–7 Some tumor cells have protrusions and some are spherical, leading to tumor heterogeneity and resistance to chemotherapy.8 Thus, promoting GSC differentiation into post-mitotic cells and reducing glioma heterogeneity may be an effective strategy to suppress tumorigenesis. This approach has been demonstrated in mouse models. For example, Achaete-scute homolog 1 (ASCL1), a proneural transcription factor, promotes GSC differentiation into neuronal cells and inhibits tumorigenicity.9 The transcription factor NeuroD1 converts glioma cells into neurons and significantly extends the life span and reduces tumor progression.10 However, the overexpression of ASCL1 and NeuroD1 may promote tumor growth.11–13 Therefore, it is necessary to develop alternative efficient and safe treatments for gliomas.

In this work, we characterized glioma cell morphologies in clinical tumor sections, classifying cells as “sphere-like” and “non-sphere-like.” Furthermore, we showed that sphere-like cells have strong proliferative capacity and are negatively associated with patient survival. Interestingly, we found that polypyrimidine tract binding protein (PTBP1) is highly expressed in sphere-like cells. The loss of PTBP1 in glioma cells induces their differentiation to neuronal-like cells and impedes tumor growth in vitro and in vivo. We demonstrated that PTBP1 knockdown significantly upregulates DUSP5 and inhibits ERK1/2 phosphorylation, thereby attenuating GSC proliferation and promoting differentiation. Moreover, utilizing molecular docking with the PTBP1 protein structure, we identified venetoclax as a candidate drug. We validated the potent inhibitory effects of its A2-PLGA/venetoclax nanoformulation on tumorigenesis in patient-derived xenograft mouse models. In summary, we demonstrate that PTBP1 is a promising target for high-grade glioma treatment.

Methods

Glioma Patient Samples Collection

Human glioma tissues were obtained from Shengjing Hospital and the First Affiliated Hospital of China Medical University with informed consent and approved by the Ethics Committee (2023PS1153K and No: 2021-364). These tissues are verified by professional pathologists. Patient information was described in Supplementary Table S1.

Deep Neural Network for Identifying Cells With Different Morphology in H&E Images

H&E images were obtained using bright-field microscopy and viewed by Motic DSAssistant 1.0 software. Cells displayed a length to width ratio of 1:1-1:1.2 are labeled as sphere-like cells and analyzed through deep neural network. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Cell Culture

HEK293 cell was gifted from Dr. Ren Sheng (Northeastern University, China). The cell was cultured in DMEM (Meilun Biotech, MA0212) containing with 10% FBS (Sigma-Aldrich, F8318),1% Penicillin-Streptomycin in cell incubator with 37°C, 5%CO2. Glioma cell line U373 and glioma stem cells (GSCs) were gifted from Dr. Anhua Wu (China Medical University). See Supplementary Methods for details.

Lentivirus Infection and Cell Differentiation

The shPTBP1 sequence was cloned into a lentiviral vector, co-transfected with pHelper plasmids into HEK293T cells. Viral supernatant was collected, concentrated, and used to infect human GSCs and U373 cells. Media was replaced post-infection for differentiation studies. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Mice

BALB/C nude mice were brought from Charles River Lab and housed in specific pathogen free environment with 1 week for experiments. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Orthotopic Xenografting

Nude mice were anaesthetized with isoflurane. After disinfection with medical alcohol, GSCs infected with shCtrl or shPTBP1 lentivirus were injected the M2 region and mice were monitored by MRI. See Supplementary Methods for details.

MRI

During MRI imaging process, mouse was anaesthetized with isoflurane and fixed on heating pad. Meanwhile, respiration was monitored and maintained at a rate of 30-50 times/min during the imaging. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Survival Analysis

Mice were injected with glioma stem cells and monitored each day. The end point of research was defined by appearance of severe neurological deficits of death, including hunched posture, partial limb paralysis and rough skin. Survival was analyzed by Kaplan-Meier with log-rank tests.

Tissue Clearing

GSC63 were stereotactically implanted into M2 of mouse brain. The brains were fixed after 7 days and 21 days of cell inoculation. Subsequently, tissue clearing method was used by PEGASOS Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. See Supplementary Methods for details.

RNA Extraction and RT-PCR Assay

Total RNA was extracted from tumor cells with RNA Isolation Kit according to the manufacturer’s protocol. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Immunofluorescence Staining

Cells and tissues were fixed, permeabilized, blocked, and stained with primary antibodies. Next, second antibodies were incubated for 1hour at room temperature. DAPI was used to cell nucleus staining at room temperature. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Western Blotting

Glioma cells were lysed, centrifuged, separated by SDS-PAGE, and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes. Membranes were blocked and incubated with primary antibodies, then horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-secondary antibodies were used. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Multi-Electrode Array Recordings

Primary neuron generated from hippocampus of newborn C57BL/6 mice was plated multi-electrode array (MEA) plates and cultured in neurobasal medium. Similarly, GSC63 with shRNA or shPTBP1 was cultured in MEA plates for recording. The neuronal activity was carried out by Maestro Pro (Axion Biosystems). See Supplementary Methods for details.

Data Collection and Processing

PTBP1 gene expression and neurogenesis-related genes raw data were from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (http://cancergemome.nih.gov/) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) (http://www.cgga.org.cn)14 performed according to the 2021 WHO CNS5 framework. PTBP1 expression analysis was focused on different types of glioma patients, such as classical, mesenchymal, neural, and proneural. Overall survival analysis data was from patients with low or high PTBP1 expression of TCGA and CGGA. All raw data were analyzed by R package Seurat 4.3.1.

RNA Sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from human glioma cells for bulk RNA sequencing. The libraries were obtained by the Illumina HiSeqTM 4,000 platform. For single-cell sequencing, human glioma patient tissues were cut into small fraction and digested. Individual nuclei were prepared for sequent sequencing and analysis. See Supplementary Methods for details.

RNA Immunoprecipitation Assay

The RNA immunoprecipitation (RIP) assay was performed using a commercial RNA-binding protein immunoprecipitation kit (Sangon Biotech) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Detection of DUSP5 mRNA Stability

To assess DUSP5 mRNA stability, an actinomycin D assay was performed. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Molecular Docking and Molecular Dynamics Drug Screening

Ten human PTBP1 structures were used to build a receptor ensemble, with NMR structures represented by their first configuration and X-ray structures limited to chain A. Domains 3 and 4 were treated separately, resulting in 13 aligned structures for virtual screening. A docking box centered on the RNA mass was used to screen via AutoDock VINA. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Surface Plasmon Resonance Assay

The surface plasmon resonance (SPR) assay was used to analyze the interaction between PTPB1 protein and small molecular drugs and tested by Biacore T200 instrument. See Supplementary Methods for details.

CCK8 Assay

Glioma cells were seeded, treated with drug concentrations, incubated with CCK-8, and analyzed by microplate reader. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Synthesis and Characterization of Nanoparticle

DSPE-PEG2000-PDP and Angiopep-2 were dissolved in DMSO. Conjugated Angiopep-2 was then able to efficiently drive nanoparticle across the BB and subsequently actively target tumor in the brain.3,15 DSPE-PEG2000, lecithin, angiopep-2-DSPE-PEG2000, and PLGA were completely solubilized in DMSO for synthesis. And the diameters of nanoparticle were determined by dynamic light scattering (DLS). The morphology imaged were using by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). See Supplementary Methods for details.

In Vivo Distribution of PLGA/DOX and A2-PLGA/DOX

To evaluate the targeting effect of A2-PLGA/venetoclax, a DOX-labeled nanoparticle was constructed and injected into glioma-bearing nude mice. The tumor was fixed and observed by fluorescence microscopy. See Supplementary Methods for details.

Anti-Tumor Effect of A2-PLGA/Venetoclax on Glioma Mice Model

GFP-labeled glioma stem cells (GSCs) were stereotactically implanted into the M2 region of nude mice brains. After 14 days of orthotopic tumor growth, mice underwent MRI monitoring and were treated with A2-PLGA/venetoclax nanoparticles for therapeutic assessment. See Supplementary Methods for details.

EdU Assay

EdU staining was performed on mice described in manufacture protocol. EdU (Sigma, 900584) was intraperitoneally injected into mice with160 mg/kg. After 4 h, the brains were fix, cut into slices and stained using EdU imaging kit and protocol (ApexBio, k1076).

Statistical Analysis

All data showed in each graph was mean±SEM and analyzed by GraphPad Prism 8.0. Significance was determined by unpaired 2-tailed Student’s t tests or 1-way ANOVA with Tukey’s, Dunnett’s using Sigmaplot 12.5. P ≤ .05 was set to statistically significant.

Results

Sphere-like Cells Are Negatively Correlated With Survival

To study the relationship between tumor cell morphology and survival in patients with glioma, we investigated histological sections stained with nestin, a GSC marker. Glioma cells showed distinct morphologies: sphere-like and non-sphere-like (eg, spindle-like) (Figure 1A). Ki67 expression, a proliferation marker, was higher in sphere-like glioma cells than in non-sphere-like cells, indicating strong proliferative activity (Figure 1A and Supplementary Figure S1A). To confirm these findings, we transplanted GSCs isolated from patients into nude mouse brains, followed by tissue clearing16 (Figure 1B). Glioma cells displayed distinct sphere-like and non-sphere-like morphologies (Figure 1C).

The population of sphere-like glioma cells increased from 70.73% (7 days) to 95.66% (21 days), while the proportion of non-sphere-like glioma cells declined (Figure 1D and E). Based on EdU staining, proliferative activity was stronger in sphere-like glioma cells that in non-sphere-like cells. Specifically, the percentage of sphere-like cells exhibiting high proliferative activity increased from 29.13% at 7 days to 55.90% at 21 days (Figure 1D and E). These findings collectively demonstrate that glioma cells exhibit distinct morphologies and rates of proliferation and are dominated by sphere-like cells.

The number of sphere-like glioma cells was higher in tumor tissues from patients with shorter survival than from patients with longer survival (Figure 1A and Supplementary Figure S1B). We collected 1196 H&E images from 65 patients with high-grade glioma (WHO Grades III-IV) to further explore the relationship between sphere-like cell counts and survival. We trained a deep neural network to identify sphere-like and non-sphere-like cells in H&E images using 35 manually annotated images as a training set (Supplementary Figure S1C; Supplementary Methods). The model showed high accuracy on both training and test sets (Figure 1F and Supplementary Figure S1C). To address immune cell infiltration in glioma tissues, we also employed a deep learning model to distinguish between immune cells and sphere-like glioma cells in H&E-stained sections (Supplementary Figure S1C; Supplementary Methods). Finally, we performed a survival analysis using the average number of sphere-like tumor cells in images. We did not detect a significant difference in the distribution of cell types across major molecular subtypes of glioma, such as mesenchymal, classical, and pro-neural (Supplementary Figure S1D). However, sphere-like tumor cells were negatively correlated with overall survival, regardless of whether patients they harbored IDH-wildtype or IDH-mutant tumors (Figure 1G and H, Supplementary Figure S1E).

PTBP1 Is Negatively Correlated With Survival

To develop novel treatments, it is necessary to identify drug targets that regulate glioma cell morphology. We evaluated correlations between glioma progression and transcription factors known to promote cell differentiation, such as ASCL1,9 based on large cohorts from TCGA and CGGA. Cox hazard regression analysis of TCGA data revealed that PTBP1, an RNA-binding protein, was most significantly associated with tumor progression (Figure 2A). Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that high PTBP1 expression results in shorter survival (Figure 2B). These results were replicated using CGGA data (Supplementary Figure S2A and B), suggesting that PTBP1 is strongly correlated with survival.

To clarify the role of PTBP1, we evaluated tumor tissues collected from 4 patients with glioma through single-cell RNA-seq (Figure 2C and Supplementary Figure S2C). In total, 47 469 cells were isolated from tissues and 6 main cell types were identified, including glioma cells, endothelial cells, immune cells, neurons, astrocytes, and oligodendrocytes (Olig) (Figure 2D and E and Supplementary Figure S2D). PTBP1 was mainly expressed in glioma cells, different from the other candidate transcription factors regulating cell differentiation and morphology (Figure 2F). Its high expression was predominantly localized to tumor tissues (Supplementary Figure S2E). Furthermore, we identified 143 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor cells with high and low PTBP1 expression, including 143 upregulated genes and 41 downregulated genes (Figure 2G). The upregulated genes included SEMA6D,17 regulating neuronal development, and CASC15,18 controlling cell proliferation and tumorigenesis. These DEGs were enriched in various Gene Ontology (GO) terms, including mitotic cell cycle process and cell morphogenesis (Figure 2H), suggesting that increased levels of PTBP1 expression are correlated with poor patient survival by potentially regulating glioma cell morphology and differentiation. This was further evidenced by the high PTBP1 expression in sphere-like glioma cells from patients with shorter survival (Figure 2I and Supplementary Figure S2F).

PTBP1 Knockdown Induces Glioma Cell Differentiation into Neuronal-like Cells and Reduces Proliferation

To investigate the role of PTBP1, we employed lentiviral transduction to reduce PTBP1 expression in patient-derived glioma stem cells (Figure 3A) and verified the significant inhibition (Supplementary Figure S3A). Following PTBP1 knockdown, GSC63 and GSC21 exhibited neuron-like morphologies (Supplementary Figure S3B and C). RNA-seq revealed that PTBP1 deletion in GSC63 increases the expression of transcription factors involved in cell fate determination, such as NeuronD119 and ASCL1.9 Neuronal markers, including DCX and Tuj1, were upregulated, whereas proliferation markers, such as PCNA and Ki67, were downregulated (Figure 3B). Consistent with RNA-seq results, quantitative RT-PCR revealed significant increases in the expression of neuronal genes, such as DCX, and decreases in proliferation-related genes on day 3 after PTBP1 depletion (Figure 3C). On days 7 and 14, GSC63 proliferation was markedly reduced. Moreover, expression levels of neuronal genes, particularly DCX and Tuj1, were substantially elevated 14 days following PTBP1 knockdown (Figure 3D and E).

Immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked decrease in Ki67 expression in GSC63 cells after PTBP1 knockdown (Figure 3F and I). Conversely, DCX and Tuj1 levels increased significantly in GSC63 cells (Figure 3G-I). We obtained similar results after PTBP1 depletion in GSC21 (Supplementary Figure S3D-G) and the glioma cell line U373 (Supplementary Figure S4); decreased PTBP1 increased the expression of neuronal markers and altered cell morphology. To evaluate whether PTBP1-deficient glioma cells expressing neuronal markers demonstrate neuronal firing capabilities, an intrinsic neuronal property,20 we employed MEA recordings (Figure 3J). Unlike the spontaneous firing activity observed in primary neurons, GSC63 cells did not exhibit spike activity, regardless of PTBP1 depletion status (Figure 3K and L). These results indicate that while PTBP1 depletion induces neuronal marker expression, the cells failed to generate electrophysiological activity. Collectively, these findings suggest that PTBP1 loss induces neuronal marker expression and suppresses tumor cell proliferation; however, it does not confer functional neuronal firing characteristics. Consequently, we designated these PTBP1-deficient glioma cells as neuronal-like cells.

PTBP1 knockdown upregulates PTBP2 (Figure 3B and C), a homolog of PTBP1, and drives glioma cells toward a neuronal-like phenotype. Earlier work in HeLa cells suggests that PTBP2 partially compensates for PTBP1 via cross‑regulation and functional redundancy.21 To examine this interplay in glioma, we performed a qPCR assay and found that PTBP2 overexpression led to decreased PTBP1 expression. Although PTBP2 overexpression for 14 days increased Tuj1 expression, it also increased Ki67 levels significantly. PTBP2 depletion reverses the PTBP1 knockdown-induced expression of DCX and Tuj1 and increased Ki67 slightly (Supplementary Figure S3H). Collectively, these results indicate that PTBP1 plays a predominant role in glioma progression, whereas PTBP2 can provide partial functional compensation, in line with previous findings.22

PTBP1 Inhibition Suppresses Tumorigenesis in Patient-Derived Xenograft Models

We verified the function of PTBP1 in a xenograft model using GSC63 cells infected with shPTBP1 lentivirus. The tumor area was monitored via MRI at 42 and 60 days after orthotopic transplantation (Figure 4A). The tumor area in the PTBP1 knockdown group was significantly lower than that in control group after 60 days (Figure 4B and C). To confirm the reprogramming effect of PTBP1 on glioma cells in vivo, we evaluated morphological differences in tumor cells between the control and shPTBP1 groups (Figure 4A). After 60 days, unlike the control group, cells with PTBP1 deletion in vivo exhibited obvious neurite outgrowth (Figure 4D). In addition, the percentage of sphere-like cells with stronger proliferation decreased significantly after PTBP1 was ablated for 60 days (Figure 4E). Furthermore, immunofluorescence staining revealed that PTBP1-deleted glioma cells exhibit decreased expression of EdU compared to the control group (Figure 4F and I). These findings demonstrate that PTBP1 regulates glioma cell morphology.

We evaluated whether PTBP1-deleted glioma cells in mouse models display neuronal markers (DCX and Tuj1), as observed in vitro. We detected more DCX+ and Tuj1+ cells in the PTBP1 inhibition group than in the control group (Figure 4G-I). However, mature NeuN+ cells were not detected in the PTBP1 knockdown group after orthotopic transplantation for 120 days, indicating that the loss of PTBP1 could induce GSC63 trans-differentiation into neuronal-like cells, not neurons (Supplementary Figure S5A). A survival analysis suggested that survival times are longer in mice with low PTBP1 expression levels than in the control group (Figure 4J). These findings indicate that PTBP1 suppresses tumorigenicity through the trans-differentiation of neuronal-like cells in GSC63 orthotopic xenograft models.

To further validate these results, we proceeded to evaluate the tumor area via MRI in a xenograft mouse model using GSC21 at 30,60 and 90 days after orthotopic implantation (Supplementary Figure S6A). In the GSC21 xenograft mouse model, PTBP1 knockdown obviously reduced the tumor area (Supplementary Figure S6B and C). Knocking down PTBP1 in GSC21 resulted in decreased Ki67-positive (Supplementary Figure S6D and G) and increased DCX- and Tuj1-positive cells (Supplementary Figure S6E-G). Additionally, mice bearing PTBP1-knockdown GSC21 showed significantly longer median survival times compared with those in the control group (Supplementary Figure S6H). In summary, PTBP1 knockdown promotes GSC trans-differentiation into neuronal-like cells, thereby impeding tumorigenesis in vivo.

PTBP1 Regulates GSC Proliferation and Differentiation via the DUSP5-ERK1/2 Signaling Pathway

RNA-seq analysis of GSC63 following PTBP1 knockdown revealed 1509 DEGs (776 upregulated and 733 downregulated genes), including MAP2K5, dual-specificity phosphatase 5 (DUSP5), and DUSP7 (Figure 5A). Functional enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs are involved in the MAPK signaling pathway, synaptic vesicle cycle, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction (Supplementary Figure S7A). The MAPK pathway (ERK1/2) influences human cell fate and differentiation.23 Thus, we hypothesized that PTBP1 regulates cell proliferation and differentiation via DUSP5, which targets ERK1/2.24,25

DUSP5 and DUSP7 were significantly upregulated in GSC63 and GSC21 after PTBP1 knockdown (Figure 5B and Supplementary Figure S7B). Under the same conditions, PDGFRA, EGFR, and NF1 expression remained unchanged, indicating their non-involvement (Supplementary Figure S7C). Furthermore, RIP-qPCR revealed that PTBP1 binds directly to DUSP5 and DUSP7 in GSCs, with stronger binding affinity for DUSP5 (Figure 5C and Supplementary Figure S7D). We then evaluated the roles of alternative splicing or post-transcriptional stability in the effects of PTBP1.26,27 RNA-seq and MATS analyses revealed that DUSP5 exon inclusion remained nearly complete upon PTBP1 knockdown (average ΔPSI ≈ 0.03), below the conventional threshold (ΔPSI > 0.1) for a biologically significant splicing change (Supplementary Figure S7E). However, the DUSP5 degradation rate decreased markedly following PTBP1 depletion (Figure 5D and Supplementary Figure S7F). These results suggest that PTBP1 regulates DUSP5 expression predominantly through modulating transcript stability, rather than through splicing control. Western blotting confirmed that DUSP5 protein levels increase (Figure 5E and Supplementary Figure S7G) and ERK1/2 phosphorylation levels decrease following shPTBP1 lentivirus treatment (Figure 5F and Supplementary Figure S7H). Concurrent knockdown of DUSP5 restored ERK1/2 phosphorylation levels (Figure 5F and Supplementary Figure S7H). These findings establish PTBP1 as a key regulator of DUSP5 and ERK1/2 phosphorylation.

We then investigated the role of the DUSP5-ERK1/2 axis in GSC proliferation and differentiation. PTBP1 knockdown reduced the percentage of Ki67+ GSCs significantly; this effect was attenuated by DUSP5 suppression (Figure 5G and Supplementary Figure S7I, J, and L). Similarly, the PTBP1 knockdown-induced increase in Tuj1 expression was attenuated by DUSP5 knockdown (Figure 5H and Supplementary Figure S7I, K, and L).

To confirm these findings in vivo, we established patient-derived xenograft models using GSC63 and GSC21. MRI revealed that DUSP5 knockdown reversed the suppression of tumor growth induced by PTBP1 depletion (Figure 5I and J and Supplementary Figure S8A and B). Consistent with in vitro findings, PTBP1 knockdown increased DUSP5 expression and decreased ERK1/2 phosphorylation in vivo; these effects were reversed by DUSP5 loss (Figure 5K and L and Supplementary Figure S8C-F). The decrease in Ki67+ GSCs upon PTBP1 knockdown was restored by DUSP5 suppression (Supplementary Figure S8G, I, J, and L), while the increase in Tuj1 expression was counteracted by DUSP5 knockdown (Supplementary Figure S8H, I, K, and L).

PTBP1 overexpression in GSCs using a lentiviral system decreased DUSP5 expression significantly (Supplementary Figure S9A-C) and increased ERK1/2 phosphorylation levels (Supplementary Figure S9D-E). Functionally, PTBP1 overexpression increased cell viability (Supplementary Figure S9F) and markedly increased the percentage of Ki67+ GSCs (Supplementary Figure S9G and H). These results support that PTBP1 regulates GSC proliferation and neuronal-like differentiation through the DUSP5-ERK1/2 pathway.

PTBP1-Targeting Venetoclax-Loaded Nanoparticles Suppress Gliomagenesis

We identified PTBP1 as a potential target to suppress tumor progression. Accordingly, we used molecular docking to identify small molecular compounds interacting with PTBP1. From over 7800 approved drugs and in-clinical trials compounds, we identified 10 candidates (Figure 6A and Supplementary Figure S10A and Supplementary Table S3). Compounds are generally considered blood-brain barrier (BBB) permeable when the predicted log (BBB) value is within −2 to 1.28,29 The log (BBB) values for the ten compounds ranged from −1.827 to −0.534 (Supplementary Table S3), suggesting that they all cross the BBB. Combined with the docking score and MM-GBSA binding free energy, we identified venetoclax, with log (BBB) = −1.747 and strong binding affinity (ΔG: −52.19 ± 8.30 kcal/mol), as a candidate for further analyses. A 500 ns molecular dynamics simulation and trajectory analysis revealed that venetoclax binds stably to the RNA-binding domain β-sheet scaffold of PTBP1 by key residues such as Leu43, Phe50 and Arg4 (Figure 6B), while inducing conformational changes in N- and C-terminal loops, forming a deep pocket to accommodate the compound (Supplementary Figure S10B).

As evaluated using SPR, venetoclax binds to PTBP1 with high affinity (equilibrium dissociation constant [KD] = 7.61 μM; Figure 6C and Supplementary Figure S10C), suggesting that it is a promising small-molecule agent targeting PTBP1. Venetoclax exhibited potent inhibition of GSC63 with an IC50 of 4.91 µM, which is substantially lower than that of the clinical chemotherapeutic agent temozolomide (GSC63: IC50 = 401.1 μM; Figure 6D and Supplementary Figure S10D). An immunofluorescence analysis confirmed that venetoclax reduces Ki67 expression significantly in GSC63 (Figure 6E). To validate its broad applicability, we cultured U373 and GSC21 cells with venetoclax at various concentrations and found IC50 values of 1.94 μM for U373 and 3.06 μM for GSC21 (Supplementary Figure S10E and F). Immunofluorescence confirmed that venetoclax markedly suppresses Ki67 expression in GSC21 (Supplementary Figure S10G). Anti-proliferative effect of venetoclax was significantly attenuated in PTBP1-knockout cells (Supplementary Figure S10H), demonstrating that the maximal anti-proliferative efficacy of venetoclax requires PTBP1. In summary, a low concentration of venetoclax suppresses glioma growth in vitro and may be an effective treatment.

To further assess the efficacy of venetoclax in glioma, we established xenograft models using GSC63 and GSC21 infected with GFP. Using the GSC63 mouse model, the tail vein injection of venetoclax (4 mg/kg) alone failed to suppress glioma proliferation (Supplementary Figure S11A and B) and did not induce apoptosis in neurons or astrocytes (Supplementary Figure S11C and D).

To enhance therapeutic efficacy, we developed a nanoparticle-based combination strategy. We constructed nanoparticles including PLGA, lecithin, DSPE-PEG2000, and A2-DSPE-PEG2000 via a single-step nanoprecipitation method, as described previously15 (Figure 6H). A DLS analysis indicated that the average size of A2-PLGA is 125.9 nm and the size of A2-PLGA/venetoclax increases to 141.1 nm (Figure 6I). TEM revealed that A2-PLGA and A2-PLGA/venetoclax were dispersed as single nanoparticles with a spherical shape (Figure 6J). To assess targeting, DOX-labeled A2-PLGA nanoparticles were injected into glioma-bearing mice. Red fluorescence in tumors was significantly stronger in the A2-PLGA/DOX group than in the free DOX and non-targeted PLGA/DOX, groups, demonstrating successful A2-mediated accumulation (Supplementary Figure S11E).

We evaluated the effect of this new therapy on nude mice harboring orthotopic GSC63 and GSC21 tumors. After confirming brain glioma through MRI at 14 days, nanoparticles were injected intravenously (Supplementary Figure S11F). The tumor area in mice injected with A2-PLGA/venetoclax was remarkably lower than that in mice administered A2-PLGA and saline after GSC63 orthotopic implantation for 42 and 60 days (Figure 6K and L) and after GSC21 implantation for 42 and 70 days (Supplementary Figure S11G and H). Immunofluorescence analyses revealed that levels of Ki67+ tumor cells in mouse mice with A2-PLGA and saline were higher than those in the A2-PLGA/venetoclax group after 60 days for GSC63 and 70 days for GSC21, consistent with the MRI results (Figure 6M and N and Supplementary Figure S11I). Furthermore, the body weight of mice treated withA2-PLGA/venetoclax did not differ substantially during the treatment period compared with that of mice in control groups (Supplementary Figure S11J and K). Mice treated with A2-PLGA/venetoclax had significantly prolonged survival times (Figure 6O and Supplementary Figure S11L). In summary, A2-PLGA loaded with venetoclax achieved a potent anti-tumor effect in glioma.

Discussion

Gliomas, especially glioblastomas, are invasive and incurable tumors within the brain and exhibit histological heterogeneity. Tumor morphology is critical in the progression of gliomas.30 Specifically, the morphological shift of tumor cells from an adhesive to a circular morphology is associated with enhanced cellular motility, accelerated glioma progression, and diminished cell adhesion.31 In this study, we revealed the relationship between glioma cell morphology and survival, offering a reference for predicting patient outcomes. We also showed that PTBP1 regulates DUSP5 expression to alter ERK1/2 phosphorylation, prompting ­glioma cells to differentiate into neuronal-like cells and inhibiting tumor growth. Additionally, we developed the A2-PLGA/venetoclax nanoparticle, a novel therapeutic targeting PTBP1. In different xenograft mouse models, this nanoformulation demonstrated superior therapeutic efficacy at a low dose compared with the clinical chemotherapeutic agent temozolomide.

PTBP1 is expressed in diverse cell types, such as astrocytes, fibroblasts, and various tumor cells, where it has critical functions. For example, PTBP1 plays important roles in the regulation of alternative splicing,32,33 neuronal development,34,35 tumor proliferation, and invasion.3,36 It is also abundant in stem cells and is downregulated in post-differentiated cells.37 PTBP1 maintains stem cell stemness and controls its differentiation.3,32 Furthermore, PTBP1 promotes cell reprogramming and morphology conversion.38 For example, PTBP1 deletion changes tumor cell morphologies and converts tumor cells to neuronal-like cells with long microtubes through the REST complex, as observed in HeLa cells (a cervical carcinoma cell line).39

While recent studies have established PTBP1 as a key regulator of alternative splicing and neuronal differentiation in glioma40 and implicated RNA-binding proteins in mediating tumor plasticity,41 the precise mechanism through which it governs glioma cell morphology and its potential as a direct therapeutic target remain to be fully defined. In this study, analyses of publicly available datasets from TCGA and CGGA, alongside direct assessment of clinical samples, revealed the significant enrichment of PTBP1 in glioma tissues. Moreover, elevated PTBP1 expression was associated with accelerated glioma progression and decreased patient survival times. In both in vivo and in vitro settings, lentiviral-mediated downregulation of PTBP1 in glioma cells induced notable morphological alterations and impaired proliferative and differentiative capacities, leading to a marked extension of survival in mouse models. The glioma cells undergoing morphological transformation exhibited a neuron-like morphology and protein expression but did not possess neuronal electrophysiological properties; therefore, they were classified as neuronal-like cells. Our study provides initial insight into the therapeutic value of PTBP1 in glioma.

The ERK signaling pathway is a key regulatory hub in cell proliferation, metabolism, cell morphology, and differentiation.42 ERK-mediated mechanisms determine cell fate decisions. For example, the activation of ERK1/2 phosphorylation can reprogram somatic cells into pluripotent stem cells.43 DUSP5, a specific regulator of the ERK1/2 pathway, has been reported to modulate tumor progression in colorectal cancer by regulating ERK1/2 phosphorylation.44 However, the role of the DUSP5-ERK1/2 signaling axis in gliomas remains poorly understood. In this study, we provide the first evidence that PTBP1 knockdown in GSCs upregulates DUSP5, thereby suppressing ERK1/2 phosphorylation and reprogramming glioma cells into neuronal-like cells, ultimately attenuating tumor progression.

Gliomas present significant treatment challenges, and traditional drug therapies have important limitations. Our findings clearly identify PTBP1 as a potential therapeutic target. Through molecular docking studies, venetoclax was identified as a novel small molecule capable of targeting PTBP1. Notably, venetoclax has been recognized for its efficacy in targeting the BCL-2 protein and has established clinical applications in treating pediatric leukemia. Of note, venetoclax showed strong anti-tumor effects on glioma in vitro and in vivo. In addition, the IC50 of venetoclax was much lower than that of temozolomide, a first-line clinical glioma medicine. Venetoclax was able to cross the BBB, reducing loss during drug absorption and distribution.45 Compared with alternative PTBP1-targeting approaches, such as inactivated virus infection and CRISPR/Cas9 systems, venetoclax therapy is safer and more conveniently regulated. However, the clinical advancement of venetoclax for brain tumors is limited by low CNS availability, evidenced by a high plasma-to-CSF ratio (385:1) and minimal CSF penetration (∼0.74%).46 This poor bioavailability necessitates high systemic doses, increasing the risk of off-target toxicity. To overcome this limitation, we and others are exploring nanotechnology as a key strategy to enhance brain delivery while mitigating systemic exposure. Nanoparticle-based products could enhance drug retention, control release, improve the BBB penetration of therapeutic agents significantly, and achieve efficient cell or tissue targeting.47,48 These benefits highlight the potential of A2-PLGA/venetoclax nanoparticles, expanding the applications of venetoclax.

Overall, our analysis revealed that PTBP1 promotes glioma growth and its loss converts glioma cells into neuronal-like cells with lower proliferation activity. A2-PLGA/venetoclax significantly inhibits glioma progression and prolongs the survival of mice bearing orthotopic GSCs. Thus, nanoparticle-mediated delivery of venetoclax is a promising therapy for further clinical development for high-grade glioma.
