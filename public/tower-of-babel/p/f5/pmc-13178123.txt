Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the systematic review and meta-analysis by Attachaipanich et al. of the efficacy of noninvasive ventilation (NIV) compared with invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) in cardiogenic shock, and we congratulate the authors for addressing an important and clinically challenging area in critical care.1 The reported associations between NIV use and lower in-hospital and 30-day mortality, and shorter hospital and intensive care unit (ICU)/coronary care unit stays, are provocative and have the potential for major implications for bedside practice if confirmed.1 However, some methodological problems provide cause for cautious interpretation of these results.

First, all six included studies were observational and judged to be at overall “serious” risk of bias using the ROBINS-I tool, primarily because of the risk of confounding and classification of interventions.1 The developers of ROBINS-I explicitly state that a judgment of “serious” risk of bias in any domain implies that the effect estimate from such a study is at serious risk of bias or worse, and that bodies of evidence based on such studies will typically be rated as low or very low certainty when applying GRADE.2,3 Pooling nonrandomized studies with serious bias, for example, into a single summary odds ratio can therefore give a misleading impression of causal certainty. The current guidance recommends that results from such evidence be interpreted in a strictly hypothesis-generating manner and not as definitive estimates of treatment effect.2,3

Second, there is a high risk of confounding by indication. Attachaipanich et al. note that IMV was preferentially used in patients with younger but more severely ill, with a higher use of vasopressors, mechanical circulatory support and revascularization.1 Confounding by indication occurs when the clinical indication leading to the choice of treatment (in this case, greater severity of the shock and the need for airway protection) may itself be a powerful determinant of outcome, leading to biased associations even after traditional multivariable adjustment.4,5 In the case of cardiogenic shock, the Society for Cardiovascular Angiography and Interventions (SCAIs) classification and subsequent validation studies have established a steep, graded increase in in-hospital and long-term mortality from SCAI stage C through D and E.6,7 Because only two of the six included studies reported SCAI stages, and because NIV was more frequently used in less advanced shock, residual confounding by unmeasured severity is highly likely to have occurred, and may fully explain the apparent mortality advantage of NIV.

Third, the management of NIV failure and NIV-to-IMV crossover is a cause for concern of immortal time bias. In the included cohorts, between 20 and 50% of patients who were initially treated with NIV eventually required intubation, and the way in which such patients were classified differed in each study.1 In observational research in the ICU, immortal time bias occurs when we misclassify as exposed person-time time periods that could not have resulted in an outcome (e.g., the time that a patient must survive on NIV before being intubated) and spuriously inflate the apparent benefit of the exposure.8,9 Critical care methodological reviews have pointed out that naive time-fixed analyses of time-varying treatments (e.g., transition from NIV to IMV) can substantially overestimate survival benefits unless appropriate time-dependent models or landmark strategies are used.8,10 Because the current meta-analysis did not harmonize and account for time-varying exposure definitions in the individual studies, the pooled estimates of mortality may be affected by immortal time bias.

Fourth, the effects of publication bias and small-study effects cannot be reliably ruled out. Attachaipanich et al. carried out funnel plot inspection and Egger's test, even though there were only four to six studies for each outcome and interpreted their non-significant tests as evidence of no publication bias.1 Methodological advice from the Cochrane Handbook and simulation work dedicated to these issues has highlighted the low power of tests for funnel plot asymmetry, which should be confined to meta-analyses that include at least 10 studies;11,12 below this threshold, non-significant outcomes are uninformative. In addition, even when there are more studies available, statistical tests of small-study effects do not often perform well, and visual funnel plot asymmetry can occur for reasons other than publication bias.11,12 Consequently, the lack of statistically significant asymmetry in this review should not be interpreted as reassurance about having no reporting bias.

Finally, the conclusions of the article seem stronger than they are justified by the underlying evidence. The abstract and the discussion indicate that NIV “was associated with improved short-term outcomes” and that NIV “may be most beneficial in early-stage cardiogenic shock.”1 Given the exclusively observational nature of the data, significant risk of bias, large amount of confounding by indication, the possibility for immortal time bias, and the limited degree of stratification by severity, such wording carries the risk of being misinterpreted to suggest benefit, rather than describe a low-certainty association that must be confirmed in well-designed prospective studies.2,9 Current expert consensus encourages careful SCAI-based risk stratification, individualized ventilatory decisions and, where possible, the utilization of randomized or carefully analyzed prospective data before changing practices in cardiogenic shock.6,7

In summary, we congratulate the authors for bringing together a growing body of data relating to NIV in cardiogenic shock, but we recommend that clinicians consider the reported benefit of NIV over IMV in reducing both mortality and length of stay with great caution in light of these methodological concerns. Further studies using high-quality prospective research and ideally incorporating SCAI-based staging, explicit time-varying exposure modeling and prespecified strategies to address the issue of confounding by indication and publication bias are necessary before firm conclusions can be drawn.
