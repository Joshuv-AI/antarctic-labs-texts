Suicidal ideation in adolescents: the role of personal and school variables
Authors: Elizabeth Cañas-Pardo, J. Francisco Estévez-García, Estefanía Estévez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, suicidal ideation has gained relevance, not only because it involves thoughts that precede suicidal behavior and attempts, but also due to its high prevalence among adolescents. However, knowledge about the social and personal factors involved in this issue remains limited. Objective Therefore, the aim of this study was to analyse the possible social factors (school climate, cybervictimisation, and cyberbullying) and personal factors (psychological inflexibility and Basic Psychological Needs -BPN-) that act as predictors of suicidal ideation in adolescents, also considering gender differences. Method The sample consisted of 2,977 Spanish adolescents (51.5% girls) aged between 11 and 17 years old (M = 14.1, SD = 1.42). Results The results indicated that, overall, the variables examined at their respective levels were associated with an increased probability of suicidal ideation, with the exception of high levels of cyberbullying, which were associated with a reduction in this probability. Furthermore, both the school environment and satisfaction with BPN were associated with a decreased probability of suicidal ideation, with the latter showing the most pronounced effect. Additionally, it was observed that gender serves as a significant predictor in estimating the probability of this variable’s manifestation. Conclusions This study highlights the need to implement educational initiatives, focusing particularly on the school environment, and aimed at parents and caregivers to identify risk factors related to suicidal ideation in adolescents.

Introduction

In recent years, suicide has been recognized as a serious global public health issue, with multifactorial causes [15]. Moreover, this problem becomes more alarming when considering the risk it poses among adolescents. In fact, the World Health Organisation has identified suicide as the third leading cause of death among young people [82]. Globally, it is estimated that the prevalence of suicide among the adolescent population ranges between 10 and 35%, and between 5 and 15% for suicide attempts [21]. Suicidal ideation is the first link in the chain of suicidal behavior [15], acting as a predictor of future attempts and consummation [83]. A meta-analysis conducted by Biswas et al. [10] of various international studies with adolescents aged 12 to 17 years found that the 12-month pooled prevalence of suicidal ideation was 14%. The data presented demonstrate the need to focus on its prevention. [47, 54].

Although suicidal behavior has received considerable empirical support in recent years, there are still serious gaps in our understanding of the personal risk factors associated with this behavior, especially in the case of adolescents. Self-determination theory [27, 68], offers a possible understanding of the role of basic psychological needs in the experience of personal risk factors for suicide. This theory asserts that well-being occurs when three basic psychological needs are satisfied: competence, autonomy, and relatedness. Specifically, the need for competence represents the feeling of capability and confidence in one's own abilities to interact effectively with the environment; the need for autonomy reflects the importance of having personal control over one's own actions; and the need for relatedness implies the human desire to feel connected to others and experience a sense of belonging within a group or community. The BPN could play a protective role against suicidal ideation, given that suicide, as described by Shneidman [72], is a behavior that stems from frustrated or distorted needs. Accordingly, the results of some research studies have shown that the satisfaction of BPN could act as a moderator between mental health issues, such as depressive symptoms, and suicidal ideation. However, this has been primarily studied in university students [62]. Specifically, this study revealed that students who reported high levels of depressive symptoms and, at the same time, high satisfaction of their relatedness needs exhibited lower levels of suicidal ideation compared to those with low satisfaction of these needs. Britton et al. [14] also indicated that young adults whose BPN are met may be less likely to consider suicide or engage in suicidal behavior. Conversely, when BPNs are not adequately met due to personal or external factors, it can have detrimental effects on mental health, potentially increasing the risk of suicidal ideation [69]. In this regard, other research indicates that people who have suicidal thoughts or commit suicidal behaviors often report unmet BPN [76].

One way to counteract frustrated needs would be to develop an excessive desire to control the unmet need, and thus create a rigid pattern of behavior. However, while this rigidity might initially alleviate the perceived frustration of the need, it would also interfere with the possible satisfaction of that need and, therefore, with individual well-being [27]. One such pattern of functioning is psychological inflexibility, characterised by rigid attempts to control, modify, or minimise unpleasant internal experiences, resulting in an inability to change and/or persist in behaviors guided by principles and values [41].

In addition to the above, to understand suicide in adolescence, it is also necessary to take into account the relational context, given the importance of social relationships during this period of development [47]. Regarding the social/school context, various elements related to the climate perceived at school by students and their degree of acceptance/rejection and victimization by peers have been identified as risk factors. In this sense, a negative school climate or a negative perception of the school environment have been associated with a higher risk of suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts [29, 32]. In the case of cyberbullying behavior, adolescents exposed to cybervictimization experience ongoing stress derived from hurtful comments and messages shared by uncontrollably large audiences [73], negatively impacting their well-being and development [20, 58]. In this sense, emotional problems resulting from cyberbullying are significantly associated with suicidal behavior [23]. Various studies observed that adolescents who suffer from cyberbullying show higher levels of suicidal ideation [55]. Other research studies suggest that cybervictims are more likely to contemplate suicide as a way to escape the intense stress of being a victim of this type of online harassment [5]. Specifically, individuals who have been victims of cyberbullying are twice as likely to experience suicidal thoughts compared to those who have not suffered this type of victimization. In the case of cyberbullies, research on suicidal behavior is limited compared to the attention given to victims of cyberbullying. A study conducted by Hinduja and Patchin [42] with high school students in the United States found that cyberbullies were more likely to attempt suicide and have more suicidal thoughts than those who did not engage in cyberbullying, but to a lesser extent than victims of cyberbullying. Another study with Canadian adolescents reported that the perpetration of cyberbullying surpassed traditional bullying in terms of suicidal ideation [12].

Concerning longitudinal studies, evidence suggests that the probability of cyberbullies reporting suicidal ideation doubled one year after engaging in such behavior compared to adolescents not involved in bullying [9]. Similarly, research conducted by Sampasa-Kanynga et al. [70] revealed that adolescents who engaged in bullying, whether online or in person, were more likely to experience suicidal thoughts and behaviors than those who did not engage in bullying. However, recent studies indicate that the connection between cyberbullying perpetration and suicidal ideation is weaker than that found in the case of cybervictims [48]. In contrast, other studies have found no relationship between cyberbullying perpetration and suicidal ideation in adolescents [66]. Therefore, further research is needed to understand the mechanisms involved in the relationship between cybervictimization and suicidal behaviors, as well as to identify potential protective factors in this connection [22].

Scientific evidence also emphasizes the need to specifically explore possible psychosocial factors related to suicidal ideation in adolescents, taking gender into account, given the variability between boys and girls in prevalence rates and risk profiles. In line with this, recent research conducted worldwide with adolescents has found a higher rate of suicidal ideation and attempts among girls than boys [34, 60]. These differences in suicidal tendencies have been explained by several gender-specific risk factors, also known as the “gender paradox,” whereby adolescent girls were also more likely than boys to report thinking about suicide and having attempted it [19]. However, completed suicide rates among adolescents and young adults are higher among males [38, 59].

Suicidal ideation not only differs according to gender, but also varies according to age within the same age group, such as adolescence. A longitudinal study revealed that males showed a peak in suicide ideation at age 12, followed by a steady decline over the 36 months of follow-up [1]. However, in girls, the peak of suicidal ideation was around 15 years of age [13, 18, 45]. These findings point to early adolescence as a critical period for addressing suicidality, given the variability in suicidal ideation patterns [6]. Moreover, adolescence is a stage when the range of social activities and peer relationships expands considerably [77], and suicide is a phenomenon that occurs due to the involvement of individual, social, and cultural factors [71].

Thus, the two objectives of this work were, on the one hand, analyze the personal (psychological inflexibility and BPN) and social/school factors (school climate, cybervictimization, and cyberbullying) that act as risk factor forsuicidal ideation in adolescents. And on the other hand, examine the interactions that gender and age may have on the personal and social/school variables in this study. Although existing research studies have documented consistent differences in the prevalence rates of suicidal ideation between boys and girls, there is a significant lack of studies that explicitly explore gender differences in the risk factors for suicidal thoughts [35], and even more so when age is taken into account.

In this regard, the hypotheses proposed in this study were as follows:H1: Psychological inflexibility (personal factor), cyberbullying, and cybervictimization (social/school factors) would act as predictors of suicidal ideation, whereas BPN (individual factor) and school climate (social/school factor) would act as protectors.H2: Gender would significantly predict suicidal ideation, such that girls are expected to report higher levels of suicidal ideation than boys.H3: Age would act as a significant predictor of suicidal ideation, especially in girls, suggesting that developmental stage may influence vulnerability to suicide differently depending on gender.

Method

Participants

The sample for this study comprised 2,977 adolescents (51.5% girls) aged between 11 and 17 years old (M = 14.1, SD = 1.42). The sample was selected using cluster random sampling from seven middle and high schools located in the regions of Aragon, Andalusia, and the Valencian Community in Spain. The primary sampling units were urban and rural areas within these three communities, while the secondary units were public secondary schools in each area. Classrooms were not selected as tertiary units because all the levels of Secondary and Higher Education were taken into account. The distribution by grade level was as follows: 25.5% in the 6th grade, 26.6% in the 7th grade, 25.4% in the 9th grade, and 25.5% in the 10th grade.

Measures

Suicidal ideation

This variable was assessed with the Suicide Scale by Paykel et al. [64], in its Spanish version adapted by Fonseca-Pedrero et al. [34]. The scale consists of five items that progressively analyze suicidal ideation (e.g., “Have you thought about taking your life even if you weren’t Yes No really going to?”). Each item has a dichotomous response format (Yes/No). In the analyzed sample, the Cronbach’s alpha for the total scale was 0.83. The scale has demonstrated predictive validity for subsequent suicide attempts in adolescent samples [34].

Basic psychological needs

For this variable, the BPN Perception Scale [37], adapted by León et al. [56], was used. This scale measures the level of satisfaction of the needs for autonomy, competence, and interpersonal relationships, with a total of 15 items rated on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 7 (strongly agree). The internal consistency of the general scale for the sample in this study was 0.89, and for the dimensions of competence (e.g., “I feel that I can do things well”), autonomy (e.g., “I feel free to make my own decisions”), and interpersonal relationships (e.g., “I feel good about the people with whom I interact”), the alphas were 0.79, 0.79 and 0.83, respectively. The scale shows a robust three-factor structure across cultures and correlates positively with well-being and intrinsic motivation [57].

Psychological inflexibility

Psychological inflexibility was analyzed using the Avoidance and Fusion Questionnaire for Youth [39], in its Spanish adaptation by Valdivia-Salas et al. [75]. This questionnaire assesses two interrelated processes of psychological inflexibility:cognitive fusion (e.g., “I am afraid of my feelings”) and experiential avoidance (e.g., “I must get rid of my worries and fears so I can have a good life”). It includes 17 items rated on a 7-point Likert-type response scale ranging from 1 (not true at all) to 7 (very true). Cronbach’s alpha in this study was α = 0.84 for Psychological Inflexibility, α = 0.77 for the Cognitive Fusion dimension, and α = 0.71 for Experiential Avoidance. The instrument demonstrates good factorial validity and correlates significantly with psychopathology measures in adolescent samples [75].

School climate

School climate was analyzed through the relational dimension of peer affiliation of the Classroom Environment Scale (CES; [74]), in the Spanish version by Fernández-Ballesteros and Sierra [33]. This version comprises 20 items and is rated on a 7-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (never) to 7 (always). It presents two subscales of classroom climate from the student’s perspective, aiming to measure the level of friendship, affiliation, and support among peers (e.g., “Students in this class get to know each other really well”), as well as teacher support, which refers to the help, trust, and friendship provided by the teacher to students (e.g., “The teacher takes a personal interest in the students”). In this study, the Cronbach’s alpha for the overall scale was 0.83, while for the subscales of affiliation and teacher support, values of 0.74 and 0.81 were obtained, respectively. The instrument shows strong factorial validity and correlates positively with academic performance and well-being indicators [31].

Cybervictimization and cyberbullying

To assess cyberbullying behavior, the Peer Harassment Screening questionnaire was administered [36]. This instrument includes 15 items assessing cybervictimization behaviors (e.g., “Have you been bullied in this way for the past year?”) and 15 items evaluating cyberbullying perpetration (e.g., “Have you attacked someone like this continuously during the past year?”), each rated on a 4-point frequency scale (0 = never, 1 = sometimes, 2 = frequently, 3 = always). The internal consistency of the cybervictimization scale was 0.89, and for the cyberbullying scale, it was 0.90. The scales demonstrate good factorial structure and correlate appropriately with psychological adjustment measures [16, 17].

Procedure

Before starting the study, approval was obtained from the ethics committees of the research institutions involved. In the first phase, a letter summarising the project was sent to the selected schools. Then, an initial phone contact was made with the school principals, followed by an informational meeting with the entire teaching staff to present the objectives and methodology of the study during a two-hour session. In parallel, a letter was sent to the parents explaining the content of the study and asking them to respond in writing if they did not wish their children to participate (1% chose this option). The remaining parents provided passive (opt-out) consent. Both the teachers and the parents expressed their interest in receiving information about the study results during a meeting with the research team, which was held once the data analysis was completed.

The students attended an informational session in which they provided their written consent (no adolescent refused to participate). The instruments were administered by a group of trained and specialised researchers in each region on the dates scheduled with the teaching staff. Each participant voluntarily and anonymously completed each of the scales in their respective schools during a normal school day. Surveys with suspicious response patterns were not included in the database (representing 1% of the total initial samples).

Statistical analysis

Sample size justification

A retrospective power analysis was conducted to justify the sample size of 2,977 adolescents. Following established guidelines for logistic regression [43], the sample exceeded the recommended 10–15 cases per predictor rule (minimum n = 165 for 11 predictors). Power calculations demonstrated that our sample provided excellent statistical power (> 0.99) to detect small to moderate effect sizes, including odds ratios as small as 1.5 (power = 1.000, α = 0.05). The sample size was 19 times larger than the minimum required (n = 157) to detect OR = 1.5 with 80% power, ensuring adequate precision for parameter estimates. Additionally, the sample was sufficient for gender-stratified analyses, with each subgroup (n ≈ 1,488) maintaining excellent power (> 0.99) to detect moderate effects (OR ≥ 2.0).

Age effects and linearity assessment

The prevalence of suicidal ideation varied modestly across ages, ranging from 49.4% at age 12 to 62.2% at age 17. Formal testing supported the linearity assumption for age. A likelihood ratio test comparing linear versus categorical age models found no significant evidence of non-linearity (χ2 = 6.00, df = 6, p = 0.423). Model comparison using Akaike’s Information Criterion confirmed that the linear model (AIC = 4074.95) provided a better fit than categorical (AIC = 4080.95) or spline alternatives (AIC = 4078.70).

Gender differences in suicidal ideation were consistent across age, with girls showing a higher prevalence than boys at all age levels. However, the Age-by-Gender interaction was nonsignificant (χ2 = 0.21, df = 1, p = 0.643), indicating that the developmental trajectory of suicidal ideation did not differ significantly between boys and girls in this sample.

Exploratory and descriptive analysis

First, an exploratory analysis was conducted to assess the suitability of the data for this study. The average percentage of missing data was 3.3%, and it never exceeded 5% for an individual measure. In terms of effectiveness and consistency, previous simulation studies have shown that, when the percentages of missing data are below 5% [30], there are no relevant biases or implications. To address these missing values, item-level imputation was performed using regression, leveraging responses from valid items within the same scales or closely related scales.

A comprehensive assessment of variable distributions was conducted using Shapiro–Wilk and Kolmogorov–Smirnov normality tests. Results revealed that 6 out of 7 variables (85.7%) significantly deviated from normality (ps < 0.001), displaying characteristic skewed distributions typical of psychological and behavioral measures in adolescent samples. Specifically, cybervictimization (skewness = 2.31, kurtosis = 6.10) and cyberbullying (skewness = 4.83, kurtosis = 36.55) showed severe positive skewness and leptokurtic distributions. Visual inspection through histograms and Q-Q plots confirmed these findings, with pronounced floor effects and asymmetric distributions observed across key study variables. Consequently, non-parametric methods were employed: Spearman correlations were used to examine bivariate relationships due to their robustness to non-normal distributions, and two-step cluster analysis was implemented for variable operationalisation.

Variable operationalisation

Both theoretical considerations and empirical data characteristics guided variable operationalisation. Suicidal ideation was dichotomised (0 = never, 1 = always) based on clinical relevance and previous validation studies [4]. This binary approach prioritises the estimation of the probability of experiencing any suicidal ideation over quantifying specific values on a limited 5-point scale (sum of 0–1 items from the Paykel scale), which aligns better with clinical decision-making and risk assessment needs. A value of 0 was assigned when responses to all items were negative (43.8%), and a value of 1 was assigned when at least one of the items was answered affirmatively (56.2%).

Independent variables were operationalised using two-step cluster analysis to create meaningful categorical predictors. This approach was particularly appropriate given the non-normal distributions characteristic of these psychological and behavioral variables, as it accommodates non-normal input data while using the BIC criterion to determine optimal cluster numbers. All cluster solutions were validated through ANOVA and chi-square tests (all ps < 0.001). This methodology reduces complexity while maintaining interpretability, preserves construct validity, and captures both natural data groupings and non-linear relationships between items.

To validate the cluster-based categorization, we compared our primary model against alternatives using continuous variables (standardized and log-transformed). The cluster-based approach demonstrated superior model fit (Δ AIC = 167, McFadden R2 = 0.150 vs 0.108) while maintaining robust primary findings—five of six predictors showed consistent effects across methods. A key finding is that cluster analysis revealed a non-linear cyberbullying pattern that continuous models simplified as linear, exemplifying how this approach can detect complex relationships that linear specifications obscure. These findings support cluster-based categorization as methodologically appropriate for our data.

Logistic regression models

After identifying the levels of the variables, unadjusted coefficients were calculated from univariate logistic regression models to determine which variables should be included in subsequent multivariate models [43]. Additionally, the sociodemographic variables of gender and age were included in these calculations, with gender coded as a binary variable (boys as the reference category) and age treated as a continuous covariate.

Before developing the final logistic regression models, several diagnostic procedures were conducted to ensure model validity. Collinearity among all independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), which ranged from 1.06 to 1.49, well below the problematic thresholds of 5–10 suggested in the literature [46, 78].

For the total sample model, interactions between gender and each of the five predictor variables were systematically investigated, while age was included solely as a covariate without interaction terms. Model selection followed a structured approach: each potential gender interaction was evaluated using likelihood ratio tests, and competing models were compared using the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) to determine optimal specifications. Only statistically significant interactions (p < 0.05) that improved model fit were retained in the final models.

Model adequacy was confirmed through several goodness-of-fit measures. All logistic regression models achieved statistical significance, and Hosmer–Lemeshow tests indicated appropriate model fit (p > 0.05), confirming that the models adequately captured the relationship between predictors and suicidal ideation. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0 [65], except for the two-step cluster analysis, which was performed using SPSS 27.0 [44].

Both personal factors showed consistent protective and risk patterns across genders, though with some magnitude differences. BPN demonstrated strong protective effects for both groups: among boys, average (OR = 0.53, p < 0.001) and high (OR = 0.36, p < 0.001) levels significantly reduced suicidal ideation odds compared to low BPN. For girls, similar protective patterns emerged with average (OR = 0.63, p = 0.002) and high (OR = 0.44, p < 0.001) BPN levels. Psychological inflexibility showed nearly identical strong risk associations in both genders (boys: OR = 3.65, p < 0.001; girls: OR = 3.78, p < 0.001).

School climate showed nonsignificant associations for both genders (boys: OR = 0.91, p = 0.424; girls: OR = 0.83, p = 0.145). Cyberbullying patterns were similar across genders, with high levels showing protective effects (boys: OR = 0.65, p = 0.024; girls: OR = 0.64, p = 0.027).

The most striking gender difference emerged in cybervictimization patterns. For boys, only high cybervictimization significantly increased suicidal ideation risk (low: OR = 1.05, p = 0.698; high: OR = 2.11, p < 0.001). However, for girls, both low and high cybervictimization levels significantly increased risk (low: OR = 1.72, p < 0.001; high: OR = 3.76, p < 0.001), indicating heightened vulnerability to cybervictimization experiences at any level.

Discussion

This study aimed to analyze personal factors (psychological inflexibility and BPN) and social/school factors (school climate, cybervictimization, and cyberbullying) that act as risk factors for suicidal ideation in adolescents. A second objective was to examine differences between boys and girls, as well as to explore the interactions that age and gender may have on the other relational variables analyzed.

Regarding Hypothesis 1, the results indicated that all the variables examined, at their respective levels, were associated with an increased probability of suicidal ideation, with the exception of high levels of cyberbullying, school climate, and BPN, which were associated with a decreased probability. Consequently, this hypothesis was partially supported. These results are consistent with various studies that have analyzed the risk factors for suicidal ideation, although in an isolated manner. For example, in terms of personal factors, some studies have found that BPN is negatively related to suicidal ideation [84]. Other studies have concluded that satisfaction of these needs is a protective factor against suicidal ideation [14]. Regarding the other personal factor, psychological inflexibility in this study acts as a significant risk factor for suicidal ideation, as it almost triples the probability compared to individuals with lower levels of inflexibility. This finding is consistent with studies suggesting that this construct was positively associated with suicidal ideation in adolescents [2, 67, 80]. Psychological inflexibility may contribute to developing suicidal thoughts in adolescence by considering suicide as a definitive method for problem-solving or for coping with situations and emotions that are uncomfortable or painful [8, 24, 26, 52]. This prevents the individual from considering other effective strategies for resolving such situations [7, 40].

In terms of social/school factors, the results of this study indicate that a positive classroom climate is a protective factor against suicide by reducing the risk of suicidal ideation [28]. This result can be explained, as Jiménez et al. [47] point out, by the importance of the relational context in understanding suicidal behavior during adolescence, since the development and psychological adjustment of adolescents are deeply rooted in social objectives that are constructed within meaningful relational contexts, such as school. In line with the relational context of adolescents, the results of this study indicate that being a victim of cyberbullying is associated with a higher probability of suicidal ideation [61]. However, cyberbullying perpetration revealed a non-linear relationship with suicidal ideation. While low levels showed no significant association, high levels were associated with reduced odds. This pattern warrants careful interpretation and may reflect several mechanisms. First, statistical suppression likely occurs due to the high correlation between perpetration and victimization, as many perpetrators are also victims [3]. Second, severe perpetration may represent an externalizing behavioral pattern that competes with internalizing symptoms such as suicidal ideation. Third, online interaction prevents cyberbullies from being aware of the distress they cause to victims, so they tend to experience lower levels of distress and less strain on their mental health [3], particularly given that adolescents respond to online peer interactions automatically, without fully understanding the magnitude of their actions [50, 51]. Importantly, this non-monotonic pattern emerged only through cluster-based operationalization,continuous variable models erroneously simplified this relationship as a linear negative effect, underscoring the value of detecting non-linear patterns in psychological data. Future research should examine the mechanisms underlying this complex relationship, particularly the interplay between perpetration, victimization, and externalizing-internalizing symptom patterns.

Regarding Hypothesis 2, a multivariate analysis was performed to evaluate the contribution of each factor in the joint prediction of suicidal ideation, observing that gender acts as a significant predictor in estimating the probability of manifestation of this variable. This finding is consistent with several studies that have explored the relationship between gender and suicidal ideation in adolescent populations [53, 81, 84]. In addition, the analysis revealed the existence of interaction effects, especially in girls, providing new insights into the predictive power of gender in combination with the relational factors investigated, which will be detailed below. On the one hand, gender appears to have a significant relationship with suicidal ideation, which is supported by previous research indicating higher levels of suicidal ideation in girls compared to boys [15, 84]. Concerning the cyber context, the resulting interaction between the level of cybervictimization and gender revealed that the probability of experiencing suicidal ideation increases among girls with low and high levels of cybervictimization. This finding is consistent with previous studies indicating that girls are more likely to be victims of cyberbullying than boys [79] and tend to present more internalizing symptoms [11]. Overall, the results support the need for continued exploration of gender, given the importance of this variable both as an independent variable and in its interaction with low cybervictimization.

Another variable significantly associated with suicidal ideation was age, especially among girls, as suggested in Hypothesis 3. Furthermore, this result is supported by research studies indicating that around the age of 15, rates of suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts are higher among females [13, 18, 45]. However, these differences were less pronounced regarding personal factors, which vary very little based on gender. Average and high levels of BPN are negatively associated with the probability of suicidal ideation in both boys and girls. In other words, unmet BPN are significantly related to suicidal ideation. From the perspective of the interpersonal theory of suicide [49], it is important to recognize that unmet BPN may reflect “thwarted belongingness” or a lack of social connection [25], which can affect both girls and boys. Additionally, psychological inflexibility is strongly associated with an increased probability of suicidal ideation among adolescents, regardless of gender. This suggests that BPN functions as a protective factor, and psychological inflexibility as a risk factor for suicidal ideation, at least within the analyzed population.

Considering developmental considerations, the linear relationship between age and suicidal ideation in the sample of the present study (11–17 years) is consistent with previous research suggesting a gradual increase in suicidal thoughts during adolescence [63]. The absence of significant non-linear age-related effects suggests that the developmental trajectory within this age range can be adequately captured by linear models. However, consistent gender differences across all ages highlight the importance of considering gender-specific risk factors throughout adolescent development. The findings suggest the potential existence of a complex interaction between these factors, warranting a more detailed examination to guide future prevention strategies.

Conclusions

This study provides a comprehensive view of suicidal ideation, suggesting that the propensity to ideate and think about suicide may result from the interaction of several factors, including the positive influence of school climate and BPN, and even being a perpetrator of cyberbullying, as well as risk factors such as psychological inflexibility, as well as risk factors such as cyberbullying and psychological inflexibility. Therefore, it is essential to create programs that promote, among other things, the development of BPN and the improvement of psychological flexibility. These programs should also incorporate tools for the early detection of at-risk youth. It is crucial to implement educational initiatives aimed at parents and caregivers to identify signs of dissatisfaction in BPN, psychological inflexibility, and suicidal ideation in adolescents.

Limitations

This study presents several limitations that should be considered for future research in this field. Primarily, its cross-sectional and quantitative design prevents establishing causal relationships between the variables analysed, as well as addressing the complexity of the relationships studied in greater depth. The analyses conducted are limited to a specific moment, preventing the assertion of temporal sequences or causes of the variables studied. Even though data collection was conducted anonymously, attempting to reduce potential response biases, the use of self-administered questionnaires entails certain issues, such as biases inherent to self-assessment, which can affect the potential generalizability and validity of the findings. Another important limitation is that this study focuses on adolescence, covering young people between the ages of 11 and 17. Therefore, the results cannot be generalised or extrapolated to students at other educational levels or ages, such as early childhood, primary, or higher education, nor to school contexts of different backgrounds and cultures. Furthermore, the study did not examine the dynamics between the roles of victim and aggressor. In cases of cyberbullying, this dual role is a complex aspect that could enrich the understanding of the phenomena studied in this work. Cluster sampling based on schools can be considered another limitation, as it may introduce dependencies among students from the same school. This could affect all the variables analyzed and limit the generalization of the results. Future research should consider multilevel or cluster-adjusted models to account for correlations within schools.

Future research directions

This study has represented a significant advance in understanding the different factors involved in suicidal ideation in adolescents. However, due to its limitations, some recommendations have been established for future studies to consider. On the one hand, qualitative measures should be included, and reports from parents and teachers should be taken into account to help verify the attitudes and behaviors self-reported by adolescents, thereby reducing the potential social desirability inherent in the use of self-report measures. It would also be interesting to analyse the dual victim-aggressor role in future studies, as it could provide a more comprehensive view of bullying and cyberbullying. However, given the difficulty of generalising the results of the present study to other contexts and stages of development, it would be interesting to replicate exploratory or unexpected findings. In turn, including the family as a social factor could provide greater insight into such a relevant issue as suicidal ideation in adolescents.

Prevention and policy implications

This study contributes to the understanding of relationships relevant to mental health, specifically between cyberbullying and suicidal ideation in adolescents, by integrating a combination of variables that had not been explored until now. The multidisciplinary perspective that emerges from this research highlights the importance of considering both gender and age to understand vulnerability to unpleasant online experiences and negative consequences for mental health, such as suicidal thoughts. It also emphasises the need for personalised interventions that address gender differences, laying the foundation for future research studies, as well as prevention programs and policies.
