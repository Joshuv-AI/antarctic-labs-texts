ZDHHC17-mediated IKKβ palmitoylation suppresses NF-κB signaling to mitigate the progression of liver fibrosis
Authors: Bin Fan, Meng Yi, Xiaolong Zhong, Shilin Li, Chunhui Yang, Peng Zhang, Daping Song, Qing Xiao, Yujia Li, Xiaoqiong Duan, Limin Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryLiver fibrosis (LF) is a common consequence of chronic liver injury and inflammation, driven by the IKK complex-mediated NF-κB signaling pathway. This study investigated the role of macrophage IKKβ palmitoylation in NF-κB signaling and LF progression. Analysis of liver tissues and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) from LF patients and healthy individuals revealed decreased ZDHHC17 expression in LF-associated macrophages. Using mouse models of LF and ZDHHC17 knockout mice, we found that ZDHHC17 deficiency accelerated LF progression. Mechanistically, ZDHHC17 palmitoylated IKKβ at cysteine 299, inhibiting its autophosphorylation and NF-κB pathway activation. This suppression reduced macrophage inflammatory responses and hepatic stellate cell activation, mitigating LF. Additionally, IKKβ palmitoylation weakened its interaction with TAK1, reducing phosphorylation at Ser177 and Ser181, further inhibiting NF-κB signaling. These findings highlight ZDHHC17 as a key regulator of IKKβ palmitoylation, suppressing inflammation, and LF progression. Targeting IKKβ palmitoylation may offer a novel therapeutic approach for LF.Subject areas: Hepatology, Immunology, Cell biology

Introduction

Liver fibrosis (LF), particularly end-stage cirrhosis, is the primary contributor to liver disease-related mortality globally.1 Diverse etiologies, including viral hepatitis, alcoholism, cholestasis, and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis, contribute to repeated liver inflammation and injury, leading to LF.2,3 LF is presently recognized as a dynamic sequence of sustained and excessive hepatic inflammation resulting from recurrent or chronic hepatocellular damage, irrespective of its causes.4 Uncontrolled chronic liver inflammation converts the self-limiting tissue repair process to a detrimental cycle that fosters the occurrence and progression of LF.5

Liver macrophages, including resident hepatic macrophages (Kupffer cells) and recruited macrophages, have been recognized as essential regulators of hepatic inflammation, and they play a crucial role in the progression or resolution of LF.6 Danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) from injured hepatocytes activate Kupffer cells and invading macrophages to secrete several cytokines to facilitate the formation and progression of LF. These cytokines damage liver parenchymal cells and promote inflammatory cell infiltration and hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) activation.7 Macrophage-derived transforming growth factor (TGF)-β has been known as an essential cytokine in the activation of HSCs.8 Moreover, numerous studies indicate that toll-like receptor (TLR)4 and TLR9 signaling pathways facilitate the interaction between inflammatory and fibro-genic components.9,10

Hepatic macrophages have been shown to facilitate LF by promoting the survival of activated HSCs in a nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB)-dependent manner.11,12,13 The activation of the NF-κB signaling system comprises two primary pathways: the “classical” and “alternative” pathways.14 TLRs and pro-inflammatory cytokines primarily stimulate the “classical” system, whereas the “alternative” pathway is predominantly triggered by signals associated with cell differentiation, although some overlap exists. In the resting state, the inhibitor of NF-κB (IκB) associates with the p65 (RelA)/p50 NF-κB dimer to prevent the activation of NF-κB signaling pathway by covering the DNA-binding site on the Rel protein.15 The assembly of the IκB kinase (IKK) complex is crucial for the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.16 Activated IKK phosphorylates IκB, resulting in its degradation and the liberation of p65/p50 NF-κB dimers, which translocate to the nucleus to induce the expression of downstream target genes, either independently or in conjunction with other transcription factors to activate the NF-κB signaling pathway ultimately.17 The activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway stimulates macrophage polarization toward M1-type, which releases pro-inflammatory cytokines, including tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS).17 These cytokines can also stimulate the transition of HSCs into myofibroblasts to facilitate LF formation.18,19

Post-translational modifications (PTMs) have been shown to play an essential role in modulating the functions and activities of many proteins.20 As a post-translational lipid modification, palmitoylation, a reversible process where a fatty acid (often 16-carbon palmitate) is attached to a cysteine residue of the target protein through a thioester bond, has been involved in many biological and diseased conditions.21 The reversible palmitoylation process is catalyzed by the enzyme aspartate-histidine-histidine-cysteine (DHHC) palmitoyl acetyltransferase family, which contains 23 members in mammals (ZDHHC1-9,11–24).22 A recent study reported that the high-fat diet promotes liver tumorigenesis through ZDHHC17- and ZDHHC24-mediated palmitoylation of the serine/threonine kinase (AKT) to affect signaling activation.23 Another study reports that the deletion of autophagy-related protein 16-like 1 (ATG16L1) in macrophages stimulates the palmitoylation of stimulator of interferon genes (STING), hence activating downstream STING signaling, resulting in hepatic steatosis and the activation of HSCs.24

In this study, we found that ZDHHC17 was significantly downregulated in both the liver tissues and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) of patients with LF. We validated these findings in CCl4-and MCD-induced LF mouse models. In addition, decreased expression or knockout of Zdhhc17 promotes LF in mouse models. Mechanistically, we identified that ZDHHC17 stimulated IKKβ palmitoylation , which interferes Ser phosphorylation at position 177 and Ser autophosphorylation at position 181 of IKKβ to suppress the NF-κB signaling cascade, leading to diminished inflammatory response in macrophages, and less activation of HSCs. Our findings suggest that palmitoylation of IKKβ is a novel regulatory mechanism and a potential target for the chronic inflammatory environment essential for the occurrence and progression of LF.

Results

ZDHHC17 is downregulated in LF and reversely correlated with LF stages

To investigate the role of palmitoylation in the pathogenesis of LF, we first analyzed the expression of 23 human ZDHHC acyltransferases in PBMCs from patients with various stages of LF. We extracted data from GSE246221 from the GEO database. Compared with the healthy control, ZDHHC3, 7, 13, 14, 15, 21, 23, and 24 exhibited elevated expression levels with increased LF (Figure S1). However, we noticed that ZDHHC17 expression was significantly downregulated in PBMCs from LF patients and progressively decreased as LF advanced (Figure 1A).

To validate the decreased expression pattern of ZDHHC17 in patients with LF, we collected PBMCs from 16 healthy individuals and 33 patients with various stages of LF, and qPCR was used to detect ZDHHC17 expression. The patient demographics are listed in Tables S1 and S2, Subgroup analysis based on etiology revealed that the decreased expression of ZDHHC17 was consistent across the two major etiologies represented in our cohort, namely viral hepatitis and NAFLD (see Table S1 for stratified data). This suggests that the downregulation of ZDHHC17 is a common event in LF progression, independent of the initial insult being viral or metabolic. As shown in Figure 1B, the expression level of ZDHHC17 decreased as the fibrosis stage progressed from F0 to F4. We also observed that the decreased expression of ZDHHC17 was mainly in monocytes rather than in lymphocytes (Figure 1C). Macrophages are regarded as crucial regulators of LF, with monocyte-derived macrophages constituting a significant subset of liver tissue macrophages. Finally, we wanted to know which cell types in the liver contributed to the observed ZDHHC17 expression in the fibrotic liver tissues. Dual-IF staining indicated that ZDHHC17 expression was predominantly diminished in macrophages (CD68 positive) within human fibrotic livers (Figure 1D; Figures S2A and S2B). Pearson’s correlation coefficiency analysis showed that the expression of ZDHHC17 was negatively correlated with the four LF markers (collagen type IV, IV-Col; procollagen type III, PIIIP; laminin, LN; and Hyaluronic acid, HA) in the sera of patients with different stages of LF (Figures 1E–1H). These findings indicated that the downregulation of ZDHHC17 expression in monocytes and hepatic macrophages was strongly correlated with LF progression.

Zdhhc17 expression is decreased in LF mouse models, and Zdhhc17KO in mice promoted LF

Subsequently, we analyzed Zdhhc17 expression in liver tissues of LF mice reported in the GEO database. We extracted data from two studies performed in LF mouse models, CCl4-induced (GSE141821) and MCD-induced (GSE35961) (Figure S3), and our results revealed that Zdhhc17 was significantly decreased in liver tissues of LF mice compared with their non-fibrotic controls (Figures 2A and 2B). We then validated the expression of Zdhhc17 in our CCl4- and MCD diet-induced LF mouse models, developed as previously reported (Figure 2C). CCl4- and MCD diet-induced LF mice models exhibited reduced body weight (Figures S4A and S4B), increased hepatic weight (Figures S4D–S4F), and an elevated liver-to-body weight ratio (Figures 2D and 2E) relative to controls. Histological staining (HE, Masson, and sirius red) revealed severe fibrotic alterations in the liver tissue of LF animal models (Figure 2F; Figures S4C, S4G, and S4I). The data above demonstrate that both models elicited hepatic fibrotic lesions. A significant decrease of Zdhhc17 mRNA expression was observed in both liver tissues and blood from LF mice (Figures 2G and 2H).

Consistent with the above histopathological findings, quantitative analysis at the molecular level in liver tissues from the fibrosis models further revealed their inflammatory and fibrogenic signatures. As shown in Figure S5, in both the CCl4-induced and MCD diet-induced classic mouse models of LF, the mRNA and protein expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tnf, Il1b, and Il12b) in liver tissues were significantly upregulated (Figures S5A and S5B). Concurrently, the transcriptional levels of fibrogenic marker genes (Acta2, Col1a1, Tgfb1, and Timp1), which are closely associated with HSCs activation and extracellular matrix metabolism, were also markedly elevated (Figure S5C). These data confirm that both modeling methods successfully triggered a robust hepatic inflammatory response and an ongoing fibrotic process, which were concordant with the pathological alterations observed in tissue sections, including extensive inflammatory infiltration, collagen fiber hyperplasia, and deposition.

And dual-IF staining indicated that a diminished expression of Zdhhc17 was mainly occurred in macrophages (F4/80) (Figure 2I), but not in hepatocytes (Krt18) (Figure S6).

Next, we generated Zdhhc17 knockout (Zdhhc17KO) mice utilizing CRISPR-Cas9 technology to investigate the role of Zdhhc17 in LF. LF was induced in Zdhhc17WT and Zdhhc17KO mice via CCl4 injection and MCD, with the genotypic identification results of the modeled mice presented in Figure S7. Zdhhc17KO mice exhibited no significant differences in body weight (Figures S8A–S8D), appearance, or mobility compared to non-modelled and WT mice (Figures 3A–3D); however, in both LF model groups, Zdhhc17KO mice displayed duller fur during modeling, reduced activity, and slower weight growth (Figures 3A–3D; Figures S8A–S8D). Furthermore, we noted markedly enlarged fibrotic livers in Zdhhc17KO mice (Figures 3B–3E), which also exhibited an increased liver-to-body weight ratio (Figures 3C–3F). Biochemical studies of mouse sera indicated that ALT and AST levels were significantly elevated in both LF models of Zdhhc17KO mice compared to the WT groups (Figure S8B, S8C, S8E, and S8F). HE, Masson, Sirius Red, and Acta2 staining indicated that LF was much more severe in the Zdhhc17KO mice compared to the WT groups (Figures 3G and 3H; Figures S8G and S8H). The quantitative analysis of liver tissues further corroborated the exacerbating effect of Zdhhc17 deficiency on disease progression. As shown in Figure S9, in both fibrosis models, compared to Zdhhc17WT mice, the transcript levels (Figure S9A) and protein expression (Figure S9B) of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tnf, Il1b, and Il12b) were significantly elevated in the livers of Zdhhc17KO mice. Concurrently, the mRNA expression of key genes representing HSCs activation and fibrogenesis (Acta2, Col1a1, Tgfb1, and Timp1) was further induced in KO mice (Figure S9C). Collectively, these data indicated that Zdhhc17 plays an inhibition role in LF occurrence and progression in mouse livers.

ZDHHC17 deficiency stimulated pro-inflammatory cytokines production in macrophages, leading to enhanced expression of fibro-genic genes in HSCs

Previous findings25 indicate that after THP-1 cells are stimulated to differentiate into M0 macrophages with PMA, these M0 macrophages can be polarized to M1 macrophages with LPS and IFN-γ (Figure 4A). To investigate the molecular mechanism by which ZDHHC17 regulates LF’s occurrence and progression, we over-expressed or knocked down ZDHHC17 in THP-1 cells, respectively (Figures 4B and 4C; Figure S10). The mRNA levels of several cytokines, including TNF-α, CXCL10, IL-1β, IL-7, IL-12β, and TGF-β, were significantly decreased in macrophages (LPS+/IFN-γ+) following ZDHHC17 over-expression (Figure 4D; Figure S11A). Conversely, the knockdown of ZDHHC17 increased the expression of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-12β in macrophages (LPS+/IFN-γ+) at the mRNA level (Figure 4E; Figure S11B). Furthermore, we assessed the protein levels of TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-12β in the culture supernatant of macrophages (LPS+/IFN-γ+) following ZDHHC17 overexpression (Figure 4F) or silence (Figure 4G) by ELISA, and the results were consistent with their mRNA expression levels.

To comprehensively characterize the inflammatory secretome of macrophages regulated by ZDHHC17, we employed multiplex ELISA technology to simultaneously analyze the levels of various cytokines in the supernatant of THP-1-derived macrophages. Consistent with our previous findings at the mRNA and specific protein levels, overexpression of ZDHHC17 significantly suppressed the secretion of a series of pro-inflammatory mediators induced by TNF-α. The suppressed factors included not only the previously confirmed TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-12p40, but also other key players in inflammation and fibrogenesis, such as: IL-6 (a core mediator of the acute-phase response and immunomodulation), IL-8 (CXCL8) (a potent neutrophil chemoattractant), and CCL3 (a chemokine involved in the recruitment and activation of critical inflammatory cells like monocytes/macrophages). Conversely, knockdown of ZDHHC17 further enhanced the production of these cytokines (Figure S12). Notably, the levels of factors predominantly derived from lymphocytes, such as IL-2, IL-4, IL-5, IL-17 A, and IFN-γ, as well as CCL4 and the soluble receptor IL-2RA, did not show significant changes in this experimental system. This indirectly supports the notion that the observed changes in the secretion profile primarily originate from alterations in macrophage-intrinsic function and suggests that the regulatory effect of ZDHHC17 may possess a certain degree of selectivity. The baseline secretion level of GM-CSF was also not significantly affected, implying that ZDHHC17 has limited regulatory influence on factors related to macrophage homeostasis. Furthermore, the secretion level of the classic anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 did not exhibit significant alteration, hinting that ZDHHC17 exerts its modulatory role in this model mainly by suppressing the pro-inflammatory network rather than by enhancing anti-inflammatory circuits. This comprehensive secretome analysis indicates that ZDHHC17 is a key negative regulatory hub in macrophage inflammatory responses. Its functional deficiency specifically amplifies the output of a set of effector molecules known to drive fibrosis (such as TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6, and chemokines), thereby potentially providing crucial microenvironmental signals for the aberrant activation of HSCs.

The enzyme activity center of the ZDHHC family comprises the DHHC structural domains, with Cys 467 serving as the essential amino acid.26,27 We, therefore, constructed an enzyme inactive mutant plasmid ZDHHC17 C467S to abolish the palmitoyltransferase enzymatic activity. We found that transfection of ZDHHC17 C467S mutant plasmid in macrophages (LPS+/IFN-γ+) did not affect the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, CXCL-10, and IL-12 compared to the ZDHHC17 WT (Figure S13), which indicated the suppressed inflammation process of ZDHHC17 in macrophages is enzyme activity-dependent.

To investigate whether the suppressed pro-inflammatory cytokines secretion following over-expression of ZDHHC17 in macrophages will affect the fibro-genic gene expression in HSCs, we performed transwell analysis by macrophage/LX-2 co-culture system in which LX-2 cells were seeded at the bottom and macrophages in the upper chamber (Figure 4A). As shown in Figure 4H, the expression of pro-fibro-genic genes, including αSMA, COL1a1, TGF-β, and TIMP-1, was significantly decreased in LX-2 cells co-cultured with ZDHHC17-overexpressed macrophages (LPS+/IFN-γ+). Conversely, the knockdown of ZDHHC17 in macrophages (LPS+/IFN-γ+) stimulated the expression of all these pro-fibrogenic genes in LX-2 (Figure 4I). Collectively, these results indicated that altered expression levels of ZDHHC17 affected inflammatory cytokine production in macrophages which in turn influences fibro-genic gene expression in HSCs. These activities were dependent on the enzyme activity of ZDHHC17.

ZDHHC17 interacts with IKKβ to inhibit NF-κB signaling, which is dependent on its palmitoyl acetyltransferase activity

To further clarify the molecular mechanism through which ZDHHC17 suppresses the inflammatory response of macrophages, we then identified the interacted proteins of ZDHHC17 by co-IP. Following over-expression of exogenous ZDHHC17 by plasmid transfection into THP-1 cells for 48 h, we collected the cells for IP using anti-ZDHHC17 antibody-coupled magnetic beads, and mass spectrometry was employed to identify those interacting proteins. As shown in Figure 5 A, we sorted out the top 15 proteins interacting with ZDHHC17, among which IKKβ, a crucial kinase in activating the NF-κB signaling pathway, was likely implicated in the aforementioned inflammatory response (Figure 5B). In order to validate the interaction between ZDHHC17 and IKKβ, we employed co-IP and simulated molecular docking techniques (Figures 5C and 5D). The GalaxyWEB platform (https://galaxy.seoklab.org/c) was utilized to predict the potential interaction between IKKβ and ZDHHC17. The results indicated that the two molecules exhibit a strong binding affinity under optimal docking conditions, forming intramolecular hydrogen bonds at Lys590 and Tyr512 of ZDHHC17 and His33 and Gly184 of IKKβ, respectively (Figure 5C). We confirmed that ZDHHC17 did interact with IKKβ by co-IP (Figure 5D).

We further investigated the biological implications of the interaction between ZDHHC17 and IKKβ. IKKβ phosphorylation is indispensable for activating the NF-κB signaling pathway because it facilitates the entry of p65 into the nucleus through the phosphorylation of IκB. Over-expression of ZDHHC17 in THP-1 cells markedly reduced TNF-α-induced phosphorylation of IKKβ (Figure 5E) and inhibited both the phosphorylation of IκB and the nuclear entry of p65 into the nuclei (Figure 5F). Conversely, the knockdown of ZDHHC17 led to increased phosphorylation of IKKβ and IκB and enhanced nuclear translocation of p65 (Figures 5G and 5H). Interestingly, the suppressed NF-κB signaling by ZDHHC17 overexpression was notably abolished by palmitoylation inhibitor 2-BP (Figures 5E and 5F or transfection of catalytically inactive ZDHHC17 (C467S) (Figure S14). These findings indicate that ZDHHC17 interacts with IKKβ to inhibit the activation of NF-κB signaling, which depends on palmitoyl acetyltransferase activity.

Identification of IKKβ palmitoylation sites

Next, we investigated whether ZDHHC17 inhibits NF-κB signaling by regulating IKKβ palmitoylation. Using the acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) method (Figure 6A), we observed that endogenous IKKβ underwent palmitoyl modification, which was significantly inhibited by palmitoylation inhibitor 2-BP (Figure 6B) or ZDHHC17 silencing (Figure 6C), while over-expression of ZDHHC17 further enhanced IKKβ palmitoylation (Figure 6D) in THP-1. To elucidate the palmitoyl modification sites of IKKβ, we employed CSS-palm 4.0, a prediction tool for protein sequence analysis (Figure 6E). 7 Cys sites (98, 114, 115, 299, 412, 524, and 662) potential for palmitoyl modification were predicted on IKKβ, and 4 of them (98, 299, 412, and 524) were also identified by mass spectrometry (Figure 6F; Figures S15A–S15C). We constructed 4 mutant plasmids to eliminate false positives and confirmed that IKKβ underwent palmitoylation at cysteine residue 299 (Figure 6G). We also co-transfected IKKβ WT or C299S mutant together with ZDHHC17 plasmid into THP-1 cells to examine the effects of IKKβ palmitoylation on the TNF-α induced activation of NF-κB signaling. As illustrated in Figure 6H, the over-expression of ZDHHC17 inhibited the phosphorylation of wild-type IKKβ, but the inhibition was attenuated in the C299S mutant transfected cells. The results collectively indicated that ZDHHC17 interacts with IKKβ and promotes IKKβ palmitoylation at Cys299, inhibiting IKKβ phosphorylation and NF-κB signaling.

Similarly, to assess the palmitoylation status of IKKβ in hepatic tissues from mouse models of LF, we performed ABE assays. The results demonstrated a significant reduction in Ikkb palmitoylation in both the MCD diet-induced and CCl4-induced fibrosis models compared to controls (Figures S16A and S16B). Furthermore, genetic ablation of Zdhhc17 resulted in a complete loss of detectable Ikkb palmitoylation in murine liver tissues (Figures S16A and S16B).

Additionally, we examined the impact of IKKβ C299S on the inflammatory response in macrophages and its influence on the profibrotic gene expression in HSCs utilizing the previously described transwell assay (Figures 6I–6K). Our results indicated that co-transfection of IKKβ and ZDHHC17 suppressed the inflammatory response in macrophages and inhibited the activation of HSCs. However, the IKKβ C299S mutant partially abolished this effect.

IKKβ palmitoylation reduces its binding ability to TAK1, thereby inhibiting NF-κB signaling

Palmitoylation modifications typically enhance protein stability; however, we found that palmitoylation modifications did not significantly influence IKKβ protein levels (Figures S17A–S17C). Normally, in the quiescent condition, IKKβ forms a stable trimer with IKKα and IKKγ, and during activation, IKKβ dissociates from the trimer and is phosphorylated.28 Consequently, we employed native PAGE to confirm whether palmitoylated IKKβ affected the dissociation of the trimer. We found that regulation of IKKβ palmitoylation through either 2-BP inhibition or ZDHHC17 overexpression did not influence the molecular weight of IKKβ in its native form, which suggests that the palmitoylation of IKKβ did not impact the dissociation of the trimer (Figure S17D).

It has been reported that TGF-beta-activated kinase 1 (TAK1) phosphorylates Ser177 of IKKβ, which then triggers the autophosphorylation of Ser181 of IKKβ.28 This is followed by the phosphorylation of Ser32 and Ser36 of IκB by the activated IKKβ complex, leading to the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway.28 To confirm whether IKKβ palmitoylation affected NF-κB pathway by TAK1, we performed the molecular docking and kinetic simulation using IKKβ (4e3c) and TAK1 (2eva) protein structures obtained from the protein databank (PDB). The radius of gyration (Rg) displayed slight variations following the insertion of palmitic acid at position 299 of IKKβ (Figure 7A). The root means square deviation (RMSD) fluctuations diminished and ultimately stabilized as the simulation advanced (Figure 7B). The RMSD and Rg value data demonstrate that the system swiftly attained equilibrium throughout the 100ns simulation, resulting in stability and convergence. The root-mean-square fluctuation (RMSF) reflects the flexibility of the amino acid site, demonstrating that the residue fluctuations following palmitic acid insertion were markedly reduced compared to the uninserted group (Figure 7C). IKKβ and TAK1 can form hydrogen bonds, and the introduction of palmitic acid resulted in a reduction of hydrogen bonds between IKKβ and TAK1 (Figure 7D).

The binding energy decomposition results for IKKβ and TAK1 indicated that the total gas phase free energy (GGAS), total solvation free energy (GSOLV), and total free energy (TOTAL) decreased following the palmitoylation of IKKβ (Figures 7E–7G), implying that the incorporation of palmitic acid obstructs the interaction between the two proteins. Ultimately, we simulated the docking of the two proteins and observed a substantial reduction in the number of hydrogen bonds generated between palmitoylated IKKβ and TAK1, consistent with the simulation results (Figures 7H and 7I; Figures S18 and S19).

Based on the aforementioned simulation results, we performed co-IP assays to examine the molecular interaction between IKKβ and TAK1. Upon overexpression of ZDHHC17, a marked reduction in the amount of TAK1 co-precipitated with IKKβ was observed (Figure S20A), suggesting that enhanced palmitoylation of IKKβ may interfere with its binding to TAK1. Conversely, in cells expressing the palmitoylation-deficient IKKβ mutant (IKKβ C299S), the abundance of TAK1 in immunoprecipitated complexes was significantly increased compared to the wild-type IKKβ group (Figure S20B). These results further support the conclusion that palmitoylation of IKKβ plays a critical role in regulating its interaction with TAK1.

ZDHHC17 regulates M1 polarization and inflammatory response in primary macrophages via enzymatic palmitoylation of IKKβ

To directly validate our findings in a physiologically relevant system, we isolated primary bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) from Zdhhc17WT and Zdhhc17KO mice for in-depth analysis. qPCR analysis confirmed that the matured BMDMs highly expressed the resting (M0) macrophage markers Adgre1 and Itgam (Figure 8A). Upon stimulation, M0 macrophages exhibited robust polarization potential: they showed a stronger propensity for M1 polarization upon LPS and IFN-γ stimulation, as indicated by significantly higher expression levels of M1 marker genes (Nos2, Tnf, and Il1b) compared to unstimulated M0 BMDMs (Figure 8B). Similarly, IL-4 stimulation induced a pronounced M2 polarization, evidenced by significantly elevated expression of M2 marker genes (Arg1 and Ym1) (Figure 8C). These results confirm that our isolated BMDMs possess complete macrophage differentiation plasticity.

At the molecular level, Zdhhc17KO BMDMs exhibited a significant reduction in Ikkb palmitoylation (Figure 8D), an enhanced interaction with the upstream kinase Tak1 (Figure 8E), a more vigorous activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway (indicated by Ikkb phosphorylation) in response to TNF-α stimulation (Figure 8F), and a substantial increase in the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines (Tnf, Il1b, and Il12b) (Figure 8G). Most crucially, rescue experiments in Zdhhc17KO BMDMs demonstrated that reintroduction of Zdhhc17WT effectively restored Ikkb palmitoylation (Figure 8H), attenuated the Ikkb-Tak1 interaction (Figure 8I), and suppressed the hyperactivation of the NF-κB pathway (Figure 8J) along with the overproduction of downstream inflammatory cytokines (Figure 8K). In contrast, the catalytically inactive mutant Zdhhc17C467S completely failed to recapitulate these rescuing effects (Figures 8H–8K). Collectively, these data obtained from primary macrophages provide direct evidence that ZDHHC17, via its palmitoyltransferase activity, catalyzes IKKβ palmitoylation. This modification subsequently weakens the IKKβ-TAK1 interaction, inhibits the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, and ultimately modulates M1 polarization and the inflammatory response in macrophages. This finding validates our mechanistic insights from cell lines in a more physiologically relevant primary cell model, establishing the central role of the ZDHHC17-IKKβ palmitoylation axis in regulating macrophage inflammatory function.

Discussion

ZDHHC-mediated palmitoylation has been reported to be involved in many liver diseases, such as steatosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). Although LF is a reversible process, incomplete understanding of the pathogenesis and lack of specific drug targets makes the treatment of LF difficult. Our current study therefore aimed to delineate the specific role of ZDHHC17-mediated palmitoylation in the inflammatory response and LF by combining clinical data analysis with Zdhhc17-deficient mouse models and mechanistic studies in primary cells. Our results demonstrate that ZDHHC17-mediated palmitoylation of the IKKβ at Cys299 site interferes with TAK1-mediated Ser177 phosphorylation and Ser181 autophosphorylation of IKKβ, leading to suppressed NF-κB signaling cascade and diminished inflammatory response in macrophages, as well as less activation of HSCs. Our findings suggest that palmitoylation of IKKβ is a novel negative regulatory mechanism for NF-κB signaling, and this PTM may shed light on the pathogenesis of LF, providing potential drug targets for LF.

Chronic, recurrent, and uncontrolled inflammation significantly contributes to altering the self-limiting tissue repair process, leading to a detrimental cycle that accelerates LF progression.29,30,31 Macrophages are recognized as crucial regulators of hepatic inflammation, influencing the progression or regression of LF.32,33,34 However, the role of palmitoylation modification in the intricate inflammatory response to LF is not yet understood. Previous study indicated that palmitoylation of macrophage CD36 modulated glucose-fatty acid metabolism, mitigated fatty degeneration of hepatocytes and HSCs activation, blocking the progression of LF.35 Our study now reveals that palmitoylation of macrophage IKKβ suppresses its inflammatory responses and the subsequent activation of HSCs. IKKβ is a key kinase in the NF-κB signaling pathway and is crucial in its activation. Prior research indicates that IKKβ activity is predominantly modulated by PTMs, such as phosphorylation, acetylation, and nitrosylation.36,37,38 Our study identifies that palmitoylation of IKKβ at the Cys299 site inhibits the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway. This palmitoylation-dependent regulation is fundamentally distinct from pharmacological inhibition by small molecules (e.g., licochalcone A) that covalently bind to other cysteines (e.g., Cys179).39 Importantly, as an endogenous and reversible PTM, it represents a physiological regulatory modality with potential for signal specificity. In our study, the introduction of palmitic acid at the IKKβ Cys299 site resulted in a marked reduction in docking affinity with TAK1 and a decrease in intermolecular hydrogen bonds. Collectively, our computational and biochemical data support a model wherein palmitoylation at Cys299 imposes steric hindrance, physically impeding the productive interaction between IKKβ and TAK1 required for phosphorylation.

A key breakthrough of this study is the replication and validation of the above mechanism in BMDMs, a model closer to the physiological state. Zdhhc17-deficient BMDMs exhibited loss of Ikkb palmitoylation, enhanced Ikkb-Tak1 interaction, hyperactivation of the NF-κB pathway, and excessive secretion of inflammatory cytokines. Most crucially, gain- and loss-of-function rescue experiments in Zdhhc17KO BMDMs provided definitive causal evidence: reintroduction of Zdhhc17WT, but not the catalytically inactive mutant (Zdhhc17C467S), restored normal Ikkb palmitoylation and suppressed the hyperinflammatory phenotype. This definitively proves that ZDHHC17’s regulation of the macrophage inflammatory response is strictly dependent on its palmitoyltransferase activity, thereby closing the mechanistic loop of the “ZDHHC17-IKKβ palmitoylation” axis in a physiologically relevant cell type and enhancing the credibility and physiological significance of our findings.

Based on these findings, we propose a reinforced model for LF progression: chronic liver injury leads to sustained inflammatory signaling, which may directly or indirectly cause downregulation of ZDHHC17 expression in macrophages (the precise mechanism awaits elucidation). Reduced ZDHHC17 leads to insufficient palmitoylation of IKKβ at Cys299, which in turn attenuates the inhibition of IKKβ-TAK1 interaction, resulting in overactivation of the NF-κB pathway. This exacerbates M1 polarization of macrophages and the pro-inflammatory cytokine storm, directly damaging hepatocytes and vigorously activating HSCs, leading to excessive extracellular matrix deposition. The persistent fibrotic microenvironment further aggravates inflammation, forming a vicious cycle with ZDHHC17 downregulation as an amplifier. Importantly, our etiology-based subgroup analysis demonstrated that ZDHHC17 downregulation occurred consistently in patients with fibrosis caused by both viral hepatitis and NAFLD. This cross-etiology consistency strongly suggests that the disruption of the ZDHHC17-IKKβ palmitoylation axis represents a common regulatory node where diverse injurious stimuli (e.g., viral antigens and metabolic stress) converge to exacerbate hepatic inflammation, rather than being an epiphenomenon specific to a single disease cause. This significantly enhances the potential broad-spectrum therapeutic relevance of targeting this axis. Therefore, the downregulation of ZDHHC17 is not merely a biomarker of disease activity but a core pathological link that drives the continuous amplification of the inflammatory network in fibrosis.

The NF-κB signaling pathway, specifically IKKβ, has emerged as a significant therapeutic target for inflammatory and metabolic diseases.40 The targeted knockdown of IKKβ in macrophages markedly decreases tumor size by effectively suppressing the expression of pro-inflammatory factors.41,42 The advancement of IKKβ inhibitors is regarded as a promising therapeutic strategy in the treatment of cancer, central nervous system disorders, and metabolic diseases.43,44,45 In this work, the ZDHHC17-mediated palmitoylation circuit acts as an endogenous “IKKβ inhibitor” module. Strategies to reinforce this module present a promising alternative. First, restoring or enhancing ZDHHC17 function specifically in macrophages (e.g., by developing transcriptional agonists or targeted nanocarrier delivery systems) could specifically “brake” local hepatic inflammation without causing systemic immunosuppression. Second, based on the structural information of the Cys299 site, designing small-molecule “palmitoylation-mimetic” compounds or stabilized peptides that simulate the steric hindrance effect could specifically disrupt the IKKβ-TAK1 interaction, enabling more precise modulation of IKKβ activity. Because the exact site and mechanism are elucidated, such strategies to enhance or mimic IKKβ palmitoylation could achieve a high degree of cellular and target specificity, potentially overcoming the broad toxicity associated with direct ATP-competitive IKKβ inhibitors. Similar studies have been reported; for instance, PCSK9 has been shown to induce lysosomal-mediated degradation of PTEN, leading to AKT activation following palmitoylation at the Cys600 locus. This process subsequently promotes the proliferation of hepatocellular carcinoma and contributes to sorafenib resistance. The authors created a biologically active peptide derived from PCSK9 that competitively inhibits the palmitoylation of PCSK9, and this peptide reduces AKT phosphorylation, and improves the antitumor efficacy of sorafenib in patients with HCC.46 The modulation of IKKβ palmitoylation through a comparable mechanism may represent a novel approach to macrophage inflammatory modulation. Given that the site and mechanism of action are well-defined, typically in a competitive manner, this approach is more specific than existing IKKβ inhibitor compounds. The finding that the ZDHHC17-IKKβ axis plays a key role in both CCl4- and MCD-induced fibrosis models suggests it may be a common core inflammatory regulatory module across different etiologies, implying that therapeutic strategies targeting this module could have broad-spectrum potential. Thus, the ZDHHC17-IKKβ palmitoylation axis emerges not only as a critical disease mechanism but also as a promising and druggable checkpoint for breaking the inflammatory cycle in LF.

Limitations of the study

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of certain limitations. First, the relatively small cohort size, particularly for patients with early-stage fibrosis, restricted our ability to balance the sex distribution during enrollment. Consequently, we could not perform a stratified analysis to definitively assess the influence of sex on the observed associations, which may affect the generalizability of our conclusions. Second, the precise upstream signals responsible for the downregulation of ZDHHC17 expression in macrophages during fibrogenesis remain undefined, representing a key area for future investigation. Third, while validated in primary cells, the complex multicellular crosstalk within the fibrotic liver microenvironment in vivo may involve additional regulatory layers. Finally, the therapeutic strategies proposed are speculative and require rigorous validation in preclinical models.

Method details

Cell lines and Culture Conditions

The human monocytic leukemia cell line THP-1 (female) and the human hepatic stellate cell line LX-2 (male) were used in this study. Both cell lines, which are wild-type for ZDHHC17, were obtained from Shanghai Shuo Cheng Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Catalog #: SC6481 for THP-1, SC6663 for LX-2). THP-1 cells were maintained in RPMI 1640 medium (Hyclone, SH30027.02), while LX-2 cells were cultured in DMEM (Hyclone, SH30285.02). Both media were supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, 26010074) and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin (Sigma, V900929). All cells were routinely cultured at 37°C in a humidified incubator with 5% CO2. Regular morphological observation was performed to monitor cell status. Furthermore, all cell lines were confirmed to be free of mycoplasma contamination.

All cell culture work was performed under sterile conditions. For cell revival, frozen vials were retrieved from liquid nitrogen and rapidly thawed in a 37°C water bath. The cell suspension was then transferred to a centrifuge tube containing 4 mL of the respective complete medium and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 5 min. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellet was resuspended in 5 mL of fresh, pre-warmed complete medium and seeded into a T25 culture flask. Following overnight incubation, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium. Cells were passaged when they reached approximately 90% confluence. For the adherent LX-2 cell line, the old medium was aspirated, and the monolayer was rinsed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) before being briefly treated with a small volume of trypsin for about 2 min at 37°C. Cell detachment was monitored under a microscope. Once most cells had rounded up and detached, the enzymatic reaction was stopped by adding complete medium containing serum. The cells were then gently pipetted to generate a single-cell suspension, centrifuged, resuspended, and subcultured at a 1:3 split ratio. The suspension THP-1 cell line was subcultured by direct dilution with fresh medium. Prior to experiments, cell concentration and viability were assessed using the Trypan Blue exclusion assay. Briefly, 10 μL of a well-mixed cell suspension was mixed with an equal volume of 0.4% Trypan Blue solution. A 10 μL aliquot of the mixture was loaded into a hemocytometer. The number of viable (unstained) cells in the four large corner squares was counted under a light microscope. The cell concentration (cells/mL) was calculated using the formula: (Total viable cell count in 4 squares / 4) × 104 × 2 (dilution factor). Only cell preparations with a viability exceeding 95% were used for subsequent experiments.

Cell transfection

Gene expression was modulated in vitro using siRNA-mediated knockdown or plasmid-mediated overexpression. Transfections were performed on human THP-1 and BMDMs under optimal confluency conditions to ensure high efficiency and cell viability.

siRNA-mediated knockdown of ZDHHC17 was achieved using lipofectamine. For transient gene silencing, cells were seeded and allowed to reach approximately 60% confluence. The transfection complex was prepared by diluting 5 μL of a 20 μM stock of ZDHHC17-targeting siRNA (designed and synthesized by Sangon Biotech) or a non-targeting control siRNA in 150 μL of Opti-MEM™ I Reduced Serum Medium. In a separate tube, 4 μL of Lipofectamine™ RNAiMAX was diluted in 150 μL of Opti-MEM™. The diluted siRNA was then combined with the diluted transfection reagent, mixed gently, and incubated at room temperature for 10 min to allow complex formation. Meanwhile, the growth medium was aspirated from the cells, and the monolayer was washed gently with sterile PBS. Following the incubation, 1 mL of serum-free medium was added to the cells, followed by the dropwise addition of the entire siRNA-lipid complex mixture. The cells were incubated with the complex for 12-15 hours at 37°C in a 5% CO2 incubator, after which the transfection medium was replaced with fresh complete growth medium. Cells were typically harvested for protein or RNA extraction 48 to 72 hours post-transfection for knockdown validation and subsequent functional assays.

Plasmid-mediated overexpression was performed using polyethylenimine. For ectopic gene expression, cells were transfected when they reached about 85% confluence. The transfection complex was prepared by diluting 2 μg of plasmid DNA in 200 μL of Opti-MEM™. In a separate tube, 4 μL of linear polyethylenimine (PEI, MW 25,000, 1 μg/μL) was diluted in 200 μL of Opti-MEM™. The diluted PEI was then added to the diluted DNA, mixed immediately by gentle vortexing, and incubated at room temperature for 15 min. During incubation, the growth medium was removed from the cells, and the monolayer was washed with PBS. Subsequently, 1 mL of serum-free medium was added to the cells. The DNA-PEI complexes were added dropwise, and the culture vessel was gently swirled. Cells were incubated with the complexes for 8-12 hours before the medium was replaced with complete growth medium. Protein or RNA was extracted 48 to 72 hours post-transfection for validation of overexpression and downstream experiments.

Identification of differentially-expressed genes (DEGs) in LF using publicly accessible datasets for bioinformatics analysis

Bioinformatics analyses were conducted using the R environment version 4.0.2. Publicly accessible datasets were retrieved from Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO). Three datasets quantifying gene expression in the liver and peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) by Affymetrix™ expression microarrays were selected. In dataset GSE141821, mice were subjected to 8-week treatment with either CCL4 (LF mice, n=9) or olive oil (control, n=8). In dataset GSE35961, mice were administered with a diet lacking in high-fat, methionine and choline (MCD) for a duration of 8 weeks (n=4 per group). Dataset GSE246221 included PBMCs from Nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) patients with different LF stages. These datasets were used to identify differentially-expressed genes between control and LF in mouse and human. R package Affy was used to filter the data, and genes with differential expression levels were detected using the R package limma, with a log2-fold change greater than 2 cutoff and an adjusted p-value less than 0.01. Data were normalized and translated into logarithmic form using the Normalise Data function.

PBMCs and liver tissues from human donors

This study utilized human peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and liver tissue samples. The study protocol was conducted in strict accordance with the Ethical Principles for Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (Declaration of Helsinki) and was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third People's Hospital of Mianyang City, Sichuan Province, China (Approval No.2023-135). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to sample collection. All patients were staged from F0 to F4 according to the METAVIR scoring system.

A cohort of 49 participants was enrolled, comprising 33 patients with LF at various stages (F0-F4) and 16 healthy controls. Fresh peripheral blood samples (10 mL) were collected from all subjects. PBMCs were isolated on the same day of blood draw using a human PBMCs separation kit (Beyotime, C0025) strictly following the manufacturer's protocol. The final PBMC cohort consisted of samples from 16 healthy volunteers and 33 patients with chronic liver disease, stratified by fibrosis stage.

Liver tissue samples were obtained from two distinct patient groups: (1) The Liver Fibrosis Group: Tissues were collected from patients diagnosed with LF who underwent either partial hepatectomy or diagnostic liver biopsy (n=3). (2) The Control Group: Control liver tissues were obtained from patients undergoing liver resection for other indications, specifically cholangiocarcinoma, hepatic hemangioma, or hepatic cysts (n=3). Key exclusion criteria for the control group included a prior medical history of diabetes mellitus, chronic alcoholism, or viral hepatitis (B or C), ensuring the absence of underlying metabolic, toxic, or viral liver disease etiologies. Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics (e.g., age, sex, fibrosis stage, relevant serological markers) for all tissue and PBMC donors are summarized in Table S1. The mean age of the patient group was 52.4 years, and 60.9% were male. The potential influence of sex on the findings is addressed in the discussion section.

Immediately following resection or biopsy, portions of fresh liver tissue were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin at room temperature for 24-48 hours. The fixed tissues were then processed through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin blocks. Serial sections of 4-5 μm thickness were cut from the blocks for staining. Standard histopathological staining was performed to evaluate liver architecture and the degree of fibrosis: Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining was used for general morphological assessment; Masson's trichrome stain and Sirius Red stain were specifically used to visualize collagen deposition, a key marker of fibrosis. All stained sections were examined by an experienced pathologist who was blinded to the clinical and experimental groupings.

Isolation of lymphocytes and monocytes from PBMCs by adherence

Monocytes and lymphocytes were fractionated from PBMCs based on their differential adherence properties. Following the initial isolation of PBMCs by density gradient centrifugation, the cell pellet was resuspended in complete RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The cell concentration was determined, and the suspension was adjusted to a density of approximately 5 × 106 cells/mL. An appropriate volume of the cell suspension was then seeded into tissue culture plates or flasks at a density of 1–2 × 106 cells/cm2. The cells were incubated at 37°C in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 for 1 to 2 hours. During this period, monocytes actively adhere to the culture surface, while lymphocytes remain in suspension.

Non-adherent lymphocytes were collected and processed. After the adherence period, the culture vessel was gently rocked. The medium containing the non-adherent cell population (enriched for lymphocytes) was carefully aspirated and transferred to a centrifuge tube. To maximize the recovery of non-adherent cells, the adherent monolayer was rinsed gently with pre-warmed PBS or serum-free medium, and the wash was pooled with the initial supernatant. The pooled cell suspension was centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min at 4°C. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellet was washed once with ice-cold PBS. Finally, the lymphocyte-enriched pellet was resuspended in an appropriate volume of complete medium or assay buffer for subsequent use.

Adherent monocytes were recovered. After removal of the non-adherent fraction, the adherent cell layer (enriched for monocytes) was washed 1-2 times with pre-warmed PBS to remove any residual non-adherent cells. To recover the adherent monocytes, a pre-warmed cell dissociation solution (e.g., trypsin-EDTA or a non-enzymatic dissociation reagent) was added to the culture vessel. The vessel was returned to the incubator at 37°C for several minutes. Detachment of the cells was monitored under a microscope. Once the majority of cells had rounded up and detached, the reaction was stopped by adding complete medium containing serum. The cell suspension was gently pipetted, transferred to a centrifuge tube, and centrifuged at 300 × g for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the monocyte-enriched pellet was resuspended in complete medium for downstream applications.

Generation of ZDHHC17 knock-out mice

Ethical Approval, Housing, and Experimental Animals: All animal experimental protocols and procedures were reviewed and approved by the Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of the Sichuan Industrial Institute of Antibiotics, China National Pharmaceutical Group Corporation (Approval No.SIIA20240604), and were conducted in strict accordance with its institutional guidelines and regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. Mice were housed under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions with a controlled 12-hour light/dark cycle, ambient temperature of 22 ± 2°C, and humidity of 50 ± 10%. Standard rodent chow and water were provided ad libitum. For all subsequent experimental modeling, 4- to 6-week-old male mice were used.

Generation and Genotyping of Zdhhc17Knockout Mice: Global Zdhhc17-deficient (Zdhhc17KO) mice on a C57BL/6N genetic background were generated via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated genome editing by Cyagen Biosciences Inc. (Suzhou, China). Single-guide RNA (sgRNA) was designed to target an early exon of the murine Zdhhc17 gene. A mixture of Cas9 mRNA and the sgRNA was microinjected into fertilized oocytes from C57BL/6N mice. Founder animals carrying targeted mutations were identified by genotyping. The genomic region flanking the target site was amplified by PCR from tail DNA using the primers: forward, 5’-GAGAGAGAGAACTCAGCCCGATGTCAG-3’; reverse, 5’-AGATGAAATGAAACACACCGCCTAAC-3’. Successful generation of frameshift mutations leading to a loss-of-function allele was confirmed by Sanger sequencing of the PCR products. The knockout allele was subsequently backcrossed for a minimum of two generations onto the C57BL/6N background to establish a stable colony. For experiments, Zdhhc17KOmice and their wild-type (Zdhhc17WT) littermate controls were used.

Experimental Design Principles: Animal assignment to experimental groups (e.g., treatment vs. control) was performed randomly. Furthermore, the investigator responsible for data collection and outcome assessment (such as histological scoring) was blinded to the genotype and treatment group of the animals throughout the analysis to minimize bias.

Isolation of bone marrow-derived macrophages from Zdhhc17WT and Zdhhc17KO mice

All procedures involving mice were performed in accordance with the approved institutional animal care protocols. Bone marrow cells were harvested from 8- to 10-week-old male Zdhhc17WT and Zdhhc17KO mice, both on a congenic C57BL/6N genetic background. Following euthanasia, femurs and tibiae were aseptically dissected from each mouse, cleaned of adherent tissue, and the ends were cut. Marrow was flushed from the bones using cold, sterile PBS and passed through a 70-μm cell strainer to obtain a single-cell suspension. After centrifugation at 300 × g for 5 min at 4°C, erythrocytes in the cell pellet were lysed using 3 mL of ACK lysis buffer for 5 min on ice. The lysis reaction was stopped by adding 10 mL of cold PBS, and the cells were centrifuged again.

The purified bone marrow progenitor cells from each genotype were processed independently. Cells were resuspended in complete BMDM differentiation medium, consisting of DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and 30 ng/mL recombinant murine macrophage colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, MCE, HY-P70263A). Cells were counted, plated in non-tissue culture-treated dishes at a density of 1 × 106 cells per 10-cm dish, and incubated at 37°C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator. On day 3 of culture, an additional 3 mL of fresh differentiation medium containing M-CSF was gently added. On day 7, the medium and non-adherent cells were removed. The adherent, fully differentiated BMDMs were washed with PBS and either detached using a cell dissociation buffer for subsequent experiments or lysed directly for analysis. BMDMs from both genotypes were characterized by their typical macrophage morphology and used for comparative polarization and signaling studies.

Construction of mouse models of LF

Model 1: Carbon Tetrachloride (CCl4)-Induced LF: LF was induced by chronic CCl4 administration. Mice received intraperitoneal injections of CCl4, which was dissolved in olive oil at a 20% (v/v) concentration, at a dose of 2 mL/kg body weight. Injections were administered twice per week for a total duration of 6 weeks. Control mice received intraperitoneal injections of an equivalent volume of olive oil vehicle on the same schedule.

Model 2: Methionine- and Choline-Deficient (MCD) Diet-Induced LF: LF was also induced via a methionine- and choline-deficient diet. Mice in the fibrosis group were fed an MCD diet (A02082002B, Ready Dietech). The dietary regimen included a 1-week acclimatization period to a standard control diet (MCS, A02082003B, Ready Dietech), followed by a 1-week transition to a 1:1 mixture of MCD and MCS diets. Subsequently, mice were maintained on the full MCD diet for 8 weeks to establish fibrosis. Control mice were fed the MCS diet for the entire equivalent period.

Tissue processing, histopathological analysis, and serum biochemistry

Liver tissue was processed for histological assessment of injury and fibrosis. Following euthanasia, mouse liver tissues were immediately harvested. A portion of each liver lobe was fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24-48 hours at 4°C. The fixed tissues were then dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Serial sections of 4-5 μm thickness were cut using a microtome (Leica) for subsequent staining.

Histological staining was performed to evaluate hepatic steatosis, inflammation, and collagen deposition. Paraffin-embedded liver sections were subjected to three standard staining protocols. H&E staining was used to assess general histoarchitecture, hepatocyte injury, lipid droplet accumulation (steatosis), and inflammatory cell infiltration. Masson’s Trichrome stain and Sirius Red stain were employed specifically to visualize and quantify fibrillar collagen deposition, the hallmark of fibrosis. All staining procedures were performed according to standardized protocols.

Stained sections were evaluated in a blinded manner by experienced histologists. Digital images of the stained sections were captured using a light microscope (Leica, Germany) equipped with a digital camera. For objective assessment, all histological evaluations (including scoring of steatosis, inflammation, and fibrosis) were performed independently by three certified histologists at Chengdu Aotron Biotechnology Co. Ltd., who were blinded to the experimental group assignments of the samples. The final scores for each parameter were derived from the consensus or average of the independent evaluations. At the experimental endpoint, blood was collected from mice via retro-orbital bleeding or cardiac puncture under anesthesia. Blood samples were allowed to clot at room temperature for 30 min and then centrifuged at 800 × g for 30 min at 4°C to separate serum. The collected serum was aliquoted and stored at -80°C until analysis. Serum levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT) and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), as biomarkers of hepatocellular injury, were quantified using standard enzymatic assay kits according to the manufacturer's instructions. All biochemical analyses were performed at Chengdu Aotron Biotechnology Co. Ltd.

THP-1 polarization and THP-1-HSCs crosstalk

THP-1 monocyte differentiation and M1 macrophage polarization were sequentially performed. To generate resting (M0) macrophages, human THP-1 monocytes were seeded at a density of 3.5 × 105 cells/mL and differentiated by stimulation with 100 ng/mL phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) for 48 hours. Subsequently, the PMA-containing medium was replaced with fresh, PMA-free complete medium, and the cells were maintained for an additional 48 hours to allow for recovery. To polarize these M0 macrophages into a classical pro-inflammatory (M1) phenotype, they were then stimulated with a combination of 1 μg/mL lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and 20 ng/mL interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) for 24 hours.

A transwell co-culture system was established to study paracrine signaling. To investigate the effect of M1 macrophage-derived factors on HSCs activation, a non-contact co-culture was established using 6-well plates with 0.4 μm pore permeable membrane inserts (Corning, CLS3412). M1-polarized THP-1 macrophages, prepared as described above, were cultured in the upper chamber. Simultaneously, human LX-2 HSCs were seeded in the lower chamber of the plate at an equal density of 3.5 × 105 cells per well. The two cell types were co-cultured in shared complete medium for 24 to 48 hours, allowing the exchange of soluble mediators without direct cell contact.

LX-2 cells were harvested post-co-culture for transcriptional analysis. Following the co-culture period, the transwell inserts containing macrophages were removed. The LX-2 cells in the lower chamber were washed with PBS and then lysed directly for total RNA extraction. The extracted RNA was used for subsequent gene expression analysis to evaluate the fibrogenic response of LX-2 cells.

Protein extraction, Western Blot, and co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP)

Total protein was extracted from cells, and concentration was determined for quantitative analysis. Cultured cells were washed with ice-cold PBS and lysed on ice using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. The cell lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C to pellet cellular debris. The protein concentration of the supernatant was quantified using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit (Beyotime, P0009) according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with bovine serum albumin (BSA) serving as the standard.

Proteins were resolved by electrophoresis, transferred to membranes, and detected with specific antibodies. Equal amounts of protein (typically 20-30 μg) were mixed with Laemmli sample buffer containing β-mercaptoethanol, denatured at 95°C for 10 min, and separated by electrophoresis on 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gels (SDS-PAGE) at a constant voltage of 120 V. The separated proteins were then electrotransferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes in an ice bath using a wet transfer system at a constant current of 200 mA for 2.5 hours. Following transfer, membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) non-fat dry milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 hour at room temperature. The membranes were subsequently probed overnight at 4°C with specific primary antibodies. After extensive washing, membranes were incubated with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 hour at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL substrate and imaged with a chemiluminescence detection system (ChemiDoc™ MP, Bio-Rad). Densitometric analysis of band intensities was performed using ImageJ software.

Protein-protein interactions were assessed by co-immunoprecipitation. For co-immunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays, cells were lysed in a non-denaturing IP lysis buffer. Cleared cell lysates (1 mL) were incubated with 5 μg of the primary antibody (or an equal amount of species-matched normal IgG as a negative control) overnight at 4°C with gentle rotation. Protein A/G magnetic beads were then added and incubated for an additional 2 hours at 4°C. The bead-bound immune complexes were washed thoroughly with IP lysis buffer, and the bound proteins were eluted by boiling in 2× Laemmli sample buffer. Both the input lysates and the co-IP samples were then analyzed by Western Blot, as described above, to verify the interaction between the proteins of interest.

RNA extraction and quantitative PCR (qPCR) assay

Total RNA was extracted, and its integrity was verified prior to downstream analysis. Total RNA was isolated from tissue samples or cultured cells using TRIzol reagent according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The concentration and purity of the extracted RNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer. Samples with an A260/A280 ratio between 1.8 and 2.0 and an A260/A230 ratio greater than 2.0 were deemed of acceptable quality for subsequent experiments, indicating minimal protein or solvent contamination.

Complementary DNA (cDNA) was synthesized from high-quality RNA. For each sample, 1 μg of total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA in a 20 μL reaction volume using the ReverTra Ace qPCR RT Master Mix (TOYOBO, FSQ-201). The reaction was performed in a thermal cycler under the following conditions: 37°C for 15 minutes, followed by 98°C for 5 minutes to inactivate the reverse transcriptase. The resulting cDNA was diluted and stored at -20°C until use.

Gene expression levels were quantified by SYBR Green-based qPCR. Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using the NovoStart® SYBR qPCR SuperMix Plus (Novoprotein, E096) on a Bio-Rad CFX96 Real-Time PCR System. Each 20 μL reaction contained 10 μL of 2× SYBR Green SuperMix, 0.4 μL of each forward and reverse primer (10 μM stock), 2 μL of cDNA template, and nuclease-free water to volume. The thermal cycling protocol was as follows: initial denaturation at 95°C for 1 minute; followed by 40 cycles of 95°C for 20 seconds, 60°C for 20 seconds, and 72°C for 30 seconds. A melt curve analysis was performed at the end of each run (from 65°C to 95°C with 0.5°C increments) to confirm the specificity of amplification and the absence of primer-dimers. No-template controls (NTCs) were included in each run to monitor for contamination.

Gene expression data were normalized and analyzed. The expression level of each target gene was normalized to the endogenous reference gene GAPDH. The comparative threshold cycle (2ˆ-ΔΔCt) method was used to calculate the relative fold change in gene expression between experimental groups.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

Secreted cytokine levels in cell culture supernatants were measured using commercial high-sensitivity ELISA kits. The concentrations of human TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-12p70 in conditioned media were determined using specific sandwich ELISA kits (Proteintech; TNF-α: KE00154, IL-1β: KE00021, IL-12p70: KE00019) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Prior to analysis, cell culture supernatants were centrifuged at 800 × g for 15 min at 4°C to remove cellular debris. The clarified supernatants were then diluted 10-fold with the provided sample diluent.

A standardized sandwich ELISA protocol was rigorously followed. All reagents and samples were brought to room temperature before use. For each analyte, 100 μL of the serially diluted protein standards or the diluted supernatant samples were added in triplicate to the antibody-precoated 96-well plates. The plate was sealed and incubated at room temperature for 1 hour. Following incubation, the liquid was aspirated, and each well was washed five times with 350 μL of the provided wash buffer using an automated plate washer. Subsequently, 100 μL of the biotin-conjugated detection antibody was added to each well, and the plate was incubated for 1 hour at room temperature. After another wash cycle, 100 μL of HRP-conjugate was added and incubated for 30 minutes at room temperature. Following a final wash, 100 μL of TMB substrate solution was added to each well. The plate was incubated in the dark at room temperature for 15-30 min for color development. The enzymatic reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL of stop solution to each well.

Absorbance was measured, and concentrations were calculated from a standard curve. The absorbance of each well was immediately measured at 450 nm with a reference wavelength of 630 nm for background correction using a BioTek Synergy H1 microplate reader. For each target cytokine, a standard curve was generated by plotting the mean absorbance of the triplicate standard samples against their known concentrations. The concentration of each unknown sample was calculated by interpolating its mean absorbance value from the standard curve and then multiplying by the dilution factor to obtain the final concentration in the original, undiluted culture supernatant.

Identification of the palmitoylation site using LC-MS/MS

Proteins were separated by SDS-PAGE, and the corresponding gel bands were excised, reduced with 5 mM DTT, and alkylated with 11 mM iodoacetamide before 1 M hydroxylamine hydrochloride was added to the gel and incubated for 3 hours to dissociate palmitate. The gel was then alkylated with 11 mM 13C iodoacetic acid, followed by in-gel digestion using sequencing-grade modified trypsin at 37°C overnight. Peptides were extracted twice using 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid in 50% aqueous acetonitrile for 30 minutes each time and subsequently dried in a Speed Vac. Peptides were reconstituted in 20 μl of 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid, and 6 μl was used for analysis by Orbitrap Elite LC-MS/MS (Thermo, USA). AIMSmass (suzhou, China) carries out LC-MS/MS detection on the prepared samples.

LC-MS/MS spectra from each run were analyzed against cGAS fasta in UniProt utilizing the in-house Proteome Discoverer program (version PD1.4, Thermo, USA). The criteria for the search were as follows: Full trypsin specificity was required, with allowance for two missed cuts. Oxidation (O), Nethylmaleimide (N), and Carbamidomethyl (C) were designated as variable modifications. The precursor ion mass tolerance was established at 20 ppm for all MS spectra obtained in the Orbitrap mass analyzer, and fragmentation for all MS2 spectra acquired was also set to 20 ppm. All acquired MS2 spectra were assigned a mass tolerance of 0.02 Da for fragmentions.

Immunofluorescent staining, confocal microscopy, and Co-localization analysis

Tissue and cell samples were prepared for immunofluorescence staining. For analysis of liver tissues, formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded sections (4-5 μm thick) were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Antigen retrieval was performed by heating the slides in citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a pressure cooker for 5 minutes. For cultured cells grown on glass coverslips, samples were fixed with 3% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 minutes.

Samples were processed through a standard immunofluorescence protocol. Following preparation, all samples (tissue sections and cells on coverslips) were blocked with 10% BSA in PBS for 1 hour at room temperature. They were then incubated overnight at 4°C with appropriate primary antibodies diluted in blocking solution. After extensive washing with PBS, samples were incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark with species-specific fluorescent secondary antibodies (e.g., Alexa Fluor 488, 647) and DAPI. Finally, coverslips were mounted onto slides using an anti-fade mounting medium.

High-resolution images were acquired using a laser-scanning confocal microscope. Fluorescent images were captured using a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope. To ensure specificity and minimize bleed-through, images for different channels were collected sequentially. Z-stack images were taken through the thickness of the cells or tissue section at optimal intervals. All imaging parameters (laser power, gain, pinhole size) were kept constant for all samples within a given experiment. Image processing and maximum intensity projections were performed using the ZEN software (Carl Zeiss).

Co-localization was quantified by analyzing the relative fluorescence intensity. To assess the co-localization of proteins of interest (e.g., ZDHHC17 and CD68), the relative fluorescence intensity was analyzed using ImageJ. For each cell of interest (e.g., CD68-positive macrophages), the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) for each channel was measured within a defined region of interest (ROI). Background fluorescence, measured from an adjacent cell-free area, was subtracted from all values. The degree of co-localization was then evaluated by calculating metrics such as the overlap coefficient or by directly comparing the intensity profiles. For each experimental condition, a minimum of 10 cells from at least 3 independent fields of view were analyzed.

Molecular docking simulation, dynamics, and binding energy calculation

Protein structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank (PDB) for IKKβ (4E3), TAK1 (2eva), and from alphafold for zdhhc17 (AF-Q8iuh5-F1). The Hdock molecular docking experiment collected 100 conformations. Ten conformations with the highest scores were chosen, and the binding sites determined the optimal conformation. Quantification and statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism software (version 8.0). Data are presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). The use of mean and SD is intended to describe the variability within each sample group.

Comparisons between two independent groups: Unpaired, two-tailed Student’s t-tests were employed for all direct comparisons between two experimental conditions (e.g., wild-type vs. knockout, control vs. treatment, normal vs. disease). The validity of the t-test rests on the assumptions that the data are (a) continuous, (b) approximately normally distributed, and (c) that the groups have similar variances (homoscedasticity).

Normality: The normality of data distribution within each group was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test.

Homogeneity of Variances: The equality of variances between the two groups being compared was verified using an F-test. If variances were found to be significantly different (p < 0.05), Welch’s correction was applied to the t-test.

Correlation analysis: The strength and direction of the linear relationship between two continuous variables were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient (r). The associated two-tailed p-value tests the null hypothesis that the correlation is zero. The linearity of the relationship was inspected visually on scatter plots prior to analysis.

The sample size (n) for each experiment is explicitly defined in the respective figure legend. In all cases, ‘n’ represents the number of biologically independent replicates (e.g., individual mice, distinct human donor samples, or independent primary cell isolations/culture experiments). Technical replicates (e.g., multiple wells from the same cell preparation) were averaged to yield a single value per biological replicate. No formal a priori sample size calculation was performed. The sample sizes used are consistent with common practice in the field for similar experimental designs.

To minimize bias, animals and samples were randomly assigned to experimental groups. Furthermore, the investigator responsible for quantitative outcome assessments (e.g., histological scoring, densitometric analysis of Western blots) was blinded to the group allocation during both data collection and analysis.

No data were excluded from the analyses based on the outcome. Pre-established exclusion criteria, applied before any statistical analysis, were strictly technical: animal death unrelated to the experimental intervention (e.g., procedural accident), failure of a primary cell culture (e.g., contamination, poor viability), or a failed experimental procedure (e.g., transfection efficiency below a pre-set threshold of 70%, as confirmed by a parallel control). All data that passed these quality control criteria are included in the reported results.

Statistical significance was defined as a two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05. Significance levels in the figures are denoted as follows: ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. The abbreviation “ns” indicates not significant (p ≥ 0.05).
