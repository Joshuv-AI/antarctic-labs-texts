Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most prevalent primary glomerular disease worldwide, particularly affecting young adults aged 20–40 years. In China, IgAN accounts for 34.0 % to 52.7 % of primary glomerular disease cases, a significantly greater percentage than in Western countries (O'Shaughnessy et al., 2018; Yim et al. 2020).Notably, 20 % to 40 % of IgAN patients progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD) within 20 years of onset, imposing a substantial burden on both society and families. Despite advances in understanding the pathogenesis of IgAN and improvements in treatment, therapeutic outcomes remain suboptimal. Therefore, further exploration of the pathological and physiological mechanisms of IgAN, alongside the development of precise prevention and treatment strategies, is crucial for delaying disease progression.

The hallmark pathological features of IgAN include IgA deposition in the glomerular mesangial area, mesangial cell proliferation, and matrix accumulation(Roberts 2014).The widely accepted "multiple hit" hypothesis links the pathogenesis of IgAN to immune-mediated inflammation, where the inflammatory cascade initiated by mesangial cells triggers interactions among mesangial cells, podocytes, and the renal tubules, collectively driving the progression of IgAN(Floege et al. 2022; Robert et al. 2015). These findings underscore the pivotal role of mesangial cells in the disease process.

Human renal mesangial cells (HMCs) are integral to the glomerular mesangial matrix and act as central hubs for intercellular communication. Upon activation, HMCs produce a variety of cytokines and signaling molecules, leading to mesangial proliferation via immune-inflammatory mechanisms. Expansion of the mesangial matrix can result in fibrosis and impaired glomerular function(Boi et al. 2023). Thus, inhibiting mesangial cell proliferation and the associated immune-inflammatory response is key to the management of IgAN.

The Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) guidelines recommend the use of renin‒angiotensin system blockers as a first-line nonimmunotherapy for IgAN, while patients at high risk of disease progression should receive hormone treatment or immunotherapy(KDIGO 2021). However, the currently available optimized supportive and immunosuppressive therapies do not fully meet clinical needs. Identifying new therapeutic targets for regulating mesangial cell proliferation and surrounding immune-inflammatory responses is therefore highly important for improving IgAN treatment outcomes.

With the growing body of research on the "gut-kidney axis" theory, mounting evidence indicates that gut microbiota dysbiosis plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of IgA nephropathy (IgAN). Elucidating the underlying mechanisms of this association is crucial. First, disruption of the gut microbiota can activate intestinal mucosal B cells, thereby altering local mucosal immunity and promoting the production of aberrantly glycosylated IgA1 (galactose-deficient IgA1, Gd-IgA1). This process drives the classic pathogenic cascade of IgAN. Second, it remains unclear whether gut microbiota dysbiosis can also exert remote pathogenic effects through additional mechanisms. Addressing this knowledge gap requires the identification of key mediators of gut microbiota disruption, particularly microbial-derived metabolites.

Sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P), a bioactive sphingolipid metabolite, is well-established as a significant contributor to a wide range of physiological and pathophysiological processes, including cell survival, proliferation, apoptosis, and migration(Schwalm et al., 2024).Our study has established that elevated levels of S1P not only serve as an indicator of IgAN progression severity but also trigger IgAN-like pathological phenotypes in glomerular mesangial cells, such as excessive proliferation and inflammation(Ran Z, et al. 2025). Our goal was to investigate the mechanisms through which S1P—a gut microbiota-derived metabolite—exerts systemic effects on IgAN progression and to discover novel therapeutic targets for this disease.

Methods

Materials

IgA1 (ab91020) was obtained from Abcam (Cambridge, MA, USA). The CCL2 inhibitor NOX-E36 (1390,630–22–4) and the Met inhibitor LY2801653 (HY-15,514) were obtained from MedChemExpress (Shanghai, China). S1P (860552P) and the S1P signaling pathway blocker FTY720 (Y0002055) were purchased from Merck Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA). The S1P ELISA kit (CEG031Ge) was acquired from Cloud-Clone Corp. (Wuhan, China). Antibodies specific for CCL2 (26,161–1-AP), Met (25,869–1-AP), p-Met (Tyr1234/1235) (30,737–1-AP), PCNA (10,205–2-AP), Cyclin D1 (26,939–1-AP), GAPDH (60,004–1-Ig), S1PR1 (55,133–1-AP), and FAK (66,258–1-Ig) were purchased from ProteinTech (Wuhan, China). Antibodies against p-Met (Tyr1349) (Ab68141) and P-FAK (Ab81298) were obtained from Abcam.

Study participants

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975) and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Minhang Hospital, Fudan University (Shanghai, China) (Approval No.2023–052–01 K). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Pathological evaluation of the renal biopsy samples was performed by the Pathology Department of Fudan University during each patient's hospitalization period. A sample of renal tissue was subjected to immunofluorescence staining. The renal tissue from the healthy control group was obtained from adjacent normal tissue preserved during nephrectomy for renal cancer. Patient stool was collected fresh in the morning in sampling tubes and stored at −80 °C.

Culture and confirmation of Escherichia-Shigella

Fresh fecal specimens were collected in sterilized containers when the recruited patients visited doctor and then transferred to the Department of laboratory medicine for Escherichia-Shigella testing within 24 h. Fecal samples were directly inoculated on MacConkey agar (OXOID, UK) and cultured at 37 °C for 24 h. Suspected Escherichia-Shigella colonies were identified by both VITEK 2 Compact (BioMérieux, France) and matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF-MS; bioMérieux).

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care Committee of Fudan University (Approval No 2022-JS-028). Male C57BL/6 J mice were used to establish an IgAN model through oral administration of bovine serum albumin (BSA, MedChemExpress) combined with staphylococcal enterotoxin B (MedChemExpress) injection. Mice received an antibiotic cocktail (vancomycin 100 mg/kg, neomycin sulfate 200 mg/kg, ampicillin 200 mg/kg, metronidazole 100 mg/kg) orally for 7 days for gut microbiota depletion.

Fecal microbiota transplantation was performed using Escherichia-Shigella isolates (5 × 10^8 CFU in 0.2 ml PBS) from IgAN patients (IgAN-ES group) or PBS controls, administered daily by oral gavage for 11 weeks. Renal function parameters (serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, albuminuria) were measured using an ARCHITECT analyzer. Gut microbiota composition was analyzed by 16S rDNA sequencing, and plasma metabolites were profiled by untargeted metabolomics. All procedures were conducted under sterile conditions.

ELISA

Serum and urine samples were prepared according to the ELISA kit protocol. Briefly, 50 μL of each sample and standard were added to the wells, incubated at 37 °C for 1 hour, and washed. Subsequently, 50 μL of enzyme-linked antibody was added, followed by a 0.5–1 hour incubation at 37 °C. After washing, 0.1 mL of TMB substrate was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 10–30 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 μL of stop solution, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader. The expression levels were determined by constructing a standard curve.

PCR amplification and 16S rDNA sequencing

The V3-V4 regions of bacterial 16S rDNA were amplified using the primers 338F (5′-ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA-3′) and 806R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′), which were tagged with specific barcodes for each fecal sample. PCR amplification (Roche Diagnostics) was performed in a 25 μL reaction mixture containing 25 ng of template DNA, 12.5 μL of PCR PreMix, 2.5 μL of each primer, and PCR-grade water. The PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 98 °C for 30 s; 32 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 54 °C for 30 s, and extension at 72 °C for 45 s; and a final extension at 72 °C for 10 min. The PCR products were confirmed by 2 % agarose gel electrophoresis, purified using AMPure XT beads (Beckman Coulter Genomics, Danvers, MA, USA), and quantified using Qubit (Invitrogen, USA). The amplicon libraries were sequenced on the NovaSeq PE250 platform(Wehbi et al. 2021).

Nontargeted metabolomics analysis

Fifty microliters (50 µL) of each blood sample and two hundred microliters (200 µL) of the extraction solution, which consisted of a methanol‒acetonitrile mixture (1:1 (v/v) ratio) containing isotope internal standards, were transferred to a 96-well protein precipitation plate. The mixture was shaken at 750 rpm for five minutes, allowed to stand for an additional five minutes, and then filtered. The filtrate was collected, and an equal volume of supernatant from each sample was combined to create a quality control (QC) sample for machine testing.

We evaluated polar metabolites using the Vanquish ultrahigh-performance liquid chromatography system from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Chromatographic separation of the target compounds was achieved with a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). Mobile phase A consisted of an aqueous solution with 25 mmol/L ammonium acetate and 25 mmol/L ammonia, while mobile phase B consisted of acetonitrile. The sample tray temperature was maintained at 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. The Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer acquired both MS1 and MS2 data, which were controlled with Xcalibur software (version 4.4, Thermo). The detailed parameters included the following: sheath gas flow rate: 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate: 15 Arb; capillary temperature: 320 °C; full MS resolution: 60,000; MS/MS resolution: 15,000; collision energy: SNCE 20/30/40; and spray voltage: 3.8 kV (positive) or −3.4 kV (negative). The raw data were converted into mzXML format using ProteoWizard software, followed by metabolite identification through a collaboratively developed R package utilizing the BiotreeDB (V3.0) database. Visualization analysis was then conducted using a self-developed R package.

Histology and immunofluorescence

Renal cortical tissues were fixed, paraffin-embedded, and stained with periodic acid-Schiff (PAS) following standard protocols. Histological features were analyzed at 40 × magnification using ImageJ software. Immunofluorescence analysis was performed on frozen kidney sections (3–5 μm thick), which were stained with a fluorescein isothiocyanate-labeled goat anti-mouse IgA antibody (diluted 1:10, Sigma, St. Louis, MO, USA) and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The sections were counterstained with DAPI, mounted, and visualized under a fluorescence microscope(Crowley et al. 2009; Zhang et al. 2020; Tajik et al. 2020).

Immunofluorescence analysis of the ccl2-met-fak pathway

Fresh kidneys were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde overnight, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 4 μm. The sections were deparaffinized and rehydrated to restore antigen activity. The sections were subsequently blocked with a 3 % BSA solution for 30 min and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following polyclonal antibodies: rabbit anti-human MCP-1/CCL2 (ProteinTech, 26,161–1-AP), FAK (ProteinTech, 12,636–1-AP), and rabbit monoclonal anti-c-Met (Abcam, Ab51067). Next, the sections were incubated in the dark at room temperature for 50 min with a fluorescently labeled secondary antibody, poly-HRP goat anti-rabbit (Shanghai SIMUWU Biotechnology Co., Ltd., SD0161). Single-label signal amplification was performed using a tyramide signal amplification (TSA) kit (Shanghai SIMUWU Biotechnology Co., Ltd., SD0201). Autofluorescence was quenched prior to counterstaining the nuclei with DAPI and mounting the sections.

Thermal polymerization of iga1

Monomeric human IgA1 (mIgA1) was subjected to thermal polymerization by incubation at 63 °C for 150 min. The supernatant was collected following centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C, and the protein was purified using a Sephacryl S-200HR molecular sieve column on an AKTA-FPLC system. The purity and concentration of thermally aggregated IgA1 (aIgA1) were assessed by SDS‒PAGE and a BCA assay, respectively.

Cell culture

Human primary glomerular mesangial cells (ScienCell, item no 4200) were cocultured with 1 mg/mL aIgA1 in mesangial cell culture medium containing 10 % FBS, 1 % penicillin/streptomycin, and 1 % mesangial cell culture additive. The cells were incubated at 37 °C with 5 % CO2 and monitored under a microscope for adherence.

CCK-8 assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) (Beyotime, C0039) by mixing cells with serum-free medium at a 1:10 ratio. After a 1-hour incubation at 37 °C, the absorbance was measured at 450 nm using an ELISA reader (Thermo Labsystems).

EdU staining

Cell proliferation was further evaluated using the BeyoClick™ EdU cell proliferation kit with Alexa Fluor 488 (Beyotime, Shanghai, China). The cells were seeded at 5 × 105 cells/well in 6-well plates and starved in serum-free medium for 24 h. EdU working solution (10 μM) was added, followed by incubation for 2 h. The cells were fixed, permeabilized, and subjected to Click reaction and Hoechst 33,342 staining. Fluorescence images were captured using a microscope.

Flow cytometry

For flow cytometry analysis, the cells (2 × 106 cells/mL) were trypsinized, fixed in ethanol, and stained with propidium iodide (PI) solution. Apoptosis was assessed by staining cells with Annexin V-FITC and PI (Invitrogen, USA), followed by analysis using flow cytometry. Fluorescence analyses were performed on a Coulter EPICS XL Flow Cytometer (Beckman) using Expo32-ADC software.

RNA sequencing

Total RNA was extracted from kidney tissues and mesangial cells, fragmented, and used as a template for cDNA synthesis. The resulting cDNA was purified, ligated with adapters, and amplified by PCR. The library was sequenced on an Illumina HiSeq2000 platform.

KEGG pathway analysis

MetaboAnalyst was utilized for data processing, statistical analysis, and pathway enrichment. Peaks with zero values in >50 % of the samples were removed, and the remaining data were subjected to univariate analysis (t-test or Wilcoxon test) and PLS-DA. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) and metabolites were subjected to KEGG pathway analysis.

Statistical analysis

SPSS v.22.0 was used for statistical analysis. The data are expressed as the means ± SDs, and comparisons between two groups were performed using t tests. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using one-way ANOVA followed by the least significant difference (LSD) test. A P value <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Further experiments revealed a strong correlation between serum S1P levels and changes in Escherichia-Shigella abundance(Ran Z, et al. 2025). Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) revealed significant differences in microbial community structure among the three groups (Fig. 2A). Both the Chao1 and Shannon indices indicated a reduction in α-diversity in the IgAN-ES and IgAN-PBS groups compared to the NC-PBS group (Fig. 2B). Column-stacking diagrams were used to visualize differences in taxonomic abundance at the phylum and genus levels (Fig. 2C and 2D). To delineate the metabolic phenotype of individuals within the IgAN-ES group, we conducted comprehensive untargeted metabolomic analyses of serum samples (PCoA) (Fig. 2F) and partial least squares discrimination analysis (PLS-DA) (Fig. 2G) were performed. Clear differences emerged between the metabolomes of the IgAN-ES and NC-PBS groups. Supervised clustering of the serum samples revealed significant differences in the metabolic profile of the IgAN-ES group compared with that of the NC-PBS group (Fig. 2H). This finding suggests that Escherichia-Shigella transplantation may lead to metabolic disorders in the context of IgAN, particularly disorders characterized by increased levels of S1P, and we further investigated the underlying mechanisms in subsequent experiments.

Bioinformatic analysis identified 50 co-expressed genes shared between S1P metabolism and IgAN, indicating a close molecular interaction relevant to IgAN pathogenesis (Fig. 2I). GO enrichment analysis highlighted biological processes such as the "sphingosine-1-phosphate receptor signaling pathway" and "sphingolipid mediated signaling pathway," underscoring the potential importance of S1P receptor signaling in IgAN. KEGG analysis further revealed enrichment in pathways related to EGFR tyrosine kinase inhibitor resistance, VEGF signaling, and C-type lectin receptor signaling—implicated in vascular dysfunction, fibrosis, and immune dysregulation, respectively (Fig. 2J, K). Additionally, significant enrichment of the platelet activation pathway suggests a novel link between endothelial injury and thrombosis in IgAN progression. Together, these results illustrate a complex interplay among S1P-related metabolic dysregulation, vascular injury, and immune dysfunction in IgAN.

We further validated the altered activation of the CCL2-MET-FAK pathway in IgAN patients and an mice with an IgAN-like phenotype. Through immunofluorescence analysis, we observed significant increases in the expression levels of CCL2-MET-FAK signaling pathway components in the renal tissues of IgAN patients compared with those of individuals in the healthy control group (Fig. 4F). Similarly, mouse kidney tissues from the IgAN-ES group exhibited significantly higher expression of CCL2-MET-FAK signaling pathway components than those from the NC-PBS and IgAN-PBS groups (Fig. 4G). Collectively, the above findings indicate that the CCL2-MET-FAK pathway plays a role in IgAN. Consequently, we focused on the CCL2-MET-FAK pathway for further mechanistic investigation of IgAN.

Pharmacological inhibition of the CCL2-MET axis significantly attenuated IgA1-induced pathogenic responses in mesangial cells. Treatment with either the CCL2 inhibitor NOX-E36 or the MET inhibitor LY2801653 effectively inhibited IgA1-induced mesangial cell proliferation and promoted apoptosis, culminating in a significant reduction in IgA1 deposition (Fig. 7A-G). Concomitantly, both inhibitors markedly downregulated the expression of key signaling molecules and proliferation markers, including CCL2, MET, phosphorylated MET (at Tyr1234/1235 and Tyr1349), PCNA, and cyclin D1 (Fig. 8A-F). Importantly, neither NOX-E36 nor LY2801653 intervention significantly altered S1P levels, as quantified by ELISA (Fig. 7I), nor did they affect the expression profiles of S1P receptors, as determined by Western blotting (Fig. 8G and H). Discussion

Recent studies have revealed significant gut microbiota dysbiosis in IgA nephropathy (IgAN) patients, characterized by decreased beneficial bacteria and increased pathogenic Escherichia-Shigella. These alterations may be linked to abnormal immune activation and inflammatory responses (Coppo 2018; Monteiro and Berthelot 2021; Han et al. 2022). Dietary modification and probiotic supplementation represent promising therapeutic approaches for IgA nephropathy (IgAN) by modulating gut microbiota composition (Zhong et al. 2020). Importantly, IgAN patients exhibit distinct metabolomic disturbances characterized by altered levels of microbial-derived metabolites including lipopolysaccharides and short-chain fatty acids in serum, urine and tissues (Dong et al. 2023; Li et al. 2022).These metabolic abnormalities contribute to disease pathogenesis through multiple interconnected mechanisms - they can disrupt intestinal barrier function, activate mucosal immunity to promote Gd-IgA1 production, and exert systemic effects on immune cells and inflammatory mediators that ultimately lead to glomerular injury. Furthermore, such metabolic perturbations may additionally exacerbate IgAN progression by interfering with cellular energy metabolism and immune regulation. These findings collectively highlight the therapeutic potential of targeting both gut microbial ecology and downstream metabolic-immune pathways in the management of IgAN.Leveraging our group's established expertise in gut-kidney axis research and microbiota-metabolite interactions in renal disease (He 2020; Liu et al. 2023), our recent work has focused on deciphering IgAN-specific pathogenesis and developing targeted interventions(Tang et al. 2022; Tang et al. 2023; He et al. 2024; Zhu et al. 2024). Collectively, this body of research substantiates the pivotal role of gut microbial dysbiosis in initiating and propagating IgAN.

To investigate the pathogenic role of gut microbiota in IgAN, we performed fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) in a murine model using Escherichia-Shigella isolated from IgAN patients. This intervention induced significant renal dysfunction, characterized by elevated serum creatinine, albuminuria, mesangial proliferation, and increased renal IgA/C3 deposition. Notably, these changes were accompanied by a marked rise in serum S1P levels.Consistent with these findings, we observed reduced microbial α-diversity and structural disruption in the gut microbiota of IgAN mice, with specific enrichment of Verrucomicrobiota, Akkermansia, and Blautia_A. Serum metabolomic analysis further revealed significant perturbations, particularly in S1P-related pathways.

Bioinformatic integration highlighted a substantial overlap between S1P metabolism and IgAN, with enriched GO terms and KEGG pathways implicating S1P signaling in vascular injury, immune dysregulation, and platelet activation. Collectively, these results suggest that Escherichia-Shigella promotes IgAN progression through S1P-mediated mechanisms, illustrating a novel gut–kidney axis in disease pathogenesis.

Specifically, our study identified gut microbiota-derived S1P as a key metabolite linked to IgAN pathogenesis, revealing novel insights into gut-kidney interactions. S1P, a bioactive lipid molecule synthesized from intracellular sphingosine and lipids, plays a crucial regulatory role in both intracellular and the extracellular environments. Extracellular S1P interacts with five G-protein-coupled receptors (S1PR1–5), activating downstream signaling pathways such as the ERK/MAPK, PI3K/Akt, and HIPPO/YAP pathways(Stepanovska et al., 2020). Recent studies have increasingly highlighted the significant role of S1P in kidney diseases. S1P regulates renal tubular epithelial cell migration, proliferation, and apoptosis, influencing both injury and repair processes. S1P receptor agonists can promote tubular epithelial cell repair and mitigate renal injury. Additionally, S1P modulates glomerular capillary permeability and inflammatory cell infiltration, impacting glomerular inflammation and injury(Drexler et al. 2021; Zhang et al. 2018; Eckes et al. 2022). The extracellular transport of S1P via its transporter Spns2 further promotes inflammatory and chemotactic factor expression. Inhibiting Spns2 can effectively reduce renal inflammation and fibrosis (Tanaka et al. 2022). Clinical studies have also established a close correlation between serum S1P levels and CKD severity and prognosis, underscoring the critical role of S1P in kidney disease pathogenesis. In-depth investigations into the mechanisms of S1P in IgAN could unveil novel therapeutic targets.

In the kidney, S1PR1, S1PR2, and S1PR3 are predominantly expressed, while recent findings highlight a pro-fibrotic role for S1PR5 in renal tissue.The sphingosine analog fingolimod (FTY720; Gilenya®) has been a critical tool in exploring S1P signaling(Eckes et al., 2022).Our findings establish critical links between gut microbiota disruption and IgAN mediated by S1P. We further validated the direct effect of S1P on IgAN via in vitro experiments, demonstrating that S1P significantly promoted mesangial cell proliferation, inhibited apoptosis, and induced significant IgA1 accumulation in the IgAN cell model. The S1P receptor antagonist FTY720 inhibited mesangial cell proliferation and increased apoptosis. Additionally, S1P binding assays indicated that S1P primarily exerts its physiological effects by tightly binding to the Gi protein and mesangial cells. FTY720 counteracted the proproliferative effect of S1P by blocking S1PR1 and S1PR3. These findings elucidate the direct effects of S1P, a gut microbiota-associated metabolite, on mesangial cells, providing a foundation for further research into how gut microbiota disruption "remotely" promotes mesangial cell phenotype transformation.

Given the established link among Escherichia-Shigella, S1P elevation, and IgAN pathology, we therefore investigated S1P's direct pathogenic mechanisms using in vitro cell models. We performed RNA-seq analysis on mesangial cells stimulated with aggregated IgA1 (aIgA1) and control cells during their log phase. This led to the identification of 190 upregulated genes in aIgA1-stimulated cells, with CCL2 exhibiting the most pronounced upregulation. KEGG pathway analysis revealed substantial differences in gene expression profiles between groups, particularly affecting pathways related to cell proliferation, growth, and the immune system. Notably, significant alterations were observed in MET-related signaling pathways, including MET activation of PTK2 signaling, MET-mediated cell motility, and MET signaling itself.

The CCL2-MET-FAK axis plays key roles in immune regulation, cell migration, and tissue repair. CCL2 regulates inflammatory cell migration and activation by binding its receptor CCR2. It can also directly bind and activate the MET receptor, promoting dimerization and kinase activity, thereby driving cell migration and invasion. MET activation triggers downstream signaling, including via the focal adhesion kinase (FAK) pathway (Kadomoto et al. 2021; Acevedo et al. 2022; Zhang et al. 2019). FAK phosphorylation regulates actin cytoskeleton reorganization and cell-matrix adhesion and activates pathways like the PI3K-AKT pathway, promoting cell survival, proliferation, and migration. Overactivation of this axis contributes to abnormal cell growth, invasion, tumor development, and metastasis. Inhibiting the CCL2-MET-FAK axis could alleviate pathological conditions linked to abnormal immune responses and inflammation (e.g., immune-inflammatory diseases) and potentially reduce tumor invasion and metastasis (Deng et al. 2023; Kanemitsu et al. 2021; Kashyap et al. 2018). Therefore, this axis represents a potential therapeutic target for inflammation-related diseases, tumors, and chronic immune-inflammatory disorders(Xu et al. 2021; Romero et al. 2023; Belvitch and Dudek 2012). This study investigated the role of the CCL2-MET-FAK axis in IgA nephropathy (IgAN) pathogenesis, revealing correlations among serum S1P levels, the gut microbiota, and mesangial cell proliferation.

We further validated the involvement of the CCL2-MET-FAK pathway in both IgAN patients and a mice with an IgAN-like phenotype model. Immunofluorescence analysis showed significantly elevated expression levels of this pathway in the renal tissues of IgAN patients compared to healthy controls. Similarly, in mouse kidney tissues, the IgAN-ES group showed significantly increased CCL2-MET-FAK pathway expression relative to both the NC-PBS and IgAN-PBS groups. Based on these findings, we focused our subsequent mechanistic investigation on the CCL2-MET-FAK pathway .

Our in vitro experiments using mesangial cells with an IgAN phenotype demonstrate that pathogenic aIgA1 stimulation triggered marked hyperproliferation of mesangial cells, an effect that was significantly attenuated by FTY720 co-treatment. Mechanistically, aIgA1 challenge induced transcriptional upregulation of CCL2 and FAK mRNA, along with increased protein expression of CCL2, MET, phosphorylated MET (Tyr1234/1235 and Tyr1349), S1PR1, and S1PR3. Notably, FTY720 treatment effectively suppressed this signaling cascade, reducing protein levels of CCL2, phospho-MET (at both activation sites), FAK, and S1PR1/3. The coordinated inhibition of proliferative mediators (CCL2/p-MET/FAK) and S1P receptors was directly associated with the mitigation of mesangial hyperproliferation. These findings indicate that FTY720, as an S1P receptor antagonist, may inhibit S1P signaling and restrain mesangial cell proliferation by disrupting the CCL2-MET-FAK axis.

Meanwhile, our findings also revealed that S1P activates the CCL2-MET-FAK signaling axis, leading to significant enhancement of mesangial cell proliferation, induction of apoptosis, and increased IgA1 deposition. Notably, pharmacological blockade of CCL2 effectively mitigated these pathological effects, resulting in marked reductions in cellular proliferation, apoptotic rates, and IgA1 accumulation. These findings strongly implicate gut microbiota-derived S1P as a critical mediator of mesangial cell activation in IgAN, operating through the CCL2-MET-FAK signaling pathway.

Our results highlight the potential of targeting the CCL2-MET-FAK axis for IgAN treatment, suggesting a new therapeutic strategy. Notably, targeting the gut microbiota and its metabolites, particularly S1P and its receptors, represents a novel and promising approach. Furthermore, S1P may contribute to renal injury in IgA nephropathy by activating the CCL2-MET-FAK axis.
