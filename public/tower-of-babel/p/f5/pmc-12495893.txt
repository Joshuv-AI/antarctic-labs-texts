Insights Into Hemoglobinopathies in Spain: A Comprehensive Review
Authors: Joan‐Lluis Vives Corrons
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHemoglobinopathies, including thalassemia and sickle cell disease (SCD), are increasingly prevalent in Spain due to migration and historical genetic patterns. Despite recent advances in screening and care, regional disparities in diagnosis and treatment persist.ObjectivesTo critically assess the current landscape of hemoglobinopathies in Spain, focusing on screening program implementation, healthcare access, therapeutic innovations, and the integration of patient‐centered outcomes.MethodsThis review synthesizes data from national registries, recent peer‐reviewed publications, and health policy documents, offering a multidisciplinary analysis of clinical, epidemiological, and public health dimensions of hemoglobinopathies in Spain.ResultsSpain has progressively implemented neonatal screening programs for SCD and thalassemias, with substantial regional variation. Innovative treatments, including gene‐editing approaches, are being piloted, yet accessibility remains limited by cost and infrastructure. Multinational collaborations and national registries have improved patient monitoring and evidence‐based care, although healthcare inequalities persist.ConclusionsSpain's efforts in diagnosis, treatment, and research of hemoglobinopathies have been significant, but further action is needed to ensure equitable healthcare access, ethical integration of gene therapies, and incorporation of patient‐reported outcomes into clinical decision‐making.Trial Registration:The author has confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Introduction

Thalassemia and hemoglobinopathies are inherited hematological disorders characterized by impaired hemoglobin production, posing significant healthcare challenges due to their lifelong management requirements. These conditions are particularly prevalent in Mediterranean populations, where regional disparities in prevalence reflect historical patterns of genetic inheritance.

Spain has seen notable advancements in the epidemiological understanding and treatment of these disorders, yet gaps remain in ensuring equitable care across all regions. Thalassemia and hemoglobinopathies exhibit considerable geographical variation, with higher prevalence rates observed in regions historically influenced by Mediterranean ancestry, such as Catalonia, Valencia, and the Balearic Islands (Figures 1 and 2). β‐Thalassemia is the most common form, manifesting in various clinical presentations, while α‐thalassemia includes hemoglobin H disease and silent carrier states. Sickle cell disease (SCD), primarily affecting individuals of African origin, has become increasingly prevalent due to migration trends.

Advances in neonatal screening programs, specialized care networks, and research collaborations have significantly improved patient outcomes. Pilot neonatal screening programs initiated after 2003 identified an overall incidence rate of 0.33%, highlighting the efficacy of these initiatives [1, 2]. The first epidemiological study on hemoglobinopathies in Spain was conducted in 1981 by Baiget et al. revealing a prevalence of 0.14% [3], which was lower than the 0.5% reported by the World Health Organization in 1995 [4]. Subsequent studies aiming to assess the prevalence of β‐thalassemia across Spain have provided further insights, but only one comprehensive study included participants representative of a broad range of Spanish geographical regions [5]. This study was conducted under the auspices of the Spanish Society of Hematology (SEHH), and demonstrated a heterogeneous distribution of β‐thalassemia, with prevalence rates ranging from 0.1% to 5%. In addition, carriers of hemoglobin variants such as HbS and HbC were predominantly identified in the southern and western regions of Spain.

Targeted control programs for couples at risk of thalassemia, in conjunction with prenatal diagnostic services, have successfully reduced the incidence of transfusion dependent β‐thalassemia (TDT) when effectively implemented [6]. However, emerging challenges, including increased migration, the impact of the COVID‐19 pandemic, and breakthroughs in gene‐editing technologies, are reshaping the epidemiological landscape of these disorders [7, 8, 9].

The Spanish healthcare system currently offers specialized resources for the diagnosis and management of hemoglobinopathies, supported by expert centers and trained healthcare professionals. This review provides a comprehensive and up‐to‐date analysis of thalassemia and hemoglobinopathies in Spain, with a particular focus on neonatal and prenatal screening program efficiency, treatment accessibility disparities, and patient‐centered outcomes in healthcare decision‐making.

Epidemiological Trends and Public Health Advances

Epidemiological studies indicate a heterogeneous distribution of hemoglobinopathies in Spain. Certain regions in Spain exhibit higher prevalence rates of β‐thalassemia and structural hemoglobinopathies, influenced by historical genetic contributions [10]. The increasing migration from geographical regions with high SCD prevalence (i.e. African continent) into Spain, has also led to a shift in disease burden and to an important impact on healthcare provision [11, 12]. Spain introduced neonatal screening programs in 2003 and fully implemented universal screening to all the regions in 2021. A 1999 screening campaign in Extremadura, involving 2818 schoolchildren, revealed an overall prevalence of 0.24%, with 0.10% of the indigenous population carrying HbS. This relatively high rate is thought to be linked to the historical malaria endemicity in the region [13]. Similarly, a 2006 study in the Canary Islands (Lanzarote), led by Calvo‐Villas et al. [14], screened 2436 pregnant women, identifying a hemoglobin variant in 0.94% of cases, predominantly among immigrant populations from Africa and Central and South America. Between 2006 and 2009, in response to the growing incidence of SCD in Catalonia, largely driven by decades of migration, a pilot neonatal screening program, screened 4696 newborns, categorized by their mother's birthplace. The results revealed an overall hemoglobinopathy prevalence of 0.5%, with an SCD‐specific prevalence of 0.021% [15, 16]. In 2009, the TV3 Marathon on Rare Diseases awarded a grant to establish the Catalan Network for the Diagnosis and Follow‐up of Severe Hemoglobinopathies (CATGLOBIN). This initiative, partially supported by the Government of Catalonia (Generalitat de Catalunya), improved diagnostics and care for severe hemoglobinopathies in the region [17] and the success of this public–private collaboration led to a pivotal development in 2015, when the Catalan Government incorporated SCD screening into its official neonatal screening program, enhancing early detection and patient care. Spain has progressively implemented universal NBS for hemoglobinopathies, notably SCD and thalassemias, across autonomous communities through its national newborn screening (NBS) initiatives and rare disease registries. These initiatives have yielded valuable epidemiological data, highlighting regional variations influenced by demographic factors. As mentioned before, the NBS for hemoglobinopathies began in 2003 and expanded progressively, achieving national coverage by 2015. Screening methods include capillary electrophoresis, high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and confirmatory molecular tests. Results have shown that the incidence of SCD varies regionally, influenced by local demographic patterns, from 1 in 3000 in Catalonia to 1 in 10,000 in Murcia. In Catalonia, the screening of 506,996 newborns, between 2015 and 2022, identified 160 cases of SCD, indicating an incidence of 1 in 3169 live births predominantly with the HbSS genotype [18 ]. In Madrid, over 1 million newborns screened, between 2003 and 2018, revealed an SCD prevalence of 1 in 5552 live births, being the majority of cases HbSS [19]. In Murcia, among 104,083 newborns, screened between 2016 and 2023, 10 SCD cases were detected, reflecting a lower incidence probably due to the lower migratory influx compared to other regions [20]. Spain's commitment to monitoring hemoglobinopathies led to the establishment of the Spanish Registry of Hemoglobinopathies by the Sociedad Española de Hematología y Oncología Pediátricas (SEHOP), later supported by the Sociedad Española de Hematología y Hemoterapia (SEHH), encompassing information on hemoglobinopathies and other rare anemias in Spain [21]. The implementation of the Spanish Registry of Hemoglobinopathies (2013) launched the Spanish Registry of Hemoglobinopathies and Rare Anemias (REHem‐AR) to gather epidemiological data on hemoglobinopathies across Spain [22]. This initiative aimed to collect comprehensive data on conditions like SCD and thalassemias to enhance understanding of these diseases and improve patient care nationwide. Overall, Spain's efforts illustrate the value of coordinated NBS and health data systems, while highlighting the need for harmonization and long‐term sustainability.

Regional Healthcare Disparities

Disparities in Spain's healthcare infrastructure significantly affect the diagnosis and management of hemoglobinopathies and other rare anemias. Factors contributing to these disparities are multifaceted and include (a) Policy fragmentation: Decentralization of Spain's healthcare system results in variability in the implementation of screening programs and access to advanced therapies. Autonomous regions often prioritize different diseases or programs based on local needs, leading to inconsistencies [23]. (b) Resource allocation: Uneven distribution of healthcare infrastructure and specialist centers leads to limited access in rural or economically disadvantaged regions. Autonomous communities with higher budgets, such as Catalonia and Madrid, can invest more in advanced diagnostics and treatments compared to less affluent regions that struggle with limited access to expertise and treatments [24]. (c) Healthcare workforce distribution: A lack of trained hematologists and genetic counsellors in rural areas exacerbates disparities in care. Specialists are often concentrated in urban centers, leaving remote regions underserved [25]. (d) Demographic and migration pattern: Regions with higher immigrant populations, such as Andalusia or Catalonia, face a disproportionate burden of hemoglobinopathies, straining existing healthcare resources [7]. To address these disparities in the diagnosis and management of hemoglobinopathies and other rare anemias several measures can be implemented (Table 2). A recent key publication details the measures implemented in Spain to address disparities and provides a comprehensive overview of the REHem‐AR [8]. This study that has been instrumental in collecting and analyzing data on patients with clinically significant rare anemias across Spain, including TDT, non‐transfusion‐dependent thalassemias (NTDT), SCD, and other hemoglobinopathies. The key measures highlighted in the publication include:​ (1) A progressive implementation of NBS across various Spanish autonomous communities, to significantly increase the early diagnosis rates;​ (2) The expansion of participating centers by the inclusion of new centers in the registry to broad the patient samples and enhance the comprehensiveness of the data collected; and (3) annual monitoring and data updates by cross‐sectional analyses to monitor patient outcomes, treatment efficacy, and emerging complications, thereby informing clinical practices and policy decisions.

Complementary actions that may contribute to address disparities are: (1) The creation of a centralized funding mechanism to support underfunded regions; (2) The expansion of telemedicine to provide remote consultations, genetic counselling, and follow‐up care; and (3) the implementation of national training initiatives to increase the number of healthcare professionals skilled in diagnosing and managing hemoglobinopathies. This can be complemented by nationwide campaigns to raise awareness about hemoglobinopathies, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and treatment [26].

By addressing these underlying causes and implementing targeted solutions, Spain can reduce regional healthcare disparities and improve outcomes for patients with hemoglobinopathies and other rare anemias.

Luspatercept is a first‐in‐class erythroid maturation agent developed to address ineffective erythropoiesis, a hallmark of β‐thalassemia. By selectively binding TGF‐β superfamily ligands, it alleviates the SMAD2/3 signaling blockade on erythroid maturation. Clinical trials have shown that luspatercept significantly reduces transfusion requirements and improves hemoglobin levels in adult patients with TDT, representing a paradigm shift in management [28, 29].

L‐glutamine increases the redox potential in erythrocytes, mitigates oxidative damage, thereby decreasing red cell sickling and hemolysis. Oxidative stress plays a central role in the pathophysiology of SCD and L‐glutamine, and its approval for patients aged 5 years and older, is supported by clinical data showing a reduction in vaso‐occlusive crises and hospitalization rates. L‐glutamine is orally administered and generally well tolerated [30, 31]. While these approaches remain vital, Spain is well‐positioned to adopt gene‐editing therapies for hemoglobinopathies, leveraging its universal healthcare infrastructure and a phased implementation model to balance innovation, cost, and access. CRISPR/Cas9‐based technologies have emerged as transformative tools, particularly for TDT and severe SCD [32, 33].

Clinical trials of exagamglogene autotemcel (Casgevy) and betibeglogene autotemcel (Zynteglo) have demonstrated significant efficacy, with patients achieving transfusion independence and resolution of vaso‐occlusive events [34]. Zynteglo, though withdrawn from the European market in 2022 due to commercial limitations, was approved by the US FDA in August 2022 for TDT in both pediatric and adult patients. Casgevy, the first CRISPR‐based therapy approved in the European Union, United Kingdom, and United States, targets the BCL11A gene to reactivate fetal hemoglobin production, offering a functional cure for eligible patients aged ≥ 12 years (EMA, 2023; FDA, 2023).

Despite promising clinical outcomes, the high cost of these therapies—exceeding $2 million per patient—raises concerns about sustainability and equitable access. Long‐term follow‐up, required to monitor efficacy and safety, further increases the economic burden on public health systems. Cost‐effectiveness analyses and health technology assessments will be essential to guide reimbursement decisions [35].

Ethical and safety considerations also persist. Somatic gene editing carries risks such as off‐target effects and unknown long‐term outcomes, necessitating informed consent and stringent monitoring. Germline editing remains ethically contentious and is not currently pursued in clinical settings [36]. While gene therapy holds substantial promise, its integration into the national health system will depend on careful ethical oversight, economic evaluation, and infrastructural readiness. For addressing ethical and financial challenges of innovative therapies for hemoglobinopathies, following recommendations can be implemented:

Public–private partnerships: Collaborations between biotech companies, research institutions, and governments to share costs and risks.

Local MANUFACTURING: Developing domestic production facilities to reduce dependency on imports and lower costs.

Outcome‐based pricing: Implementing payment models that link costs to patient outcomes ensuring the treatment efficacy and long‐term benefits.

Expansion of clinical trials: Facilitate broader participation in gene therapy trials to improve accessibility.

Regulatory oversight and ethical compliance: Ensure that gene‐editing treatments align with European Union guidelines, particularly concerning ethical implications. To ensure safety, it is necessary to establish robust frameworks to monitor ethical compliance and ensure safety.

Multinational Collaborations and Global Comparisons

Spain's active role in the European Network for Rare and Congenital Anaemias (ENERCA) and other European initiatives, has significantly strengthened its capacity for the research, diagnosis, and management of rare anemias. By collaborating with leading European institutions, Spain has gained access to cutting‐edge technologies, shared expertise, and harmonized guidelines for diagnosing and treating these conditions. ENERCA, established in 2002, has been pivotal in uniting health professionals, researchers, and patient associations across Europe to improve the diagnosis, treatment, and awareness of rare anemias. Spain's leadership in this network has facilitated the development of standardized diagnostic criteria, the creation of specialized databases, and the promotion of multidisciplinary research [37, 38]. These collaborative efforts have not only advanced scientific understanding but also improved patient care through the dissemination of best practices and the training of healthcare professionals. Furthermore, Spain's involvement in ENERCA has positioned Spain as a key contributor to the advancement of knowledge of rare anemias fostering international partnerships, addressing the challenges associated with the low prevalence and high clinical complexity of these disorders. This collaborative approach has facilitated the creation of specialized databases, the promotion of multidisciplinary research, and the establishment of standardized diagnostic criteria, ensuring more accurate and timely identification of rare anemias. After 2016, with the establishment of the European Reference Networks (ERN) for Rare Diseases, ENERCA broadened its scope of healthcare services to include other rare hematological diseases (RHD), rebranding itself as EuroBloodNet [39]. EuroBloodNet, led by France, is continuing to play a pivotal role in the research, diagnosis, and management of rare anemias, contributing significantly to advancements in this field [40]. In 2024, a groundbreaking study led by Spanish researchers demonstrated, for the first time, the efficacy of gene therapy in treating Fanconi anemia, a rare hereditary disorder. This innovative approach involved genetically modifying the patients' bone marrow stem cells and reintroducing them, resulting in the replacement of damaged cells without the need for chemotherapy. The success of this trial represents a significant milestone in developing less aggressive treatments for rare anemias [41].

In addition, Spain has been instrumental in establishing and maintaining comprehensive registries for rare anemias. The SEHOP continues to update its database, now including detailed records of over 1000 patients, enabling advanced cohort studies and policy planning [42]. More recently, The REHem‐AR has enabled detailed descriptions of the management of conditions such as β‐thalassemia, providing valuable insights into patient demographics, complications, and treatment outcomes. This registry has been crucial in analyzing morbidity and mortality rates, thereby informing better clinical practices [8]. Through these efforts, Spain has solidified its position as a leader in the fight against rare anemias, contributing to significant advancements in research, diagnosis, and patient care.

Challenges and Future Directions

Spain has made commendable progress in managing thalassemia and hemoglobinopathies through screening programs, treatment advancements, and multinational collaborations. However, challenges in screening efficiency, in access to diagnostics in treatment accessibility, and patient‐centered care, still persist. Accordingly, regional disparities in hemoglobinopathy care be addressed through four key initiatives.

National registries: REHem‐AR collects data to identify care gaps and improve access to diagnostics and treatment.

Neonatal screening programs: Spain has progressively implemented neonatal screening for hemoglobinopathies in its autonomous community's. Early detection through these programs allows for timely intervention, which is crucial for effective management and reducing long‐term complications associated with these disorders.

Specialized treatment centers: The Spanish Ministry of Health has designated Reference Centers, Services, and Units (CSUR) for hereditary erythropathology to provide expert, multidisciplinary care for rare hematological diseases, ensuring consistent, and high‐quality treatment nationwide. There are, at least, three CSUR‐designated units for hereditary erythropathology. Notably, the SJD Barcelona Children's Hospital (Hospital Sant Joan de Deu), in collaboration with Hospital de la Santa Creu i Sant Pau, and, more recently, the Vall d'Hebron University Hospital. t‐Through these concerted efforts, Spain aims to minimize regional disparities and ensure that all patients with hemoglobinopathies receive consistent, high‐quality care regardless of their location.

Integration of advanced therapies: Spain is incorporating gene‐editing treatments through strategic public healthcare integration, cost management (public–private partnerships, local production, and negotiated pricing), expanded genetic screening, decentralized care, long‐term monitoring via national registries, regulatory oversight, and public education. However, the approach to gene‐editing will require clear regulatory policies. (a) Ethical oversight: ensuring compliance with EU‐wide and national guidelines on gene‐editing technologies, particularly for germline interventions. (b) Accelerated approvals: Streamlining clinical trial approvals for somatic gene therapies, with stringent postmarket surveillance.

Patient‐centered outcomes: The clinical burden of hemoglobinopathies extends beyond medical management to significantly impact patient quality of life. Incorporating patient perspectives into research and healthcare strategies is essential for a holistic approach. Despite the availability of treatments, many patients report challenges in daily life, including: (a) Chronic pain and fatigue: SCD patients frequently experience pain crises, leading to physical and emotional distress, (b) Psychological distress and social stigma: which are often underrepresented in clinical discussions, and (c) Healthcare Navigation Barriers: Complex treatment regimens require multidisciplinary care, which is not always accessible or coordinated effectively.

To address these challenges, Spain's healthcare system should consider implementing: (a) Quality of life assessments: Routine evaluations using validated tools such as the SF‐36 or PedsQL can help monitor the psychosocial burden of the disease [43]. (b) Psychosocial support services: Expand access to mental health professionals and patient support groups, particularly in underserved regions. (c) Personalized treatment plans: Integrate patient‐reported outcomes into clinical decision‐making of healthcare providers to tailor treatments to individual needs and preferences and to improve patient's adherence and satisfaction. (d) Telemedicine initiatives: Leverage digital platforms for remote monitoring and consultations, reducing the burden on patients requiring frequent follow‐ups.

Addressing these concerns will lead to a more holistic and patient‐centered approach to hemoglobinopathy management in Spain

Conclusions

Throughout the last 5 years, thanks to the implementation of The Spanish Undiagnosed Rare Diseases Program, a great push has been provided to the implementation of neonatal screening for hemoglobinopathies and especially for SCD. After the dissemination of neonatal screening to most of the autonomous communities and the inclusion of patients of all ages in the registry, a high number of cases have been registered to allow the follow‐up of each one of them and to increase their survival by more than 95.5% at 20 years of age in SCD, and 96.7% in thalassemia major. The NBS has become the most widely used diagnostic method, which has made it possible to pave the way for future cohort studies and to be able to compare data with other European registries, as has been done during the first stage of the ENERCA project. This has greatly contributed to the awareness of doctors and the general population against this imported disease that affects a large number of European countries. It is also important to mention that with this new dimension adopted to face the health problem, a great advance has been made in the process of the transition of SCD patients from childhood to adulthood through communication between pediatric hematologists and their adult counterparts. Moreover, this has facilitated the possession of evolutionary data from the beginning, which may have an impact on the creation of new protocols and updating of existing guidelines and recommendations. The consolidation of the registry led by SEHOP will make it possible to propose the realization of new multicenter studies since just by keeping the registry up to date, the epidemiological studies that are deemed necessary can be promoted by the pertinent health authorities.
