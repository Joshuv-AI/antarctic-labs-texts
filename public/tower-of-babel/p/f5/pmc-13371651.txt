Cross-sectional study on the knowledge, attitude, and experience of emergency contraception among female students of the University of Sierra Leone
Authors: Michael Lahai, Isha Abie Kamara, Ahmed Vandy, Eugene Conteh, Mohamed Bawoh, Marie Kolipha-Kamara, Foday Marah, Mohamed H Samai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmergency contraception (EC) is a safe and effective method of preventing pregnancy after unprotected sexual intercourse, but its use requires good knowledge and a positive attitude. This study assessed the knowledge, attitude, and experience of EC among female students of childbearing age at the University of Sierra Leone.MethodsThis cross-sectional study was conducted at the three colleges of the University of Sierra Leone: Fourah Bay College, College of Medicine and Allied Health Sciences, and the Institute of Public Administration Management. A self-administered questionnaire was used to collect data from the 320 female undergraduate students. Knowledge about EC was measured and scored as “good knowledge” and “poor knowledge”. An independent sample t-test was used to determine the significant difference between the socio-demographic characteristics and knowledge score, with statistical significance shown from a p-value < 0.05. The data was analysed using Statistical Package for Social Sciences 21.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 24.1 years. More than half of the participants (58.9%) had good knowledge of EC, with significant differences in knowledge scores observed by age and academic discipline. Older students (25–64 years) scored higher (M = 63.26, SD = 21.92) than younger students (18–24 years) (M = 55.93, SD = 21.56; p = 0.003). Health science students also demonstrated greater knowledge (M = 70.25, SD = 25.35) compared to non‑health science students (M = 55.42, SD = 19.95; p < 0.001). Most of the respondents (85.6%) were sexually active, and more than half of them had previously used EC, in the form of I-pills (99.5%). Only 7.8% reported experiencing an unintended pregnancy. While 65.9% believed EC to be safe, misconceptions persisted: 54.4% viewed EC as a form of abortion, 56.9% considered it sinful, and 63.2% believed it promotes promiscuity.ConclusionMore than half of the students demonstrated good knowledge of emergency contraception, with higher scores among older and health science participants. Misconceptions about safety and morality remain widespread. Expanding tailored education, peer‑led initiatives, and integrating sexual health into non‑health science curricula, alongside accessible and respectful services and community engagement, is essential to dispel myths, reduce stigma, and promote informed use among young women.Keywords: Contraception, Emergency contraceptive, Birth control, Pregnancy, University of Sierra Leone

Background

The global landscape of unintended pregnancies remains a critical public health concern, with approximately 250 million pregnancies occurring annually, of which about one-third are unintended and nearly 20% of this number result in induced abortions [1]. It has been estimated that over 3.9 million adolescent girls across the globe undergo unsafe abortions, contributing significantly to maternal mortality and health complications [2].

Emergency contraception (EC) offers a safe and effective means of preventing unintended pregnancies following unprotected sexual intercourse or contraceptive failure. If utilised within 72 h or even up to 120 h in some cases after intercourse, it has the potential to prevent 75% to 95% of pregnancies [3–5]. EC can be provided in the form of pills (levonorgestrel or ulipristal acetate) or intrauterine devices (IUDs) such as copper IUDs [6]. Emergency Contraceptive pills are widely available in many countries, including Sierra Leone, where they can be obtained over the counter from pharmacies or health facilities [7].

Sierra Leone, a low-income country in West Africa, faces a high burden of maternal mortality and morbidity [8, 9]. According to the 2019 Sierra Leone Demographic and Health Survey, 37.2% of women were aware of EC, and its use tends to be higher in educated women compared to those without education [10]. The use of modern contraceptives among married women was also reported to have increased from 7% in 2008 to 21% in 2019, with higher uptake in urban areas 26% than in rural settings 18% [10].

Despite the availability of EC, their utilisation remains low in sub-Saharan Africa, given the high rate of unwanted adolescent pregnancies in this region [11]. For instance, in Sierra Leone, more than 86% of girls aged 15 to 19 years have never used contraceptives, and by age 19, 30% would have given birth [12]. University students represent a particularly important group, as they are generally within the reproductive age bracket, often sexually active, and may face unique barriers to contraceptive use such as stigma, misinformation, cost, and provider attitudes [13–15]. Evidence indicates that nearly 1 in 5 college students encounter barriers to obtaining hormonal contraception, while 1 in 4 reports going without contraception altogether [15]. These gaps underscore the importance of understanding EC knowledge, attitudes, and experiences among university populations, where improved access and awareness could directly reduce unintended pregnancies and their associated health consequences.

This study aimed to evaluate the knowledge, attitude, and experience of EC use among female students of childbearing age at the University of Sierra Leone. The study also examined the sociodemographic differences in knowledge among the participants. The findings provide evidence on the factors shaping awareness and utilisation of EC, which will inform reproductive health programming and policy interventions aimed at improving EC access and uptake among young women in higher education.

Method

Study setting, design, and period

A quantitative cross-sectional study was conducted from April to October 2021, among undergraduate female students at the University of Sierra Leone (USL), which includes three colleges: Fourah Bay College (FBC), College of Medicine and Allied Health Sciences (COMAHS), and Institute of Public Administration Management (IPAM).

Sampling method

A multistage sampling technique was employed. Firstly, departments from the three institutions were listed, followed by the categorisation of all departments into health science and non-health science. One health science and one non-health science department per university was selected (except for COMAHS, which lacks a non-health science department). Finally, two classes were randomly chosen from each selected department of the institution for participation.

Data collection and analysis

Data collection was done using a self-administered questionnaire adapted from a study conducted in southwest Ethiopia by Shiferaw et al. and modified to our study context [17]. The tool covered four domains: socio‑demographic characteristics, knowledge, attitudes, and experiences of emergency contraception. It was pre‑tested with 16 students to ensure clarity and cultural relevance.

Knowledge assessment was done using ten structured multiple‑choice questions. Each correct answer was awarded one point, with a cumulative maximum of ten. Respondents scoring above five were categorised as having “good knowledge,” while those scoring five or below were classified as having “poor knowledge.” This grading criterion was consistent with prior studies on EC in Ethiopia [17]. Attitudes toward EC were measured using a five‑point Likert scale, later collapsed into three categories (agree, neutral, disagree) for ease of analysis. Experiences of EC use were assessed quantitatively through structured questions on sexual and reproductive history, prior contraceptive use, EC utilisation, perceived safety, reported side effects, and sources of information.

Data were cleaned and imported into the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) version 21 for analysis. A normality check was done using histograms, Q‑Q plots, and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Independent sample t‑tests were used to examine differences in knowledge scores across socio‑demographic variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethics statement

The study protocol went through a full review and was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the College of Medicine and Allied Health Sciences, University of Sierra Leone, with IRB number COMAHS/IRB/003/2024. The participants were informed about the purpose of the study and their voluntary rights to participate or withdraw from the study at any time without any consequences. Written informed consent was obtained from each participant before administering the questionnaire. The data collected from the participants were treated with confidentiality and anonymity by coding identifiable details of the participants. The data was stored and used only for the analysis of the study findings. The principles of the Declaration of Helsinki regarding the ethical conduct of research involving human participants were adhered to in this study.

Results

Socio-demographic and other characteristics of the participants

A total of 320 female students participated in the study. The average age of the respondents was 24.1 years, with the youngest and oldest being 18 years and 49 years, respectively. Most of the participants (75%) were in the non-health science department at FBC (43.8%). The majority of the respondents (65.9%) were Christians and were unmarried (83.4%). About 58.9% of the respondents had good knowledge of contraception (Table 1).

Discussion

This study assessed the knowledge, attitude, and experience of female students at the University of Sierra Leone towards EC. The findings provide valuable insights into the current situation for promoting safe and effective contraceptive practices.

A total of 320 female undergraduate students from diverse backgrounds participated in this study. Most reported being sexually active, and a majority had already used emergency contraception. More than half of the participants demonstrated good knowledge of EC, a level higher than that reported in earlier studies from Mizan‑Tepi University in Ethiopia and Northwestern Nigeria [18, 19]. In the present study, significant differences in knowledge scores were observed by age and academic discipline, with older students and those enrolled in health sciences scoring higher than younger and non‑health science students. The age range and amount of health science students in our study can be a possible reason for more participants having good knowledge about EC in comparison with previous studies done at Mizan-Tepi University, South West Ethiopia and at a Tertiary Institution in Northwestern Nigeria, where the majority of the participants were less than 24 years and had fewer amounts of health science students [18, 19]. Health science students have more privileges to learn and acquire information about EC through their curriculum learning and other academic activities [20]. This is further supported by findings seen in other studies conducted in Kenya among undergraduate health science students, for which 72.9% had good knowledge about EC, and in Spain, where all of the nursing students (100%) had good knowledge and positive attitudes towards EC [21, 22].

Although most of the participants in this study have experienced unprotected intercourse at some point, only 15% got pregnant, of which half were unintended. The widespread use of EC among most of the participants in this study can be used as a likely justification for the fewer cases of unintended pregnancies reported as compared to a study conducted in Ethiopia, where less than half of the participants had used EC, one-third had experienced pregnancy before the age of 20 years, and 75.3% of this was unintended [23].

While contraceptive failure accounted for approximately 15% of unintended pregnancies in a comparable study from Northwestern Nigeria, evidence consistently shows that EC remains a safe and reliable option. When taken correctly after unprotected intercourse, EC can reduce the risk of pregnancy by an estimated 75% to 95%, offering individuals a critical window of protection and reassurance [3, 4, 18]. As unsafe sex practices are common among young adults, especially in universities and other higher institutions, preventing unintended pregnancy is essential for their health [24]. Unintended pregnancy can have various negative impacts, such as reducing the educational opportunities of young women, affecting their labour-market prospects, increasing crime rates, leading to more abortions, creating more household stress, and other related outcomes [25].

The majority of the participants in this study were certain about the safety of EC, which is contrary to a study conducted in Cameroon, where most of the participants believed EC is unsafe [26]. However, some participants in our study believed EC can cause side effects such as infertility (52.8%) and irregular menstruation (20.9%). Similar side effects were reported by participants in a study conducted at a private university in Nigeria [27]. The attitudes assessment revealed that most of the respondents agreed with the right of all females to EC, and the use of EC after unsafe sexual intercourse can prevent unwanted pregnancy. More than half of the participants believe that EC may cause infertility in a woman. Although a Cameroon study found that levonorgestrel, an EC pill, may reduce tubal motility and increase ectopic pregnancy risk [28], a trial of 332 pregnant women who took levonorgestrel-only Emergency Contraceptive pills found no higher risk of birth abnormalities [29]. Fear of side effects was one of the reported reasons for not using EC in a study conducted in Northern Nigeria [18]. To mitigate the risk of side effects and public health safety, it is worth noting that ECs are not recommended for regular use, as side effects like menstrual irregularities have been reported from their frequent use; however, this can be minimised by limiting their frequent use [30]. Many other contraceptive methods can be used on a regular basis instead of EC. Other misconceptions and negative attitudes towards the use of EC in this study are religious and judgmental views, which continue to make the use of EC seem like a taboo in our society [31]. EC should be accessed and utilised by adolescents without prejudice.

In general, higher education female students are exposed to many sexual and reproductive health problems, among which is unintended pregnancy, which could be prevented by early use of EC. However, as shown in this study, there are still some knowledge gaps and the utilisation of EC among University of Sierra Leone students is limited; therefore, policymakers should consider ways of developing appropriate evidence-based learning strategies to provide more insight to higher learning institutions on sexual reproductive health and the use of EC.

This study has limitations, including a small sample size, which restricts the generalizability of findings to all universities in Sierra Leone, particularly rural areas where EC access and perceptions may differ. The sensitivity of the topic may have led some students to withhold information or provide socially desirable responses, potentially introducing bias.

Conclusion and recommendations

This study found that more than half of the female students demonstrated good knowledge of emergency contraception and many had previously used it, most often in the form of I‑pills. Despite this, important gaps remain, with misconceptions and negative attitudes still shaping practice. Differences in knowledge were evident by age and academic discipline, with older students and those in health sciences scoring higher than their younger and non‑health science peers. Peer networks, particularly female friends, were the dominant source of information, underscoring the informal pathways through which EC knowledge is shared.

To address these gaps, tailored education and awareness programs are needed. Peer‑led workshops and the integration of sexual health education into non‑health science curricula could strengthen knowledge and reduce misconceptions. Public awareness campaigns led by the Ministry of Health and Sanitation, using digital platforms, social media, and youth influencers, should clarify EC’s safety and mechanism of action, countering myths such as infertility or equivalence with abortion. Ensuring EC is more available and affordable, alongside training health care providers to deliver services respectfully and without judgment, will further improve uptake. Finally, engaging religious and community leaders can help reduce stigma and address moral concerns, fostering more supportive environments for young women’s reproductive health.
