Data-Driven Spectral Prediction of Black Dyeing in Recycled Polymer Microfibers via Multi-Output Regression
Authors: Hyeokjun Cho, Seung Geol Lee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study presents a spectrum-based data-driven modeling framework for predicting the wavelength-dependent K/S spectrum from dye recipes in the dyeing process of black dope-dyed recycled microfiber fabrics. Instead of relying only on scalar color coordinates, the proposed framework directly predicts the K/S spectrum over the 400–700 nm range. This enables a more direct interpretation of color reproducibility while preserving the physical basis of color formation. Using samples dyed with combinations of yellow, red, and blue dyes, the problem was formulated as a multi-output regression task in which the K/S value at each wavelength was treated as an output. Because black-shade data are characterized by high inter-wavelength correlation and limited spectral variation, eight regression models were systematically compared under these constrained conditions, and partial least-squares regression (PLSR) was selected as the final model. The selected model showed high agreement between the predicted and measured K/S spectra. When the predicted spectra were converted into CIELAB coordinates, the mean color difference between predicted and measured colors was 0.79 in terms of ΔEab* , indicating a high level of color reproducibility. These results show that the proposed framework can effectively model black-shade dyeing data with a limited number of samples while maintaining both spectral agreement and perceptual accuracy. The framework provides practical support for data-driven dye recipe design and quality validation and may contribute to improved color control, process efficiency, and reproducibility in polymer-based microfiber dyeing processes.

Introduction

Accurate color control is essential in textile dyeing, and this requirement becomes particularly critical for black shades because even small color differences can lead to a noticeable loss of visual uniformity. − Black shades are often produced using different dye combinations, and similar final appearances may be obtained even when the recipe differs. − This difficulty becomes more pronounced when additional dyeing is applied to black dope-dyed yarn, as in this study, because the substrate is already located within a narrowly constrained black-shade region. ,, Under these conditions, practical recipe adjustment can be regarded as a low-variance inverse problem, in which different dye combinations may produce subtle differences in appearance. As a result, repeated dyeing and color measurement are often required in practice, leading to inefficient use of time and resources. − These limitations highlight the need for a data-driven method that can reliably model the relationship between dye combinations and color response while reducing dependence on trial-and-error experimentation. −

Most previous prediction studies in textile coloration have focused on color coordinates such as L*, a*, and b*, or on color-difference values derived from them. , Although these descriptors are useful for summarizing color, they do not fully capture the underlying spectral characteristics of color formation. A more direct way to represent color is through visible-range spectral measurement, because the reflectance property at each wavelength is physically linked to visual color perception. Therefore, instead of predicting only single color-coordinate values, predicting the full spectrum across the 400–700 nm range makes it possible to address the physical basis of color reproduction more directly. This is particularly important for black dope-dyed systems, where spectral shapes are closely clustered and overall variation is limited, so that subtle spectral differences may accumulate and appear as perceptible differences in final appearance. − In this study, the wavelength-dependent K/S spectra of dyed samples were defined as the prediction target, and the problem was formulated as a multi-output regression task that directly estimates the spectrum from combinations of dye concentrations.

Multi-output spectral prediction differs from general single-output regression because the outputs are strongly correlated across wavelengths. The K/S values at neighboring wavelengths often show high correlation due to the continuity of the spectral pattern, and this tendency becomes more pronounced in black-shade data, where the overall spectra remain similar even when the dye combinations differ. − Conventional regression approaches may be limited in this setting because they often emphasize residual minimization without fully exploiting the covariance structure across wavelengths, particularly under small-sample and low-variance conditions. − A key unresolved challenge, therefore, is to develop a model that can directly predict the full wavelength-dependent spectrum from dye recipes while remaining robust under these constrained conditions.

One representative method that can address this requirement is partial least-squares regression (PLSR). Unlike conventional regression methods that primarily focus on minimizing output residuals, PLSR constructs latent variables by reflecting the covariance structure between inputs and outputs. − This characteristic is advantageous for spectral prediction problems in which both the input variables and the wavelength-wise outputs are strongly correlated. In particular, such a latent-variable framework can be beneficial for small-sample black-shade data, where subtle spectral differences must be predicted from highly constrained recipe-response relationships.

In this study, the K/S spectra in the 400–700 nm range were obtained from black dope–dyed fabrics additionally dyed with combinations of three dyes, yellow (Y), red (R), and blue (B), and these spectra were used as the prediction target. More specifically, this study formulated dyeing as a multi-output spectral prediction problem, systematically compared eight regression models under constrained black-shade conditions, and identified PLSR as the most appropriate model for the present task, demonstrating its effectiveness in capturing latent spectral correlations. Model performance was evaluated through cross-validation, wavelength-wise error analysis, and residual analysis. In addition, the predicted K/S spectra were converted into CIELAB coordinates so that the spectral prediction error could also be interpreted in terms of final color quality.

Because black-shade spectra are closely clustered, average performance metrics alone may not sufficiently explain model stability or the response of the predicted spectra to changes in the inputs. Therefore, local sensitivity analysis was performed to examine how small changes in Y, R, and B affect the predicted K/S spectra across the full wavelength range. In addition, the variable importance in projection (VIP) score was used as an interpretive metric to compare the relative contribution of each input term to the overall explanatory power of the model from the perspective of the full wavelength range. − These analyses were intended to complement predictive performance evaluation by providing a model-based interpretation of how dye combinations are reflected in the spectral response.

The objective of this study is to develop a dye recipe-based prediction model for wavelength-dependent K/S spectra and to quantitatively evaluate its performance using black-shade dyeing data characterized by a limited number of samples, low spectral variance, and strong output correlation. The proposed framework provides a basis for shifting recipe design from repeated dyeing and color measurement to a more data-driven workflow, with potential benefits in lead-time reduction, reproducibility, resource efficiency, and sustainability.

Experimental Section

Materials and Reagents

The fabric used in this study was prepared by blending 50% recycled polyethylene terephthalate (rPET) microfiber and 50% polycyclohexylene dimethylene terephthalate (PCT) microfiber, followed by impregnation with 30% polyurethane (PU) resin. To evaluate changes in the color properties of black fabrics, dyeing was carried out using three dyes, C.I. Solvent Yellow 163, C.I. Disperse Red 86, and C.I. Disperse Blue 77. Each dye was applied at various concentrations, and the dye bath was prepared by adding the dispersing agent Sunsolt RM-340S (NICCA KOREA Co., Ltd., Japan) and acetic acid CH3COOH (SAMIL Co., Ltd., Korea). After dyeing, reduction clearing was performed to remove unfixed dyes remaining on the fabric surface. The reduction clearing solution was prepared using the reducing agent Sera Con M-FAS (DyStar, Singapore) and alkali NaOH (SAMIL Co., Ltd., Korea).

Dyeing and Reduction Clearing Procedure

The dye bath used for dyeing was prepared at a liquor ratio of 1:20 by adding 2% dispersing agent at 7 mL/L and 5% acetic acid until the pH of the dye bath reached 4.8. Dyeing was carried out using an infrared dyeing machine (DL-6000, Daelim Starlet Co., Korea) at 110 °C for 40 min. Reduction clearing was performed under the same liquor ratio of 1:20 after adding 6 g/L of reducing agent and 6 mL/L of NaOH, and the treatment was maintained at 80 °C for 20 min. After reduction clearing, the fabrics were rinsed twice with hot water at 60 °C and once with water at 25 °C, and then dried at 70 °C for 45 min using a convection oven (OF4-P, JeioTech, Korea) to obtain the final dyed fabrics. Color Measurement

To evaluate the dyeability of the dyed fabrics, surface reflectance was measured in the visible range of 400–700 at 20 nm intervals using a spectrophotometric color measurement instrument (SCINCO Co., Ltd., Seoul, Korea) under a 10° observer and D65 illuminant, and the K/S value at each wavelength was obtained. The K/S value was calculated using eq . 1 KS=(1−R)22R where K, S, and R denote the absorption coefficient, scattering coefficient, and reflectance, respectively. To minimize measurement error, each fabric sample was measured four times, and the average value was used as the final data.

Data Set Construction

This study constructed a data set for predicting the K/S spectrum of each sample after dyeing black dope-dyed yarn fabrics under 100 conditions generated by randomly combining three dyes, Y, R, and B. Because of the characteristics of black shades, the spectral shapes were observed to cluster within a limited range even when the dye combinations differed, and this clustering behavior defines the characteristics of the data set used in this study. The input variables consisted of dye concentration combinations defined by the dyeing recipe of each sample, and each sample was represented by the concentration values of three dyes. The output variable was the K/S spectrum measured in the visible range, and the K/S values obtained from 400 to 700 at 20 nm intervals were organized into a single vector. Accordingly, the output of each sample was defined as a multi-output vector composed of K/S values at 16 wavelength points. A visual overview of the data is presented in Figure .

Machine Learning Approach

The prediction target in this study was a multidimensional output composed of wavelength-dependent K/S values, with high correlation among output dimensions due to the continuity between neighboring wavelengths. In addition, because the spectral distribution is clustered in data based on black dope-dyed yarn, a modeling strategy that can generalize stably from a limited number of samples is required. Reflecting these characteristics, this study formulated the problem as a multi-output regression task using 100 dyed fabric samples and selected the final model after comparing several regression algorithms under the same data-splitting condition, in which 80 samples were used for training and 20 samples were used for testing (Train: Test = 80:20).

The model inputs consisted of dye concentration combinations defined in each recipe. To account for the possibility that the spectral response to recipe changes may not follow only a simple linear pattern, the inputs were expanded using second-order polynomial terms. Specifically, all second-order polynomial combinations of Y, R, and B were included without a bias term. Therefore, the final input vector was composed of nine terms, Y, R, B, Y2, R2, B2, Y·R, Y·B, and R·B. No subset selection was applied to these polynomial terms, and no third-order or higher-order terms were included. This expansion was used to reflect limited nonlinear concentration effects and pairwise dye-dye interaction effects while avoiding excessive model complexity relative to the sample size. The expanded input variables were standardized to prevent differences in scale from affecting model training, and the same transformation was applied consistently to both the training and test sets. The output K/S spectrum was represented as a single vector of wavelength-dependent values and used in a multi-output form for model training.

PLSR was adopted as the final model. PLSR constructs latent variables by jointly considering the relationship between the input matrix X and the output matrix Y, and these latent variables are defined to capture a large covariance between X and Y. This characteristic can be advantageous in problems with high correlation among output dimensions, such as multi-wavelength spectra, and it may also allow relatively stable learning in situations where multicollinearity among input variables and a limited sample size are present at the same time. − In addition, because PLSR performs prediction by summarizing input information through latent variables, it is also suitable for preventing the prediction response to small input changes from becoming excessively unstable in the clustered black spectral region.

To provide a conventional reference for evaluating the practical benefit of the proposed data-driven framework, an additional baseline model was constructed based on the K/S additivity concept of the Kubelka–Munk (K-M) framework. In conventional color matching, K-M-based approaches commonly relate dye concentration to wavelength-wise K/S values through additive color-strength contributions. However, the data set used in this study consisted of mixed-dye recipes and final K/S spectra, rather than independently measured single-dye calibration spectra. Therefore, a full K-M recipe prediction model was not directly constructed. Instead, a K-M-inspired additive baseline was used as a conventional reference model. This baseline assumes that the wavelength-wise K/S value can be approximated by the additive contributions of Y, R, and B concentrations, as expressed in eq . 2 (K/S)λ=β0,λ+βY,λY+βR,λR+βB,λB where (K/S)λ is the K/S value at wavelength λ, β 0,λ is the baseline term, and β Y,λ, β R,λ, and β B,λ are the wavelength-wise additive coefficients for Y, R, and B, respectively.

Evaluation Metrics for Prediction Performance

In this study, mean absolute error (MAE), root-mean-square error (RMSE), and coefficient of determination (R) were used as the basic performance metrics to quantitatively evaluate the accuracy of the predicted K/S spectra. Because the prediction target consisted of an output vector composed of multi-wavelength K/S values in the 400–700 nm range, the performance metrics were presented by aggregating the wavelength-wise values to summarize the overall error level across the full wavelength range. Each metric was calculated on the test set using the measured K/S values and the corresponding predictions generated by the model.

MAE is the mean of absolute prediction errors, and it allows the average magnitude of the prediction error to be interpreted intuitively without being overly sensitive to outliers. RMSE is the square root of the mean of squared errors, and it gives a larger penalty to large errors, making it useful for identifying whether large prediction errors occur at specific wavelength ranges. In this study, MAE and RMSE were presented together to evaluate both the average error level and the sensitivity to large errors. R2 indicates how much of the variance in the measured values is explained by the prediction, and it was used to compare the explanatory power of the model across wavelengths. The equations for MAE, RMSE, and R2 are given in eq , , and , respectively. 3 MAE=1n∑i=1n|yi−ŷi| 4 RMSE=1n∑i=1n(yi−ŷi)2 5 R2=ESSTSS=1−RSSTSS=1−∑i=1n(yi−ŷi)2∑i=1n(yi−y̅)2 where, n is the number of observations, yi is the measured value, ŷ i is the predicted value, and y̅ is the mean of the measured values. ESS, RSS, and TSS represent the explained sum of squares, residual sum of squares, and total sum of squares, respectively. In addition, mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) was used as an additional metric. Because MAPE is a relative error metric that normalizes the error by the measured value, it can be useful for comparing relative performance across wavelength ranges with large differences in K/S magnitude. Using these metrics, the predicted and measured spectra for the entire test set were compared, and the residual distribution was analyzed together to further evaluate prediction characteristics of the model that could not be fully captured by a single summary metric.

Results and Discussion

Model Comparison across Various Algorithms

To predict the K/S spectrum in the 400–700 nm range, consisting of 16 wavelength points, from dye recipe-based inputs, eight regression algorithms, namely PLSR, random forest (RF), extra trees (ET), support vector machine (SVM), k-nearest neighbor (kNN), multilayer perceptron (MLP), XGBoost (XGB), and Gaussian process regression (GPR), were compared under the same conditions. All models were configured to share the same input variable structure and preprocessing framework, and for multi-output spectral prediction, each model generated predictions for all wavelengths simultaneously. A comparison of the eight regression algorithms is presented in Figure .

Comparison of eight regression algorithms for multi-output K/S spectrum prediction (a) actual K/S spectra of the test set, predicted K/S spectra generated by (b) PLSR, (c) RF, (d) ET, (e) SVM, (f) kNN, (g) MLP, (h) XGB, and (i) GPR.

Figure a shows the measured K/S spectra of the test set, whereas Figure b–i show the spectra predicted by PLSR, RF, ET, SVM, kNN, MLP, XGB, and GPR, respectively. In terms of overall quantitative performance, PLSR showed the highest mean R2 and the lowest MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, indicating the most balanced performance on this data set. ET also showed performance close to that of PLSR in terms of mean R2 and RMSE, and SVM showed relatively strong performance in mean R2 and MAPE, suggesting that kernel-based regression can also be competitive for spectral prediction. In contrast, kNN showed the least favorable mean R2, RMSE, and MAPE, indicating that simple distance-based local estimation may have difficulty providing stable generalization under conditions such as those in this study, where correlation among output dimensions is high and spectral shapes are closely clustered. Under these data conditions, models that can use the strong correlation structure of spectral outputs while maintaining a relatively low risk of overfitting in a small-sample setting showed an advantage, and PLSR provided the most consistent performance among them. In addition, to examine whether the above comparison was overly affected by a specific data split, 5-fold cross-validation (CV) was further performed, and the results are presented in Figure S1.

Figure S1 summarizes the mean and standard deviation of the performance metrics obtained from each fold, where Figure S1a–d present R2, MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, respectively. The 5-fold CV results also showed that PLSR provided the best and most consistent performance. PLSR achieved the highest mean R2 of 0.8533 ± 0.0224, with the smallest variation across folds. In addition, it showed the lowest error values, with an MAE of 0.3009 ± 0.0281, an RMSE of 0.3733 ± 0.0312, and a MAPE of 2.5236 ± 0.2413%. Therefore, the superiority and stability of PLSR were confirmed again in the 5-fold CV results, and further analysis of wavelength-wise performance variation and residual structure was conducted based on PLSR.

Wavelength-Dependent Prediction Performance

Before examining the wavelength-dependent prediction performance of PLSR, the number of latent variables in the PLSR model was determined by 5-fold CV. Because the polynomial expansion generated nine input terms, the candidate number of components was examined from 1 to 9. As shown in Figure S2, the CV prediction performance varied depending on the number of components, and the lowest RMSE was obtained when eight components were used. Therefore, eight latent variables were retained in the final PLSR model for subsequent wavelength-wise prediction analysis. Since the absolute values and ranges of variation in the K/S spectrum can differ across wavelengths, the prediction performance of PLSR was examined separately for each wavelength. Figure presents scatter plots comparing the measured and predicted K/S values at each wavelength in the test set, together with the linear regression equation and R2 for each wavelength.

Wavelength-wise linear regression results between true and PLSR-predicted K/S values at 400–700 nm.

Across the full wavelength range, the measured values and the values predicted by PLSR showed generally good linear agreement, with R2 ranging from 0.7200 at 400 nm to 0.9699 at 620 nm. In particular, high explanatory power was observed in the 580–640 nm range, and the highest R2 values were obtained at 600 and 620 nm, with values of 0.9670 and 0.9699, respectively. In contrast, the lowest R2 was found at 400 nm (0.7200), and relatively low R2 values were also observed at 480 and 500 nm, at 0.7513 and 0.7532, respectively, indicating that prediction performance varied with wavelength. The slope and intercept of the regression line indicate the tendency of systematic bias at each wavelength. At 480 and 500 nm, the regression slopes were 0.7819 and 0.7042, and the intercepts were 2.5608 and 3.4188, respectively, indicating that the predictions tended to compress the actual range of variation to some extent. In contrast, at 600 and 620 nm, the slopes were close to 1, at 0.9651 and 0.9789, and the intercepts were small, at 0.2844 and 0.1132, indicating relatively small systematic bias and the best linear agreement in these wavelength ranges. At 700 nm, the slope was 0.8585 and the intercept was 1.2738, suggesting that the compressive tendency increased again to some extent. In addition, Figure presents the wavelength-wise error metrics based on the K/S prediction results obtained by PLSR at each wavelength.

Wavelength-dependent prediction error of the PLSR model (a) MAE, (b) RMSE, and (c) MAPE across 400–700 nm.

Figure a–c presents MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, respectively. Across the full wavelength range, MAE ranged from 0.1881 at 700 nm to 0.4198 at 540 nm, and RMSE ranged from 0.2336 at 700 nm to 0.4929 at 520 nm. MAPE ranged from 1.6500 at 400 nm to 3.4330 at 520 nm and remained within 3.5% across all wavelengths, although a clear increase was observed around 520–540 nm. In contrast, MAPE was relatively low at 400–460 and 700 nm, with values of 1.6500–1.9710% and 1.8920%, respectively, indicating relatively stable prediction in these ranges.

The relatively increased error around 520–540 nm may be associated with a spectral transition region where the contributions of multiple dyes overlap, rather than with the independent effect of a single dye. However, because the individual absorption spectra of the dyes were not independently measured in this study, the relationship between this wavelength range and the absorption maximum of a specific dye should be interpreted cautiously.

Residual analysis was further conducted to examine how these wavelength-wise differences appeared in the residual structure and whether the errors were concentrated in specific samples.

Residual Analysis

Although the average performance metrics of PLSR summarize the overall prediction accuracy, spectral prediction also requires examination of sample-level reproducibility and wavelength-wise errors. Accordingly, for the 20 test samples, the comparison between measured and predicted spectra, the evaluation of shape similarity based on spectral angle mapper (SAM), and the wavelength-wise residual distribution was analyzed in sequence. Here, the residual was defined as the difference between the measured K/S value and the predicted K/S value, that is, K/S true – K/S pred . To compare the measured and predicted spectra, Figure presents the wavelength-wise measured K/S values and PLSR-predicted K/S values for the 20 test samples.

Comparison of true and PLSR-predicted K/S spectra for test samples.

Overall, the predicted spectra showed similar curve shapes and wavelength-dependent trends to the measured spectra, and the two curves were closely overlapped for most samples. However, in some samples, the deviation of the predicted curve from the measured curve was relatively more pronounced around 500–560 nm, which is consistent with the previously observed wavelength–dependent region of reduced performance. In addition, to quantify spectral shape reproducibility, the angle between the measured spectral vector and the predicted spectral vector of each sample was calculated, and SAM was used to evaluate the similarity between the two spectra. SAM was calculated using eq . 7 SAMi=cos−1(Strue,i×Spred,i∥Strue,i∥∥Spred,i∥) where, S true,i and Spred,i denote the measured spectral vector and the predicted spectral vector, respectively, and ||S true,i || and ||S pred,i || denote the magnitude of each vector. SAM evaluates shape similarity by expressing the directional difference between two spectral vectors as an angle, and a smaller value indicates a smaller relative shape difference between the predicted and measured spectra. , The SAM results calculated in this way are presented in Figure S3.

The SAM values of the test samples ranged from 0.0089 to 0.0308 radians (0.5099–1.7647°), with a mean of 0.0191 ± 0.0069 radians (1.0943 ± 0.3953°). Compared with the reference line of Y = 0.1 radians (5.7°) shown in Figure S3, all samples were well below this threshold, indicating that the PLSR predictions consistently maintained high similarity in terms of spectral shape. The smallest SAM was observed for Test_10 at 0.0089 radians (0.5099°), whereas the largest SAM was observed for Test_2 at 0.0308 radians (1.7647°). In other words, even when errors occurred in individual samples, the overall spectral shape was not greatly distorted, and the difference was more likely to appear as a magnitude difference in local wavelength ranges. In addition, to examine the regions where such local errors occurred and to identify the characteristics of the wavelength-wise residual distribution, the wavelength-wise residuals of the test samples are presented in Figure S4.

Across all wavelengths and all samples, the residuals were generally distributed around zero and showed small amplitudes in most regions without large systematic bias. The range of the 320 residual values was −0.8088 to 1.0391, indicating that locally large magnitude errors could occur in some regions. However, these cases were limited, and the overall tendency was for the residuals to cluster near zero. The residual amplitude was not completely uniform across wavelengths. Relatively larger variation was observed in the middle wavelength range, whereas the amplitude tended to decrease toward the long-wavelength end. This wavelength-dependent pattern is also consistent with the trends in MAE, RMSE, and MAPE, indicating that prediction uncertainty may increase relatively in specific wavelength ranges.

In addition, the sample-wise residuals summarized as a heatmap are presented in Figure S5. This figure allows the magnitude and distribution of the residuals to be compared intuitively across both the wavelength and sample axes.

Interpretability and Consistency Analysis

After converting the PLSR-predicted spectra into CIELAB coordinates, residual analysis of the L*, a*, and b* values and visual analysis in a three-dimensional space were performed to examine prediction consistency in the color space through comparison with the measured values. However, VIP is a metric that shows the relative contribution of variables and does not directly explain the direction of the effect, such as whether the response increases or decreases at a specific wavelength. To complement this, wavelength-wise local sensitivity was compared in both signed and absolute forms in Figure c–f, where the concentration change was evaluated at 1% owf intervals. Figure c and 9d show local sensitivity based on the PLSR predictions, whereas Figure e and 9f show local sensitivity based on the measured data. Signed sensitivity indicates the direction of K/S change with increasing concentration, whereas absolute sensitivity reflects only the magnitude of the effect regardless of direction.

As shown by the signed local sensitivity results in Figure c and 9e, Y and R generally maintained positive sensitivity, whereas B showed a pattern in which the sign of sensitivity changed with wavelength. This indicates that B affected the increase or decrease in K/S differently depending on the wavelength range and showed a relatively large effect, particularly in the middle-to-long wavelength region. The fact that this pattern appeared similarly in both the prediction-based and measured-data-based results suggest that PLSR reproduced the wavelength-wise response direction with reasonable consistency.

In the absolute local sensitivity results shown in Figure d and f, the effects of the dyes were more clearly distinguished across wavelength ranges. Overall, Y showed a relatively larger effect in the short-wavelength region, R in the middle-wavelength region, and B in the long-wavelength region, indicating that the main contributing dye changed depending on the wavelength range. In addition, by presenting the signed and absolute results together, the actual magnitude of the effect, which could otherwise be masked by offsetting due to opposite signs, could be interpreted in a complementary way.

This wavelength-dependent sensitivity pattern also provides a possible explanation for the relatively increased prediction error around 520–540 nm observed in Figure . This region can be regarded as a transition region between the middle-wavelength response associated with R and the stronger contribution of B at longer wavelengths. Therefore, the increased error in this region may reflect the difficulty in predicting local K/S values where dye-dependent spectral responses overlap.

The prediction–based local sensitivity and the measured–data–based local sensitivity showed generally similar overall curve shapes and peak positions. This indicates that PLSR did not simply match the magnitude of the predicted values, but also learned the structural relationship of the spectral response to recipe changes with reasonable stability. Therefore, the model was confirmed to provide meaningful performance not only in terms of prediction accuracy, but also in terms of color–space reproducibility and interpretability of spectral response.

Conclusion

This study developed a multi-output regression framework that directly predicts the K/S spectrum for black dope-dyed rPET/PCT microfiber fabrics. Under conditions where the spectral distribution was closely clustered and the correlation among output dimensions was high due to the characteristics of black shades, several regression algorithms were compared under the same conditions. Among them, PLSR was identified as the most balanced model when mean R2 and error metrics were considered together. In addition, the relative performance trends among the models were maintained in the 5-fold cross-validation results, confirming that the comparison did not rely excessively on a single data split.

The wavelength-wise analysis of PLSR prediction performance showed that the measured and predicted spectra in the test set had high linear agreement overall. Although the errors were not completely uniform across wavelengths, a pattern of relatively increased error in specific regions and decreased error toward the long-wavelength end was observed together. Residual analysis showed that most residuals were distributed near zero, with locally large errors appearing only in some samples and at some wavelength ranges. This indicates that the model reproduced the overall spectral shape stably, although magnitude errors could increase in specific wavelength ranges. The SAM–based evaluation of shape similarity also showed sufficiently low values for all test samples, confirming that spectral shape reproducibility was generally excellent.

To verify the color reproducibility of the predicted spectra, the K/S values were converted into CIELAB coordinates and compared with the measured values. The comparison of L*, a*, and b* showed that the measured and predicted values were generally close to each other. Based on ΔEab* , the 20 test samples showed values ranging from 0.2712 to 1.3865, with a mean of approximately 0.7913, and 14 samples satisfied a ΔEab* value of 1.0 or lower. In addition, the residuals of L*, a*, and b* were clustered around the Y = 0 reference line for each axis, and the residual points in the three-dimensional CIELAB space were also concentrated near the origin. These results show that spectral prediction accuracy was consistently translated into color-space reproducibility.

PLSR also provided meaningful information in terms of interpretability. In the VIP analysis, terms related to the blue dye, particularly B, B2, and interaction terms including B, showed relatively large contributions, suggesting that blue concentration played an important role in explaining the variation in the clustered black spectra. In addition, in the local sensitivity analysis, the prediction-based sensitivity and the measured-data-based sensitivity showed generally similar curve shapes and peak positions. In the signed sensitivity results, B showed a wavelength-dependent sign-change pattern, whereas the absolute sensitivity results showed a region-specific dominance pattern across the wavelength range. This indicates that PLSR went beyond simply reducing average error and learned the structural relationship of the spectral response to recipe changes with reasonable stability.

Overall, this study demonstrated that PLSR-based spectral prediction is effective in terms of accuracy, color reproducibility, and interpretability for black-shade dyeing data with a limited number of samples and high output correlation. The proposed framework suggests that data-driven prediction can complement the trial-and-error recipe search process based on repeated dyeing and color measurement, and it may contribute in practice to shorter process lead times and improved reproducibility of quality. In addition, by reducing repeated experiments and rework, the framework also has potential value in lowering the use of dyes and chemicals, water consumption, energy consumption, and wastewater generation.
