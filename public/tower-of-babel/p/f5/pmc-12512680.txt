Impact of antibiotic post-prescription authorization in resource-limited emergency rooms and acute care units during the COVID-19 pandemic
Authors: Natthanan Kingsuvangul, Witranut Boonchaikamonarkorn, Pinyo Rattanaumpawan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAntimicrobial overuse is a major problem in various healthcare settings, including emergency rooms (ERs) and acute care units (ACUs). This study aimed to evaluate the impact of the post-prescription authorization (PPA) of antibiotics in these settings.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included ER and ACU patients at Siriraj Hospital. In August 2020, a PPA for piperacillin/tazobactam, meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin, and ertapenem was implemented. These antibiotics were unrestricted for the first 72 h; thereafter, infectious disease physician approval was required. Data from pre-implementation (July 2020) and post-implementation (September 2020) periods were compared.ResultsTwo-hundred and six patients were included in the study (103 patients each from the pre- and post-implementation periods). There were no significant differences between the groups regarding male sex (49.5% vs. 47.6%;p = 0.78) and age (67.63 ± 22.9 vs. 66.94 ± 17.4 years;p = 0.27). The respiratory tract was the most common infection site, and piperacillin/tazobactam was the most frequently first-prescribed antibiotic. Using too narrow-spectrum antibiotics was the most common reason for inappropriate antibiotic use. The day of therapy/outpatient-day of the target antibiotics was significantly lower in the post-implementation group (0.85 ± 0.41 vs. 0.72 ± 0.39;p = 0.02). Favorable clinical outcomes were significantly higher in the post-implementation group (42.7% vs. 62.1%;p = 0.005).ConclusionsThis study demonstrates the positive impact of PPA in emergency settings, with a 15% reduction in antibiotic consumption and a 20% increase in favorable clinical outcomes. Future studies should evaluate PPA early in the course of treatment to identify the most effective interventions for improving the quality of care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12873-025-01363-0.Keywords: Antibiotic authorization, Antimicrobial stewardship, Emergency room

Background

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has been increasing over the past decades [1, 2]. This can lead to increased morbidities and mortalities, long hospital stays and high hospital expenditures [3, 4]. Previous studies have confirmed the association between the increased use of broad-spectrum antibiotics and the development of AMR [5].

Antimicrobial overuse is problematic not only in the hospital and outpatient units but also in emergency units [6, 7]. A study conducted in an Ethiopian emergency department (ED) reported that one-fourth of the encounters had antibiotics, and half of those prescriptions were administered parenterally [7]. An Australian study reported that approximately one-third of ED antibiotic prescriptions were inappropriate [6]. A recent study from an Italian emergency department found that 50% of bloodstream infections caused by multidrug-resistant organisms were treated with inappropriate initial antibiotic therapy, which was associated with prolonged hospitalization [8]. Antimicrobial over prescription may be because of a lack of ED regulatory systems and short physician–patient encounter times for careful evaluation.

The 2016 guidelines for implementing an antimicrobial stewardship (AMS) program endorsed by the Infectious Diseases Society of America and the Society for Healthcare Epidemiology of America recommend antibiotic authorization and prospective audits and feedback as effective strategies [9]. Effective strategies that promote appropriate antimicrobial therapy are needed to reduce the emergence of AMR [10–14].

A previous study of hospitalized patients at Siriraj Hospital revealed that post-prescription authorization (PPA) of broad-spectrum antibiotics, including piperacillin/tazobactam, imipenem/cilastatin and meropenem, significantly improved treatment response, reduced antimicrobial consumption and reduced hospital expenditures [13]. Since then, PPA has been implemented for all inpatients at Siriraj Hospital. However, such intervention has never been implemented in emergency rooms (ERs) and acute care units (ACUs). The objective of this study was to evaluate the clinical and economic impacts of the PPA of broad-spectrum antibiotics in the ER and ACU at Siriraj Hospital.

Methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a retrospective observational study of adult patients (age ≥ 15 years) who visited the ER and the ACU at Siriraj Hospital from July to September 2020. The study protocol was approved (certificate of approval #888/2020) by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board, with an informed consent waiver. This study was carried out in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki. The study flow chart and the STROBE checklist are available as online supplementary materials.

Siriraj Hospital is the largest tertiary- and quaternary-care medical centre in Bangkok, Thailand. It has approximately 5 million outpatient visits per year. As a quaternary care center, our hospital receives a high volume of referrals from other institutions and manages patients with multiple comorbidities. As a result, a substantial proportion of our patients are severely ill and experience frequent hospital admissions. A triage team evaluates all walk-in patients. Non-traumatic walk-in patients are categorized into three groups: (1) an emergent group (those with emergency conditions, such as unstable vital signs, heart attacks or acute strokes); (2) an urgent group (those whose conditions require medical care within a few hours, such as pneumonia, heart failure or upper gastrointestinal bleeding with stable vital signs); and (3) a non-urgent group (those who do not have emergent or urgent medical problems, such as prolonged fevers, common colds or lung masses). A patient with an emergent condition is triaged to the ER, while a patient with an urgent condition is designated to the ACU. A non-urgent patient needs to make an appointment with a doctor in a regular clinic. Because of the limited availability of hospital beds, some patients requiring hospitalization may receive definitive medical care at the ER or the ACU for several days before a bed becomes available. Some patients on the hospitalization waiting list may die, be discharged home or be referred elsewhere before a hospital bed becomes available.

The AMS program at Siriraj hospital

The hospital antibiotic formulary includes three classes of antibiotics: general, controlled and restricted antibiotics. General antibiotics can be freely prescribed by any physician without restriction. Controlled antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam, meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin and ertapenem) can be prescribed by any physician for use within the first 72 h of the index prescription, after which a PPA from the AMS team is required. Restricted antibiotics (such as colistin and linezolid) require approval from an ID physician before dispensing (pre-prescription authorization). While the pre-prescription authorization of restricted antibiotics has been implemented in both outpatient and inpatient settings, the PPA of controlled antibiotics has only been implemented in the inpatient setting. All controlled antibiotics can be freely prescribed without PPA in outpatient settings, including the ER and the ACU.

PPA implementation in the ER and ACU

In August 2020, the PPA of controlled antibiotics was implemented in the hospital’s ER and ACU as part of a quality improvement program. The protocol involves a responsible physician filling out a paper-based drug use evaluation form to prescribe a target antibiotic. The target antibiotic can be freely dispensed within the first 72 h, after which approval for continuation is required from the AMS team (including an infectious disease physician and a clinical pharmacist). The team conducts bedside rounds every official morning, typically lasting 1–2 h in total or approximately 10–15 min per case. The decision to continue the target antibiotics is classified as ‘approved for 14 days’, ‘temporarily approved for 3 days’ or ‘not approved’. If a target antibiotic is inappropriately prescribed and does not receive approval from the AMS team, it is discontinued. The AMS team may also add comments regarding the antibiotic prescription (i.e. recommendation of a new antibiotic regimen, dose or duration).

Study subjects

Patients who received at least one dose of the target antibiotics in the ER or ACU during the study period were eligible. We excluded patients who died or were discharged before receiving the target antibiotic. Patients who had the encounter before the implementation period (1–31 July 2020) were enrolled in the control group, while patients who had the encounter after the implementation period (1–30 September 2020) were enrolled in the intervention group. The first month of implementation (1–31 August 2020) was considered the washout period. The study was entirely conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic lockdown.

Data collection

Patients’ medical records were retrospectively reviewed to collect data, including patient demographics, comorbidities, details of antibiotic prescriptions, durations of overnight stays at the ER or ACU, clinical and microbiological outcomes, antibiotic and other medical costs and discharge status. Prescription details included choice, dose and indication of the target antibiotics (piperacillin/tazobactam, meropenem, imipenem/cilastatin and ertapenem). Antibiotic consumption was also recorded as defined daily dose (DDD) and days of therapy (DOT). Details of DDD and DOT calculations are available online on the World Health Organization’s website [15]. DDD/OPD-day is defined as the defined daily dose administered per outpatient-day, whereas DOT/OPD-day refers to the days of therapy administered per outpatient-day, representing patients who received treatment on an outpatient basis while awaiting admission to an inpatient bed.

A favorable clinical outcome was defined by a study physician based on resolution of fever, improvement in clinical symptoms, and laboratory parameters such as leukocytosis.

Antibiotic allergy was classified as any immune-mediated adverse reaction recorded by a non-study team pharmacist, including immediate reactions (e.g., anaphylaxis, urticaria, angioedema) and delayed reactions (e.g., maculopapular rash, serum sickness).

All study processes started after a certificate of approval was granted by the Siriraj Institutional Review Board in October 2020.

Statistical analysis and sample size calculation

Based on data from a pilot study, the appropriateness of antimicrobial therapy was estimated to be 50% and the intervention increased the appropriateness by 20%. Using a 2-sided p-value of 0.05 and a study power of 80%, the calculated sample size was 93 cases per group. An additional 10% adjustment for loss to follow-up increased the final sample size to 103 cases per group or a total of 206 cases.

All variables were reported using descriptive statistics. Either percentages, frequencies, means and standard deviations, or medians and ranges were used as appropriate. The variables between the two groups were compared using the chi-square test, Mann–Whitney U test, unpaired Student t-test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test as appropriate. The statistical analysis was performed using STATA/IC version 14.2 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA). A 2-sided p-value of 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

There were 103 patients (164 prescriptions) in the pre-implementation group and 103 patients (180 prescriptions) in the post-implementation group. Table 1 shows the baseline characteristics of the two groups. For the pre-implementation versus post-implementation groups, approximately half were male (49.5% vs. 47.6%; p = 0.78) with a comparable mean age (67.63 ± 22.9 vs. 66.94 ± 17.4; p = 0.27). The three most common comorbidities were hypertension (61.2% vs. 59.2%; p = 0.79), diabetes mellitus (40.8% vs. 42.7%; p = 0.79) and dyslipidemia (35.9% vs. 34.0%; p = 0.77). Approximately two-thirds of both groups visited the ER or the ACU because of hospital-acquired infections (63.8% vs. 67.0%; p = 0.96). The three leading types of infection were respiratory tract infections (40.0% vs. 40.0%; p = 1.00), urinary tract infections (37.9% vs. 35.0%; p = 0.66) and bacteremia (29.1% vs. 29.1%; p = 1.00). The most common first-prescribed antibiotic was piperacillin/tazobactam (73.8% vs. 79.6%;p=0.57), followed by meropenem (24.2% vs. 19.4%; p= 0.57) and ertapenem (1.9% vs. 1.0%; p = 0.57). There was no prescription of imipenem/cilastatin during the study period. All antibiotics were prescribed empirically (100.0% vs. 100.0%; p = 1.00).

Antibiotic consumption, antibiotic cost and clinical outcomes of patients in the pre-implementation period and the post-implementation period

The days of therapy per outpatient-day (DOT/OPD-day) of overall antibiotics for the pre- and post-implementation groups were comparable (1.09 ± 0.57 vs. 0.96 ± 0.66 days; p = 0.14). The DOT/OPD-day of the target antibiotics were significantly lower after the implementation (0.85 ± 0.41 vs. 0.72 ± 0.39 days; p = 0.02). There was no significant difference in the DOT/OPD-day of each target antibiotic.

The costs of overall antibiotics/OPD-day in the pre- versus post-implementation periods were US$ 12.39 ± 8.23 vs. US$ 12.02 ± 7.84, respectively (p = 0.74), while the costs of the target antibiotics/OPD-day were US$ 11.04 ± 7.89 vs. US$ 10.51 ± 6.47, respectively (p = 0.59). There were no significant differences between the groups regarding the cost/OPD-day of each target antibiotic.

The hospitalization rate was slightly higher during the pre-implementation period than during the post-implementation period (72.4% vs. 60%; p = 0.23). This resulted in a longer median duration of outpatient overnight stay in the post-implementation group (2.07 ± 1.09 days vs. 4.25 ± 3.03 days; p = 0.003). However, the favorable clinical outcomes of patients at the time of hospitalization, transfer or discharge were significantly higher during the post-implementation period (42.7% vs. 62.1%; p = 0.005). Occurrences of antibiotic allergies were comparable between both groups (23.3% vs. 19.4%; p = 0.50).

Details of PPA compliance in the post-implementation period

Of the 103 patients in the post-implementation group, only 36 (35.0%) were evaluated by the AMS team, while 67 were not evaluated. The evaluation could not be carried out for the following reasons: hospitalization before the evaluation (68.6%), discontinuation of the target antibiotic before the evaluation (19.4%) or referral or discharge before the evaluation (14.5%).

Of the 36 AMS-evaluated prescriptions, 11 (30.6%) were approved, 20 (55.5%) were unapproved and 5 (13.9%) were temporarily approved. Of the 16 approved and temporarily approved prescriptions, 8 (50.0%) were initiated without a loading dose and 12 (75.0%) were prescribed at a reduced maintenance dose due to underlying chronic kidney disease. However, a renally adjusted dose of β-lactams is generally unnecessary during the first 48 h of sepsis. Therefore, the AMS team recommended escalating the antibiotic dose to ensure adequate early exposure. Of the 20 unapproved prescriptions, 17 (85.0%) antibiotic regimens were too narrow and 3 (15.0%) were too broad. Nearly all unapproved prescriptions were suggested to be changed (90.0%). Discussion

Our study revealed that all the target antibiotics were prescribed empirically, and piperacillin/tazobactam was the most commonly prescribed antibiotic. Although the DDD/OPD-day and cost of target antibiotics/OPD-day were not significantly lower in the post-implementation period, the DOT/OPD-day of the target antibiotics was significantly lower after implementation.

The discrepancy in these outcomes can be attributed to dosage adjustments. During the post-implementation period, the AMS team not only recommended the appropriate antibiotic choice but also ensured an accurate dosing. Many patients with serious infections received escalating doses, per the AMS team’s suggestions. Although the AMS team reviewed only one-third of prescriptions, nearly all of approved prescriptions were dose-adjusted. Therefore, these targeted adjustments may have contributed to improved clinical outcomes.

Our study revealed that the length of OPD stay during the post-implementation period was significantly higher than the pre-implementation period. These findings may have resulted from the worsened COVID-19 lockdowns in the post-implementation period. In August 2020, some general wards were converted into COVID-19 cohort wards. Therefore, non-COVID-19 patients with severe infections stayed longer in the ER or ACU because of the limited space for hospitalization.

Among the 103 patients in the post-implementation group, only one-third of prescriptions were evaluated. A majority of the prescriptions involved study subjects who were no longer in the ER/ACU or target antibiotics that have been discontinued; therefore, they were not evaluated. These findings suggest that the PPA should have been implemented at an earlier time point. Although the PPA on day 3 was beneficial in many previous studies conducted among hospitalized patients [10, 14, 16] it may be overly delayed in emergency settings.

Furthermore, 20 out of 36 prescriptions were considered inappropriate: three prescriptions were too broad and 17 prescriptions were too narrow. These findings emphasize the high proportion of inappropriate antibiotic therapy in emergency settings. In some cases, the AMS team not only discontinued the inappropriate antibiotic therapy but also recommended broader spectrum antibiotics and proper dosing. For example, in a patient with urosepsis previously colonized with ESBL-producing Enterobacterales in the urine, we recommend switching from piperacillin/tazobactam to meropenem. These could explain the significantly higher favorable clinical outcomes in the post-implementation group than in the pre-implementation group.

Our study has several strengths. First, only a few studies have focused on antibiotic authorization in emergency settings. Second, our study focused not only on antibiotic consumption and cost but also on clinical responses and other related outcomes.

The study also has some limitations. First, the study was conducted in a resource-limited setting during the COVID-19 pandemic and within a relatively short time frame. During the post-implementation period, the average waiting time for hospitalization was 4.25 days, which was remarkably long. Therefore, the study results may not be applicable to other settings. Furthermore, this factor may have introduced negative bias in outcomes such as length of stay and antibiotic consumption. To mitigate this bias, we calculated antibiotic consumption per outpatient-day (OPD-day) rather than directly comparing the raw values. Second, our study’s retrospective observational design raises internal validity issues that would have been addressed by a randomized controlled design. Without a multivariate analysis, the positive impact of the intervention may be influenced by differences in baseline outcomes or by changes over time. Last, the appropriateness of antimicrobial therapy, which was our primary outcome, could not be fully evaluated due to the high proportion of antibiotic discontinuation before assessment. However, differences observed in other outcomes of interest may still suggest a potential positive effect of the intervention.

Conclusions

Our study confirmed the positive impact of antibiotic PPA on target antibiotic consumption and clinical outcomes, but found no difference in antibiotic cost—although this finding may not be entirely certain, given the pre-post study design. A high proportion of antibiotics prescribed in this setting were underdosed, with overly narrow spectra. Achieving a favorable clinical outcome was more important than reducing antibiotic consumption or cost. Importantly, our findings suggest that PPA is feasible in the emergency and acute care units, may reduce days of therapy (DOT), and has the potential to improve patient outcomes. However, the study was limited by a short time frame and a high proportion of missing data, which may have affected the robustness of our findings. Larger, longer-term, and methodologically robust evaluations are needed to confirm these benefits and to identify the most effective strategies for implementing PPA in these settings. Future studies that incorporate early implementation assessments may help optimize the quality of care in emergency settings.
