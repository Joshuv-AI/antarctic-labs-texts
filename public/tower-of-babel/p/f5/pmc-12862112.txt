New Graduate Nurses' Experiences of Decision‐Making Uncertainty in Intensive Care: An Interpretive Description
Authors: Maude Crétaz, Patrick Lavoie
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundNew graduate nurses entering intensive care units (ICUs) directly after graduation often face challenges in clinical decision‐making, contributing to a sense of uncertainty—the inability to predict the outcome of a patient situation. Although uncertainty has been studied in other healthcare contexts, it remains underexplored among new graduate nurses in the ICU.AimTo explore the experience of uncertainty among new graduate nurses working in ICUs.Study DesignThis qualitative interpretive descriptive study was guided by a theory of uncertainty, which examines its conditions, causes, manifestations, remediation strategies and consequences. Targeted support strategies—such as mentorship, structured feedback and reflective learning opportunities—are essential to foster confidence, clinical competence and professional integration.Keywords: clinical decision‐making, critical care nursing, new graduate, nurses, uncertainty

Introduction

The COVID‐19 pandemic, the ongoing nursing shortage and the ageing population have accelerated the integration of new graduate nurses (NGNs) into intensive care units (ICUs) immediately after graduation [1]. Regardless of experience level, ICU nurses are expected to assess and manage patients with life‐threatening conditions, requiring advanced knowledge of pathophysiology and clinical care, as well as an understanding of patients' psychosocial needs [2]. Their ability to administer high‐risk medications and operate complex technologies is closely tied to their decision‐making skills, which are essential for timely, accurate judgements and decisions that support optimal patient outcomes [2, 3]. Clinical decision‐making in the ICU draws on prior experiences and interprofessional collaboration to assess patients' conditions and determine appropriate actions [4, 5, 6].

New graduate nurses often enter the ICU with limited clinical experience, having had few encounters with critical or urgent situations. This makes adaptation to specialised tasks, a highly technological environment, and the high acuity of ICU patients particularly challenging [1]. As a result, new graduate nurses are particularly prone to uncertainty in their clinical decision‐making [1, 7, 8, 9]. Uncertainty is defined as a limitation of knowledge or information that impedes an individual's ability to predict the outcomes of a patient's situation [10, 11, 12].

While uncertainty has often been studied in relation to patient well‐being and medical outcomes [11], less attention has been paid to its impact on nurses. Yet, decision‐making uncertainty in ICU nurses may contribute to stress, anxiety and burnout [8, 13, 14, 15]. The ICU is a demanding environment, with working conditions contributing to fatigue, dissatisfaction and high turnover [15, 16, 17]. Indeed, retention is a critical issue: A recent meta‐analysis found that 36% of new graduate nurses in ICUs consider leaving their positions, compared to 28% of experienced ICU nurses [18]. Poor retention of new graduate nurses affects care quality and places a financial burden on healthcare systems, with estimated losses of up to $ US 20 500 per nurse, half of which are related to decreased productivity [19].

To our knowledge, no studies have specifically examined clinical decision‐making uncertainty among new graduate nurses in ICUs. Existing research has focused primarily on acute care settings, such as medical and surgical units, where uncertainty influences new graduate nurses' transition into professional practice [7, 20, 21]. As more new graduate nurses integrate the ICU and uncertainty remains inherent to health care, a deeper understanding of their reality and experiences is essential.

Aim and Objectives of Study

This study aimed to explore the experience of uncertainty in clinical decision‐making among new graduate nurses working in ICUs. Specifically, we sought to gain a deeper understanding of the conditions, causes, manifestations, remediation strategies and consequences among this population.

Design and Methods

This research used an interpretive description approach [22, 23], a qualitative methodology designed to identify and interpret the key elements of a phenomenon while accounting for its complexity within a specific practice context [23]. This approach is particularly suited to exploring participants' experiences and generating meaningful insights for nursing education and practice. Grounded in real‐world questions and inherently contextualised, interpretive description provides knowledge relative to the context in which it is generated [23, 24]. Conducting this study exclusively within the ICU context enabled a focused description of new graduate nurses' experiences with uncertainty, yielding insights into real‐world practice‐related problems while accounting for the organisational, professional and social complexities inherent to this setting and population. Importantly, this methodology yields practice‐based professional knowledge that can be translated into concrete implications [22], aligning with nursing's commitment to grounding knowledge in practical use [23]. Understanding new graduate nurses' experiences with uncertainty can thus inform concrete strategies to support them in clinical practice.

Interpretive description is a flexible approach that invites researchers to adapt traditional qualitative methods to advance nursing knowledge more directly while maintaining methodological rigour [24]. This flexibility requires preserving a consistent conceptual thread between the research question, data collection and analysis [22, 23]. Following Thompson Burdine et al.'s [22] recommendations, we ensured that methodological decisions regarding sample selection, data collection and analysis remained closely aligned with the research question and new graduate nurses' experiences with uncertainty.

Theoretical Framework

This study was guided by Cranley et al.'s [10] grounded theory of Recognizing and Responding to Uncertainty as a theoretical framework. Interpretive description permits integrating prior empirical studies and existing conceptual models to provide an initial point of departure for data interpretation [22, 24]. Cranley et al.'s [10] framework offers a comprehensive understanding of nurses’ experiences of uncertainty in acute care settings, encompassing five interrelated dimensions: conditions (contextual factors), causes (patient‐related triggers), manifestations (how uncertainty is perceived and expressed), remediation strategies (responses to uncertainty) and consequences. Although developed from a study with experienced nurses across diverse clinical environments, this framework's comprehensive nature provided a solid foundation for designing the interview guide and organising the analysis. It served as a preliminary structure to support critical examination of the data while allowing findings to be refined and challenged to reflect the specific context of new graduate nurses working in ICUs accurately.

Cranley et al.'s [10] theory conceptualises uncertainty in clinical decision‐making as dynamic and evolving, shaped by factors related to the patient, environment and the nurse. Uncertainty is often triggered by acute, unanticipated patient conditions, unfamiliar physician orders or ethical dilemmas. Manifestations include heightened assessment, reflection, questioning and difficulty predicting outcomes. In response, nurses draw on cognitive resources to assess situations, seek consultation from colleagues and engage with evidence to determine appropriate interventions. Consequences include resolution, persistent doubt and learning opportunities. This framework provided a conceptual anchor for exploring the specific ways new graduate nurses experience and respond to uncertainty during their transition to critical care practice.

Setting and Sample

Eligible participants were nurses who had graduated from a college or university within the past 24 months and worked in ICUs in Quebec, Canada. All ICUs (e.g., adult, paediatric, neonatal) were eligible for inclusion.

Purposive sampling was used to recruit participants, with a target of 5–8 individuals to allow in‐depth exploration within the study's time constraints. Data saturation was not sought, in line with the understanding that the uniqueness of individual experiences can lead to infinite variations [22, 25]. The number of participants was deemed sufficient when the data were sufficient to address the study's purpose [26]. Recruitment was conducted through a digital poster shared on social media (LinkedIn, Facebook, and Instagram). This approach enabled broad yet targeted outreach to nurses meeting the study criteria [27]. Interested individuals emailed the researchers and received a study summary, a consent form and an invitation to a one‐on‐one interview.

Data Collection Tools and Methods

Data collection consisted of a sociodemographic questionnaire and semi‐structured individual interviews. The questionnaire was completed immediately before each interview. The first author conducted interviews in person or online via Zoom (Zoom Video Communications, San José, USA) or Microsoft Teams (Microsoft Corporation, Redmond, USA). Based on Cranley et al.'s [10] framework, the interview guide explored the five dimensions of uncertainty (Appendix A). Interviews were recorded and transcribed verbatim.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics from the sociodemographic questionnaires were generated using Microsoft Excel (2021). Interview recordings were manually transcribed and analysed using MAXQDA 2022 (VERBI Software, Berlin, Germany).

A constant comparative method guided the analysis through five iterative steps [22, 28]. It involved five steps: (1) data immersion, (2) development of a thematic plan, (3) organisation of themes, (4) data condensation and (5) comparison and contrast of the data.

Data immersion began during the interviews and continued throughout transcription and analysis. This process enabled initial reflection on emerging themes and minor adjustments to the interview guide to better align with the study's purpose and participants' realities [22, 28]. Developing a thematic plan began with the creation of a codebook in MAXQDA 2022, which was revised and refined as the analysis progressed. Organisation of themes involved categorising codes from broader concepts to more specific subcodes [22, 29]. Data condensation involved reviewing and merging subcodes into overarching themes to develop a concise, coherent coding structure [22]. Persistent codes were identified as integrative themes of the study [28]. Finally, comparisons and contrasts were conducted across interviews to identify patterns, similarities and differences. While the participants had relatively similar profiles, the analysis focuses on areas of divergence and convergence to enrich interpretation [22, 28].

Rigour and Reflexivity

The researchers acknowledged that their personal experiences, beliefs and preconceptions could have influenced the data collection and analysis processes. The first author's direct experience with uncertainty in the ICU—having been a new graduate nurse in that setting—served as a key motivation for the study. Her experiential knowledge provided valuable contextual insight into participants' accounts, enhancing interpretive depth and supporting the transferability of findings. At the same time, her prior assumptions regarding the causes, manifestations and consequences of uncertainty, shaped by clinical experience and extensive reading on the topic, may have influenced how the data were approached and interpreted.

To mitigate potential biases and maintain the study's rigour, a reflexive journal was maintained, documenting thoughts, assumptions and analytic decisions to enhance transparency and minimise bias [22, 30]. The authors also regularly debriefed to critically examine and challenge emerging interpretations, supporting analytic rigour and validity [26]. An audit trail was maintained in MAXQDA to track codebook revisions, thereby supporting confirmability [22]. Credibility was further strengthened through participant validation during the interviews, thereby ensuring the accuracy and trustworthiness of the collected data [31]. Since some participants were coworkers of the first author, they were instructed during the interview not to discuss any situations in which they might have been involved together to mitigate potential overlap and bias.

Ethical and Institutional Approval

This study was approved by the institution's Science and Health Research Ethics Committee (Project 2023‐4849) on October 6, 2023. All participants provided informed written consent and were reminded of their right to withdraw at any point. Each participant received CAD 25 compensation (±US 20). Given the sensitive nature of uncertainty, which can involve difficult relationships with colleagues or superiors and potentially emotionally triggering situations, the first author reiterated the confidentiality of the interview and ensured a safe environment, thereby fostering trust and encouraging open expression. Participants were informed of their right to decline to answer any question or to terminate the interview at any time, particularly if a question elicited memories of an unpleasant experience.

Findings

The six participants had a median age of 25 years (IQR = 2.8) and reported 7–17 months of nursing experience, including 3–16 months in an ICU dedicated to adult patient care (median = 13 months, IQR = 7.8). One participant had worked in the ICU for 12 months following graduation before transitioning to another clinical setting, the nature of which was not specified. The sample included five female and one male nurse. The median interview duration was 58 min (SD = 6 min). Only one interview was conducted per participant.

Overall, participants expressed uncertainty when faced with novel or acute patient conditions, particularly when they could not anticipate how a situation might evolve. They described three types of clinical scenarios that triggered uncertainty: decision‐making during cardiorespiratory arrest (n = 3), managing intoxicated and agitated patients (n = 1) and titrating medications to control blood pressure (n = 2). These scenarios were marked either by their novelty or by their patient's critical condition.

Urgent or unfamiliar scenarios require new graduate nurses to make rapid decisions and implement interventions using equipment, techniques or medications with which they have little or no experience. Participants emphasised that unfamiliarity—rather than complexity—was often the primary source of uncertainty. Despite the stress these situations created, participants recognised them as valuable opportunities for growth. They appreciated the focused attention such cases demanded and the benefits of a one‐to‐one nurse‐to‐patient ratio, which enabled deeper learning and skill development.

Both clinical situations and interactions with the nursing team shaped participants' experiences of uncertainty. By integrating their personal knowledge with colleagues' insights and evidence, they navigated uncertainty in a way that fostered experiential learning. Although uncertainty was considered inevitable during early ICU practice, it was also viewed as formative. Participants expected that repeated exposure to high‐acuity situations would build confidence and develop their ability to manage uncertainty over time.

Uncertainty among new graduate nurses emerged as an experiential learning loop, characterised by a dynamic, iterative learning process, conceptualised in three key themes: (1) causes of uncertainty, (2) questioning as a signal of uncertainty and (3) the contribution of knowledge (see Figure 1).

Scrutiny From the Nursing Team

The nursing team actively supported, advised and, at times, intervened in participants' clinical care. This scrutiny could alleviate uncertainty caused by unfamiliar situations by offering informal feedback and guidance, but it could also introduce performance pressure and fear of judgement.

Participants expressed greater comfort when working with nursing teams that trusted their skills, encouraged their participation in emergencies and fostered mutual respect. These supportive dynamics were more common during night shifts, where lower staffing levels increased opportunities for new graduate nurses to be actively involved in emergencies. Conversely, during day shifts, experienced nurses tended to take over more quickly, limiting new graduate nurses' participation and reinforcing feelings of exclusion and uncertainty.

Whether it's day or night, you're quickly pushed aside. […]. In teams with a lot of staff, if you don't have the experience, you're not seen as the most suitable person to act. […] Now, I work with the night team, which is “low staff” and very open to the experiences of young nurses, and involves nurses based on their capacity. (P1)

In my ICU, the day nurses are older, they are more intense. So, I might have been pushed aside, and I think I would have learned a lot less. I would have been less autonomous. (P6)

Participants also feared being discussed negatively by colleagues, especially when feedback on their clinical decisions was not communicated directly. Informal conversations, such as those in break rooms, contributed to a sense of inadequacy.

Nurses talk a lot about other people. In the break room, experienced nurses often talk about situations that happened during the day. And they're like, “She shouldn't have done this, she shouldn't have done that.” So, it's out of fear that we talk about ourselves negatively. (P1)

The lack of timely, constructive feedback was seen as a barrier to professional development and a source of persistent uncertainty about the appropriateness of their clinical decisions.

Questioning as a Signal of Uncertainty

Uncertainty was experienced most acutely during unexpected patient deterioration. Participants described constant internal questioning as the main signal of their uncertainty, reflecting their inability to predict, anticipate or respond confidently. They questioned the patient's trajectory, appropriate interventions, care techniques and the medications to be administered.

It's like, what's happening, and what do we do? I was thinking: Is it just monitoring, do I need to give them medication? Are there things I need to do? I don't know. (P4)

Participants feared that their actions could inadvertently cause harm or delay appropriate interventions. Even when supported by the team, they often revisited their decisions, questioning their competence and whether they were the right person to manage the situation. It's like it's beyond my abilities, even though everything went really well. My whole team supported me very well. But I think uncertainty is more of a feeling of being overwhelmed, like: “Am I the right person for this? Do I know how to administer this medication?” (P6)

This self‐questioning extended to how the nursing team would perceive their actions. Participants feared being judged for intervening too quickly or not quickly enough, for unnecessarily asking for help or for not asking when they should have. They often compared themselves to more experienced nurses, aspiring to achieve similar levels of clinical confidence.

Self‐questioning may persist after decisions are made, as participants doubted the appropriateness of their actions throughout the situation. Thus, reflection was a key coping strategy. By reviewing their decisions, the course of events, team interactions and their emotional responses, participants came to understand that multiple factors shape clinical outcomes and that responsibility is shared within the healthcare team.

Contribution of Knowledge

To navigate uncertainty, participants initially relied on their personal knowledge acquired through academic sources (e.g., simulations, clinical placements) or prior work experience. They also drew on patient data—such as vital signs, lab results and reports—and compared it with theoretical knowledge to validate their concerns and guide decision‐making.

When these resources were insufficient or failed to yield the expected outcomes, participants sought guidance from colleagues or evidence. However, they found consulting research, guidelines or clinical algorithms in urgent scenarios impractical. Many questioned the applicability of scientific evidence, given patient‐specific variability and a preference for immediate advice from experienced team members.

What do my skills and knowledge tell me I need to do right now? Do it, and if it doesn't work, then seek help. (P3)

In emergencies, participants sought guidance and delegated unfamiliar tasks to experienced colleagues, ensuring safe and efficient care. These interactions also provided informal learning moments, as team members used them to offer support and practical instruction.

There was a [floating nurse] who told me, “No, it's not that sheet, use the other one.” Then, she taught me how to fill out the sheet during the code. So, that person turned out to be a resource for me. I didn't even know who she was; I had never met her. (P5)

In non‐urgent situations, new graduate nurses often acted independently but later sought validation by explaining their reasoning and actions to more experienced colleagues. These informal check‐ins offered reassurance that their decisions were appropriate, reducing lingering uncertainty.

I would say, “Hey, what do you think about the [epinephrine]?” And I remember it was exactly that, from their experience: “Oh, I do it this way.” She was like, “Yeah, take it easy with the weaning, I think we should go more cautiously.” (P2)

Ultimately, participants recognised that knowing everything as a new graduate nurse in the ICU was impossible. They relied heavily on the accessibility, knowledge, experience and support of the nursing teams to navigate everyday and high‐stakes situations.

Discussion

This study explored the experience of uncertainty among new graduate nurses working in ICUs. Findings revealed that uncertainty often stemmed from the novelty and acuity of clinical situations, prompting new graduate nurses to question their actions, decisions and overall competence. To reduce this uncertainty, new graduate nurses drew upon their personal knowledge, sought guidance from colleagues and consulted available resources. Interestingly, these strategies closely align with those identified in Cranley et al.'s [10] theory, in which nurses manage uncertainty by drawing on cognitive resources to assess the situation, seek consultation from colleagues and engage with evidence to determine appropriate interventions.

Initially, new graduate nurses relied on their prior knowledge—acquired from academic education, clinical placements and previous work experiences—before turning to others for support. This process aligns with Kolb's experiential learning theory, which views learning as a dynamic, learner‐centred process in which knowledge is constructed through experience [32, 33]. Although new graduate nurses are no longer in formal education, recalling past clinical experiences enabled them to formulate strategies and cope with uncertainty. Consequently, uncertain scenarios in the ICU can serve as catalysts for experiential learning, where active involvement in patient care fosters adaptive clinical reasoning and future preparedness [33, 34]. These findings echo prior studies showing that personal and professional experience shape nurses' decision‐making, notably through pattern recognition [5, 35]. Despite their limited time in practice, new graduate nurses in this study demonstrated an ability to blend reflective practice with theoretical knowledge to navigate complex situations—suggesting that experience not only sharpens clinical judgement but also strengthens communication and collaboration in high‐stakes situations [5].

While Kolb's theory focuses on individual learning processes, the nursing team also significantly shaped new graduate nurses’ experiences of uncertainty. Team members contributed to decision‐making by providing feedback, validation and assistance. These interactions often mitigated uncertainty but could also amplify it, particularly when the nursing team's expectations were unclear or when feedback was indirect. Similar dynamics were observed among experienced nurses in Cranley et al.'s [10] study, in which collaboration and shared expertise were key strategies for navigating uncertainty.

However, for new graduate nurses, the scrutiny from the nursing team could be a double‐edged sword. While supportive behaviours enhanced confidence, inconsistent or critical interactions undermined their sense of belonging and competence. This aligns with the literature on professional socialisation, which highlights how interactions with colleagues shape nurses' knowledge, skills, attitudes, values and identities as they fulfil their professional roles [36, 37, 38]. Through contact with the nursing team, the NGN learns professional behaviours and understands the expectations and responsibilities of ICU nurses [37, 38]. Positive experiences with teamwork, collaboration and mentorship promote confidence and engagement [38], while dismissive or critical feedback can lead to self‐doubt and demotivation [36, 39]. Thus, while the nursing team is a critical support resource for new graduate nurses and can enhance their feeling of competence [37, 38], its influence can also exacerbate uncertainty when team dynamics are unsupportive.

New graduate nurses also responded to uncertainty by consulting protocols and evidence—much like experienced nurses in Cranley et al.'s [10] study. However, participants noted that clinical protocols were not always practical or applicable in urgent situations. The complexity and unpredictability of ICU patients led many new graduate nurses to favour easily accessible, experience‐based knowledge from colleagues over time‐consuming evidence‐based resources. This preference is supported by Scott et al. [40], who argue that nurses adapt their knowledge use to situational demands. In fast‐paced environments like the ICU, the urgency of care shapes which types of knowledge are accessed and applied. Experience, whether personal or from colleagues, is readily accessible and can be the quickest and easiest way to respond to uncertainty [40]. Through collaboration with experienced team members and the use of clinical resources, such as protocols and guidelines, new graduate nurses enrich their experiential knowledge, as each clinical action and decision becomes a learning opportunity in the intensive care context.

Importantly, this study suggests that uncertainty is not only rooted in clinical complexity but also in interpersonal dynamics. Consistent with Cranley et al.'s [10] framework, uncertainty is a multifaceted experience shaped by patient acuity, team interactions and contextual factors. These findings highlight the need to proactively support new graduate nurses in their transition to ICU practice and offer targeted resources to help them navigate uncertainty constructively.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, recall bias may have influenced the data, as participants occasionally struggled to remember details of past clinical events. To mitigate this, the interviewer used probing techniques to elicit reflections [41]. Second, recruitment challenges limited the sample's diversity. Despite offering financial incentives, new graduate nurses showed limited interest, which may affect the transferability of findings. Although the target of 5–8 participants was met, a larger and more diverse sample across various ICU contexts would have strengthened the findings. The small sample size and the relative homogeneity of participants—who shared similar backgrounds and practised in comparable ICU environments—may have limited the range of perspectives captured.

While this study sought to deepen the understanding of new graduate nurses’ lived experiences and to illustrate the conditions, causes, manifestations, remediation strategies and consequences, it cannot claim to fully represent its complexity. The findings highlight key patterns; however, as new graduate nurses seem to develop through experiential learning, a deeper exploration of the contributing factors could provide a more comprehensive understanding of uncertainty in critical care. Cranley et al.'s [10] framework highlights the role of the patient, the environment and the nurse in shaping their uncertainty. Thus, this aspect was addressed only briefly in the present study, which primarily focused on how new graduate nurses navigate and mitigate uncertainty. Future research could further explore how contextual factors and individual nurse characteristics—such as educational background—shape the manifestation and evolution of uncertainty in critical care settings. A better understanding of the interplay between contextual and individual factors, using both qualitative and quantitative approaches, could identify intervention points and inform educational strategies to enhance nurses’ preparedness for practice in ICUs.

Implications for Practice and Further Research

This study underscores the importance of both clinical and social experiences in learning to manage uncertainty and making decisions in critical care environments. Academic programs and workplace‐based support structures, such as mentorship or residency programs, may effectively prepare new graduate nurses for the realities of ICU practice [42]. These programmes help reduce stress and provide comprehensive support—clinical, emotional and professional—through educational strategies such as simulation, preceptorship and case‐based learning [9, 42].

Although many of these programmes aim to develop technical competencies, they often do not directly address the experience of uncertainty. Integrating uncertainty management into training could strengthen new graduate nurses' clinical judgement and emotional resilience. Future iterations of these programs could include structured debriefings, peer discussions or reflective exercises to better support new graduate nurses' cognitive and emotional processing of uncertain situations. Given the pivotal role of the nursing team in integrating new graduate nurses, they could benefit from increased sensitivity to the unique needs and challenges, such as uncertainty, encountered early in practice. To facilitate a positive transition and encourage professional development, targeted training for nursing teams may prove beneficial. As new staff members dedicate significant effort to establishing themselves within the ICU, a supportive and responsive team environment could further enhance their autonomy and professional integrity [43].

Conclusion

This qualitative study explored how uncertainty in clinical decision‐making is shaped by the ICU environment, team dynamics and new graduate nurses' evolving knowledge base. Despite their limited experience, new graduate nurses are competent professionals who can contribute meaningfully to ICU care. Uncertainty emerged as both a challenge and a learning opportunity, supporting the development of clinical judgement and professional identity. As such, uncertainty should not be viewed solely as a barrier to competence but as an integral part of experiential learning and professional development. Understanding and supporting new graduate nurses' experience of uncertainty can ultimately enhance the quality of patient care in critical care settings.
