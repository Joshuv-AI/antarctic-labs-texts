Targeted biomimetic fusogenic liposome enhances tumor accumulation, penetration, and therapeutic efficacy of siRNA therapeutics
Authors: Xuejuan Zhao, Keyun Wu, Yuluo Zhang, Hui Zhang, Xianghan Zhang, Qianqian Ni, Zhongliang Wang, Yuqiong Xia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Blocking the CD47–SIRPα signaling pathway to promote macrophage phagocytosis represents a promising strategy for cancer therapy. RNA interference offers a safe and efficient means to disrupt this pathway, highlighting the importance of developing effective siRNA delivery systems to improve antitumor efficacy. In this study, we developed a biomimetic and fusogenic liposome capable of deep tumor penetration and selective tumor cell targeting for the coordinated cytosolic delivery of CD47 and PLK1-targeting siRNAs. CD47 siRNA downregulated CD47 protein expression, suppressing the “don't eat me” signal, while PLK1 siRNA induced tumor cell apoptosis and enhanced the “eat-me” signal by facilitating the translocation of calreticulin to the cell surface. This biomimetic platform enabled efficient tumor cell specific cytosolic delivery and deep tissue penetration of siRNAs. The combined silencing strategy significantly enhanced macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells and improved antitumor outcomes in a 4T1 tumor-bearing mouse model. Overall, this study presents a biomimetic fusogenic liposomal system that amplifies macrophage phagocytosis and suppresses tumor growth, providing a promising therapeutic avenue for triple-negative breast cancer.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is recognized as the most aggressive subtype of breast cancer, characterized by poor prognosis and low survival rates [[1], [2], [3]]. Surgery, radiation, chemotherapy can temporarily inhibit TNBC progression but suffer from recurrence and metastasis owing to the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME). Tumor associated-macrophages (TAMs) [4], derived from circulating monocytes, can constitute up to 50 % of the tumor mass within the TME [5]. Although macrophages serve as key immune defenders by engulfing pathogens, senescent or damaged cells, accumulating evidence suggests that TAMs contribute to tumor immune evasion due to impaired phagocytic function, posing a significant challenge to effective cancer immunotherapy. CD47, a transmembrane protein frequently overexpressed on tumor cell surfaces, plays an important role in modulating TAM phagocytic function [6]. By interacting with signal regulatory protein alpha (SIRPα) on macrophages, CD47 delivers a “don't eat me” signal that inhibits phagocytosis, allowing tumor cells to evade immune surveillance and clearance. Blocking the CD47-SIRPα axis has demonstrated promising potential in promoting macrophage-mediated tumor clearance [7,8]. However, monotherapy targeting CD47-SIRPα often fails to achieve satisfactory therapeutic outcome.

Augmenting CD47 blockade with pro-phagocytic signals such as calreticulin (CRT) has emerged as a promising strategy. CRT, when exposed on the tumor cell surface, acts as an “eat-me” signal binding to LRP1 (also known as CD91) on macrophages, thereby promoting the macrophage phagocytosis and tumor cell clearance [9]. Normally residing in the endoplasmic reticulum, CRT translocates to the cell surface during cell apoptosis. Apoptosis-inducing treatments—including chemotherapy, sonodynamic therapy, photothermal therapy, photodynamic therapy and gene therapy—have been shown to increase CRT exposure, enhancing “eat me” signaling on tumor cells [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Polo-like kinase 1 (PLK1), a member of serine/threonine protein kinase family, has a crucial role in mitosis, cell division and DNA replication [14]. High PLK1 expression correlates with poor prognosis in TNBC [15]. Notably, PLK1 inhibition induces apoptosis [16,17], suggesting a role in CRT upregulation. Thus, in addition to direct toxicity in cancer cells, inhibiting PLK1 may enhance prophagocytic signals and synergize with CD47 blockade therapy.

Various modalities—including small molecules, antibodies and gene drugs— have been explored to modulate the CD47-SIRPα signaling. Small molecules like RRx-001 [18] disrupt the CD47–SIRPα pathway through covalent modification of cysteine residues on both CD47 and SIRPα. However, RRx-001 exhibits limited efficacy as a monotherapy and is associated with adverse effects such as infusion reactions, pseudo progression, and induction of TGF-β [19]. In contrast, antibody-based drugs targeting CD47 bind to CD47 expressed on red blood cells and leukocytes, which can trigger Fc-mediated macrophage phagocytosis of these cells. This off-target effect may lead to anemia and thrombocytopenia [20,21]. Other antibody-based agents, such as those targeting SIRPα, face several challenges: they often require combination with opsonizing antibodies to achieve efficacy, may cross-react with SIRPγ and disrupt T-cell function, and undergo rapid internalization, necessitating high or frequent dosing [22,23]. Gene drugs, especially small interfering RNAs (siRNAs), offer targeted gene silencing capabilities against key oncogenic pathways involved in proliferation, signaling and apoptosis [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. siRNAs have shown potential in selectively inhibiting cancer-related genes with minimal off-target effects, offering a powerful therapeutic approach for treating TNBC [[27], [28], [29]]. In the context of blocking CD47–SIRPα signaling, CD47 siRNA offers a significantly improved safety profile compared to antibody-based approaches. This advantage stems from its intracellular mechanism of action: siRNA targets mRNA and indirectly modulates protein expression. Since red blood cells and platelets lack the mRNA translation machinery required for protein synthesis, they remain unaffected by siRNA treatment, thereby reducing the risk of hematological toxicities. Additionally, siRNA enables simpler tumor targeting via nanocarrier EPR effects and endocytosis, avoiding complex antibody engineering. Nonetheless, the clinical translation of siRNA drugs is hindered by their poor stability, negative charge, immunogenicity and poor membrane permeability. An efficient delivery system must therefore be capable of protecting siRNA payloads and overcoming multiple biological barriers. Fusogenic liposomes have emerged as a promising approach for siRNA delivery due to their ability to transverse tumor tissues via transcytosis and facilitate direct cytosolic entry through membrane fusion [[30], [31], [32]]. Previously, we developed a filamentous actin (F-actin) mimicking liposome, CyBI7-IL (CIL), which achieves nearly 100 % membrane fusion efficiency, bypasses endocytosis, and enables efficient intracellular delivery of protein and gene drugs [33,34].

In this study, we report the development of an F-actin mimicking liposome platform for the co-delivery of CD47 and PLK1 siRNAs to enhance macrophage phagocytosis and improve antitumor therapy. By incorporating RGD modified DSPE lipids (DSPE-RGD, R) and DSPE-PEG2K (P) into CIL, we created a stable, fusogenic and tumor-targeted delivery system termed CRPIL. The RGD modification facilitates binding to αvβ3 integrin overexpressed on the surface of tumor cells [35,36], minimizing the liposome-cell distance and promoting efficient cellular uptake via membrane fusion (Scheme 1). This targeted and non-endocytic approach enables deep tissue penetration and selective tumor cell cytosolic siRNA delivery. CRPIL-mediated delivery of siCD47 significantly enhanced macrophage phagocytosis and tumor cell inhibition compared to delivery via Lipofectamine 3000. Co-delivery of siCD47 and siPLK1 further improved macrophage phagocytic activity and tumor suppression. Additionally, in vivo studies in TNBC xenograft mouse models demonstrated the therapeutic potential and gene regulatory effects of CRPIL/siRNA complexes. Experimental

Materials

1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (DOPE), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DOPC), 1,2-dioleoyl-3-trimethylammonium-propane (chloride salt, DOTAP), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[amino(polyethylene glycol)-2000] (DSPE-PEG2k), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-[4-(p-(cysarginylglycylaspartate-maleimidomethyl)cyclohexane-carboxamide] (sodium salt, DSPE-RGD), 1,2-dipalmitoyls-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(7-nitro-2-1,3-benzoxadiazol4-yl) (ammonium salt, NBD-PE), and 1,2-dipalmitoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(lissamine rhodamine B sulfonyl) (ammonium salt, Rho-PE) and cholesterol (chol) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids; CyBI7 was synthesized following a reported protocol [37]. Lipofectamine 3000 (LPF3K) was purchased from Invitrogen. The negative control siRNA (siNC), fluorophore-labeled siNC (siNC-FAM), siRNAs targeting CD47 (siCD47, antisense strand, 5′-UGGUGAAAGAGGUCAUUCCdTdT-3’ [38]) and PLK1 (siPLK1, antisense strand, 5′-GCGAAGAAGGAGAAUAGGGdTdT-3′) were purchased from Shanghai GenePharma Co., Ltd. Triton X-100 was purchased from J&K Chemical Ltd. RPMI-1640, DMEM and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were purchased from Gibco (Grand Island, USA); 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI), trypsin and Penicillin Streptomycin (P/S, 10000 U mL−1) were purchased from HyClone; The MTT Cell Viability Assay Kit was purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. The Super Real PreMix Plus (SYBR Green) real-time PCR was purchased from Tiangen Biomedical (Beijing) Co.Ltd. The Prime Script RT Reagent Kit with gDNA Eraser was purchased from TaKaRa. The LysoTracker Blue DND-22, Trizol, DiO (3,3′-dioctadecyloxacarbocyanine perchlorate), DiR (1,1′-dioctadecyl-3,3,3′,3′- tetramethylindotricarbocyanine iodide), and DEPC water was purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology. Deionized water (18.2 MΩ cm, 25 °C) were used in the study.

Preparation and characterization of liposomes

The liposomes (CIL, CPIL, CRPIL) were prepared using thin-film hydration method. The CIL liposome (DOPE/DOTAP/CyBI7 50/50/5) was prepared following the procedure described in our previous work [39]. For the preparation of CPIL (5 %), the membrane components includingCyBI7 (0.4 μmol), DOPE (4 μmol), DOTAP (3.6 μmol), and DSPE-PEG2k (0.4 μmol) were dispersed in 3 mL of a mixed organic solvent (chloroform/methanol, 9:1 v/v) and added into a pear-shaped flask. The organic solvent was removed by rotary evaporation for 15 min, followed by overnight vacuum desiccation. The resulting dry lipid membrane was then hydrated with 4 mL of water to form multilamellar vesicles. The vesicles were further homogenized after extrusion ten times through a 100-nm polycarbonate membrane under nitrogen. Finally, the homogenized liposomes were dialyzed against water to remove any unencapsulated CyBI7. The preparation of CPIL (10 %) followed a similar procedure, with the only modification being the increase in DSPE-PEG2k to 0.8 μmol and a decrease in DOTAP to 3.2 μmol. For CRPIL, the preparation was the same as for CPIL (5 %) except that DSPE-RGD (0.08 μmol) was added to the lipid mixture. The sizes and zeta potentials of the liposomes were measured by dynamic light scattering (Malvern ZEN3690). The encapsulation efficiency of CyBI7 in the liposomes was determined by measuring the near-infrared (NIR) absorbances of CyBI7 at 819 nm in disrupted liposomes using methanol on a UV–Visible Spectrophotometer (TU1900). The loading contents of CyBI7 in the liposomes were calculated as the amount of total entrapped dyes divided by the total lipids (mol/mol).

Membrane fusion measurement

The membrane fusion efficiencies of CPIL and CRPIL were assessed using a protocol described in our previous work [39], with slight modifications. The cell-mimicking liposome, FRET-L (DOPC/chol, 10:1 M ratio), was synthesized using the film-hydration method. Liposomes (CIL, CPIL, and CRPIL) at a concentration of 2 mM (90 μL each) were mixed with FRET-L (2 mM, 10 μL) and subsequently diluted in phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH = 7.4, 500 μL). The fluorescence energy transfer (FRET) between NBD-PE and Rho-PE was monitored at different time points (0.5, 5, 10, 15, and 20 min) by exciting at 475 nm and monitoring the emission from 500 to 700 nm to track the membrane fusion process. The ratio of acceptor to donor fluorescence (592 nm/516 nm) was recorded as Rt. After reaching the 20-min time point, Triton-X100 (1 %, 66.66 μL) was added to the sample to fully disrupt the liposomes and mimic the composition of the mixed liposomes, following 100 % fusion of FRET-L and the liposomes. Subsequently, the fluorescence spectra were recorded. The fusion efficiency was calculated using the following formula:η=(Rt−R0)/(R100−R0)×100%where R0 is the ratio of acceptor to donor fluorescence for FRET-L (2 mM, 10 μL) in phosphate buffer (590 μL) and R100 represents the ratio of acceptor to donor fluorescence in the FRET-L/liposome mixture after the addition of Triton-X100.

Agarose gel electrophoresis

A 5 % agarose gel was prepared by mixing 2.5 g of agarose with 50 mL of TAE buffer. The liposome (2 mM, CRPIL) and siRNA (10 μM) were thoroughly mixed according to different N/P ratios (5.6, 2.8, 1.4, 0.7, 0.35), and the mixture was then incubated at room temperature for approximately 15 min. After adding the DNA loading buffer, the sample was carefully loaded into the wells of the gel. Electrophoresis was then conducted at 120 V for 30 min. Finally, the gel was placed under ultraviolet light to capture images.

Cyro-transmission electron microscope imaging

Cryo-transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM) imaging of liposomes was performed using a cryo-electron microscope (FEI Talos F200C). For sample preparation, CRPIL (2 mM) was concentrated tenfold through ultrafiltration (MWCO 10 kDa). It was then thoroughly mixed with siRNA (10 μM) at an N/P ratio of 0.7. The sample was subsequently frozen and fixed for 15 min.

Cell culture

Murine breast cancer cells (4T1), mouse mononuclear macrophages (J774A.1) were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). The 4T1 cells were cultured in RPMI medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % P/S, while J774A.1 cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10 % FBS and 1 % P/S.

Biosafety of CRPIL/siNC complexes in vitro

To evaluate the biosafety of CRPIL/siNC complexes, 4T1 cells were treated with free siNC, LPF3K/siNC and CRPIL/siNC complexes (at N/P ratios of 0.35, 0.7, 1.4 and 2.8) for 2 h. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh medium and the cells were incubated for an additional 44 h. Cell viability was then measured using the MTT assay.

Cellular uptake of CRPIL/siNC complexes

4T1 cells (1 × 105 cells per mL, 1 mL) were seeded in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h. Liposomes/siNC-FAM complexes were then added to the cells and incubated for 2 h. After incubation, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS and observed under a fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica). For flow cytometry analysis, cells cultured in 12-well plates were treated in the same manner, collected and analyzed using flow cytometry (AccuriC6, BD).

Uptake of CRPIL/siRNA in tumor spheroids

4T1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate (8000 cells/well) containing 1 % agarose gel, prepared with serum-free RPMI-1640 cell medium. After the formation of 3D tumor spheroids, the spheroids were transferred to a new 96-well plate with fresh serum-free medium. The tumor spheroids were then treated with CIL/siNC-FAM, CPIL/siNC-FAM or CRPIL/siNC-FAM or LPF3K/siNC-FAM complexes (siNC: 200 nM) for 12 h. After treatment, the spheroids were washed three times with PBS. Z-stack confocal images of the spheroids were acquired at 10 μm intervals from the bottom to the middle of the spheroids using a confocal microscope (FV3000, Olympus).

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

To evaluate the gene silencing efficiency of siCD47 delivered by different liposomes, 4T1 cells were treated with PBS (control), CRPIL/siCD47 complexes or LPF3K/siCD47 complexes (siCD47 200 nM) for 2 h. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh RPMI 1640 medium and the cells were incubated for an additional 44 h. The cells were then collected and total RNA was isolated using Trizol (Bioteke Corporation, Beijing, China) and chloroform according to the manufacturer's protocol. For reverse transcription, 1 μg RNA per sample was reverse transcribed into cDNA using the PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). Subsequently, 0.5 μL of cDNA was used for qPCR analysis targeting siCD47 and β-actin, conducted on ABI 7300 system (Applied Biosystem, USA) with RealMaster Mix (SYBR Green) (TIANGEN BIOTECH CO., LTD, Beijing, China). The forward and reverse primers of CD47 are 5′-CAAAACTACCACATTCCCTACCC-3′ and 5′-ACATACAAACACGCCCACAAAC-3′, respectively. The amplification program consisted of 1 cycle at 95 °C for 15 s, followed by 40 cycles at 95 °C for 10 s, 58 °C for 20 s, and 72 °C for 31 s with a final cycle at 4 °C for 30 min. β-actin was used as the endogenous reference, and the data were normalized before statistical analysis.

To evaluate the gene silencing efficiency of CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 complexes, 4T1 cells were treated with PBS (control), CRPIL/siCD47 (siCD47 200 nM), CRPIL/siPLK1 (siPLK1 200 nM) or CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 complexes (siCD47 100 nM, siPLK1 100 nM) for 2 h. After treatment, the medium was replaced with fresh RPMI 1640 medium and the cells were further incubated for 44 h. The cells were collected, total RNA was extracted, and cDNA was synthesized similarly. Next, 1 μL of cDNA was used for qPCR analysis targeting siCD47, siPLK1 and β-actin, using ABI 7300 system RT-qPCR. The forward and reverse primers of PLK1 are 5′-CTCAATTCACCGCAGCCTAGC-3′ and 5′-TCAGTGCCTTCCTCCTCTTGTG-3′, respectively.

Immunofluorescence imaging of calreticulin

To evaluate the expression of calreticulin in tumor cells, CRPIL/siCD47 (siCD47 200 nM), CRPIL/siPLK1 (siPLK1 200 nM), and CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 (siCD47 100 nM, siPLK1 100 nM) complexes were incubated with 4T1 cells for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were fixed with 4 % (w/v) paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1 % (w/v) Triton X-100, and blocked with a cell sealing solution. The cells were then incubated with rabbit anti-calreticulin (Beyotime, Cat. No. AF1666) for 1 h, followed by incubation with FITC-labeled goat anti-rabbit IgG (H + L) (Beyotime, Cat. No. A0562) as the secondary antibody for an additional hour. The nuclei were stained with DAPI for 5 min. Finally, the cells were observed under a fluorescence microscope.

Macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells

To evaluate the phagocytosis of tumor cells by macrophages following liposome/siCD47 treatment, J774A.1 cells (6 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates and labeled with the cell membrane dye DiO for 20 min. Meanwhile, 4T1 cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with PBS, free siCD47, LPF3K/siCD47, or CRPIL/siCD47 (siCD47, 200 nM) in serum-free medium for 2 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. Following treatment, the 4T1 cells were labeled with the cell membrane dye DiR for 20 min, digested with trypsin, and subsequently added into the J774A.1 cells. The co-cultured cells were maintained for an additional 8 h. Afterwards, the co-cultured cells were imaged using a fluorescence microscope. DiO-labeled J774A.1 cells and DiR-labeled 4T1 cells were visualized in the FITC and Cy5 channels, respectively. Phagocytosis was calculated as the percentage of cells that appeared in both channels within the Cy5 channel.

Further, the phagocytosis of tumor cells by macrophages after co-treatment with CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 was measured similarly. Both the siCD47 and siPLK1 were used at a concentration of 100 nM.

Macrophage-mediated inhibition of tumor cells

To evaluate the inhibition of tumor cells by macrophages following liposome/siCD47 treatment, 4T1 cells (3 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates and treated with PBS, free siCD47, LPF3K/siCD47, or CRPIL/siCD47 (siCD47, 200 nM) in serum-free medium for 2 h at 37 °C and 5 % CO2. After treatment, J774A.1 cells (6 × 104 cells/well) were added to the 4T1 cells, and the co-culture was maintained for an additional 46 h. The viability of 4T1 cells was then measured using the MTT assay. The viability of 4T1 cells was calculated as the following formula: (MTT value of co-cultured cells – MTT values of macrophages)/(MTT value of co-cultured cells in PBS group – MTT values of macrophages) × 100 % [40].

Further, tumor cells inhibition by macrophages after CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 co-treatment was evaluated similarly. The concentration of siCD47 and siPLK1 used were both 100 nM.

Animals and tumor xenograft model

Female BALB/c mice (4–5 weeks old, weighing 15–20 g) were obtained from the Animal Center of the Fourth Military Medical University (Xi'an, China). All animal care was conducted in compliance with the Guidance Suggestions for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals. To establish the 4T1 tumor model, 1 × 106 murine breast cancer 4T1 cells were subcutaneously injected into the right lower limb of each mouse.

Intratumoral distribution of siNCs upon local administration

When the tumor size reached approximately 100 mm3, the 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 3) and injected intratumorally with LPF3K/siNC-FAM, CPIL/siNC-FAM or CRPIL/siNC-FAM complexes (siNC-FAM: 0.4 mg/kg). Four hours later, the tumors were isolated and imaged using the IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III). To further examine the intratumoral distribution of siRNAs, tumor slices were prepared and imaged using a confocal microscope.

In vivo biodistribution of siNCs upon systematic administration

When the tumor size reached approximately 100 mm3, the 4T1 tumor-bearing mice were randomly divided into four groups (n = 3) and intravenously injected with PBS, LPF3K/siNC-FAM, CPIL/siNC-FAM or CRPIL/siNC-FAM complexes (siNC-FAM: 2 mg/kg). Mice were anesthetized with 5 % isoflurane in oxygen at selected time points (0.5 h, 1 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, 48 h post-injection), and whole-body NIR fluorescence images were captured using the IVIS imaging system (PerkinElmer IVIS Lumina III). To analyze the tissue distribution of siNCs, the mice were euthanized at 24 h post-injection, and the main organs and tissues, including heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney, stomach, intestine, and tumor, were collected for ex vivo imaging.

In vivo antitumor efficacy

To evaluate the antitumor efficacy of siCD47 delivered by different liposomes, PBS, LPF3K/siCD47, CRPIL/siCD47 complexes (siCD47 = 5 mg/kg, n = 5) were intravenously administrated to 4T1-tumor bearing mice every two days for a total of seven treatments. Tumor volumes and body weights of each mouse were recorded every two days for 16 days. Tumor volumes were calculated using the formula (tumor length) × (tumor width/2)2. On day 16, tumors from different treatment groups were harvested for H&E staining and CD86 immunofluorescence staining.

To assess the antitumor efficacy of siCD47/siPLK1 co-treatment, PBS, CRPIL/siCD47, CRPIL/siPLK1 and CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 complexes (siCD47 = 2 mg/kg, siPLK1 = 2 mg/kg, n = 5) were intravenously administrated to 4T1-tumor bearing mice every two days for six treatments. Tumor volumes and body weights of each mouse were recorded every two days for 14 days. On day 14, the tumors from different treatments were harvested for H&E staining, TUNEL staining and CRT staining.

Western blot assay

To investigate the expression levels of CD47 and PLK1 after treatment, Western blotting assays were performed. Tumor tissues from each group were excised, lysed in ice-cold Pierce RIPA buffer and protein concentrations were determined using Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Yeasen, Cat. No. R21250-500T). Equal amounts of total protein were loaded onto SDS-polyacrylamide precast gels (Epizyme, Cat. No. PG112) and transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Beyotime, Cat. No. FFN02). Membranes were blocked by NcmBlot Blocking Buffer (NCM Biotech, Cat. No. P30500) for 10 min at room temperature and incubated overnight at 4 °C in TBST containing HRP-conjugated Goat Anti-Rabbit IgG(H + L) (Proteintech Group, SA00001-2). After washing, the membranes were incubated with CD47 Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech Group, 20305-1-AP), PLK1 Polyclonal antibody (Proteintech Group, 10305-1-AP) or GAPDH Monoclonal antibody (Proteintech Group, 60004-1-Ig) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence kit (Proteintech, Cat. No.PK10003) with NcmBlot Blocking Buffer (Genecompany, GeneGnome).

Biosafety evaluation

For safety evaluation, major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) from mice were harvested at the end of the treatment, fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde and sliced for H&E staining. The stained slices were scanned using a slide scanner microscope (OPLENIC, D2000).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 9. Data were reported as mean ± s.d. Statistical significance was determined using a two-tailed unpaired Student's t-test for comparisons between two groups and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for comparisons across multiple groups. Specific statistical tests are described in figure legends.

Results and discussion

Characterization of CRPIL/siRNA complexes

The complex of siRNA and CRPIL (CyBI7/DSPE-RGD/DSPE-PEG2000/DOPE/DOTAP) was prepared in two steps: (i) CRPIL liposomes were fabricated using the film hydration method and (ii) CRPIL/siRNA complexes were formed by vigorous mixing (Fig. 1(a)). As control groups, CIL (CyBI7/DOPE/DOTAP), CPIL (CyBI7/DSPE-PEG2000/DOPE/DOTAP, 5 % DSPE-PEG2000) and CPIL (CyBI7/DSPE-PEG2000/DOPE/DOTAP, 10 % DSPE-PEG2000) were prepared. As shown in Fig. 1(a), CRPIL exhibited a narrow size distribution, with an average size of CRPIL of 105 nm. The positive surface charge (+28 mV) facilitated the electrostatic encapsulation of siRNAs within CRPILs. Given that assembly of CyBI7 is critical for membrane fusion [39], we next evaluated whether CyBI7 had been assembled successfully into the biomimetic liposomes (CRPIL). The absorption spectrum of CRPIL exhibited a 20 nm red shift compared to free CyBI7 in methanol (Fig. 1(b)), indicating that CyBI7 formed an assembly within CRPIL. We then assessed the fusion kinetics of CRPIL with cell-mimicking liposomes using fluorescence resonance energy transfer [39], revealing that CRPIL exhibited strong membrane fusion capability, exhibiting 91 % of membrane fusion within 5 min of incubation (Fig. 1(c)). The membrane fusion efficiency of CRPIL was comparable to that of CIL, CPIL (5 %) and CPIL (10 %) (Fig. S1), suggesting that the incorporation of DSPE-PEG2000 and DSPE-RGD in CRPIL did not impair the membrane fusion efficiency. Additionally, both CRPIL and CPIL (5 %) demonstrated excellent serum stability, with negligible change in size and membrane fusion efficiency (Fig. S2). In contrast, CIL precipitated in serum, aggregated and exhibited a lower percentage of membrane fusion (Fig. 1(d) and (e) and S2). The siRNA loading capability of CRPIL was investigated across different nitrogen-to-phosphate (N/P) ratios. siNCs (negative control siRNAs), which do not target any known genes, were used to optimize the N/P ratio. As the N/P ratio increased from 0.7 to 5.6, the size of the complexes remained within the range of 150–250 nm, while the zeta potential rose correspondingly with the increasing proportion of positively charged liposomes (Fig. S3). The entrapment efficiency (EE) of siNC in CRPIL/siNC complexes at various N/P ratios was determined via agarose gel electrophoresis. As the N/P ratio increased, the EE of siNC also increased, while the drug loading capacity (DLC) decreased from 25.4 % to 2.6 % (Fig. 1(f) and (g)). Based on the evaluation of EE and DLC, we selected an N/P ratio of 0.7 to balance siRNA entrapment and loading efficiency, with encapsulation efficiency exceeding 60 % and drug loading rate of approximately 16 % (w/w). We further used cryo-transmission electron microscopy (Cryo-TEM) to investigate the structure of CRPIL/siNC complexes at an N/P of 0.7 (Fig. 1(h)). The complexes exhibited a dark annulus attached with an empty circular arc. We inferred that the abundant siRNAs were in the dark annulus sandwiched between two lipid bilayers, while the empty circular arc represented a half-fused liposome (Fig. 1(i)).

These results confirm that CRPIL exhibits high fusion efficiency, excellent serum stability and a satisfactory siRNA loading capacity, making it an excellent candidate for siRNA delivery.

CRPIL mediated siRNA delivery

To investigate the siRNA delivery capability of CRPIL in vitro, we employed FAM-labeled fluorescent negative control siRNAs (siNC-FAM) instead of unlabeled siNCs. CRPIL/siNC-FAM, CPIL/siNC-FAM, CIL/siNC-FAM, Lipofectamine 3000 (LPF3K)/siNC-FAM complexes were incubated with 4T1 cells for 2 h in serum-free medium. As shown in Fig. 2(a), CIL, CPIL and CRPIL efficiently delivered abundant siNCs into the 4T1 cells, whereas LPF3K delivered significantly fewer siNCs. Notably, siNCs delivered by biomimetic liposomes dispersed well throughout the cytoplasm, indicating successful cytosol delivery. In contrast, siNCs delivered by LPF3K appeared as aggregates, suggesting that they remain localized in the endosomes/lysosomes. Further quantification by flow cytometry of intracellular siNC-FAMs revealed that the siRNA delivery efficiency follows this order: CRPIL > CPIL > CIL > LPF3K (Fig. 2(b) and (c)). The enhanced siRNA delivery by CRPIL compared to LPF3K is attributed to its biomimetic properties, including the CyBI7 assembly for improved fusion, the stability conferred by DSPE-PEG and the tumor cell targeting with DSPE-RGD. Next, we investigated the cellular uptake mechanism of CRPIL/siNC-FAM complexes using various endocytosis inhibitors. Treatment at 4 °C, which blocks energy-dependent endocytosis, did not affect the siRNA uptake, suggesting that CRPIL/siNC-FAM complexes bypassed energy-dependent endocytosis. Furthermore, pre-treatment with genistein (a caveolae-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), chlorpromazine (CPZ, a clathrinid-mediated endocytosis inhibitor), and methyl-β-CD (a lipid raft-mediated inhibitor) did not significantly alter siRNA uptake compared to control groups Fig. 2(d) and (e). It is crucial to deliver genes not only to the tumor cells near the blood vessels but also to the cells located deep within the tumor tissue [41]. To investigate whether CRPIL/siRNA complexes can penetrate deeper regions of a tumor, we prepared three-dimensional (3D) 4T1 cell spheroids. After incubating the spheroids with CRPIL/siNC-FAM in serum-free medium for 12 h, we detected siRNAs as deep as 60 μm from the spheroid surface (Fig. 3(a)). Other biomimetic liposome/siRNA complexes exhibited similar tumor penetration abilities (Fig. S4). This result suggests that the tumor penetration capability of CRPIL is attributable to its fusogenic property. In contrast, the siRNAs delivered by LPF3K were only detectable at the periphery of the tumor spheroid, with trace amounts detected on the outermost layer (Fig. 3(a)). To further explore the mechanism underlying the deep penetration of CRPIL/siRNA, we performed a transwell assay [42] to assess whether siNC in the upper layer of 4T1 cells could be transported to the cells in the lower layer (Fig. 3(b)). The results showed that a significant amount of siRNA was present in the lower chamber of the CRPIL/siRNA group, whereas very little was detected in the LPF3K group (Fig. 3(c)). This result further demonstrates the deep-penetration capability of CRPIL. Moreover, pre-treatment of 4T1 cells in the upper chamber with exocytosis inhibitor (vacuolin) lead to significantly reduced fluorescence intensity of siNC-FAM in the lower-chamber (Fig. S5). Since extracellular vesicles (EVs) secreted by tumor cells can mediate the intercellular transport of drugs in the tumor microenvironment [43] and the exocytosis inhibition reduced the siRNA transport to the lower chamber, we speculated that the siRNA in the lower chambers was transported via EVs from the cells in the upper chambers. These findings demonstrate that CRPIL/siNC complexes enter tumor cells through membrane fusion. This fusogenic capacity not only enhanced cytosolic delivery of siRNAs, but also facilitated deep penetration in tumor spheroids through exosome-mediated transport. Furthermore, CRPIL/siNC complexes exhibited excellent biocompatibility and serum stability, as evidenced by similar siNC uptake in serum-contained medium (Figs. To examine CRPIL/siRNA-mediated delivery of siNC-FAM in tumor tissues in vivo, we began by assessing peritumoral administration. Specifically, LPF3K/siNC-FAM, CPIL/siNC-FAM, and CRPIL/siNC-FAM complexes were peritumorally injected in 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice. Four hours after injection, the accumulation of siRNA in the dissected tumors followed the order: CRPIL/siNC-FAM > CPIL/siNC-FAM > LPF3K/siNC-FAM (Fig. S8). Fluorescence imaging of tumor slices revealed that both CRPIL/siNC-FAM and CPIL/siNC-FAM groups, which possess membrane fusion ability, exhibited significant accumulation of siRNA in both the interior and peripheral zones of the tumor tissue, validating the tissue penetration ability of fusogenic CRPIL and CPIL. Among these, the CRPIL/siNC-FAM group, which also has tumor targeting ability, exhibited a more pronounced siRNA uptake. In contrast, the LPF3K/siNC-FAM group, lacking membrane fusion ability, showed only minimal siRNA presence in the peripheral region of the tumor (Fig. 3(d)). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that fusogenic liposomes can penetrate tumor tissue and RGD ligand in CRPIL can increase the tumor cell accumulation of siRNAs.

To evaluate the systemic delivery efficiency of CRPIL, we compared the tumor accumulation of CIL, CPIL, and CRPIL following intravenous administration. The results showed that CRPIL exhibited the highest level of tumor accumulation (Fig. S9a), suggesting that the RGD ligand in CRPIL also enhances siRNA delivery to tumors during systemic circulation. Next, we compared the biodistributions and tumor accumulation of siNC-FAMs delivered by LPF3K and CRPIL after intravenous injection. Twenty-four hours after injection, siRNAs were primarily distributed in the kidneys and tumors, with minimal distribution in the lungs in both CRPIL/siNC-FAM and LPF3K/siNC-FAM groups (Fig. S9b and S9c). The renal accumulation of siRNA drugs was also reported in Cui's work [44]. We speculate that the renal accumulation of siNCs stems from their leakage from liposome/siNC complexes and their relatively small molecular weight (∼16 kDa), which enables them to traverse the glomerular filtration barrier (∼30 kDa). Comparative analysis revealed that the tumor accumulation of siRNA in the CRPIL/siNC-FAM group was 1.3-fold higher than in the LPF3K/siNC-FAM group.

Tumor cell selectivity is crucial for cancer gene therapy, as it can effectively minimize side effects on normal cells within the tumor. To investigate the tumor cell selectivity of CRPIL, we intravenously administered CRPIL and CPIL to 4T1 tumor-bearing mice and harvested the tumor tissues 24 h post-injection. After labeling various immune cells and 4T1 tumor cells within the tumors with specific fluorescent antibodies, we analyzed the cellular uptake of CRPIL and CPIL using flow cytometry. The results indicated that CPIL exhibited tumor cell selectivity (Fig. 3(e) and S10),suggesting that the fusogenic liposomes inherently possess differential membrane fusion capabilities toward tumor versus normal cells, which is consistent with previous reports [45]. In contrast, CRPIL, which incorporates an RGD ligand, demonstrated higher uptake efficiency and enhanced tumor cell selectivity due to its improved affinity for tumor cells (Fig. 3(f)), aligning with literature findings that targeted ligand modification promotes the uptake of fusogenic liposomes [46]. The superior tumor cell selectivity of CRPIL ensures precise siRNA delivery and reduced off-target toxicity.

These gene transfection experiments confirmed that the exceptional membrane fusion ability of CRPIL enables cytosolic siRNA delivery via membrane fusion, deep tissue penetration, and selective tumor cell targeting. The tumor-targeting capability also contributes to enhanced tumor accumulation and higher siRNA delivery efficiency.

CRPIL-mediated siCD47 delivery to enhance macrophage phagocytosis for tumor therapy

Given the outstanding siRNA delivery capability of CRPIL, we employed it to deliver CD47 siRNA (siCD47) to block the CD47–SIRPα pathway, thereby enhancing macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells. We first assessed the gene silencing efficiency of CRPIL/siCD47 in tumor cells. Quantitative real-time PCR data showed that the CRPIL/siCD47 group (equivalent to 200 nM siCD47) reduced CD47 mRNA levels to 45.8 %, whereas the LPF3K/siCD47 group only reduced CD47 expression to 76.6 % (Fig. 4(a)). This demonstrates that CRPIL/siCD47 achieved more effective gene silencing in 4T1 cells. We next investigated whether CRPIL/siCD47 can enhance phagocytotic efficiency (Fig. 4(b),(c) and 4(d)). J774A.1 macrophages exhibit robust phagocytic capacity [47] and high sensitivity to CD47 inhibition [48], rendering them ideal models for investigating the phagocytosis of 4T1 cells treated with siCD47. Specifically, 4T1 cells were pretreated with PBS, LPF3K/siCD47 or CRPIL/siCD47 for 24 h. The 4T1 cells were then labeled with DiR and co-cultured with DiO-labeled J774A.1 macrophages. After 8 h of co-cultivation, J774A.1 macrophages engulfed 4T1 cells (evidenced by cell colocalization) and the phagocytosis efficiency in CRPIL/siCD47 group reached 84.0 %, which was significantly higher than in PBS (44.8 %), free siCD47 (45.0 %), and LPF3K/siCD47 groups (64.5 %). Given the effective gene silencing of CD47 mRNA and the enhancement of macrophage phagocytic ability by CRPIL/siCD47, we further evaluated whether CRPIL/siCD47 could augment tumor cell inhibition by macrophages. 4T1 cells were pretreated with PBS, LPF3K/siCD47 or CRPIL/siCD47 for 24 h, and then J774A.1 macrophages were added. The cell viability of 4T1 cells was assessed using an MTT assay (Fig. 4(e)). Forty-eight hours after co-cultivation, both LPF3K/siCD47 and CRPIL/siCD47 groups showed effective tumor cell inhibition (Fig. 4(f)).

Given the excellent siRNA delivery ability of CRPIL in tumor tissues, we next evaluated the in vivo antitumor efficacy of CRPIL/siCD47 using a 4T1 subcutaneous tumor model. Specifically, PBS, LPF3K/siCD47, and CRPIL/siCD47 (2 mg/kg siCD47) were intravenously administered to 4T1 tumor-bearing mice every two days for seven times (Fig. 4(g)). Tumor volumes were measured every two days over a 16-day period. As shown in Fig. 4(h) and S11, both the LPF3k/siCD47 and CRPIL/siCD47 groups significantly inhibited 4T1 tumor growth. Notably, CRPIL/siCD47 exhibited potent antitumor activity with a tumor inhibition rate of 69.5 %, which was superior to LPF3K/siCD47 (59.4 %). After the treatment, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed (Fig. 4(i) and (j)). Among the groups, the CRPIL/siCD47-treated mice had the lowest tumor weight. Furthermore, Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of the excised tumors revealed significantly fewer densely packed cells in the CRPIL/siCD47-treated tumors compared to those treated with PBS and LPF3K/siCD47 (Fig. S12). The small difference between CRPIL and LPF3K in vivo could be due to the limited effect of single CD47 inhibition in TNBC therapy.

To explore the molecular mechanism underlying the potent antitumor activity of the CRPIL/siCD47 complexes, western blot assays were performed to assess CD47 expression levels in tumor tissues following different treatments. The CRPIL/siCD47 group significantly downregulated CD47 expression by 61.6 %, compared to only a 3.6 % reduction in the LPF3K/siCD47 group (Fig. 4(k) and S13). It is well-established that the reducing CD47 expression in tumor cells enhances the infiltration of anti-tumor M1-typed macrophages, contributing to the improved tumor suppression through CD47 gene silencing [49]. In our study, the tumor infiltration of CD86-labeled M1-typed macrophages was markedly higher in the CRPIL/siCD47 group, compared to the other treatment groups (Fig. 4(l) and S14). Moreover, the minor changes in hematological data, body weight, and H&E staining of major organs after single and multiple doses further confirm the excellent biocompatibility of CRPIL/siCD47 (Fig. S15 and S16). In addition, the CRPIL/siCD47 based siRNA therapeutics showed similar erythrocytes and platelets level to normal mice (Fig. S17), exempting the side effect of anemia and thrombopenia of CD47 antibody-based therapy.

The siPLK1 co-treatment boosts macrophage phagocytosis for tumor therapy

To further boost macrophage phagocytosis against tumor cells, we co-delivered CD47 and PLK1 siRNAs, as PLK1 inhibition can induce tumor cell apoptosis [50] and was anticipated to upregulate the prophagocytic signal CRT.

To verify that CRPIL can deliver siCD47 and siPLK1 simultaneously, we first assessed their gene silencing effects on CD47 mRNA and PLK1 mRNA. Specifically, siCD47 and siPLK1 were mixed at a molar ratio of 1:1 and electrostatically complexed with CRPIL to prepare the CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 complexes. CRPIL/siCD47 and CRPIL/siPLK1 were also prepared separately as control groups, which were described as siCD47/siPLK1, siCD47 and siPLK1 in the following text. As shown in Fig. 5(a), the siCD47/siPLK1 group (100 nM siCD47 + 100 nM siPLK1) resulted in CD47 mRNA and PLK1 mRNA levels decreased to 67.3 % and 55.6 % respectively, comparable to the gene silencing efficiencies observed for the individual siCD47 (200 nM) and siPLK1 (200 nM) treatments, despite the halved concentration of each siRNA. Next, we examined 4T1 cell apoptosis after treatment with siCD47, siPLK1, and both. The results showed that siPLK1 can induce significant tumor cell apoptosis (31.2 %), while siCD47 induced mild apoptosis (8.14 %) and siPLK1/siCD47 induced highest apoptosis (37.7 %) (Fig. S18). Further, we evaluated changes in the “eat-me” signal calreticulin (CRT) following various treatments by immunofluorescence imaging. The results demonstrated that both siPLK1 and siCD47 significantly enhanced “eat-me” signals with the combined siCD47/siPLK1 treatment eliciting the strongest effect (Fig. S19a and S19b). Given the simultaneous decrease in the “don't eat me” signal and the increase in “eat me” signal, we further investigated the phagocytic efficiency of macrophages toward tumor cells following different treatments. The results showed the phagocytic efficiency of the siCD47/siPLK1 group reached 95.4 %, significantly higher than the efficiencies observed for the siCD47 group (84.4 %) and siPLK1 groups (77.5 %) (Fig. 5(b) and S19c). Further investigation on M1 polarization indicated that all of CRPIL/siCD47, CRPIL/siPLK1, and siCD47/siPLK1 treated tumor cells can significantly promote M1 macrophage polarization (Fig. S20). Furthermore, we investigated the in vivo anti-tumor effect of the CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 complexes. Specifically, CRPIL/siPLK1 complexes (siPLK1, 2 mg/kg), CRPIL/siCD47 complexes (siCD47, 2 mg/kg), and CRPIL/siCD47/siPLK1 complexes (siPLK1, 1 mg/kg; siCD47, 1 mg/kg) were intravenously administered to 4T1 subcutaneous tumor bearing mice every two days (Fig. 5(c)), which were referred to as siPLK1, siCD47, and siCD47/siPLK1, respectively. As shown in Fig. 5(d) and S21, the siCD47/siPLK1 group exhibited significant tumor inhibition, whereas the siCD47 or siPLK1 single treatments resulted in only moderate inhibition. Upon completion of the therapy, the mice were euthanized, and the tumors were collected, photographed, and weighed (Fig. 5(e) and (f)). The siCD47/siPLK1 group had the lowest tumor weight. Tumors harvested on day 14 were processed for Western blot analysis of CD47 and PLK1, as well as staining with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E), terminal deoxynucleotidyl-transferase-mediated dUTP-biotin nick end labeling (TUNEL) and calreticulin (CRT). As shown in Fig. 5(g) and Fig. S22, the siCD47/siPLK1 group achieved a 53.3 % reduction in CD47 mRNA and a 37.4 % reduction in PLK1 mRNA levels. These reductions were comparable to the gene silencing efficiencies of siCD47 (60.0 %) and siPLK1 (31.4 %), despite the halved concentration of each siRNA. The tumor cell density was markedly reduced in both the siCD47 and siPLK1 groups, with the combination treatment (siCD47/siPLK1) exhibiting the lowest cell density (Fig. 5(h)). The siCD47/siPLK1-treated group displayed the highest apoptotic signals in the TUNEL staining and the most pronounced “eat me” signals in CRT staining (Fig. S23). These findings suggest that CRPIL/siRNA complexes effectively co-delivered siCD47 and siPLK1 in vivo, leading to efficient gene silencing, suppression of “don't eat me” signals, enhancement of “eat me” signals, and ultimately, improved tumor inhibition. Moreover, the negligible change in body weight and the H&E staining images of the major organs from the siCD47/siPLK1 group suggested excellent biocompatibility of the combination therapy (Fig. S24).

Conclusions

In summary, we successfully developed a targeted biomimetic fusogenic liposome, CRPIL, for the co-delivery of siCD47 and siPLK1. The membrane fusion capability enables selective siRNA entry into tumor cells via membrane fusion, bypassing lysosomal degradation and promoting deep tumor penetration. The incorporation of targeting ligands in CRPIL further enhances tumor-specific accumulation of siRNA and improves the therapeutic efficacy of siRNA-based drugs. By co-delivering siCD47 and siPLK1, CRPIL/siRNA complexes effectively downregulated the "don't eat me" signal and upregulated the "eat me" signal, thereby enhancing macrophage-mediated phagocytosis of tumor cells and achieving highly efficient gene therapy in a 4T1 tumor model. Overall, our findings provide a biomimetic liposomal co-delivery system that enhances macrophage phagocytosis and tumor suppression, offering a promising avenue for TNBC treatment.
