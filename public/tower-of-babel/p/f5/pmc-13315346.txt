Early Postrranscatheter Aortic Valve Replacement Heart Failure Hospitalization and Outcomes in Older Patients
Authors: Harun Kundi, Martin Leon, David J Cohen, Alexandra Popma, Yukari Kobayashi, Bjorn Redfors, Lauren S Ranard, Maria C Alu, Julia B Thompson, Jennifer von Stein, Philipp von Stein, Guido Ascione, Afek Kodesh, Juan F Granada, Jeffrey J Popma

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTranscatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) improves symptoms and survival in patients with severe aortic stenosis, yet the incidence and prognostic significance of early postprocedural heart failure (HF) hospitalization remain incompletely characterized. Our objective is to determine the incidence, clinical characteristics, prognostic implications, and predictors of early HF hospitalization after TAVR.MethodsUsing the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review database, we identified fee‐for‐service beneficiaries ≥65 years who underwent TAVR between January 1, 2017, and November 30, 2021. Early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization was defined as at least 1 HF readmission occurring between 31 and 365 days after the index procedure. Patients were classified according to the presence or absence of HF hospitalization during this period. Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association with mortality after adjustment for clinical, procedural, and sociodemographic factors.ResultsAmong 233 309 patients undergoing TAVR, 45 502 (19.5%) experienced HF hospitalization within 1 year. Patients with early HF hospitalization had markedly higher mortality compared with those without HF hospitalization, including 1‐year mortality (43.8% versus 3.6%) and 5‐year mortality (80.6% versus 41.0%) (log‐rank P<0.001). After multivariable adjustment, early HF hospitalization was strongly associated with increased mortality (adjusted hazard ratio [HR], 3.17 [95% CI, 3.11–3.22]). The strongest predictors of early HF hospitalization were advanced cardiac damage stage (adjusted‐subdistribution HR, 1.95 [95% CI, 1.88–2.02]) and high‐risk frailty (adjusted‐subdistribution HR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.58–1.72]), with graded risk across stages.ConclusionsNearly 1 in 5 older adults undergoing TAVR experience early HF hospitalization, which is associated with markedly increased short‐ and long‐term mortality.Keywords: cardiac damage, clinical outcomes, frailty, heart failure, social vulnerability, transcatheter aortic valve replacementSubject Categories: Aortic Valve Replacement/Transcather Aortic Valve Implantation

Transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) has changed the management of severe symptomatic aortic stenosis in older adults, providing a less invasive alternative to surgical aortic valve replacement in selected populations with symptomatic 1 , 2 , 3 and asymptomatic 4 aortic stenosis. Patients enrolled in clinical trials and registries may have a different disease risk than older patients treated with TAVR in real‐world clinical practice. 5

Heart failure (HF) hospitalization after TAVR, among other factors, 6 , 7 , 8 may serve as a clinically meaningful indicator for identifying patients at higher risk of adverse post‐TAVR outcomes. Clinical comorbidities, including frailty, have been associated with all‐cause and HF‐related hospitalization, 9 , 10 and 30‐day and 1‐year rehospitalizations are associated with late mortality after TAVR. It is also well established that the extent of baseline cardiac damage affects late mortality after TAVR 11 , 12 ; however, prior studies have not comprehensively studied the frequency, prognostic significance, and determinants of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization or clarified its relationship with baseline cardiac damage and long‐term mortality.

Our objectives were (1) to characterize the incidence of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, defined as HF readmission within 1 year after TAVR; (2) to evaluate the association between early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization and short‐ and long‐term mortality; (3) to identify independent predictors of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, including the extent of baseline cardiac damage; and (4) to assess the association between baseline cardiac damage severity and 5‐year mortality. To address these objectives, we used a nationwide database of older patients undergoing TAVR for aortic valve disease.

METHODS

Data Availability, Transparency, and Openness Statement

This study used administrative claims data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services. Due to data use restrictions and patient privacy protections, the data and analytic code are not publicly available. Access to the data may be granted to qualified researchers through the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services with an approved Data Use Agreement. The analytic methods are described in sufficient detail within the article to allow replication of the study findings.

Ethics Statement

This study was approved by the Sterling Institutional Review Board with a waiver of informed consent due to the use of deidentified administrative claims data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services.

Study Population

We used the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review database from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services to identify beneficiaries aged 65 and older who underwent TAVR between January 1, 2017, and November 30, 2021. TAVR procedures were identified using International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Procedure Coding System (ICD‐10‐PCS) codes 02RF38H and 02RF38Z. The Medicare Provider Analysis and Review database captures 100% of inpatient hospitalizations for Medicare fee‐for‐service beneficiaries, representing ~50% of patients aged 65 years and older in the United States. 13

To ensure complete ascertainment of baseline characteristics, we included only those with continuous enrollment in Medicare Parts A and B, without Medicare Advantage coverage, for at least 12 months before the index hospitalization. To ensure adequate follow‐up for outcome assessment, beneficiaries were also required to have continuous Medicare coverage for at least 12 months following the TAVR procedure unless death occurred earlier. For patients with multiple qualifying TAVR procedures during the study period, only the first procedure was included. Although data were available through the end of 2022, we limited the inclusion period to procedures performed through November 30, 2021, to ensure a complete 1‐year follow‐up period for capturing HF readmissions after TAVR. We applied a 30‐day blanking period after TAVR to exclude patients who either died or were readmitted due to any reason within the first 30 days postprocedure. This approach ensured that HF readmissions used in the analysis reflected the postdischarge clinical trajectory rather than periprocedural complications or events related to acute recovery.

HF hospitalizations were identified using ICD‐10 Clinical Modification (ICD‐10‐CM) diagnosis codes. We searched the subsequent diagnosis fields in each inpatient claim after discharge from the index hospitalization and flagged readmissions with any diagnosis beginning with I501, I502, I503, I504, I509, I110, I130, or I132. 14 , 15 To ensure clinical relevance and capture only acute events present at the time of admission, we required the corresponding present on admission indicator to be coded as “Y.”

Baseline Comorbidities

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were determined using secondary diagnosis codes marked as “present on admission=Y” during the index hospitalization, as well as by diagnosis codes from all hospitalizations within 1 year before the index hospitalization. Comorbidities were defined using the Elixhauser comorbidity index. Race and ethnicity were grouped into White, Black, and Other (including individuals who were not classified as White or Black, including Asian, Hispanic, North American Native, multiracial, and other unspecified groups) categories. Frailty was assessed using the Hospital Frailty Risk Score (HFRS), a validated administrative tool developed in the United Kingdom. 16 The score was calculated for each patient based on 109 ICD‐10 diagnostic codes derived from all hospitalizations occurring within the 12 months before the index admission, as well as secondary diagnoses marked as “present on admission=Y” during the index hospitalization, as detailed in Table S1. Based on established thresholds, patients were categorized into 3 groups: low frailty risk (HFRS <5), intermediate frailty risk (HFRS 5–15), and high frailty risk (HFRS >15). 16 Social vulnerability was evaluated using the 2020 Social Vulnerability Index (SVI) developed by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry. Patients' ZIP codes, obtained from Medicare records, were mapped to county‐level Federal Information Processing Standards codes and subsequently linked to the publicly available SVI data set. 17 The index includes 4 domains: socioeconomic status, household composition and disability, minority status and language, and housing type and transportation. 18 An overall SVI score was computed from these domains and used to categorize each patient's community into quartiles of vulnerability. Quartile 1 (Q1) represented counties with the lowest vulnerability (SVI score<0.25), and Quartile 4 (Q4) represented those with the highest vulnerability (SVI score>0.75). 17

Extent of Cardiac Damage

Cardiac damage was categorized using a hierarchical staging system based on structural and functional abnormalities documented via administrative claims in the year preceding the index hospitalization. 11 Patients were assigned to 1 of 4 mutually exclusive stages: Stage 0 (no cardiac damage): absence of any relevant cardiac structural or functional disease; Stage 1 (left ventricular damage): evidence of left ventricular dysfunction or cardiomyopathy; Stage 2 (left atrial or mitral valve damage): presence of mitral valve disease or atrial fibrillation; Stage 3 (right‐sided cardiac damage): right ventricular failure, tricuspid valve disease, or pulmonary hypertension. Each patient was assigned the highest applicable stage. Detailed definitions and corresponding ICD‐10‐CM codes for each stage are provided in the Table S2.

Clinical Outcome Assessment

The primary outcome of the study was 1‐year HF readmission, used to classify patients based on the presence or absence of HF hospitalization after TAVR. Patients who experienced at least 1 HF hospitalization between days 31 and 365 following the TAVR procedure were classified as having early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, whereas those with no HF hospitalization during this window were classified as having no HF hospitalization (31–365 days). The secondary outcome was all‐cause mortality, obtained from the Medicare Master Beneficiary Summary File. Mortality follow‐up began at day 30 after the index procedure and continued through a maximum of 5 years. Follow‐up was administratively censored on December 31, 2022.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean±SD and categorical variables as counts and percentages. Baseline characteristics between patients with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization and those with no HF hospitalization (31–365 days) were compared using chi‐square tests for categorical variables and independent 2‐sample t tests for continuous variables. Given the large sample size, parametric methods were considered appropriate under the central limit theorem. To identify predictors of 1‐year HF readmission, a Fine–Gray subdistribution hazard model was employed, treating death as a competing risk. To account for clustering at the hospital level, a random intercept was included in the model. Covariates were prespecified and selected a priori based on established clinical relevance, prior literature on TAVR outcomes, and biological plausibility. The objective of multivariable modeling was confounder adjustment rather than the development of a predictive risk score, and no automated variable selection procedures were used. These included the following covariates: age, sex, race, comorbidities, TAVR access site (transfemoral versus transapical), frailty, social vulnerability, and cardiac damage stages. Significant predictors of HF readmission identified from the model are presented as subdistribution hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% CIs. A time‐varying exposure approach was used to classify patients: all individuals entered the analysis in the no HF hospitalization group and were reclassified at the time of their first HF hospitalization occurring within 1 year after TAVR, if applicable. Survival probabilities were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier curves to compare 5‐year all‐cause mortality between patients with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization and those with no HF hospitalization (31–365 days).

To evaluate whether the relationship between HF hospitalization status and long‐term outcomes differed among prespecified subgroups, we constructed 3 separate multivariable Cox proportional hazards models examining effect modification by age category (65–74, 75–84, ≥85 years), cardiac damage stage (Stage 0, 1, 2, 3) and frailty risk (low, intermediate, high risk). In each model, we included an interaction term between HF hospitalization status and the subgroup variable being tested for interaction, while retaining the other 2 key covariates (eg, in the age‐stratified model, adjustment was made for frailty and cardiac damage stage). Models were adjusted for sex, race, comorbidities, access site (transfemoral versus transapical), and social vulnerability. The subgroup of patients without HF hospitalization (31–365 days) within the lowest‐risk stratum (eg, age 65–74, stage 0, or low frailty) served as the reference category. Mortality rates at 1 and 5 years were derived from Kaplan–Meier survival curves. Interaction P values were calculated using Wald tests for joint significance. Statistical significance was defined as a 2‐sided P value of <0.05. All analyses were conducted using Stata version 17.0 (StataCorp) and SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute).

RESULTS

Patient Classification and Baseline Clinical Findings

The initial cohort consisted of 286 407 Medicare beneficiaries aged 65 years and older who underwent TAVR between January 1, 2017, and November 30, 2021. After excluding patients who died within 30 days post procedure (n=5840) or experienced hospitalization during the same period (n=48 724), a final analytic cohort of 233 309 patients was included. These patients were followed longitudinally and classified into 2 groups based on HF readmission status within 1 year: no HF hospitalization (31–365 days) (n=187 807, 80.5%) and early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization (≥1 HF hospitalization between 31 and 365 days; n=45 502, 19.5%) (Figure 1). Among patients with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, the median (Q1, Q3) time from TAVR to first HF hospitalization was 117 (46–14) days.

Baseline demographic and clinical findings in the study cohort are stratified by early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization status (Table 1). The mean age of the overall cohort was 80.1±7.4 years, with a higher proportion of patients experiencing early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization in the oldest age group (85+ years: 34.9% versus 29.2%, P<0.001). Compared with patients without HF hospitalization (31–365 days), those with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization were more likely to be Black (4.8% versus 3.4%, P<0.001) and were more likely to have comorbidities including renal failure, complicated diabetes, chronic pulmonary disease, liver disease, coagulopathy, fluid and electrolyte disorders, deficiency anemia, depression, and weight loss (all P<0.001). The mean Elixhauser comorbidity index was significantly higher among patients with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization compared with those with no HF hospitalization (31–365 days) (7.5 versus 5.6, P<0.001). Survival Outcomes by Early Post‐ TAVR HF Hospitalization Status

Patients with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization experienced substantially higher 5‐year mortality compared with those with no HF hospitalization (31–365 days) during the same period (Table 3, Figure 2). At 1 year, the cumulative all‐cause mortality was 43.8% among patients with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, in contrast to just 3.6% among those without HF hospitalization. This mortality gap continued to widen over time, with 5‐year mortality reaching 80.6% versus 41.0%, respectively. The difference in survival between the 2 groups was statistically significant (log‐rank P<0.001) (adjusted HR [aHR], 3.17 [95% CI, 3.11–3.22]).

Subgroup and Cardiac Damage Stage–Specific Outcomes

Across all prespecified subgroups, early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization was consistently associated with significantly higher 1‐year and 5‐year mortality compared with no HF hospitalization (31–365 days) (Table 3). Among patients aged ≥85 years, those with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization had a 5‐year mortality of 86.3% (aHR, 5.41 [95% CI, 5.24–5.58]), compared with patients aged 65 to 74 years with no HF hospitalization (31–365 days). Among patients with high‐risk frailty, early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization was associated with an 88.3% 5‐year mortality (aHR, 4.25 [95% CI, 4.07–4.43]), compared with patients with low‐risk frailty and no HF hospitalization. Similarly, in the cardiac damage stage subgroup analysis, patients with Stage 3 cardiac damage who experienced early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization had a 5‐year mortality of 85.4% (adjusted HR, 4.11 [95% CI, 3.98–4.24]), compared with those without HF hospitalization. Interaction terms between HF hospitalization status and each subgroup were statistically significant (P for interaction <0.001).

Predictors of Early Post‐TAVR HF Hospitalization

Within 1 year of TAVR, the cumulative incidence of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization increased progressively with greater baseline cardiac damage. Early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization occurred in 11.0% of patients with Stage 0 cardiac damage and 15.7% with Stage 1. The rates were substantially higher in advanced stages: 24.9% in Stage 2 and 31.6% in Stage 3 (Figure 3A). In addition, at 5 years, the mortality rate was 38.4% for Stage 0, 39.1% for Stage 1, 52.7% for Stage 2, and 58.8% for Stage 3 (Figure 3B).

Predictors of Early Post‐TAVR HF Hospitalization

Predictors of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization are shown in Figure 4. Older age (aHR, 1.01 per year [95% CI, 1.01–1.01]), chronic pulmonary disease (aHR, 1.33 [95% CI, 1.31–1.36]), lymphoma (aHR, 1.32 [95% CI, 1.24–1.41]), diabetes (complicated) (aHR, 1.34 [95% CI, 1.31–1.37]), renal failure (aHR, 1.27, [95% CI, 1.24–1.30]), and transapical access (aHR, 1.12 [95% CI, 1.05–1.20]) were significantly associated with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization. However, the strongest were the presence and severity of cardiac damage, which demonstrated a stepwise association with increased risk (Figure 4). Compared with patients without cardiac damage (Stage 0), those with Stage 1 (left ventricular damage) had a 18% higher risk of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization (aHR, 1.18 [95% CI, 1.14–1.23]), those with Stage 2 (left atrial or mitral valve damage) had a 78% higher risk (aHR, 1.78 [95% CI, 1.72–1.83]), and those with Stage 3 (right‐sided cardiac damage) had a 95% higher risk (aHR, 1.95 [95% CI, 1.88–2.02]). Frailty was also a strong independent predictor of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization: intermediate‐risk frailty was associated with a 42% higher risk (aHR, 1.42 [95% CI, 1.39–1.46]), and high‐risk frailty with a 65% higher risk (aHR, 1.65 [95% CI, 1.58–1.72]), compared with patients at low risk of frailty.

DISCUSSION

In our analysis of more than 230 000 older patients undergoing TAVR in the United States, we found that early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization within 1 year occurred in ~1 in 5 patients, despite remaining free of hospitalization events within 30 days after TAVR. Patients who experienced early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization had a >12‐fold higher risk of 1‐year mortality and an ~2‐fold higher risk of 5‐year mortality. By 5 years, nearly 80% of these patients had died, underscoring the substantial long‐term prognostic implications of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization. To our knowledge, this is the first nationwide study to characterize the frequency and prognostic significance of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization using an objective, time‐based definition, while simultaneously incorporating both cardiac damage staging and frailty into a unified risk framework. This approach provides a clinically meaningful framework for identifying high‐risk patients, enabling targeted care strategies that may improve long‐term outcomes.

Early (30‐Day) Readmission After TAVR

We did not include patients with hospitalizations occurring within 30 days after TAVR in the definition of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, as periprocedural readmissions are frequently related to procedural complications or postprocedural fluid management and may not reflect the patient's underlying clinical status beyond the early recovery period. Previous studies have highlighted the clinical significance of early (<30‐day) readmissions after TAVR, including those due to HF. A validated risk score predicting 30‐day readmission has demonstrated a wide range of risk estimates, from 4.6% to 80.4%, depending on patient characteristics. 18 Zahid et al. 19 specifically evaluated early HF readmissions after TAVR and reported a 3.6% 30‐day HF readmission rate. Predictors of early HF readmissions included chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, atrial fibrillation, chronic HF, preexisting pacemaker, complete heart block during the index hospitalization, paravalvular regurgitation, chronic kidney disease, and end‐stage renal disease. Moreover, patients with early HF readmissions experienced significantly higher 30‐day mortality compared with those with non‐HF readmissions (4.9% versus 3.3%, P<0.01). 19 By excluding these early events, our study focused on patients who initially appeared to recover well from the procedure but experienced persistent or recurrent HF burden in the later post‐TAVR period.

Early Post‐TAVR HF Hospitalization

We defined early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization as any HF hospitalization occurring between 31 days and 1 year after TAVR, using both primary and secondary diagnosis fields. This approach aimed to maximize the capture of clinically meaningful HF events, as prior studies have demonstrated that reliance on the primary diagnosis alone may substantially underestimate HF burden. Inclusion of secondary diagnoses and present on admission flags has been shown to improve the accuracy of claims‐based event identification. 14 , 15 We acknowledge that the incorporation of secondary diagnosis fields may introduce variability in event specificity; however, this approach reflects the complexity of HF‐related hospitalizations in real‐world clinical practice. Importantly, we do not interpret all post‐TAVR HF hospitalizations as procedural failure. Rather, early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization is treated as a pragmatic, claims‐based marker of persistent HF burden and adverse post‐TAVR clinical trajectory. Although some patients with substantial baseline HF burden may still derive clinical benefit from TAVR despite subsequent HF hospitalization, our findings indicate that patients experiencing early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization constitute a distinct high‐risk subgroup warranting closer clinical surveillance and targeted management.

Multiple factors contribute to favorable clinical outcomes after TAVR, including improvement in ventricular function and reverse remodeling. Prior studies have shown that ~25% of patients experience an absolute ≥5% improvement in LVEF, which is associated with a 52% reduction in clinical events. 6 Other studies have demonstrated that the extent of cardiac damage, even beyond left ventricular dysfunction, predicts adverse outcomes. 7 , 8 Approximately 25% of patients show improvement in left ventricular function after TAVR, defined as an absolute improvement of the left ventricular ejection fraction by ≥5%; these patients had a 52% reduction in clinical events compared with those patients who did not improve their left ventricular function. 6 Five‐year mortality has been shown to increase progressively from 24% in patients with no cardiac damage to 57% in those with advanced right‐sided dysfunction. 6 Others have also studied the importance of rehospitalization after TAVR. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 , 24 , 25 , 26 Patients with readmissions had a higher burden of comorbidities including higher Society of Thoracic Surgeons score, 21 prior HF, 9 , 22 extent of myocardial damage, 23 mitral regurgitation, 24 stroke, 22 atrial fibrillation, 9 conduction disturbances, 22 pulmonary disease, 21 peripheral arterial disease, 20 diabetes, 21 immunosuppression, 20 prior percutaneous coronary interventions, 20 ECG evidence of left ventricular strain, 25 dialysis, 20 , 21 and socioeconomic factors. 26 Readmissions are associated with 2‐fold or higher 1‐year mortality risk. 9 , 20 Our study expands on these findings by showing that the extent of baseline cardiac damage was the strongest predictor of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization. Patients with left atrial/mitral valve abnormalities (Stage 2) or right‐sided structural abnormalities (Stage 3) had the highest risks of late HF hospitalization. These pathophysiologic changes may be only partially reversible with aortic valve replacement, supporting the notion that earlier referral and intervention—before irreversible remodeling—may optimize TAVR benefit.

Our study is the largest to examine predictors of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization among older patients undergoing TAVR. The strongest predictors were related to the extent of preexisting cardiac damage. Patients with left atrial or mitral valve damage (Stage 2) and right‐sided cardiac damage, including pulmonary hypertension or right ventricular dysfunction (Stage 3) had the highest predictive risk of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization. This pathophysiologic cascade may not be fully reversible even after relief of aortic stenosis, limiting the clinical benefit of TAVR in late‐stage disease. These findings underscore the importance of timely referral and intervention, ideally before irreversible structural cardiac damage develops to maximize symptomatic and prognostic gains from TAVR. The extent of cardiac damage was also associated with higher 5‐year mortality rates.

In addition to previously reported predictors of rehospitalization, we found that frailty also emerged as a powerful independent predictor of an early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, demonstrating a clear incremental risk with the higher grades. Compared with low‐risk patients, those classified as high‐risk had nearly a 65% higher risk of HF readmission, even after adjustment for age, comorbidities, and procedural factors. These results underscore the need to integrate frailty assessment into routine pre‐TAVR evaluation—not only as a prognostic tool for procedural risk but as a means to guide longitudinal management and recovery planning. Given that frailty reflects a diminished physiologic reserve, these patients may benefit from early postdischarge follow‐up, cardiac rehabilitation, and nutritional support. Our findings also raise important considerations for clinical management at 2 critical junctures: (1) the time of TAVR and (2) the period following a first HF hospitalization. Given the high mortality among patients with early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, particularly those with advanced cardiac damage or frailty, there may be value in more aggressive periprocedural medical optimization.

Limitations

Our study has several important limitations. First, as with all analyses based on administrative claims data, there is a risk of miscoding or misclassification of diagnoses, procedures, and comorbid conditions. Second, administrative data lack granular clinical detail such as left ventricular ejection fraction, valve gradients, residual valvular disease, and echocardiographic parameters that could help refine risk stratification and characterization of post‐TAVR clinical trajectories. Third, the definition of cardiac damage stage used in our analysis was based on diagnosis codes rather than imaging data and has not been formally validated. Therefore, we were unable to confirm whether the observed cardiac damage was directly attributable to aortic stenosis, and it may reflect cardiac pathology from other causes. Fourth, we were unable to assess the presence of procedural complications, residual hemodynamic abnormalities, or device‐specific variables, all of which may influence post‐TAVR outcomes. Fifth, we did not have access to information on medication use, adherence, or adjustments in HF therapy following discharge. As a result, we could not evaluate whether optimization of guideline‐directed medical therapy contributed to improved outcomes or reduced readmissions. Sixth, although we included data from 2017 through 2021, the COVID‐19 pandemic may have influenced hospitalization patterns, coding practices, and access to follow‐up care during the 2020 to 2021 period. These disruptions may have introduced bias or variability in the observed rates of HF readmission and mortality, particularly during the pandemic era. Finally, residual confounding factors may have influenced both the likelihood of HF readmission and long‐term mortality. Furthermore, although death, particularly from cardiovascular causes, could reasonably be considered a marker of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, cause‐specific mortality is not available in the Medicare Provider Analysis and Review data. Therefore, we treated death as a competing risk rather than incorporating it into the definition of the primary end point.

CONCLUSIONS

In our analysis of >230 000 older patients undergoing TAVR, we identified nearly 20% experienced early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization, defined as HF hospitalization occurring between 31 days and 1 year after the procedure. Our findings demonstrate that cardiac damage stage, frailty, and multiple comorbid conditions are key predictors of early post‐TAVR HF hospitalization and should be considered for longitudinal care planning. These results support comprehensive risk assessment and targeted postdischarge strategies to improve clinical outcomes and reduce readmissions among high‐risk TAVR recipients.
