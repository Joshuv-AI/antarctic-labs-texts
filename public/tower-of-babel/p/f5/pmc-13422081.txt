Goals of care discussions in hospitalised patients: systematic review of randomised trials

Introduction

For hospitalised patients at risk for serious illness,1 goals of care discussions (GOCDs) between patients, their substitute decision makers (SDMs) and healthcare providers (HCPs) ensure that treatment decisions are informed and aligned with the patient’s values and goals, while considering realistic treatment outcomes for the patient’s current clinical context and prognosis.2 3 Despite their importance, up to 75% of patients will lack decision-making capacity at the time of their serious illness,4 5 with fewer than 25% having communicated their treatment preferences with either their SDMs or HCPs. Consequently, as many as one in four patients who die in hospital receive unwanted and/or non-beneficial care at the end of life.6–8 This has led some to describe the failure to engage in GOCDs with at-risk hospitalised patients as a medical error.1 9

Numerous barriers limit HCP engagement in GOCDs with patients and SDMs.10–13 GOCDs are a complex clinical skill,14 yet few HCPs receive formal training, in part because GOCDs are not viewed as the exclusive responsibility of any particular specialty. This challenge is compounded by the limited evidence on the most effective communication strategies.15 16 Time constraints, competing clinical priorities, identifying the right patients at the right time during hospitalisation and the absence of standardised processes further compromise the utilisation and quality of GOCDs between HCPs and hospitalised patients and their SDMs.12 17 18 In response, various communication tools and decision aids have been developed to support GOCDs;16 19 20 however, few have been rigorously evaluated in randomised trials, and there is a lack of consistent reporting of clinically important patient-centred and HCP-centred outcomes.

The objective of this systematic review is to identify and summarise evidence from randomised trials evaluating communication tools and decision aids developed to facilitate GOCDs between HCPs and hospitalised patients and their SDMs.

Methods

This systematic review is an update of a previous review by Myers et al.19 In contrast to that review, we restricted inclusion to randomised trials published from 2015 onward to avoid the high risk of bias associated with the inclusion of non-randomised trials. We restricted inclusion to hospitalised patients only because GOCDs are an essential service for all hospitalised patients regardless of any pre-existing advanced care plan (ACP). ACPs identify SDMs and prepare patients and SDMs for future healthcare decisions but are generally ineffectual in guiding specific treatment decisions that must be made in patients facing acute and serious changes in health that require complex shared decision making and consent.19 This time frame and narrower population focus were selected to inform the protocol development, by the current trial authors, of a randomised trial evaluating a novel communication tool to facilitate GOCDs between HCPs and hospitalised patients and their SDMs.

We did not define eligible outcomes a priori; instead, all randomised trials were included regardless of their primary outcomes. For the evidence synthesis, we restricted our summary to primary outcomes only, as these trials were typically powered to test those hypotheses.

Search for randomised trials

We adapted the search strategy from Myers et al19 to identify eligible trials; as such, a protocol was not necessary. The search was conducted in the following databases: Cochrane CENTRAL, PubMed, Web of Science and CINAHL Complete.

Literature search strategy

All databases were searched from January 2015 to July 2025 using the literature search strategy provided in online supplemental table 4. We conducted backward citation searching by reviewing the reference lists of all included trials. In addition, we searched ClinicalTrials.gov (https://clinicaltrials.gov/expert-search—accessed 4 July 2025) for protocols of randomised trials and, when available, retrieved and included their completed trial results.

Titles and abstracts identified through the searches were screened independently by one reviewer (CD). When uncertainty regarding eligibility arose, all authors independently reviewed the title and abstract, and inclusion for full-text review was determined by consensus. Full-text articles were then assessed for final inclusion by the same reviewer (CD) with any uncertainties again resolved through independent review by all authors and consensus decision-making.

Data extraction and assessment of trial quality and potential for bias

Data from the included trials were independently extracted by one reviewer (CD) and audited by a second reviewer (GD). Discrepancies were resolved through independent review by all authors and consensus decision-making.

We applied the Core GRADE (Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation) framework to evaluate and summarise evidence from the included trials.21 This approach provides a transparent, structured and reproducible method to rate the certainty of evidence for each clinically important outcome.

Trial data were synthesised, and certainty of evidence was assessed using the Core GRADE framework,21 which considers risk of bias,22 inconsistency,23 indirectness,24 imprecision25 and publication bias.21 For each outcome, we prepared a Summary of Findings table that reported absolute and relative effects, the number of participants and trials, certainty ratings (high, moderate, low, very low) and a plain language summary. This approach provides decision-makers, clinicians and researchers with a transparent and methodologically rigorous synthesis of the available evidence from the included trials.

The quality of evidence was downgraded by one level according to the following criteria: (1) imprecision (continuous outcomes with a sample size <400 participants per group, or for binary outcomes with a CI ratio ≥3 for risk ratio (RR) or ≥2.5 for OR);25 (2) inconsistency (statistically significant heterogeneity [I2>50%–75%]);23 (3) risk of bias (trials met one or more of the important risk of bias criteria outlined in the Core GRADE Risk of Bias article);22 (4) publication bias (trials were small and industry-sponsored)22 and (5) indirectness (one population, intervention, comparator, outcome domain is not directly related to the research question).24

Data synthesis and analysis

Primary outcome data were reported as both relative and absolute effects. Relative effects were reported as ORs and 95% CIs for binary outcomes, HRs and 95% CI for time-to-event outcomes and RRs for continuous outcomes. All relative effects values >1 favoured intervention outcomes. Absolute effects were reported as risk differences and 95% CI for binary outcomes, and mean differences and 95% CI for continuous outcomes. All absolute effects >0 favoured intervention outcomes.

Whenever there was acceptable homogeneity across primary outcomes, data were pooled using random effects meta-analysis. All analyses were performed using STATA/MP V.18.0 for Mac (StataCorp).

Results

Search for randomised trials

Literature search results

A search for randomised trials yielded 2836 documents. Of these, 58 underwent a full-text review. From this, 19 trials26–44 were included in the final analysis. In addition, the backward citation and ClinicalTrials.gov search resulted in three further trials45–47 for the final analysis. Trial characteristics

The included trials randomised different units, including patients, hospital wards and hospitals. Trial designs included pilot studies, parallel arms, stepped-wedge and clustered approaches. The interventions were both patient-facing and HCP-facing, frequently using multimodal approaches. Educational videos, non-physician facilitators and previously validated ACP tools were commonly employed. Most targeted populations had pre-existing, life-limiting illnesses. The sample sizes of the included trials ranged from 22 to 44 235, with five trials enrolling fewer than 100 participants. Detailed information on the included trials can be found in online supplemental table 1.

Core GRADE certainty of evidence

Given that the trials varied widely in their primary outcomes, the certainty of evidence was assessed individually for each trial. Online supplemental table 2 summarises the certainty ratings and the rationale for these ratings. Among the included trials, 17 trials26–33 35–37 40 41 43–46 were rated as moderate certainty of evidence, and the other 5 trials34 38 39 42 47 as low certainty of evidence.

Risk of bias

Trials assigned moderate certainty were predominantly randomised trials with concealed allocation, blinding and intention-to-treat analyses.22 Trials rated as low certainty typically used quasi-experimental designs and had inadequate random sequence generation, poor allocation concealment, missing outcome data and lack of blinding among participants, providers, data collectors, outcome assessors and/or analysts.22 These limitations increased uncertainty in the findings and reduced confidence in the estimates.22

Imprecision

Trials assigned moderate certainty had adequate sample sizes; however, some had wide CIs, leading to moderate imprecision.25 Trials rated as low certainty were typically trials with small sample sizes or small pilot trials with wide CIs, limited events or outcomes underpowered to detect meaningful differences.25

Inconsistency

Trials assigned moderate certainty showed consistent effects across outcomes or clusters.23 Trials rated as low certainty had variability due to single-centre results, heterogeneous interventions or proxy outcomes.23

Indirectness

Trials assigned to moderate certainty measured outcomes directly relevant to the clinical question, whereas trials assigned to low certainty often used proximal, process or surrogate outcomes, with population, intervention, comparator and outcome definitions that did not align with the research focus.24

Publication bias

Publication bias was not considered a significant concern, as the majority of trials, irrespective of their certainty rating, reported outcomes transparently.22 However, some low-certainty trials were small, single-centre pilot trials, increasing the potential for selective reporting and unpublished null results.22

Documentation of GOCDs

Nine trials28–31 33 39 40 45 46 investigated the effect of different interventions on the documentation of GOCDs. Pooled log OR favoured the intervention with a random-effects estimate of 0.38 (95% CI 0.04 to 0.72), corresponding to an OR of 1.46 (95% CI 1.04 to 2.05) (figure 2).

Log OR for GOCD documentation. Values >0 favour interventions. GOCD, goals of care discussion.

Individual trial estimates varied, with several small trials showing stronger positive effects while the larger trials were closer to the null effect. Heterogeneity appeared moderate, consistent with the variability in study populations and variety of different interventions used. Publication bias and small-study effects were subsequently analysed using a funnel plot (online supplemental table 5). Visual inspection of the funnel plot demonstrates asymmetry, with larger studies clustered symmetrically around the pooled estimate, and smaller trials disproportionately showing larger treatment effects. Further analysis using the non-parametric trim-and-fill imputed two potentially missing studies on the left-hand side of the funnel plot, resulting in an attenuation of the log OR to 0.25 (95% CI −0.12 to 0.63), thus crossing the null effect. Taken together, the pooled effect may be influenced by publication bias or selective reporting of small, positive studies.

Preferences for life-sustaining treatment

Nine trials27 33 34 40–44 46 investigated the effect of different interventions on resuscitation preferences. Six trials33 40 41 43 44 46 provided the opportunity to pool data for a No CPR preference outcome. Pooled log OR did not favour the intervention with a random-effects estimate of 0.61 (95% CI −0.05 to 1.28), corresponding to an OR of 1.84 (95% CI 0.95 to 3.60) (figure 3).

Similar to GOCD documentation, several smaller trials showed stronger positive effects while the larger trials were closer to the null effect. Heterogeneity was moderate, consistent with the variability from different study populations and interventions used in the trials. Subsequent analysis using the funnel plot demonstrated asymmetry (online supplemental table 6), with a subsequent analysis suggesting influence from publication bias or selective reporting of small, positive studies.

Psychosocial outcomes

Two trials36 37 investigated the effect of different interventions on psychosocial outcomes. The results could not be pooled as they used different validated measurement tools to assess distress, emotional functioning and quality of life (online supplemental table 3). Regardless of the outcome measurement, there was no improvement in any of these primary outcomes in the intervention groups.

Resource utilisation

Three trials26 38 40 investigated the effect of different interventions on healthcare resource utilisation. These results could not be pooled, as they measured different aspects of utilisation, from hospital length of stay to place of death (online supplemental table 3). None of the trial interventions were shown to reduce healthcare resource utilisation.

Concordant care

Four trials34 38 40 41 investigated the effect of different interventions on concordant end-of-life care or concordance in preferences between SDMs and patients’ preferences (online supplemental table 3). There were mixed effects observed for both outcomes, with some trials demonstrating improved rates of concordant care at the end of life, while others demonstrated no effect, and similar heterogeneity was observed in the intervention effects on concordance between SDMs’ and patients’ treatment preferences.

GOCD processes

Four trials28 32 35 47 investigated the effect of different interventions on various GOCD care processes. These ranged from improving elicitation of patient values, ethical decision making, timing of GOCDs and feasibility of intervention uptake (online supplemental table 3).

Discussion

This systematic review of randomised trials demonstrates that communication tools and decision aids can increase the occurrence and documentation of GOCDs in hospitalised patients. Interventions were most effective when they included structured, patient-facing or clinician-facing prompts, including video decision aids, Jumpstart guides or electronic health record (EHR) alerts.26–33 39–41 43–47 Evidence for changes in treatment preferences, care concordance or clinical outcomes was less consistent.34–38 42

Previous literature

Our findings extend those of Myers et al,19 which included both randomised and non-randomised studies across outpatient and inpatient settings. That review concluded that few tools had sufficient evidence to recommend widespread implementation. By restricting inclusion to hospitalised populations and randomised designs, our review provides a higher-certainty evidence base. We confirm that interventions reliably increase process outcomes, such as GOCD documentation,28–31 33 39 40 45 46 but find limited evidence of impact on patient-centred outcomes such as quality of life, goal-concordant care or healthcare utilisation.27 34 36–38 41 These findings align with broader critiques in the literature. Very few studies provide detailed descriptions of the clinical interaction, and there is a lack of consensus on the essential components and expected outcomes of GOCDs.48 Practices vary in whether GOCDs are considered distinct from or synonymous with decision-making discussions.48 The use of physician orders for life-sustaining treatment also differs across jurisdictions and HCPs, further highlighting the variability in current practice.49

Strengths

Some of the key strengths of this review include the use of a comprehensive and systematically developed search strategy across multiple databases, the strict inclusion of randomised controlled trials enhances the internal validity of the evidence base by reducing potential biases inherent to non-randomised studies. Additionally, this review is among the first to apply the Core GRADE methodology, thereby providing a transparent and structured assessment of certainty in the evidence that can inform both clinical practice and future research priorities.21

Limitations

Despite these strengths, the current systematic review has some limitations. First, the number of trials evaluating goals-of-care tools used in hospitalised patients was limited, reflecting a relatively underdeveloped evidence base for interventions aimed at improving GOCDs. The included studies were heterogenous in both intervention design and outcome measurement, which precluded quantitative synthesis for all outcomes and reduced comparability across trials. Many of the available studies were pilot or single-centre trials with modest sample sizes, contributing to imprecision and limiting confidence in the pooled evidence. Moreover, most reported outcomes were process-oriented, such as documentation rates or billing codes, rather than patient-centred outcomes, such as quality of communication or quality of dying. These factors collectively underscore the need for larger, multicentre randomised trials that evaluate outcomes directly meaningful to patients and families.

Implications for practice

The evidence suggests that structured prompts (videos, guides or EHR nudges) are effective at initiating GOCDs in hospital settings.26–33 39–41 43–47 However, clinicians should be cautious in assuming downstream effects on treatment intensity or end-of-life outcomes. Adoption of these tools should be coupled with training, system-level supports and evaluation frameworks to ensure that increased documentation translates into meaningful patient-centred and family-centred care.14 Interprofessional approaches are particularly important. Non-physician providers, such as nurses, can enhance the patient experience and address physician time constraints.14 Training willing care providers across disciplines, as demonstrated in the Respecting Choices model, may be critical for improving patient outcomes.37 38 40

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that communication tools and decision aids reliably increase the documentation of GOCDs in hospitalised patients, with moderate certainty of evidence. Effects on treatment preferences, concordant care, psychosocial outcomes and healthcare utilisation remain inconsistent. Future research should prioritise large, randomised trials that evaluate clinically important patient and healthcare system outcomes, explore which elements of GOCDs can be standardised and consistently implemented, and assess the effectiveness of interprofessional approaches. Integrating structured tools with targeted care provider training and interprofessional collaboration can enhance the quality and consistency of GOCDs, helping to ensure that discussions are patient-centred and aligned with individual values.
