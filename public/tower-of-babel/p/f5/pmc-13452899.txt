Re: “Safety and heterogeneity of mRNA-based seasonal Influenza vaccines: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials”
Authors: Said Umair Shah, Syed Muhammad Momin, Haris Akbar, Syed Hatim Hussain, Laiba Malik, Hassan Albargy
Published: 2026

To the Editor: We read with interest the systematic review and meta-analysis by Albargy and Othman evaluating serious adverse events (SAEs) and adverse events of special interest (AESIs) after messenger RNA (mRNA)-based seasonal influenza vaccines.1 Their synthesis is timely as mRNA platforms move toward possible routine seasonal use, and the focus on serious safety outcomes is clinically important. To refine interpretation of the reassuring pooled findings, three methodological issues deserve further consideration.

First, the review included 7 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) but analyzed 12 datasets, apparently because some multi-arm trials contributed more than one vaccine–comparator contrast. Although this preserves randomized comparisons, it may introduce a unit-of-analysis concern if multiple contrasts share participants, protocols, investigators, comparator structures, or follow-up procedures. Treating such comparisons as fully independent may give disproportionate weight to particular trial families and make confidence intervals appear more precise than warranted. Cochrane guidance recommends avoiding double-counting in multi-arm studies by combining relevant groups, splitting shared groups, or using models that account for dependent comparisons.2 Therefore, a trial-level sensitivity analysis in which each RCT contributes only one independent contrast, or a multilevel meta-analysis accounting for within-trial correlation, would help clarify whether the null SAE/AESI findings remain robust.2

Second, interpretation of rare vaccine safety outcomes is sensitive to how zero-event data are handled. The authors used a 0.5 continuity correction for single-zero studies and retained double-zero studies while applying random-effects restricted maximum likelihood pooling.1 This approach is not necessarily inappropriate, but for sparse outcomes such as AESIs, the pooled risk ratio may depend materially on the chosen zero-event method. Xu et al emphasized that zero-event meta-analyses require an explicit synthesis framework, and different one-stage or two-stage approaches can yield different inferences.3,4 The review would therefore be strengthened by reporting the number of single-zero and double-zero studies for each outcome and by conducting sensitivity analyses using rare-event methods such as Mantel-Haenszel approaches, generalized linear mixed models, beta-binomial models, and risk-difference analyses.3,4 Presenting absolute effects, such as risk difference per 10 000 vaccinated persons, would also improve clinical interpretability.

Third, the funnel-plot assessment should be interpreted cautiously. The authors reported no clear visual evidence of publication bias or small-study effects;1 however, funnel plots are poorly informative when few independent studies are available, especially with sparse binary outcomes. Sterne et al and Cochrane guidance caution against strong conclusions from funnel-plot symmetry when fewer than approximately 10 studies contribute to an analysis.2,5 Thus, absence of asymmetry should not be interpreted as meaningful reassurance that relevant safety evidence is complete. A more useful approach would be a trial-registry reconciliation table listing all identified mRNA influenza vaccine trials, completion status, publication status, availability of SAE/AESI results, and reasons for exclusion from quantitative synthesis, consistent with PRISMA 2020's emphasis on transparent reporting of records from databases and registers.6

These points do not undermine the value of the authors' contribution. Rather, they emphasize that reassuring safety conclusions for emerging vaccine platforms are strongest when correlated trial datasets, sparse zero-event outcomes, and potentially missing safety evidence are handled transparently. Addressing these issues in future syntheses would strengthen both statistical credibility and clinical interpretation.

Reply

We thank the correspondents, Dr. Shah et al for their comments regarding our study entitled “Safety and heterogeneity of mRNA-based seasonal Influenza vaccines: A systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials”.1 Their observations provide an opportunity to clarify the analytical structure of the review and to refine interpretation of the safety findings.

We agree that multi-arm and multi-cohort vaccine studies require careful handling to avoid inappropriate double-counting of participants or control groups.2 In our review, the term “dataset” referred to an individual treatment-comparator contrast extracted from a randomized cohort, rather than to an entirely separate publication. Several included publications reported multiple randomized cohorts, trial parts, age strata, or separately conducted trials. For example, the two Kandinova3 datasets represented distinct phase 3 trials, whereas the Soens4 datasets represented separately randomized study parts

We acknowledge, however, that the wording “7 randomized controlled trials comprising 12 datasets” may not have sufficiently distinguished publications, parent trials, study parts, and randomized contrasts. The intended analytical unit was the randomized comparison rather than the publication. Multiple contrasts reported within a publication were included only where separate intervention and comparator data could be extracted for distinct randomized cohorts or study components.

To improve transparency, we provide a concise supplementary reconciliation table (at the bottom of this file) describing the parent publication, trial or cohort structure, intervention-comparator contrast, and zero-event classification for each analytic row. This table is intended to clarify the relationship between publications and randomized comparisons and to facilitate assessment of the unit-of-analysis structure. We agree that future syntheses would benefit from more explicit reporting of trial-family structure and prespecified handling of related reports.2

We also agree that AESIs were rare and that interpretation of relative-effect estimates can be sensitive to the selected approach for zero-event studies.5,6 However, zero-event comparisons were not overlooked in our original analysis. As described in the Methods, we applied a continuity correction of 0.5 for comparisons with zero events in one study arm to permit estimation of risk ratios, while retaining double-zero comparisons to preserve information regarding outcome rarity. We also explicitly reported that AESIs were rare, with several included comparisons reporting zero events in one or both arms.

Following re-examination of the extracted event data, the SAE analysis comprised 3 single-zero comparisons and 1 double-zero comparison, whereas the AESI analysis comprised 3 single-zero comparisons and 5 double-zero comparisons. To examine whether the published AESI finding was robust to alternative approaches for sparse zero-event data, we conducted complementary AESI sensitivity analyses. Using a Mantel-Haenszel risk-ratio analysis that excluded double-zero comparisons and applied a continuity correction only to single-zero comparisons, the pooled AESI estimate was RR=0.77 (95% CI: 0.42-1.41). This was materially consistent with the primary random-effects estimate reported in the original article (RR= 0.76, 95% CI: 0.41-1.36). A complementary risk-difference analysis estimated approximately 0.3 additional AESIs per 10 000 vaccinated participants, with a 95% CI ranging from 2.1 fewer to 2.7 more AESIs per 10,000 participants. Thus, both alternative sparse-event approaches produced results consistent with the published analysis and did not identify a statistically detectable excess AESI risk among mRNA vaccine recipients. These findings should not be interpreted as establishing the absence of risk, particularly for extremely rare adverse outcomes. Rather, the available randomized evidence did not identify a statistically significant increase in SAEs or AESIs.

Finally, we agree that our original wording concerning funnel plots had a definitive tone. Given the relatively lower number of independent studies and the sparse nature of SAE and AESI outcomes, the funnel plots are best to be regarded as descriptive visual displays only and are suggested be treated cautiously when trying to interpret the establishing the absence of publication bias, selective reporting, or small-study effects.2,7

Clinical trial registries were screened during the review process to identify completed and ongoing studies. Registry-only records were not included in quantitative synthesis because sufficiently detailed participant-level SAE and AESI data were unavailable for effect estimation. We agree that future reviews would benefit from a more explicit registry reconciliation table documenting identified trials, completion status, publication status, outcome availability, and reasons for exclusion from quantitative synthesis, consistent with PRISMA 2020 guidance.8

In summary, we appreciate the feedback on transparent handling of related randomized comparisons, sparse-event meta-analysis, and the limitations of funnel-plot interpretation. These considerations do not alter the central observation that the currently available randomized evidence did not identify a statistically significant excess of SAEs or AESIs associated with mRNA-based seasonal influenza vaccines. Nevertheless, the evidence should be interpreted cautiously, particularly for very rare, delayed, cumulative, or post-marketing safety outcomes. Continued pharmacovigilance and large real-world safety studies remain essential as mRNA-based influenza vaccines progress toward broader use.
