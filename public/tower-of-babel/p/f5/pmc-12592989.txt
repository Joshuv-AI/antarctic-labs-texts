Comparison of the Efficacy of Epinephrine vs Norepinephrine in Clinical Outcomes among Children with Postcardiac Arrest Shock: A Hospital-based, Double Blind, Randomized Controlled Trial
Authors: Arun Prasad, Arnab Ghorui, Pradeep Kumar, Pritam Halder
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsPostcardiac arrest shock in pediatric patients poses significant clinical challenges due to high morbidity and mortality rates. Despite advancements in resuscitation techniques, managing postcardiac arrest syndrome remains complex. Traditionally, epinephrine has been used for postresuscitation shock, but recent studies suggest potential adverse effects. Norepinephrine, commonly used in adults, may offer advantages but lacks sufficient evidence in pediatric populations. Highlights

This double blind, randomized controlled trial at an Indian tertiary hospital compares norepinephrine vs epinephrine in children (1 month–18 years) with postcardiac arrest shock. Primary outcome: In-hospital mortality. Secondary outcomes: Vasopressor duration, mean arterial pressure (MAP) response, neurological status, and arrhythmias. Aims to address evidence gaps in pediatric shock management, with ethical approval and trial registration (CTRI/2025/03/081706).

Introduction

Pediatric cardiac arrest continues to present significant global challenges, with substantial regional disparities in outcomes. According to data from the American Resuscitation Outcomes Consortium Epidemiological Registry (ROC-Epistry), the incidence of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) in the pediatric population is 8.3 per 1,00,000 person-years. Incidence rates are highest among infants at 75.3 per 1,00,000 person-years, while children and adolescents have lower rates at 3.7 and 6.3 per 1,00,000 person-years, respectively. Yearly survival rates for pediatric OHCA range between 6.7 and 10.2%.1,2 In Southeast Asia, data from the Pan-Asian Resuscitation Outcomes Study (PAROS) indicate that the overall survival to hospital discharge for children experiencing OHCA is approximately 8.6% (ranging from 0 to 9.7%), with only 3.7% achieving favorable neurological outcomes. These low survival rates are largely attributed to limited bystander CPR, inadequate emergency medical services (EMSs), and delayed response times.3 In India, pediatric OHCA data remain sparse.

In the United States, the average yearly incidence of pediatric in-hospital cardiac arrest (IHCA) is estimated to be 15,200 cases, comprising 7,100 pulseless events and 8,100 non-pulseless events. Globally, pediatric IHCA outcomes are relatively favorable, with survival to hospital discharge rates ranging from 35 to 45%, and up to 35% of survivors achieving favorable neurological recovery, largely attributable to immediate intervention and continuous monitoring.4 A multicenter study conducted by López-Herce J et al., across 48 hospitals in 12 developed countries, reported that 69.5% of pediatric patients achieved return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) after IHCA. Of these, 39.2% survived to hospital discharge, with 88.9% of survivors exhibiting good neurological outcomes.5 In contrast, resource-limited settings such as Southeast Asia and India continue to experience high IHCA mortality rates, although comprehensive data from these regions remain limited.

Over time, simulation-based training has improved healthcare workers’ knowledge and skills in cardiopulmonary resuscitation. Ongoing refresher sessions reinforce learning, enhance performance quality, and collectively contribute to higher rates of ROSC following cardiac arrest.6

Postresuscitation shock is a common and serious complication in pediatric patients following ROSC after cardiac arrest. It is characterized by persistent hypotension and inadequate tissue perfusion after the ROSC, often necessitating fluid resuscitation and the use of vasoactive medications. The incidence of post-ROSC shock in children has been reported to range from 30 to 60%, reflecting the severity of postcardiac arrest syndrome in this population.7 The American Heart Association (AHA) also highlights the high prevalence of post-ROSC shock and emphasizes the need for early recognition and management as a critical component of postcardiac arrest care to improve outcomes.8

Despite advancements in resuscitation techniques, achieving ROSC is only the first step in a complex recovery process that often leads to postcardiac arrest syndrome, characterized by hemodynamic instability and multi-organ dysfunction (Fig. 1).9,10

Postcardiac arrest shock is marked by hypotension and inadequate tissue perfusion, necessitating the use of vasopressors to stabilize hemodynamics. The choice of vasopressor is critical, as it can significantly influence patient outcomes. Traditionally, epinephrine has been the primary agent used to manage cardiac arrest and postresuscitation shock due to its potent α- and β-adrenergic effects.7,11 In postcardiac arrest shock, vasoplegia is a significant and often overlooked complication that can occur after ROSC in patients who have experienced cardiac arrest. This condition is characterized by profound vasodilation and low systemic vascular resistance, leading to hypotension and inadequate organ perfusion despite adequate cardiac output. In the context of postcardiac arrest, vasoplegia may arise from a systemic inflammatory response triggered by the arrest itself, leading to endothelial dysfunction and excessive production of vasodilatory mediators such as nitric oxide. This results in failure of vascular smooth muscle to constrict adequately, causing severe hypotension and compromised organ perfusion. Thus, postcardiac arrest shock not only results from cardiac dysfunction but is also significantly complicated by profound vasoplegia, resembling septic shock—hence the term “sepsis-like syndrome.”12 In cases of septic shock, norepinephrine is the preferred first-line vasopressor, used either alone or in combination with other agents to maintain adequate MAP and organ perfusion.13 Norepinephrine, mainly an α-adrenergic agonist with some β-1 effects, has attracted interest for its ability to sustain MAP and enhance organ perfusion, while avoiding adverse effects linked to epinephrine, such as elevated heart rate and increased myocardial oxygen consumption.14 The AHA and European Resuscitation Council (ERC) guidelines recommend maintaining a MAP of at least 65 mm Hg in adults following ROSC. For pediatric patients, this corresponds to maintaining an MAP at or above the 5th percentile for age, underscoring the critical importance of adequate hemodynamic support in postresuscitation care.15

Additionally, there is a higher risk of arrhythmias postcardiac arrest due to ischemic myocardial injury, which is further exacerbated by the use of epinephrine, known to be more arrhythmogenic than norepinephrine.

An observational study by Bougouin et al., involving 766 patients with postresuscitation shock after OHCA, found that all-cause in-hospital mortality was significantly higher in the epinephrine group (83%) compared to the norepinephrine group (61%), with an odds ratio of 2.6 (p = 0.002). Additionally, cardiovascular-specific mortality was significantly greater in the epinephrine group, with an adjusted OR of 5.5 (95% CI: 3.0–10.3; p < 0.001). The proportion of patients with unfavorable neurological outcomes—defined as a cerebral performance category (CPC) score of 3–5 at discharge—was also higher in the epinephrine group (adjusted OR 3.0; 95% CI: 1.6–5.7; p = 0.001), suggesting worse neurological recovery. These findings indicate that norepinephrine may be associated with better survival and neurological outcomes in this population.16 However, a recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Lawson et al. found only a non-significant trend toward increased in-hospital mortality with epinephrine, possibly due to heterogeneity among the included studies.17

Research Gap

Despite its well-established role in the management of septic shock, the use of norepinephrine in postcardiac arrest shock—which closely resembles a sepsis-like syndrome—remains inadequately explored.11 Most existing studies have primarily focused on adult populations, leaving a significant gap in evidence regarding its use in pediatric patients.

This study aims to compare the efficacy and safety of continuous intravenous norepinephrine vs epinephrine as vasopressor agents in children with postcardiac arrest shock.

Through this investigation, we aim to provide critical insights into the optimal vasopressor choice for pediatric patients recovering from cardiac arrest. The findings could significantly influence clinical practice, guiding treatment protocols and improving outcomes for this vulnerable population. As we explore the comparative effects of norepinephrine and epinephrine, we hope to contribute to the broader understanding of postcardiac arrest care and advance pediatric resuscitation standards.

Research Hypothesis

There is no significant difference in clinical outcomes or hemodynamic parameters between norepinephrine and epinephrine in children with postcardiac arrest shock.

Study Design

This study will be a single-centered, double blind, randomized controlled trial (RCT) in the Department of Paediatrics of a tertiary care hospital, All India Institute of Medical Sciences, Patna. In this study, the investigator and analyst will be blinded, and patients, by default, will be blinded.

Study Participants

All children aged between 1 month and 18 years who present to the pediatric emergency department in cardiac arrest (OHCA) “or” children admitted under the Department of Paediatrics who are not on any inotropic support develop cardiac arrest due to a noncardiac cause, “and” achieve ROSC after the resuscitation, “and” exhibit features of postresuscitation shock.

The study will include all children aged 1 month to 18 years who present to the pediatric emergency department in cardiac arrest (OHCA) “or” those admitted to the Department of Paediatrics who are not on any inotropic support and experience cardiac arrest due to a noncardiac cause. In addition, participants must achieve ROSC following resuscitation and exhibit features of postresuscitation shock.

Sampling

Sampling population: All children aged between 1 month and 18 years who present to the pediatric emergency in cardiac arrest OHCA “or” those admitted to the Department of Paediatrics who are not on any inotropic support, develop cardiac arrest due to a noncardiac cause.

Sample size calculation: A multicenter observational study by Bougouin et al. assessed the clinical outcomes of epinephrine vs norepinephrine in patients with postresuscitation shock following cardiac arrest. The study found a significantly higher in-hospital mortality rate in the epinephrine group (83%) compared to the norepinephrine group (61%) (p < 0.001).15 Based on these findings, the sample size for the current RCT was calculated using the Statulator online sample size calculator (https://statulator.com/), specifying an α error of 3% and 90% power. Accounting for an anticipated 20% dropout rate, the final sample size was determined to be 125 patients per group, yielding a total of 250 participants.

Sampling technique: Consecutive sampling till the sample size in each arm is achieved.

Patient Selection and Informed Consent

All children aged between 1 month and 18 years who present to the pediatric emergency in cardiac arrest (OHCA) “or” children admitted under the Department of Paediatrics who are not on any inotropic support develop cardiac arrest due to a noncardiac cause, “and” achieve ROSC after the resuscitation, “and” exhibiting features of postresuscitation shock. Prior to enrollment, informed consent will be obtained from the parents.

Enrollment and Randomization

After excluding ineligible cases according to the predefined exclusion criteria, eligible participants will be enrolled in the study. Participants will then be randomly assigned to either the epinephrine infusion group or the norepinephrine infusion group using a pre-generated randomization sequence. This sequence will be created by permuted block randomization with variable block sizes via an online web-based randomization tool (https://www.randomizer.org/). The allocation sequence will be securely maintained by the head of the department, who will not be involved in any other aspect of the study to ensure allocation concealment.

Interventions

Following enrollment and randomization, patients will receive either an epinephrine or norepinephrine infusion as per the study protocol. The infusion will start at 0.1 µg/kg/min and may be titrated up to a maximum of 0.3 µg/kg/min. This will be administered in a blinded manner for study purposes. If shock persists despite following the study protocol, the treating team may decide to use inotropes as open-label, based on the type of shock.

Blinding and Matching

Both adrenaline and noradrenaline come as transparent solutions with similar concentration (1 mg of base per mL) in ampoules. These ampoules will be labeled as either “Solution A” or “Solution B” by a senior nursing officer (SNO) who is not involved in the study or patient care. The solutions will be made available at all working stations in the Department of Pediatrics. This arrangement ensures that blinding is maintained throughout the study.

The dose ranges for both drugs are similar, with a starting dose of 0.1 µg/kg/min and a maximum recommended dose of 0.3 µg/kg/min. This allows us to achieve matching in this study.

Data Collection

The demographic profile and baseline characteristics will be noted. Whether the child achieved resolution or not will be noted. The need for additional inotropic support (like Dobutamine, Dopamine, Vasopressin, Milrinone, Levosimendan) will be noted. The time required for the resolution of shock after starting the inotropes will be noted. The overall mortality outcome will be recorded. Neurological outcomes at hospital discharge using the CPC scale will be noted. Any adverse events in terms of arrhythmias will be noted.

Data Analysis

An intention-to-treat (ITT) approach will be used for all analyses. Outcome variables—including overall mortality, time to shock resolution, neurological outcomes at hospital discharge, and the incidence of adverse events—will be compared between the two study groups: those who received drug A and those who received drug B.

Unblinding

After the completion of the statistical analysis, unblinding will be conducted by the SNO responsible for maintaining the blinding procedures. At this stage, the identities of drug A and drug B will be disclosed. Following unblinding, the study results will be disseminated accordingly.

Patient and Public Involvement

Patients and the public did not participate in the design, execution, reporting, or dissemination of this research.

Statistical Analysis Plan

Data will be collected using Microsoft Excel 2019. Statistical analysis will be performed with JAMOVI software version 2.3134. All analyses will be conducted using an ITT approach. Continuous variables will be presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), or as median if outliers are present. Categorical variables will be expressed as proportions. Categorical data will be compared using the Chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, while continuous data will be analyzed using either the independent student's t-test or the Mann–Whitney U test, as appropriate. All statistical analyses will be conducted at a 95% confidence interval, with statistical significance defined as a p-value < 0.05.

What is Already Known on This Topic?

Epinephrine and norepinephrine are commonly used in postcardiac arrest shock. Epinephrine increases myocardial oxygen consumption and arrhythmias, while norepinephrine maintains blood pressure with less impact on heart rate, reducing metabolic demand.

What this Study Hopes to Add?

This study aims to fill the knowledge gap by comparing the efficacy and safety of norepinephrine vs epinephrine in pediatric patients experiencing postcardiac arrest shock.

How this Study might Affect Research, Practice, or Policy?

This study's findings could significantly impact clinical practice by guiding the selection of vasopressors in pediatric postcardiac arrest care. If norepinephrine is found to be safer or more effective than epinephrine in children, it could lead to changes in treatment protocols and guidelines.
