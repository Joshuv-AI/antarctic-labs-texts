Clinical and biochemical variability in composite pheochromocytomas: a report of 3 cases
Authors: Audrey Lee, Simona Stefan, Robert Berkenblit, John McAuliffe, Yanhua Wang, Beatrice Y Wong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractComposite pheochromocytomas are a rare subset of pheochromocytomas composed of chromaffin cell tumors and neurogenic tumors. Their clinical manifestations may be atypical for pheochromocytoma. Here, we present 3 cases that illustrate the broad spectrum of disease seen in composite pheochromocytomas. A high level of clinical suspicion is needed to diagnose composite pheochromocytomas given their rarity and diverse clinical presentations.

Introduction

Composite pheochromocytomas are rare tumors containing both pheochromocytoma and neurogenic components, most frequently ganglioneuroma [1]. They account for approximately 1% to 9% of pheochromocytomas [2, 3]. Given their low prevalence and rarity, their clinical behavior and management remain poorly characterized. We present 3 cases and review their clinical presentation, diagnosis, and management.

Case presentation

Case 1

A 63-year-old woman with uncontrolled hypertension and insulin-dependent type 2 diabetes presented with abdominal pain and persistent left flank pain. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) revealed a 1.5 × 1.8 × 1.5 cm right adrenal incidentaloma with an unenhanced CT attenuation of 28 Hounsfield units (HU) (Fig. 1). She denied the classic symptoms of pheochromocytoma, including headache, palpitations, and diaphoresis. Blood pressure was controlled on multiple antihypertensive agents.

Abdominal CT scans (A) without contrast, (B) postcontrast, and (C) 15-minute delayed postcontrast imaging demonstrate a 1.8 × 1.6 cm right adrenal nodule (arrow) that measures 28 HU on precontrast imaging and heterogeneous enhancement on postcontrast imaging.

Abbreviations: CT, computed tomography; HU, Hounsfield units.

Case 2

A 69-year-old man with heart failure, coronary artery disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and a 15-pack/year smoking history presented with shortness of breath and back pain. He denied palpitations, diaphoresis, and headaches. Vital signs were notable for persistent hypertension with systolic blood pressures up to 180 mmHg.

Case 3

A 45-year-old woman with hypertension and a known left-sided 1.2 cm adrenal mass presented with worsening abdominal and back pain, headaches, and palpitations. Her blood pressure was well controlled, and her heart rate was normal.

Diagnostic assessment

Case 1

Biochemical evaluation showed elevated plasma free metanephrines of 66 pg/mL (335 pmol/L) (reference range, <57 pg/mL [<289 pmol/L]), normetanephrines of 229 pg/mL (1250 pmol/L) (reference range, <148 pg/mL [<808 pmol/L]), and total metanephrines of 295 pg/mL (1529 pmol/L) (reference range, <205 pg/mL [<1062 pmol/L]). Two separate 24-hour urine metanephrine collections were normal: metanephrine 121 µg (660 nmol) and 168 µg (917 nmol) (reference range, 90-315 µg/24 hours [491-1720nmol]), normetanephrine 237 µg (1400 nmol) and 336 µg (1990nmol) (reference range, 122-676 µg/24 hours [721-3990 nmol]), and total metanephrines 358 µg (1950nmol) and 504 µg (2750 nmol) (reference range, 224-832 µg/24 hours [1220-4540 nmol]). CT abdomen 14 months later demonstrated growth of the right adrenal mass to 1.7 × 2.0 × 2.0 cm with 49% contrast washout.

Case 2

Chest CT revealed a spiculated left upper lobe lung nodule and an indeterminate right adrenal nodule measuring 2.4 × 2.2 × 2.9 cm. Positron emission tomography-CT showed nonspecific mild fluorodeoxyglucose uptake of the adrenal nodule, and Gallium-68 dodecane tetraacetic acid tyrosine-3-octreotate scan was negative. Abdominal magnetic resonance imaging showed a 1.7 × 2.1 × 3.1 cm right adrenal lesion with a mildly thickened enhancing rim and central enhancing nodular component (Fig. 2).

(A) Chest CT with IV contrast demonstrates a 2.4 × 2 cm right adrenal nodule. This nodule (arrow) is seen to be cystic on (B) MRI T2-weighted imaging, which has nodular enhancing component on (C) postcontrast MRI.

Abbreviations: CT, computed tomography; MRI, magnetic resonance imaging.

Biochemical evaluation was notable for elevated plasma free metanephrines of 194 pg/mL (1060 pmol/L), normetanephrines of 474 pg/mL (2800 pmol/L), and 24-hour urine metanephrines of 389 µg (2120 nmol) with normal urine normetanephrines of 428 µg (2530 nmol).

Case 3

Ten years prior, she was found to have a 1.2 cm left adrenal incidentaloma on CT. Repeat imaging at presentation showed a circumscribed, heterogenous left adrenal mass measuring 4.4 × 3.0 cm with an unenhanced CT attenuation of 45 HU, enhanced CT attenuation of 74 HU, and delayed enhanced CT attenuation of 63 HU (Fig. 3). Biochemical evaluation was significant for elevated plasma free metanephrines of 162 pg/mL (839 pmol/L), normetanephrines of 522 pg/mL (3089 pmol/L), 24-hour urine metanephrines of 761 µg (4150 nmol) (reference range, 58-203 µg/24 hours [317-1108 nmol]), and urine normetanephrines of 895 µg (5290 nmol) (reference range, 88-649 µg/24 hours [521-3840 nmol]).

Axial CT scans (A) without IV contrast, (B) post-IV contrast, and (C) 15-minute delayed post-IV contrast demonstrate a 4.1 × 3.3 cm left adrenal nodule (arrow). The nodule measures 45 HU on precontrast imaging and heterogeneous enhancement on postcontrast imaging.

Treatment

Case 1

Due to interval growth and atypical imaging features of the adrenal mass, the patient underwent uncomplicated laparoscopic right adrenalectomy. She did not receive preoperative α blockade since the adrenal mass was deemed biochemically inactive at the time. Pathology revealed a composite pheochromocytoma with ganglioneuroblastoma (Fig. 4). The Pheochromocytoma of the Adrenal Gland Scaled Score (PASS) was 7, suggestive of a higher risk for aggressive disease.

Histopathology of the right adrenal mass stained with hematoxylin and eosin stain (low magnification) showing (A) composite pheochromocytoma (blue arrow) with ganglioneuroblastoma (black arrow). High magnification 20X (B) shows only ganglioneuroblastoma (40X), and (C) shows only pheochromocytoma.

Case 2

Due to the concern for metastatic lung cancer in the setting of the spiculated left upper lobe lung nodule, percutaneous adrenal biopsy was performed by the oncology team prior to endocrine consultation and before biochemical testing for pheochromocytoma. The biopsy was completed without preprocedural α blockade. The patient was hypertensive during the biopsy but did not develop hypertensive crisis or other catecholamine-related complications. This is a high-risk procedure that is not recommended and should only be performed with multidisciplinary involvement after pheochromocytoma is ruled out. Adrenal biopsy confirmed pheochromocytoma, and phenoxybenzamine was initiated prior to right adrenalectomy. Due to posterior adherence to the inferior vena cava, the planned laparoscopic approach was converted to an open procedure. Pathology confirmed a composite pheochromocytoma with a small ganglioneuroma component (Fig. 5). The patient subsequently underwent left upper lobe wedge resection of the lung, which confirmed squamous cell carcinoma.

Histopathology of the right adrenal mass stained with hematoxylin and eosin stain (20X) showing (A) pheochromocytoma with (B) positive synaptophysin staining (20X) and (C) positive chromogranin staining (20X).

Case 3

The patient was started on doxazosin and metoprolol preoperatively and underwent an uncomplicated laparoscopic left adrenalectomy. Pathology confirmed a composite pheochromocytoma with ganglioneuroma. The pheochromocytoma stained positive for synaptophysin and chromogranin, while the ganglioneuroma component stained positive for neurofilament protein (Fig. 6). The PASS was less than 4.

Histopathology of the left adrenal mass stained with hematoxylin and eosin stain showing (A) pheochromocytoma (low magnification) with (B) positive synaptophysin (20X), (C) chromogranin (20X), and (D) ganglioneuroma component (20X) that (E) stained positive for neurofilament protein (20X).

Outcome and follow-up

Case 1

Postoperatively, blood pressure improved and was controlled on a single antihypertensive agent. Hemoglobin A1c decreased from 11% to 7.1%, allowing discontinuation of insulin. The patient declined genetic testing. Surveillance imaging and plasma metanephrine levels have remained normal. She remains disease-free 4 years after surgery.

Case 2

Serum metanephrines initially normalized but were mildly elevated at 18 months, metanephrines to 79 pg/mL (431 pmol/L) and normetanephrines to 341 pg/mL (2051 pmol/L), without an identifiable cause for false-positive results. Subsequent imaging, including Gallium-68 dodecane tetraacetic acid tyrosine-3-octreotate, remained negative for recurrence. Genetic testing revealed a pathogenic variant of unknown significance in LZTR1 (c.2406+60C>T). No pathogenic variants were found in SDHx, RET, VHL, NF1, TMEM127, or MAX genes.

Case 3

One-month postoperative imaging showed no residual disease. Serum metanephrines normalized and palpitations resolved. The patient declined genetic testing.

Discussion

Composite pheochromocytomas are uncommon tumors composed of pheochromocytoma and a second component, typically of neurogenic origin. Histologically, both components must constitute at least 10% of the tumor, with prominent areas of Schwann cells as the hallmark finding [4]. A recent review identified only 110 reported cases, with most diagnosed between the ages of 40 and 60 and a slight female predominance [5, 6].

Composite pheochromocytomas are clinically, biochemically, and radiologically indistinguishable from ordinary pheochromocytomas [7]. The diagnosis of composite tumors is made retrospectively after surgical resection and histopathological analysis. In our series, all 3 patients were initially diagnosed with adrenal incidentalomas on imaging performed for unrelated symptoms. None of the patients endorsed the full triad of catecholamine excess, episodic headaches, palpitations, and diaphoresis, highlighting the possibility of subclinical or atypical presentations.

The biochemical diagnosis of pheochromocytomas is strongly suggested by plasma free or urine metanephrines and catecholamine levels that are 3 times the upper limit of normal (ULN). Retrospective analyses report that symptomatic pheochromocytomas typically have metanephrine levels that are 13.8 times the ULN, whereas incidental pheochromocytomas commonly have metanephrine levels that are 6.4 times the ULN [8]. The cases presented demonstrate considerable variability in biochemical profiles (Table 1). Case 1 had plasma metanephrine levels 1.16 times the ULN and normal 24-hour urine metanephrines. Case 2 had plasma metanephrines 3.40 times the ULN and mildly elevated 24-hour urine metanephrines 1.23 times the ULN. Case 3 had elevated plasma metanephrines 2.84 times the ULN and 24-hour urine metanephrines 3.75 times the ULN. The more significantly elevated metanephrines in case 3 may be in part due to the larger tumor size. Of the 3 cases presented here, only patient 3 had both plasma and urine labs consistent with the expected 3 times the ULN of metanephrine and catecholamine levels. These findings reflect those reported by Najjar et al, who described composite pheochromocytoma-ganglioneuromas with subclinical plasma metanephrine levels ranging from 1.07 to 1.36 times the ULN and normetanephrine levels that were normal to 1.08 times the ULN [4]. Overall, this data suggests that composite tumors may produce lower levels of catecholamines than classic pheochromocytomas, particularly in the presence of ganglioneuroma or ganglioneuroblastoma components.

Approximately 15% of pheochromocytomas present with metastasis in the lifetime, although the rate in composite tumors is unknown [9]. In our series, only 1 tumor had a high PASS score of 7, suggesting metastatic potential. The remaining tumors had PASS scores <4 and no evidence of aggressive tumor behavior. Metastatic potential in pheochromocytomas remains difficult to predict, but a PASS score >4 is generally considered worrisome.

Currently, there is not enough evidence to suggest that the management of composite pheochromocytomas differs from that of typical pheochromocytomas. All patients underwent surgical resection. Preoperative α blockade prior to adrenalectomy was only performed in 2 of the cases as the adrenal mass in the first case was deemed biochemically inactive. Postoperatively, 2 patients experienced complete resolution of symptoms and normalization of metanephrines. The patient in case 2 developed persistent mild elevations in plasma metanephrines despite negative imaging. This likely reflects biochemical variability rather than recurrence and highlights the limitations of current thresholds for defining biochemical remission. Postoperative laboratory results should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical status, imaging findings, and known assay limitations to minimize the risk of overdiagnosis or unnecessary intervention [10].

Genetic testing is recommended for all patients with pheochromocytoma due to potential associated genetic syndromes, including neurofibromatosis 1, multiple endocrine neoplasia type 2A and 2B, and von Hippel-Lindau disease [4]. The overall rate of genetic disorders seen in cases of composite pheochromocytoma varies. In 1 retrospective study, 47.4% of patients with composite pheochromocytomas had an underlying genetic disorder compared to 23.2% of patients with standard pheochromocytomas [11]. In a systemic review of composite pheochromocytomas, genetic syndromes were identified in 28% of patients [9]. Pathogenic variants in RET, SDHx, CDKN1B, NF1, and VHL have been reported in patients with composite pheochromocytoma [11]. In our case series, only 1 patient underwent genetic testing and was found to have a pathogenic variant of unknown clinical significance in LZTR1. This gene has been shown to be associated with Schwannomas and glioblastomas [12, 13]. The role of LZTR1 in composite pheochromocytoma remains unclear but merits further investigation.

Prognosis is generally favorable after complete resection, but long-term follow-up is recommended due to the risk of recurrence or metastasis. The probability of recurrence is low; however, distant metastases have been reported in rare cases [4]. In our series, outcomes were favorable overall, with sustained remission in most patients and 1 case of persistent biochemical variability without radiographic recurrence. Current surveillance guidelines do not specifically address composite tumors, but expert opinion supports lifelong follow-up, especially in cases with atypical pathology or elevated risk for aggressive disease [10, 14].

Composite pheochromocytomas are rare tumors that may present with atypical clinical and biochemical features. Our case series highlights their clinical heterogeneity, including variable biochemical activity and diagnostic complexity. These findings emphasize the importance of histopathological evaluation, individualized postoperative surveillance, and continued investigation into emerging genetic associations such as LZTR1.
