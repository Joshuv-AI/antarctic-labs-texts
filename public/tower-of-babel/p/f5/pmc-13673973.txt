Mesh erosion after tracheobronchoplasty is an uncommon but serious complication.

Central Message

Persistent excessive central airway collapse and mesh erosion after tracheobronchoplasty complicate both reoperation and recovery. These operations should be performed in complex airway centers.

Tracheobronchoplasty (TBP) is a technically complex operation reserved for highly selected, severely symptomatic patients with excessive central airway collapse (ECAC).1,2 ECAC results in a spectrum of symptoms, including intractable barking cough, dyspnea, recurrent respiratory infections, and even respiratory failure.1,2 Traditionally, this operation is performed to stabilize the posterior trachea and mainstem bronchi against collapse with a polypropylene mesh.2, 3, 4 In contemporary practice, robot-assisted TBP has been performed with excellent technical results and patient outcomes, which are measured via quality-of-life (QoL) questionnaires and functional tests, such as 6-minute walk tests.3,4

To date, there are no studies that examine the outcomes of redo TBP or the management of patients with recurrent or persistent ECAC after an initial TBP. The failure rate of primary TBP has not been succinctly described.

Methods

The case describes a patient who underwent a redo TBP operation for persistent, severe, ECAC and mesh erosion after the index TBP. Identifying information is omitted, and informed consent was obtained. Institutional review board approval was not necessary.

Case Presentation

A 46-year-old man with a history of obstructive sleep apnea and diabetes mellitus presented with persistent ECAC 8 months after undergoing a robot-assisted TBP using polypropylene mesh at an outside institution. Preoperatively, he had airway stent trials with favorable outcomes, indicating he would likely benefit from TBP, although this was complicated by granulation tissue formation. Postoperatively, he required restenting for respiratory failure. He experienced intractable coughing fits, severe exertional dyspnea requiring supplemental oxygen, and excessive mucus production with difficulty in clearance. In the previous year, he had 3 hospitalizations for severe pneumonia and was unable to continue working.

He underwent the standard workup for ECAC at our institution.3 Pulmonary functional tests demonstrated an obstructive pattern. QoL assessments revealed significant impairment (Table 1). Dynamic airway computed tomography showed near-complete collapse of the mid trachea and bilateral mainstem bronchi during forced expiration (Figure 1). Bronchoscopy confirmed severe collapse of the distal trachea, right mainstem bronchus (RMSB), and left mainstem bronchus (LMSB), with mesh eroding into the distal trachea and LMSB. Re-epithelialization of the defect was attempted with hyperbaric oxygen therapy, but the mesh remained exposed at reoperation. He developed a persistent, multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas airway infection.

The redo TBP was performed through a right thoracotomy with careful explantation of the adherent mesh from the airway. Tracheobronchomegaly was evident, with the trachea measuring nearly 6 cm, the LMSB 2.6 cm, and the RMSB 3.5 cm in transverse dimension. The cartilage was flattened or even upturned anteriorly in some areas, creating near-complete lumen obliteration. A 3-cm × 1.5-cm defect in the distal trachea and proximal LMSB was identified. The posterior airway in the trachea (well superior to the defect) and RMSB was restabilized with polypropylene mesh and 4-0 polypropylene suture.1 The distal trachea (adjacent to the defect) and LMSB were reconstructed using 4-0 polydioxanone suture placed above and below the membranous defect to create a suture bronchoplasty, after which an intercostal muscle flap was used to cover the residual defect that could not be closed primarily. Chest tubes were placed, and the thoracotomy was closed. Esophagogastroscopy confirmed no full-thickness esophageal defects at the conclusion of the 12-hour procedure. He received broad-spectrum antibiotics, remained intubated, and underwent a planned tracheostomy the following day.

His 6-month hospital stay was marked by complications, including respiratory failure requiring venovenous extracorporeal membrane oxygenation, hemothorax necessitating washout, respiratory failure requiring repeat airway stenting, renal failure, and multidrug-resistant pneumonia. At the conclusion of his hospitalization, the airway stent was removed, and he was discharged to a rehabilitation facility for continued recovery.

Several years later, he experienced significant improvement in his symptoms and QoL, with limitations now confined to mild cough and dyspnea. He was able to resume regular gym attendance. Discussion

This case highlights the considerable morbidity of reoperative TBP in patients with persistent or recurrent ECAC at its extreme. Although we demonstrate the technical feasibility of TBP revision after a large area of mesh erosion, we caution that operative complexity and lengthy postoperative recovery should be anticipated. Thankfully, our patient survived his arduous hospital course and had significant improvement in QoL after his redo TBP, although this outcome was years in the making.

The etiology of recurrent ECAC after TBP is unclear, and there are no published case series on redo TBP. We posit several contributing factors, including chronic inflammation, infection, connective tissue disorders, and influences on healing, such as steroid use or immunosuppression. In this case, adequate airway reshaping was not achieved after his index operation; thus, airway collapse was persisted. Persistent anatomic stabilization of the airway after TBP has been demonstrated up to 5 years, with minimal degradation, in a longitudinal study.5

It is unclear what caused mesh erosion after his index operation. Potential technical contributors include airway devascularization from excessive dissection during exposure and imprecise full-thickness suture placement. The recurrence of erosion was not entirely unexpected due to repeat dissection and the presence of a multidrug-resistant Pseudomonas infection, which he had been unable to clear prior to reoperation. This represented a Catch-22; ideally, his airway would have been free of infection at reoperation, yet persistent collapse limited his ability to eliminate the pathogen.

Fortunately, with improved tracheobronchial clearance following redo TBP, he was ultimately able to clear the infection. Trimming of the exposed mesh enabled re-epithelialization, but this would not have been feasible after the primary operation, given the persistent collapse and inability to resolve secretions and infection.

Conclusions

Persistent ECAC and central airway mesh erosion may complicate TBP. Reoperative TBP is an option but must be undertaken after careful consideration, given the risks of severe, life-threatening complications, prolonged hospitalization, and additional debilitation. We recommend that TBP be performed at experienced complex airway centers.
