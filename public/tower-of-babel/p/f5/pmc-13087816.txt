Clinical Outcomes in Bacillus Calmette-Guérin–Exposed Non–Muscle-Invasive Bladder Cancer
Authors: Maria Giatsoglou, Dimitrios Chatzistavrou, Joan Mazzarelli Sopczynski, Gang Feng, Roger Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat are the actual burdens and clinical outcomes experienced by individuals with Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG)–exposed non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC)?FindingsIn this cohort study of 26 876 patients, those with BCG-exposed NMIBC represented a large, underserved population with recurrence occurring in 83%, accounting for more than 80% of disease progression and cystectomy cases. Adequate BCG maintenance therapy was uncommon, and most patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC received inadequate maintenance.MeaningThese findings suggest that patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC represent the majority of those experiencing progression and cystectomy, highlighting an urgent need for new therapeutic strategies and clinical trial designs targeting this population.

Introduction

Non–muscle-invasive bladder cancer (NMIBC) accounts for approximately 70% of newly diagnosed bladder cancer cases and is characterized by a high risk of recurrence and progression.1,2 Bacillus Calmette-Guérin (BCG) immunotherapy remains the standard of care for intermediate-risk and high-risk NMIBC, particularly carcinoma in situ (CIS). Despite its widespread use, completion of guideline-recommended induction and maintenance is often suboptimal, which may affect outcomes and access to subsequent therapies.

Regulatory and clinical frameworks define clinically relevant subgroups based on response to BCG. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the International Bladder Cancer Group have outlined criteria for identifying BCG-unresponsive and BCG-exposed NMIBC.3,4 These classifications have subsequently been adopted to guide clinical trial eligibility and therapeutic decision-making. However, limited clinical data exist to quantify the size and characteristics of these patient populations.

The aim of our study was to examine the landscape of NMIBC treatment in the US using a large, linked dataset of deidentified electronic health record (EHR) and claims data. The primary objectives were to (1) estimate the proportion of patients receiving adequate BCG therapy, (2) characterize recurrence and downstream outcomes following BCG treatment, and (3) classify patients into BCG-unresponsive, BCG-exposed, BCG-treated, and BCG-naive populations using regulatory definitions adapted for clinical treatment patterns, with emphasis on CIS.

Methods

Study Design and Data Source

This cohort study was conducted using Optum’s deidentified Market Clarity Data (Optum Market Clarity), which deterministically linked US medical and pharmacy claims with EHR data from January 1, 2007, to June 30, 2024. These data are certified as deidentified in accordance with the Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act Privacy Rule. Under US federal regulations, research using deidentified data does not constitute human participants research. Accordingly, institutional review board review and informed consent were not required based on this regulatory determination. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline for cohort studies.

The dataset included data from more than 50 health care organizations, covering more than 700 hospitals and 7000 clinics, representing a population of more than 104 million patients. The dataset combined structured claims data (pharmacy, physician, and facility) with EHR information, such as vital signs, laboratory results, diagnoses, procedures, and medications administered. Patients were initially selected based on diagnostic codes consistent with bladder cancer (International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision; International Statistical Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision; and Systematized Nomenclature of Medicine). A key limitation of this dataset was the lack of detailed pathology reports, including information distinguishing between high-grade and low-grade NMIBC. To circumvent this limitation, we used the CIS diagnosis code as a proxy for high-grade NMIBC, focusing on this subset of confirmed high-risk cases.

Eligible patients were identified using diagnostic codes consistent with bladder cancer and were required to have received at least 1 full course of BCG induction therapy. Patients with insufficient follow-up after induction or adequate maintenance (if applicable) were excluded from outcome analyses, as described later. The derivation of the analytic cohorts, including inclusion and exclusion at each step, is summarized in the eMethods and eTable 1 in Supplement 1.

The primary outcome of this descriptive cohort study was high-grade disease recurrence within 24 months following completion of BCG therapy (induction or adequate maintenance). Recurrence was defined a priori using a clinically informed composite indicator, receipt of transurethral resection of bladder tumor (TURBT) and/or initiation of a new BCG induction course following prior therapy, to account for the absence of centralized pathology review and to better reflect actual clinical practice.

Secondary outcomes included progression to muscle-invasive bladder cancer and radical cystectomy within the same follow-up window. Exploratory outcomes included progression-free survival (PFS) and cystectomy-free survival (CFS), which were evaluated to characterize longitudinal clinical trajectories among patients exposed to BCG and unresponsive to BCG.

Defining Adequate BCG Therapy

Adequate BCG therapy was defined using procedure, diagnosis, and medication codes. Induction course was identified as a series of a minimum of 4 BCG instillations with no more than 3 weeks between doses.5,6 Maintenance therapy was defined as BCG treatments occurring within 1 year following the final dose of induction. To be considered adequate, the maintenance phase must have begun within 6 months of the induction start date and included at least 2 doses within a period of 3 months.7,8 A reinduction course initiated within 6 months may be counted for adequate maintenance in accordance with FDA guidance.3 With these assumptions, induction courses with more than 5 BCG doses had a median duration of 36 days, with less than 0.5% extending beyond the 70-day limit specified in previous claims-based reports.9 Full criteria are outlined in eTable 2 and eFigure 1 in Supplement 1.

Identifying Recurrence, Progression, and Cystectomy Following BCG Therapy

We used procedure, diagnosis, and medication codes to ascertain cancer recurrence and/or progression. To account for uncertainty in identifying disease recurrence due to the lack of pathology reports, 2 clinical indicators were used that coded for TURBT following BCG treatment and the initiation of a new BCG induction regimen following completion of a prior course. Together, these 2 indicators provide strong evidence for a high-grade recurrence requiring TURBT and reinduction BCG. Progression to muscle-invasive bladder cancer or metastasis was estimated by the presence of any of the following: radiotherapy administration; use of systemic antineoplastic agents; diagnosis of metastasis; or cancer diagnosis codes associated with common metastatic sites, such as the liver, lungs, or bones, as previously described.10 Intuitively, radical cystectomy was identified by any record of a complete or radical cystectomy procedure. Unless specified, progression or cystectomy was regarded as an indicator of recurrence. Patients were required to have continuous follow-up data available after initiation of BCG therapy. For analyses of clinical outcomes, only patients with at least 2 years of follow-up after completion of BCG induction or adequate maintenance therapy were included to ensure adequate time for outcome ascertainment. Patients with shorter follow-up were retained in the overall cohort for descriptive analyses of treatment patterns but were excluded from outcome comparisons. Details of cohort construction and attrition are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Disease Categorization

Following FDA guidance and International Bladder Cancer Group definitions,4 and adapting for clinical data constraints, we stratified patients with NMIBC into 4 groups. These groups were labeled as BCG-unresponsive, BCG-exposed, BCG-treated, and BCG-naive.

BCG-Unresponsive NMIBC

BCG-unresponsive classification was limited to patients with a CIS diagnosis who experienced persistent or recurrent disease within 12 months of adequate BCG therapy. Papillary recurrences beyond 6 months may have been misclassified, potentially inflating this group. Patients with T1 high-grade recurrence at 3 months post induction were not included.

BCG-Exposed NMIBC

Patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC were defined as those with CIS who did not meet BCG-unresponsive criteria and experienced delayed relapse (12-24 months) after either adequate or inadequate BCG. For adequate BCG, relapse was measured from completion of therapy; for inadequate BCG (≥5 of 6 induction doses), relapse was measured from the first dose. Patients with high-grade Ta or CIS recurrence at 3 months post induction were not included.

BCG-Treated NMIBC

This group included patients who received incomplete induction and did not meet criteria for BCG-unresponsive or BCG-exposed NMIBC. It may include patients who were intolerant to BCG or affected by treatment disruptions, such as BCG shortages.

BCG-Naive NMIBC

Patients in this group had no record of intravesical BCG therapy either due to clinical decision, patient refusal, or lack of access. All had a bladder cancer diagnosis and at least 1 TURBT procedure.

Statistical Analysis

Outcome analyses were restricted to patients with at least 2 years of follow-up after completion of BCG therapy. Patients with shorter follow-up were included in descriptive analyses of treatment patterns but were excluded from outcome comparisons. Statistical analyses were performed using Python, version 3.8 (Python Software Foundation). Analyses of the primary outcome (recurrence) and secondary outcomes (progression and cystectomy) were prespecified and summarized descriptively. Time-to-event analyses for exploratory outcomes, including PFS and CFS, were conducted using the Kaplan-Meier method and compared between patient groups using the log-rank test. Patients undergoing cystectomy were censored at the time of surgery in PFS analyses.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, with P < .05 considered statistically significant. No adjustments were made for multiple comparisons as analyses beyond the primary outcome, including maintenance-adequacy subgroup contrasts, were intended to be descriptive and hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory.

Because of limitations inherent to routinely collected clinical data, analyses were not fully adjusted for potential confounders, such as age, comorbidity burden, access-to-care factors (including race and ethnicity in the data source [Asian, Black or African American, White, or other (not listed or recorded as other or unknown)]), or proxies of disease severity. Accordingly, subgroup comparisons were conducted for descriptive purposes and interpreted cautiously.

Missing data were handled implicitly through cohort selection and follow-up requirements; patients with insufficient follow-up were excluded from outcome analyses. The extent and implications of missing or unavailable clinical detail (eg, pathology grade) are discussed in the Limitations. Additional methodological details are provided in the eMethods in Supplement 1.

Results

The study included 26 876 patients with NMIBC (median [IQR] age at BCG initiation, 73 [65-77] years; 20.4% female and 79.5% male, 0.9% categorized as Asian, 3.3% as Black or African American, 85.7% as White, and 10.1% as other race and ethnicity), including 9639 patients with CIS. Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were similar across induction and maintenance subgroups (Table 1).

Patient Distribution by Risk Group and BCG Treatment Adequacy

Among high-risk patients with CIS (n = 9639), 7495 (77.8%) received adequate BCG induction therapy (Table 2). However, only 2037 patients (27.2%) with adequate induction went on to receive adequate maintenance therapy, representing just 21.1% of the total CIS cohort. In the overall study population of 26 876 patients, 19 889 (74.0%) received adequate induction therapy. Among them, 4951 (24.9%) received adequate maintenance therapy, accounting for 18.4% of the total cohort.

Association of Adherence to the Standard BCG Protocol With Risk of Progression and Cystectomy

To further elucidate the association of adherence to the standard BCG protocol with clinical outcomes, Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were conducted. These analyses included all patients with bladder cancer who received adequate induction therapy, as well as a focused subgroup of patients with high-risk NMIBC with CIS who also received adequate induction therapy (Figure, A). Unless otherwise specified, analyses of recurrence, progression, and cystectomy were restricted to patients meeting the prespecified minimum follow-up requirement.

Survival curves measured the time from the first dose of BCG induction to either disease progression (PFS) or radical cystectomy (CFS).11 In both the high-risk CIS subgroup and the overall cohort, patients who received adequate maintenance therapy experienced more favorable outcomes compared with those who received inadequate maintenance therapy. Specifically, PFS was higher in the adequate maintenance group, with hazard ratios of 0.74 (95% CI, 0.66-0.84; P < .001) for the high-risk cohort and 0.78 (95% CI, 0.72-0.85; P < .001) for the overall cohort. Similarly, CFS favored patients receiving adequate maintenance therapy, with hazard ratios of 0.59 (95% CI, 0.48-0.73; P < .001) in the high-risk group and 0.57 (95% CI, 0.47-0.68; P < .001) in the overall cohort (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1).

Validating Methodology to Identify High-Risk Recurrences

Among 7495 patients with CIS-containing tumors who received adequate BCG induction therapy, 5271 (70.3%) had sufficient follow-up to be included in outcome analyses (eMethods in Supplement 1). Using a clinically informed definition for disease recurrence that incorporated patients undergoing either a TURBT procedure or initiation of a new BCG induction course following adequate BCG induction, 3134 patients (59.3%) were identified as having disease recurrence within 24 months. In contrast, applying a narrower definition that considers only the initiation of a new BCG induction as evidence of recurrence identified 1198 of the 5271 patients with sufficient follow-up (22.7%) (eTable 3 in Supplement 1).

Incidence of BCG-Exposed vs BCG-Unresponsive NMIBC

Among 5271 patients with CIS who received adequate BCG induction therapy, recurrence outcomes varied by maintenance therapy status. Of the total cohort, 3766 patients (71.4%) received inadequate maintenance therapy, while 1505 (28.6%) received adequate maintenance therapy.

As shown in Table 3, 2604 patients (49.4%) were classified as BCG-exposed, with the majority (2442 [64.8%]) originating from the inadequate maintenance group. Only 162 patients (10.8%) with BCG-exposed NMIBC had received adequate maintenance therapy. By definition, BCG-unresponsive disease requires that all affected patients have received adequate maintenance therapy; accordingly, 530 patients (10.0%) met criteria for BCG-unresponsive disease, and all were within the adequate maintenance group (representing 35.2% [530 of 1505] of that subgroup). The remaining 2137 patients (40.5%) had no evidence of recurrence within 24 months, with a higher proportion of recurrence-free outcomes observed in the adequate maintenance group (813 patients [54%]) compared with the inadequate maintenance group (1324 patients [35.2%]). Overall, patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC represented 83.1% (2604 of 3134) of all recurrences, whereas patients with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC represented 16.9% (530 of 3134).

Disease Progression and Cystectomy Following BCG-Exposed NMIBC

The clinical trajectory of patients with recurrence after adequate BCG induction therapy revealed a disproportionate burden among those classified as BCG-exposed. The Sankey diagram mapping patients’ flow from exposure classification to clinical outcomes (Figure, B) shows that patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC accounted for most experiencing disease progression and cystectomy compared with the BCG-unresponsive group.

Of the 441 patients with progression, 366 (83.0%) were exposed to BCG, while 75 (17.0%) were unresponsive to BCG (Table 4). Similarly, among the 384 patients who underwent cystectomy, 337 (87.8%) were exposed to BCG compared with 47 (12.2%) who were unresponsive to BCG.

Unfavorable outcomes, defined as progression or cystectomy, occurred in 23.0% (122 of 530) patients with BCG-unresponsive NMIBC and 27.0% (703 of 2604) of those with BCG-exposed NMIBC (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). Most patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC who progressed or underwent cystectomy had received inadequate maintenance therapy (330 of 366 [90.2%] and 327 of 337 [97.0%], respectively) (eTables 5 and 6 in Supplement 1).

Discussion

This cohort study found that patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC represent a large, underserved population and account for most progression and cystectomy events after BCG exposure. Bacillus Calmette-Guérin–exposed disease was substantially more prevalent than BCG-unresponsive disease by approximately fivefold. Notably, most patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC had received inadequate maintenance therapy. These patients were not eligible for therapies approved for the BCG-unresponsive population,12 which underscores the urgent need for new treatment options and clinical trial designs that address patients in the BCG-exposed population.

The majority of BCG-exposed NMIBC cases involved disease progression and radical cystectomy. More than 80% of patients who progressed or underwent cystectomy were BCG exposed, underscoring the clinical relevance of this population and the consequences of limited treatment options. As detailed in the Results, most of these patients had received inadequate maintenance therapy, with progression and cystectomy outcomes disproportionately concentrated among those with incomplete BCG exposure. This pattern highlights the higher risks that patients with BCG-exposed NMIBC face and reinforces the need for bladder-sparing therapeutic strategies tailored to their clinical context.

Limitations

This study had some limitations. First, the data lacked pathology grade and clinical detail, which required use of CIS coding to define a high-risk subgroup and may have excluded high-grade disease without CIS. Second, because recurrence was the sole primary outcome and no multiplicity adjustment was applied, secondary and exploratory findings should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis generating. Third, comparisons by maintenance adequacy may be influenced by immortal time bias and residual confounding because adequate maintenance requires patients to remain event-free long enough to complete dosing. Furthermore, analyses were not fully adjusted for age, comorbidities, access-to-care factors, or disease severity proxies. Finally, the data source represented insured patients within participating US health systems and may not fully represent all patients with NMIBC nationwide.

Conclusions

This retrospective cohort study of patients with NMIBC treated with BCG found that BCG-exposed disease in a distinct and underserved population was associated with most progression and cystectomy events.4,13 These findings may inform clinical trial design, regulatory considerations, and development of bladder-sparing therapies beyond the currently approved treatments.
