The current landscape of Ohio otolaryngologists and associated conditions: a state-level analysis
Authors: Samuel Borgemenke, Elizabeth A Beverly
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs demographics continue to shift in the United States, the access gap to physicians in specialized fields remains of utmost importance. This study examined the prevalence of otolaryngologists and otolaryngology subspecialists in Ohio, emphasizing related head and neck conditions and associations with social determinants of health.MethodsThis cross-sectional study utilized data from the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) and the CDC’s Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) mortality file. Health outcomes for all 88 counties in Ohio, Appalachian versus non-Appalachian county status, and otolaryngologist distribution were examined using t-tests and proportion tests. T-tests also assessed disparities in the prevalence of hearing impairment and oral malignancies, Pearson correlation tests evaluated associations between otolaryngologists and various social determinants of health.ResultsA total of 361 otolaryngologists practiced in Ohio. The mean number of otolaryngologists per county differed across Appalachian and non-Appalachian regions (1.3 ± 1.7 vs. 5.7 ± 14.0; P = 0.02) and southeastern regions (1.2 ± 1.4 vs. 4.6 ± 12.3; P = 0.03). However, the population-adjusted mean did not differ between Appalachian and non-Appalachian counties (P = 0.4) or southeastern and non-southeastern counties (P = 0.8). The prevalence of hearing impairment was higher in non-Appalachian counties (6.1 ± 1.2% vs. 5.6 ± 1.1%; P = 0.03); no difference was observed in southeastern and non-southeastern counties. In counties reporting at least one case of oral cancer, Appalachian counties had a higher prevalence of oral cancers compared to non-Appalachian counties (5.6 ± 2.3 vs. 4.4 ± 1.3, P = 0.04). In relation to social determinants of health, a significant negative relationship was observed between otolaryngologists and rurality (R=-0.2, P = 0.02) and long commutes (R=-0.2, P = 0.01).ConclusionWe observed lower otolaryngologist density and greater access challenges in Appalachian and southeastern counties, which may require patients to travel longer distances to receive specialty care or rely on less specialized providers. Targeted outreach initiatives and regional clinics are needed to address these disparities.Keywords: Appalachia, Ohio, Otolaryngology, Cancer, Hearing impairment

As demographics continue to shift in the United States (US), the access gap to physicians in specialized fields remains of utmost importance [1, 2]. Research indicates that the United States is moving towards an older population with increasing ethnic and racial diversity [2]. National workforce analyses consistently demonstrate shortages of physician specialists, with shortages concentrated in rural areas [1]. However, national estimates may conceal within-state variation, thereby limiting state-level workforce planning and policy development.

With approximately 361 physicians per 100,000 people, Ohio falls below the national average exemplifying the ongoing challenge to fulfill physician workforce needs [3]. Ohio mirrors the national demographic trends, with an aging population and growing racial and ethnic diversity [4]. Ohio also contains substantial rural areas, comprising approximately 30% of Ohio’s residents [5]. In addition, workforce planning and policy are largely enacted at the state level through mechanisms such as residency program funding, Medicaid reimbursement rates, and loan repayment incentive programs. For this reason, Ohio provides an ideal context to examine specialist workforce shortages.

The gap in access to care is starker for specialized medicine. The medical specialty of otolaryngology deals with disorders of the ears, nose, and throat. It makes up ~ 1% of all practicing physicians in the US, with 3.6 otolaryngologists per 100,000 individuals [3, 6]. Population density is one element that affects the distribution of otolaryngologist practices [7] Significant regional differences in access to otolaryngologists have been reported—otolaryngologists prefer to practice in urban settings instead of rural ones [8–10]. According to one survey, 92% of otolaryngologists in practice said they worked in urban environments [10].

Beyond prevalence of physicians and subspecialists, additional barriers impact access to quality healthcare in rural areas. Rural areas lack access to public transit, internet, and healthcare facilities [11, 12]. Moreover, socioeconomic and educational disparities further perpetuate challenges in delivering and receiving healthcare [13, 14]. The diverse nature of these challenges necessitates a comprehensive and individualized approach to help bridge the gaps in medical care.

Within Ohio, the Appalachian region experiences higher rates of chronic health conditions and greater barriers to healthcare access compared with non-Appalachian areas [14, 15]. Conditions commonly treated by otolaryngologists, including oral cancer, sleep apnea, and hearing loss, are more prevalent in Appalachian Ohio [16–18]. Despite this burden, Appalachian Ohio has 12% fewer primary care physicians, 28% fewer physician specialists, and 35% fewer mental health providers per 100,000 people [19]. To date, no research has examined the distribution of otolaryngologists, corresponding conditions, and barriers to care in Ohio.

This study examined the prevalence of otolaryngologists and otolaryngology subspecialists in Ohio. The purpose of this study was to map Ohio otolaryngologists’ locations by county, with a focus on head and neck conditions. We hypothesized that southeastern and Appalachian Ohio counties would exhibit lower concentrations of otolaryngologists, higher rates of hearing impairment and oral malignancies, and increased barriers to healthcare compared to other regions in the state. By furthering our understanding of this relationship, this study aims to offer guidance for policymakers and healthcare systems in Ohio. The ultimate goal is to enhance the quality and accessibility of essential healthcare services in southeast and Appalachian Ohio.

Methods

Ethics approval

This research makes use of de-identified, publicly available data. As a result, this study did not require Institutional Review Board (IRB) review and does not qualify as human subjects research under federal regulations. The Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were followed in this study [20].

Data source

The data utilized in this research investigation were sourced from two primary databases, namely the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services (CMS) and the CDC’s Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (CDC WONDER) mortality file [21, 22]. CMS provided data concerning otolaryngologists operating within the state of Ohio, as well as health outcome metrics obtained through the Mapping Medicare Disparities tool [23]. Meanwhile, CDC WONDER provided comprehensive county-level data related to malignant neoplasms [22].

Inclusion criteria

Inclusion of participants for this study required the following: data on otolaryngologists in Ohio, health outcomes for Ohio in the 88 counties, and classification of Appalachian counties versus non-Appalachian counties in Ohio. This project focused on otolaryngologists and chose not to expand on other providers (i.e., audiologists, speech language pathologists, etc.) in order to assess physician workforce distribution and access to medical and surgical services relating to the ears, nose, and throat.

Statistical analysis

This study compared the number of otolaryngologists in different regions of Ohio. Counties were classified as either non-Appalachian or Appalachian based on the Appalachian Regional Commission’s classification [27]. Moreover, the counties were classified as southeastern or non-southeastern using the standards the Southeast Ohio Regional Prevention Council set [28]. The number of otolaryngologists per 100,000 people in different regions was compared using t-tests. To evaluate the distribution of otolaryngologists in each region, t-tests were also used to compare the mean count of otolaryngologists per county. Proportion tests assessed the concentration of otolaryngologists across regions and the percentage of counties with at least one otolaryngologist. An analogous approach was applied to examine the prevalence of otolaryngologists in these regions.

To gauge the demand for otolaryngologists, the study also compared different conditions affecting the ears, nose, and throat by region, precisely conditions related to hearing and oral health. A t-test was conducted to analyze disparities in the prevalence of hearing impairment and oral malignancies.

The connections between otolaryngologists and other socioeconomic determinants of health were investigated via Pearson correlation tests. All calculations were adjusted for population size. The population-adjusted number of otolaryngologists was regressed against several factors, including rural areas, access to primary care physicians, commute times, and socioeconomic status.

The significance level for all tests in this study was set at α = 0.05. To facilitate the analysis, the collected variables and data were merged using R-Studio, version 2023.03.1 + 446 (statistical computing program).

Results

A total of 361 otolaryngologists were practicing in Ohio [Table 1]. Of the 88 counties, 13 (15%) were considered in southeast Ohio and 32 (36%) in the Appalachian region. All 13 counties in southeast Ohio (Athens, Gallia, Hocking, Jackson, Lawrence, Meigs, Morgan, Perry, Pike, Ross, Scioto, Vinton, and Washington county) are part of the Appalachian area.

The proportions test found a significant difference (P = 0.001) between Appalachian and non-Appalachian counties in the percentage of otolaryngologists, with 43/361 (11.9%) and 318/361 (88.1%), respectively. Southeast counties likewise had a smaller proportion of otolaryngologists than non-southeast counties (P = 0.001), with 15/361 (4.2%) versus 346/361 (95.8%).

The mean number of otolaryngologists per county differed significantly (P = 0.02) across Appalachian and non-Appalachian regions. The average number of otolaryngologists per county in Appalachian counties was 1.3 (standard deviation (SD) = 1.7). Non-Appalachian counties, on the other hand, had a mean of 5.7 (SD = 14.0). The average number of otolaryngologists per county in the southeast counties was significantly lower, at 1.2 (SD = 1.4), which was significantly lower (P = 0.03) than the average for non-southeast counties, which was 4.6 (SD = 12.3).

There were 0.4 (SD = 0.6) otolaryngologists per 100,000 individuals in Appalachian counties. Non-Appalachian counties averaged 0.6 (SD = 0.5) otolaryngologists per 100,000 persons. The population-adjusted mean number of otolaryngologists did not differ significantly between Appalachian and non-Appalachian counties (P = 0.4), nor between southeast and non-southeast counties (P = 0.8). Furthermore, no statistically significant difference in the proportion of counties with at least one otolaryngologist existed between southeast and non-southeast counties (P = 0.7) or Appalachian and non-Appalachian counties (P = 0.1). Nonetheless, counties having at least one otolaryngologist were less common in the southeast and Appalachian regions.

In Ohio, 154 otolaryngologists subspecialized by completing a fellowship [Table 2]. Appalachia was home to ten (6.5%) of the 154 specialists, only one (0.6%) practiced in southeast Ohio [Figure 1]. The percentage of specialists in Appalachian and non-Appalachian counties differed significantly (P = 0.001). Similarly, the proportion of specialists in southeast and non-southeast counties differed significantly (P = 0.001).

Furthermore, a statistically significant difference in the average number of specialists per county was observed between the southeast and non-southeast regions (P = 0.03). The average number of specialists per county in the southeast was 0.1 (SD = 0.3) and 2.0 (SD = 7.6) in non-southeast counties.

The population-adjusted mean number of specialists between Appalachian (mean = 0.1, SD = 0.2) and non-Appalachian counties (mean = 0.1, SD = 0.3) did not exhibit a significant difference (P = 0.4). Likewise, the population-adjusted mean number of specialists between southeast (mean = 0.1, SD = 0.2) and non-southeast counties (mean = 0.1, SD = 0.3) were not significantly different (P = 0.4). Additionally, there were no statistically significant differences in the proportion of counties with at least one specialist between Appalachian (6/32, 18.8%) and non-Appalachian (15/56, 18.8%) counties (P = 0.2) or between southeast (1/13, 7.6%) and non-southeast (20/75, 26.7%) counties (P = 0.1). While not statistically significant, Appalachian and southeast counties had fewer counties with at least one otolaryngology specialist.

In Ohio counties, the average prevalence of hearing impairment was 5.9% (SD = 1.2) [Table 3]. Interestingly, a significant difference was found between Appalachian and non-Appalachian counties, with the Appalachian counties showing a lower mean prevalence of hearing impairment (P = 0.03). The mean prevalence in Appalachian counties was 5.6% (SD = 1.1). However, the mean prevalence in non-Appalachian counties was 6.1% (SD = 1.2).

There was no statistically significant difference in the prevalence of hearing impairment between the southeast (mean = 6.4%, SD = 1.2) and non-southeast (mean = 5.8%, SD = 1.2) counties (P = 0.1).

Appalachian counties had a mean rate of 3.0 deaths per 100,000 people (SD = 3.3) for oral cancers. Non-Appalachian counties had a mean rate of 2.4 deaths (SD = 2.4). The rate of deaths in southeast counties was 2.7 (SD = 4.0) compared to 2.6 (SD = 2.5) in non-southeast counties. Between Appalachian and non-Appalachian counties (P = 0.4) and between southeast and non-southeast counties (P = 0.9), no statistically significant variations were found. However, Appalachian counties (mean = 5.6, SD = 2.3) showed substantially higher prevalence of these diseases compared to non-Appalachian counties (mean = 4.4, SD = 1.3) among counties that reported at least one case of oral cancer (P = 0.04).

The concentration of otolaryngologists per 100,000 was analyzed in relation to four selected variables representing social determinants of health and access to care using Pearson correlation tests [Figure 2]. The social determinants of health (socioeconomic status, percent rural, commute time, and number of primary care physicians) were used to predict the response variable, the number of otolaryngologists per 100,000 in Ohio counties. A significant negative relationship was observed between otolaryngologists and both percent rural (R=-0.2, P = 0.02) and long commute (R=-0.2, P = 0.01). Additionally, otolaryngologists and primary care physicians had a significant positive relationship (R = 0.3, P = 0.002). The analysis revealed no significant relationship between otolaryngologists and socioeconomic status (R = 0.1, P = 0.6).

Discussion

To address the region’s specific health needs and improve quality of life, Appalachian Ohio residents need reliable access to skilled otolaryngologists. This study examined the availability and geographic distribution of otolaryngologists and subspecialists across Ohio counties, in alignment with the conditions they treat and the populations they serve. By gaining a deeper understanding of this relationship, the study aimed to provide valuable guidance for Ohio’s policymakers and healthcare systems to improve the quality and accessibility of vital otolaryngological services in southeast and Appalachian Ohio.

Consistent with our hypothesis, southeastern and Appalachian counties in Ohio had fewer otolaryngologists and more significant social determinants of health than other regions of the state. This discrepancy can point to possible differences between the healthcare systems in the Appalachian and southeastern areas [29]. Otolaryngologist practices in this study ranged from private practice to hospital-employed to academic. The differences in propensity to be in private practice versus hospital-employed versus academic could further demonstrate these variances between the regional healthcare systems. The percentage of counties with at least one otolaryngologist was not statistically different across regions when the study was adjusted for population. Although not reaching statistical significance, it is noteworthy that counties in the southeast and Appalachian regions often had a lower percentage of counties with access to at least one otolaryngologist. One possible explanation for the lack of statistical significance could be the limited sample size, which led to more computational variability.

Out of the 154 otolaryngology subspecialists, 10 (6.5%) practiced in Appalachia and one (0.6%) in southeast Ohio. The Appalachian and southeast areas significantly differed in the proportion of subspecialists. Furthermore, the raw population-adjusted mean number of specialists varied significantly across counties in the southeast and those in the non-southeast. Differences in the number of otolaryngology subspecialists suggest that populations in the southeast and Appalachia lack access to high-quality healthcare, which could result in them having to travel farther for care or having providers with less specialty-specific training treat them.

The Appalachian counties had a lower mean prevalence of hearing impairment regarding otolaryngology issues. Previous literature showed Appalachia had a higher reported hearing loss than the rest of the United States [30, 31]. This difference in findings may be attributed to variable definitions, population age structure, coding differences, or Medicare enrollment patterns.

There were no significant differences regarding oral cancers. However, among counties that reported at least one instance of oral malignancy, Appalachian counties demonstrated significantly higher rates of these conditions when contrasted with their non-Appalachian counterparts. These results may help determine which regions best suit prospective outreach clinics and focused healthcare programs. Further researching age distributions and small-number effects of hearing impairment and oral cancers may enhance public health efforts.

Previous studies have shown that providing specialized services through outreach programs effectively addresses distance, communication, and cultural appropriateness issues [32–34]. This has led to a notable increase in consultations with people living in remote locations [33]. Outreach clinics are a valuable strategy for improving patient access to specialized services and improving the efficiency and caliber of healthcare delivery in remote areas [32, 34]. Outreach programs may be essential in closing the healthcare gap in Appalachian and southeast Ohio and guaranteeing that marginalized communities have fair access to vital medical treatment. Potential recruitment incentives to otolaryngologists to encourage rural practice, telehealth programs, or mobile clinics could increase access to healthcare in these areas.

Several social determinants of health showed significant correlations with the number of otolaryngologists per 100,000 people. A significant negative association exists between the percentage of rural residents, long commute times, and otolaryngologists’ availability. This research highlights the difficulties that southeastern and Appalachian Ohio residents may encounter when trying to access healthcare services. These difficulties may include long travel times, appointment waiting periods, unfulfilled medical needs, or relying on providers with less specialty-specific training because of the need for more otolaryngologists [15]. As a result, meeting the healthcare requirements of people living in rural areas requires an integrated approach beyond healthcare to include transportation and other general accessibility challenges [12].

Providing fair access to healthcare services requires a multifaceted approach. Policymakers and healthcare professionals must identify and confront geographic and financial obstacles. By doing this, they ensure that otolaryngology healthcare services are distributed equitably and open the door for significant advancements in improving overall health outcomes. This challenging goal requires incorporating cultural knowledge and sensitivity into healthcare delivery. Customized programs aimed at specific communities with limited resources strengthen this strategy even further.

This study’s limitations should be considered when interpreting the results. First, concentrating on the Appalachian region may limit the results’ generalizability because this region has distinct traits and healthcare dynamics that might not apply to other regions or populations. As a result, caution must be used when applying the results to other situations. An additional constraint pertains to the study’s sample size. The statistical robustness of the results may have been limited by the relatively small number of southeastern counties included (n = 13) in the analysis. Finally, pediatric otolaryngologists, academic clinicians, and those billing through institutional National Provider Identifier (NPIs) may have been undercounted in the CMS provider files, and lower the number of these respective otolaryngologists in the study.

Conclusion

This study examined the distribution of otolaryngologists and subspecialists across Ohio, with a focus in southeastern and Appalachian counties. Our hypothesis of lower otolaryngologist concentrations and greater access challenges in these regions was largely confirmed, with a scarcity of subspecialists in southeast and Appalachian areas, which may require patients to travel longer travel distances or rely on less specialized providers. These findings underscore the need for targeted outreach initiatives and regional clinics to address disparities. The findings also highlight the association between otolaryngologists and social determinants of health, emphasizing the difficulties residents in southeastern and Appalachian Ohio face when accessing healthcare. The study calls for a multifaceted approach to ensure equitable healthcare access and cultural competence between skilled healthcare providers and policymakers.
