Efficacy of Impella Microaxial Left Ventricular Assist Device as Bridge to Transplant in Children With End-Stage Heart Disease
Authors: Bahaaldin Alsoufi, Deborah Kozik, Bradley Oelkers, Sarah Wilkens, Joshua Sparks, Jaimin Trivedi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThe Impella microaxial left ventricular assist device (Abiomed) is increasingly used in adults as extended support bridging to heart transplantation. In recent years, its off-label use in children has also expanded. This review summarizes current experiences with Impella support in paediatric patients listed for heart transplantation and compares outcomes with contemporaneous cohorts who either received no support or other ventricular assist device support.MethodsWe analysed data from the United Network for Organ Sharing registry between January 2018 and July 2024, including all children listed for heart transplantation. Longitudinal outcomes were assessed using Kaplan-Meier analysis, with comparisons made between children supported with Impella, those receiving other device support, and those managed without mechanical support.ResultsImpella utilization increased steadily over time. The Impella cohort included 59 children, with a median age of 14 years (IQR 13-16) at listing and 15 years (IQR 13-16) at transplant. The underlying pathologies were as follows: cardiomyopathy (75%, n = 44), congenital heart disease (14%, n = 8), and other conditions (12%, n = 7). Impella device types used were as follows: 5.0/5.5 (59%, n = 35), CP (33%, n = 20), and RP (8%, n = 4). Ten patients (17%) were converted to a durable ventricular assist device with a median support of 5 days (IQR 2-11). Forty-two patients (71%) received transplantation while on Impella support (median 15 days, IQR 8-22). Five patients (8%) died while awaiting transplantation. Among those who received transplantation, 4 deaths occurred during follow-up. Three-year post-transplant survival was not statistically significantly different for children who received Impella, other device, and no device prior to transplantation.ConclusionsImpella use in children as a bridge to heart transplantation has increased, with favourable early outcomes. The Impella 5.5 has contributed to this trend, likely due to its high-flow capacity and mobility potential. Further assessment of its advantages, efficacy, and safety is needed.

INTRODUCTION

Advances in the management of adult patients with end-stage heart failure have significantly reshaped clinical strategies, particularly through the development of new ventricular assist devices (VADs). These devices, designed for both short- and long-term support, have demonstrated improved efficiency and safety, especially with advancements in anticoagulation therapies.1 Furthermore, they have facilitated patient mobilization and discharge, contributing to better overall outcomes. However, similar progress has not been realized in the paediatric population, where treatment options remain more limited and several unique challenges persist. Key issues include the small size of paediatric patients, the complexities of managing VADs in growing children, the extended duration of support often required due to a shortage of small donor organs, and the higher likelihood of sensitization in children with congenital heart disease, particularly those with a history of prior surgeries. These factors contribute to a high risk of complications such as infection, thrombosis, and device failure.2,3 Currently, available mechanical circulatory support options for children are limited to devices like the Excor Berlin Heart, which poses significant challenges in mobilization and rehabilitation, as well as high morbidity; the HeartMate 3, which is primarily used in larger paediatric patients; and the Impella microaxial device, which is suitable for short-term support in larger children.2,3

The Impella microaxial device represents a significant advancement in short-term mechanical circulatory support. Designed for minimally invasive percutaneous insertion, the device provides immediate circulatory assistance by directly unloading the left ventricle, improving cardiac output and enhancing end-organ perfusion.4 Although originally intended for short-term use, its application in adult patients has expanded to include longer-term support, such as bridging to heart transplantation (HT).5 This is especially true for the Impella 5.5, which is inserted via the axillary artery, allowing for patient mobilization and rehabilitation while awaiting a donor heart. While the device’s primary use has been in adults, there is increasing interest in its potential for paediatric patients, particularly larger children, as a short-term support option during acute decompensation or as a bridge to more definitive therapies. However, the use of the Impella in paediatric populations is limited by factors such as device size, patient selection, and the risk of complications, including hemolysis, thrombosis, and vascular injury.6–10 Recent modifications to implantation techniques, such as using the innominate artery for placement, have addressed some of these challenges, potentially enabling off-label use of the Impella in smaller paediatric patients and extending its duration of use, similar to its application in adult populations.6,11,12

In this manuscript, we review the current national experience with the use of the Impella device in children listed for HT. Our objective is to analyse trends in the utilization of the Impella device and assess outcomes, comparing these with those of children who received HT who are supported by other devices or who do not require device support prior to transplantation.

METHODS

Statement of IRB and ethics board approval

This study used publicly available United Network for Organ Sharing (UNOS) registry’s de-identified data. This registry data is national-level database publicly available for research purpose at the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN) platform (https://optn.transplant.hrsa.gov/data/view-data-reports/request-data/).

Approval to conduct research using the UNOS database was obtained from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) under protocol IRB 14.0343, originally granted on 17 August 2022, and renewed annually thereafter. The IRB determined that this study does not constitute human subjects research and therefore exempted it from human subjects research regulations. As such, individual patient consent was not required.

Patients and methods

Patient cohort

This is a retrospective analysis of data from the UNOS. We focused on patients under the age of 18 who were listed for HT between 2018 and 2024. Our primary cohort consisted of children who received Impella support while on the waiting list. The objective of this study was to assess clinical outcomes in listed children managed with Impella support. VAD recipients and children listed without device support were included as comparator groups, notwithstanding significant differences in baseline characteristics (Table 1). To mitigate these discrepancies, the analysis was restricted to non-congenital cardiomyopathy patients aged 14-18 years, with a body mass index >14, body weight exceeding 29 kg, and UNOS urgency status 1 (Table 2).

Statistical analysis

Non-parametric univariate statistical methods were initially used to evaluate baseline characteristics. Continuous variables were analysed using Kruskal-Wallis test, and the categorical variables were analysed using Fisher’s exact test. Mantel-Haenszel was used to evaluate Impella use trends. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were computed to assess the actuarial survival. Overall survival was compared using the log-rank test. All the data is presented as median with IQR or N (%). The statistical analysis was done using SAS 9.4 software (SAS Inc., Cary, NC) at a 95% confidence level. Patients with missing data (<1%) of the cohort were excluded from analysis.

RESULTS

Patient characteristics

The cohort included 59 children who received Impella support, of whom 54 were listed after 2018. Detailed characteristics of these 54 patients are summarized in Table 1. The group comprised 41 boys (76%) and 13 girls (24%). The median age at listing for HT was 14 years (IQR, 13-16), and the median age at transplant was 15 years (IQR, 13-16). The median body mass index (BMI) at transplant was 24 kg/m2 (IQR, 19-29). By race, 27 patients (50%) were White, 19 (35%) Black, and 8 (15%) of other racial backgrounds. The predominant aetiology of heart failure was dilated cardiomyopathy (47 patients, 87%), with congenital heart disease accounting for the remaining 7 patients (13%).

The distribution of Impella device types used in our cohort was as follows: Impella 5.0/5.5 in 32 patients (59%), Impella CP in 20 patients (37%), and Impella RP in 2 patients (4%). Notably, there was a clear trend towards increased use of the Impella device over time, as illustrated in Figure 1. This upward trend appears steady and linear. For instance, in 2018, only 0.14% of children listed for HT received Impella support, whereas by the first half of 2024, this proportion had risen to 4.2% (Mantel-Haenszel P-value <.01). Another notable observation was the geographic variation in Impella use among children, with Region 11 reporting the highest number of cases (14 patients, 24%) (Figure 2).

Outcomes after Impella device

The outcomes for children who received Impella support are summarized in Figure 3. Of the 54 Impella patients listed, 2 died before transplant, 42 underwent direct HT, and 10 were bridged to a durable VAD, with one dying before transplant and 9 receiving HT. At the time of HT, 3 patients (6%) required mechanical ventilation and 28 (54%) were receiving additional inotropic support. The median duration of Impella support was 5 days (IQR, 2-11) in patients converted to durable VAD, and 15 days (IQR, 8-22) in those proceeding directly to HT. Post-transplant, 7 patients (14%) required dialysis, none experienced stroke, and 6 (12%) were treated for acute rejection prior to hospital discharge.

DISCUSSION

The primary aim of our study was not to examine the entire spectrum of Impella use in children, as this would not be fully captured in the UNOS database. Impella is often used for acute support towards recovery, but our focus was specifically on its evolving role in supporting children listed for HT. The present study reveals a notable upward trend in the use of the Impella device among children listed for HT. This trend appears to follow a linear progression, with Impella being utilized in approximately 2.7% of children listed in 2023 and 4.2% of those listed during the first half of 2024. Interestingly, this increase has not been accompanied by a corresponding decrease in the use of other VADs within this patient population, suggesting that while Impella may be serving as an alternative, it has only replaced a small fraction so far of other VAD options. Additionally, regional variations in the adoption of Impella were observed across the 11 geographic regions of the OPTN, highlighting differences in the rate of integration of this novel approach to supporting children awaiting HT in the United States. We acknowledge the existence of institutional variability in the philosophy and clinical approach to the use of Impella devices. These differences are not unexpected and are likely to continue evolving as collective experience grows and new data emerge. In our study, despite the recognized heterogeneity in practice, we did not observe any regional differences that reached statistical significance with regard to Impella use alone versus as a bridge to another device, likely reflecting both the relatively small cohort size and the early stage of experience with this technology in paediatric patients. As with many aspects of clinical care, practice patterns will continue to shift over time, informed by ongoing research and accumulating clinical experience; this remains a work in progress.

Our data also reveals that the majority of children who received Impella after being listed for HT were older and larger, which is not unexpected, given that the Impella is an FDA-approved device primarily indicated for adult use. However, there are emerging reports detailing the use of Impella in smaller paediatric patients.6,12 Notably, a report from our institution described the use of Impella in the smallest child on record to date. In this case, Impella was employed as a bridge to HT in a 9-year-old boy weighing 22 kg, who was suffering from end-stage dilated cardiomyopathy, worsening end-organ dysfunction, and increasing pulmonary vascular resistance. The Impella 5.5 device was successfully placed in the innominate artery using a partial sternotomy approach, and the child was supported for 61 days until he received an HT.12 More recently, although unpublished, we utilized the Impella 5.5 to support a 7-year-old girl weighing 18.5 kg for 105 days until she underwent HT. According to discussions with the Impella representatives, this patient is currently the smallest child to have been supported by Impella. The extended use of Impella 5.5 in smaller children is made possible by a fitting study conducted using CT angiography (CTA). This imaging technique allows for precise measurement of both the vessel diameters (the vessel diameter fit), helping determine the optimal site for Impella insertion (either axillary or innominate), and the distance from the aortic valve to the apex of the heart (the transvalvular fit), ensuring the dilated heart can accommodate the device appropriately.

In addition to the increasing use of Impella in smaller children, there are several reports in the literature highlighting the versatility of the device in addressing various complex anatomical and clinical situations.13,14 One such report by Morray et al describes the use of Impella in children with single ventricle physiology who have undergone a Fontan procedure.13 In older patients with Fontan physiology and failing systemic ventricles, ensuring a proper transvalvular fit allows the device to be accommodated in systemic ventricles, including those with right ventricular morphology.

In our experience, we have successfully used the Impella 5.5 inserted into a graft sutured to the innominate artery in children with congenital heart disease and prior sternotomy. This was accomplished through an oblique suprasternal notch incision, providing sufficient access without the need for a partial sternotomy. This approach suggests that the Impella 5.5 can be utilized in patients with a prior sternotomy, eliminating the need for a redo sternotomy and expanding the potential applications of the device.

Additionally, there have been reports of combining Impella with extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) in children, referred to as the “ECPELLA” strategy.15 In this approach, ECMO provides cardiac support while Impella offers left ventricular unloading. The ECPELLA strategy holds promise as a temporary support option for the right ventricle, with the potential to discontinue ECMO and transition to long-term Impella support of the left ventricle. This strategy could be particularly beneficial for children presenting with biventricular failure. Another potential option in these cases involves using both the Impella 5.5 for left ventricular support and the newer Impella RP for right ventricular support.16 While there have been a few clinical uses of this combined use in children, there are no published reports to date. Current expectations suggest that the Impella RP is intended for short-term rather than prolonged support of the right ventricle. Nevertheless, these experiences point to the potential for Impella to serve as a viable alternative to other VADs in children with biventricular failure, particularly in those with a recoverable right ventricle that may not require extended support.

While the majority of children in our cohort who were supported solely with Impella until HT represented a significant portion of the increased use of Impella in recent years, another important clinical application was its use as a bridge to a more durable VAD, most commonly the HeartMate 3.17,18 Notably, the outcomes of HT in patients supported by Impella alone, or as a bridge to another VAD, were excellent and comparable. Although the survival rates were not significantly different from those supported by VADs alone, there was a trend towards improved survival with Impella, though this did not reach statistical significance. This finding is promising and underscores the potential for shifting support strategies to more frequently incorporate Impella as a sole long-term support option or as a temporary bridge to an implantable VAD.

However, determining which patients are appropriate candidates for Impella as a bridge to HT versus as a bridge to an implantable VAD remains an ongoing challenge. The need for long-term biventricular support may favour the use of implantable VADs. Additionally, the anticipated rehabilitation time required for a patient to become a candidate for HT will likely influence the choice of device. In patients with severe debilitation and end-organ dysfunction, many centres may not consider them for HT listing, making implantable devices a more attractive option due to the extended rehabilitation process required before they can become transplant candidates. Conversely, in patients with reasonable functional status and acute decompensation, where immediate listing for HT is a consideration, the Impella device may emerge as a more favourable option. For patients in an intermediate clinical state, an Impella bridge to decision strategy could be employed, allowing for a choice between HT or an implantable VAD depending on the patient’s progression. Lastly, various factors such as patient size, blood type, sensitization, certain anatomical considerations, and the potential need for multiorgan transplantation can influence waitlist duration. These factors should be carefully considered when selecting the appropriate device, as implantable VADs may still be the preferred option for those with anticipated prolonged waitlist times before a donor heart becomes available.

Our dataset does not permit an evaluation of the efficacy or complications associated with prolonged Impella support, particularly as a bridge to HT. While the Impella device is designed to unload the left ventricle and enhance cardiac output, its effectiveness in achieving left ventricular unloading and reducing mitral regurgitation, especially when compared to implantable devices like the HeartMate 3, remains inadequately studied. It is still uncertain whether patients may benefit more from an implantable device in terms of superior left ventricular unloading, improved lung dynamics, and reduced pulmonary vascular pressures. Additionally, the risk of haemolysis associated with prolonged use of the Impella device raises concerns that cannot be fully assessed with our dataset. However, based on our institution’s experience with the Impella device in 6 paediatric patients, we have not observed significant haemolysis, even with usage durations up to a maximum of 101 days in one patient.

Summary

In summary, the study highlights a growing adoption of the Impella device among HT candidates, particularly in children who are older or larger, aligning with the device’s FDA approval for adult use. While regional differences in adoption were observed, the increasing use of Impella does not seem to be replacing other VADs on a large scale. The device’s versatility is evident in its application across complex cases, including smaller children and those with congenital heart conditions. Overall, the outcomes for children supported by Impella, whether as a bridge to transplant or to other VADs, were favourable, though determining the ideal candidates for Impella versus other devices remains an area for further study. Despite some unanswered questions regarding the efficacy and potential risks of prolonged Impella use, particularly in comparison to implantable VADs, the device presents a viable option for paediatric heart failure management. We acknowledge that this is an observational study utilizing registry data, and that the relatively small sample size may limit the strength of certain analyses and the generalizability of the conclusions. The primary value of this manuscript is to highlight the potential expanded role of the Impella device in supporting children listed for HT. There remains much to learn from this growing experience, and future multicentre studies, potentially through ACTION, will be essential to further advance our understanding of this technology, define its optimal indications, and identify the most suitable patient candidates.
