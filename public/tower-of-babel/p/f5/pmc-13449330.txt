Composite C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose–adiposity indices and incident cardiovascular disease in the CHARLS cohort: a prospective cohort study
Authors: Peng Wang, Zhaobin Zheng, Shuang Wang, Jun Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground & aimsCardiovascular disease (CVD) is influenced by metabolic dysfunction, chronic inflammation, and adiposity. The C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI) integrates inflammatory and metabolic information, but the associations of CTI-derived adiposity indices with incident CVD remain insufficiently characterized. We aimed to examine the associations of several CTI-derived adiposity indices with incident CVD and compare their relative performance in a nationwide cohort of middle-aged and older adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from 6,161 participants aged ≥ 45 years without baseline CVD from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). Four CTI-derived adiposity indices were evaluated: CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI. Cox proportional hazards models were used to estimate hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for incident CVD. Dose–response relationships were assessed using restricted cubic splines (RCS). Kaplan–Meier, discrimination and reclassification, cumulative exposure, trajectory, subgroup, component-outcome, mediation, and sensitivity analyses were also performed.ResultsDuring a median follow-up of 9 years, 1,503 participants (24.39%) developed CVD. Higher levels of CTI-derived adiposity indices were associated with increased CVD risk. In fully adjusted models, participants in the highest quartile had higher CVD risk than those in the lowest quartile for CTI-WHtR (HR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.24–2.00), CTI-BMI (HR = 1.55, 95% CI 1.23–1.97), CTI-BRI (HR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.11–1.78), and CTI-WWI (HR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.74), with significant trends across quartiles. RCS analyses showed predominantly linear dose–response relationships. Discrimination was modest, with AUCs ranging from 0.558 for CTI-WWI to 0.573 for CTI-WHtR, compared with 0.522 for CTI. All CTI-derived indices improved continuous net reclassification improvement and integrated discrimination improvement beyond the conventional risk factor model. Higher cumulative exposure and higher trajectory clusters of CTI-derived indices were also associated with increased CVD risk. Mediation analyses suggested interrelationships between metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation and adiposity in relation to CVD.ConclusionsCTI-derived adiposity indices were associated with incident CVD in middle-aged and older adults, with CTI-WHtR showing the most consistent overall performance among the evaluated indices. These findings support the epidemiological relevance of integrating metabolic–inflammatory burden with adiposity-related phenotypes in cardiovascular risk assessment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12933-026-03229-6.Keywords: Cardiovascular disease, CTI, Adiposity indices, CHARLS

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading cause of death worldwide, accounting for an estimated 17.9 million deaths annually and imposing a substantial global health burden [1]. The continuing rise in cardiometabolic disorders has further intensified this burden, as dysglycemia, insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and excess adiposity jointly contribute to atherosclerosis and adverse cardiovascular outcomes [2, 3]. Consequently, there is growing interest in simple and accessible biomarkers that can integrate multiple cardiometabolic risk domains and better characterize cardiovascular risk than conventional single indicators.

CVD development is increasingly understood as the result of interrelated metabolic and inflammatory disturbances rather than isolated abnormalities [4–6]. The triglyceride–glucose (TyG) index has been widely used as a surrogate marker of insulin resistance, whereas C-reactive protein (CRP) is a well-established indicator of systemic inflammation and vascular injury [7, 8]. In 2022, Ruan et al. proposed the C-reactive protein–triglyceride–glucose index (CTI) as a composite biomarker integrating inflammatory and metabolic information, and subsequent studies have reported associations between CTI and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes [9–11]. By combining information on insulin resistance-related metabolic dysfunction and systemic inflammation, CTI may provide a useful summary of metabolic–inflammatory burden relevant to cardiovascular risk.

Adiposity is another central determinant of cardiometabolic health and may further modify or amplify the adverse effects of metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation. In particular, central and visceral fat accumulation has been more closely linked than general obesity to insulin resistance, endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and cardiovascular events [12, 13]. Although body mass index (BMI) remains the most commonly used anthropometric measure, it does not adequately capture body fat distribution or visceral adiposity [14]. Alternative anthropometric indices, including waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), body roundness index (BRI), and weight-adjusted waist index (WWI), have therefore gained increasing attention as markers of adverse adiposity phenotypes and cardiovascular risk [15–17].

Given the close interconnection among inflammation, metabolic dysfunction, and adiposity, combining CTI with anthropometric measures may provide an integrated characterization of related cardiometabolic risk domains. Previous studies have mainly focused on TyG-related derivatives combined with obesity indicators such as BMI, WHtR, BRI, and WWI [18–21]. However, evidence directly examining and comparing CTI-derived adiposity formulations remains limited, particularly in relation to incident CVD in a prospective population-based cohort. Clarifying whether these related composite indices show consistent associations and how their relative performance compares may help refine the epidemiological characterization of metabolic–inflammatory and adiposity-related cardiovascular risk.

Using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), the present study investigated the associations of four CTI-derived adiposity indices—CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI—with incident CVD in a nationwide cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults. We further compared their association patterns, dose–response relationships, and relative epidemiological performance within a unified prospective analytic framework.

Methods

Data source and study design

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is a nationally representative prospective cohort designed to collect multidimensional data on the health and socioeconomic status of Chinese adults aged 45 years and older. The baseline survey was conducted in 2011–2012 and enrolled 17,705 participants from 450 urban and rural communities across 28 provinces using a multistage, stratified probability sampling design. Follow-up surveys were conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Data were collected through face-to-face interviews by trained investigators using standardized questionnaires and measurements. The CHARLS protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB 00001052–11015) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants provided written informed consent. Detailed information on the study design and sampling procedures has been described previously and is available on the official CHARLS website [22]. For the present study, participants were eligible if they were aged ≥ 45 years at baseline and had data required to calculate CTI and the related composite indices. Participants were excluded if they had self-reported CVD at baseline, had missing key demographic information, lacked laboratory measurements required for CTI calculation, had missing anthropometric measurements, or were lost to follow-up. After these exclusions, 6161 participants were included in the final analytic sample (Fig. 1). Missingness of key baseline variables is summarized in Supplementary Table S1. The complete-case sensitivity analysis included 5931 participants.

Calculation of CTI and its derivative indices

Fasting venous blood samples were collected during the CHARLS baseline survey following standardized protocols. Serum triglycerides (TG), fasting blood glucose (FBG), and CRP levels were measured in certified laboratories using standard biochemical assays. Anthropometric measurements, including height, weight, and waist circumference (WC), were obtained by trained investigators according to standardized procedures. BMI was calculated as weight (kg)/height2 (m2). CTI was calculated as CTI = 0.412 × ln(CRP [mg/L]) + ln(TG [mg/dL] × FBG [mg/dL])/2 [9]. WHtR was calculated as WHtR = WC/height. BRI was calculated as BRI = 364.2 − 365.5 × √(1 − (WC [m]/2π)2/(0.5 × height [m])2) [23], and WWI was calculated as WWI = WC (cm)/√weight (kg) [24]. Based on these measures, four CTI-derived indices were constructed: CTI-BMI = CTI × BMI, CTI-WHtR = CTI × WHtR, CTI-BRI = CTI × BRI, and CTI-WWI = CTI × WWI.

To assess long-term exposure, cumulative CTI (cuCTI) and cumulative composite indices (cuCTI-BMI, cuCTI-WHtR, cuCTI-BRI, and cuCTI-WWI) were calculated using repeated measurements from Wave 2 (2012) and Wave 3 (2015). Cumulative exposure was estimated as the product of the average value across the two time points and the time interval, according to the formula: cuCTI = (CTI2012 + CTI2015)/2 × (2015 − 2012). The same approach was applied to calculate cumulative values of the derived composite indices. This method reflects long-term exposure and is consistent with previously described approaches for cumulative metabolic indices in CHARLS cohorts [25, 26].

Definition of CVD

Incident CVD was identified based on self-reported physician diagnoses during follow-up interviews. Participants were asked whether they had ever been diagnosed by a doctor with heart disease (including heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems) or stroke. CVD was defined as the presence of either heart disease or stroke. This definition is consistent with previous studies using CHARLS data [11]. Follow-up time was defined as the interval from the baseline interview in 2011 to the first follow-up interview at which incident CVD was reported, the last available follow-up interview, or the end of follow-up in 2020, whichever came first. In the analytic sample, the median follow-up duration was 9 years (range, 2–9 years).

Covariates

Covariates included sociodemographic characteristics (age, sex, marital status, education level, smoking status, and alcohol consumption), health indicators (systolic blood pressure [SBP], diastolic blood pressure [DBP], hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia), and laboratory parameters (CRP, FBG, TG, total cholesterol [TC], high-density lipoprotein cholesterol [HDL-C], low-density lipoprotein cholesterol [LDL-C], glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c], serum creatinine [Scr], and blood urea nitrogen [BUN]). Hypertension was defined as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg, DBP ≥ 90 mmHg, or self-reported physician-diagnosed hypertension. Diabetes was defined as FBG ≥ 7.0 mmol/L, HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, or self-reported physician-diagnosed diabetes. Dyslipidemia was defined as TC ≥ 240 mg/dL, TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL-C ≥ 160 mg/dL, or self-reported dyslipidemia.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) for normally distributed data and as median (interquartile range) for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables are presented as number (percentage). Differences in baseline characteristics between participants with and without incident CVD were compared using the Student’s t test or the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables and the χ2 test for categorical variables. Pearson correlation analysis was used to evaluate the relationships among CTI-derived composite indices.

Associations between baseline CTI-derived adiposity indices and incident CVD were examined using Cox proportional hazards models. Each index was analyzed as a continuous variable and by quartiles, with Q1 as the reference. Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age, sex, marital status, residence, and education level; and Model 3 was further adjusted for smoking status, drinking status, and hypertension. Linear trends were tested by modeling quartile medians as continuous variables. Dose–response relationships were examined using three-knot restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses based on Model 3.

To assess whether composite indices provided information beyond their components, additional Cox models compared CTI alone, each adiposity indicator alone, CTI plus the corresponding adiposity indicator, and the respective CTI-derived index, using AIC and Harrell’s C-index for comparison. Secondary analyses included cumulative exposure analyses using repeated measurements from 2012 to 2015, k-means trajectory analyses between 2012 and 2015, subgroup analyses, and separate analyses for incident heart disease and stroke. For trajectory analyses, three clusters were selected according to the elbow criterion, group size, and interpretability, with stability improved using multiple random initializations (nstart = 25). ROC analyses compared discrimination for incident CVD during the overall follow-up period, and AUC differences were assessed using the DeLong test with Holm correction. Incremental predictive performance beyond conventional risk factors was evaluated using continuous NRI, IDI, and median improvement at the median follow-up time.

Exploratory mediation analyses used repeated measurements from 2011 to 2015. CTI measured in 2011 was treated as the exposure, adiposity indices measured in 2015 as continuous mediators, and incident CVD during follow-up as the binary outcome. Reverse mediation models used 2011 adiposity indices as exposures and 2015 CTI as the mediator. Regression-based mediation analyses with bootstrap resampling estimated indirect effects, direct effects, total effects, mediation proportions, and 95% CIs. Sensitivity analyses used sex-specific quartiles, complete-case data, and multivariable logistic regression models. Missing data for key variables were summarized as numbers and percentages.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

Baseline characteristics of the study population are shown in Table 1. Among the 6161 participants, 1503 (24.39%) developed CVD during follow-up. Compared with those without CVD, participants who developed CVD were older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia, as well as higher levels of BMI, WC, SBP, DBP, FBG, HbA1c, TC, TG, LDL-C, and CRP, and lower HDL-C levels (all P < 0.05). Regarding the exposure variables, CTI and adiposity-related indices (BRI, WHtR, and WWI) were significantly higher in participants who developed CVD (all P < 0.001). The composite indices CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI were also significantly higher in the CVD group (all P < 0.001), and the proportion of CVD cases increased progressively across their quartiles (all P < 0.001). Correlation analysis showed significant positive correlations among the CTI-derived adiposity indices (all P < 0.001), with the strongest correlation observed between CTI-WHtR and CTI-BRI (r = 0.95), followed by CTI-WHtR and CTI-WWI (r = 0.93). The lowest correlation was observed between CTI-BMI and CTI-WWI (r = 0.69), indicating substantial but variable overlap across indices (Fig. S1).

Associations of CTI-derived composite indices with incident CVD

As shown in Table 2, higher levels of CTI-derived adiposity indices were associated with an increased risk of incident CVD. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI were all significantly associated with incident CVD. Compared with the lowest quartile, the highest quartile was associated with increased CVD risk for CTI-WHtR (HR = 1.57, 95% CI 1.24–2.00), CTI-BMI (HR = 1.55, 95% CI 1.23–1.97), CTI-BRI (HR = 1.40, 95% CI 1.11–1.78), and CTI-WWI (HR = 1.37, 95% CI 1.08–1.74), with significant trends across quartiles for all indices (all P for trend ≤ 0.019). RCS analyses further showed positive dose–response relationships between CTI-derived indices and CVD risk (Fig. 2). The overall associations were significant for CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, and CTI-BRI (P for overall < 0.001) and for CTI-WWI (P for overall = 0.016), with no evidence of nonlinearity (P Por nonlinear > 0.05).

Kaplan–Meier analysis and discrimination/reclassification performance of CTI-derived indices

Kaplan–Meier analyses showed progressively higher cumulative CVD incidence across increasing quartiles of all CTI-derived indices (all log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 3). ROC analysis based on incident CVD during the overall follow-up period showed modest discrimination, with AUCs ranging from 0.558 for CTI-WWI to 0.573 for CTI-WHtR, compared with 0.522 for CTI (Fig. 4). After Holm correction, CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, and CTI-BRI had higher AUCs than CTI, whereas CTI-WWI did not (Supplementary Table S2). In reclassification analyses, all CTI-derived indices improved both continuous NRI and IDI beyond the conventional risk factor model, with CTI-WHtR showing the largest continuous NRI (0.092, 95% CI 0.050–0.130) and an IDI of 0.003 (95% CI 0.001–0.007; both P < 0.001) (Supplementary Table S3).

Comparison of composite indices with their individual components

Additional Cox regression analyses compared CTI alone, each adiposity indicator alone, CTI plus the corresponding adiposity indicator, and the respective composite index (Supplementary Table S4). Compared with the corresponding dual-component models, the composite indices showed lower AIC values and higher C-indices, particularly for CTI-WHtR (AIC: 11183.99 vs. 11185.14; C-index: 0.613 vs. 0.612), CTI-BMI (11191.86 vs. 11195.30; 0.609 vs. 0.607), and CTI-BRI (11191.49 vs. 11193.97; 0.609 vs. 0.608). A similar but smaller difference was observed for CTI-WWI (11196.61 vs. 11199.37; 0.607 vs. 0.606).

Trajectory patterns of CTI-derived indices and risk of incident CVD

Trajectory patterns of CTI-derived indices from 2012 to 2015 are shown in Fig. 5, where participants were classified into three clusters representing low, moderate, and high trajectories. The three-cluster solution was supported by elbow plots based on total within-cluster sum of squares for all four indices (Figure S2). As presented in Table 3, higher trajectory clusters were associated with increased CVD risk. In the fully adjusted model (Model 3), compared with cluster 1, cluster 2 and cluster 3 of CTI-WHtR were associated with higher CVD risk (HR = 1.39, 95% CI 1.09–1.79 and HR = 1.51, 95% CI 1.12–2.04; P for trend < 0.001). Similar associations were observed for CTI-BMI (HR = 1.41, 95% CI 1.11–1.78 and HR = 1.50, 95% CI 1.11–2.03; P for trend < 0.001) and CTI-WWI (HR = 1.32, 95% CI 1.00–1.74 and HR = 1.46, 95% CI 1.07–2.00; P for trend < 0.001). For CTI-BRI, only cluster 3 was significantly associated with higher CVD risk (HR = 1.48, 95% CI 1.11–2.00; P = 0.008).

Associations of cumulative CTI-derived indices with incident CVD

As shown in Supplementary Table S5, higher cumulative CTI-derived indices were associated with increased risk of incident CVD. In the fully adjusted model, cumulative CTI-WHtR was positively associated with CVD risk as a continuous variable (HR = 1.03, 95% CI 1.01–1.06, P = 0.015), and the highest quartile was associated with increased CVD risk (HR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.16–2.24; P for trend < 0.001). Similar associations were observed for cumulative CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI, with Q4 HRs of 1.78, 1.71, and 1.48, respectively, and significant trends across quartiles for all indices.

Component-outcome analyses for incident heart disease and stroke

To assess whether the associations were consistent across components of the composite CVD endpoint, we separately examined incident stroke and incident heart disease. In the fully adjusted model, the highest quartiles of CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI were associated with increased risk of incident stroke, with HRs of 2.58, 2.40, 2.56, and 2.21, respectively (Supplementary Table S6). Similar patterns were observed for incident heart disease, with corresponding HRs of 1.54, 1.43, 1.37, and 1.31, respectively (Supplementary Table S7). Significant trends across quartiles were observed for all indices in both component outcomes.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses showed that the positive associations between higher quartiles of CTI-derived adiposity indices and incident CVD were generally consistent across strata of age, sex, smoking status, drinking status, hypertension, diabetes, and dyslipidemia (Supplementary Table S8). The highest quartile was typically associated with increased CVD risk within most subgroups. No significant interactions were observed for CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, or CTI-WWI across the examined stratification variables, with all P values for interaction > 0.05.

Mediation analysis

As shown in Figure S3, mediation analyses suggested interrelationships between CTI and adiposity indices in relation to incident CVD. WHtR, BMI, and BRI partially mediated the association between CTI and CVD, with mediation proportions of 19.47%, 27.84%, and 24.76%, respectively. The corresponding indirect effects were 0.049 (95% CI, 0.030–0.073) for WHtR, 0.032 (95% CI, 0.011–0.055) for BMI, and 0.032 (95% CI, 0.012–0.053) for BRI. The indirect effect for WWI was not statistically significant (0.012; 95% CI, − 0.012 to 0.050). Reverse mediation analyses showed that CTI mediated 20.27%, 17.57%, 18.74%, and 25.02% of the associations of WHtR, BMI, BRI, and WWI with CVD, respectively.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses using sex-specific quartiles yielded results consistent with the main findings (Supplementary Table S9). In the fully adjusted model, the highest quartiles of CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI remained associated with increased CVD risk, with HRs of 1.46, 1.43, 1.40, and 1.36, respectively, and significant trends across quartiles for all indices.Complete-case analyses also showed similar results (Supplementary Table S10). The highest quartiles of CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI were associated with higher CVD risk, with HRs of 1.54, 1.54, 1.36, and 1.31, respectively. Consistent findings were further observed in logistic regression analyses (Supplementary Table S11).

Discussion

In this nationwide prospective cohort of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, higher levels of CTI-derived adiposity indices were consistently associated with an increased risk of incident CVD after adjustment for sociodemographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic factors. These associations were observed for CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, CTI-BRI, and CTI-WWI, and were further supported by restricted cubic spline analyses showing predominantly positive and largely linear dose–response relationships. Among the four indices, CTI-WHtR showed the most consistent association pattern and the most favorable, although still modest, discriminative performance. This finding may partly reflect the ability of WHtR to capture central adiposity relative to body size, a phenotype more closely linked than general obesity to insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and atherosclerotic progression [27–30]. Because CTI integrates metabolic and inflammatory information, its combination with WHtR may provide a concise summary of metabolic–inflammatory burden and central adiposity in relation to cardiovascular risk.

CTI-BMI and CTI-BRI were also associated with incident CVD, but their performance was not clearly superior to CTI-WHtR. Although BRI was designed to better characterize body roundness and abdominal fat distribution, its advantage over BMI was not evident in the present study, possibly because these measures share overlapping adiposity information when combined with CTI. CTI-WWI showed the least favorable overall pattern, which may reflect the broader body composition phenotype captured by WWI rather than a more specific abdominal adiposity signal [31]. In older adults, WWI may also reflect lower body weight related to reduced muscle mass, frailty, or age-related catabolic changes, which could reduce its specificity for visceral adiposity-related cardiovascular risk [32–34]. The strong correlations among the CTI-derived indices further indicate substantial shared variance across these measures. Taken together, these findings suggest that the differences among CTI-derived adiposity indices were mainly comparative in magnitude, with CTI-WHtR showing the most consistent overall performance in this cohort.

These findings are consistent with and extend previous evidence on CTI and related composite indicators. Prior CHARLS-based studies have reported that higher cumulative CTI levels and unfavorable CTI trajectories are associated with increased cardiovascular risk, with largely linear exposure–response relationships [35, 36]. The present study builds on this evidence by showing that combining CTI with adiposity-related measures, particularly central adiposity indicators, provides additional epidemiological information on cardiovascular risk. This observation is also consistent with studies of cerebrovascular outcomes, in which CTI-derived anthropometric indices showed better discrimination than CTI alone, with waist-related indices generally performing most favorably [37]. More broadly, studies of TyG-related indices have similarly shown that combining insulin resistance surrogates with WHtR or other waist-based measures is associated with stronger associations or modestly better discrimination for cardiovascular outcomes than TyG alone [38–40].

An important methodological question is whether CTI-derived adiposity indices provide information beyond their individual components. In additional analyses comparing CTI alone, each adiposity indicator alone, CTI plus the corresponding adiposity indicator, and the respective composite index, the composite formulations showed more favorable AIC and C-index values than the corresponding dual-component models, particularly for CTI-WHtR, CTI-BMI, and CTI-BRI. Although the magnitude of these differences was limited, these results support the role of CTI-derived adiposity indices as parsimonious composite summaries of joint metabolic–inflammatory and adiposity-related risk. This interpretation is consistent with the overall objective of comparing related composite indices within a unified prospective analytic framework rather than establishing fundamentally distinct biological constructs.

Several biological mechanisms may plausibly underlie the observed associations. Insulin resistance, chronic low-grade inflammation, and visceral adiposity are closely interconnected processes that contribute to endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerotic progression, and cardiovascular events [41–44]. CTI-derived adiposity indices may therefore reflect the combined burden of metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation and adverse adiposity phenotypes more comprehensively than either domain alone. In line with this interpretation, the cumulative exposure and trajectory analyses showed that greater long-term burden and persistently higher patterns of CTI-derived indices were associated with increased CVD risk. These secondary analyses provide complementary longitudinal context, suggesting that the observed associations may reflect not only baseline metabolic–inflammatory and adiposity status, but also sustained exposure over time [45].

Mediation analyses further suggested a close interrelationship between adiposity and metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation. WHtR, BMI, and BRI partially mediated the association between CTI and incident CVD, whereas CTI also mediated the associations between adiposity indices and CVD. These bidirectional mediation patterns are consistent with the biological interdependence between adiposity, insulin resistance, and systemic inflammation. Prior prospective studies have shown that insulin resistance and other cardiometabolic risk factors partly explain the associations of general and central obesity with incident CVD [46–49]. These findings further support the relevance of considering adiposity-related phenotypes and metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation as interconnected domains in CVD development.

The component-outcome analyses provided additional support for the robustness of the findings. When incident heart disease and stroke were examined separately, higher CTI-derived adiposity indices remained associated with increased risk of both outcomes, suggesting that the associations observed for the composite CVD endpoint were not driven solely by one component. From a clinical and public health perspective, CTI-derived adiposity indices may serve as simple composite markers for characterizing the joint burden of metabolic–inflammatory dysregulation and adiposity. The required variables—fasting glucose, triglycerides, CRP, and basic anthropometric measurements—are inexpensive and routinely available in clinical and community-based settings. In the present study, these indices, particularly CTI-WHtR, showed more favorable comparative performance than CTI alone and provided reclassification improvement beyond conventional risk factors. Nevertheless, given their modest discriminative performance, they are better viewed as complementary markers for epidemiological risk characterization rather than stand-alone tools for clinical risk stratification.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, incident CVD was based on self-reported physician diagnosis rather than adjudicated clinical events, which may have introduced recall bias and outcome misclassification. Second, residual confounding remains possible despite adjustment for multiple sociodemographic, lifestyle, and cardiometabolic factors. Third, because the study population consisted of middle-aged and older Chinese adults, the generalizability of the findings to younger populations or other ethnic groups requires further evaluation. Fourth, the mediation analyses should be interpreted as descriptive and hypothesis-generating. Despite these limitations, the consistency of findings across primary, secondary, and sensitivity analyses supports the robustness of the observed associations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CTI-derived adiposity indices were associated with incident CVD in middle-aged and older adults, with CTI-WHtR showing the most consistent overall performance. These findings support the epidemiological relevance of integrating metabolic–inflammatory burden with adiposity-related phenotypes, particularly central adiposity, in cardiovascular risk assessment.
