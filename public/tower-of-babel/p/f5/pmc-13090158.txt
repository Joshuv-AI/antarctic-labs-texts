FGFR1 Promotes Malignant Progression in Lung Squamous Cell Carcinoma Through Activation of Wnt/β‐Catenin Signaling
Authors: Xingyuan Li, Shuya Mu, Yinjia Sun, Yongfeng Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThis study aims to elucidate the role of FGFR1 in activating the Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway and the underlying mechanisms by which it promotes malignant progression in lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC). By integrating multi‐omics analysis with functional experiments, the clinical heterogeneity of FGFR1 amplification, signaling crosstalk, and their regulatory networks governing tumor phenotypes were revealed.MethodsUsing TCGA data (n = 490), we analyzed the relationship between FGFR1 copy number variation (CNV) and mRNA expression in LUSC, and validated the correlation with protein expression in a clinical cohort (n = 38). GSEA and single‐gene GSEA were performed to identify signaling pathways associated with high FGFR1 expression. The interaction between FGFR1 and the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway was investigated by immunohistochemistry, immunofluorescence, stable cell lines, Western blot, qPCR, and functional assays.ResultsFGFR1 amplification correlated with increased mRNA and protein expression. The top 25% FGFR1 high‐expression group enriched Wnt/β‐catenin, PI3K‐Akt, and cAMP pathways. Mechanistically, FGFR1 promoted β‐catenin nuclear accumulation and enhanced β‐catenin signaling through PKA‐associated phosphorylation and Akt/GSK3β‐related regulation of β‐catenin stability, and these effects were attenuated by AKT inhibition. CTNNB1 knockdown significantly inhibited proliferation, migration, invasion, and tumor growth of LUSC cells.ConclusionsOur findings indicate that FGFR1 activates Wnt/β‐catenin signaling through coordinated regulation of β‐catenin phosphorylation, stability, and subcellular localization, thereby promoting malignant progression in LUSC. These results provide a rationale for targeting the FGFR1‐Wnt/β‐catenin axis as a potential therapeutic strategy.Keywords: epithelial–mesenchymal transition, FGFR1, lung squamous carcinoma, targeted therapy, Wnt/β‐catenin pathway

Introduction

Lung cancer, the leading cause of cancer incidence and mortality worldwide, consists of small cell lung cancer (SCLC) and non‐small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) [1]. NSCLC includes adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), and large cell carcinoma [2]. Although significant advancements have been made in treating NSCLC, especially with immune checkpoint inhibitors and targeted therapies, LUSC presents challenges due to fewer high‐frequency driver mutations. In previously untreated patients with advanced LUSC, long‐term follow‐up from the KEYNOTE‐407 trial demonstrated a median overall survival (OS) of 17.2 months with pembrolizumab plus chemotherapy compared with 11.6 months with chemotherapy alone, with 5‐year OS rates of 18.4% and 9.7%, respectively [3]. In contrast, non‐squamous NSCLC with mutations can achieve a median OS of over 3 years with targeted therapies like tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) [4, 5]. This gap underscores the urgent need to further explore the molecular mechanisms underlying LUSC and to identify novel therapeutic targets.

The fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) family includes FGFR1 to FGFR4, each containing a receptor tyrosine kinase structure with an extracellular ligand‐binding domain, a transmembrane helix, and an intracellular tyrosine kinase domain [6]. The 22 fibroblast growth factors (FGFs) activate FGFRs, triggering key downstream pathways like RAS–RAF‐MAPK, PI3K‐AKT, STAT, and PLCγ, which regulate cell proliferation, survival, and microenvironment remodeling [7]. Aberrant FGFR signaling drives tumor cell proliferation, survival, drug resistance, as well as angiogenesis and immune evasion within the tumor microenvironment (TME) [8]. FGFR1 gene amplification, found in 12%–20% of squamous lung carcinoma cases, is associated with poorer prognosis [9, 10]. Despite this, clinical trials, including LUME‐Lung‐1 and a phase II trial, showed limited benefits for FGFR‐targeted therapies like nintedanib and dovitinib in LUSC patients [11, 12].

The Wnt/β‐catenin signaling pathway is essential for various cellular processes, including proliferation, differentiation, apoptosis, migration, invasion, and tissue homeostasis [13, 14]. Increasing evidence shows its involvement in the initiation and progression of cancers, including solid tumors and hematologic malignancies [15]. Aberrant Wnt/β‐catenin signaling promotes tumor stem cell renewal, proliferation, and epithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) [16]. This pathway can be activated by mutations in key components like β‐catenin and APC, dysregulation of Wnt ligand and receptor expression, and epigenetic modifications such as DNA methylation and histone alterations [17, 18].

In our previous research, we found that FGFR1 gene amplification occurs in 17% of the LUSC population in China [19]. Subsequent investigations demonstrated that aberrant FGFR1 signaling promotes tumor invasiveness and metastatic progression by sustaining cancer stemness and inducing EMT, as evidenced in orthotopic tumor models and in vivo metastasis assays [20, 21]. Transcriptomic analysis showed significant enrichment of Wnt pathway genes in FGFR1‐high‐expressing LUSC, and in vitro experiments confirmed that FGFR1 overexpression activates β‐catenin and its downstream targets, including c‐Myc and Cyclin D1, whereas silencing FGFR1 inhibits their expression [22]. However, the precise molecular mechanism by which FGFR1 regulates β‐catenin stability and activity, and how this contributes to LUSC proliferation and metastatic dissemination, remains unclear. Therefore, this study aims to elucidate the molecular basis of the FGFR1–Wnt/β‐catenin axis in LUSC and to further define its role in tumor progression and metastasis, thereby providing potential therapeutic insights.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture and Reagents

Cell lines (H1703, H520, EBC‐1, SK‐MES‐1, H2170, and HEK293T) were purchased from Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology and cultured according to the manufacturer's guidelines. According to the supplier's quality control documentation, the cell lines were authenticated and confirmed to be free from mycoplasma contamination before use. bFGF (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), FGFR1 inhibitor AZD4547 (Selleck, China), and Akt inhibitor MK‐2206 (Selleck, China) were stored at −20°C.

Lentiviral Transfection and Stable Cell Line Construction

Lentiviruses carrying FGFR1 and CTNNB1 shRNA, FGFR1 overexpression plasmids, and control vectors were obtained from Shanghai Zuorun Biotechnology. Stable LUSC cell lines were constructed as per the manufacturer's instructions.

Western Blot

Cells were lysed using RIPA buffer and then mixed with sample buffer. After separation by SDS‐PAGE, proteins were transferred to a PVDF membrane. The primary antibodies used included Akt (pan) (4691; CST), Phospho‐Akt (Ser473) (4060; CST), GSK3β (67329‐1‐Ig; Proteintech), Phospho‐GSK‐3β (Ser9) (5558; CST), c‐Myc (18583; CST), Cyclin D1 (55506; CST), β‐catenin (ab32572; Abcam), Phospho‐β‐catenin (Ser675) (4176; CST), FGF Receptor 1 (9740; CST), non‐phospho (Active) β‐catenin (Ser45) (19807; CST), Phospho‐FGF Receptor 1 (Tyr653/654) (52928; CST), PRKACA (67491‐1‐Ig; Proteintech), E‐Cadherin (3195; CST), N‐Cadherin (13116; CST), Vimentin (5741; CST), Snail (3879; CST), GAPDH (5174; CST), and β‐actin (4970; CST).

RNA Extraction, cDNA Synthesis, and qPCR

RNA was extracted from the cells after lysis, and the samples were used for western blot analysis or cDNA synthesis and qPCR, as previously described [23]. The primers used for qPCR are listed in Table S1.

Immunofluorescence Assay

Cells were seeded into 6 cm confocal culture dishes (BS‐20‐GJM; Biosharp), and then treated with the corresponding reagents as described previously [20]. Immunofluorescence staining was performed, with the secondary antibody being donkey anti‐rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 488 (A21206; Thermo Fisher Scientific) and anti‐rabbit IgG conjugated to Alexa Fluor 647 (P0180; Beyotime). DAPI reagent (DAPI02; Xiaopeng) was added for nuclear staining. Stained cells were observed and imaged using a fluorescence microscope.

CCK8 Proliferation Assay

H1703 or EBC1 cells were cultured in 96‐well plates for 0, 24, 48, and 72 h. CCK8 reagent was added according to the manufacturer's instructions, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader for each well. The readings from all wells were recorded, and a proliferation curve reflecting the changes in cell proliferation over time was plotted.

Colony Formation Assay

Cells were cultured in 6‐well plates for 14 days to form visible colonies. The colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde solution, stained with crystal violet, and allowed to air dry. Colonies in each well were observed and photographed under a microscope. The number of colonies was counted using ImageJ software.

Scratch Assay

Cells that reached 90% confluence in 6‐well plates were scratched, and the scratch area was observed under a microscope at 0 and 24 h. The scratch healing rate was calculated using the formula: (0 h area – 24 h area)/0 h area, reflecting the degree of scratch healing.

Invasion Assay

5 × 104 cells were seeded in 1% FBS medium onto Matrigel‐coated Transwell chambers (356231; Corning), which were placed in 24‐well plates (3422; Corning). After cell attachment, 10% FBS medium was added to the lower chamber of the 24‐well plate. After 24 h, invading cells were stained according to the method described in Ref. [24]. Three random microscopic fields were selected for counting and photographing for each well.

In Vivo Subcutaneous Lung Cancer Model

The EBC‐1 cells (1 × 107 cells) were inoculated subcutaneously into the right axillary region of 6‐week‐old BALB/c nude mice. Tumor formation was observed after 8 days, and tumor volume was measured every 3 days thereafter. Tumor volume was calculated using the formula (L 2 × W)/2, where L represents the longest diameter and W represents the shortest diameter. Mice were euthanized when the tumor's longest diameter reached 17 mm, and the tumors were excised and photographed.

Analysis From TCGA Database

FGFR1 copy number and mRNA expression data from the LUSC cohort (n = 490) were downloaded from the cBioPortal platform (www.cbioportal.org). Differential gene analysis and GSEA were conducted using R Studio. Single‐gene GSEA was performed using GSEA software.

LUSC Tissue

The tumor samples were obtained from patients who underwent surgical resection at Shanghai Chest Hospital. All cases were pathologically diagnosed as LUSC. Next‐Generation Sequencing (NGS) technology was employed to conduct genetic testing on the tumor tissues.

Immunohistochemistry

All surgically resected LUSC tissue blocks were fixed, paraffin‐embedded, and sectioned at 10 μm. After dewaxing and antigen retrieval, immunostaining was performed with FGFR1 and β‐catenin antibodies (as previously described) [25]. Stained sections were imaged using a digital pathology scanner (Ocus, Grundium, Finland), and the average optical density was analyzed with ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

Data processing and figure generation were performed using Microsoft Excel (Version 2502, Build 16.0.16227.20202) and GraphPad Prism (Version 8.0.2). Homogeneity of variance was assessed using Levene's test before parametric analysis. Comparisons between two groups were performed using Student's t‐test. Comparisons among three or more groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Dunnett's multiple comparisons test. For datasets involving two independent variables, such as proliferation curves over time, two‐way ANOVA followed by Sidak's multiple comparisons test was used. Categorical variables were analyzed using chi‐square (χ 2) tests or Fisher's exact tests, as appropriate, Pearson's correlation analysis was used to assess the relationship between two continuous variables. A two‐sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with p‐values < 0.05, < 0.01, and < 0.001 indicated as *, **, and ***, respectively.

Results

FGFR1 High Expression Characteristically Activates the Wnt Signaling Pathway

To assess the correlation between FGFR1 copy number status and mRNA expression, 490 LUSC samples from cBioPortal were categorized into four groups based on the GISTIC 2.0 standard: amplification (14.1%), gain (41.5%), diploid (32.5%), and shallow deletion (9.5%). FGFR1 mRNA expression showed a significant stepwise difference across these groups (p < 0.001), with the amplification group exhibiting the highest expression level (Figure 1A). To further validate this association at the protein level, we collected surgical resection specimens from 50 LUSC patients at Shanghai Chest Hospital between May 2018 and February 2019. Among them, 25 patients had FGFR1 amplification, and 25 had no FGFR1 amplification. FGFR1 protein expression was successfully evaluated in 22 cases from the amplification group and 19 cases from the non‐amplification group. FGFR1 protein expression was significantly higher in the amplification group than in the non‐amplification group (Figure 1B).

mRNA expression data from TCGA‐LUSC were analyzed by dividing patients into an FGFR1 high‐expression group (top 25%, n = 125) and an FGFR1 low‐expression group (bottom 25%, n = 125). Differential expression analysis identified 271 DEGs between the two groups, including 101 upregulated and 170 downregulated genes in the FGFR1 high‐expression group (Figure S1A,B). GSEA further showed that FGFR1 high expression was significantly associated with enrichment of several oncogenic pathways, including Wnt/β‐catenin, PI3K‐AKT, cAMP, and Ras/MAPK signaling (Figure 1C). These results suggest that FGFR1 overexpression may promote LUSC malignancy through coordinated activation of multiple tumor‐related pathways. Notably, analysis at the single‐gene level further linked high FGFR1 expression to key Wnt pathway components, including WNT5A, FZD7, and LRP6 (Figure 1D).

FGFR1 Exhibits a Negative Correlation With β‐Catenin Expression at the Cell Membrane

In the LUSC cohort of the cBioPortal database, mutation frequencies of key components of the β‐catenin destruction complex were relatively low, including APC (5%), AXIN1 (2.4%), and AXIN2 (3%), whereas the mutation frequency of CTNNB1, which encodes β‐catenin, was only 1% (Figure S2A). In addition, no significant co‐mutation pattern was observed between FGFR1 and these genes (Figure S2B). These findings suggest that Wnt pathway dysregulation in LUSC may not primarily arise from genetic alterations in the destruction complex or in CTNNB1 itself, but may instead involve non‐genetic mechanisms, such as epigenetic or post‐transcriptional regulation.

A weak but significant positive correlation between FGFR1 and CTNNB1 mRNA expression was observed in TCGA‐LUSC samples (Figure 2A). When the cohort was further stratified into FGFR1 high‐expression (n = 251) and low‐expression (n = 251) groups, CTNNB1 expression was modestly but significantly higher in the FGFR1 high‐expression group, with a mean difference of 0.227 TPM (Figure 2B). These data suggest a transcriptional association between FGFR1 and CTNNB1 expression. Given the well‐recognized discrepancy between mRNA and protein abundance, together with the critical role of post‐translational modifications in controlling β‐catenin activity and stability, we further hypothesized that FGFR1 may regulate Wnt/β‐catenin signaling through both modulation of CTNNB1 expression and post‐translational control of β‐catenin stability.

To further assess the relationship between FGFR1 and β‐catenin protein localization, we analyzed LUSC samples stained for both FGFR1 and β‐catenin proteins, dividing them into high‐expression (n = 19) and low‐expression (n = 19) groups (Figure 2C). Cross‐tabulation analysis showed that 84.2% of the FGFR1 high‐expression group had low β‐catenin on the membrane, whereas 84.2% of the low‐expression group had high β‐catenin expression (Figure 2D). Correlation analysis revealed a significant negative association between FGFR1 expression and membranous β‐catenin levels (Figure 2E). These findings suggest that elevated FGFR1 expression is associated with reduced membrane localization of β‐catenin, raising the possibility that FGFR1 may facilitate β‐catenin redistribution from the membrane to other cellular compartments.

FGFR1‐PKA Axis Mediates β‐Catenin Nuclear Translocation

To investigate whether FGFR1 activation facilitates β‐catenin nuclear translocation, FGFR1 expression was first examined in 11 lung cancer cell lines. H226, H1703, EBC‐1, and H520 were identified as FGFR1‐high cell lines, whereas SK‐MES‐1 and H2170 showed relatively low FGFR1 expression (Figure S3A,B). Based on these results, H1703 and EBC‐1 cells were selected for subsequent experiments. Upon stimulation with 30 ng/mL bFGF, phosphorylation of FGFR1 at Tyr653/654 was markedly increased at 10 min, whereas total FGFR1 levels remained unchanged (Figure 3A). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that β‐catenin exhibited reduced membranous localization and enhanced nuclear accumulation after 1 h of bFGF stimulation (Figure 3B). Quantitative fluorescence intensity profiling demonstrated increased overlap between β‐catenin and DAPI signals in the nuclear region following bFGF treatment (Figure 3C). These results support the notion that activation of the bFGF‐FGFR1 axis facilitates β‐catenin nuclear translocation in LUSC cells.

To investigate the mechanism by which FGFR1 promotes β‐catenin nuclear translocation, we established stable FGFR1‐knockdown cell lines in the FGFR1‐high LUSC cell lines H1703 and EBC‐1. Efficient silencing of FGFR1 was confirmed by Western blotting and qPCR (Figure 3D,E), and shFGFR1‐3, which showed the highest knockdown efficiency, was selected for subsequent experiments. As the cAMP‐PKA pathway was significantly enriched in the FGFR1 high‐expression group, and PKA‐dependent phosphorylation of β‐catenin at Ser675 has been implicated in its nuclear translocation [26], we examined whether FGFR1 regulates β‐catenin through PKA‐associated signaling. Western blot analysis showed that FGFR1 knockdown significantly reduced p‐PKA (Thr197) levels without affecting total PKA expression. Consistently, p‐β‐catenin (Ser675) levels were also decreased following FGFR1 silencing (Figure 3F). These findings suggest that FGFR1 may promote β‐catenin signaling, at least in part, by enhancing PKA activation and subsequent β‐catenin Ser675 phosphorylation.

FGFR1‐Akt‐GSK3β Axis Mediates β‐Catenin Stability

To investigate whether FGFR1 regulates β‐catenin protein stability, we treated the FGFR1‐high LUSC cell lines H1703 and H520 with the FGFR1‐selective inhibitor AZD4547. After 24 h of treatment, Western blot analysis showed a significant decrease in phosphorylated FGFR1 (Tyr653/654). Notably, the levels of active (non‐phosphorylated) β‐catenin and its downstream target c‐Myc also decreased (Figure 4A), suggesting that FGFR1 activity is positively associated with β‐catenin stabilization and canonical Wnt/β‐catenin signaling output.

To further validate this finding, we established stable FGFR1‐overexpressing SK‐MES‐1 and H2170 cells using lentiviral transduction, and successful overexpression was confirmed by qPCR (Figure 4B). Western blot analysis showed that FGFR1 overexpression significantly increased active β‐catenin levels and upregulated the downstream Wnt/β‐catenin target proteins c‐Myc and Cyclin D1 (Figure 4C). Together, these results support a positive role for FGFR1 in maintaining β‐catenin stability and enhancing downstream Wnt/β‐catenin‐associated transcriptional activity.

GSEA further showed enrichment of the PI3K‐Akt pathway in FGFR1‐high LUSC samples. Consistent with this, FGFR1 knockdown significantly reduced Akt phosphorylation at Ser473 and decreased GSK3β phosphorylation at Ser9 (Figure 4D). These findings suggest that FGFR1 may modulate β‐catenin stability, at least in part, through PI3K‐Akt‐related signaling and altered GSK3β regulatory status. To further assess whether AKT signaling is involved in FGFR1‐mediated β‐catenin redistribution, immunofluorescence staining of β‐catenin was performed in control, FGFR1‐overexpressing, and FGFR1‐overexpressing cells treated with 1 μM AKT inhibitor MK‐2206 for 24 h. Compared with control cells, FGFR1 overexpression markedly increased nuclear accumulation of β‐catenin. In contrast, treatment with MK‐2206 significantly attenuated FGFR1‐induced β‐catenin nuclear localization, as evidenced by reduced nuclear β‐catenin fluorescence and decreased colocalization with DAPI (Figure 4E). These findings further support the notion that AKT activity is involved in FGFR1‐mediated β‐catenin stabilization and nuclear translocation.

β‐Catenin Knockdown Inhibits Invasion and Metastasis of LUSC Cells In Vitro

To assess the functional contribution of β‐catenin signaling to FGFR1‐driven LUSC progression, stable CTNNB1‐knockdown H1703 and EBC‐1 cells were generated. Efficient silencing of β‐catenin was confirmed by both qPCR and Western blotting, and shCTNNB1‐2, which exhibited the greatest knockdown efficiency, was selected for subsequent experiments (Figure 5A,B).

Transwell invasion assays demonstrated that CTNNB1 knockdown significantly reduced the number of invading cells in both H1703 and EBC‐1 compared with the control groups (p < 0.01). Consistently, wound‐healing assays showed that CTNNB1 knockdown markedly impaired wound closure in H1703 (p < 0.01) and EBC‐1 cells (p < 0.001) (Figure 5C,D). In addition, Western blot analysis showed that β‐catenin silencing increased E‐cadherin expression while decreasing N‐cadherin and Snail levels (Figure 5E). Collectively, these results support a pro‐tumorigenic role for β‐catenin in FGFR1‐high LUSC cells and suggest that β‐catenin promotes invasion and EMT‐related phenotypes, at least in part, through regulation of Snail.

FGFR1‐Wnt/β‐Catenin Axis Promotes LUSC Cell Proliferation In Vitro and In Vivo

CTNNB1 knockdown significantly inhibited the proliferation of H1703 and EBC‐1 cells, as shown by CCK‐8 assays over 72 h (Figure 6A). Consistently, colony formation assays demonstrated a marked reduction in clonogenic growth following CTNNB1 silencing, with colony numbers decreasing by 51.6% in H1703 cells and 49.3% in EBC‐1 cells relative to controls (both p < 0.001) (Figure 6B). These results indicate that β‐catenin depletion markedly impairs the proliferative and clonogenic capacities of LUSC cells.

To determine whether this effect could also be observed in vivo, a subcutaneous xenograft model was established in nude mice. Tumors derived from CTNNB1‐knockdown cells grew significantly more slowly than those in the control group (Figure 6C). On Day 17, tumor volume was significantly lower in the knockdown group than in the control group (p < 0.001), and the final tumor weight was also markedly reduced (p < 0.001) (Figure 6D,E). Together, these findings support a growth‐promoting role for β‐catenin signaling in LUSC both in vitro and in vivo.

To further explore the molecular basis of β‐catenin‐mediated proliferation, we examined the expression of key downstream effectors of canonical Wnt signaling. Western blot analysis showed that CTNNB1 knockdown significantly decreased the protein levels of c‐Myc and Cyclin D1 (Figure 6F). These findings suggest that β‐catenin may promote malignant proliferation in FGFR1‐high LUSC, at least in part, through regulation of the c‐Myc/Cyclin D1 axis.

Discussion

FGFR1 is considered a promising therapeutic target in LUSC, yet the benefit of currently available FGFR inhibitors remains limited [27, 28]. This discrepancy suggests that FGFR1 dependency in LUSC may not be fully captured by genomic amplification alone. Increasing evidence indicates that FGFR1 mRNA and protein expression may better reflect biological activity and therapeutic vulnerability than copy number status alone [29, 30]. Consistent with this concept, our transcriptomic analysis showed that high FGFR1 expression was associated with enrichment of several tumor‐promoting pathways, among which Wnt/β‐catenin signaling emerged as a prominent candidate. Importantly, unlike colorectal cancer and other Wnt‐driven malignancies, LUSC appears to harbor relatively infrequent mutations in core Wnt pathway genes such as APC, AXIN, and CTNNB1 [9, 31, 32]. In our cohort, CTNNB1 mutation was rare and showed no significant co‐occurrence with FGFR1 alteration, arguing against a mutation‐driven mechanism of Wnt activation in this disease context. Instead, these findings raise the possibility that β‐catenin signaling in LUSC is activated through non‐genetic mechanisms, potentially involving FGFR1‐related transcriptional and post‐translational regulation. This provides a mechanistic framework linking FGFR1 overexpression to aberrant Wnt/β‐catenin activity and may help explain the aggressive phenotype observed in FGFR1‐high tumors.

FGFR1 appears to contribute to β‐catenin dysregulation in LUSC at multiple levels [31, 33]. In the TCGA‐LUSC cohort, FGFR1 expression showed a weak but significant positive correlation with CTNNB1 mRNA expression, whereas immunohistochemical analysis revealed a significant negative association between FGFR1 and membranous β‐catenin in clinical samples. Moreover, bFGF stimulation promoted nuclear accumulation of β‐catenin in FGFR1‐high LUSC cells, supporting a role for FGFR1 in modulating β‐catenin localization. Given the established dual function of β‐catenin in cell adhesion and Wnt‐dependent transcription, this redistribution may have important functional consequences for tumor progression [34, 35].

Mechanistically, our transcriptomic analysis showed that high FGFR1 expression was associated with activation of both the cAMP‐PKA and PI3K‐AKT pathways. Given that PKA‐mediated phosphorylation of β‐catenin at Ser675 has been reported to promote its stability and nuclear accumulation [36, 37, 38], and that Akt‐mediated phosphorylation of GSK3β at Ser9 is generally linked to suppression of its kinase activity [39, 40], we further examined these signaling axes in FGFR1‐knockdown LUSC cells. Western blot analysis showed that FGFR1 silencing reduced p‐PKA (Thr197), p‐β‐catenin (Ser675), p‐Akt, and p‐GSK3β (Ser9) levels. These findings suggest that FGFR1 may enhance β‐catenin signaling, at least in part, through phosphorylation‐associated mechanisms involving both PKA‐ and Akt/GSK3β‐related pathways. However, further biochemical and rescue experiments will be required to establish the direct causal relationship and define the precise hierarchy among these downstream events.

The biological significance of this axis was supported by our CTNNB1 loss‐of‐function experiments. β‐catenin depletion significantly impaired proliferation in vitro and reduced tumor growth in vivo, accompanied by decreased expression of c‐Myc and Cyclin D1, indicating that β‐catenin contributes to the proliferative phenotype of FGFR1‐high LUSC cells. In parallel, CTNNB1 knockdown also markedly suppressed migration and invasion and was associated with increased E‐cadherin and decreased Snail and N‐cadherin expression, indicating partial reversal of the EMT phenotype. Notably, Vimentin expression did not change significantly after β‐catenin depletion, suggesting that FGFR1/β‐catenin‐driven plasticity in LUSC may not represent a complete epithelial‐to‐mesenchymal transition, but rather a partial or intermediate EMT state [41]. This observation is consistent with the current view that EMT is a dynamic process and that hybrid epithelial/mesenchymal states may contribute to tumor progression and treatment adaptation [42, 43, 44, 45].

From a translational perspective, the FGFR1‐Wnt/β‐catenin axis identified in this study should also be considered in the broader therapeutic landscape of LUSC. Compared with other molecularly defined NSCLC subtypes, precision treatment options remain relatively limited in LUSC, and immunotherapy‐based regimens have become a major therapeutic backbone [46, 47, 48]. However, durable benefit is still restricted to a subset of patients, highlighting the need for mechanism‐based biomarkers that better reflect tumor plasticity and treatment adaptability [3, 49]. In this context, the FGFR1‐Wnt/β‐catenin axis may represent a biologically relevant framework linking receptor tyrosine kinase signaling to EMT‐associated phenotypes and potentially to therapeutic resistance. In addition, the present findings provide a rationale for the future development of small‐molecule inhibitors targeting the FGFR1–Wnt/β‐catenin axis. More broadly, FGFR1‐high/β‐catenin‐active tumors may define a more treatment‐adaptive subgroup, supporting further exploration of rational combination strategies involving FGFR inhibition, β‐catenin/EMT‐directed approaches, and immune‐modulatory therapies in future studies.

In conclusion, our study supports a model in which aberrant FGFR1 activation promotes malignant progression in LUSC through coordinated regulation of β‐catenin signaling, including its expression, subcellular redistribution, and downstream transcriptional output (Figure 7). By integrating multi‐omics analyses with functional validation, this work expands the current understanding of how FGFR1 overexpression may contribute to Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activation in LUSC and provides a rationale for exploring combined therapeutic strategies targeting both FGFR1 and β‐catenin‐associated signaling.

Schematic model of the FGFR1–Wnt/β‐catenin axis in LUSC. Schematic diagram showing the proposed mechanism by which FGFR1 may enhance Wnt/β‐catenin signaling in LUSC. Following bFGF stimulation, activated FGFR1 may promote β‐catenin release from the E‐cadherin complex at the cell membrane, enhance PKA‐associated phosphorylation of β‐catenin at Ser675, and activate PI3K/Akt‐related signaling, which is associated with increased GSK3β Ser9 phosphorylation and reduced β‐catenin degradation. These events may contribute to β‐catenin stabilization and nuclear accumulation, leading to activation of TCF/LEF‐dependent transcription and the induction of proliferation‐ and EMT‐related programs.
