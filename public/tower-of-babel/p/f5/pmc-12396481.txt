Complexity of interstitial lung disease disproportionality signal analysis in pharmacovigilance databases
Authors: Romane Freppel, Alex Hlavaty, Imen Bari, Charles Khouri, Aurélie Grandvuillemin
Published: 2025

Zhu et al. reported a disproportionality analysis of interstitial lung disease (ILD) associated with novel antineoplastic agents indicated in breast cancer, using the FAERS and JADER databases.1 While the authors followed READUS recommendations for reporting their study,2 we believe methodological aspects warrant further discussion to strengthen the validity and interpretation of the results.

First, use of the narrow instead of broad SMQ “ILD” excludes several clinically relevant terms (especially acute respiratory distress syndrome and organising pneumonia), potentially missing signal of disproportionate reporting for some events, global time-to-onset and fatal cases estimates. Sensitivity analyses with both narrow and broad SMQs are essential to capture a more complete and clinically meaningful spectrum of ILD presentations.

Then, it appears that the authors used events rather than Individual Case Safety Reports (ICSRs) as the unit of analysis, although this is not clearly stated in the Methods section. Disproportionality analysis based on events seems not relevant—particularly for ILD–given several coded terms may relate to a single disease—e.g., “lung opacity” co-reported with “alveolar damage”. Considering each coded terms as a separate event would inflate the number of reports and may bias the disproportionality estimates, especially if the average number of terms per ICSR differs between the event of interest (e.g., ILD) and the background. Using ICSRs better reflects clinical cases. Moreover, in eTable 3, the total number of events “a + b + c + d” considered in the analyses does not remain constant across drugs, which obscures the definition of the data used by authors to generate signals of disproportionate reporting.

Third, data collected in pharmacovigilance databases, which are adverse event reporting data, do not permit the calculation of either hazard ratios (HRs) nor odds ratios (ORs). Indeed, only a variable proportion of adverse events are reported in these databases and the real number of exposed patients to these drugs is unknown. Only reporting odds ratios (RORs) can be estimated. Moreover, the use of HR using adverse event reporting data is for us highly problematic. The HR method is widely used to compare the risk of an event between two groups in time-to-event survival studies. The methods applicable to cohort studies are not applicable to data from pharmacovigilance databases. Indeed, the number of patients which initiated the drugs of interest is not available, the patients are not followed over time and frequent missing data on dates of drug initiation and adverse events impair a robust identification of time-to-events for analysis. The authors therefore wrongly considered the total number of adverse events reported for each drug as the number of patients at risk. Moreover, considering other drugs with variable safety profile and indications as comparator is hazardous and is not compatible with proportional hazards assumptions necessary to estimate HR. Therefore, we cannot support the interpretation of authors of their analysis in eFigure 2.

Finally, important clinical considerations are missing: bias due to known ILD overreporting in Japan,3 time-to onset not stratified by ILD subtype–variations among clinical presentations,4 and no mention of the Weber effect or treatment-line bias–novel agents often follow prior pneumotoxic drugs,5 possibly confounding the observed association with ILD.

In conclusion, to improve the robustness and clinical relevance of disproportionality analyses to explore drug-associated ILD in pharmacovigilance databases, we recommend the following methodological best practices:

-Assess potential diagnostic challenges and account for the heterogeneity of MedDRA coding.

-Clearly specify the unit of analysis (ICSR vs. event) and provide a rational for the choice, especially given that multiple coded terms may correspond to a single clinical case.

-Consider geographic variations in reporting practices and coding, which may introduce regional bias in signal detection.

-Consider time variations, indication and notoriety bias, particularly those related to the Weber effect characterized by a peak in adverse event reports shortly after a drug’s market introduction, followed by a gradual decline over time.

ILD is a serious and potentially life-threatening condition that is rarely observed during clinical trials, highlighting the critical role of pharmacovigilance and post-commercialisation studies for exploring iatrogenic causes of this pathology. Yet, relevant methods and cautious interpretation of the results are needed to detect robust safety signals and improve the knowledge of this disease.
