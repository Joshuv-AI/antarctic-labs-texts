Integrated summary data-based Mendelian randomization and colocalization analysis reveals TNNT3 as a target of Deer-Antler-Ginseng-formula for triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Jianling Jia, Yuan Ma, Di Wu, Yanhong Dou, Jingyi Yuan, Feng Liu, Aiping Shi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs an aggressive tumor type with poor prognosis, high recurrence and metastasis tendency, and lack of effective therapeutic targets, triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) not only poses a remarkable treatment challenge, but also necessitates continuous exploration of new therapy targets.MethodsWe used Summary data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) analysis, Colocalization (Coloc) analysis, and comprehensive gene expression analysis to probe deep into the genetic causal correlation between the therapeutic target genes of Deer-Antler-Ginseng-Formula (DAGF) and TNBC, and conducted animal experiments to detect the protein expression of the identified drug targets.ResultsFirst and foremost, 1471 drug-target genes of DAGF were retrieved from the TCMSP and BATMAN-TCM databases. Subsequent to the intersection with the differentially expressed genes of breast cancer in TCGA, 221 target genes of DAGF were selected for more comprehensive and systematic analysis. As evidently demonstrated by SMR analysis, 9 genes were genetically causally linked to TNBC. It is particularly noteworthy that 6 genes revealed a negative association with TNBC; 3 genes suggested a positive correlation with TNBC. Nonetheless, nothing more than SPTBN1, PTGFR, and TNNT3 passed the coloc analysis, which demonstrated a significant genetic association with TNBC. Subsequent gene-expression verification and animal experimental models demonstrated TNNT3 is the potential drug target of DAGF for TNBC.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates that DAGF functions as an inhibitor of tumor growth in TNBC xenograft models, with preliminary evidence identifying TNNT3 as a potential therapeutic target. Introduction

As a subtype of breast cancer characterized by the absence of estrogen receptors (ER), progesterone receptors (PR), and human epideral growth factor receptor-2 (HER-2), Triple negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for 10%–20% of all breast cancer cases [1] Aside from that, TNBC tends to occur in younger individuals, exhibits aggressive behavior with high rates of distant metastasis [2], and is bound up with a favorable prognosis and high mortality rate [3–6]. As a consequence of its distinct biological characteristics, TNBC presents inadequate reactivity toward endocrine or HER-2 targeted therapies. Current treatment approaches primarily encompass surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and molecular-targeted therapy. Nevertheless, the development of drug resistance constitutes a major hurdle during the treatment process. On that account, it raises a pressing necessity to identify novel genetic effector genes associated with TNBC and develop innovative therapeutic targets for its management.

Genome-wide association studies (GWAS) have successfully identified thousands of genetic variants associated with human diseases, many of which are represented by single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) [7]. Nonetheless, extensively characterization studies are rarely reported to probe deep into the specific genes corresponding to these variants and their roles in modulating disease traits. An expression quantitative trait loci (eQTL) refers to a chromosomal region where SNPs regulate gene expression levels [8]. Zhu et al. developed a summary-data-based Mendelian Randomization (SMR) method, which integrates GWAS and eQTL data to infer correlations between gene expression levels and specific diseases or genetic traits. By leveraging genomic research findings, this approach can effectively identify novel drug targets [9]. Public databases have conducted an indispensable role in drug target research on account of their advantages, such as ease of access, cost-effectiveness, and large sample sizes [10]. On top of that, Nelson et al. put forth a standpoint that drug targets supported by genetic evidence exhibit a more conspicuous inclination to succeed in clinical trials and gain FDA approval [11].

Distinguished as a traditional Chinese medicine, deer antler has been recognized for its anti-inflammatory and anticancer properties. As evidently demonstrated by our prior studies, deer antler acts on the NF-κB pathway, which presents center potential for inhibiting the migration and invasion of TNBC cells [12]. Developed on the basis of deer antler and incorporating the clinical expertise of renowned Chinese medicine scholars, the Deer Antler Formula (DAGF) functions as a standardized combination of eight traditional Chinese herbs: deer antler, ginseng, scutellaria, angelica, white peony, bupleurum, cyperus, and Fritillaria. Nonetheless, it remains unexplored and unproven with regard to the specific effects of DAGF on TNBC and its molecular targets.

For the time being, network pharmacology has been universally employed to delve into the mechanisms of action of traditional Chinese medicines in treating diseases [13]. Nevertheless, the proposed solution reveals fundamental flaws in its implementation, encompassing a deficiency of causal inference between drug targets identified through network pharmacology and disease pathology, as well as the absence of standardized protocols for data processing [14]. SMR analysis can address these shortcomings by providing robust causal evidence, thereby ensuring that the identified drug targets are more scientifically valid and rigorous. For this reason, this study integrates SMR and coloc analyses to dive into the role of DAGF in TNBC and to identify potential therapeutic targets.

Materials and methods

The flow chart of this study is suggested in Fig. 1.

The flow chart of this study.

Acquisition of drug targets

Above all, the drug targets of the DAGF were retrieved from the TCMSP database (https://old.tcmsp-e.com/tcmsp.php) and BATMAN-TCM (http://bionet.ncpsb.org). Additionally, 1217 cases of RNA-seq data for breast cancer tumor samples and normal tissue samples were acquired from the TCGA database (https://www.cancer.gov/). In RStudio (version 4.4), the data were read, merged, converted to gene names, and annotated. The “DESeq2” package was employed for gene differential expression analysis. In this study, genes with |log2 fold change|>2 and PFdr < 0.05 were regarded as differentially expressed genes, and the analysis results were visualized by adopting volcano plots. Ultimately, the intersection of the deer antler prescription-related genes and the differentially expressed gene results was selected as the target gene set for subsequent SMR analysis.

Acquisition of eQTL data and GWAS data for TNBC

Whole blood eqtl data (31,684 individuals) were obtained from the eQTLGen Consortium [15]. Breast tissue and whole blood eqtl data were retrieved from GTExV8 as supplementary data [16]; GWAS data for TNBC were obtained from the Breast Cancer Association Consortium (BCAC), consisting of 18,016 cases and 118,987 controls [17]. The eQTL and GWAS data originated solely from European cohorts. Given that all data originated from publicly accessible repositories, ethical approval was deemed inessential.

SMR analysis

The exposure data and outcome data SNPs were matched to ensure that the analysis was on the basis of the same SNPs; the 1000G reference panel was adopted to remove linkage disequilibrium; To ensure directional accuracy, effect alleles were matched in exposure and outcome datasets. The cis-eqtl within the 1000 kb range and the target gene set obtained above were selected and used together with the smr-1.3.1-win.exe analysis software to perform SMR analysis, retaining the significant results of the SMR analysis (P < 0.05). The HEIDI (Heterogeneity in Dependent Instruments) test was conducted to systematically assess the heterogeneity of the SNP effect. An insignificant HEIDI test result (P >0.01) ruled out significant pleiotropy of the instrumental variable, confirming SMR analysis validity [18].

Coloc analysis

In an effort to determine whether they share common genetic factors within a specific genomic region, colocalization (coloc) analysis serves to investigate two potentially related phenotypes. The underlying principle of colocalization analysis is to gauge how single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) correlate with each other in a particular region and each trait. An association summary for each trait and SNPs is generated by employing a vector composed of multiple 0 s or a single 1. For this reason, 1 represents the existence of a causal SNP (assuming that each trait has only one causal SNP). Afterwards, the posterior probabilities of all possible configurations are computed, along with the posterior probabilities of shared configurations between the traits. There are five distinct scenarios in total: H0: No significant genetic linkage was detected between the trait and the SNP within this genomic region; H1: Only trait 1 demonstrates statistically significant genetic correlation with SNP; H2: Only trait 2 shows significant genetic linkage to SNP; H3: Although both traits correlate with the SNP, the causal variables driving these associations are distinct; H4: Both traits are associated with SNP and share a causal variable [19]. In the present study, we deemed the standard of coloc is the PPH3 + PPH4 >0.6. The coloc analysis was conducted, and the results were visualized in RStudio (version 4.4) using the “coloc” R package.

Gene expression analysis

For the purpose of elevating the accuracy level of SMR and coloc analysis results at the gene expression level, we initially downloaded RNA data information of distinct types of breast cancer from the TCGA database. Afterwards, we systematically the core gene expression levels between normal groups and diverse types of breast cancer groups.

Experimental conditions of composition analysis of DAGF

High performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) analysis was conducted to identify the main chemical components of DAGF. HPLC was performed on LC- MS (Thermo, Vanquish, Q Exactive HF) equipped with a Zorbax Eclipse C18(1.8 μm*2.1 mm*100 mm). The mobile phase eluted with (A) 0.1% formic acid aqueous in water (B) and acetonitrile in gradient mode. The chromatographic conditions were set as follows: flow rate at 0.3 mL/min, column temperature maintained at 30 ℃, and sample injection volume of 2 µL.

Molecular docking

The 3D structure file of the target proteins TNNT3, SPTBN1 and PTGFR was retrieved from the PDB database. The protein structure was inspected by utilizing PyMOL software (version 2.3.0) to prepare it for docking. Ligand molecules were defined as active constituents identified via HPLC analysis with a content exceeding 1%. Their 3D structures were downloaded from the PubChem database and subsequently optimized by employing the MMFF94 force field within the OpenBabel software to obtain the minimum energy conformation. AutoDock Tools (version 1.5.6) was employed to add hydrogen atoms to the protein and to assign hydrogen atoms and rotatable bonds to the ligand molecules. The prepared protein and ligand were both saved in PDBQT format. The docking search space was defined by adopting the Grid module, with specific parameters for each protein detailed in Table 1. Docking simulations were performed by employing a semi-flexible approach with an exhaustiveness level of 25. The Lamarckian genetic algorithm (LGA) within AutoDock Vina (version 1.2.0) was executed to generate 20 distinct binding poses. The records were made of both the resulting binding free energies and docking conformations.

Cell culture

The 4T1-luc cell line was purchased from Zhongqiao Xinzhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). It was propagated in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. The cells were cultured in a constant temperature incubator at 37 °C and 5% CO2.

Tumor model construction and drug administration

Twenty-five female BABL/c mice (15–17 g, 6weeks) were purchased from Weitonglihua Experimental Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing). The experimental animal protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Translational Medicine, the First Hospital of Jilin University (Permit: 20240924-01). The mice were kept in an SPF barrier. In the wake of a one-week meticulous adaptation, 4T1-Luc cells (1 × 106/100ul) were injected into the mammary pad of the fourth pair of mammary glands of the mice. The animals underwent random allocation into five distinct groups [20], namely the Control group, DAGF dissimilar dose groups high-dose, medium-dose, and low-dose), Docetaxel group, and Docetaxel + medium-dose group. Rooted in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia and clinical experience, the daily dosage of DAGF for humans was determined by a renowned professor of Traditional Chinese Medicine. The daily human dosage of DAGF is 65 g [21] (comprising Panax ginseng 3 g, Deer antler 12 g, Angelica sinensis 10 g, Bupleurum chinense 5 g, Poria cocos 5 g, Cyperus rotundus 10 g, and Fritillaria thunbergii 10 g). On the basis of the body surface area conversion factor between mice and humans, the dosing concentration for mice was calculated in accordance with the 3rd edition of Xu Shuyun’s “Pharmacological Experimental Methodology”, which is 0.0026. Assuming a mouse body weight of 20 g, the mouse equivalent dose was calculated as follows: 65 × 0.0026/0.02 = 8.45 g/kg. The gavage volume administered to each mouse was 0.2 ml. Accordingly, the final dosing concentration for mice was determined as: (8.45 g/kg × 0.02 kg)/0.2 ml = 0.85 mg/ml. On this basis, the mouse dosing concentrations were divided into low, medium, and high dose groups, namely, 0.49 mg/ml, 0.85 mg/ml, and 1.70 mg/ml, separately. Docetaxel was injected intraperitoneally once a week, with each dose being 0.2 ml/10 mg/kg. In the docetaxel + medium-dose group, docetaxel was injected intraperitoneally at week 1 and week 4, and medium-dose DAGF (0.85 mg/ml) was administered by gavage every day for the rest of the time. Tumor volume (calculated as length × width 2 × 0.4) and weight were measured at 6-day intervals. The experimental endpoint was reached 30 days later, and D-luciferin (1.5 mg/10 g) was injected intraperitoneally. After 10 min, imaging was performed by adopting luciferase imaging (Xenogen IVIS-200). In the aftermath of the above steps, the mice underwent euthanasia, followed by immediate collection of tumor tissues, lung tissues, and blood for subsequent analysis.

Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) staining

The lung tissue and kidney of mice were immersed in 10% neutral formalin, dehydrated with ethanol, embedded in paraffin, and then sliced to a thickness of 3 mm. HE staining was performed to observe the metastasis of lung tumor tissue (The lung metastatic index (%) = Tumor metastatic Area/Total Lung Area × 100%) and the integrity of kidney tissue structure.

Western blot

In the wake of washing the tumor tissue, 100 mg was cut and added to 100 mg/ml RIPA lysis buffer. Subsequently, the tissue was broken into a homogenate using a homogenizer, lysed at 4 °C for 30 min, centrifuged at 15,000 rpm for 20 min. Afterwards, the supernatant was collected and the protein concentration was determined by employing a BCA protein concentration kit. Protein bands were resolved by SDS-PAGE and subsequently transferred onto a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Thereafter, they were incubated with primary antibodies at 4℃ overnight. The primary antibodies directed against TNNT3 (~ 32 kDa), SPTBN1 (~ 275 kDa), and GADPH (~ 36 kDa) were procured from Proteintech (Wuhan, China). In the aftermath of three washes, the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. In accordance with the protocol, an enhanced chemiluminescence western blot detection system was ultimately utilized to visualize the immunoblot.

Due to the wide variation in molecular weights of the proteins of interest, different gel sections were transferred selectively to PVDF membranes. This selective transfer was performed to optimize transfer efficiency, prevent interference from unrelated proteins, and ensure clear detection for each target protein. Only the portion of the gel corresponding to each target’s molecular weight range was transferred.

Construction of PPI network

Protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis was performed by referring to the online STRING database (version 12.0; http://string-db.org). An interaction score threshold of ≥ 0.7 was established to filter interactions with high confidence [22].

GO and KEGG analysis

KEGG pathway and GO enrichment analyses were performed by employing the “clusterProfiler” package in R software to functionally characterize PPI network genes. Module genes underwent similar analysis. Significantly enriched terms (Pfdr < 0.05) were visualized with “ggplot2” and “GOplot”.

Safety evaluation of DAGF in triple-negative breast cancer mice

In vivo safety assessments of DAGF included the analysis of biochemical parameters and hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining of renal tissues. Parameters linked with hepatorenal function covered aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), albumin (ALB), and creatinine (CREA). Evaluation of all parameters was performed at the pre-defined study termination points.

Statistical analysis

The experimental data underwent systematic analyses by employing Image J and GraphPad Prism9.5.1, and the statistical analysis results were plotted by utilizing GraphPad Prism9.5.1 software. All data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). One-way ANOVA is used for inter-group comparisons when data are normally distributed, whereas non-parametric tests are applied for non-normal data. A threshold of P < 0.05 indicates statistical significance, with lower values suggesting greater evidence against the null hypothesis.

Results

Drug target acquisition results

The drug target genes of Deer Horn Prescription were obtained from TCMSP and BATMAN-TCM. Subsequent to the removal of duplicate values, 1471 drug target genes were obtained (Supplementary Table S1). In this study, a total of 1217 breast cancer samples and 1217 normal samples were screened from the TCGA data set. Afterwards, 1809 breast cancer differential genes were obtained by employing “DESeq2” for gene differential expression analysis. Among them, there were 1132 up-regulated genes and 677 down-regulated genes (Fig. 2A). Subsequently, the differential genes of TCGA and the drug target genes of Deer Horn Prescription were intersected to obtain 221 intersection genes (Fig. 2B), which were the target gene set for subsequent SMR analysis.

In general, a binding energy of less than 0 kcal·mol⁻¹ demonstrates spontaneous binding between the receptor and ligand without requiring external energy. Animal experiment in vivo validation

In the aftermath of the above steps, we constructed a TNBC tumor-bearing mouse model to verify the above research findings. The drug administration flow chart of the animal experiment is demonstrated in Fig. 5A. Subsequent to the tumor formation, the tumor volume was detected at 6-day intervals, and its change trend is depicted in Fig. 5B. The in vitro images and weight of the tumor size are suggested in Fig. 5C, D. In contrast to the control group, the tumor size of the DAGF-L, DAGF-M, DAGF-H, DTX group, and DTX + DAGF-M group displayed a downturn inclination in turn. The above phenomenon suggests that DAGF has an inhibitory effect on the growth of TNBC mouse tumors. Among them, among the different doses of DAGF, DAGF-H possesses the most noticeable inhibitory effect on tumors. Nonetheless, because it exceeds the human safety dose specified in the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, we hold a steadfast viewpoint that DAGF-M functions as a safe and effective dose. Accordingly, a thorough comparison was merely conducted on the Control group, DAGF-M, DTX and DTX + DAGF-M in subsequent experiments. Tumor volume was immensely reduced in the DTX group in comparison with the DAGF-L group. More crucially, the DTX + DAF-M combination group exhibited the smallest tumor volume among all cohorts, which were significantly smaller than both DTX monotherapy and all DAF dose groups (P < 0.05, Fig. 5C). This striking abatement in tumor burden provides robust phenotypic evidence of enhanced therapeutic efficacy. As demonstrated by in-vivo imaging findings in Fig. 5E, only a small number of lung metastases were observed in the Control group, and no lung metastases were detected in the other groups. In a more detailed and comprehensive manner, the H&E staining results (Fig. 5F) clearly displayed the lung metastasis of tumors in different groupscontrol. In comparison with the control group, the lung m.tastasis area in the DAGF-M, DTX, and DTX + DAGF-M groups decreased successively, suggesting that DAGF can inhibit the lung metastasis of TNBC, and DTX + DAGF + M exhibits the most significant effect. This phenomenon evidences that DAGF can lessen the frequency of DTX use. Aside from that, the lung metastasis area was markedly reduced in the DTX group when contrasted with the DAGF-M group, though no statistically significant difference was observed.

Western blot results

The results of WB are revealed in Fig. 6A, B. In contrast to the Control group, TNNT3 exhibits the most remarkable expression level in the DAGF group (P < 0.0001). In particular, the expression of TNNT3 in the DTX group was also higher than that in the Control group (P < 0.01). This corresponds with the trend of SMR predicting that TNNT3 functions as a protective factor for TNBC. The above finding illustrates that TNNT3 constitutes a drug target gene for DAGF, and DAGF can increase the expression level of TNNT3. Nevertheless, the expression of SPTBN1, which is also anticipated to be a protective factor, underwent remarkable augment in the Control group, and reduction in the DAGF and DTX groups. This suggests that SPTNB1 may not be a protective factor for TNBC. This divergence likely stems from multilayered dynamic regulation. To put it another way, SMR captures lifelong protective effects (long-term genetic risk) through population variants, whereas WB mirrors short-term therapeutic responses in pre-existing tumors. Apart from that, the established mechanism of miR-145-mediated SPTBN1 down-regulation in pancreatic cancer suggests analogous upstream regulatory pathways in TNBC where DAGF bioactive components may suppress SPTBN1 expression [23]. Critically, emerging evidence demonstrates that IGF2BP3 recognizes m6A modifications on SPTBN1 pre-mRNA and recruits NUDT21 to proximal polyadenylation sites (PAS), thereby generating functionally distinct splice variants [24]. We propose that DAGF might modulate this m6A-dependent RNA processing to alter SPTBN1 expression and functionality. Collectively, these findings illuminate the sophisticated integration of temporal dynamics, post-transcriptional control, and RNA modifications governing SPTBN1 regulation in TNBC pathogenesis. Also, as demonstrated in Fig. 6B, a statistically significant difference (P < 0.05) was observed between DAGF-M and DTX groups, with substantially lower TNNT3 expression in the DTX cohort. These findings collectively indicate that DAGF exerts an independent effect in promoting TNNT3 up-regulation. In addition, down-regulated expression of TNNT3 and SPTBN1 proteins in the DTX group relative to DAGF-M by Western blot analysis (Fig. 6B). Altogether, these findings demonstrate reinforced anti-tumor activity of DTX treatment, potentially mediated through modulation of the TNNT3/SPTBN1 pathway.

GO and KEGG analysis result

By using R software, we performed GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses to further elucidate the functional roles of TNNT3 and its interacting genes. The GO analysis encompassed three categories: Biological Process (BP), Molecular Function (MF), and Cellular Component (CC). Results demonstrated significant enrichment for terms associated with muscle contractile activity in GO analysis (Figures S3A and S3B). KEGG pathway analysis illustrated substantial correlations with cytoskeletal organization in muscle cells (Figures S3C and S3D). As the above findings suggest, TNNT3 contributes to TNBC pathogenesis primarily through involvement in cytoskeletal formation and contractile machinery. This mechanistic insight may provide novel therapeutic avenues for TNBC treatment.

DAGF is safe during drug administration in mice

The findings derived from DAGF safety test are depicted in Fig. 6C–H. HE staining of kidney tissue revealed clearly intact glomerular structures with no evidence of metastatic tumor cells. Only ALT levels displayed marked changes with statistical significance. In contrast to the control group, ALT in the DTX group was significantly increased (P < 0.01), while no significant statistical differences were observed in the change levels of AST, ALB, ALP and CREA between dissimilar groups (P > 0.05). Hence, the experimental results substantiate that DAGF-M at 0.85 mg/ml is non-toxic to both liver and kidney tissues.

Discussion

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) displays substantial heterogeneity and resistance to endocrine and targeted therapies, thereby rendering its treatment tremendously challenging [25]. At the eQTL level, we employed SMR integrated with coloc analysis to identify drug targets within the deer antler prescription that are causally associated with TNBC. Subsequently, we validated the expression of these predicted drug target proteins in vivo using a tumor-bearing mouse model.

For the time being, systemic treatment options for TNBC remain limited [26]. Despite the fact that chemotherapy is an effective systemic treatment, it is often accompanied by severe side effects, encompassing fatigue, insomnia, liver and kidney damage, gastrointestinal mucosal injury, and bone marrow suppression [27]. In addition, only 20% of TNBC patients achieve a pathologic complete response (pCR) with neoadjuvant therapy, demonstrating that chemotherapy is effective for only a subset of TNBC patients [28]. Apart from that, the expression of multi-drug resistance proteins such as ABCC1/MRP1, ABCG2/BCRP, and ABCC11/MRP8 in TNBC contributes to chemotherapy resistance [29]. The latest progress in immune-based and small-molecule targeted approaches has yielded encouraging results for TNBC therapy. For instance, clinical trials such as KEYNOTE-173 [30] and KEYNOTE-522 [31] demonstrated that pembrolizumab combined with chemotherapy tremendously ameliorates the pCR rate and event-free survival in TNBC patients. PARP inhibitors, mTOR inhibitors, and CDK4/6 inhibitors have also exhibited varying degrees of therapeutic efficacy against TNBC [32]. Nevertheless, these therapies frequently entail severe side effects and substantial financial costs, imposing considerable economic strain on patients, families, and society at large.

In the face of the treatment challenges posed by TNBC, Chinese medicine has recently demonstrated significant anti-tumor effects in numerous studies [33–35]. Additionally, Chinese medicine offers a wide spectrum of advantages, encompassing low cost, simple production processes, easy accessibility, and reduced side effects in comparison with chemotherapy drugs [36]. The DAGF is formulated in accordance with the fundamental principles of traditional Chinese medicine and comprises eight herbal components: deer antler, ginseng, scutellaria, angelica, white peony root, bupleurum, cyperus rotundus, and Fritillaria thunbergii. In the classical Chinese medical text Compendium of Materia Medica (Bencao Gangmu), deer antler (HA) is described as a remedy that “nourishes yin and invigorates qi, enriches blood, improves immunity, and reduces and eliminates lumps on women’s breasts”. In particular, as previously demonstrated by our investigation, deer antler can inhibit the invasion and metastasis of TNBC cells in vitro [12]. Ginsenoside has also been suggested to induce G1 arrest in MDA-MB-231 cells, inhibit cell proliferation and migration, down-regulate cyclin D1 expression, as well as suppress the growth and metastasis of TNBC in BALB/c mice, with efficacy superior to that of paclitaxel [37]. These findings collectively support the anti-tumor effects of DAGF. Our findings from animal experiments provide further confirmation that DAGF inhibits the growth and metastasis of TNBC tumors. In particular, the combination of DAGF with docetaxel (DTX) displays strengthened efficacy in comparison with either treatment alone, while maintaining safety and avoiding toxic side effects. This combination tremendously lessens the frequency of chemotherapy drug administration, underscoring the high potential clinical value of DAGF in future applications. As published studies demonstrate, ginseng and antler-derived extracts (core components of DAGF) can modulate macrophage polarization, enhance T-cell activity, and suppress pathological angiogenesis [38–40], Docetaxel also influences cancer cells and other cells within the tumor microenvironment (TME) by regulating mechanisms such as immune system responses, angiogenesis, and metastasis [41]. The above findings illustrate that the combination of DAGF and DTX may be involved in immunomodulation within the TME.

This study employed a combination of SMR and coloc analyses to identify three drug target genes with potential causal associations with TNBC: SPTBN1, PTGFR, and TNNT3. It is paramount to mention that SPTBN1 and TNNT3 were identified as protective factors, functioning as tumor suppressor genes that inhibit the onset of breast cancer. On the contrary, PTGFR emerged as risk factors that promote TNBC development. As subsequent gene expression analyses demonstrated, merely SPTBN1 and TNNT3 exhibited expression patterns in normal and TNBC tissues consistent with the SMR predictions. Apart from that, Western blot (WB) analyses of mouse tumor tissues demonstrated that nothing more than TNNT3 displayed expression trends aligned with both the SMR and gene expression results.

As also renowned for troponin T type 3, TNNT3 encodes a fast skeletal troponin T protein [42]. Genome-wide association studies demonstrate that TNNT3 significantly correlates with mammographic density [43]; To be specific, fatty acids and ceramides collectively function as a regulator of TNNT3 alternative splicing by activating protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) [44]; TNNT3 modulates target gene transcription by binding to conserved sequences in promoters of p53-pathway genes (a mechanism relevant to age-related pathologies) [45]; TNNT3 directly interacts with the cytoskeletal protein vimentin [46]; TNNT3 is aberrantly up-regulated as a downstream effector of L23MRP in Wilms’ tumors [47]. As collectively suggested by the above findings, TNNT3 may contribute to TNBC tumor suppression through transcriptional regulation, cytoskeletal reorganization, and phosphatase-mediated signaling. TNNT3 acts as a novel target characterized by substantial ntapped potential but inherent translational challenges. Its core competence lies in its groundbreaking novelty and no prior studies have implicated TNNT3 in TNBC pathogenesis or explored its therapeutic relevance. This positions TNNT3 as a promising candidate f’r first-in-class therapy development, which potentially mitigates critical limitations of current targeted therapies and immunotherapies, particularly for resistant TNBC subtypes. In particular, the above effect is achieved by offering a mechanistically distinct intervention strategy that could enrich clinical therapy alternatives and simultaneously ameliorate survival outcomes. Nonetheless, this novelty concurrently presents substantial challenges: the complete absen’e of foundational mechanistic data necessitates de novo investigation into TNNT3’s oncogenic role (e.g., in proliferation, metastasis, or metabolic reprogramming), while its unknown expression profile and druggability in TNBC cells create significant barriers to drug development. On top of that, translating this target requires extensive validation, lead compound discovery, and clinical testing, an inherently longer and higher-risk pathway than optimizing existing targets. Our SMR and Coloc analysis provides the inaugural evidence supporting TNNT3’s causal association with TNBC, establishing a critical foundation for overcoming these challenges through future mechanistic and therapeutic exploration.

Altogether, this study makes an array of contributions. For one thing, it innovatively integrates SMR and coloc analyses to dig into the causal relationships between traditional Chinese medicine drug targets and TNBC. It is universally acknowledged that TCM formulas perform therapeutic effects through a diverse spectrum of active components acting synergistically on multiple targets and pathways. Notwithstanding the fact that conventional TCM target screening predominantly hinges significantly on network pharmacology, our study pioneers an integrated approach by employing SMR coupled with coloc analysis, which constitutes a brand new methodology not previously applied in this context. This strategy not only enables high-performing and cost-effective identification of drug targets, but also simultaneously establishes causal relationships between targets and TNBC at the genetic level. It is paramount to note that the coloc component employs fine-mapping to verify whether GWAS and eQTL signals share identical underlying causal variants, thereby eliminating residual pleiotropy inherent in SMR analyses. Within disease-relevant biological contexts driven by shared variants, this integrated framework tremendously strengthens testing reliability, lessens false positives, and ameliorates target specificity, which offers a statistically robust advancement for prioritizing mechanistically validated TCM targets. For another, both the therapeutic effects of the DAGF and the expression of SMR-predicted drug targets in vivo were sufficiently validated by establishing a tumor-bearing mouse model, thereby further demonstrating the scientific rigor and reliability of our research methodology. Nonetheless, this study is less satisfactory and fruitful in some aspects. For one thing, the GWAS data for eQTLs and TNBC derive predominantly from European-ancestry populations. On the basis of no more than a single ancestral group, findings may not generalize to populations with distinct genetic backgrounds, allele frequencies, and linkage disequilibrium (LD) patterns. More importantly, this population bias potentially constrains the broader applicability of the TNNT3 discovery. By using eQTL and GWAS data from non-European populations, follow-up studies should validate the association to mitigate this limitation. We further plan to employ refined fine-mapping approaches integrated with functional genomics data to identify causal variants or gene-level signals with potentially greater cross-population stability. For another, this study exclusively utilized eQTL data from whole blood and breast tissue. We failed to incorporate finer-resolution eQTL analyses of tumor-infiltrating immune cells or stromal compartments in our SMR and coloc analyses. For this reason, it is highly advisable for mechanistic researchers to prioritize the integration of single-cell eQTL data from these cellular subpopulations into SMR and coloc frameworks to delineate cell-type-specific regulatory mechanisms. On top of that, our innovatively established TNBC model established represents only specific molecular subtypes of human TNBC. Given the substantial heterogeneity inherent to TNBC, featured by striking variations in gene expression profiles, molecular features, and clinical behaviors across dissimilar subtypes, existing animal models cannot fully recapitulate this complexity. As a result, the extrapolation of our findings may be limited. Apart from that, genomic alterations occurring during in vitro tumor cell propagation and interspecies differences in drug metabolism are likely to exert certain influence on the translational relevance of observed drug responses. These discrepancies may restrict the direct generalizability and translational significance of our results across the entire TNBC patient population. On that account, future studies are encouraged to employ models that better recapitulate human disease pathology, such as patient-derived xenografts (PDX) or organoids, so as to comprehensively validate the therapeutic efficacy and mechanisms of DAGF observed. Additionally, our assessment of hepatorenal toxicity was grounded in nothing more than short-term preliminary monitoring, encompassing liver function parameters (ALT, AST, ALP, ALB), renal function parameter (CREA), and histopathological examination of kidney tissue (H&E staining). So far, we have not yet conducted long-term toxicological studies or evaluated the drug’s potential carcinogenicity, genotoxicity, cardiotoxicity, and reproductive toxicity. In subsequent endeavors, we will exert our utmost to perform systematic multi-organ toxicity assessments to comprehensively evaluate drug safety. Last but not least, it remains incompletely characterized with reference to the precise mechanism underlying DAGF-mediated regulation of TNNT3 expression. In the future, we will knockout or overexpress TNNT3 in different TNBC cell lines to test its role and function in tumors. RNA-seq technology will be employed to dig into the signaling pathways associated with TNNT3 and to demonstrate its impact on tumor phenotypic functions. Additionally, TNNT3 will be examined in clinical samples from TNBC patients to validate its clinical relevance.

Conclusion

To sum up, existing in vivo findings suggest that DAGF inhibits tumor growth in TNBC models. TNNT3 exhibits tight association with the occurrence and development of TNBC and the mechanism of action of DAGF.
