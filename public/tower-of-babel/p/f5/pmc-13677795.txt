Cannabis use, affect and emotion regulation in daily life: A 3‐week experience sampling method study in people who smoke cannabis almost daily
Authors: Ana Paula Gonçalves Donate, Janna Cousijn, Emese Kroon, Kristine Rømer Thomsen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsAlthough many people who use cannabis report mood‐enhancing effects, affective responses are thought to vary within and between individuals. This study examined the bidirectional associations between momentary affect and cannabis use and the potential moderating role of emotion regulation in this.DesignIntensive longitudinal observational study using experience sampling method (ESM).SettingDenmark.ParticipantsAdults in Denmark who use cannabis near daily (n = 113; Age: mean = 31.57 years, median = 29 years, standard deviation = 10.28, inter‐quartile range = 23–36; 60.2% male).Method and measurementsParticipants completed a baseline survey (substance use and emotion regulation difficulties) and 21 days of experience sampling (three assessments daily; affect and use). We used multilevel models to assess: (1) the associations between positive affect (PA) and negative affect (NA) and subsequent cannabis use, (2) whether cannabis use predicted changes in affect shortly after use (at the same assessment) and (3) whether cannabis use predicted changes in affect at a later moment (at the next assessment). Next, we assessed whether emotion regulation difficulties moderated associations tested in models 1 and 2.FindingsHigher PA predicted a greater likelihood of cannabis use before the next assessment [odds ratio (OR) = 1.05, 95% confidence interval (CI) = 1.01–1.09], and cannabis use predicted increased PA at the same assessment (b = 0.59, 95% CI = 0.27–0.92), but not at the next assessment. NA was not related to use or vice versa at group level. Emotion regulation difficulties did not statistically significantly moderate the associations between PA or NA and subsequent cannabis use; however, emotion regulation difficulties moderated short‐term effects of cannabis use on NA (b = −0.014, 95% CI = −0.027 to −0.002), with individuals with higher emotion regulation difficulties showing lower NA shortly after use compared with non‐use moments.ConclusionsWithin individuals who use cannabis near daily, relatively higher momentary positive affect, but not negative affect, appears to precede and follow cannabis use. Cannabis use appears to be associated with higher positive effect at the time of assessment but does not predict positive affect hours later. Individuals with greater emotion regulation difficulties may experience short‐term reductions in negative effect after cannabis use.

INTRODUCTION

Several theories argue that affect regulation, through positive reinforcement [enhancing positive affect (PA)] or negative reinforcement [relieving negative affect (NA)] [1], constitutes a central mechanism in the maintenance of cannabis use. Many individuals report using cannabis to cope with NA (e.g. sadness, regret and anxiety) or enhance PA (e.g. happy, satisfied and excited) [1, 2]. Enhancement of PA is common among recreational cannabis users [3], whereas coping motives are strongly associated with problematic cannabis use, psychological distress and perceived stress [3, 4, 5]. This association may be stronger among individuals with generally high NA and greater emotion regulation difficulties [1]. Given the substantial heterogeneity in individual cannabis use patterns and reasons for use, examining daily temporal dynamics between affect and cannabis use may help distinguish affective antecedents from affective consequences of use.

Because emotion regulation processes unfold dynamically over time, understanding their role in cannabis use requires approaches like experience sampling method (ESM), which captures within‐person variation in affect and behavior. Previous ESM studies have produced mixed findings regarding affective antecedents of cannabis use. In one study, higher momentary PA—operationalized as happy mood—predicted a greater likelihood of cannabis use later in the day [6]. Similarly, another study found that higher PA predicted stronger intentions to use cannabis at the next assessment among university students [7]. However, a study of young medical outpatients found no association between PA and time periods preceding cannabis use. Instead, NA was elevated during the 24 hours before use, although this association attenuated when examining only the moment immediately before use [8]. Other studies have reported inconsistent associations between NA‐related states and subsequent cannabis use. An early ESM study found that state anxiety neither predicted subsequent cannabis use nor changed after use in university students [9]. Similarly, another study found that higher depressed mood predicted lower cannabis use, whereas anxious mood showed no association with use in young adults [6]. More recently, lower, rather than higher, NA was associated with stronger intentions to use cannabis at the next assessment among university students [7]. These discrepancies may reflect differences in key design features, including sample characteristics (e.g. age, community versus clinical population, level of cannabis use), operationalization of affect, outcome definitions (e.g. use versus intention to use) and temporal windows (proximal versus distal).

Beyond affective antecedents, ESM studies have examined the affective consequences of cannabis use. Among university students, more recent cannabis use was associated with higher PA and lower NA, consistent with short‐term reinforcement processes [7]. Another ESM study found that PA was higher at cannabis use moments compared to non‐use moments, while NA varied by social context, being higher when using alone and lower when using with others [10]. In adolescents recently discharged from psychiatric inpatient care, cannabis use was associated with higher PA and lower anger/irritability, but was not associated with NA, including sadness, guilt, hopelessness and shame [11].

Overall, existing ESM evidence suggests that PA tends to predict cannabis use or the intention to use and to increase following use, particularly in student samples. Findings regarding NA‐use associations are more mixed and may depend on individual or contextual factors. Emotion regulation difficulties may partly explain this heterogeneity. Emotion regulation refers to processes through which individuals influence the experience and expression of emotions and difficulties in these processes are a key risk factor for substance use disorders [12]. Consistent with this, Cavalli and Cservenka [13] found that stressful life events and perceived stress were more strongly associated with problematic cannabis use among individuals with greater difficulties in emotion regulation difficulties, and that greater emotion regulation difficulties were associated with more severe problematic cannabis use, with associations differing by sex [14].

The present study examined within‐person daily associations between affect (PA/NA) and cannabis use and tested whether emotion regulation difficulties moderated these associations among individuals who use cannabis near daily (see pre‐registration: https://osf.io/c74ur). We assessed whether PA/NA influenced the subsequent likelihood of using cannabis, and whether cannabis use influenced PA/NA shortly after use and at a later moment during the day. We also tested the moderating role of trait‐level emotion regulation difficulties in these associations. Prior ESM studies have mainly examined student and lighter use samples, with limited generalizability to older and near‐daily users, for whom cannabis may be more embedded in everyday affective contexts. Although prior ESM findings provide stronger evidence for PA‐related use, theoretical accounts of negative reinforcement and emotion regulation provide a clear rationale for predicting NA‐driven use in this population [2]. Therefore, we expected higher NA to increase the likelihood of subsequent cannabis use and cannabis use to be associated with lower NA shortly after use. We further expected these associations to be stronger among individuals with greater emotion regulation difficulties. No directional hypotheses were preregistered for PA.

METHOD

Participants

Participants were recruited through online and offline strategies targeting adults who use cannabis frequently. Offline recruitment included flyers and posters distributed at the Copenhagen Cannabis Expo 2023, Aarhus University locations (e.g. study rooms, library and corridors) and in cannabis‐related community settings, including cannabis stores. Online recruitment included advertisements in cannabis‐related forums with moderator approval, email invitations to individuals who had consented to future cannabis‐related research and snowball dissemination through cannabis community networks in Denmark. A study website provided information on eligibility, procedures and contact details. Further information on the cannabis‐specific context [15] in which the study was conducted is available in the Data S1.

Inclusion criteria were assessed using an online screener. Participants were required to be 18 to 65 years old, understand Danish, smoke cannabis at least three times per week during the last month, have a smartphone with a Danish phone number for SMS notifications and to have used no other illicit substances in the last month. After completing all baseline questionnaires (n = 191), participants were invited to take part in the ESM phase the following day, and 165 initiated ESM. For analysis, consistent with pre‐registration, we only included participants who reported cannabis use on at least 1 day in each protocol week (n = 113), ensuring within‐person variability in cannabis use across the ESM period.

Procedure

The study was registered with the Danish Data Protection Agency and approved by the Research Ethics Committee at Aarhus University (approval number: BSS‐2023‐051). Participants received study information, were informed that participation was voluntary and that they could withdraw at any time without consequences, and they had the opportunity to contact the researchers with questions and provided online informed consent. After eligibility confirmation, participants completed an online baseline questionnaire, including a video briefing on the ESM procedure.

The following day, participants began the 21‐day ESM period, with three daily assessments sent within 20 minutes of predefined anchor hours (11:00, 16:00 and 21:00). Prompts were scheduled from late morning to evening to capture a broad waking‐day window while avoiding early‐morning disruption. This sampling frequency and duration enabled repeated assessment of affective states and cannabis use events across weekdays and weekends, while minimizing participant burden to maximize compliance in near‐daily cannabis users. Surveys were intended to be completed within 60 minutes, but remained open thereafter. Data were collected from January to October 2024, using REDCap, hosted by Aarhus University [16]. Participants received 300 DKK (approximately 40 EUR) if they completed at least 80% of ESM surveys within 60 minutes of the prompt. No compensation was provided otherwise.

Measures

The baseline questionnaire included socio‐demographic data (age, gender, education and occupation) and substance use. Past month cannabis use was assessed via Timeline Followback; (TLFB) [17] and past month use of alcohol and nicotine with single items. The Alcohol Use Disorders Identification Test (AUDIT) [18] was used to assess alcohol consumption and related problems during the past 6 months. The Cannabis Use Disorders Identification Test‐Revised (CUDIT‐R) [19] was used to assess cannabis use and related problems during the past 6 months, with scores 8 or more indicating hazardous consumption [19]. In individuals who used nicotine daily, the Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence (FTND) [20] was used to assess nicotine dependence.

Furthermore, participants completed the Difficulties in Emotion Regulation Scale‐16 (DERS‐16) [21] to assess emotion regulation difficulties, with higher scores indicating greater difficulties.

Momentary affect assessments (ESM) included five PA items (happy, satisfied, excited, relaxed and proud) and five NA items (anxious, regret, angry, guilt and frustration) from the short version of the Positive and Negative Affect Schedule (PANAS). The items were rated on a scale from 1 (very little/not at all) to 5 (extremely much) in response to the question ‘Right now, I feel…[affect]?’ The PA and NA items were chosen to capture a broad range of valence and arousal properties, following and extending previous studies [2, 7]. Sum scores were created for the PA and NA sub‐scales separately, with total scores ranging from 5 to 25 and higher scores indicating more intense affect.

Momentary cannabis use assessments (ESM) included ‘Have you used cannabis today?’ (1 = yes; 0 = no). Participants who replied ‘yes’ reported when they last used cannabis. Based on this information, we created a momentary binary indicator of cannabis use (C t0; 1 = yes, 0 = no). This study is reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines; the completed STROBE checklist is provided in Supplementary Materials S2.

ANALYTIC METHOD

Pre‐processing

After exporting the data, coherence and ordering checks were performed on the timestamps (start, end and last cannabis use hour) to identify and correct any temporal inconsistencies [22]. When a participant completed an evening survey the following morning, or reported cannabis use in the morning survey, referring to consumption that occurred the previous evening, the use timestamp was adjusted to the corresponding date (i.e. previous day, to reflect the timing of the event). No major temporal inconsistencies were found.

Using the esmtools package [22] in RStudio (Version: 2025.09.2 + 418; 23), time‐series features were created using the lag/lead functions for PA/NA and minutes‐based cannabis use indicators (e.g. time since last use, time until next use).

Statistical modeling

To examine bidirectional relationships between cannabis use and affect, while modelling both within‐ and between‐person associations, we used multi‐level linear modeling (MLMs) with random intercepts to account for repeated assessments within participants. Models were estimated separately for each affect (PA and NA). All analyses were pre‐registered. In addition to pre‐registered analyses, two explorative analyses were conducted [model 3 (M3) and model 5 (M5)].

Within‐person predictors included affect (PA, NA), time until next use and time since last use, all derived from ESM surveys. Between‐person predictors included cannabis use days in the last month (derived from baseline TLFB) and DERS‐16 scores. Within‐person variables were person‐mean‐centered to estimate within‐person associations, whereas between‐person variables were grand‐mean‐centered [23]. Cannabis use days in the last month (baseline) were left‐skewed, but as reversing and transforming the scale did not improve the fit, we maintained the non‐transformed grand‐mean centered version for the final models. All time variables (e.g. minutes until next use and minutes since last use) were log‐transformed after centering to reduce skewness. All models were estimated using bobyqa optimizer, however, the M4 (i.e. PA) produced a convergence warning and was refitted using nloptwrap optimizer.

Model diagnostics indicated no major violations of assumptions, and missing data were assumed to be missing at random. For logistic multi‐level models, fixed effects were exponentiated and reported as ORs with 95% CIs. For linear multi‐level models, fixed‐effect and 95% CIs were estimated using cluster‐robust standard errors with the CR2 variance estimator, with Satterthwaite degrees of freedom. Model fit was summarized using Akaike information criterion, Bayesian information criterion and marginal/conditional R 2. Unadjusted P‐values were reported, given the exploratory nature of the study. All analyses were conducted in R Studio (Version 2025.09.2 + 418) [24] using the lme4 package [25].

Model specifications

M1 examined whether momentary affect at t0 (PA t0 or NA t0) predicted the likelihood of cannabis use at the subsequent assessment (t1, 1 = use, 0 = no use). We used logistic multi‐level models, including days of cannabis use in the last month (between‐person) and time until next use (within‐person) as covariates. See Table 1 for more details.

M2 examined whether cannabis use since the previous assessment (i.e. measured at the same assessment, t0; 1 = use, 0 = no use) was associated with momentary affect at t0 (t0; PA t0 or NA t0). We used linear multi‐level models, including pre‐use affect, measured at the previous assessment (t−1), days of cannabis use in the last month (between‐person) and time since last use (within‐person) as covariates.

Capturing potentially delayed effects, M3 examined whether cannabis use since the previous assessment (t0; 1 = use, 0 = no use) was associated with momentary affect at the next assessment (t1; PA t1 or NA t1). We used linear multi‐level models, including pre‐use affect measured at the previous assessment (t0), days of cannabis use in the last month (between‐person) and time since last use (within‐person) as covariates.

M4 examined whether individual differences in baseline emotion regulation difficulties moderated the association between momentary affect and subsequent cannabis use. To test this, we repeated the analysis described as M1, adding the interaction between momentary affect at t0 (PA t0 or NA t0) and baseline emotion regulation difficulties (between‐person; PA/NA × emotion regulation) as the predictor of interest.

M5 tested whether individual differences in baseline emotion regulation difficulties moderated the association between cannabis use and affect shortly after use. To test this, we repeated the analysis described as M2, adding the interaction between cannabis use (t0, 1 = yes, 0 = no use) and baseline emotion regulation difficulties (between‐person; PA/NA × emotion regulation) as the predictor of interest.

Statistical power

No formal a priori sample size calculation was conducted. The target sample size was initially based on feasibility and prior ESM studies with a similar design [7]. We conducted a simulation‐based post hoc power analysis using the simr package [26]. This approach estimates power from fitted mixed‐effect models while preserving key features of the observed ESM structure, including random intercepts, unequal numbers of observations across participants and model‐specific missingness. Simulated power for the observed fixed‐effects estimates was 69.4% for M1, 99.7% for M2, 47.6% for M4 and 96.9% for M5. Sensitivity analyses across pre‐specified ranges of assumed fixed‐effect coefficients selected separately for each model family and focal predictor, indicated that the study had limited power to detect smaller effects, particularly cross‐level interactions. For logistic models, assumed fixed‐effect coefficients ranged from 0.02 to 0.10 for the PA main effect (M1) and from −0.03 to −0.15 for the PA × emotion regulation difficulties interaction (M4). For linear models, assumed coefficients ranged from 0.20 to 1.00 for the cannabis use main effect on PA (M2) and from −0.005 to −0.030 for the cannabis use and emotion regulation difficulties interaction predicting NA (M5). These findings were used to guide interpretation of non‐significant or weak interaction effects, not as post hoc confirmation of the observed findings.

RESULTS

Sample characteristics (baseline)

A total of 113 people who use cannabis near daily were included (Table 2). Most of the participants (60.2%, n = 68) were male, and the mean age was 31.6 (SD = 10.4; range, 18–63). At baseline, participants used cannabis on an average of 27.06 days (SD = 6.72) in the last month, and the average CUDIT‐R score was 14.36 (SD = 5.57), indicating hazardous use [19]. The majority used alcohol (68.5%) and nicotine (75%) in the last month (Table 2).

Descriptive summary of momentary data

The analyzed data included 7119 valid observations provided by 113 participants. On average, participants completed 85.72% of the ESM surveys (SD = 15.69; median = 92.1%). Completion rates only varied slightly between assessment times: morning, 87% (SD = 16.35); afternoon, 85.54% (SD = 16.54); and evening, 84.61% (SD = 18.16) and across weekday (86.4%; SD = 14.94) and weekend (84.1%; SD = 19.52). Individual response rates ranged from 36.5% to 100%.

Across all ESM surveys, participants reported between 7 and 63 cannabis use events [mean (M) = 27.1, SD = 14.8; median = 24]), indicating between‐person variability in use frequency. Overall, cannabis use was reported in 43% of all events, corresponding to an average of 27 events of use during the 21 days assessment period (SD = 14.76). The median time since last cannabis use was 5.8 hours [interquartile (IQR) = 1.02–21.03], whereas the median time until next cannabis use was 11.06 hours (IQR = 4.23–23.08). Mean PA was 14.92 (SD between‐person = 4.33, SD within‐person = 2.90, ICC = 0.691) and mean NA was 6.46 (SD between‐person = 2.03, SD within‐person = 1.90, ICC = 0.534).

Associations between affect and subsequent cannabis use

Higher momentary PA (t0) was associated with a greater likelihood of cannabis use at the subsequent assessment (t1; OR = 1.05, 95% CI = 1.01–1.09, P = 0.018) (Table 3). In contrast, momentary NA (t0) did not influence the likelihood of cannabis use at t1 (OR = 1.01, 95% CI = 0.95–1.07, P = 0.808) (Table 3).

Associations between cannabis use and subsequent affect

Cannabis use (t0; use since previous assessment) was not significantly associated with higher PA (t1; b = −0.03, 95% CI = −0.38 to 0.32, P = 0.858), or NA (t1; b = −0.18, 95% CI = −0.38 to 0.02, P = 0.076), compared to non‐use events (Table 4).

The moderating role of emotion regulation in the association between momentary affect and subsequent cannabis use

The interaction between PA (t0) × emotion regulation difficulties was not significant (OR = 0.91, 95% CI = 0.81–1.01, P = 0.067) (Figure 1 and Table 5), indicating that emotion regulation difficulties did not moderate the association between PA (t0) and subsequent cannabis use. Similarly, the interaction with NA (t0) × emotion regulation difficulties was not significant (OR = 1.08, 95% CI = 0.98–1.20, P = 0.126), indicating that emotion regulation difficulties did not moderate NA antecedents of cannabis use.

DISCUSSION

This study examined within‐person, bidirectional associations between affect and cannabis use among individuals who use cannabis near daily. Contrary to our expectations, NA did not significantly predict the likelihood of subsequent cannabis use. However, higher PA was linked to increased likelihood of subsequent cannabis use, and PA was higher immediately after use than at moments with no recent use. Furthermore, baseline emotion regulation difficulties did not moderate the association between PA nor NA and subsequent cannabis use. In contrast, looking at NA, individuals with greater baseline emotion regulation difficulties reported lower NA after use relative to non‐use moments.

Affect and subsequent cannabis use

Contrary to our hypotheses, higher NA was not associated with a greater likelihood of subsequent cannabis use in this community sample of near daily users. This pattern is consistent with earlier ESM research in young adult samples showing null or inverse associations between NA and cannabis use [6, 7]. Furthermore, we observed unexpectedly low overall levels of NA and small moment‐to‐moment variability in NA (Figure 1), consistent with research suggesting relatively limited emotional variability among individuals who regularly smoke cannabis [27]. Our assessment frequency (three daily assessments) may have missed brief NA elevations commonly expected shortly before use (e.g. craving). Low levels and reduced moment‐to‐moment variability in NA can constrain the ability to detect NA‐use coupling in ESM data, particularly when NA elevations are brief or unfold over pre‐use windows rather than at the immediate preceding assessment, as observed in previous ESM studies [8, 28].

In this sample, higher PA was linked to an increased likelihood of subsequent cannabis use. This finding is consistent with previous ESM studies in non‐clinical samples [6, 7, 29], but may not generalize across populations, as ESM studies in young and clinical samples have reported null PA‐use associations [8]. This pattern suggests that near daily cannabis use observed in community samples may be primarily pleasure‐driven rather than triggered by negative reinforcement [29], similar to student samples. Higher momentary PA before use may reflect internal hedonic states or cue‐triggered motivation [30]. From a reinforcement‐learning perspective, repeated pairing of cannabis use with positive states may strengthen cue‐response associations, increasing the incentive salience of cannabis‐related cues and the likelihood of subsequent use [28, 31, 32]. Elevated PA may also signal contexts in which cannabis use is more available or socially reinforced, such as leisure time, social interaction, group identity and shared practices. These factors remain understudied and should be examined in future cross‐cultural research [8, 29].

Affect following cannabis use

Following cannabis use, we observed higher levels of PA shortly after use, but this effect did not extend to the next assessment of PA. Based on previous findings indicating transient, reduced NA following cannabis use [29] and theories of negative reinforcement and self‐medication [33], we expected lower NA following cannabis use compared with non‐use moments. However, results showed that cannabis use did not affect NA levels, possibly because of the combination of limited moment‐to‐moment variability and overall low levels of NA in this sample, as discussed above. Importantly, increases in PA and reductions in NA reflect partially distinct affective processes, and the presence of acute hedonic effects does not necessarily imply a parallel decrease in NA. Our findings align with previous studies that suggest that individuals can experience higher PA immediately after cannabis use [7, 34], consistent with short‐term pharmacological effects. One prior clinical study found elevated PA and reduced anger/irritability during cannabis use moments [11]. Because anger/irritability represents a specific facet of NA, such reductions may occur without producing a detectable change in a global NA scale, potentially explaining the absence of NA differences in the present study, where composite NA scores were used [11].

Moderating role of emotion regulation on the relationship of affect before cannabis use

Emotion regulation difficulties did not moderate the association between PA and NA at t0 and subsequent cannabis use. This suggests that participants with higher emotion regulation difficulties were not more likely than those with lower difficulties to use cannabis following momentary increases in either PA or NA. This pattern does not contradict reinforcement‐based theories of cannabis use [35, 36]. However, it suggests that emotion regulation difficulties, at least as captured by a baseline self‐report measure, may not be the most proximal factor explaining short‐term affect‐to‐use associations. One possible interpretation is that emotion regulation difficulties may operate through more distal motivational or expectancy‐based pathways, such as coping motives, craving or expectancies that cannabis will help regulate affective states. Prior studies have linked emotion regulation difficulties to coping‐oriented cannabis motives, and coping motives have been shown to connect emotion regulation difficulties with cannabis related problems in clinically vulnerable samples [37, 38]. Momentary craving and daily affect‐related motivational processes may also be more proximal to the decision to use cannabis than baseline emotion regulation difficulties alone [39, 40].

However, this null finding should be interpreted cautiously, given restricted NA variability, generally low NA levels, relatively low levels of baseline emotion regulation and simulation‐based evidence that the study had limited power to detect smaller effects in M4. Replication is needed in samples with higher and greater affect variability, potentially including individuals with comorbid psychiatric symptoms or stronger cannabis‐specific coping motives, craving or affect regulation expectancies, because these individuals are thought to be at higher risk of negative reinforcement‐driven cannabis use [35, 38, 39].

Moderating role of emotion regulation on the relationship of affect shortly after cannabis use (consequences)

Individuals with greater emotion regulation difficulties reported lower NA at use versus non‐use moments, whereas no such difference emerged among those with lower emotion regulation difficulties. This indicates that relief‐like reductions in NA following cannabis use emerged selectively among individuals with elevated emotion regulation difficulties, rather than as a general consequence of use. Importantly, this moderation effect was observed only for NA, and not for PA, indicating that emotion regulation difficulties shape the NA consequences of use, rather than its PA outcomes, which may explain why no main effect of cannabis use on NA was observed in the full sample. Unlike prior ESM studies that generally did not detect group‐level NA differences at use versus non‐use moments [8, 10, 11], our findings suggest that person‐level differences may obscure such associations when data are averaged across participants with heterogeneous emotion regulation profiles [41, 42, 43]. This interpretation is also consistent with the simulation‐based sensitivity analysis, which indicated high power for the observed cannabis use and emotion regulation difficulties affect predicting same‐assessment NA.

Together, these findings indicate two reinforcement processes: a positive‐reinforcement pathway reflected in PA involvement and a relief‐like NA pathway that emerges specifically among individuals with elevated emotion regulation difficulties. This person‐level heterogeneity aligns with reinforcement‐based models proposing that cannabis use may be maintained by distinct affective functions across individuals [1].

Clinical and applied implications

Across the full sample, cannabis use was consistently linked to PA, suggesting positive reinforcement represents a common affective pathway. Clinically, this finding indicates that cannabis use among individuals who use cannabis near‐daily may not be driven only by attempts to reduce negative affect, but also by short‐term increases in positive affect, pleasure, relaxation or reward‐related experiences. This distinction is important because interventions that focus exclusively on distress reduction may miss positively reinforced patterns of use.

At the same time, baseline emotion regulation difficulties did not differentiate participants affect‐short term use. This suggests that self‐reported emotion regulation difficulties alone may not be sufficient to identify who is more likely to use cannabis following momentary increases in PA and NA. In applied settings, it may, therefore, be more informative to assess proximal mechanisms, such as coping, cannabis‐related expectancies or function of use.

In contrast, individuals with higher emotion regulation difficulties showed selective NA‐related patterns, consistent with transdiagnostic mechanisms such as experiential avoidance, affective rigidity and reduced self‐regulatory capacity [44, 45]. This distinction may help explain why relief‐based use does not emerge uniformly across participants despite being reported in cross‐sectional studies and highlights the need to tailor interventions to individuals' affective profiles.

From a public health perspective, these micro‐level dynamics highlight the importance of targeting how vulnerability processes may accumulate and stabilize over time, contributing to persistent cannabis use. Short‐term positive reinforcement, repeated use in specific contexts and affect–behavior feedback loops may gradually stabilize into persistent patterns of use. Prevention and intervention efforts may, therefore, benefit from helping individuals identify when, why and under which affective or contextual conditions cannabis use occurs, rather than a single relief‐based pathway across all frequent users.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, social norms and legal factors surrounding cannabis in Denmark may have influenced reporting, potentially leading to under‐reporting of NA because of stigma and social desirability. Future ESM designs could incorporate passive or multi‐modal affective data, such as wearables monitoring physiological outcomes, to complement self‐report. Second, cannabis use often co‐occurs with alcohol and tobacco/nicotine, particularly when smoked in joints mixed with tobacco. Such co‐use may influence affective processes and should be assessed more precisely in future studies, capturing both between‐person differences and within‐person variation across the day. Third, the sample consisted of Danish community adults who used cannabis almost daily and were recruited through convenience sampling, limiting generalizability to less frequent users and other cultural contexts. Self‐selection bias may also have occurred, as individuals willing to join an ESM study on cannabis may differ from non‐participants in research interest or openness to self‐monitoring. Fourth, behavioral reactivity cannot be ruled out, as intensive self‐monitoring may have influenced participants' affect or cannabis use patterns [46]. Fifth, the lack of event‐level data on motives, potency, strains, routes of administration and perceived intoxication may have introduced heterogeneity in affective responses and reduced precision in estimates linking cannabis use to affect. Momentary motives would help distinguish episodes driven by coping, enhancement, habit or context. Sixth, methodological constraints include potential habituation, floor/ceiling effects, timing inaccuracies in cannabis‐use reports, unobserved missingness mechanisms and software constraints limiting survey closure. Finally, simulation‐based post hoc power analyses indicated that power varied across model families and effect sizes. Although some observed effects showed high simulated power, the study was less able to reliably detect smaller effects, particularly in M4. Accordingly, non‐significant findings should be interpreted cautiously and should not be taken as definitive evidence for the absence of an effect.

CONCLUSION

This study shows that cannabis use in daily life is closely tied to PA, both as a precursor and a short‐term outcome, among individuals who use cannabis near daily. In contrast, NA played a minimal role, suggesting that negative reinforcement is not the primary momentary driver of use in this population. Emotion regulation difficulties shaped these dynamics by strengthening use‐related reductions in NA. These findings suggest that cannabis may serve different affective functions across individuals: pleasure‐oriented use for some, and selective momentary relief among those with greater emotion regulation difficulties.
