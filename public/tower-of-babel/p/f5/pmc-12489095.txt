Introduction

The COVID-19 pandemic has had a profound and far-reaching impact on global health, resulting in significant morbidity and mortality across populations. As of July 2022, over six million deaths have been attributed to the virus1, with many survivors experiencing a range of long-term health issues, commonly referred to as post-COVID conditions or long COVID. These conditions can include neurological symptoms, chronic fatigue, and mental health disorders, which collectively contribute to a deterioration in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among affected individuals2.

Emerging evidence suggests that the burden of long COVID is substantial, with studies from the United Kingdom, United States, and South Asia consistently reporting declines in physical and mental well-being months after recovery from the acute phase3–5. For instance, a UK-based cohort study found that nearly one-third of individuals reported reduced HRQoL six months post-infection, with anxiety and depression being predominant concerns4. Similarly, a multicounty longitudinal study observed that individuals with mild initial symptoms experienced enduring fatigue and cognitive disturbances, further impacting social and occupational functioning3. These findings underscore that long COVID is not confined to severe or hospitalized cases and highlight the importance of assessing HRQoL in broader, non-clinical populations. The pandemic has not only strained healthcare systems but has also disrupted essential health services for both communicable and noncommunicable diseases, leading to a backlog of untreated conditions and exacerbating existing health disparities6. A recent study revealed that the overall HRQoL for more than one-third of participants across the globe was significantly affected by the pandemic, particularly in the domains of anxiety and depression7.

The rationale for this study stems from the urgent need to assess HRQoL in a community-based context, as existing literature has primarily concentrated on hospitalized patients. A systematic review identified a wide range of HRQoL issues associated with COVID-19, including symptoms such as fatigue, anxiety, and cognitive impairment that persist long after the acute phase of the illness2,8. These findings underscore the necessity of understanding how these long-term effects impact individuals in their daily lives outside clinical settings. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that factors such as age, gender, and educational status significantly influence HRQoL outcomes post-COVID-199. By focusing on a community-based sample rather than solely on hospitalized patients, this study seeks to provide a more comprehensive understanding of how COVID-19 has affected individuals in everyday settings10. A systematic review further noted a gap in understanding the persistence and predictors of long COVID symptoms, particularly among non-hospitalized individuals11.

Despite the increasing recognition of long COVID and its implications for HRQoL, there remains a significant gap in knowledge regarding the specific factors that contribute to health outcomes among non-hospitalized COVID-19 patients. Many existing studies have relied on generic measures or focused on clinical parameters, which may not fully capture the breadth of experiences faced by individuals recovering from COVID-19 in their communities12.

This multicentric community-based cross-sectional study aims to evaluate the long-term HRQoL among patients who have recovered from COVID-19 using the EuroQol 5-Dimensional 5-Level (EQ-5D-5L) tool. The EQ-5D-5L is a validated instrument widely used to assess HRQoL across diverse populations and settings, capturing dimensions such as mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. By examining various socio-demographic factors and treatment contexts within a community setting, this study will provide valuable insights into how COVID-19 continues to affect individuals long after their initial recovery.

This study aims assess HRQoL using EuroQol (EQ 5D 5L) among patients suffered from COVID-19 in community settings. The study examines the long-term health impacts on daily life, including non-clinical factors, within a community setting.

Methodology

Study design and participants

This study conducted a multicentric, community-based cross-sectional design to assess the long-term health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among patients who have recovered from COVID-19. The target population were individuals aged 18 years and older who have been diagnosed with COVID-19 and have completed their recovery as defined by the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria. Recovery was operationally defined as the resolution of major symptoms and completion of at least 4 weeks post-discharge or post-isolation, whichever was applicable, consistent with WHO guidance. Participants were recruited through systematic household sampling across selected wards in two cities (Delhi and Bhubaneswar), ensuring diversity in socioeconomic backgrounds and health statuses.

Sampling framework and sample size

A multistage sampling procedure was used to select the necessary number of houses in each of the two cities (Delhi and Bhubaneswar). Wards (12–16) were chosen at random from both Delhi and Bhubaneswar, encompassing all four directions of the city: east, west, north, and south. A sufficient number of localities were then chosen at random from each of the chosen wards. Household lists from municipal ward offices were used where available, and in their absence, the systematic sampling interval was derived based on estimated household counts. Finally, systematic sampling was used to choose roughly 30–40 households from each of the chosen localities. As a result, both Delhi and Bhubaneswar were adequately represented (Fig. 1).

Flow chart for sampling strategy for selecting study participants.

The cumulative percentage of COVID-19 infections for each state (www.covid19india.org) has been adjusted upwardly to account for under-reporting, for which there is evidence, in order to estimate the sample size for the cross-sectional study. A 95% confidence interval and suitable precision values were then used to determine the final sample size for each state, taking into account the proportion of COVID-19 cases. The formula incorporated a design effect of 1.5 to account for clustering and a 15% adjustment for anticipated non-response. The standard error margin was set at 5% with an assumed prevalence of 50% to maximize sample size stability. Equal sample sizes (n = 250 from each city) were used to ensure comparability and facilitate stratified analysis between the two diverse urban populations.

Data collection

A self-administered semi structured questionnaire has been used for capturing the socio demographic characteristics and questions related to COVID-19 treatment and recovery. Data collection was carried out between October and December 2023. A Computer Assisted Personal Interview tool (CAPI) was used to minimise enumerator errors in collection and data entry. CS Pro software was used to create the tool. The Euro QoL EQ 5D 5L questionnaire has been administered at a community level for both the cities. The EQ-5D-5L is a widely recognized generic patient-reported outcome measure (PROM) that evaluates HRQoL across five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression13. This tool is particularly relevant for assessing the long-term impacts of COVID-19 on patients’ quality of life. The collection process typically begins with recruiting a multicentric cohort of participants who have been infected with COVID-19. This can include both hospitalized patients and those treated as outpatients. The EQ-5D-5L instrument was administered in English in both Delhi and Bhubaneswar, with interviewers providing clarifications in Hindi or Odia as needed to ensure linguistic and cultural comprehension in line with EuroQol administration guidelines.

To ensure data quality, field investigators underwent intensive training, including mock interviews and inter-observer reliability checks. Supervisors conducted regular field spot-checks and daily back-checks on 10% of completed interviews. Real-time consistency checks were embedded in the CAPI software to flag missing or contradictory responses. The tools were pre-tested and refined before formal data collection began.

Study outcome

After receiving prior approval from the EuroQol Research Foundation, we employed the EQ-5D-5L tool13. Various studies have validated the reliability of the English version of the EQ-5D-5L across different Indian populations, demonstrating its effectiveness as a measure of health-related quality of life (HRQoL) for Hindi speakers14. The EQ-5D-5L consists of two main components: the EQ-5D-5L descriptive system, which outlines health states, and the EQ-VAS, which allows individuals to assess their health perception using a visual analog scale13.

The EQ-5D-5L encompasses five dimensions: mobility, self-care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression. Each dimension has five levels of severity: no problems (1), slight problems (2), moderate problems (3), severe problems (4), and extreme problems (5). Responses are combined to create a five-digit number that represents the respondent’s health status, ranging from 11,111 (indicating no problems in any dimension, or full health) to 55,555 (indicating extreme problems in all dimensions, or worst health). The EQ-5D-5L scores are then standardized to produce an index from 0 (representing death) to 1 (representing full health), with negative values reflecting health states worse than death15. The second component of the tool is a vertical visual scale that ranges from 0 to 100, where 100 represents the “Best imaginable health state” and 0 represents the “Worst imaginable health state.”15.

These health states were then converted into utility index values using the India-specific EQ-5D-5L value set, developed through a time trade-off (TTO) and discrete choice experiment (DCE) study published in 202316. The Indian value set assigns disutility weights to each level beyond ‘no problems’ (level 1) in each domain, reflecting the health preferences of the Indian population16. The final utility score was computed by subtracting the sum of disutility weights for each applicable level (i.e., level 2–5) from 1. For example, if a respondent reported a level 2 in self-care and level 4 in pain/discomfort, the corresponding disutility values for those levels were summed and deducted from 1. The model also includes an intercept and specific interaction penalties (such as for having at least one domain at level 4 or 5), which were incorporated as per the specifications of the Indian tariff model. This approach yields a utility score ranging from less than 0 (representing health states worse than death) to 1 (indicating full health).

This scoring system allows the transformation of qualitative self-reports into a quantitative index of HRQoL, enabling population-level comparisons and regression-based analysis of health outcomes.

Data management and analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using STATA v15.1 (Stata Corp LLC, College Station, TX, USA). Descriptive statistics of categorical variables were presented in frequency and percentage, while continuous variables were presented in mean ± SD. Measures of internal consistency showed that the Cronbach’s alpha of EQ-5D-5L for COVID-19 was 0.8922. Based on the correlation matrix, the EQ-5D-5L dimensions demonstrate good internal validity. Strong and statistically significant correlations among key domains—particularly between mobility, self-care, and usual activities (r > 0.78, p < 0.001)—indicate a coherent underlying construct related to physical functioning. Moderate correlations between physical and psychological domains (e.g., pain and anxiety/depression, r ≈ 0.61) further support the scale’s multidimensional structure without excessive overlap. These findings suggest that the EQ-5D-5L dimensions are internally consistent yet sufficiently distinct, reinforcing its validity as a comprehensive measure of health-related quality of life.

In the context of analysing long-term health-related quality of life (HRQoL) among patients infected with COVID-19, the Tobit model has been used as a robust statistical approach to address the challenges posed by censored data. This methodology is particularly relevant when the dependent variable—such as HRQoL scores—may be subject to upper or lower limits, resulting in incomplete observations. The Tobit model has been used for the analysis. The Tobit model accounts for the censoring of HRQoL data at both upper (1.0) and lower (0.0 or negative) bounds. It assumes an underlying latent variable and provides consistent estimates of regression coefficients even when a large proportion of values lie at the ceiling or floor of the scoring scale, which is common with EQ-5D data.

Variables that were statistically significant at the bivariate level of analysis (p < 0.05) were considered potential predictors of changes in EQ-5D-5L and EQ- Utility scores. Consequently, two multiple censored regression models were developed. To satisfy the regression assumptions, the skewness in the EQ-5D-5L scores was addressed through normalization (scores minus the minimum value divided by the range), resulting in a scale from zero to one, followed by a log10 transformation (with a skewness coefficient of − 0.76173). The homoscedasticity and normality of the data were confirmed through P–P plots and scatterplots. The absence of multicollinearity was verified by checking the variance inflation factor (VIF) values, all of which were below 5, indicating that this assumption was met. For the EQ-5D-5L scores, no significant interactions between the various exposures were found, except for the relationship between asthma and receiving the seasonal influenza vaccine. The model was deemed appropriate, with an adjusted R2 of 0.2242.

Ethical statement

The study was conducted after approval from the Institutional Ethics Clearance (IEC) committee of the Indian Institute of Public Health Delhi (IIPHD). Interviews were conducted after obtaining written consent and no personal identifiers were recorded. The data was archived at the IIPHD research data repository as per institutional norms. The study has been conducted in accordance with the guidelines of Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Table 1 summarizes the socio-demographic characteristics of the study participants from Delhi and Bhubaneswar, along with their EQ-5D-5L index scores, which measure health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The mean age of participants in Delhi was 40.55 years (SD = 12.63), while in Bhubaneswar, it was higher at 42.62 years (SD = 13.34). The overall mean age across both locations was 41.59 years (SD = 13.02). The EQ-5D-5L index scores indicated a mean of 0.89 (95% CI 0.87, 0.91) for Delhi, compared to a lower mean score of 0.66 (95% CI 0.62, 0.71) for Bhubaneswar. Higher HRQoL scores (0.89 for Delhi and 0.62 for Bhubaneswar) were associated with individuals not living in slums, with 92% of them in Delhi and 45.6% in Bhubaneswar. Conversely, those living in slums accounted for 8% in Delhi and 54.4% in Bhubaneswar, showing lower HRQoL scores (0.87 for Delhi; 0.70 for Bhubaneswar). Among male participants, who comprised 53.6% in Delhi and a higher percentage (76.4%) in Bhubaneswar, the average HRQoL score was 0.88 (95% CI 0.85, 0.91) for Delhi and significantly lower at 0.70 (95% CI 0.66, 0.75) for Bhubaneswar. Female participants represented 46.4% in Delhi and only 23.6% in Bhubaneswar, reporting higher HRQoL scores of 0.90 (95% CI 0.87, 0.93) in Delhi but a notably low score of 0.54 (95% CI 0.42, 0.67) in Bhubaneswar. The majority of participants were Hindu (87.6% in Delhi; 97.2% in Bhubaneswar), with EQ-5D-5L scores of 0.89 (95% CI 0.86, 0.91) for Delhi and lower at 0.67 (95% CI 0.63, 0.71) for Bhubaneswar. Muslim participants reported an index score of 0.91 (95% CI 0.86, 0.97) in Delhi but only 0.64 (95% CI − 0.05, 1.34) in Bhubaneswar. Regarding educational status, participants with ten or more years of education constituted the majority (83.6% in Delhi; 90.4% in Bhubaneswar), reporting the highest HRQoL scores (0.89 for Delhi; 0.68 for Bhubaneswar). Finally, a small percentage of participants had health insurance (2.4% in Delhi; 6.8% in Bhubaneswar), which correlated with lower HRQoL scores compared to those without insurance coverage. The average household monthly income was significantly higher in Delhi (INR 132,588) compared to Bhubaneswar (INR 46,104), correlating with higher EQ-5D-5L index scores (0.89, 95% CI 0.87, 0.91 for Delhi; compared to 0.66, 95% CI 0.62, 0.71 for Bhubaneswar).

Table 2 presents the hospitalization and vaccination status of the study participants from Delhi and Bhubaneswar, along with their corresponding EQ-5D-5L index scores, which measure health-related quality of life (HRQoL). The vast majority of participants were vaccinated, with 98.00% in Delhi and 97.60% in Bhubaneswar, totalling 97.80% across both locations. The data from both cities were pooled in the regression model, as identical study designs, instruments, and training protocols were used across sites. Moreover, preliminary tests showed no significant interaction effects between city and key predictors, validating the use of a combined analysis. Table 4 represents the Censored regression for the associated factors and the health states among the study participants. The results of the censored regression analysis reveal several significant associations between participant characteristics and health states among the study participants. Age was negatively correlated with health states, showing a crude coefficient of − 0.003 (95% CI − 0.005, 0.001) with a significant p value of 0.002, although the adjusted coefficient was − 0.002 (95% CI − 0.005, 0.001), indicating a loss of significance when controlling for other factors. Gender did not demonstrate significant differences in health states, as both males and females had coefficients near zero and high p values (p = 0.397 for males; p = 0.954 for females). In terms of religion, while Muslims had a crude coefficient of 0.077 (95% CI − 0.053, 0.208) with a p value close to significance (p = 0.4892), the adjusted coefficient was less informative at 0.055 (95% CI − 0.101, 0.212). Educational status showed more pronounced effects; individuals with less than 10 years of schooling had a crude coefficient of − 0.193 (95% CI − 0.388, 0.001) and an adjusted coefficient of − 0.289 (95% CI − 0.544, − 0.035), both statistically significant, indicating that lower educational attainment is associated with poorer health states. Regarding occupation, professionals served as the reference group, with significant negative coefficients observed in the “Other” category (− 0.094) and “Clerical” occupations (− 0.062), while agricultural and service workers showed negligible effects. Marital status indicated that currently married individuals had a crude coefficient of -0.084 (p = 0.0184), suggesting that marital status may negatively influence health states compared to never married individuals. The availability of health insurance emerged as a critical factor; those with insurance had a crude coefficient of -0.307 (p < 0.001) and an adjusted coefficient of − 0.233 (p = 0.002), indicating that lack of insurance is strongly associated with worse health outcomes. Vaccination status did not show significant effects on health states. Finally, participants treated at home exhibited significantly better outcomes compared to those admitted to hospitals or self-medicated, highlighting the importance of treatment setting in influencing health states among participants.

Discussion

Our study findings emphasize the significance of demographic and socioeconomic characteristics, particularly age, educational status, marital status, health insurance, and treatment settings. Each of these factors plays a crucial role in shaping the HRQoL of individuals recovering from COVID-19.

The negative correlation between age and HRQoL observed in our study is consistent with a substantial body of literature indicating that older adults are disproportionately affected by the long-term consequences of COVID-19. The crude coefficient of − 0.003 (p = 0.002) suggests that as age increases, individuals experience a decline in their perceived health states. This finding aligns with research indicating that older patients are more likely to experience long COVID symptoms, such as fatigue and cognitive impairment, which can significantly impact their quality of life9,17. Recent studies from India have similarly reported that older adults are at increased risk for prolonged COVID-related symptoms, functional decline, and poorer post-recovery quality of life, particularly among those with comorbidities18. The diminished significance in the adjusted model may suggest that other factors such as educational attainment or socioeconomic status may mediate this relationship, warranting further investigation into how these variables interact.

Our analysis revealed a strong association between lower educational attainment and poorer HRQoL, with individuals having less than 10 years of schooling reporting significantly worse health states (crude coefficient: − 0.193; adjusted coefficient: − 0.289). This finding corroborates existing literature linking lower education levels with limited health literacy and reduced access to healthcare resources19. An Indian study by found that individuals with lower education had poorer awareness and utilization of post-COVID recovery services, contributing to persistent health issues20. Individuals with lower educational attainment may struggle to navigate the healthcare system effectively, leading to inadequate management of post-COVID symptoms and resulting in poorer health outcomes21,22. Furthermore, education often correlates with better employment opportunities and income levels, which can further influence access to healthcare services and overall well-being23. Addressing educational disparities through targeted public health interventions could be vital for improving HRQoL among vulnerable populations affected by COVID-19.

The unexpected finding regarding marital status where currently married individuals reported lower HRQoL compared to never married individuals merits further exploration. This counterintuitive result may reflect the complexities inherent in marital relationships during periods of illness or stress. Previous research has indicated that caregiving responsibilities can place significant emotional and physical burdens on spouses, potentially leading to decreased well-being for both partners9. Qualitative studies in Indian urban households during COVID-19 have highlighted increased emotional strain and caregiving burnout among spouses, which may negatively influence perceived health status24,25. Additionally, married individuals may experience stress related to financial responsibilities or caregiving duties that could negatively impact their health perceptions. Future research should consider qualitative approaches to better understand the dynamics of marital relationships during health crises and how they influence individual health outcomes.

The significant association between health insurance availability and HRQoL underscores the critical role financial security plays in accessing necessary healthcare services. Participants with insurance reported better outcomes (crude coefficient: − 0.307; adjusted coefficient: − 0.233), highlighting systemic inequalities faced by uninsured individuals who may forgo necessary medical care due to cost concerns9,17. This finding is consistent with literature demonstrating that lack of insurance is associated with poorer health outcomes across various populations26. The stark contrast in HRQoL between insured and uninsured participants emphasizes the need for policies aimed at expanding healthcare coverage and reducing financial barriers to care, particularly for those recovering from COVID-19.

The type of treatment facility significantly influenced participants’ HRQoL, with those treated at home reporting better outcomes than those admitted to hospitals or self-medicating. This finding aligns with existing literature suggesting that home-based care can provide a more supportive environment conducive to recovery27. Home treatment may reduce stress associated with hospital stays and allow patients to recover in familiar surroundings, positively impacting their mental well-being. Moreover, studies have shown that patients prefer home care due to its perceived comfort and flexibility28. As healthcare systems continue to adapt post-COVID-19, integrating home-based care options into standard practices could enhance patient satisfaction and improve overall health outcomes.

Although the vaccination status was not a significant finding for the study but the vaccination status is an associated factor. Vaccination not only reduces the severity of illness but also mitigates the risk of long COVID symptoms, thereby enhancing overall health outcomes17. A multicentric study in India has confirmed the protective role of full vaccination in reducing the duration and severity of long COVID symptom29. By integrating vaccination status into the analysis, the research can better assess how access to vaccines and public health initiatives affect HRQoL in the community context. Community-driven interventions have proven effective in increasing vaccination rates by addressing barriers to access and promoting vaccine confidence through tailored messaging and outreach strategies30. This focus on community engagement ensures that public health efforts are aligned with the specific concerns and preferences of local populations, ultimately leading to improved health outcomes and quality of life for those affected by COVID-19. In summary, the community-based approach utilized in this study not only enhances our understanding of the long-term impacts of COVID-19 on HRQoL but also emphasizes the critical role of vaccination in promoting recovery. By prioritizing the voices and experiences of community members, this research aims to inform targeted interventions that can effectively address the ongoing challenges faced by COVID-19 survivors.

Limitations and future directions

While our study provides valuable insights into factors affecting HRQoL among COVID-19 patients, it is important to acknowledge its limitations. The cross-sectional design restricts causal interpretations; thus, longitudinal studies are needed to assess changes in HRQoL over time and identify predictors of recovery trajectories. Additionally, reliance on self-reported measures may introduce bias; incorporating objective assessments could strengthen future research. Future studies should also explore additional variables such as mental health status, social support networks, and lifestyle factors that may further elucidate the complexities surrounding HRQoL post-COVID-19.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our findings corroborate existing literature on the determinants of HRQoL among COVID-19 patients, emphasizing the roles of age, educational status, marital status, health insurance availability, and treatment setting. Addressing these factors through targeted interventions and policy changes is essential for improving health outcomes in this population. As we continue to navigate the ongoing impacts of the pandemic, understanding these dynamics will be crucial for developing effective support systems for COVID-19 survivors.
