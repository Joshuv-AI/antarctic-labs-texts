Cardiovascular disease risk associated with housing disadvantages among middle-aged and older populations in China: a cohort study
Authors: Chengzhe Tao, Tingya Wan, Jingying Zhu, Zhi Li, Yuna Huang, Mingxue Shu, Shuwen Han, Hong Qian, Qiaoqiao Xu, Yun Fan, Xinliang Ding, Bing Lu, Chuncheng Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence for associations of housing disadvantages with cardiovascular disease (CVD) is scarce. This study aimed to assess the association of housing disadvantages with CVD among middle-aged and older populations in China.MethodsWe conducted analyses by data from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study, 2011–2018. CVD events were ascertained by self-reported data. A total of 9,843 individuals were included in stroke sub-cohort, and 8,752 individuals were included in heart disease sub-cohort. Cox regression was used to estimate hazard ratio (95% confidence interval (CI)) of housing disadvantages with CVD. Population attributable fractions were calculated through Levin’s formula and Monte-Carlo simulation to estimate the CVD incidence associated with housing disadvantages in China.ResultsWe found that using crops (HR = 1.35; 95% CI: 1.08–1.68) and coal fuel (HR: 1.71, 95%CI: 1.40–2.10) as heating energy sources were positively associated with heart disease risk compared to clean fuels. Unsafe sanitation was found to be associated with 43% (HR: 1.43, 95%CI: 1.23–1.66) higher heart disease risk and 30% (HR: 1.30, 95%CI: 1.06–1.60) higher stroke risk, and unsafe water source was positively associated with stroke risk (HR: 1.30, 95%CI: 1.08–1.56). Geographical disparities in CVD cases associated with housing disadvantages at a provincial level were found. Annually 1,380,000 cases (15.1%), 718,000 cases (7.9%), and 1,471,000 cases (16.1%) of heart diseases were associated with coal heating energy use, crop heating energy use, and unsafe sanitation, separately. 360,000 cases (12.1%) and 189,000 cases (6.4%) of strokes were associated with unsafe sanitation and unsafe water sources. Regions with lower socio-economic status had higher PAF.ConclusionsThese findings suggested that housing disadvantages were associated with CVD risk in China and highlighted geographical inequality in cardiovascular diseases associated with housing disadvantages.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24996-z.Keywords: Housing disadvantages, CHARLS, Cardiovascular diseases, Burden estimation

Introduction

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) are a group of common non-communicable diseases (NCDs) that are associated with lower life expectancy [1]. In China, the age-standardized prevalence of CVD increased by 14.7% from 1990 to 2016 [2]. By 2020, CVD accounts for 4.58 million deaths in China [3, 4]; Moreover, two major types of CVD including stroke and heart disease emerged as the leading contributors to years of life lost (YLLs) among middle-aged and older populations in China [5]. Apart from the mortality burden, CVD is associated with lower life qualities by causing cognitive decline, dementia, and disability, particularly in middle-aged and older adults [6–9].

Numerous studies have indicated that adverse environmental exposures are linked to increased risk of CVD, raising concerns in recent years [10]. Housing disadvantages are harmful factors within a residence which can result in adverse health outcomes, such as unsafe sanitation, household air pollution, and unsafe water sources. In China, solid fuel use as a typical housing disadvantage was found to cause higher levels of air pollution and was a leading risk factors for CVD [11]. However, evidence regarding links of other housing disadvantages such as unsafe water sources or unsafe sanitation with CVD risk is scarce. Moreover, few studies have attempted to comprehensively quantify the CVD cases associated with housing disadvantages at a geographically finer scale. Apart from this, previous studies only investigated the overall CVD risks associated with solid fuel use, and the detailed associations between solid fuel types and CVD risks remained to be investigated. Taken together, it is necessary to investigate the associations of various housing disadvantages with CVD risks within a prospective cohort to fill the current knowledge gaps.

Here, we analyzed nationally representative data of 2011–2018 China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). We aimed to estimate associations between housing disadvantages and CVD risk; Based on estimates from CHARLS and other sources of statistics, we aimed to further understand the geographical disparities in proportions of CVD incidence associated with housing disadvantages.

Method

Study design and participants

This cohort study analyzed data from CHARLS, 2011–2018. Briefly, CHARLS is a national-representative survey conducted by Peking University. With the multi-stage sampling design, the study enrolled 17 708 individuals and collected the socio-demographic characteristics, health status, medical conditions, physical measurements and household data with standardized questionnaires and measurements, the overall participation rate of baseline survey was 80.5% [12].The baseline data were collected in 2011–2012 [12], and follow-up data were collected every 2 years until 2018 [13].

Assessment of housing disadvantages

Housing disadvantages data were obtained from structured interviews, including four dimensions: heating energy source, cooking energy source, housing unsafe sanitation and housing unsafe water source. Energy sources for heating were coded as clean fuels (solar, natural gas, electric and liquefied petroleum gas), crop (including wood) fuels, coal fuels and other fuels [14]. Cooking energy source was coded as clean fuels (marsh gas, natural gas, electric and liquefied petroleum gas), crop fuels, coal fuels and other fuels [14]. Unsafe water and sanitation exposure was determined based on whether participants reported having running water and a flushable toilet, with negative responses indicating exposure to unsafe settings.

Ascertainment of CVD outcome

Self-reported data of cardiovascular outcomes were obtained from follow-up survey waves conducted in 2013, 2015, 2018 [15]. CVD events were coded following the questions “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina, congestive heart failure, or other heart problems?” or “Have you been told by a doctor that you have been diagnosed with a stroke?” Individuals who answered “yes” in the follow-up period was defined as self-reported heart disease or stroke. The timing of CVD diagnoses was confirmed through questionnaires. Follow-up time was defined as the interval from the baseline survey to the year of first reported CVD diagnosis for cases, or to the end of follow-up in 2018 for participants without reported CVD. We created two cohorts: one for CVD patients and one for stroke patients.

Covariates

We included risk factors within demographic, lifestyle, and physical measurement data as covariates in our models [16–18]. Briefly, age, gender, residence, smoking status, alcohol consumption, self-reported living quality, marital status, educational level, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP) and body mass index (BMI) were used as covariates. Age was calculated based on difference between the birth year and survey year. Gender (binary variables, male as the reference), smoking status (binary variable; non-smoker as the reference), alcohol consumption of last year (ordinal variable; drink more than once a month, drink but less than once a month or never drink; coding according to the questionnaires), self-reported living quality which were used as socioeconomic measure (ordinal variable; very high, relatively high, average, relatively poor or poor, coding according to the questionnaires), marital status (categorical variable; married, separated, divorced/widowed, or never married), educational level (ordinal variable; none, primary school or below, middle school, high school, college/vocational school or above), and residence (binary variable; rural or urban) were defined by data from standardized questionnaire. Of note, the residence (rural or urban) was identified with where the primary sample units located of each individual’s address. SBP and DBP (continuous variables) were calculated using the average values of the three manual measurements. BMI (continuous variable) was calculated through dividing measured weight in kilograms by the square of height in meters. SBP, DBP, weight and height were measured by trained interviewers with standardized protocols during the survey; the details can be seen on the CHARLS website (https://charls.charlsdata.com/pages/Data/2011-charls-wave1/en.html).

Statistical analysis

Estimating associations between housing disadvantages and CVD

Participants over 45 years were included for the present analysis. We excluded participants with missing covariate data, biologically implausible anthropometric values (height < 1 m or weight ≥ 150 kg), or with extreme weight measurements (below the 0.1st or above the 99.9th percentile). Due to the missing rate of self-reported CVD and stroke data, we performed our analyses based on two outcome-specific sub-cohorts to avoid loss of statistical power. For each outcome, we generated two sub-cohorts and excluded individuals with (1) missing values for follow-up data of outcomes, (2) reported outcomes in the baseline. We included 8,752 individuals for heart disease-cohort and 9,843 individuals for stroke-cohort to enter the subsequent analysis. For analyses of each housing disadvantage, we further exclude those with missing housing disadvantages data. The detailed flow chart of the data collection could be seen in Fig. S1, and the design of our cohort analysis was shown in Figs. S2-S4.

Demographic characteristics of our study populations were reported as mean with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and numbers with percentages for categorical variables. The directed acyclic graph of the study was shown in Fig. S5. Cox proportional hazard regression models were utilized to calculate hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for associations of housing disadvantages with cardiovascular events [19]. Schoenfeld residuals were applied to validate the proportional hazards assumptions, and no violation was observed [20]. Confounding including age, gender, residence, smoking status, alcohol consumption, self-reported living quality, marital status, educational level, SBP, DBP and BMI were used for adjustment. We did not adjust for comorbidities in the primary analyses considering these as potential mediators lying on the causal chain between housing disadvantages and CVD. We calculated false discovery rate (FDR) of each finding for addressing the multiple comparisons [21]. Subgroup analyses were also performed through estimating the associations in sub-populations, P for interaction was calculated to assess modifying effects of covariates on the association [22]. In the analysis, we only considered two-way interactions due to the interpretability of the results.

Estimating population attributable fractions and numbers of CVD associated with housing disadvantages

We assumed that the estimates from the CHARLS cohort were approximately causal. Using data on estimated effect size and population-weighted prevalence of housing disadvantages in 2013, population attributable fractions (PAFs) of CVD associated with housing disadvantages at the provincial level were calculated based on Levin’s formula [23]. We further estimated associations of socio-economic status (SES) with PAFs through linear regression models. SES indicators including provincial education levels, unemployment rate, average income, and average consumption level were obtained from National Bureau of Statistics and natural-log transformed [24].

To investigate regional disparities in the cardiovascular burden attributable to housing disadvantages across different levels of socio-economic development, we computed SES score for each region by classifying each SES component into 4 points through their quantiles and summing the points. Provinces were categorized into low-, middle- and high SES groups according to the tertiles and CVDs associated with housing disadvantages were estimated in the regions stratified by SES.

We multiplied the total CVD cases in populations aged over 45 with estimated PAFs to calculate the CVD cases associated with housing disadvantages. The data of population-level cardiovascular incidences was extracted from global burden of disease (GBD) database [25]. In regions stratified by SES, the CVD cases were computed with consistent methods.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed to validate the robustness of our results. First, we estimated the associations in individuals with follow-up time 2 years or over to avoid reverse causality. Second, we additionally adjusted for the total cholesterol, triglyceride and high-density lipoprotein considering the potential effects of metabolic function on cardiovascular risk [26]. These biomarkers were measured through Enzymatic colormetric test in the laboratory. Third, we computed the E values with 95% CI limit (CL) for evaluation of unadjusted confounders [27]. As defined, the E-values can be determined as the effect sizes of unmeasured confounding that may nullify the observed association.

All analyses were performed using R (R Core Team). Packages including survey, ggplot2, forestplot, survival were applied for computation and visualization. Results with two-sided P < 0.05 and FDR < 0.05 were considered significant. The study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guidelines. Monte-Carlo simulation was applied for estimating the uncertainty intervals (UIs) of PAFs, we generated 1000 samples of effect sizes (HR was applied assuming log normal distribution) and prevalence of exposure (binomial proportions), separately. The sampling data was combined and 1000 PAFs were computed subsequently. The lower and upper 95% UIs of the PAFs were defined as 2.5th percentile and 97.5th percentile of the samples, and the point estimation was defined as median of the samples [28].

Association between housing disadvantages and CVD risk

Both unadjusted and fully adjusted models indicated that housing disadvantages were associated with heart disease and stroke risk (Table S1, Fig. 1). Compared to clean fuels, using crop or coal fuel for heating energy sources was observed to be associated with 35% (HR = 1.35, 95%CI: 1.08–1.68) and 71% (HR = 1.71, 95%CI: 1.40–2.10) higher risk of heart disease, respectively. The HRs for unsafe sanitation on heart disease and stroke were 1.43 (95%CI: 1.23–1.66, P < 0.001) and 1.30 (95%CI: 1.06–1.60, P = 0.013). Moreover, unsafe water source was positively associated with higher stroke risk (HR: 1.30, 95%CI: 1.08–1.56, P = 0.006).

We also computed the E values of each significant result (Table S2). For heart disease, the E values of crop heating energy source, coal heating energy source and unsafe sanitation were 2.04 (CL: 1.37), 2.81 (CL: 2.15), and 2.21 (CL: 1.76). The results showed that residual confounding of the models might not substantially affect our results. All the results were observed to be robust after accounting for the multiple comparison (all FDR < 0.05; Fig. S6). Results of sensitivity analysis remained consistent with the results of main analyses (Table S3-S6).

Stratified analysis

In subgroup analysis, the associations between housing disadvantages and CVD risk remained consistent (Fig. 2; Table S7-S10). Individuals with lower educational level exhibited stronger associations of crops (HR = 1.32, 95%CI: 1.04–1.67) and coal heating energy use (HR = 1.74, 95%CI: 1.39–2.17) with heart disease risk. Similarly, individuals with lower self-reported living quality showed stronger associations of heating solid fuel use and heart disease risk. We further estimated that individuals with lower educational level had higher risks of stroke when exposed to unsafe sanitation (HR = 1.31, 95%CI: 1.05–1.64) and unsafe water source (HR = 1.32, 95%CI: 1.08–1.60). Stronger associations were also observed in individuals with lower living quality. Regarding the effect modifications of covariates, we observed significant interactions between crop heating energy use and self-reported living quality on heart disease (P for interaction = 0.013) (Table S11).

PAFs of CVD associated with housing disadvantages

PAFs of heart diseases and stroke associated with housing disadvantages in middle-aged and older populations were estimated, and we found significant geographical disparities in the attributable heart diseases burden (Fig. 3, Table S12-S16.). For unsafe sanitation and solid heating energy fuel use (crop and coal). Notably, for coal heating energy use, we observed that PAFs in northern provinces displayed higher PAFs of heart disease than the southern provinces (P < 0.001). For stroke, similar geographical disparities were observed. The PAFs of unsafe water source varied from 1.1% (95%UI: 0.0%, 3.4%) in Shanghai (Eastern region of China) to 15.6% (95%UI: 4.0%, 26.4%) in Qinghai (Western region of China). For stroke associated with unsafe sanitation, Gansu (PAF: 18.3%, 95%UI: 4.7%, 31.6%) in northeastern region of China was province with highest PAFs.

Associations between geographical SES and estimated PAFs

The estimated associations between geographical PAFs and SES variables indicated that provincial average income was negatively associated with PAFs of housing disadvantages (P < 0.05), while average consumption was significantly associated with lower PAFs, except for those of heart diseases associated with crop heating energy use (Table S17). Regarding educational levels, significant negative associations were observed only for cardiovascular diseases related to unsafe sanitation.

Discussion

In this cohort study, we investigated the associations between housing disadvantages and cardiovascular diseases with data of nationally representative populations in China. The use of solid fuel for heating and unsafe sanitation was associated with an elevated risk of heart disease, and unsafe water source and unsafe sanitation were observed to be linked to an increased risk of stroke. Moreover, we revealed substantial geographical disparities in the PAFs of CVD associated with housing disadvantages. These findings contribute to a deeper understanding of the cardiovascular implications of inadequate housing conditions in China and offer important evidence to inform policy development in specific areas.

Multiple studies have focused on the associations between solid fuel and cardiovascular health in China. A rural cohort study found that using solid fuels for cooking and heating was associated with 20% and 39% higher risk of cardiovascular mortality [19]. A study found a 69% higher atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) risk associated with cooking using solid fuels compared to clean fuels [29]. Evidence from China Kadoorie Biobank (CKB) further demonstrated that solid fuel use for winter heating was associated with a 70% higher risk of stroke mortality and a 49% higher risk of CVD mortality [30]. These reported results were generally consistent with the findings in our study. Moreover, our study was the very first resource that comprehensively estimated associations between other housing disadvantages (unsafe water source and unsafe sanitation) and cardiovascular disease and further reported geographical PAF of CVD associated with these housing disadvantages in China.

Crop and coal are predominant solid fuels used as domestic energy for cooking and heating in China [31, 32]. The combustion of these solid fuel can produce higher levels of household gaseous pollutants such as particulate matter 2.5 (PM2.5), carbon monoxide, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs), oxides of sulfur, and heavy metals [33], and these toxic compounds may increase the risk of cardiovascular diseases and deaths through pathways including systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, cardiac autonomic dysfunction and atherosclerotic thrombosis [34–36]. Animal studies have also revealed that particulate matter exposure can cause excessive inflammation and activation of apoptosis, which were linked to cardiovascular risks. We also identified substantial regional disparities, with northern provinces having higher proportions of preventable heart disease associated with coal-based heating, likely due to differences in energy infrastructure. In northern China, coals were materials which are mostly used for heating and the main source of air pollutants [37]. Nationally, coal use accounted for higher PAFs of heart disease cases than crop residues, this may be influenced by structures of energy use in China that coal use was more widespread as heating energy.

Unsafe water sources have been observed to be correlated with increased stroke risk. Research has established that contaminated water frequently contains excessive microorganisms and heavy metals, which may induce oxidative stress, metabolic dysfunction, and systemic inflammation. All these are important pathways contributing to cardiovascular pathogenesis. Unsafe sanitation was found to be associated with stroke and heart disease risk; Although the mechanisms were largely unknown but one possible explanation is the elevated systemic inflammation caused by adverse building environment. Our analyses identified that Qinghai, Anhui, and Inner Mongolia as provinces with higher PAFs of stroke or heart disease associated with unsafe sanitation and unsafe water source. This may be caused by the socio-economic disparities among the provinces [38].

In our study, living qualities were identified as effect modifiers of the association between housing disadvantages and CVD risk. Low living quality were determined as a main contributor of low socioeconomic status (SES), which has been shown to be significantly associated with higher risk of CVD [39, 40]. Lower SES class had limited accessibility to high-quality health care, greater psychological stress, and higher prevalence of unhealthy lifestyle, which may exacerbate the CVD risks linked to housing disadvantages. In addition, the relations of SES and urbanization with housing disadvantages and related cardiovascular risks remained to be investigated. The disadvantages of housing might be a downstream consequence of imbalanced urbanization and socioeconomic disadvantages, which were required further investigation in the future.

Moreover, people within provinces of lower socioeconomic status may have limited access to health care, and thus the diagnoses of CVD and CVD-related risk factors may substantially delay and therefore cause excess CVD cases associated with housing disadvantages across the populations. Therefore, future studies should further evaluate the values for the avoidable CVD cases by removing exposure of unsafe sanitation and unsafe water sources.

We estimated CVD cases associated with housing disadvantages, and the relevant PAFs were comparable to those of smoking and ambient air pollution [18, 41]. Although the data sources and methodology were different and the comparisons should be explained carefully, these findings have collectively indicated that eliminating the prevalence of housing disadvantages might be essential for reducing cardiovascular diseases across China. Challenges remained. Future studies should focus on the causal inference for cardiovascular effects of housing disadvantages. In addition, the intervention study and cost-effectiveness analysis should be done to further understand the benefit of improving housing disadvantages.

Several limitations exist in this study. First, since this study was conducted using standardized questionnaires to collect exposure data in a time-fixed manner, quantification of the detailed pollution was limited. Instrumental measurements of duration, intensity and dynamics of housing disadvantages are recommended to precisely quantify the exposure and address the time-varying confounding [42]. Second, the regional PAFs were estimated through pooled HR and regional-specific prevalence of housing disadvantages, following by the GBD study. Although such methodology is plausible, we recommend applying region-specific HR when applicable to estimate the results more accurately. Third, the prevalence we used was derived from 2013 due to the relatively high missing rates of housing disadvantage variables in the subsequent visit of CHARLS [14]. Fourth, the data for CVD morbidity burden associated with housing disadvantage in the latest year was limited and may bias the estimates. Future studies should be conducted to further update the estimates. Fifth, the CVD outcome in this study was self-reported, and future studies using electronic records and register of hospitalization would be recommended to further validate our findings. Finally, due to the nature of observational studies, the causality cannot be confirmed. Reverse causality may exist within our studies and influence the estimates calculated from the Levin’s formula, which assumed the causal effects of exposure on the outcomes. Cohorts with longer follow-up periods and larger sample size should be conducted to strengthen the evidence.

Conclusion

Housing disadvantages were associated with increment of CVD morbidity risk in general Chinese populations. Efforts should be taken to eliminate the inequalities of cardiovascular burden attributable to housing disadvantages in China.
