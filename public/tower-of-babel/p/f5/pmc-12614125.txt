Predictors of Specialty Outpatient Palliative Care Utilization Persons With Serious Illness
Authors: Paige Comstock Barker, Rebecca Liddicoat Yamarik, Oluwaseun Adeyemi, Allison M Cuthel, Mara Flannery, Nina Siman, Keith S Goldfeld, Corita R Grudzen, EMPallA Investigators
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractContext.Outpatient Palliative Care (OPC) benefits persons living with serious illness, yet barriers exist in utilization.Objectives.To identify factors associated with OPC clinic utilization.Methods.Emergency Medicine Palliative Care Access is a multicenter, randomized control trial comparing two models of palliative care for patients recruited from the Emergency Department (ED): nurse-led telephonic case management and OPC (one visit a month for six months). Patients were aged 50+ with advanced cancer or end-stage organ failure and recruited from 19 EDs. Using a mixed effects hurdle model, we analyzed patient, provider, clinic and healthcare system factors associated with OPC utilization.Results.Among the 603 patients randomized to OPC, about half (53.6%) of patients attended at least one clinic visit. Those with less than high school education were less likely to attend an initial visit than those with a college degree or higher (aOR 0.44; CI 0.23, 0.85), as were patients who required considerable assistance (aOR 0.45; CI 0.25, 0.82) or had congestive heart failure only (aOR 0.46; CI 0.26, 0.81). Those with higher symptom burden had a higher attendance at the initial visit (aOR 1.05; CI 1.00, 1.10). Reduced follow up visit rates were demonstrated for those of older age (aRR 0.90; CI 0.82, 0.98), female sex (aRR 0.84; CI 0.71, 0.99), and those that were never married (aRR 0.62; CI 0.52, 0.87).Conclusion.Efforts to improve OPC utilization should focus on those with lower education, more functional limitations, older age, female sex, and those with less social support.Trial Registration ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier:NCT03325985.Keywords: Emergency medicine, palliative care, outpatient palliative care, advanced illness

Introduction

Specialty outpatient palliative care (OPC) provides multiple benefits to persons living with serious illness. Studies have demonstrated decreased symptom burden,1-5 improved quality of life,1,2,6-9 increased satisfaction,1 decreased caregiver depression2,6,9,and improved survival3,8,10. Despite these benefits, high no-show rates are common, with rates as high as 40% in the literature.11-18 The low rate of utilization of specialty OPC make factors that contribute to non-attendance an important factor to analyze.

Low utilization in specialty OPC can be related to patient, provider, clinic, or healthcare system factors. In one retrospective study of an OPC clinic, patients who missed their first visit were younger, lived further away from the clinic, and had worse functional status (ECOG >/ = 2).19 In other studies among patients referred to OPC, patients who were older, minoritized, unpartnered, too ill to attend, or had Medicaid had lower utilization of initial appointments. Those who attended a single visit were more likely to miss follow-up visits if they were older, male, non-English speaking, lower functional status or had a non-cancer diagnoses.16-18,20 In one study of patients living with HIV, those with mood disorders were more likely to attend a palliative care visit suggesting that higher symptom burden may increase utilization.21 Specialty OPC was initially designed for cancer patients, and the majority of users are cancer patients. Literature demonstrates that palliative care utilization is much lower and later in organ failure patients as compared to cancer patients.22-25 Non-cancer patients have different needs and there is concern that specialty OPC as it currently exists may not adequately meet the needs of organ failure patients as well as it does for cancer patients.22-27 Additionally, several studies have identified system factors such as shorter notice for appointments or longer wait between appointment request and scheduled appointment have also been associated with non-attendance.15,28-31

The aim of this study was to assess the patient, provider, clinic and healthcare system factors associated with utilization outcomes such as attending initial and follow-up specialty OPC clinic visits. We hypothesized that those with worse functional status, lower symptom burden, and a diagnosis other than cancer would be less likely to utilize specialty OPC.26,32 We additionally hypothesized that better clinic access would have a positive impact on patient utilization due to increased likelihood of ability to schedule appointment sooner after enrollment.

Methods

Study Design and Population

Patients randomized to the outpatient arm of the Emergency Medicine Palliative Care Access (EMPallA) trial between April 2018 and June 2022 were included. The EMPallA trial was a pragmatic two-arm, multisite, randomized controlled trial (RCT) that compared nurse-led telephonic palliative care with specialty OPC for patients that visited 19 EDs in nine US states and was designed with insight from a Study Advisory Committee (SAC) which included a variety of stakeholders including patients, caregivers, and members of the community.33 Prior to patient enrollment, trained research coordinators identified eligible patients in the electronic health records, approached the patients in the ED or by telephone, and conducted interviews to confirm eligibility. To ensure participants were properly informed about the study during the consent process, research coordinators used a description of palliative care included in a “tip sheet” that also included suggested answers to frequently asked questions about palliative care and was created by one site clinical lead and vetted by the SAC. Prior to participating in recruitment, research coordinators participated in role-play with a SAC member to ensure tone, verbiage and description of palliative care was not only consistent but patient-centered. Patients who consented and completed a baseline survey were randomized to either the nurse-led telephonic or specialty OPC arm. Patients randomized to the outpatient arm were encouraged by the research coordinator to attend specialty OPC visits once a month for six months with reminder phone calls (to both participant and caregiver if enrolled) and reminder mailed postcards for those who could not be reached. Research coordinators worked closely with clinic schedulers and clinicians to coordinate visits to the OPC clinic. For example, the scheduler was critical in ensuring the patient was scheduled for an EMPallA specialty outpatient appointment on a day that they were already visiting the health system for other appointments. Participants in the OPC arm also received SAC vetted materials including a bro-chure and letter from the clinic outlining what to expect in advance of the clinic appointment. Appointments were in-person before the COVID-19 pandemic, but most clinics transitioned tooffereing both in-person and telehealth appointments at some point in 2020. Patients were given a $25 gift card for each clinic appointment attended during study enrollment to assist with any travel costs or co-payments. The SAC advised the study from start to finish, focusing on culturally sensitive interactions of research staff with patients and caregivers in areas such as patient facing materials, gift cards, and telephonic interactions between research coordinators and patients/caregivers.34 A hospice and palliative medicine board-eligible or board-certified physician or nurse-practitioner delivered care across the eight domains of quality palliative care–goals of care, physical, psychological, social, spiritual, cultural, prognosis, and ethics/legal aspects of care.35 Additional trial details have been published elsewhere.33,36 Enrollment and follow-up of participants was completed in August 2023. For the purposes of this analysis, we included only those randomized to the specialty OPC arm. Subjects provided written or verbal consent for participation in accordance with the New York University Grossman School of Medicine’s Institutional Review Board (IRB: s17-01211) as well as all participating sites’ IRB boards and the larger trial is registered in ClinicalTrials.gov (ID: NCT03325985).37

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

Eligible patients were ED visitors 50 years or older with advanced cancer (metastatic solid tumor), class III or IV congestive heart failure (CHF), end stage renal disease (ESRD) and/or class III or IV chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) who were scheduled for dis-charge or placed in observation status. An advanced cancer eligibility table was created by a subset of participating site oncologists at study inception, using language specific to cancer types found in the electronic health record (EHR) to assist the research staff in screening for eligibility (see Appendix 1). The trial excluded patients who did not have a working telephone, health insurance accepted by the outpatient clinic, did not live in the geographical area of the enrollment site, lacked proficiency in English or Spanish, as well as those with a history of hospice use, more than one palliative care visit in the previous six months, and those residing in long-term care or assisted living. Persons with newly diagnosed cancer were excluded to prevent our research team from delivering this news before the physician, as the patient may have been unaware of their diagnosis. Persons with dementia listed in the EHR problem list were also excluded as requested by our central IRB, given the difficulties receiving informed consent from these persons. The full study protocol has been previously published.33

Between April 2018 and June 2022, a total of 1284 were enrolled in the larger trial and 644 were randomized to specialty OPC (Fig. 1). Amongst those 644, there were missing values in the responses to educational attainment (n = 21; 3.3% of 644), marital status (n = 4, 0.6%), income (n = 2, 0.3%), Edmonton Symptom Assessment Scale-Revised (ESAS-r) score (n = 4, 0.6%), and site characteristics (n = 10; 1.6%). For these missing values, we performed a listwise deletion consistent with the principles of handling missing data.38,39 Hence, our final sample size was 603.

Outcome Measures

The outcomes of interest were: 1) initial utilization defined as attending an initial specialty OPC clinic visit; and 2) sustained utilization defined as attending at least one follow-up specialty OPC visit after an initial visit. Data on clinic attendance were extracted from the EHR.

Predictor Variables

We obtained data from EHRs, administrative data, and patient surveys. All covariates were predetermined a priori and included in the model based on our belief that they could be potential predictors of lack of attendance. The predictor variables we selected were largely grouped into 1) patient, 2) provider, 3) clinic, and 4) healthcare system factors.

Patient characteristics included age, sex, race/ethnicity, educational attainment, marital status, income, primary language, presence of a caregiver, loneliness, illness type, COVID-19 period during which the patient was recruited, functional status, and symptom burden. Age was measured both as a continuous and categorical variable, and for the regression model, we centered age at its mean and divided the centered age by 10. Loneliness was measured using the Three-Item Loneliness Scale.40 Illness type was measured as a categorical variable–COPD only, ESRD only, CHF only, cancer only, or two or more of the illnesses. Functional status was measured using the Karnofsky Performance Scale (KPS) and measured as a five-level ordinal variables-dis-abled (0–40), requires considerable assistance (50), requires occasional assistance (60), cares for self/unable to do a normal activity (70) and normal activity (80–100).41 COVID-19 period was defined as before (April 2018 to March 2020), transition phase of (April 2020 to April 2021), and postadaptation to (May 2021 to June 2022) the pandemic. The ESAS-r, a measure of symptom burden, was treated as a continuous variable.42 The ESAS-r is scored from 0 to 60–higher scores suggest higher symptom burden. In the bivariate analysis, we defined ESAS-r as either low (0–30) or high (31–60) symptom burden. In the regression model, we divided the ESAS-r score by 10. Hence, each unit increase in symptoms score represents a 10-point increase in ESAS-r. The Functional Assessment of Cancer Therapy General (FACT-G) measures quality of life (QOL) across four domains: physical, social/family, emotional, and functional well-being with a possible range of 0–108. Higher scores indicate better QOL.43 These health characteristics, including responses to the Three-Item Loneliness Scale and FACT-G were obtained from the baseline survey.

Provider factors included the number of full-time palliative care physicians, nurse practitioners, and registered nurses, and clinic factors included clinic type (embedded versus free-standing) and the number of appointment slots for new and follow-up visits. Healthcare system factors included hiring freezes for clinicians or schedulers, clinicians or schedulers on leave, not enough clinicians, clinic closure, and clinician and administrative staff turnover. Provider, clinic and healthcare system factors were extracted from a 58-item self-administered survey (see Appendix 2). The survey items were first distributed to three of the 19 site Co-Investigators (Co-Is) for face validation. Suggested changes to the survey text across the three reviewers were incorporated before dissemination by the research team. The final survey was administered to all 19 EMPallA Co-Is through REDCap.44 All Co-Is were encouraged to obtain necessary administrative information in advance of survey completion.

Analysis

First, we described baseline patient, provider, clinic, and healthcare system characteristics. Next, we reported how these characteristics varied among those who attended initial and follow-up visits and those who did not. We merged all clinic, provider, and healthcare system factors to individual patient characteristics to generate a composite dataset measured at the individual level. We were concerned that some predictors might be highly correlated, so after formally assessing multicollinearity, we narrowed our set of covariates. Variables that exhibited correlation coefficient values of 0.3 and higher and whose variance inflation factor was higher than three were excluded from the model.45 Functional status was an ordered variable with the reference category being normal activity, and the significant variable was in reference to “normal activity.” We estimated a mixed effects hurdle model to assess factors associated with attending an initial OPC clinic visit, as well as factors associated with the number of follow-up visits among those who had an initial visit.46 The model included fixed effects for patient, provider, clinic and healthcare system characteristics as well as site-specific random effects for the 19 participating EDs. We reported the adjusted odds ratios (AOR) and 95% confidence interval (CI) of attending an initial visit as well as the adjusted rate ratios (RR) and 95% CIs for the number of follow-up visits.

Results

Six hundred and three patients were randomized to the OPC arm and included in the analysis. The mean age was 66.7 years (SD: 10.3); 295 (49%) patients were female, 319 (53%) were non-Hispanic White, 171 (28%) were non-Hispanic Black, and 77 (13%) were Hispanic (Table 1). A total of 77 patients (13%) had less than a high school education and 197 (33%) had a college degree or higher. Also, 273 (45%) were married, 225 (37%) had income less than $25,000 and 18 (3%) were Spanish speaking. Three hundred seventy three (62%) had a caregiver, 211 (35%) had cancer as the only comorbidity, and 304 (50%) required at least occasional assistance to care for themselves. The mean baseline ESAS-r score was 37.5 (SD: 18.8) and the mean FACT-G score was 65.1 (SD: 18.3).

Just over half of patients (54%) completed initial utilization defined as attending at least one clinic visit during the study period (Table 3). Patients aged 75 years and older had fewer initial clinic visits (46%) than those below 75 years (56%). The proportion of those who demonstrated sustained utilization, defined as attending more than one clinic visit, were highest among non-Hispanic Whites, those with college degrees and higher, married or living with partner, earning $100,000 or more, English-speakers, and those with a caregiver (Table 3). Patients with cancer had the highest sustained utilization (63%) while those with CHF only had the lowest (39%). Additionally, the proportion of those who attended at least one clinic visit were highest among those without functional limitations, recruited during the transition phase of the COVID-19 pandemic, and those who visited an embedded clinic.

Table 4 represents the results of the hurdle regression that modeled the odds of initial utilization and the rate ratio of sustained utilization. Attending an initial clinic visit, or initial utilization, was associated with educational level, comorbidity type, functional status, symptom burden, and period of recruitment. Compared to those with a college degree or higher, those with less than high school education, high school, and some college were 56% (AOR 0.44; 95% CI 0.23, 0.85), 46% (AOR 0.54; 95% CI 0.33, 0.89), and 42% (AOR 0.58; 95% CI 0.36, 0.94) less likely to utilize specialty OPC, respectively. Compared to those with only a cancer diagnosis, patients with CHF were 54% less likely to utilize specialty OPC (AOR: 0.46; 95% CI 0.26, 0.81). Patients who required considerable assistance were 55% less likely to utilize specialty OPC compared to those with normal activity (AOR 0.45; 95% CI 0.25, 0.82). For every 10-point increase in symptom burden, the clinic utilization rate increased by 5% (IRR 1.05; 95% CI 1.00, 1.10). Finally, patients recruited during the transition phase of the COVID-19 pandemic were 2.4 times (AOR 2.41; 95% CI 1.52, 3.83) more likely to complete initial utilization compared to those recruited before the pandemic.

Table 4 also examines the likelihood of sustained utilization, defined as attending at least one follow-up visit. Age, sex, and marital status were significantly associated with rates of follow-up clinic attendance after an initial visit demonstrating sustained utilization. For every 10-year increase in age, the sustained utilization rate for follow-up visits reduced by 10% (IRR: 0.90; 95% CI 0.82, 0.98). Compared to males, females’ sustained utilization rate reduced by 16% (IRR 0.84; 95% CI 0.71, 0.99). Also, compared to those who were married, the sustained utilization rate of those that were never married reduced by 32% (IRR 0.62; 95% CI 0.52, 0.87).

Discussion

Overall, our study highlights the operational challenges of engaging patients in specialty OPC clinics, with 46% of patients with advanced illness recruited from the ED randomized to the clinic arm never attending any OPC visits. This is higher than the “no-show” rate previously reported in other studies11-17 despite significant involvement of research coordinators to assist with patient engagement. This highlights the challenges of referring patients from the ED to palliative care. Nearly half of clinics felt they did not have enough providers which may have hindered scheduling. Patients who were male, younger, white, with higher education, cancer diagnosis, better functional status, and recruited during the transition phase of the pandemic were more likely to complete initial utilization, highlighting the disparities that exist in American healthcare generally and palliative care specifically.17,47,48 Women, older patients and those who were never married were less likely to attend follow-up visits after an initial visit showing decreased sustained utilization.47,48 It is possible that perceptions or fear of palliative care may have hindered minoritized patients from utilizing clinic.17 Our results bear a striking resemblance to a study examining clinic utilization at a California university palliative care clinic which also showed lower initial attendance for Black and Latinx patients, older patients, those with Medicaid, those referred from inpatient as opposed to primary care, and with diagnoses other than cancer.17 These results are nearly identical to ours and highlights the challenge of reaching poorer, minoritized, and socially isolated patients. Patients referred from inpatient providers vs. primary care may be similar to our finding that patients referred from the ED have lower clinic utilization possibly related to ED and inpatient clinicians lacking sufficient time to build a trusted relationship with patients.49,50

We found that having less education and lower functional status reduced the odds of initial utilization. In particular, participants who required considerable assistance were less likely to have an initial visit. Other studies have also shown a correlation between lower functional status and non-attendance.16,22 Functional status and education did not further predict attendance of a follow up visit after initial visit. Participants enrolled during the transition phase of the COVID-19 pandemic were much more likely to attend an initial visit. We suspect this is related to the fast introduction and uptake of telemedicine across the clinic sites increasing patient access. A study by Drerup et al.51 supports the hypothesis that telehealth may improve attendance rates, showing a much lower no-show rate to telehealth appointments (8%) in a primary care clinic during the COVID-19 pandemic when compared to in-person visits prior to (29%) or during the COVID-19 pandemic (36%). Higher symptom burden was also correlated with attending an initial visit. Correlation with symptoms and attendance was also demonstrated in a study of patients with advanced cancer referred to OPC, which showed a lower odds of missing an appointment if the patient was referred for symptom control.15 Additionally, compared to cancer patients, patients with CHF only were less likely to complete initial utilization of specialty OPC, but disease type did not predict rate of sustained utilization. Historically, palliative care has had the most utilization by patients with a cancer diagnosis and less often for those with organ failure. Specifically, several studies of patients with CHF have shown that patients with CHF rarely receive palliative care.23 Several reasons for this difference have been suggested including, increased prognostic uncertainty in CHF compared to malignancy, a less robust relationship between palliative care physicians and cardiologists compared to between palliative care physicians and oncologists, and CHF being less often recognized as an incurable, progressive, and terminal condition.

Of those that attended an initial visit, we found that older patients were less likely to demonstrate sustained utilization. This contrasts findings in other studies where younger patients were less likely to attend a visit in palliative medicine clinic,19 general practice clinic,52 and in studies that included multiple specialties.19,28,53 In a study of palliative patients with similar mean age to our study, older patients were also less likely to attend an initial visit or follow up visit.17 These findings suggest that age may have a bimodal pattern of utilization. Female patients and those that were never married were also less likely to demonstrate sustained utilization. This contrasts with the California study where males were less likely to attend follow-up visits.17

Our study has several strengths. It is a large group of patients recruited from EDs across many sites that are geographically diverse, increasing generalizability. Non-white patients made up nearly half of the group, with non-Hispanic Black patients comprising 28% of the sample, a group that has low general engagement with palliative care.54 Our study population also included a larger percentage of organ failure patients, a population that also has lower utilization of palliative care. As this study was part of a larger RCT, a great deal of information was known both about the patients and clinics, allowing for a robust analysis of factors related to clinic attendance. Limitations include the inability to understand in more detail why patients did not attend appointments despite research coordinator’s best efforts including whether it was due to perceptions around palliative care or logistical barriers to access,. Recruitment occurred only from the ED, which is a chaotic and often difficult place to initiate a patient-provider relationship, which may have caused unexplainable limitations.

Our findings suggest that focusing efforts to reduce missed appointments among older patients, those with less social support, those with lower education and higher functional limitations, may have the greatest impact on ambulatory palliative care utilization. Improving palliative care utilization is important since earlier studies report that patients with advanced malignancy who missed their appointment with the palliative clinic are two times more like to visit the ED and had a shorter median survival compared to participants that attended their first appointment with the palliative clinic.15 Investigators have suggested telephone reminders prior to appointments,12,55 triaging referrals and offering home-based support to patients with geographic or symptomatic barriers to attendance,19 and improving access to virtual care17,19,56 may improve engagement with specialty OPC.17

Conclusions

Despite specialty OPC demonstrated benefits, the volume of patients exceed the current provider capacity and utilization remains a challenge for those with serious illness. Efforts to improve specialty OPC utilization should focus on those with lower education, more functional limitations, older age, female sex, and those with less social support.
