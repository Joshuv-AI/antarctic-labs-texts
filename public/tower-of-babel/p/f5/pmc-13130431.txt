The influence of Neorautanenia brachypus (Harms) C.A.Sm. supplementation on the lipid profile of broiler chickens
Authors: F Musekiwa, T Mutibvu, G B Nyamushamba, A Makiwa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe experiment evaluated the effects of Neorautanenia brachypus supplementation, alone or combined with a vitamin-mineral premix, on the lipid metabolism of broiler chickens. Experimental groups (E0, E1, E2, E3, and E4) received distinct dietary treatments: E0 (control), E1 (2g/kg N. brachypus powder in feed), E2 (vitamin-mineral premix only), E3 (2g/L N. brachypus powder in water + vitamin-mineral premix), and E4 (2g/kg N. brachypus powder in feed + vitamin-mineral premix). Broilers were maintained under uniform controlled husbandry conditions. Serum very low density lipoprotein (VLDL), low density lipoprotein (LDL), high density lipoprotein (HDL), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG) were analysed using standardised enzymatic colorimetric assay methods. Data were exposed to ANOVA in the General Linear Models procedure in Statistical Analysis System (SAS) vers. 9.4. The results showed that N. brachypus supplementation significantly reduced serum very low-density lipoprotein, low-density Lipoprotein, total cholesterol, and triglycerides levels, while significantly increasing high-density lipoprotein concentrations. The most pronounced effects (p < 0.05) were observed in groups E3 and E4, where N. brachypus was administered through drinking water and in feed combined with micronutrients, Neorautanenia brachypus supplementation significantly reduced harmful lipoproteins (VLDL, LDL) and triglycerides, while increasing high-density lipoprotein across all treatment groups. Reductions in total cholesterol and total glycerides were evident (p < 0.05) across all N. brachypus treated groups, regardless of the mode of administration or co-supplementation. The vitamin-mineral premix alone (E2) did not significantly impact lipid profiles relative to the control group (E0). Neorautanenia brachypus exhibited strong potential as a natural hypolipidemic feed additive for broiler chickens. Future research should focus on determining the optimal dosage across production phases, evaluating long-term physiological and health effects, as well as investigating possible interactions with other phytogenic or micronutrient additives to further enhance its lipid-modulating potential.Keywords: Neorautanenia brachypus, Broiler chickens, Lipid profile, Functional feed additives

Introductıon

The global poultry industry has experienced substantial growth due to increasing demand for affordable and high-quality animal protein [20]. Among the most intensively reared poultry species are broiler chickens, known for their rapid growth, feed efficiency, and short production cycle. However, intensive farming practices have been associated with several metabolic complications, particularly those related to lipid metabolism. Elevated levels of blood lipids such as total cholesterol, low density lipoprotein (LDL), and triglycerides (TG) in broilers are not only indicators of internal physiological stress but may also negatively affect meat quality, carcass composition, and the overall health of the broilerss [5]. Antibiotic growth promoters (AGPs) were widely incorporated into broiler diets to enhance growth, improve feed conversion, and prevent disease. The growing concerns about antimicrobial resistance, food safety, and consumer health have led to the restriction or outright ban of AGPs in many countries [9]. This shift has prompted researchers and poultry producers to explore natural, safe, and effective alternatives to maintain performance and health in broilers. One alternative that has gained considerable attention is the use of herbal feed additives, natural substances derived from medicinal plants that are incorporated into poultry diets for their health-promoting properties [25]. Herbal additives, also known as botanicals or phytogenics, include a variety of plant based substances such as essential oils, leaves, seeds, roots, and bark. These substances are rich in bioactive compounds like polyphenols, flavonoids, saponins, and alkaloids, which possess multiple biological activities. Of particular importance in poultry nutrition is the ability of herbal additives to modulate lipid metabolism [18]. Various studies have shown that certain herbs can significantly influence blood lipid profiles in broilers by inhibiting hepatic cholesterol synthesis, enhancing lipid digestion, improving bile acid excretion, and altering lipid absorption at the intestinal level [14]. Well known herbal plants have demonstrated beneficial effects on lipid parameters in broilers. Garlic (Allium sativum) is widely recognized for its hypocholesterolemic properties, primarily due to its active compound allicin [1]. Garlic supplementation in broiler diets has been associated with reductions in total cholesterol, LDL, and triglycerides, alongside improvements in growth performance and meat quality. Turmeric (Curcuma longa), which contains the antioxidant curcumin, has been shown to support liver function and reduce lipid peroxidation, ultimately resulting in lower blood lipid levels and increased high-density lipoprotein (HDL) concentrations [23]. Another promising herbal additive is fenugreek (Trigonella foenum-graecum), whose seeds are high in saponins and dietary fiber, which help reduce intestinal cholesterol absorption. Research indicates that fenugreek supplementation can lower total cholesterol and triglyceride levels in broilers while enhancing feed efficiency [21]. Moringa oleifera, a nutrient-dense plant rich in polyphenols and antioxidants, has also demonstrated hypolipidemic effects in broilers, with significant reductions in serum cholesterol and triglycerides [2]. Ginger (Zingiber officinale), known for its digestive and anti-inflammatory properties, promotes bile secretion and has been linked to lower LDL and triglyceride levels in poultry [22]. In addition to the commonly studied herbs, several other botanicals are gaining attention for their lipid-lowering effects in poultry. Green tea (Camellia sinensis), which is high in catechins, has been shown to improve lipid profiles by reducing LDL and triglycerides while increasing HDL levels [26]. Cinnamon (Cinnamomum verum), known for its blood sugar-regulating properties, contains cinnamaldehyde and eugenol, which positively influence lipid metabolism. Thyme (Thymus vulgaris) and oregano (Origanum vulgare) are valued for their essential oils, particularly carvacrol and thymol, which enhance gut health and nutrient absorption, leading to improved lipid regulation [24]. Amla (Phyllanthus emblica), also known as Indian gooseberry, has demonstrated potent antioxidant activity and a strong ability in reducing total cholesterol and LDL levels in broilers. Holy basil (Ocimum sanctum), which contains eugenol and other phytochemicals, has shown potential to reduce lipid parameters and improve liver enzyme profiles in poultry [11]. Despite the growing list of herbal additives used in broiler production, many indigenous herbal plants remain unexplored. One such plant is Neorautanenia brachypus, a leguminous shrub native to sub Saharan Africa. The herb is traditionally used in ethnomedicine for its antimicrobial, antioxidant, and anti-inflammatory properties, N. brachypus contains several bioactive compounds including flavonoids, tannins, alkaloids, and glycosides. These phytochemicals are known for their antioxidant and lipid-modulating effects, making the plant a potential candidate for improving lipid metabolism in broilers [19]. However, scientific evidence regarding the use of N. brachypus in poultry diets is limited.

Materıals and methods

Animal handling ethical considerations

Ethical oversight for this broiler chicken study was provided by the National Animal Research Ethics Committee (NAREC) Reference Number NAREC/012/24, which granted full approval for all animal care and experimental protocols. These procedures were carried out strictly following the optimal welfare and husbandry practices described in the Irvine's Broiler Production Handbook [16]. The N. brachypus root tubers used for feed supplementation was sourced from the Bikita, Matsai area in the south-east lowveld of Zimbabwe. The N. brachypus tubers were authenticated at the National Herbarium under voucher specimen number MusekiwaF1.

Experimental design and treatments

The study was conducted using 400 day-old Cobb 500 broiler chicks, purchased from Irvines Inscor Africa (Pvt) Ltd [10]. These chicks were subsequently housed in a controlled deep litter system for the duration of the 35-day experimental period to track their growth performance. A Randomized Complete Block Design (RCBD) experimental design was employed. This design allocated the chicks into 20 experimental pens, with each pen serving as a replicate housing n = 20 chicks, distributed evenly among five distinct treatment groups, yielding four replicates per group.

Temperature management followed a systematic, gradual reduction schedule designed to meet the optimal thermoregulation needs of the growing broilers. The initial brooding temperature was established at 33ºC upon placement. This temperature was then progressively decreased by 3ºC per week until a stable maintenance temperature of 24ºC was achieved. Specifically, the temperature transitioned from 33ºC to 30ºC after the first week, from 30ºC to 27ºC after the second week, and finally settled at 24ºC from the third week onward. To maximise feeding activity and growth rates characteristic of early-stage broiler development, the chicks were provided with 24 h of continuous light throughout the duration of the study. Access to both feed and water was ensured ad libitum.

Experimental treatments and feeding regimen

The core of the study involved investigating the impact of Neorautanenia. brachypus supplementation in isolation and in combination with a standard vitamin-mineral premix. The vitamin-mineral premix (manufacturer: Hamish Cameron (Pvt) Ltd, a veterinary and nutritional specialist company based in Harare, Zimbabwe, was included at 0.5% of the total feed. The premix provided essential vitamins (A, D₃, E, K, B-complex) and minerals (Ca, P, Zn, Mn, Se, Cu, Fe, I). The N. brachypus root tubers used for feed supplementation was sourced from the Bikita, Matsai area in the south-east lowveld of Zimbabwe. The N. brachypus tubers were authenticated at the National Herbarium under voucher specimen number MusekiwaF1. The dried tubers were milled into raw powdered material, which was incorporated into the feed at 2 g/kg, and the same raw powdered tuber material was dissolved in drinking water at a concentration of 2 g/L for the water-soluble supplementation.

The five treatment groups (E0,E1,E2,E3, and E4) were strategically formulated to isolate the effect of each variable: E0 served as the Control, receiving only the basal diet; E1 tested the exclusive effect of the N. brachypus supplement mixed into the basal feed; E2 isolated the effect of the vitamin-mineral mix incorporated into the feed; E3 investigated the combined effect of a water-soluble N. brachypus form (in drinking water) and the vitamin-mineral mix (in feed); and E4 assessed the combined effect of N. brachypus and the vitamin-mineral mix when both were incorporated directly into the feed. To optimise nutrient delivery corresponding to the physiological needs of the chicks, a two-phase feeding program was implemented, consisting of a starter phase (0–21 days) followed by a finisher phase (22–35 days).

In adherence to established biosecurity and health management protocols, the control groups E0 (Basal Diet only) and E2 (vitamin-mineral mix only) which had zero inclusion of the test supplement, received mandatory vaccinations. This health intervention provided protection against critical avian diseases: chicks were vaccinated against Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD) on days 12 and 16, and against Newcastle Disease (ND) on day 21. This measure ensured that performance differences observed across the treatment groups were attributable to the dietary interventions and not confounded by the incidence of preventable diseases.

Subsequent to the growth performance a laboratory phase was conducted to evaluate the serum cholesterol and lipid profile of broiler chickens subjected to different dietary treatments. The experimental design allowed for a structured assessment of dietary effects on key lipid parameters using consistent sampling and analytical methods. On day 35 of age, blood samples were collected from broilers representing each dietary treatment group. A total of 15 birds (n = 3 per replicate and n = 12 treatment) were randomly selected from across both experimental blocks. Sampling was carried out between 08:00 and 10:00 h to reduce variability due to diurnal fluctuations in lipid metabolism. Blood was collected from the jugular vein using vacutainer serum separator tubes to facilitate serum separation. Samples were centrifuged at 3,000 revolutions per minute (rpm) for 15 min using a Hematocrit centrifuge (Model PKL PPC512). The separated serum was stored at −20 °C until laboratory analysis.

Broiler housing and management

The broiler chicks were housed under controlled environmental conditions typical of modern commercial broiler production. Brooding and housing conditions were carefully managed to provide a stable, welfare appropriate environment for optimal broiler growth and health. The birds were reared in an open sided poultry house designed to maximise natural ventilation through solid lower walls and adjustable side curtains, enabling effective moderation of heat, airflow, and moisture within the facility. Curtains were used to regulate airflow, thereby maintaining the desired internal temperature as well as preventing stressful environments, particularly during cooler nights. The poultry house was partitioned into 20 equal sized pens, each measuring 2.0m2 consistent with the requirements of the Randomised Complete Block Design (RCBD). These 20 pens accommodated the five treatment groups, with each group comprising four replicates with (n = 20) chicks per replicate.

The broilers were reared using the deep litter system, a standard commercial practice, pens were bedded with a 10 cm thick layer of clean, dry wheat straw. This material was selected for its excellent insulation properties, moisture absorption capacity, and the ability to maintain a stable microenvironment at the floor level, which is crucial for early chick health and comfort.

The provision of supplementary heat during the early stages of life was managed to meet the physiological requirements of the chicks. The house temperature was initially set at 33 °C on the day of placement and was gradually decreased by 3 °C per week. This progressive reduction simulated the chick's increasing ability to thermoregulate as they mature. The finisher phase temperature was maintained at a constant 24 °C from the point it was reached around on day 22 until the conclusion of the study. This temperature range is considered optimal for the growth and feed efficiency of finisher broilers.

The lighting regimen was designed to maximise feed intake and promote rapid early growth, crucial for the Cobb 500 genetic strain. 150 W infrared lamps were strategically positioned above the pens to serve the dual function of providing both supplementary heat during the brooding phase and continuous light. The lamps were operated 24 h a day throughout the entire study period. This constant light exposure was specifically implemented to encourage continuous feeding activity, which is essential for achieving optimal body weight gain and maximising growth potential during the initial and subsequent phases of broiler rearing.

Relative humidity (RH) between 50 to 70% was maintained throughout the growing period to prevent excessive litter moisture, respiratory stress, and elevated ammonia production. This optimal Relative Humidity range promoted balanced moisture levels and prevents wet litter as birds grow, dust levels and dehydration risk. Feed and water were provided ad libitum. The brooding and housing conditions reflected a controlled, welfare compliant production environment combining appropriate stocking density, thermoregulation, ventilation, litter management, lighting, and humidity targets. These measures collectively contributed to creating an optimal microclimate supportive of healthy growth, efficient nutrient utilisation, and reduced stress in broiler chickens.

Vaccination and health management

Maintaining the health and well-being of the experimental flock was paramount to the study's integrity, ensuring that any observed differences in performance were attributable solely to the dietary treatments rather than disease challenges. A strict, standardised health management and vaccination programme was implemented, particularly for the control groups.

According to established commercial poultry health production, a targeted vaccination schedule was followed to protect the broilers against the most common and economically significant avian diseases prevalent in the region. The schedule was applied to the groups that had zero inclusion of the N. brachypus extract (Groups E0 and E2), ensuring protection of the broilers’ health status.

A targeted vaccination programme was implemented to protect the control groups (E0 and E2) against two major viral diseases of economic significance in broiler production; Infectious Bursal Disease (IBD) and Newcastle Disease (ND). Nobilis® Gumboro D78 for IBD and Nobilis® ND Clone 30 for Newcastle Disease vaccines were both administered at the manufacturer’s (Intervet/MSD Animal Health, Netherlands) recommended dose and guidelines while utilising Fivet Animal Health, Zimbabwe as the local source of vaccine procurement, providing an effective and ethically sound disease prevention, protecting flock health and preserving the integrity of the experimental outcomes.

The first vaccine, Nobilis® Gumboro D78, is a live, freeze-dried intermediate vaccine designed for active immunisation of chickens against IBD. It contains the D78 strain of Gumboro virus and is recommended in the national poultry vaccination guidelines for protection against bursal immunosuppression. The manufacturer (Intervet/MSD) specifies that the vaccine can be administered through the drinking water, coarse spray, or intranasal/eye-drop routes. However, in this study, administration was through the drinking water, following standard water vaccination procedures widely used in Zimbabwe. Uptake of water was withheld for two hours before vaccination, allowing the birds to develop sufficient thirst. The freeze dried vaccine was dissolved in cool, clean, chlorine free water, in accordance with the manufacturer’s recommendations, thereby protecting the viral titre from chemical degradation. Chickens were then allowed to consume the medicated water, which was fully taken up within the recommended two hour window. Vaccination against infectious bursal disease with Gumboro D78 was carried out on Day 12 and Day 16, providing timely protection during the period when birds are most vulnerable to bursal damage and immunosuppression.

The second vaccine administered Nobilis® ND Clone 30, also a live, freeze dried vaccine against Newcastle Disease was administered via drinking water on Day 21, in accordance with the national broiler vaccination programme and manufacturer guidelines. The preparation procedure mirrored that of the Gumboro vaccine where n this case, the most suitable option was to use two 200-dose vials, providing a total of 400 doses, exactly equivalent to the number of biroilers. The birds were conditioned to drink the medicated water quickly and uniformly, access to ordinary drinking water was withdrawn for two hours before vaccination. The water restriction made the broilers slightly thirsty without causing stress, encouraging them to consume the vaccinated water rapidly once offered.

At 14 days of age, the recommended volume of water for vaccination is 20 to 30 L per 1000 birds. For a flock of 400 broilers, this translated to roughly 8 to12 liters of water. To preserve the viability of the live vaccine virus, skim milk vaccine stabiliser was added to the water before the vaccine is introduced at 2 g per liter of water. to protect the vaccine virus from chlorine, metal ions, and other substances that may be present in drinking water and that could potentially inactivate the vaccine. The skim milk was thoroughly mixed into the water to create a protective medium before the vaccine vials are opened. The two 200-dose vaccine vials were opened carefully under water to prevent the loss of any live organisms, and gently, mixed. The medicated water was then dispensed evenly into all cleaned and dried drinkers in the poultry house.

Continuous health monitoring was conducted across all experimental groups (E0-E4) throughout the study. All pens were inspected multiple times daily by experienced personnel for signs of illness, listlessness, altered gait, or abnormalities. Strict biosecurity measures were maintained within the facility to prevent the introduction of pathogens. This included foot dips, controlled access to the pens, and the use of dedicated protective clothing. Any chicks exhibiting severe disease symptoms or distress were immediately, isolated, under the supervision of a veterinarian, following to minimise suffering and prevent the spread of potential infection. The strict health management regime ensured that the observed effects of N. brachypus were evaluated under conditions of minimal disease challenge.

Feeding program and nutritional management

The nutritional management of the experimental flock was executed through a two phase feeding programme, designed to meet the changing nutrient demands of the broiler chicks during their rapid growth while serving as the primary delivery mechanism for the experimental treatments. The 35 day study period was divided into two distinct feeding phases to optimise growth performance. This strategic approach ensured that the concentration of energy, protein, and amino acids aligned closely with the physiological capacity and requirement of the birds at different stages of development:

Starter Phase (Day 0 to 21) the initial phase is characterised by rapid growth, particularly skeletal and muscular development. The starter diet was formulated to be highly nutrient dense, levels of crude protein and essential amino acids Lysine and Methionine to support maximum early weight gain and muscle deposition. This foundation is critical for setting the overall growth performance of broilers.

Finisher Phase (Day 22 to 35); the finisher phase focused on efficient energy deposition and carcass quality. The diet transitioned to a slightly lower crude protein and amino acid content, offset by an increased energy to protein ratio. This formulation promotes the efficient laying down of fat and ensures that the final carcass characteristics are optimized prior to market age.

Neorautanenia brachypus preparation and processing

The authenticated root tubers were thoroughly washed with clean water to remove soil and adhering debris. They were then manually cut into smaller pieces to facilitate efficient drying. The sliced material was subjected to shade-drying for 20 days at ambient temperatures approximately 18 °C to 22 °C and humidity range of 56% to 73% in a sheltered, well-ventilated area protected from direct sunlight before milling into powdered material. Drying was carried out under sheltered, indirect sunlight conditions to avoid direct solar radiation and minimise thermal degradation of heat sensitive phytochemicals. Once completely dry and brittle, the N. brachypus dried pieces were milled using a small scale SLSY 220 V Electric Grinding Mill Machine (3000W; Shanghai ShangQuan Wuliu Co., Ltd., China) fitted with a 2 mm mesh screen to ensure a consistent, fine particle size, guaranteeing maximum surface area for efficient subsequent solvent extraction. The resulting fine powder was stored in a cool dark, dry container until the procedure commenced.

Diet formulation and delivery

The basal diets for both the starter and finisher phases were formulated using conventional, readily available feed ingredients, primarily maize and soybean meal to meet the nutritional requirements recommended in the Irvines Poultry Production Handbook. The formulation strategy ensured that maize served as the principal energy source, while soybean meal contributed the majority of dietary crude protein. Additional key nutrients, including supplemental fat from sunflower oil, structural fiber, calcium, phosphorus, lysine, and a vitamin–mineral premix, were incorporated to achieve balanced nutrient profiles across all diets. The inclusion of these nutrients was carefully standardized, and the complete set of ingredient inclusion levels is presented in Table 1, providing a clear overview of the nutrient supply across treatments.

Neorautanenia brachypus was included exclusively in the experimental diets E1, E3, and E4, with the form of inclusion (either as a feed additive in kg of feed or as a water-soluble supplement in g/L) varying according to the treatment design. The control diet (E0) and the premix-only diet (E2) contained no N. brachypus. All diets were formulated to remain isocaloric and isonitrogenous, ensuring that any observed physiological or metabolic effects could be attributed to the inclusion of N. brachypus and the vitamin–mineral premix, rather than differences in basal nutrient density.

To confirm that the formulated diets met targeted nutrient specifications, the proximate composition of the basal starter and finisher diets was analytically determined. This verification step ensured nutrient adequacy, consistency, and alignment with recommended broiler nutrient profiles. Laboratory analysis included determination of crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, calcium, available and total phosphorus, amino acids, moisture, and ash using standardized AOAC (2005) laboratory procedures [8]. The analyzed nutrient values for crude protein, crude fat, crude fiber, and mineral fractions are presented alongside formulation values in Table 1, illustrating consistency between the calculated and analyzed composition of the diets.

Metabolisable energy (ME) was estimated using established predictive equations based on the chemically determined proximate components. This approach is widely used in poultry nutrition to provide accurate ME estimates in the absence of direct calorimetric testing. Total phosphorus values in included both available and unavailable fractions. Available phosphorus was derived using standard digestibility coefficients for di-calcium phosphate (DCP), considering that phytase was not included in the basal diets. Similarly, amino acid values reflect laboratory-determined total amino acids, from which standardized ileal digestible (SID) values were estimated using previously published digestibility coefficients applicable to soybean meal-based diets. To support optimal growth and skeletal development, the calcium-to-phosphorus ratio was carefully adjusted to maintain the recommended Ca:P balance for broilers.

The vitamin–mineral premix used in this study was sourced from Hamish Cameron (Pvt) Ltd, Zimbabwe, and incorporated at an inclusion rate of 0.05% of the complete feed, as outlined in Table 1 below. This inclusion level aligns with manufacturer recommendations for broiler starter and finisher phases and ensures adequate dietary supply of essential micronutrients, including vitamins A, D₃, E, K, and B-complex vitamins, along with key trace minerals such as manganese, zinc, copper, iodine, and selenium. No additional antioxidants were included in the vitamin-mineral premix.

Euthanasia and necropsy procedures

On day-35 of the trial broilers were humanely slaughtered using a two-step method to ensure immediate, irreversible unconsciousness, adhering to the highest ethical and research standards. The procedure began with placing each bird in a cone for calm restraint, followed instantly by Primary Stunning using a non-penetrating captive bolt delivered to the cranial midline. This delivered a concussive shock, causing cerebral trauma and achieving instantaneous insensibility. Immediately thereafter, the Secondary Killing step was performed: exsanguination via a complete bilateral severance of the neck vessels. This rapid, massive blood loss ensured death occurred before the bird could recover from the stun. Death was formally confirmed by the cessation of all vital reflexes and movements for a minimum of two minutes, ensuring the procedure was both effective and humane. No injectables nor chemicals were applied in the euthanasia protocol.

Laboratory analysis

The assessment of the serum lipid profile was conducted using standard enzymatic colorimetric methods, alongside commercially available diagnostic kits, to ensure precision, reproducibility, and consistency in the results. The lipid parameters evaluated included very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL), low-density lipoprotein (LDL), high-density lipoprotein (HDL), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG). These parameters are essential indicators of lipid metabolism and were analyzed to determine the effects of the dietary treatments on the lipid status of the broiler chickens.

All biochemical analyses were carried out using an automated biochemical analyzer (Model BS-200E, 2015), which provided efficient and accurate quantification of the lipid components.

Statistical analysis

Data were first tested for normality and analysed using the General Linear Model (GLM) procedure in SAS ver. 9.4. Least square means (LSMEAN ± standard error) were computed for each treatment group to summarize the central tendency and variability. To determine significant differences among treatment means, Turkey’s Honest Significant Difference (HSD) test was applied. Statistical significance was established at a p < 0.05. Results and dıscussıon

Results

This study assessed how dietary inclusion of N. brachypus powder, alone or in combination with a vitamin-mineral premix, influenced serum levels of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) and low density lipoprotein (LDL) in broiler chickens. All experimental groups were exposed to consistent housing and feeding conditions, with treatments being the sole variable. The least square means (LSMEAN) ± standard error values were statistically analysed, with a significance level set at P < 0.05.

Effects of dietary treatments on Very Low Density Lipoprotein (VLDL) and Low Density Lipoprotein (LDL) concentrations in broiler chickens

Serum very low density lipoprotein concentrations

There were statistically significant differences in VLDL concentrations across all treatment groups (P < 0.0001). Broilers in the control group (E0), which received a basal diet without any additives, exhibited the highest VLDL value (42.1 ± 0.98 mg/dL). Broilers in group E1, receiving 2 g/kg of N. brachypus in their feed, had significantly reduced VLDL levels (36.7 ± 0.98 mg/dL), indicating a favorable effect on lipid metabolism. Experimental group E2, supplemented with only the vitamin-mineral premix, showed VLDL concentrations (42.1 ± 0.98 mg/dL) comparable to the control group, suggesting that micronutrient addition alone did not significantly affect circulating VLDL levels.

Both administration routes of N. brachypus effectively reduced VLDL concentrations, with the water based delivery potentially offering a slightly enhanced effect. All treatments involving N. brachypus demonstrated statistically significant reductions in VLDL when compared to the control and vitamin- mineral only groups, with low standard error values reinforcing data precision and consistency.

Serum low density lipoprotein concentrations

Significant treatment effects were also evident for LDL levels (P < 0.0001). The control group (E0) exhibited the highest LDL concentration (56.8 ± 0.90 mg/dL), a value close to the observed results in group E2 (57.3 ± 0.90 mg/dL), which received only the vitamin-mineral premix. These results indicated that vitamin-mineral supplementation alone did not modify LDL levels. The broilers receiving 2 g N. brachypus either alone (E1) or in combination with the vitamin-mineral premix (E3 and E4) displayed remarkably lower LDL values. Group E1 (2 g/kg N. brachypus in feed) recorded an LDL concentration of 18.9 ± 0.90 mg/dL, representing a reduction of over 65% compared to the control. Group E3 (2 g/L N. brachypus in water + premix) and E4 (N. brachypus + premix in feed) exhibited nearly identical LDL values of 18.6 ± 0.90 mg/dL and 18.5 ± 0.90 mg/dL, respectively as indicated in Table 2.

High-Density Lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol

High-density Lipoprotein (HDL) concentrations were significantly influenced by N. brachypus supplementation. There was a remarkable increase in serum HDL levels. The control group (E0) had an HDL LSMEAN of 86.6 ± 1.72 mg/dL, whereas the vitamin-mineral premix group (E2) exhibited a slightly lower value of 84.3 ± 1.72 mg/dL. This minimal change was statistically non-significant, indicating that vitamin-mineral supplementation alone did not exert a meaningful effect on HDL levels.

N. brachypus treated groups exhibited significant increases in HDL cholesterol. Group E1 demonstrated a LSMEAN of 107.9 ± 1.72 mg/dL and a p-value < 0.0001, indicating high statistical significance. Group E3 showed an even greater elevation, with a LSMEAN of (111.3 ± 1.72 mg/, p < 0.0001), while group E4 displayed the highest HDL concentration of 112.49 ± 1.72 mg/dL, p < 0.0001).

The supplementation of N. brachypus at 2 g/kg in feed or 2 g/L in water led to a highly significant reduction in total cholesterol (by up to 65.9 ± 1.72 mg/dL, p < 0.0001) and a significant elevation in HDL cholesterol (by up to 25.9 ± 1.72 mg/dL, p < 0.0001) in broiler chickens. These changes were not observed in the groups not receiving N. brachypus, demonstrating that the lipid-modulating effects are attributable to N. brachypus specifically.

Total Cholesterol (TC) profile

Total cholesterol concentrations were significantly affected by N. brachypus inclusion. The control group (E0), which received a standard basal diet, exhibited a least squares mean (LSMEAN) of 203.8 ± 3.98 mg/dL, while the vitamin/mineral group (E2) showed a statistically indistinguishable LSMEAN of 204.7 ± 3.98 mg/dL. The biological effect (BE) between E2 and E0 was only + 0.95 mg/dL, indicating no meaningful physiological or statistical difference between these two groups. In contrast, the groups receiving N. brachypus exhibited substantial and statistically significant reductions in total cholesterol. Group E1 (2 g/kg N. brachypus in feed) presented a LSMEAN of 140.3 ± 3.98 mg/dL, a biological effect of −63.48 mg/L relative to E0, and a p-value < 0.0001. Group E3 (2 g/L N. brachypus in drinking water + vitamin-mineral premix) exhibited the lowest total cholesterol at 137.9 ± 3.98 mg/dL.

Group E4, which received N. brachypus (2 g/kg) along with a vitamin-mineral premix in feed, had a LSMEAN of (140.5 ± 3.98 mg/dL, p < 0.0001). The comparable outcomes of E1 and E4 suggested that co-supplementation with vitamins and minerals did not enhance or reduce the cholesterol-lowering efficacy of N. brachypus. The statistical significance across all treatment groups (p < 0.0001) reinforces the reliability of the observed differences.

Discussion

The study explored the effects of N. brachypus supplementation alone or in combination with a vitamin-mineral premix on lipid metabolism in broiler chickens. The results demonstrated that N. brachypus significantly influenced the lipid profile by reducing atherogenic lipoproteins (VLDL, LDL), total cholesterol (TC), and triglycerides (TG), while simultaneously increasing high-density lipoprotein (HDL) levels. These outcomes support its potential as a bioactive feed additive with comprehensive lipid-regulating capabilities, similar to other phytogenic compounds previously studied in poultry nutrition [4].

Broilers supplemented with N. brachypus (groups E1, E3, and E4) exhibited significant reductions in serum VLDL levels compared to both the control (E0) and the vitamin/mineral premix group (E2). The most notable decrease occurred in experimental group E3, where N. brachypus was administered in drinking water, suggesting increased bioavailability and absorption through this route [15]. In contrast, unchanged VLDL levels in E2 highlight that the lipid-lowering effect is specific to N. brachypus, and not a result of micronutrient enrichment alone.

A parallel trend was observed for LDL levels. Groups receiving N. brachypus showed marked reductions over 65% lower than control values with LDL concentrations between 18.6 and18.9 mg/dL. These results confirm an antiatherogenic effect, independent of supportive micronutrient action [3]. Similar effects have been reported in broilers supplemented with plant based additives rich in polyphenols and saponins [13].

Total cholesterol levels followed patterns seen in VLDL and LDL, with the highest values in E0 and E2 (204 mg/dL) and the lowest in E3. This suggests a potential advantage of water-based delivery, likely due to enhanced absorption dynamics [7]. However, since similar cholesterol-lowering effects were noted in E1 and E4, the active compound in N. brachypus appears to be the primary contributor regardless of delivery method.

In contrast to its suppressive effect on TC, N. brachypus supplementation significantly elevated HDL levels. Group E4 recorded the highest HDL values, with E3 and E1 also demonstrating increases compared to control. As HDL plays a critical role in reverse cholesterol transport and cardiovascular protection, this increase indicates a shift toward a more favorable lipid profile [12]. The inverse relationship between total cholesterol and HDL further reflects the regulatory impact of N. brachypus on lipid homeostasis. Triglyceride levels were significantly lower in the N. brachypus-supplemented groups compared to both the control and the vitamin/mineral-only group. TG values in E1, E3, and E4 ranging from 134 to135 mg/dL, reinforcing the hypolipidemic potential of the supplement. The presence of bioactive compounds such as phytosterols, polyphenols, and saponins may cause this effect, possibly by modulating intestinal lipid absorption and enhancing metabolic clearance [6].

The ability of N. brachypus to simultaneously lower atherogenic lipids and elevate HDL suggests multifaceted mechanisms of action, possibly involving hepatic lipid regulation, improved transport dynamics, and enhanced gut metabolism [17]. Unlike conventional lipid-lowering supplements that typically act via a single pathway, N. brachypus seems to offer a broader mode of action. The minimal effects seen in E2 across all lipid parameters show the unique activity of N. brachypus, rather than generic micronutrient influence. The comparable efficacy of feed and water based administration also offers practical flexibility for poultry operations.

Conclusion

Neorautanenia brachypus supplementation significantly improved the lipid profile of broiler chickens by reducing VLDL, LDL, total cholesterol, and triglycerides, while increasing HDL levels. Water-based delivery showed a slight advantage, but all forms were effective. These findings highlight N. brachypus as a promising natural feed additive for enhancing cardiovascular health in poultry. The promising effects of N. brachypus on the lipid modulation in broiler chickens, it becomes imperative to explore its broader implications on meat quality, particularly during storage. Microbial stability and pH are critical parameters influencing shelf-life, safety, and organoleptic properties of poultry meat. The antimicrobial potential of phytogenic feed additives, such as N. brachypus, may contribute to reducing microbial load in meat products, thereby extending their usability and enhancing consumer safety.
