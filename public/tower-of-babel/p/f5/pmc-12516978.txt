Association of anthropometry indices and metabolic syndrome in Iranians: a cross-sectional study of the Fasa adult cohort study
Authors: Maryam Seyhoon, Afsane Ahmadi, Morteza Zare, Mahdi Honardoust, Najmeh Hejazi, Mohammad Ali Mohsenpour, R Homayounfar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate the associations between anthropometric indices and components of metabolic syndrome (MetS), including blood pressure, fasting blood sugar (FBS), triglycerides, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol and waist circumference (WC) in Iranian adults.DesignCross-sectional analysis of baseline data from a population-based cohort.SettingFasa adults’ cohort study, a rural community-based cohort in Fars province, Iran.ParticipantsA total of 1550 adults aged 35–70 years with MetS, identified from among 10 118 cohort participants using the National Cholesterol Education Programme Adult Treatment Programme III criteria.Primary outcome measuresThe anthropometric indices include abdominal volume index (AVI), a body shape index (ABSI), atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), body roundness index (BRI), body adiposity index (BAI), conicity index, ponderal index and visceral adiposity index (VAI).ResultsParticipants (56.1% female) with a mean age of 49.8±9.5 years. AVI was significantly associated with systolic blood pressure (SBP) (β=0.010, p<0.001) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) (β=0.031, p<0.001). BAI was linked to FBS (β=0.053, p<0.001), DBP (β=0.006, p=0.022) and WC (β=0.001, p=0.034). BRI was positively associated with FBS (β=0.001, p=0.049), SBP (β=0.006, p=0.006) and DBP (β=0.023, p<0.001). AIP was significantly correlated with FBS (β=0.002, p=0.003), SBP (β=0.017, p<0.001) and DBP (β=0.030, p<0.001). VAI was associated with SBP and DBP in both men and women (eg, DBP in men: β=0.023, p<0.001). ABSI showed a negative association with SBP (β=–0.006, p=0.015).ConclusionsAnthropometric indices, including VAI, AIP, BAI, BRI and AVI, exhibit significant associations with key components of MetS in Iranian adults, particularly blood pressure, glycaemic markers and central adiposity. Among these, BAI showed the strongest correlation with MetS parameters, while ABSI displayed the weakest.Keywords: Hypertension, Blood Pressure, Body Mass Index

Introduction

One of the major concerns in global health is metabolic syndrome (MetS). According to the criteria defined by the National Cholesterol Education Programme Adult Treatment Programme III (NCEP-ATP III), the simultaneous presence of various metabolic abnormalities, such as hypertension, dyslipidaemia, insulin resistance and central obesity, is the characteristic of this syndrome.1 MetS prevalence is growing around the world.2 The MetS prevalence in Iran is 0.26, and the incidence is 97.96 per 1000.3 Metabolic risk factors can largely affect chronic diseases, particularly cardiovascular diseases and diabetes. These factors have a crucial role in increasing the prevalence and severity of these conditions.4 It has been indicated that individuals diagnosed with MetS experience a significantly higher mortality rate compared with those who do not have MetS.5 The impact of central obesity, body fat and their effect on the development of MetS is considered a significant challenge in clinical research.6

Anthropometric measurements are commonly acknowledged as non-invasive, cost-efficient and uncomplicated methods that can efficiently evaluate body fat distribution, general health status and potential risk of developing diseases.7 8 Frequently used anthropometric measurements encompass hip circumference (HC), waist-to-hip ratio, waist circumference (WC) and body mass index (BMI).9 Nevertheless, there is still debate over whether routine anthropometric measurements alone can adequately assess and assist in the risk management of MetS.10 Additionally, despite the incorporation of indices such as body adiposity index (BAI) in epidemiological studies, their utilisation in clinical practice remains limited, and the association between these indicators and MetS has not been definitively established.11

An increase in central adipose tissue is an early warning sign for the initiation of MetS and deterioration of its components. Thus, a careful examination of body measurements using anthropometric indices, including BAI,12 conicity index (C-Index)13 and a body shape index (ABSI)14 might be an effective clinical preventive approach.10 Studies have indicated that these unique indicators can provide a more precise risk assessment of diseases correlated to body fat accumulation and obesity. This is due to their increased sensitivity and specificity compared with conventional anthropometric measurements.15 16

Although the relationship between high visceral fat mass and MetS is well established globally, Iranian research has primarily relied on traditional measures such as BMI and WC, which may fail to capture regional variations in fat distribution or obesity-related risks across Iran’s diverse groups. Within Iran, anthropometric research using a comprehensive set of body fat indices is limited. In addition, no study has applied multiple indices using data from the Fasa adult cohort study (FACS)17 as the representative of the southwestern population of Iran. Moreover, the relation between latent body composition profiles and metabolic disorders was assessed previously, but the possible association between indices such as abdominal volume index (AVI), body roundness index (BRI), BAI, visceral adiposity index (VAI), ponderal index (PI) and ABSI and MetS has not been explored. Therefore, the current research examined the correlation between anthropometric indices, including ABSI, AVI, BRI, BAI, atherogenic index of plasma (AIP), C-Index, PI and VAI, with MetS components in Iranian adults.

Material and methods

Participants’ selection and description

This cross-sectional study assessed the subjects’ baseline characteristics included in FACS. It was a longitudinal population-based cohort survey planned for a 15-year follow-up duration. The research methodology employed in this study was a census sampling approach. The FACS consisted of 10 118 subjects aged 35–70 years living in Fasa, Fars province, southwest of Iran.18 The FACS was a part of Prospective Epidemiological Research Studies in Iran (the PERSIAN Cohort Study).19 Shiraz University of Medical Sciences approved the research under the approval code of IR.SUMS.REC.1399.1262. All the subjects gave their informed consent before the study.

FACS participants were eligible if they were Iranian, aged 35–70 at the baseline assessments of FACS, resided in the study area for more than 1 year, could communicate verbally and suffered from MetS based on the NCEP-ATP III definition. On the other hand, if any participants withdrew from the study, took any medications to control blood glucose, lipid profile or blood pressure, had incomplete data, used herbal supplements or weight loss medications, suffered from chronic diseases or were pregnant, they were excluded from the study.

Demographic characteristics

The FACS database contained demographic variables, including marital status, gender, age, physical activity, energy and race.

Metabolic syndrome

In compliance with NCEP-ATP III criteria, participants with at least three abnormalities were considered as MetS patients, including fasting blood glucose (FBS) (>100 mg/dL), increased WC (>88 cm or >102 cm for women and men, respectively), triglycerides (TG) (>150 mg/dL), reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL) (<40 mg/dL for men or <50 mg/dL for women) and hypertension (diastolic blood pressure (DBP) over 85 mm Hg or systolic blood pressure (SBP) over 130 mm Hg).20

Blood biochemical and blood pressure measurements

The participants’ fasting blood samples were collected after 10 to 14 hours of fasting. The sampling process was carried out by experienced sampling personnel using advanced tools and stored at −70°C after serum separation. The participants’ FBS, HDL and TG levels were extracted from the FACS database. The detailed blood biochemical measurement was described previously.18 For FACS, blood pressure was accurately measured and documented by expert nurses from both arms sequentially with a 15 min time interval.18

Anthropometric measurements and indices

The participants’ height was assessed without shoes using a stadiometer with an accuracy of 0.1 cm. Weight and body composition and weight were recorded by the Bioelectrical Impedance Analysis technique (Tanita BC-418, Tanita Corp, Japan) following the standard procedure. WC (the narrowest part of the abdomen between the last rib and the iliac bone) and HP (at the widest part of the hips without applying pressure) were assessed using a non-stretch measuring tape with an accuracy of 0.1 cm in line with the FACS protocol. These procedures were implemented uniformly to minimise measurement error and ensure data reliability.18

Patient and public involvement

No patients or members of the public were formally involved in setting the research questions, selecting outcome measures or shaping the study design. On publication, we will share plain-language summaries of the findings. With all participants via the local health centres, present results at community meetings and post open-access infographics on the non-communicable diseases research centre website.

Statistical analyses

Data analyses were done using SPSS V.21 software (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA). Quantitative data were summarised by the mean and SD. A univariate regression model was conducted to find the correlation between anthropometric indices and MetS. To address the potential confounding factors, we added significant level variables (below 0.2) in the univariate regression model in the multivariate regression analysis to determine the combined impact of these variables. Physical activity, energy intake, sex and age were adjusted in multivariate regression. A p value <0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Of the 10 118 adults enrolled in the FACS, we first excluded 1377 individuals who reported taking any blood sugar-lowering, blood pressure-lowering or blood lipid-lowering medications to avoid confounding effects from pharmacological treatment on MetS components. No intake of herbal supplements or weight-loss medications was reported. From the remaining 8741 participants, we identified cases of MetS according to the NCEP ATP III and excluded 7191 participants who did not meet the criteria for MetS. ABSI was inversely associated with SBP (β=– 0.006, p=0.015). Clinically, this means that for every 1 mm Hg increase in SBP, the ABSI decreases by approximately 0.6%, suggesting an inverse relationship between BSI and vascular load. AVI exhibited a strong positive association with both SBP (β=0.010, p<0.001) and DBP (β=0.031, p<0.001). Interpreted clinically, each 1 mm Hg rise in SBP corresponds to a 1% increase in AVI, while a 1 mm Hg rise in DBP corresponds to a 3.1% increase in AVI, highlighting its sensitivity to diastolic pressure and central adiposity. The regression coefficient suggests that the strongest association existed between AVI and DBP. BAI demonstrated significant correlations with fasting blood sugar (FBS) (β=0.053, p<0.001), WC (β=0.001, p=0.034) and DBP (β=0.006, p=0.022). Clinically, for every 1 mg/dL rise in FBS, BAI increases by approximately 5.3%, suggesting that fat distribution measured by BAI is strongly associated with dysglycaemia and insulin resistance in MetS. The positive association with DBP (0.6% increase per 1 mm Hg) indicates that BAI may also reflect early vascular alterations linked to fat accumulation patterns. These findings highlight BAI as a dual marker for both glycaemic control and blood pressure regulation. Notably, the regression coefficient analysis revealed the strongest correlation between BAI and FBS.

BRI was significantly associated with DBP (β=0.023, p<0.001), SBP (β=0.006, p=0.006) and FBS (β=0.001, p=0.049). Each 1 mm Hg increase in DBP was associated with a 2.3% rise in BRI, suggesting that body roundness, as measured by this index, is particularly sensitive to diastolic pressure. The link with FBS, although smaller in magnitude, supports the role of BRI in reflecting both vascular and glycaemic risk factors. Based on the regression coefficient, BRI had the highest correlation with DBP.

C-Index exhibited significant positive associations with SBP (β=0.008, p<0.001) and DBP (β=0.017, p<0.001). In clinical terms, each 1 mm Hg increase in DBP corresponds to an approximate 0.17% rise in C-Index, underscoring its sensitivity to blood pressure and its potential utility in capturing central adiposity–vascular interactions in MetS. Analysis of the regression coefficient revealed that C-Index exhibited the strongest correlation with DBP.

On the other hand, PI was strongly associated with WC (β=0.071, p<0.001). For every 1 cm increase in WC, PI rises by about 7.1%. This large effect size highlights PI as a powerful index for central adiposity, with potential applications in early identification of abdominal fat–driven metabolic risk.

Table 4 demonstrates the relationship between AIP, VAI and MetS components. As shown, AIP showed significant positive associations with DBP (β=0.030, p<0.001), SBP (β=0.017, p<0.001) and FBS (β=0.002, p=0.003). Clinically, each 1 mm Hg increase in DBP was associated with a 3% rise in AIP, linking this lipid-based index strongly to blood pressure dysregulation and cardiometabolic risk. The relationship with FBS highlights the interplay between lipid metabolism and glycaemic control in MetS. The analysis of regression coefficients highlighted that the most prominent association was found between VAI and DBP.

VAI demonstrated significant positive associations with SBP and DBP in both sexes. In men, VAI was associated with SBP (β=0.011, p<0.001) and DBP (β=0.023, p<0.001), while in women the associations were SBP (β=0.011, p<0.001) and DBP (β=0.022, p<0.001). Clinically, this indicates that for every 1 mm Hg increase in DBP, VAI rises by approximately 2.3% in men and 2.2% in women. The effect sizes were slightly higher in men, suggesting that visceral adiposity may contribute more prominently to blood pressure regulation and vascular risk in males. These sex-specific differences support the consideration of gender when applying VAI for MetS risk stratification, as men may experience stronger cardiometabolic effects from similar degrees of visceral fat accumulation compared with women.

Discussion

This cross-sectional research included the data of 1550 participants from FACS and sought to find the link between MetS components and anthropometric indices in Iranian adults. There was a strong positive relationship between AVI and DBP. The AIP exhibited a significant correlation with FBS, as well as SBP and DBP. Additionally, there was a significant association between BAI and FBS, WC and DBP. Similarly, BRI displayed a significant correlation with FBS, SBP and DBP. The C-Index demonstrated a significant association with SBP and DBP. Furthermore, PI showed a significant correlation with the waist size, while VAI showed a significant association with SBP and DBP. Notably, there was a meaningful inverse correlation between ABSI and SBP.

Anthropometric assessments are crucial in clinical practice to diagnose obesity.27 The fat accumulation in the abdominal area and around the viscera was observed in abdominal obesity or central obesity.28 Conventional anthropometric measurements are considered simple indicators of obesity, consisting of height, weight, WC and HC.29 Previous studies have indicated that measures such as BAI, BMI and ABSI could potentially be linked to predicting long-term cardiometabolic risk.16 30 31 Krakauer and Krakauer14 put forward a new anthropometric index called ABSI in 2012. The aim of this index is to provide a more precise investigation of the association between WC, central obesity and clinical consequences. In the current research, there was a link between ABSI and SBP. Meanwhile, in comparison with the results of the current investigation, in the study by Bertolli et al on 6081 Caucasian adults, they found a positive connection between ABSI and serum TG levels and FBS. They also indicated a negative association with HDL levels. Notably, their study did not reveal any significant connection between ABSI and blood pressure.32

The AVI serves as an anthropometric measure for assessing the overall volume of the waist. Studies have indicated a strong correlation between this index and disorders in glucose metabolism.33 This study emphasises a clear and significant association between AVI and independent variables of SBP and DBP. Guerrero-Romero and Rodríguez-Morán22 stated a positive link between AVI and the development of diabetes and glucose intolerance, which contrasts with the present research results. In a study on Spanish adults diagnosed with MetS, a meaningful positive relationship was identified between AVI and WC among the participants.33 Ghannadiasl and Hoseini34 also observed a direct connection between AVI and WC among female participants seeking services at Ardabil nutrition clinic and expressing dissatisfaction with their body image. An inverse association was demonstrated by Arjmand et al35 between AVI and HDL levels.

The AIP serves as a reliable indicator of lipid levels, adiposity and the presence of cardiovascular conditions.36 Rabiee et al37 revealed a meaningful and noteworthy connection between the AIP and three independent variables, including FBS, SBP and DBP. The research aimed to examine the link between AIP and lipid profile, as well as atherosclerotic index, in two distinct groups: individuals with coronary artery disease and a healthy control group. They reported a positive connection between elevated levels of AIP and the likelihood of developing coronary artery disease. These findings are consistent with the outcomes observed in the present research. According to the results of the current investigation, Shen et al38 found that individuals with abdominal obesity exhibited significantly elevated levels of AIP compared with those without abdominal obesity. The researchers noted a positive association between PAI and various factors, including total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein levels (LDLs), SBP, DBP, BMI, FBS and serum uric acid. Furthermore, a direct correlation was revealed between AIP and WC. Specifically, each centimetre increase in WC corresponded to a 0.0175 increase in the AIP.

The current research demonstrates a clear and statistically significant correlation between BAI and various independent variables, including FBS, WC and DBP. Quaye et al39 conducted a study to evaluate the predictive value of obesity anthropometric indicators for MetS in healthy Ghanaian adults. Their findings revealed a positive relationship among BAI and WC, HDL, total cholesterol, TG and SBP. The observed relationship with DBP aligns with the present study results. A cross-sectional study that studied individuals with type 2 diabetes revealed a positive correlation between BAI, FBS, WC and haemoglobin A1C. The results were in line with the current study.40

The present study establishes a direct and significant link between the BRI and independent variables of FBS, SBP and DBP. In line with this investigation, a study examined the predictive capacity of BRI concerning MetS among 1518 healthy individuals (952 women and 566 men) from Peru. The study results showed a positive connection between BRI levels and blood pressure, fasting glucose and TG, while revealing an inverse link between HDL and BMI levels.41 Nkwana et al42 observed a significant and positive connection between BRI and LDL levels in a study on rural youth in South Africa, which is not in line with the current study.

The C-Index is recognised as a significant clinical measure used to assess obesity and body fat distribution.13 This index comprises height, weight and WC variables, assuming that individuals with greater abdominal fat exhibit a conical body shape, whereas those with less central obesity may possess a cylindrical body shape.13 43 In the present investigation, a direct association was found between the C-Index and independent variables of SBP and DBP. Previous studies have revealed similar data, indicating a positive relationship between C-Index and FBS, SBP and TG. In contrast to the current study, a study showed an inverse relationship between C-Index and HDL levels. Researchers have identified a direct link between C-Index and the incidence of dyslipidaemia and insulin resistance.44,47 Nonetheless, Zhang et al48 state that BMI is not a superior anthropometric factor for evaluating the risk of MetS compared with waist-to-height ratio, WC and BRI.

In the current research, there was a direct and meaningful correlation between PI and the independent variable of WC. Consistent with this study, a previous study on 8464 individuals aged 10–20 years revealed a positive relationship between PI and WC, as well as with SBP and DBP, TG levels, FBS, low-density lipoprotein and total cholesterol. Conversely, there was a negative connection between PI and HDL levels. Researchers concluded that PI, when adjusted for age and gender, could be used as a screening tool in clinical settings.49

The current study reveals a significant and direct relationship between VAI in both sexes and variables of SBP and DBP. The differences observed between males and females in the association of VAI with MetS components likely reflect inherent sex-specific effects. Men typically accumulate more visceral (android) fat, while premenopausal women store more subcutaneous (gynoid) fat. After menopause, hormonal changes, especially declining oestrogen levels, can shift fat distribution toward a more visceral pattern and alter blood pressure and lipid metabolism. These biological differences may explain why the strength of association varies by sex in our findings and has been reported in previous Iranian and Asian cohorts. Therefore, gender-specific thresholds or interpretation of VAI may be necessary in clinical screening.50 In contrast to the current study, researchers have documented a direct and highly robust association between VAI and metabolic disorders related to glucose and fat.15 A study carried out in 2010 highlighted a significant negative relationship between insulin sensitivity and VAI.25 The correlation between central obesity and an increased risk of metabolic complications has been demonstrated in different research.51,53

Hence, anthropometric measures such as BAI, C-Index and ABSI can be employed for assessing excess weight and obesity.54 By increasing visceral fat accumulation, BAI, C-Index and ABSI increase.55,57 The presence of MetS is directly linked58,61 to visceral fat and inversely linked to muscle mass.62 63 The build-up of visceral fat alters the secretion of adipokines, which are biologically active molecules derived from fat cells. They include C-reactive protein, tumour necrosis factor alpha, resistin, interleukin-6 and plasminogen activator inhibitor-1, which increase the synthesis of free fatty acids and glycerol.64 65 The increase in free fatty acids within the hepatic blood flow can elevate blood glucose levels and reduce insulin sensitivity. Furthermore, the substantial presence of free fatty acids, in both acute and chronic scenarios, contributes to diminished insulin sensitivity in muscle cells and pancreatic beta cells, respectively. Increased glycerol and free fatty acid production are correlated with decreased HDL levels and increased LDL.66,70 However, elevated free fatty acids result in heightened angiotensin II production, the principal mediator of the Renin-Angiotensin System, causing elevated blood pressure. Additionally, angiotensin II has the potential to induce inflammation within blood vessel walls, further contributing to elevated blood pressure.71 72 More specifically, the associations of anthropometric indices with MetS components are underpinned by established physiological mechanisms.

VAI reflects both visceral fat accumulation and its metabolic activity. It is strongly associated with insulin resistance, systemic inflammation (eg, elevated CRP) and altered adipokine secretion, particularly lower adiponectin and higher leptin levels.25 73 AIP, defined as log (TG/HDL-C), serves as a surrogate for atherogenic small dense LDL particles and is linked to endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress and insulin resistance.74 BAI estimates body fat percentage and correlates positively with insulin resistance, fasting glucose and blood pressure. It is also associated with inflammatory cytokines and adverse lipid profiles.39 ABSI, though less predictive of inflammation than traditional indices, is designed to reflect body shape independent of BMI and has been shown to relate to cardiometabolic mortality risk.41 AVI estimates the volume of abdominal fat and has demonstrated strong links with impaired glucose tolerance and type 2 diabetes, due to its close correlation with visceral fat.75 BRI approximates body fat percentage and visceral adipose tissue and is associated with elevated blood pressure, TG and decreased HDL levels.76 C-Index, derived from WC, height and weight, reflects central obesity and has been correlated with hypertension, hyperglycaemia and dyslipidaemia in various populations.46 PI, traditionally used in paediatric populations, has recently been linked with cardiometabolic risk in adults as well. Studies have reported associations with WC, blood pressure and lipid disturbances, suggesting it may be a useful screening tool when adjusted for age and sex.77

The primary strength of the present research lies in its pioneering investigation into the correlation between MetS parameters and obesity-related indicators among individuals with MetS, using data from the FACS.19 Despite variations in demographic, geographic and lifestyle factors across the study populations worldwide, both the present study and previous research indicate the usefulness of anthropometric indicators in recognising people at a higher risk of MetS within this region. The observed discrepancies between our findings and some previous studies may partly arise from methodological differences. Variations in population characteristics (eg, age distribution, sex ratio, ethnicity and rural vs urban lifestyle) can influence anthropometric profiles and metabolic risk factors. Furthermore, differences in measurement techniques, such as the type of device used for blood pressure or anthropometric assessments, fasting duration before biochemical sampling or formula variations in calculating indices like AVI and VAI, could contribute to inconsistent results across studies. These methodological variations should be considered when interpreting comparative findings.

However, the cross-sectional design of the study facilitates the evaluation of the relationship between two variables but does not establish the cause-and-effect relationship. In addition, dietary intake and medical history were based on self-report, making them vulnerable to recall and reporting biases. On the other hand, the study population is rural adults from a single province, limiting external validity and generalisability to urban settings or other ethnic groups. Moreover, this study is limited to Iranian adults; thus, future studies are needed to confirm these findings in different populations.

Our findings have several potential applications in clinical and public health practice. The strong associations observed between AVI, AIP, VAI and key MetS components suggest these indices could be integrated into routine screening programmes in primary care to identify individuals at high cardiometabolic risk earlier and more accurately than BMI or WC alone. Because these measures are derived from simple anthropometric data, they can be implemented cost-effectively in community and rural health centres without advanced equipment. In clinical settings, these indices could be incorporated into electronic medical record systems to flag patients at risk during routine check-ups, prompting early lifestyle interventions or further diagnostic testing. Developing population-specific cut-off values for Iranian adults would allow their integration into national screening algorithms, complementing existing MetS criteria. Furthermore, due to their non-invasive nature and minimal training requirements, these indices are particularly suitable for large-scale public health programmes and resource-limited settings where laboratory testing may not be feasible.

On a policy level, adopting Iranian-specific cut-offs for these indices in national guidelines may improve early detection of MetS and its complications. It is recommended that direct methodologies, including DEXA and BIA methods, be used to accurately determine body fat levels. In addition, future prospective studies are warranted to evaluate the predictive performance of these anthropometric indices for long-term cardiometabolic outcomes, such as incident cardiovascular diseases or type 2 diabetes. Moreover, assessing their responsiveness in intervention programmes, such as lifestyle modification or weight reduction strategies, would further establish their clinical utility.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that anthropometric indices, including VAI, AIP, BAI, BRI and AVI, exhibit significant associations with key components of MetS in Iranian adults, particularly blood pressure, glycaemic markers and central adiposity. Among these, BAI showed the strongest correlation with MetS parameters, while ABSI displayed the weakest.
