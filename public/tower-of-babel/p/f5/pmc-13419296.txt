Cooking with the curriculum: expanding nutrition knowledge in medical education through a longitudinal, peer teaching, culinary medicine model
Authors: Alyssa Tenney, Molly Hurd, Sarah DR Krumholz, Harsimran Multani, Estefania Obando, Sarah Pfreundschuh, Isabel Thomas, Eli Zettler, Lauren Iacono, Whitney Calkins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMedical students often receive limited practical nutrition education, despite the important role of dietary modification in chronic disease management and prevention. Cooking with the Curriculum is an extracurricular program developed at the Larner College of Medicine to teach nutrition tenets through a culinary medicine lens while fostering leadership and teaching skills.MethodsThe program uses a longitudinal learning and leadership development model. First-year medical student participants attend 5 sessions focused on dietary counseling for chronic disease. Participating second-year medical students serve as teaching assistants and facilitate sessions. Third and fourth-year medical students can continue to participate as leaders, training teaching assistants, taking responsibility for the administrative responsibilities of the program and facilitating case-based discussions in the session.ResultsProgram evaluation included post-program surveys for first- and second-year students, pre- and post-program knowledge assessments for first-year students and a follow-up survey distributed to students from the 2023 pilot cohort during their third year of medical school.Seventy first-year medical students participated across 2024–2025, with a total of 33 students completing the post survey. Of those respondents, 97% agreed or strongly agreed that others in medical school would benefit from taking the program during their medical education training. All respondents who served as TAs reported that their participation enhanced their knowledge of culinary medicine. Among pilot cohort respondents now in their third year of medical school, 80% reported continued application of concepts during clinical rotations, with 90% reporting use of skills covered in the curriculum to guide patient counseling.ConclusionsCooking with the Curriculum supports that a peer-teaching model can serve as a sustainable approach to enhancing nutrition education in medical training. Participants demonstrated increased confidence in nutrition-related knowledge and leadership skills, supporting the value of incorporating targeted educational initiatives into medical training. Limitations include a small sample size, and low post-program response rates, which may have introduced response bias, as well as reliance on internal funding which limits generalizability and scalability. Despite these limitations, the program provides encouraging preliminary evidence that nutrition-focused leadership training can help prepare future physicians to address nutrition-related health challenges.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12909-026-09555-3.

Background & need for innovation

Non-communicable diseases (NCDs), including diabetes, heart disease, stroke, and cancer, remain leading causes of death and disability in the United States. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), 90% of the nation’s $4.9 trillion in annual healthcare expenditures are spent on individuals with chronic and mental health conditions [1]. Heart disease and stroke account for 1 in 4 deaths nationally, while more than 38 million Americans have diabetes, 98 million adults have prediabetes, and obesity affects 21% of children and 40% of adults [1]. Diet plays a well-established role in the prevention and management of these conditions.

Nutrition education in undergraduate medical education (UME) has gained increasing attention, particularly following the 2022 U.S. House bipartisan resolution calling for “meaningful nutrition education for medical trainees” [2]. Despite this momentum, current data suggest limited improvement in nutrition training and competency within UME. In 2015, 29% of medical schools met the recommended 25 h of nutrition education across UME, whereas a 2023 study found that only 7.8% of medical students reported receiving 20 or more hours of nutrition education during medical school [3]. Additionally, a 2024 survey reported that only 32.26% of responding medical schools offered a standalone nutrition course, while 61.29% integrated nutrition into other coursework and the remaining respondents offered no nutrition education at all [4]. Despite the strong association between dietary patterns and NCDs, there are currently no nationally required nutrition competencies in UME, only recommended competencies.

Review of commonly cited barriers to increasing nutrition education

A recent consensus of nutrition competencies in medical education identified 32 comprehensive competencies for undergraduate medical education [2]. There was variation in support of proposed competencies, with the highest consensus in the competencies focusing on providing evidence-based, culturally sensitive nutrition recommendations for disease prevention and treatment, appropriate screening for food insecurity, and the importance of a multidisciplinary team [2]. However, the authors also acknowledged substantial barriers to implementation of the proposed competencies [2].

Sustainable funding is among the most cited barriers to implementing nutrition and culinary medicine programs [5]. Program costs may include food, instructor and staff time, and access to teaching kitchen facilities [5]. Licensing culinary medicine curricula can cost thousands of dollars, while maintaining or renting teaching kitchens may be financially prohibitive for many institutions [6–8]. In addition, faculty instructors, including physicians, registered dietitians, and chef educators require protected time and financial compensation, creating further implementation challenges [9].

Non-financial barriers also limit integration of culinary medicine into medical education, particularly limited curricular time and low institutional prioritization [10–12]. Although extracurricular programming may reduce strain on existing curricula, medical students often face competing academic demands and may receive little or no academic credit for participation [7, 13]. Programs that lack flexible scheduling to align with academic calendars may experience lower engagement and attendance [14, 15]. Additionally, institutions may have limited faculty members who feel adequately trained to lead these programs [3, 9].

Validation of peer teaching in medical education

Peer teaching, defined as “teaching performed by a person who is the same age, social position, or ability level as other members of the group,” is widely used in undergraduate and postgraduate medical education in both formal and informal settings [16]. As previously discussed, barriers to implementing educational programming often include limited faculty training and compensation. Peer teaching offers a mutually beneficial adjunct to faculty-led instruction.

Peer teaching benefits student-learners through cognitive and social congruence. Cognitive congruence arises because peer teachers better understand learners’ baseline knowledge and common challenges when learning new concepts, and they may better recall strategies that helped them master the material themselves. Social congruence helps create a comfortable learning environment that encourages open discussion and exchange of ideas [16–18]. In a review of peer teaching in medical education, Benè and Bergus found that students taught by peers often achieved higher standardized test scores than those taught by faculty, demonstrated similar clinical skills performance at the end of clerkships, and rated peer teachers more favorably than faculty preceptors [17].

Student teachers also benefit by strengthening content mastery and fostering professional skills, including facilitation, teaching, feedback delivery, leadership, autonomy, and open-mindedness [16–18]. Benè and Bergus suggest that peer teachers may be more motivated to learn the material they teach, which is associated with improved learning outcomes. One study found that peer teachers spent nearly three times longer reviewing course content compared to other students, and multiple studies reported improved examination performance among peer teachers [17]. Peer teaching has also been associated with increased confidence, an important factor in physician professional development and reduction of imposter syndrome [16, 17].

Despite these benefits, peer teaching has important limitations, including inconsistent training requirements, knowledge gaps, and insufficient preparation time for student instructors [18]. In a national survey of medical schools, all respondents reported using peer teaching in their curriculum, yet fewer than half provided formal instruction on effective teaching principles, suggesting limited standardization in peer teacher preparation that may significantly affect the learner experience [19]. Additionally, 95% of formal peer teaching programs occur during the fourth year of medical school, limiting opportunities for students to develop and refine these skills longitudinally [19].

Cooking with the curriculum: development and implementation

Culinary medicine is an emerging discipline that has been shown to improve nutrition knowledge and support student wellness [10]. This educational approach uses active cooking instruction and teaches students how to incorporate healthy habits into their lives. Cooking with the Curriculum, developed at the Larner College of Medicine (LCOM) in 2023, is a unique program grounded in the tenets of culinary medicine that offers an introductory nutrition and culinary medicine curriculum, provides teaching, and offers leadership opportunities for students. Survey data gathered from the 2023 cohort was previously published [20]. The initial development of Cooking with the Curriculum incorporated many of the competencies laid out in Eisenberg et al. [2, 20], and in subsequent years, has further incorporated additional competencies (Table 1).

MS1 cohort demographics

Overview of MS1 programming

The goals of the programming for first-year medical students (MS1) are to equip students with a basic understanding of nutrition and its role in chronic disease, discuss the impact of social determinants of health (SDoH) on access to food, provide resources for clinical practice as it relates to SDoH, enhance student confidence in counseling patients on diet using motivational interviewing and goal setting techniques, and make cooking fun and accessible all while building community with peers and future colleagues. This is accomplished in 5, 2-hour sessions split into a 1-hour didactic lecture and 1-hour of experiential cooking. The programming has been developed by an interdisciplinary team of medical students, registered dieticians, physicians, and chef educators. Importantly, the program provides current evidence-based practices, a patient-centered approach to diet conversations, and underscores the need for an interdisciplinary team for optimal patient care. Program feedback, gathered by anonymous surveys at the end of each program, continues to shape curriculum development.

Overview of MS2 programming

The programming for second-year medical students (MS2) is designed to build upon the foundational knowledge in nutrition, nutritional counseling, and cooking acquired during the MS1 programming through a semi-structured, peer-to-peer teaching model. In this model, MS2s serve as teaching assistants (TAs) for MS1 participants, with the inaugural TA cohort serving in 2024, chosen from 2023 participants. All students who complete the MS1 programming are eligible to apply to become a TA, and students are chosen on a first-come, first-serve basis after an email inquiry is sent out. MS2 students begin the year with a TA orientation led by third-year medical student (MS3) leaders and fourth-year medical student (MS4) leaders, during which they are guided through their responsibilities. Such responsibilities include co-leading a session with a peer TA, completing a “practice run” during which TAs present their session content to MS3 and MS4 leaders and revise their presentations. TAs are also provided with the opportunity to participate in curriculum development sessions. While TAs lead only one session with MS1 participants, they are invited to attend all other sessions to provide additional support. Through the TA experience, MS2 students refine the nutritional knowledge and skills acquired during their first-year of Cooking with the Curriculum, apply their knowledge through facilitation, and additionally gain leadership experience through actively participating in curriculum development and delivery.

Overview of senior leadership (MS3 & MS4) programming

The goal of participation for MS3 students is continued leadership development through peer-to-peer teaching, and assistance with administrative tasks of Cooking with the Curriculum. TAs interested in assuming leadership roles were invited to apply for several administrative positions. These roles included: Student Liaison, responsible for communicating with MS1s; TA Liaison, who supported TAs in organizing and delivering their session presentations; Faculty Liaison, who coordinated meetings with faculty to discuss program funding and curriculum continuity; Community Liaison, who established partnerships with community organizations to facilitate service-learning opportunities; and In-Class Support, a role dedicated to assisting TAs and experiential cooking during sessions. They are also integral to curriculum development, updating each session annually with new statistics, nutrition content, and addressing previous cohort feedback. All MS3 leaders present at sessions also helped lead case-based, small-group discussions with MS1 participants. Leading the small group, case-based discussions gave MS3 students the opportunity to share their experience applying knowledge learned in the clinical setting, and furthering peer-to-peer relationships. MS4 leadership is a continuation of the MS3 roles, with the goal of augmenting the leadership of MS3 students and providing further peer mentorship.

Cooking with the curriculum: program evaluation and outcomes

MS1 programming evaluation

The MS1 student experience was evaluated using the previously validated, post-program survey, which details the responses of the 2023 cohort [20]. For data analysis, questions were evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale with 1 corresponding to ‘strongly disagree’ and 5 corresponding to ‘strongly agree’. Additionally, there were several open-ended questions regarding what students found most meaningful in the program, areas for improvement, and additional comments or thoughts they had to share. All survey answers were collected anonymously using Qualtrics software to avoid potential bias. The program enrolled n = 70 first-year medical students across 2 years. As the same post program survey and knowledge check were administered to students in both 2024 and 2025, the data from the two cohorts are reported here. 47% of participants responded to the post-program survey (n = 33). Of those respondents, 55% percent (n = 18) self-identified as female, and 67% (n = 22) self-identified as white. The program had a 61% retention rate (n = 20) across the 5 sessions, with 94% of respondents attending at least 4 sessions (Table 2).

MS1 programming was additionally evaluated using a pre- and post-program knowledge assessment designed to measure change in core content knowledge following participation in Cooking with the Curriculum. The knowledge check was administered before the first session and at the completion of the program to evaluate change. The assessment consisted of a 10-item multiple-choice survey addressing key concepts including nutrients relevant to dietary conditions, diet-related counseling techniques, and appropriate dietary modification strategies, with some questions having multiple right answers. Surveys were administered anonymously via Qualtrics to reduce response bias. The full survey is provided in Supplementary Table S1.

Two items were inadvertently mis-coded in Qualtrics as single-response questions rather than “select all that apply.” In the 2024 cohort, this occurred for Question 4; in the 2025 cohort, for Question 8. For analysis, question 4 was excluded from the 2024 cohort responses, and question 8 from 2025. Scores were then reported out of 9 questions.

Because the same knowledge check was administered to both cohorts, data were combined for analysis (pre-program n = 67; post-program n = 20). Scores were analyzed using Microsoft Excel, and pre- and post-program means were compared using an independent samples t-test.

The mean pre-program knowledge check score was 62.90% ± 0.18, which increased to 77.78% ± 0.13, post program, representing a statistically significant improvement (Fig. 2A). Additionally, when results were further broken out by specific questions, the proportion of correct responses increased or remained unchanged across all items post program, compared to pre- (Fig. 2B).

Feedback on cooking with the curriculum through the lens of clinical students

To investigate the impact of Cooking with the Curriculum on student’s clinical experience, a survey was disseminated to participants from the 2023 pilot cohort, who participated in Cooking with the Curriculum as MS1s and are now in their third-year clerkships. The survey addressed frequency with which MS3s encountered dietary concerns in clinical practice, type of pathology within those encounters, students perceived confidence at addressing nutrition concerns and the skills they learned and have continued to develop. Additionally, students were asked to identify barriers to addressing nutrition in clinical practice and given space to suggest improvements to Cooking with the Curriculum. They were also asked the extent to which they have continued to use lessons learned in Cooking with the Curriculum in their own life. For data analysis, questions were evaluated on a 5-point Likert scale with 1 corresponding to ‘definitely no’ and 5 corresponding to ‘definitely yes’. All survey answers were collected anonymously using Qualtrics software to avoid potential bias. The full survey is provided in Supplementary Table S3.

Of the 26 first-year medical students enrolled in the pilot program in 2023, 38% responded to the survey (n = 10). 80% of respondents (n = 8) reported that they have observed or participated in a patient encounter that involved counseling on diet choices, with most encounters falling into topics covered by Cooking with the Curriculum sessions (Fig. 4A). When asked if participating in Cooking with the Curriculum enhanced their confidence in counseling about diet in a clinical setting, 90% of respondents reported “definitely yes” or “probably yes” (n = 9). 90% of respondents reported using skills covered in Cooking with the Curriculum in patient care/diet counseling (Fig. 4B). When asked about perceived barriers to utilizing skills such as SMART goal setting and motivational interviewing in the clinical setting, 10% of students identified confidence in skills, and 20% of students identified time.

Discussion

Nutrition plays a critical role in the prevention and management of many NCDs including cardiovascular disease, diabetes, and mental health disorders [21, 22]. Given the rising prevalence of these diet-related conditions and their significant impact on morbidity and mortality in the United States, there has been a growing interest in nutrition education in medical schools, culminating in the recent publishing of a consensus statement of proposed nutrition competencies [2]. Culinary medicine, which combines the art of cooking with the science of medicine, offers an exciting approach to integrate meaningful nutrition education into UME, as Cooking with the Curriculum successfully demonstrates.

Cooking with the Curriculum seeks not only to provide first-year medical students with a foundational knowledge of nutrition and its role in chronic disease, but also to cultivate leadership, teaching, and curriculum development skills among participating students as they progress through training. By integrating peer teaching, thoughtful scheduling, and cost-conscious design, the program offers a sustainable model for incorporating culinary medicine into medical education while addressing many of the barriers that have been cited as limiting program implementation.

Results from the extensive program surveys instituted to evaluate Cooking with the Curriculum support this as a promising avenue for increasing nutrition competencies in undergraduate medical education, while simultaneously promoting student teaching and leadership. Results from the MS1 post program survey reinforce the findings from the 2023 pilot study, indicating that this program continues to be valuable for MS1 students. Additionally, the results from the pre- and post-program knowledge check provide an objective measure of knowledge gained through participation in the program. The statistically significant increase in average scores between pre-program and post-program checks suggests that the curriculum was effective in improving students’ understanding of nutrition concepts. Additionally, for almost all questions, a greater percentage of students answered correctly in the post- knowledge check.

Each year, on a first-come, first-serve basis, 8 students are recruited to act as TAs for the incoming MS1 class, and both years, at least 8 students have been interested in that role, indicating that students find that role valuable. Results from the TA surveys echo that sentiment, with all students agreeing that their teaching role increased their confidence in counseling and most feeling that it improved their knowledge of diet and disease. Importantly, the TA role, and leadership in MS3 year allows students to use spaced repetition to apply nutrition knowledge and provides an avenue for leadership continuity throughout their medical education.

Though a small number of respondents, the data from the MS3 feedback survey supports that students are participating in patient care that involves diet counseling, and the topics covered by Cooking with the Curriculum address the most common diseases presenting in clinical practice, reiterating the utility of this type of programming. Almost all respondents agree that participating in the program increased their counseling confidence, suggesting the importance of this training for clinical care. Additionally, even 2 years later, many respondents continue to use tenets taught in Cooking with the Curriculum in their own life, indicating a lasting impact not only on clinical care, but on student-wellbeing.

Cooking with the curriculum addresses commonly cited barriers

As extensively discussed here, despite the need for innovative, practical, and sustainable educational approaches to nutrition education in medical schools, program implementation remains challenging [2, 23, 24]. Cooking with the Curriculum is an example of a program model that addresses many of the common barriers that limit the implementation of culinary medicine programs in medical schools.

The program addresses curricular time limitations by strategically scheduling the five, two-hour sessions in the evenings between first-year exams to minimize conflicts with academic responsibilities.

Financial barriers have been addressed in several ways. Important to the success of the Cooking with the Curriculum program was obtaining internal grant funding to purchase reusable cooking equipment which allowed for the creation of a fully stocked “mobile kitchen”, curated to include utensils, cutting boards, and slow cookers which could be used year after year. This eliminated the cost of a dedicated teaching kitchen, as active cooking instruction took place in medical school classrooms. Additionally, to reinforce a cost-conscious approach to healthy eating that students can apply in personal and clinical contexts, recipes used in the program were intentionally selected based on affordability and accessibility. Recipes that use frozen or canned ingredients and require little preparatory work were prioritized, an example of how SDoH and conversations on food insecurity are emphasized throughout the program.

Cooking with the Curriculum’s peer teaching approach in which MS2s serve as facilitators for MS1 participants, offers a potential solution to several more barriers. Peer-assisted learning has been widely validated within medical education as an effective and well-accepted approach that can enhance knowledge acquisition and reinforce teaching skills [16–18]. Students serving as peer educators can reduce faculty time commitments and associated costs while simultaneously reinforcing their own knowledge and professional development. Leveraging peer teaching within culinary medicine programming may therefore represent a sustainable strategy to develop longitudinal nutrition education while minimizing resource demands. These tenets are further augmented by MS3 and MS4 participants, who are instrumental in the core leadership team, and act as peer teachers to both the MS2 and MS1 participants.

Limitations and future directions

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings. Specific to the knowledge check results, a coding error was identified in two of the knowledge check questions during data analysis, and these questions were excluded in the analysis. Additionally, the knowledge check consisted of multiple-choice questions, which may not fully capture higher-order understanding or the ability to apply nutrition concepts in clinical settings or identify improvements in counseling skills. It is important to note the uneven response rate to pre and post surveys, which limits the generalizability of the findings as post-program feedback is underrepresented in the data analysis. The voluntary nature of the survey resulted in a lower response rate than expected, introducing the potential for non-response bias. Students who responded to the post-survey may have had interest in the program or had greater nutrition knowledge than those participants who did not respond. Therefore, the results may not fully reflect the experiences or characteristics of the wider population.

Responses to the knowledge checks were collected anonymously and were not linked to individual participants. As a result, analyses were limited to comparisons between group averages before and after the program, rather than tracking knowledge improvement at the individual level. This limits the ability to determine the degree of learning achieved by specific participants. Future iterations of the program may consider using coded identifiers to allow for paired analyses while maintaining participant confidentiality. If a knowledge check is used again, it may be beneficial to adjust the survey to include questions that align with any new program content. Despite these limitations, the knowledge check remains a helpful benchmark to inform curricular adjustments, particularly as some questions did not see an improvement score from pre- to post-test, indicating that more time may need to be dedicated to those topics.

Additionally, as an elective program offered at a single institution, the findings may not be generalizable to other medical schools. Selection bias was likely present, as students elected to take the program and may have been more motivated, more interested in nutrition, or more inclined to perceive the experience positively. Nonresponse bias may also have influenced results, as not all students who participated completed the post-program evaluations, and those that did may have been more engaged or satisfied with the experience. This was particularly relevant to the MS3 survey, which had a very low response rate. The results are therefore not generalizable and cannot be used to assess long-term outcomes of the program. Reproducibility may be limited by the program’s reliance on internal grant funding as well as faculty who were willing to share their expertise. Institutions without similar financial or structural support may face barriers to implementation.

More general limitations for the program include scalability, as offering the programming to the entire class would incur great expense and curricular limitations. Although extracurricular programs, such as the Cooking with the Curriculum model, have the benefit of not adding more content to the formal curricula, participating students often face competing academic responsibilities and may receive little or no academic credit for participation [7, 13]. For large scale implementation to UME, adapting to the recommendations put forth by Eisenberg et al. may be more achievable. The curriculum taught in Cooking with the Curriculum could be adapted to a longitudinal format across clinical domains. However, upscaling the experiential cooking aspect would be difficult and costly. Currently, costs for food and space constraints are low because of the extracurricular nature of the program. If this curriculum were formalized and offered to 100% of the MS1 class for credit, costs and space constraints would significantly increase.

An additional limitation of the curriculum is the lack of formal, standardized training for teaching assistants. While the peer-teaching model offers meaningful leadership and educational opportunities for second-year students, variability in prior teaching experience may affect instructional quality and learner experience. To address this, future iterations of the program will include a structured orientation for MS2 facilitators that will include foundational instruction in effective teaching strategies. This orientation, led by program faculty, will focus on lecture-based teaching, small-group facilitation, learner engagement, and feedback, with the goal of enhancing student confidence and effectiveness as peer educators. Formalizing this training will strengthen the sustainability of the peer-teaching model while also supporting the development of medical students as future clinician-educators.
