Heat stroke management: clinical phenotypes and implications for cooling strategies
Authors: Lan Chen, Sunying Wu, Xiangliang Wu, Huan Zhang, Jingnan Ren, Liyun Lu, Huimin Ma, Dingping Jin, Michelle Patch, Xiuqin Feng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHeat stroke is a life-threatening condition demanding rapid core temperature reduction. However, optimizing cooling strategies is challenging due to pronounced patient heterogeneity. This study aimed to identify distinct clinical phenotypes of heat stroke to enable personalized cooling management.MethodsThis two-stage study analyzed heat stroke patients from seven tertiary hospitals in China. The prospective cohort was derived from an ongoing, prospectively registered clinical trial (NCT05923931). Latent class analysis was applied to this prospective cohort to derive a phenotype model, which was validated in an independent retrospective cohort and then applied to the total cohort (N = 298) for analysis. Phenotype-specific cooling responses were evaluated by testing interactions between phenotype and cooling modalities and by comparing the effectiveness of practical cooling bundles within each phenotype. Associations between early infusion volume and 48-hour biomarker trajectories were explored using generalized additive mixed models.ResultsThe analysis identified two distinct clinical phenotypes: a non-severe phenotype (47%) and a severe phenotype (53%). The severe phenotype was characterized by more profound organ injury, a slower cooling rate, and higher mortality. Crucially, the efficacy of core cooling modalities was phenotype-specific (P for interaction = 0.038): evaporation cooling was highly effective in the non-severe phenotype but had attenuated effect in the severe phenotype. For the severe phenotype, a bundled strategy of high-volume cold-fluid infusion combined with evaporation cooling was necessary to achieve the fastest cooling (β = 0.03, p < 0.001), whereas either modality alone was insufficient. Exploratory analysis further revealed that early infusion volume was differentially associated with subsequent changes in organ stress biomarkers between the two phenotypes. The direction of all key associations was consistent across cohorts, and the primary phenotype-specific response patterns were reproduced, confirming the robustness of the findings.ConclusionsThis study identifies a clinically actionable phenotype classification for heat stroke. Our findings suggest a phenotype-guided management protocol: for non-severe cases, prioritize evaporation cooling with conservative fluids; for severe cases, a bundled strategy of high-volume cold-fluid infusion plus evaporation is necessary. This hypothesis-generating framework offers a potential pathway toward early, tailored intervention at the point of care, pending confirmation in future studies.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-026-08284-x.Key Words: Heat stroke, Cooling rate, Cold fluid infusion, Evaporation, Latent class analysis

Introduction

Global climate change has led to an increased frequency of extreme heat events, with both the intensity and duration of heatwaves rising annually, thereby posing a significant threat to public health. In 2023, the average number of days individuals were exposed to heatwaves in China reached 16, more than three times the historical average recorded between 1986 and 2005 [1]. The number of deaths attributed to heatwaves also rose by 1.9 times between 2019 and 2023 compared to 1986–2005, with an estimated 37,254 deaths recorded in 2023 alone, highlighting a sharp escalation in the public health burden from heat-related illnesses [1]. Heat stroke (HS), the most severe form of heat-related illness, is characterized by hyperthermia (above 40°C) and central nervous system dysfunction, commonly presenting with tachypnea, tachycardia, hypotension, and laboratory derangements such as elevated transaminases, acute kidney injury (AKI), coagulopathy, and lactic acidosis [2, 3]. These clinical and laboratory features reflect a systemic inflammatory response and multi-organ failure, contributing to the high mortality associated with HS [2, 3].

Studies have demonstrated that the extent of cellular damage in HS patients correlates closely with the highest body temperature and the duration of hyperthermia [2]. Therefore, rapid cooling has become a crucial strategy for improving the prognosis of HS patients [4]. Reducing core body temperature below 40 °C within 30 minutes of onset has been shown to significantly decrease mortality [5, 6]. Effective cooling within the first two hours of emergency presentation is also vital for long-term prognosis; faster temperature reduction correlates with lower mortality rates and a decreased incidence of organ dysfunction [7]. Consequently, efficient cooling and multi-organ support are central to the treatment of HS.

Although rapid cooling is essential for treating HS, clinical observations show substantial variability in its effectiveness. Despite the use of the same cooling methods, some patients experience rapid temperature reduction without complications, while others progress to multiple organ failure or even death. This heterogeneity is evident not only across different populations—cold water immersion (CWI) is particularly effective for managing exertional HS (EHS) in young individuals, athletes, and military personnel, whereas elderly patients often tolerate CWI poorly, which may exacerbate complications and increase mortality [8, 9]—but also reflects the impact of patients’ intrinsic status. Vascular tone, blood volume, and blood flow velocity directly determine the efficiency of heat transfer from the core to the body surface [3, 10]. Consistent clinical observations indicate that critically ill patients presenting with hypotension, acidosis, or a low Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) score exhibit greater resistance to cooling and require significantly longer time to achieve target temperature [11–13]. These findings suggest that disease severity and underlying health status are core factors influencing cooling efficacy and prognosis [11, 12], and that different patient subgroups may benefit from distinct cooling approaches. This heterogeneity underscores the need for personalized cooling strategies tailored to the individual characteristics of each patient.

However, existing studies have largely focused on specific cooling techniques, with limited attention to the interaction between patient characteristics and cooling strategies. Moreover, most studies are single-center and retrospective, which limits the generalizability of their conclusions. Therefore, large-scale, multi-center studies are necessary to establish evidence-based guidelines for personalized cooling interventions. Furthermore, traditional research methods are insufficient for accurately identifying patient subgroups. Therefore, there is a pressing need to develop a precise classification system to optimize cooling strategies.

Here, utilizing a multi-center cohort, we applied latent class analysis (LCA)—an established method for identifying clinically meaningful subgroups in heterogeneous conditions [14, 15]—to derive a phenotype classification for HS. We identified two distinct phenotypes—severe and non-severe—using a prospective cohort, and validated the classification in an independent retrospective cohort. We then explored whether the effectiveness of foundational cooling modalities differs between these phenotypes, and examined the association between early infusion strategies and biomarker trajectories. Our findings suggested a preliminary, phenotype-informed approach to emergency cooling that warrants prospective validation, and may ultimately translate heterogeneous patient data into a practical clinical decision rule to facilitate tailored intervention at the bedside.

Research methods

Study design

This study utilized a multi-center, retrospective and prospective cohort design. The retrospective cohort included patients admitted between January 1, 2022, and September 30, 2022, whose characteristics have been previously published [7]. The prospective cohort was derived from an ongoing, prospectively registered clinical trial (NCT05923931), which was primarily designed to compare the effectiveness of different surface cooling modalities. The present analysis utilizes this rigorously collected cohort to address a distinct scientific question—the identification of clinical phenotypes and the derivation of phenotype-specific cooling strategies—which was not the primary objective of the registered trial. Patients were enrolled consecutively from June 1, 2023, to September 30, 2025. Both cohorts adhered to identical inclusion and exclusion criteria. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine (Approval Number: 023–0517). Written or verbal informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal representatives in the prospective cohort, in accordance with ethical standards.

Location

The study was conducted across seven tertiary comprehensive hospitals in Eastern China, a region characterized by a subtropical monsoon climate. Summers are hot and rainy, with daily maximum temperatures frequently exceeding 35 °C and reaching over 40 °C during extreme weather events.

Participants

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The study included patients presenting to the emergency department (ED) with a diagnosis of HS and a core body temperature exceeding 39.5 °C. Diagnosis was based on the expert consensus guidelines for HS in China [16]. Exclusion criteria comprised: (1) patients requiring immediate cardiopulmonary resuscitation upon ED arrival; (2) those with severe comorbidities (e.g., acute intracerebral hemorrhage, traumatic brain injury, or hepatic abscess) that could confound outcome assessment; (3) patients with missing data for two or more critical variables essential for LCA; (4) patients discharged within 4 hours of ED visit with an unknown outcome.

Patient population

Our cohort reflects the regional epidemiology of HS. Cases predominantly occurred between July and August. Classical HS (CHS) primarily affected elderly patients with multiple chronic comorbidities, while EHS occurred predominantly in outdoor agricultural workers, industrial laborers in high-temperature environments, and vulnerable individuals with impaired self-care abilities exposed to extreme heat. Consequently, compared to cohorts focusing on athletes or military personnel, our patient population exhibits an older age distribution (mean age > 65 years) and a higher prevalence of comorbidities. The median pre-hospital delay was 1 hour, often resulting in patients presenting with severe manifestations, including impaired consciousness and circulatory failure.

Data collection

We collected comprehensive data including: (1) basic demographics and medical history; (2) time from onset to ED visit (interval between initial collapse or symptom manifestation and ED presentation); (3) vital signs at presentation (body temperature, heart rate, systolic blood pressure, peripheral oxygen saturation, and GCS); (4) temperature changes within 2 hours of presentation; (5) laboratory results within 0.5 hours of presentation (complete blood count, coagulation profile, blood gas analysis, and organ function markers such as creatinine and troponin I) and serially over the following 48 hours to assess biomarker trajectories; (6) detailed cooling interventions; (7) hospitalization outcomes (organ dysfunction e.g., AKI, acute heart failure [AHF], and in-hospital mortality), based on definitions from our prior study [17]; (8) for the prospective cohort, long-term outcomes were also collected, including all-cause mortality and neurological sequelae at the 3-month follow-up.

Cooling interventions and protocol evolution

Upon ED arrival, all patients underwent rapid assessment and received a standardized bundle of care aimed at rapid core temperature reduction. This bundle included four core components: (1) environmental cooling (ambient temperature controlled to 20–24°C); (2) removal of patient clothing to maximize skin exposure; (3) intravascular cooling via rapid infusion of 4 °C ice-cold saline, which served the dual purpose of core temperature reduction and volume resuscitation; and (4) surface cooling. All treatment decisions were at the discretion of the attending clinicians based on patient condition and clinical judgment; no interventions were randomly assigned or withheld for the purpose of this study.

The specific modalities within this bundle evolved over the study period, reflecting an ongoing effort to optimize cooling efficacy based on emerging evidence. In the retrospective cohort, surface cooling primarily utilized ice packs and cooling blankets, and cold-fluid infusion was administered at clinician discretion [7]. Subsequently, based on internal audit and external evidence supporting its efficiency, the protocol was formally optimized to recommend (a) evaporation-convection as the primary surface cooling method and (b) a rapid infusion protocol with a target of administering 1000 mL of ice-cold saline within the first 0.5 hours of ED presentation, consistent with established cooling benchmarks [2]. The following sections detail how we measured the effectiveness of these interventions and the analytical strategy employed to evaluate their phenotype-specific benefits.

Outcome measures, data harmonization, and study analytical framework

A key outcome was the cooling rate, calculated as the change in body temperature from ED presentation to 0.5 hours post-presentation. Unlike many studies that use the onset of cooling interventions as the reference time [18], we used the time of ED presentation as the baseline for all calculations. This is because the total duration of hyperthermia is a critical prognostic factor in HS, and in the emergency setting, the timely initiation of rapid cooling from the moment of arrival is essential. We also assessed the rate at which patients reached target temperatures within 0.5 and 2 hours, based on established reference standards [7]. To address methodological differences in temperature measurement between the retrospective (primarily tympanic) and prospective (rectal) cohorts, we standardized values by adding 0.5 °C to tympanic measurements in accordance with established guidelines [19]. The robustness of our findings to this adjustment was confirmed through sensitivity analyses. Notably, cooling rate calculations were exempt from this standardization, as they are derived from within-patient temperature differentials over time, ensuring internal consistency regardless of the initial measurement method. Furthermore, based on nursing records, intravenous fluid orders, and medical orders, we calculated the volume of 4 °C cold fluid administered within 0.5 hours of presentation.

All data were extracted from electronic medical records and cross-verified with nursing charts and medical orders to ensure accuracy.

Analytical considerations regarding cohort heterogeneity

It is important to clarify the analytical implications of the protocol evolution described above. This study is not designed to directly compare the retrospective and prospective cohorts as independent groups. Instead, the pooled cohort is used to address two primary scientific questions: First, to identify patient phenotypes—based on early organ injury patterns—that are robust and generalizable across different treatment epochs. To achieve this, we employed a sequential LCA strategy: deriving the phenotype model exclusively from the prospective cohort, validating it in the retrospective cohort. The fixed model was then applied to the total cohort for all subsequent analyses. Second, to evaluate whether the optimized cooling strategy (evaporation-convection combined with high-volume cold-fluid infusion) demonstrates a phenotype-specific benefit when analyzed across the entire population. This approach allows us to control for fundamental patient heterogeneity while assessing the effectiveness of the current standard of care in a real-world, heterogeneous sample.

Data analysis methods

Phenotype identification and validation

Clinical phenotypes were identified and validated through a sequential, three-stage analytical process to maximize robustness.

Model Development in the Prospective Cohort. LCA was performed exclusively on the prospective cohort to derive the phenotype model. We selected six key laboratory and clinical indicators upon ED presentation, representing major organ systems: creatinine (renal), troponin I (cardiac), D-dimer (coagulation), GCS (neurological), lactate (metabolic/circulatory), and PCO₂ (respiratory). Continuous variables were Z-score normalized prior to analysis. The optimal number of classes was determined by evaluating model fit statistics, including the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC), the bootstrap likelihood ratio test (BLRT), and entropy. The LCA model was estimated using the expectation-maximization (EM) algorithm within the poLCA package in R. This package uses full-information maximum likelihood to handle missing data directly, without requiring prior imputation.

These six indicators capture core early pathophysiology in HS. Although liver transaminases (e.g., ALT) are relevant, they had a higher missing data rate (16.21%) in the development cohort. Comparison of patients with and without ALT measurements revealed no significant differences in baseline characteristics or outcomes, suggesting the missing data were likely random. A sensitivity analysis directly incorporating ALT yielded a model with both poorer statistical fit and no gain in clinical interpretability; consequently, ALT was excluded from the primary model. Arterial PCO₂ was chosen as a clinically obtainable proxy for respiratory status in lieu of less reliably recorded oxygen therapy parameters.

Cross-Cohort Validation. To establish generalizability, the LCA model parameters (item-response probabilities) from the prospective cohort were held fixed and applied to the independent retrospective cohort. We calculated the posterior probability of class membership for each patient in the retrospective cohort. Classification performance was assessed by the mean posterior probability and the proportion of patients classified with high certainty (posterior probability > 0.8).

Unified Classification of the Total Cohort. Following successful validation, this same fixed model was applied to classify all patients in the combined total cohort, ensuring a consistent phenotype definition for all subsequent analyses.

Characterization of phenotypes

After classifying all patients, we compared baseline demographics, vital signs, laboratory values, treatments administered, and clinical outcomes between the phenotypes. Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range) and analyzed using independent sample t-tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were expressed as percentages and analyzed using chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests.

Analysis of phenotype-specific cooling responses

We evaluated the clinical utility of the phenotypes by assessing their relationship with cooling efficiency and treatment response in three sequential steps. Given the exploratory nature of these analyses, no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied; findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

First, we compared the overall cooling dynamics between phenotypes using linear regression for cooling rate and logistic regression for the achievement of target temperature within defined timeframes.

Second, we tested whether phenotype modified the effectiveness of key cooling modalities. This involved: (i) testing for a statistical interaction between phenotype and the use of evaporation cooling on cooling rate using multivariable linear regression, and (ii) modeling the relationship between early infusion volume (within the first 0.5 hours) and cooling rate separately within each phenotype using generalized additive models (GAM) to allow for non-linear associations.

Third, to assess the dynamic effect of interventions on core temperature trajectories, we modeled the association of early infusion volume (stratified by high [≥1000 mL within 0.5 hours] vs. low [<1000 mL within 0.5 hours]) and evaporation cooling with body temperature trajectories over the first 2 hours in the severe phenotype using generalized additive mixed models (GAMM), adjusted for admission temperature and other cooling methods.

Fourth, to derive actionable clinical guidance, we evaluated and compared the effectiveness of four practical cooling strategy bundles—defined by combinations of early infusion volume (low/high) and evaporation (without/with)—separately within each phenotype using multivariable linear regression.

Exploratory analysis of infusion volume and biomarker trajectories

To explore potential organ-system effects, we used GAMM to assess associations between early infusion volume and 48-hour trajectories of B-type natriuretic peptide (BNP), creatinine, and lactate, stratified by phenotype. Models were adjusted for measurement time, admission temperature, cooling methods, and cohort.

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

The stability of our primary findings was verified through comprehensive sensitivity analyses:

Prospective and Retrospective Cohort Validation: To ensure the robustness of our findings against cohort heterogeneity, all core analyses were repeated independently within both the prospective cohort and the retrospective cohort.

Model Robustness: We assessed the impact of (a) directly including ALT alongside the six core indicators in the LCA model, and (b) using admission body temperature to predict class membership, comparing these alternative classifications to our primary model.

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.0;http://www.r-project.org)and EmpowerStats (http://www.empowerstats.com). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study cohorts

A total of 298 patients with HS were included in the final analysis: 187 in the retrospective cohort and 111 in the prospective cohort (Fig. 1). The cohorts were similar in demographics and key markers of initial disease severity (Supplementary Table S1). However, cooling strategies differed, reflecting an evolution in clinical practice. The retrospective cohort primarily used ice packs and cooling blankets for surface cooling, whereas the prospective cohort protocol recommended evaporation-convection as the primary method. Similarly, the infusion protocol was intensified: the median volume of cold saline infused within 0.5 hours was significantly higher in the prospective cohort (1300 mL, IQR: 1000–2000) than in the retrospective cohort (700 mL, IQR: 400–1000; p < 0.001).

In the total cohort, the population was predominantly elderly (mean age 65 ± 15 years) with a median pre-hospital delay of 1.0 hour. The mean admission core temperature was 41.2 ± 0.8 °C, and multi-organ impairment was common upon presentation.

Identification and validation of clinical phenotypes

LCA was performed exclusively on the prospective cohort using six early organ injury indicators. The two-class model was selected as optimal based on the lowest BIC, high classification certainty (entropy = 0.96), and clinical interpretability (Table S2; Fig. 2). Three- and four-class models, while statistically significant, did not improve clinical interpretability and were less parsimonious. Further details on class assignment probabilities and the distribution of original clinical variables within the prospective cohort are provided in Figures S1 and S2, respectively. The model parameters were then applied to the independent retrospective cohort for validation, demonstrating excellent performance (mean posterior probability 0.96; 90.4% of patients classified with probability > 0.8; Fig. 3, Table S3). Finally, the model was applied to the total combined cohort, yielding reliable classification (mean posterior probability 0.91).

Clinical characterization of the phenotypes

The total cohort was classified into Class 1 (n = 140, 47.0%) and Class 2 (n = 158, 53.0%). Based on clinical profiles, Class 1 and Class 2 were designated as the ‘non-severe’ and ‘severe’ phenotypes, respectively. Compared to the non-severe phenotype, the severe phenotype presented with higher core temperature, more profound multi-organ injury (elevated creatinine, troponin I, D-dimer, lactate; lower GCS and PCO₂), and significantly worse clinical outcomes (all p < 0.001; Table 1 and S4). These phenotypic differences were consistently observed in both cohorts individually (Tables S5–S6), and the severe phenotype also predicted worse 3-month outcomes (neurological sequelae and mortality; prospective cohort, p < 0.05).

Phenotype-specific cooling responses

Overall cooling efficiency differs by phenotype

The severe phenotype had a significantly slower cooling rate than the non-severe phenotype (adjusted β: −0.01, p = 0.025; Table 2). Consequently, the severe phenotype had higher body temperatures at both 0.5 and 2 hours after presentation and was less likely to achieve target cooling temperatures. This cooling disadvantage was most pronounced in the prospective cohort, where standardized protocols led to higher overall cooling efficiency (Supplementary Table S7).

Differential Response to Core Cooling Modalities

The effect of evaporation cooling was significantly modified by phenotype (P for interaction = 0.038; Table 3). Evaporation was strongly associated with faster cooling in the non-severe phenotype (p < 0.001) but had a markedly attenuated effect in the severe phenotype (p = 0.080). The relationship between early infusion volume and cooling rate also differed fundamentally by phenotype (Fig. 4): in the severe phenotype, higher infusion volume was linearly associated with faster cooling, whereas in the non-severe phenotype, the benefit plateaued beyond approximately 1500 mL.

Cooling efficacy of individual modalities in the severe phenotype

We next quantified the cooling benefit of each intervention over the first 2 hours specifically within the severe phenotype. Patients who received a high-volume infusion (>1000 mL within 0.5 hours) cooled 0.47 °C per hour faster than those with a low-volume infusion (p < 0.001), after adjusting for other cooling measures (Figure S3A, Table S8). Similarly, within the severe phenotype, evaporation cooling was associated with a significantly faster cooling rate (β: −0.63; p < 0.001) compared to its non-use (Figure S3B, Table S8). These findings were consistent across different temperature standardization strategies (Figure S4). In contrast, the use of ice packs or cooling blankets did not show a similar independent association with faster cooling in this phenotype (Figure S5).

Optimized cooling strategy by phenotype

For the severe phenotype, the bundle of high-volume infusion combined with evaporation cooling was associated with the fastest cooling rate and showed a significant improvement over the reference bundle (low-volume infusion without evaporation; adjusted β = 0.03, p < 0.001; Table 4). Bundles involving only one modality did not show statistically significant improvement. In the smaller prospective cohort, the point estimate for the combined strategy remained positive but did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.229), reflecting limited sample size (Supplementary Table S9). Similarly, in the retrospective cohort, the number of severe patients receiving the combined bundle was too small (n = 3) for meaningful assessment.

For the non-severe phenotype, evaporation alone (whether paired with low- or high-volume infusion) was consistently associated with increased cooling rates across all cohorts (e.g., total cohort adjusted β = 0.03, p < 0.001). In contrast, increasing the infusion volume without evaporation provided no additional benefit beyond that achieved by evaporation. This finding was replicated across both cohorts (Supplementary Table S9), confirming that evaporation is the dominant effective modality for patients with the non-severe phenotype.

Association between infusion volume and biomarker trajectories

The impact of early infusion volume on 48-hour biomarker trajectories differed markedly by phenotype (Figure S6).

In the severe phenotype, creatinine decreased linearly with higher infusion volume (p < 0.001), while lactate exhibited a biphasic response: initial decrease followed by a sharp rise beyond approximately 1500 mL (p = 0.001). BNP showed a non-linear declining trend.

In the non-severe phenotype, creatinine remained stable, and BNP showed a trend toward increase with higher infusion volume (p = 0.051), while lactate exhibited a mild decreasing trend.

These patterns were validated in the prospective cohort, where U-shaped relationships for BNP and lactate were more pronounced, with nadirs around 1300–1500 mL (Figure S7). Collectively, these findings suggest a potential safety threshold near 1500 mL for severe patients, beyond which lactate may rise, while higher infusion volume in non-severe patients was associated with a trend toward increased cardiac wall stress (rising BNP).

Sensitivity and robustness analyses

We conducted two sensitivity analyses. First, we assessed whether the exclusion of liver injury markers influenced the results. An alternative LCA model incorporating ALT yielded a higher BIC than our primary model (Table S10), indicating inferior fit, without altering the fundamental two-class structure or clinical interpretability. Second, to validate the pathophysiological relevance of the classification, we evaluated admission body temperature as a predictor of latent class membership. In a model adjusted for the six core indicators, higher admission temperature was a strong, independent predictor of assignment to the severe phenotype (adjusted OR per 1 °C increase = 6.55, p = 0.010) (Table S11), confirming that the identified organ injury patterns are strongly linked to the severity of the initial hyperthermic insult.

Discussion

The rising burden of HS demands optimized emergency care [1]. However, the clinical management is profoundly challenged by pronounced patient heterogeneity, which complicates cooling strategy optimization and underscores the need for a personalized approach. In this multicenter study, we derived and validated a simple phenotype model based on early, routinely available indicators of organ dysfunction. We identified two distinct phenotypes—severe and non-severe—that showed divergent responses to core cooling modalities, with these phenotype-specific patterns consistently observed across independent cohorts. To our knowledge, this is the first study to use LCA for phenotype classification in HS, and the finding of phenotype-specific cooling efficacy represents the primary contribution of this work. However, these findings are exploratory and require prospective validation in future studies.

A pivotal finding of our study was the significantly impaired cooling efficiency in the severe phenotype. We hypothesize that circulatory failure may be a central mechanism underlying this thermoregulatory deficit. Effective surface cooling requires sufficient cardiac output and cutaneous perfusion to convect heat for dissipation [3, 20]—a condition that may be compromised in severe HS, as suggested by the clinical signs of circulatory compromise (e.g., tachycardia, hypotension) observed in our severe phenotype. This framework could help explain the spectrum of cooling efficacy across clinical contexts: from highly effective CWI in young, healthy individuals [18, 21, 22] to its diminished returns and practical constraints in the older, comorbid patients typical of ED presentations [23]. This gradient suggests that the patient’s physiological reserve, particularly circulatory competence, may be a key determinant of cooling success, highlighting the need for evidence-based alternatives tailored to the high-risk, complex patient.

Building on this understanding, we found that evaporative cooling alone was insufficient for the severe phenotype, whereas a bundled approach of high-volume cold-fluid infusion combined with evaporation was most effective. We speculate that high-volume infusion provides direct central cooling [24] and volume resuscitation, thereby improving peripheral perfusion and potentially enhancing the efficacy of concurrent evaporative cooling [20]. Consequently, for severe patients, the phenotype-guided synergy of central cooling (via rapid infusion) and surface cooling (via evaporation) may address the dual challenge of reducing core temperature while supporting the compromised cutaneous perfusion necessary for heat dissipation.

Cold-fluid infusion serves the dual purpose of direct central cooling and volume resuscitation. However, this aggressive approach introduces a critical trade-off: rapid cooling must be balanced against the risk of volume overload, particularly with potential myocardial injury [16, 25]. Our exploratory analysis revealed phenotype-specific patterns in the association between early infusion volume and 48-hour biomarker trajectories. To explain this divergence, we propose a “cooling-volume dual-pathway model” (Fig. 5). For severe patients with circulatory failure, high-volume infusion provides dual benefit: direct cooling plus vital volume resuscitation, reversing organ hypoperfusion. However, exceeding an optimal rate may precipitate fluid overload. For non-severe patients with preserved circulation, high-volume infusion primarily increases volume load, potentially explaining the rise in cardiac wall stress. This model suggests that the same intervention can exert opposing effects on organ perfusion and stress depending on the underlying phenotypic severity, arguing strongly for a personalized approach to infusion volume. We emphasize that the identified threshold (~1500 mL within the first 0.5 hours) is data-derived from exploratory analyses and should be interpreted as a hypothesis-generating signal rather than a definitive safety limit; prospective validation is required before incorporation into clinical protocols.

Phenotype-guided cooling strategy for heat stroke. Upon ED presentation, patients are classified into non-severe and severe phenotypes based on early organ injury markers. Ice-cold fluid infusion activates dual pathways (volume resuscitation and central cooling) in both phenotypes, but with differential intensity: minimal benefit in the non-severe phenotype (preserved circulation) and strong synergistic benefit in the severe phenotype (circulatory failure). Recommended strategies are evaporation cooling with conservative fluid for non-severe patients, and high-volume infusion plus evaporation cooling for severe patients, with an optimal threshold of approximately 1300–1500 mL within the first 0.5 hours. This framework enables individualized cooling therapy from ED arrival

Our LCA deliberately excluded admission body temperature from the LCA model to separate the initial hyperthermic insult from the downstream host response. By classifying patients based solely on early markers of organ dysfunction, we aimed to capture heterogeneity in host response independent of the absolute temperature. A supplementary analysis confirmed that higher admission temperature was a strong independent predictor of the severe phenotype, indicating that the resulting phenotypes remain biologically anchored to the severity of the initial insult.

Our study has several limitations. First, we did not perform a formal quantitative comparison with established severity scores like the Heatstroke Severity Score (HSSS) [26], as key components were not available in this hyper-acute dataset. However, both frameworks conceptually align in using multi-organ injury to define severity, with our model offering the pragmatic advantage of using data available within minutes of ED presentation. Second, the generalizability of our findings may be influenced by the inclusion of patients solely from tertiary hospitals in Eastern China. However, the patient demographics—primarily elderly individuals with comorbidities—are representative of a major HS phenotype encountered in similar settings globally [27]. Third, we acknowledge the heterogeneity between the retrospective and prospective cohorts, particularly in cooling methods and the precision of infusion volume recording. We addressed this through a tiered analytical strategy, which confirmed the robustness of the core phenotype-specific findings across different clinical contexts. Fourth, the retrospective design component carries inherent risks of information bias and missing data, and establishing definitive causality remains challenging. Fifth, the use of PCO₂ as a proxy for respiratory status is a limitation, as direct measures such as PaO₂/FiO₂ were not reliably available and PCO₂ can be influenced by both metabolic and respiratory derangements. Additionally, direct hemodynamic measures (e.g., cardiac output) were not available, so the proposed mechanism of circulatory failure remains speculative. Sixth, the cooling rate was calculated from ED presentation rather than from the start of cooling interventions. Pre-hospital cooling or delays may have influenced body temperature at presentation, but these factors are inherently reflected in the presenting clinical status, which is the starting point for our analysis. Finally, given the exploratory nature, no adjustment was made for multiple comparisons, and some subgroup analyses were underpowered. The consistency of effect directions across cohorts does not fully compensate for these limitations.

Future research should build upon this framework in several key directions. First, prospective multicenter studies are needed to validate the generalizability of our phenotype model and its associated treatment recommendations across diverse healthcare settings. Second, the impact of phenotype-guided cooling strategies on hard clinical outcomes, including long-term neurological recovery and mortality, requires evaluation through extended follow-up. Third, integrating real-time hemodynamic assessment (e.g., point-of-care ultrasound) could help validate the hypothesized mechanism of circulatory failure and enable more precise, real-time phenotyping to personalize fluid and cooling management.

Conclusion

This exploratory study identifies a simple, clinically applicable phenotype classification for HS based on early available data. Our findings suggest that the efficacy of core cooling modalities may be phenotype-specific, supporting a hypothesis for a stratified management approach: for the non-severe phenotype, evaporation cooling may be key, while a conservative fluid strategy could help avoid complications related to circulatory overload. For the severe phenotype, aggressive cooling with high-volume cold-fluid infusion combined with evaporation may be necessary, although the identified threshold of approximately 1500 mL within the first 0.5 hours is exploratory and requires prospective validation. This hypothesis-generating framework offers a potential pathway toward precision management in the emergency care of HS, pending confirmation in future studies.
