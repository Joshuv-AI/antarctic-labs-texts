Horizontal Ridge Augmentation Using an rhBMP‐2 Loaded Volume‐Stable Collagen Matrix Compared to Guided Bone Regeneration: A Preclinical In Vivo Study
Authors: Jin‐Young Park, Gi‐Seo Song, Seung‐Hyun Park, Lorenzo Tavelli, David T. Wu, Daniel S. Thoma, Ronald E. Jung, Ui‐Won Jung, Jae‐Kook Cha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate whether incorporation of recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein‐2 (rhBMP‐2) into a volume‐stable collagen matrix (VCMX) enhances horizontal ridge augmentation compared with VCMX alone or guided bone regeneration (GBR).MethodsChronic horizontal ridge defects were created in five canines. Sites randomly received: (1) control, (2) VCMX, (3) VCMX with rhBMP‐2 or (4) GBR (deproteinised bovine bone mineral with a collagen membrane). After 8 weeks, outcomes were assessed by profilometry, micro‐computed tomography and histomorphometry.ResultsVCMX/rhBMP‐2 showed significantly greater new bone volume (181.50 ± 142.32 mm3) than GBR (36.06 ± 25.86), VCMX (8.40 ± 6.15) and control (7.32 ± 4.03 mm3) (all p < 0.05). Histometric new bone thickness was greater in VCMX/rhBMP‐2 than GBR at all levels (crestal: 2.31 ± 1.93 vs. 1.09 ± 0.41 mm; p = 0.038). Coronal contour gain was higher in VCMX/rhBMP‐2 than VCMX (p = 0.007), with no difference versus GBR (p = 0.654). At middle and apical levels, all augmentation groups exceeded control (p ≤ 0.011).ConclusionsRhBMP‐2 delivered via VCMX enhanced horizontal bone regeneration with augmented dimensions comparable to GBR.

Introduction

Guided bone regeneration (GBR) is a well‐established technique for horizontal and vertical ridge augmentation (Dahlin et al. 1988; Naenni et al. 2019; Sanz‐Sanchez et al. 2015; Thoma et al. 2019; Urban et al. 2019). However, in large, non‐contained defects with limited intrinsic healing potential, regenerative outcomes may be compromised. In particular, newly formed tissue in crestal and peripheral regions often exhibits limited maturation, likely due to restricted angiogenesis and reduced cellular ingrowth (Song et al. 2021). To overcome these biological limitations, growth factors such as recombinant human bone morphogenetic protein‐2 (rhBMP‐2) have been investigated to enhance mesenchymal cell recruitment and osteogenic differentiation (Benic et al. 2017; Jung et al. 2015; Kim et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2018; Thoma, Lim, et al. 2017).

RhBMP‐2 has been investigated as an osteoinductive adjunct to enhance bone regeneration. When delivered using an absorbable collagen sponge (ACS), it has been shown to promote de novo bone formation and increase osteogenic activity in alveolar defects (Fiorellini et al. 2005). Histological and clinical studies further suggest that rhBMP‐2 enhances tissue maturation, as evidenced by increased lamellar bone formation and graft‐to‐bone contact (de Freitas et al. 2016; Jung et al. 2003). Collectively, these findings support its potential to improve regenerative outcomes in ridge augmentation (Galarraga‐Vinueza et al. 2024).

Despite the osteoinductive capacity of rhBMP‐2, its regenerative efficacy depends on the properties of the carrier. ACS lacks mechanical stability and does not provide sustained space maintenance in non‐contained defects (Galarraga‐Vinueza et al. 2024; Jung et al. 2003). In contrast, a volume‐stable collagen matrix (VCMX) has demonstrated sustained dimensional stability, vascularisation and progressive tissue integration in clinical settings (Hammerle et al. 2023; Thoma et al. 2016). These characteristics indicate that VCMX may offer a more favourable scaffold for growth factor delivery (Tavelli et al. 2022). Combining rhBMP‐2 with such a matrix may therefore enhance osteoinduction while preserving the regenerative space.

Recent experimental studies have explored VCMX as a carrier for rhBMP‐2, demonstrating sustained release, preserved augmented volume and enhanced new bone formation (Cha et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2026). These findings suggest that such matrices may function as both space‐maintaining scaffolds and growth factor carriers.

Therefore, the aim of this preclinical in vivo study was to evaluate the regenerative potential of an rhBMP‐2‐loaded VCMX for horizontal ridge augmentation in a canine model. The null hypothesis was that there would be no difference in regenerative outcomes between the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group and a conventional GBR group.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

Five healthy adult male beagle dogs (aged 15–24 months, weighing approximately 15 kg) were included. In each animal, four chronic lateral alveolar ridge defects were created in the maxilla and randomly allocated to one of the following groups: Control: no re‐entry. VCMX group: VCMX soaked with 200 μL saline. VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group: VCMX loaded with 200 μL rhBMP‐2 solution (2.5 mg/mL). GBR group: 0.5 g deproteinised bovine bone mineral (DBBM; Bio‐Oss, Geistlich Pharma, Switzerland) covered with a natural collagen membrane (Bio‐Gide, Geistlich Pharma; 25 × 25 mm), stabilised with fixation pins (Membrane pin; Genoss, Korea).

The VCMX used was Geistlich Fibro‐Gide, which is a sterile, porous, resorbable, volume‐stable porcine collagen matrix. Detailed information on its composition, cross‐linking, porosity and degradation behaviour is provided in Appendix S1. In the present study, a 15 × 20 × 6 mm matrix was used according to group allocation.

All procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Yonsei University Health System; no. 2024‐0174) and conducted under standard laboratory conditions in accordance with relevant guidelines.

Sample Size

The sample size (five animals; 20 defects) was determined a priori based on ethical and logistical constraints inherent to large‐animal research based on the principle of 3Rs (replacement, reduction, and refinement) rather than formal power calculations. Post hoc power calculations demonstrated approximately 77% achieved power for the primary contrast (VCMX/rhBMP‐2 vs. GBR) (Appendix S2).

Surgical Procedures

Anaesthesia

All surgical procedures were performed under general anaesthesia induced with Zoletil (5 mg/kg, intramuscularly) and medetomidine (0.02 mg/kg, intravenously), followed by orotracheal intubation and maintenance with 2%–3% isoflurane. Local infiltration anaesthesia was administered using 2% lidocaine with 1:100,000 epinephrine.

Defect Creation

A full‐thickness mucoperiosteal flap was elevated via a sulcular incision in the maxilla. The second and fourth premolars were extracted, and the third premolar was hemisected with removal of the distal root, while the mesial root was preserved and protected with a calcium hydroxide–based material (Dycal) (Figure 1a).

(a, b) Experimental model and surgical procedures. Standardised chronic non‐contained lateral ridge defects (15 × 10 × 3 mm) were created in the maxilla following tooth extraction and hemisection. (c) After 10 weeks of healing, sites were re‐entered with full‐thickness flap elevation. (d) Augmentation procedures included GBR (DBBM with collagen membrane). Control sites received no treatment.

Standardised rectangular lateral defects (15 × 10 × 3 mm) were created by buccal bone reduction, resulting in non‐contained ridge morphologies (Figure 1b). Primary closure was achieved using 4–0 absorbable sutures (Monosyn, B. Braun, Korea).

Main Surgery

Following 10 weeks of healing, augmentation procedures were performed at all experimental sites except control sites. At each edentulous experimental site, a mid‐crestal incision and two vertical incisions were made, and full‐thickness mucoperiosteal flaps were elevated to expose the defect areas (Figure 1c).

In the GBR group, 0.5 g of DBBM was placed and covered with a collagen membrane (25 × 25 mm). The membrane was stabilised with three fixation pins on the buccal aspect and two on the lingual aspect, which resulted in over‐augmentation beyond the bony housing (Figure 1d). In the VCMX groups, a volume‐stable collagen matrix (15 × 20 × 6 mm) was positioned over the defect and secured to the lingual flap using a horizontal mattress suture. In the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group, the matrix was pre‐soaked in 200 μL of rhBMP‐2 solution (2.5 mg/mL) for 15 min prior to placement, based on previous studies (Cha et al. 2021; Kim et al. 2026). Periosteal releasing incisions were performed at the buccal flap as needed to achieve tension‐free primary closure, followed by suturing with 6–0 absorbable monofilament (Monosyn, B. Braun, Korea).

Sutures were removed after 10 days. After 8 weeks, the animals were euthanised and specimens were harvested and fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin.

Micro‐CT Analysis

Micro‐computed tomography (SkyScan 1173, SkyScan, Aartselaar, Belgium) was performed on harvested specimens (Appendix S3). Images were acquired in DICOM format and reconstructed using dedicated software (OnDemand3D, Cybermed, Seoul, Korea) for volumetric analysis.

The region of interest (ROI) was defined mesio‐distally by the crestal bone attachment of adjacent teeth and set to encompass only the augmented volume. Tissue outside the ROI, including native bone of the surrounding ridge, was excluded from quantification.

Within the augmented ROI, grayscale thresholds for tissue components were determined by direct comparison between histological sections and the corresponding spatially matched micro‐CT cross‐sections. For each calibration section, the boundaries among residual graft material, newly formed bone and non‐mineralised tissue were identified histologically by an experienced researcher (J.‐K.C.). The thresholds derived from this histology‐guided calibration were then applied consistently to the entire augmented volume (> 75: residual material; 50–75: newly formed bone; < 50: non‐mineralised tissue) (Figure 2). Histomorphometric Analysis

Specimens were decalcified, dehydrated, embedded in paraffin and sectioned in the bucco‐palatal plane (5 μm). Central sections were stained with Masson's trichrome, digitised (PANNORAMIC 250 Flash III; 3DHISTECH, Budapest, Hungary) and analysed using image analysis software (CaseViewer; 3DHISTECH; Adobe Photoshop) by a blinded, calibrated examiner.

To ensure alignment between histologic and micro‐CT sections, digitised histological images were calibrated using the scale bar and overlaid with the corresponding micro‐CT cross‐sections in Adobe Photoshop by adjusting the magnification and matching the anatomical landmarks (Figure 2e). An additional micro‐CT slice including the adjacent tooth root was used to define its long axis.

The most coronal point of the palatal bone crest served as the primary reference landmark, and the long axis of the adjacent tooth root was used as the vertical reference. Horizontal lines were drawn at 1, 2 and 3 mm apical to the crest, and thickness measurements were obtained in the bucco‐palatal direction (Saulacic et al. 2018). The following parameters were measured at each level: Augmented tissue thickness (AGT): the horizontal thickness of the entire augmented area measured from the native bone margin to the periosteum.New bone thickness (NBT): the horizontal thickness of the new bone area measured from the native bone wall to the outermost boundary of the new bone.

Each level was measured twice by the same examiner, and mean values were used for analysis (Figure 2). Intra‐examiner reliability was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.93).

Profilometric Analysis

Intraoral scans were obtained at baseline (before extraction), 10 weeks after extraction (before augmentation) and 8 weeks after augmentation prior to sacrifice (after augmentation). STL files were imported into SMOP (v2.21.2, Swissmeda AG, Switzerland) and superimposed using tooth‐supported registration based on adjacent non‐extracted teeth (Figure 2g).

Linear contour changes at the mid‐buccal region of the defect were calculated as the difference between pre‐ and post‐augmentation datasets at 1‐mm intervals from 0 to 10 mm apical to the baseline buccal gingival margin (Figure 2h) (Lee,Park, et al. 2022; Lee, Shin, et al. 2022). To minimise the influence of post‐extraction remodelling, the 0–3 mm region was excluded; measurements at 4, 7 and 10 mm were analysed as coronal, middle and apical levels, respectively. Measurements were performed in duplicate by a single examiner (ICC = 0.92).

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (v29.0.2.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). NBV was the primary outcome, with the defect site as the observational unit. A linear mixed‐effects model (LMM) was fitted, with treatment group as a fixed effect and dog as a random effect to account for clustering of multiple defects within each animal; pairwise comparisons used Tukey adjustment. As only five animals served as clustering units, the between‐animal random effect was estimated from a small number of clusters, and the mixed‐model results should therefore be regarded as exploratory.

Model assumptions were checked using the Shapiro–Wilk and Levene tests. Sensitivity analyses—LMM on log‐transformed TAV and NBV, and non‐parametric tests (Kruskal–Wallis with Mann–Whitney post hoc) for histomorphometric and profilometric outcomes—supported the principal findings. Adjusted p‐values < 0.05 were considered significant. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation.

Results

Healing was uneventful at all sites, and no adverse events, including excessive swelling, infection or wound dehiscence, were observed.

Micro‐CT Analysis

Three‐dimensional reconstructed micro‐CT images showed substantial mineralised tissue formation in both the GBR and VCMX/rhBMP‐2 groups. In the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group, the original rectangular shape of the VCMX appeared to be preserved. Additionally, the VCMX and VCMX/rhBMP‐2 groups exhibited a thicker crestal soft tissue profile compared with the control and GBR groups (Figure 3).

For NBV (new bone volume), the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group (181.50 ± 142.32 mm3) showed significantly greater volume compared with the control, VCMX and GBR groups (7.32 ± 4.03 mm3, 8.40 ± 6.15 mm3, and 36.06 ± 25.86 mm3, respectively, all p < 0.05) (Figure 4, Table S1). For TAV (total augmented volume), both the GBR and VCMX/rhBMP‐2 groups (433.07 ± 171.40 mm3 and 246.97 ± 219.20 mm3, respectively) showed significantly greater volumes than the control group (7.93 ± 4.43 mm3, p < 0.001). In addition, both the GBR and VCMX/rhBMP‐2 groups showed significantly greater volume than the VCMX group (8.88 ± 6.23 mm3, p < 0.05). No significant difference was observed between the GBR and VCMX/rhBMP‐2 groups after adjustment for multiple comparisons (p = 0.06, Figure 4, Table S1).

Descriptive Histology

In the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group, the original defect margin was largely indistinguishable from the newly formed bone after 8 weeks of healing (Figure 5). Lamellar bone at the defect wall was remodelled and continuous with newly formed bone, resulting in structural integration throughout the augmented region. The newly formed bone predominantly consisted of a woven bone with marrow spaces, extending towards a superficial layer of dense fibrous tissue resembling periosteum. The original rectangular shape of the VCMX appeared preserved.

In the GBR group, bone formation was primarily confined to areas adjacent to the original defect wall. The central portion of the augmented area consisted mainly of dense but unmineralised extracellular matrix populated by osteoblastic cells. In contrast, the peripheral region was largely occupied by residual graft particles embedded within loose connective tissue.

In the VCMX group, the collagen matrix was observed adjacent to the defect wall. Matrix degradation and the remaining thickness varied among specimens. At the matrix periphery, vascular infiltration and integration with surrounding connective tissue were evident, with the matrix in contact with both underlying bone and overlying soft tissues.

Discussion

The present experimental in vivo study, conducted in a chronic non‐contained horizontal ridge defect model, yielded three main observations: (i) the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group showed significantly greater new bone formation than the GBR group; (ii) overall augmented dimensions were comparable between the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 and GBR groups despite differences in the amount and location of newly formed bone; and (iii) VCMX/rhBMP‐2 showed significantly greater coronal contour gain than the control and VCMX groups, with contour changes comparable to GBR.

The feasibility of VCMX as a carrier for rhBMP‐2 has recently been demonstrated in a contained maxillary sinus model (C. E. Kim et al. 2026). To our knowledge, the present study is the first to evaluate this combination in a chronic non‐contained lateral ridge defect, an environment lacking bony containment and continuously exposed to soft tissue tension, in which graft stabilisation and space maintenance are critical. Whereas conventional GBR typically requires membrane fixation with multiple tacks or sutures (Park et al. 2022), VCMX placement was achieved with a single horizontal mattress suture (Thoma et al. 2016). Although not formally evaluated, this simplified fixation may offer practical handling advantages and avoids fixation‐pin removal at a second surgery.

In the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group, the augmented area consisted predominantly of newly formed woven bone, with minimal residual scaffold detectable after 8 weeks; the addition of rhBMP‐2 was associated with extensive replacement of the scaffold by newly formed bone on histological examination, with the augmented region maintaining its original shape. This pattern is consistent with the osteoinductive response previously reported for BMP‐2 (Cha et al. 2014; Han et al. 2021; Lee et al. 2015), although the underlying signalling cannot be confirmed without molecular or immunohistochemical analyses. In contrast, the VCMX group showed matrix persistence with predominantly connective tissue integration, suggesting that VCMX functions as a permissive scaffold whose tissue phenotype is determined by the presence of an osteoinductive stimulus.

The regenerative efficacy of rhBMP‐2 is influenced by dose, delivery site and carrier properties. The current FDA‐approved protocol for ridge augmentation involves delivery of rhBMP‐2 on an absorbable collagen sponge at doses ranging from 1 to 10 mg (McKay et al. 2007; Ramly et al. 2019). While the osteogenic effect of rhBMP‐2 is generally dose‐dependent, higher doses have been associated with adverse events, including severe postoperative swelling, bruising and ectopic bone formation (Galarraga‐Vinueza et al. 2024; James et al. 2016), motivating interest in lower doses.

Urban et al. (2019) reported enhanced vertical ridge augmentation with approximately 1 mg of rhBMP‐2, and Chao et al. (2021) demonstrated bone regeneration with as little as 5–50 μg in a canine peri‐implant defect model. In the present study, ∼0.5 mg per defect, which is well below the FDA‐approved range, produced enhanced bone formation, suggesting that effective osteoinduction may be achievable at reduced doses when rhBMP‐2 is delivered via VCMX, possibly through sustained local release (Kim et al. 2026). The previously reported release profile, however, was characterised at 0.1 mg/mL, and release from collagen‐based carriers is concentration‐dependent; release kinetics may differ at clinical concentrations.

In the GBR group, bone formation was largely confined to regions adjacent to the original defect wall and diminished towards the crest, with much of the augmented volume occupied by residual graft and non‐mineralised tissue. Comparable patterns have been reported in extensive lateral ridge augmentation, where bone formation beyond the original contour remains limited (Song et al. 2021). Although fibrovascular regions may further mineralise over time, this typically requires prolonged healing. While alveolar phenotype may influence long‐term remodelling (Quirynen et al. 2023), the present findings indicate that, in large non‐contained defects, regenerative limitations are primarily biological, with defect dimensions likely exceeding the osteogenic capacity of passive scaffold‐based GBR alone.

Augmented thickness and volume were comparable between GBR and VCMX/rhBMP‐2. At the coronal level, VCMX/rhBMP‐2 showed greater contour gain than VCMX alone, suggesting improved early volumetric stability. Although VCMX undergoes progressive resorption with dimensional reduction over time (Cairo et al. 2026; Cosyn et al. 2022; Ferrarotti et al. 2025; Hammerle et al. 2023), the addition of rhBMP‐2 appeared to enhance early volume preservation, possibly through osteoinductive conversion of the matrix into mineralised tissue.

Longer term studies are warranted to evaluate tissue maturation and volumetric stability, particularly to characterise remodelling of the woven bone observed with VCMX/rhBMP‐2 and the fate of the fibrovascular tissue within GBR sites. Inclusion of a GBR/rhBMP‐2 positive control would help isolate the contribution of the carrier system in non‐contained defects.

The flap design also merits consideration. The trapdoor‐like configuration with limited elevation created a confined subperiosteal compartment that may benefit rhBMP‐2 application by limiting diffusion and reducing the risk of ectopic bone formation; similar principles have been described for retentive or minimally elevated flap approaches in GBR (Park et al. 2023). Differences in flap tension may, however, have contributed to the variability in augmented volume in the VCMX/rhBMP‐2 group.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the canine model and 8‐week healing period preclude conclusions regarding long‐term maturation or clinical translation. Second, clinical use of rhBMP‐2 in dentistry is restricted to delivery via ACS, and application with alternative carriers such as VCMX is not yet established; the present findings should therefore be regarded as a proof of concept. Although the recognised adverse effects of rhBMP‐2—including severe swelling, ectopic bone formation, and adipogenic differentiation—were not observed in this controlled setting, careful dose selection, surgical confinement and patient monitoring would be essential in any clinical application. Third, local diffusion of rhBMP‐2 to non‐BMP sites cannot be excluded; any such diffusion would tend to enhance bone formation in the control groups, yielding a conservative estimate of the treatment effect. Fourth, although histology‐calibrated, threshold‐based micro‐CT segmentation cannot fully separate newly formed bone, native bone and residual graft owing to overlapping mineral densities. Excluding native bone at ROI definition addresses the former boundary, but some misclassification at the new bone–graft interface remains inherent. Finally, the small number of animals limits power to detect smaller effects, and the findings should be regarded as exploratory pending confirmation in adequately powered studies.

Within the limitations of this preclinical study in a chronic non‐contained defect model, the combination of rhBMP‐2 with a VCMX was associated with greater new bone formation compared with conventional GBR while maintaining comparable augmented dimensions. These findings suggest that incorporating an osteoinductive stimulus into a volume‐stable scaffold may offer an alternative for regenerating horizontal ridge defects. Further long‐term and translational studies are warranted to evaluate tissue maturation, volumetric stability, and clinical applicability.
