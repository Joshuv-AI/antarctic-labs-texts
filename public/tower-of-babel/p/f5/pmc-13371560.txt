Aquablation for dual burden disease - BPH and chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome: a single-center prospective pilot study
Authors: Asem F Ghanim, Marcio Covas Moschovas, Jessica Injiac, Andrea Chiriboga, Steven Allegro, Tyler Rivera, Michael McDonald
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) accounts for approximately 90–95% of prostatitis cases and is defined by pelvic pain or discomfort persisting for at least three of the preceding six months in the absence of identifiable infection. Despite its high prevalence, management remains challenging, and no standardized treatment approach has been established. In men with concomitant benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), bladder outlet obstruction may contribute to symptom persistence or severity. Aquablation, a robot-assisted, waterjet-based therapy used for the treatment of BPH, enables precise, heat-free prostatic tissue removal and may provide symptomatic benefit in this clinical setting.MethodsWe performed a prospective pilot study including 20 men aged 45 years or older with diagnoses of both BPH and CP/CPPS who reported clinically significant symptoms, defined as an NIH-CPSI score of 8 or greater at screening. All patients underwent Aquablation between July and December 2024 and were evaluated at 1, 3, and 6 months following the procedure. Primary outcome measures included changes in International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) and NIH-CPSI, with assessment across pain, urinary, and quality-of-life (QoL) domains.ResultsSignificant improvements in both IPSS and NIH-CPSI scores were observed by 3 months postoperatively (p < .001), with further modest and sustained improvements noted at 6 months. The greatest symptom reductions were seen in pain and quality-of-life domains. No major adverse events occurred during the study period.ConclusionsAquablation was associated with improvement in both lower urinary tract symptoms and CP/CPPS-related symptom measures in men with coexisting BPH and CP/CPPS. These preliminary findings support further investigation of Aquablation as a potential treatment option for selected patients with this challenging clinical overlap.Keywords: Aquablation, Benign prostatic hyperplasia, Chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome, NIH Category III prostatitis, Lower urinary tract symptoms, International Prostate Symptom Score, NIH-Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index, Minimally invasive prostate surgery, Quality of life

Introduction

Chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (CP/CPPS) represents the most common form of prostatitis, accounting for approximately 90–95% of all cases [1–3]. The condition is defined by chronic, nonbacterial pelvic pain and/or discomfort persisting for at least three of the preceding six months, in the absence of documented urinary tract infection or other identifiable causes [4, 5]. As a diagnosis of exclusion, CP/CPPS remains one of the more challenging conditions encountered in urologic practice, owing to its poorly understood pathophysiology, heterogeneous clinical presentation, and inconsistent response to standard therapies [6–10].

The NIH categorizes prostatitis into four clinical entities: acute bacterial prostatitis (Type I), chronic bacterial prostatitis (Type II), chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome (Type III), which is further subdivided into inflammatory (Type IIIA) and non-inflammatory (Type IIIB) forms, and asymptomatic inflammatory prostatitis (Type IV) [3, 11].

Management of CP/CPPS typically begins with empirical medical therapy, including antibiotics, alpha-blockers, 5-alpha reductase inhibitors, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), phytotherapy, or combinations thereof. In patients with refractory symptoms, treatment strategies may expand to include centrally acting pain-modulating therapies such as gabapentinoids and antidepressants, including tricyclic antidepressants and serotonin-norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors, particularly when neuropathic pain mechanisms or central sensitization are suspected. These approaches are typically used as part of a multimodal strategy alongside pelvic floor physical therapy, behavioral interventions, and other symptom-directed therapies, with surgical intervention reserved for carefully selected cases [12–15]. Despite the breadth of available options, evidence supporting the effectiveness of these therapies remains limited. With the exception of a small number of uncontrolled studies with methodological constraints, robust data are lacking, and no standardized treatment algorithm has been established. Consequently, clinical management remains variable and largely focused on symptom control rather than disease modification [6, 16].

An overlap between benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) and prostatitis-like symptoms has been reported in approximately 5–20% of men [17]. Pain serves as the primary distinguishing feature between these two conditions. Notably, a diagnosis of chronic prostatitis has been associated with a 70% increased likelihood of requiring subsequent treatment for BPH [18]. Several mechanisms may underlie this association. Bladder outlet obstruction related to BPH may increase voiding pressure, promote urinary reflux into prostatic ducts, and contribute to intraprostatic inflammation, thereby exacerbating pelvic pain and prostatitis-like symptoms [17, 19, 20]. Conversely, chronic inflammation, pelvic floor dysfunction, and neural sensitization associated with CP/CPPS may amplify lower urinary tract symptoms and worsen symptom perception in men with underlying prostatic enlargement [6, 13, 21, 22]. Together, these interacting processes suggest that BPH and CP/CPPS may not simply coexist, but may be clinically interrelated and contribute to persistent urinary and pain-related symptom burden [17, 18].

Given this anatomical, symptomatic, and pathophysiological overlap, surgical intervention may be a reasonable consideration in carefully selected patients with refractory CP/CPPS when the prostate is suspected to play a central role in symptom generation. Aquablation is an FDA-approved, minimally invasive, robot-assisted therapy developed for the treatment of BPH that enables precise, heat-free tissue resection with preservation of surrounding structures. Its non-thermal, anatomically targeted approach may offer potential advantages in this population by reducing collateral injury to surrounding tissues, including neural elements, compared with heat-based resection. The present study explores the role of Aquablation in men with concurrent BPH and CP/CPPS, with particular emphasis on symptom relief and quality-of-life outcomes over time.

Materials/subjects and methods

This single-center, prospective pilot study was conducted to evaluate the effect of Aquablation on symptom outcomes in men with BPH and coexisting CP/CPPS. Consecutive patients were enrolled from the outpatient urology clinic between July and December 2024. The study employed a pre–post design, with evaluations performed at baseline and at 1, 3, and 6 months following surgery. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. The study protocol was approved by the AdventHealth Institutional Review Board (IRB no. 2070285) and was conducted in accordance with applicable ethical and regulatory standards.

The diagnosis of CP/CPPS was established through a combination of patient-reported symptom questionnaires, clinical assessment, and exclusion of chronic infection. As part of routine clinical care, all men presenting with lower urinary tract symptoms suggestive of BPH completed both the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) and the National Institutes of Health Chronic Prostatitis Symptom Index (NIH-CPSI) at initial evaluation. Patients were considered eligible if they met NIH-CPSI criteria consistent with Category III CP/CPPS in conjunction with a compatible clinical history, physical examination findings, and absence of chronic bacterial infection on urinalysis and urine culture. Patients were not systematically subclassified as NIH Category IIIA or IIIB, as formal inflammatory localization testing was not performed in this pilot study. Additional laboratory testing and imaging studies were obtained as clinically indicated to exclude alternative diagnoses.

The IPSS is a validated questionnaire assessing prostate-related lower urinary tract symptoms and consists of seven symptom questions (total score range 0–35, with higher scores indicating greater severity) and a separate quality-of-life (QoL) item. It is routinely used to quantify symptom severity, track changes over time, and guide treatment decisions [23, 24]. The NIH-CPSI is a validated 13-item instrument designed specifically for CP/CPPS and evaluates symptom burden across pain, urinary, and QoL domains [25, 26]. Both instruments were administered at baseline and at all postoperative follow-up visits.

All patients underwent Aquablation under general anesthesia using the AquaBeam® Robotic System (PROCEPT BioRobotics, San Jose, CA, USA). Prophylactic intravenous antibiotics were administered preoperatively. Hemostasis following resection was achieved with minimal focal bladder-neck cautery with a bipolar resectoscope following waterjet resection. Following the procedure, patients were observed overnight and discharged according to routine institutional postoperative care protocols. Patients were routinely discharged with a Foley catheter, which was removed in clinic after 5 days.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint of this prospective pilot study was improvement in symptom questionnaire scores, specifically IPSS and NIH-CPSI, in men with BPH and NIH Category III CP/CPPS at 1, 3, and 6 months following Aquablation. The secondary endpoint was comparison of outcomes observed in this cohort with published results from other institutions reporting Aquablation or transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) in similar patient populations.

Inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were men aged 45 years or older with a clinical diagnosis of BPH and NIH Category III CP/CPPS, confirmed by medical history, physical examination, and validated symptom questionnaires, consistent with the age range represented in the pivotal prospective Aquablation trials [27, 28] and with the population in whom BPH-related outlet obstruction is more likely to be clinically relevant. All patients were deemed appropriate candidates for Aquablation by the treating urologist. Clinically significant CP/CPPS symptoms were required, defined as an NIH-CPSI score of 8 or greater at screening [29], along with bothersome lower urinary tract symptoms attributable to BPH as documented by IPSS. Additional requirements included a recent negative urinalysis and urine culture to exclude active infection, prostate cancer screening in accordance with institutional practice (including PSA testing and biopsy when indicated), and prostate volume within device-appropriate parameters on imaging. Patients were required to be able to safely discontinue anticoagulation according to perioperative protocol, provide informed consent, and comply with scheduled follow-up visits.

Exclusion criteria

Patients were excluded if they had acute prostatitis, prostatic abscess, active urinary tract infection, known bleeding disorders, inability to tolerate anesthesia, or inability to safely discontinue anticoagulant therapy within one week prior to surgery. Patients who were unable to complete study questionnaires or adhere to follow-up requirements were also excluded.

Aquablation technology and surgical procedure

Aquablation is a minimally invasive, robot-assisted surgical therapy for BPH performed using the AquaBeam® Robotic System (PROCEPT BioRobotics, San Jose, CA, USA). This FDA-cleared platform integrates real-time transrectal ultrasound imaging with a high-velocity saline-based waterjet to resect prostatic tissue without the use of thermal energy [27, 28, 30–35]. The heat-free nature of the procedure is intended to limit collateral tissue injury and reduce the risk of damage to surrounding structures, including the external urinary sphincter and ejaculatory ducts [34, 35].

The procedure may be performed under general or spinal anesthesia. Following placement of the transrectal ultrasound probe, the surgeon develops an individualized resection plan using the system console, targeting obstructing adenomatous tissue while preserving key anatomical landmarks. Waterjet ablation typically lasts 4–8 min, with total operative time averaging 40–60 min depending on prostate size. A three-way Foley catheter is placed at the conclusion of the procedure, and most patients are discharged within 24 h [28, 33].

Follow up

Postoperative follow-up was conducted at 1, 3, and 6 months. At each visit, patients completed repeat IPSS and NIH-CPSI questionnaires. Uroflowmetry and bladder ultrasound were performed as clinically indicated. Adverse events were monitored from the time of surgery through the final study visit. Data collection was performed either in person or by telephone, depending on visit type and patient availability. No additional CP/CPPS-specific treatment protocol was implemented during follow-up beyond routine postoperative care.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.5.0 (R Core Team, 2025). Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. For symptom score outcomes, both mean (standard deviation, SD) and median (interquartile range, IQR) values are reported to account for potential non-normality in score distributions. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages.

Changes in IPSS, IPSS QoL scores, and NIH-CPSI domain scores (pain, urinary, and QoL) between baseline and each postoperative time point (1, 3, and 6 months) were evaluated using paired t-tests for normally distributed data and Wilcoxon signed-rank tests for non-normally distributed data. Overall changes across multiple time points were assessed using repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) for parametric data or Friedman tests for non-parametric data. Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were adjusted using the Bonferroni correction. Statistical significance for the primary ANOVA models was defined as p < .05, with a Bonferroni-adjusted threshold of p < .008 applied to post-hoc comparisons. Clinical relevance was additionally assessed using minimal clinically important difference (MCID) thresholds. A clinically meaningful improvement was defined as a decrease of at least 3 points in IPSS [36] and at least 6 points in total NIH-CPSI score [37]. Responder analyses were performed by calculating the proportion of patients meeting these thresholds at 1, 3, and 6 months.

Results

Between July and December 2024, a total of 23 men meeting eligibility criteria were enrolled in the study. Three patients were excluded from the final analysis: one patient died from causes unrelated to the study intervention, and two patients were lost to follow-up. The remaining 20 patients completed all scheduled follow-up visits and constituted the final analytic cohort.

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. The median age of participants was 67 years (IQR, 61.8–72.2 years), and the median prostate volume was 62.6 mL (IQR, 46.0–94.5 mL). These findings are consistent with a cohort affected by both BPH and chronic pelvic pain symptoms. Baseline infection assessment did not demonstrate evidence of active or chronic bacterial infection in any participant. Although one patient had a positive “bacteria, urine” urinalysis field, urine culture was negative in all participants, and NIH Category III CP/CPPS classification was based on clinical assessment together with the absence of culture-confirmed chronic bacterial infection at baseline.

Significant improvements were observed in both the IPSS and the NIH-CPSI at all postoperative time points, including 1, 3, and 6 months following Aquablation. Reductions in NIH-CPSI scores were observed across all evaluated domains, including pain, urinary symptoms, and quality of life. Improvements were similarly reflected in IPSS total and quality-of-life scores. The magnitude and durability of these changes over time are detailed in Tables 2 and 3 and illustrated in Figs. 1 and 2. The observed symptom changes also exceeded commonly cited thresholds for clinical relevance. Mean improvement in IPSS from baseline was 12.6 points at 1 month, 15.9 points at 3 months, and 16.35 points at 6 months, exceeding the minimal clinically important difference (MCID) threshold of 3 points. Mean improvement in NIH-CPSI total score was 13.85, 16.85, and 18.1 points at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively, exceeding the commonly cited clinically meaningful threshold of 6 points. In responder analyses, 17/20 (85%), 19/20 (95%), and 19/20 (95%) patients achieved clinically meaningful improvement in IPSS at 1, 3, and 6 months, respectively. For NIH-CPSI total score, 15/20 (75%), 17/20 (85%), and 17/20 (85%) patients achieved clinically meaningful improvement at the same timepoints.

No major procedure-related adverse events were observed during the study period. Specifically, no patient required blood transfusion, no bleeding event required return to the operating room, and no study participant returned to the emergency department during follow-up. Overall patient retention was high, with the majority of participants completing all planned assessments through the 6-month follow-up.

Discussion

In this single-center, prospective pilot study of men with BPH and NIH Category III CP/CPPS, Aquablation was associated with significant and durable improvements in both lower urinary tract symptoms and chronic pelvic pain. Substantial reductions in IPSS were observed at 3 and 6 months postoperatively, accompanied by parallel improvements in IPSS QoL scores. Similarly, NIH-CPSI scores declined across all domains, including pain, urinary symptoms, and quality of life, resulting in overall score reductions consistent with clinically meaningful benefit. However, given the single-arm design and lack of a comparator group, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory. Importantly, the observed symptom reductions were not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful, with mean changes exceeding accepted MCID thresholds for both IPSS and NIH-CPSI and the majority of patients meeting responder criteria at each follow-up timepoint.

These findings are directionally consistent with the limited literature evaluating transurethral surgical treatment in men with overlapping obstructive and prostatitis-related symptom phenotypes. In a recent single-center retrospective study, transurethral enucleation with bipolar (TUEB) in men with BPH and CP/CPPS was associated with substantial improvement in NIH-CPSI scores over time, with mean NIH-CPSI total score decreasing from 21.7 at baseline to 11.1 at 1 month, 7.9 at 3 months, 4.8 at 6 months, and 4.5 at 12 months, with parallel improvement across pain, urinary, and quality-of-life domains [38]. In parallel, randomized and long-term prospective studies of Aquablation in general BPH populations have consistently demonstrated robust improvement in LUTS, with reported mean IPSS reductions generally in the range of 15–17 points [27, 34]. In the WATER trial, Aquablation and TURP both produced marked symptom improvement at 6 months, meeting the prespecified noninferiority endpoint [27]. Five-year follow-up data further demonstrated durable symptom improvement, with mean IPSS improvement of 15.5 points for Aquablation and 13.5 points for TURP [34]. Although these Aquablation studies were not conducted specifically in men with NIH Category III CP/CPPS and did not assess NIH-CPSI, they provide important context for the magnitude and durability of urinary symptom improvement observed in the present cohort [27, 34]. Taken together, the available literature supports the possibility of benefit across both urinary and prostatitis-related symptom domains while highlighting the need for condition-specific prospective comparative studies.

Aquablation possesses several procedural characteristics that may be particularly relevant in patients with coexisting CP/CPPS. Its image-guided, heat-free waterjet resection allows precise removal of adenomatous tissue while minimizing collateral damage to surrounding structures. This may be beneficial in this population by relieving bladder outlet obstruction and reducing prostatic tissue that may contribute to inflammation or symptom generation. Avoidance of thermal injury may also limit pro-inflammatory effects and reduce collateral injury to adjacent tissues, potentially including neural and sphincteric structures, which could partly relate to the observed improvements in pain and quality-of-life domains. However, preservation of specific neural structures was not directly assessed in this study, and any mechanistic relationship to pain improvement should be interpreted cautiously.

CP/CPPS remains a notoriously challenging condition to manage and is frequently refractory to conventional therapies, including antibiotics, alpha-blockers, phytotherapy, and anti-inflammatory agents. The clinical complexity of CP/CPPS reflects its multifactorial pathophysiology, which likely involves a combination of inflammatory processes, neuropathic pain mechanisms, pelvic floor dysfunction, and bladder involvement. When CP/CPPS coexists with BPH, bladder outlet obstruction may further exacerbate symptoms and perpetuate pain. In this setting, surgical approaches that simultaneously address obstruction and prostatic tissue involvement may represent a rational therapeutic strategy for patients who have exhausted conservative options.

Our findings also contribute to the growing body of literature supporting Aquablation as a viable alternative to TURP for the management of BPH. Previous studies have demonstrated Aquablation to be non-inferior to TURP with respect to symptom relief, while offering favorable safety and functional outcomes, including preservation of ejaculatory function—an important consideration for younger or sexually active patients [28, 30–32, 34, 35]. In addition, emerging real-world evidence suggests that these benefits extend beyond the setting of highly selected clinical trial populations. A recent comparative analysis of the international collaborative aquablation research urology society (ICARUS) database demonstrated that real-world Aquablation outcomes were broadly comparable to those reported in the pivotal WATER and WATER II trials, supporting the generalizability and effectiveness of this therapy in routine clinical practice [39]. However, the role of Aquablation in patients with CP/CPPS has not been well characterized. To our knowledge, this study is among the first to prospectively evaluate Aquablation in a population enriched for CP/CPPS, providing early evidence for its potential utility in this underserved subgroup.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. The small sample size and single-arm design limit the generalizability of the findings and preclude definitive conclusions regarding causality. In the absence of a control group, the observed improvements may reflect the effect of intervention, natural symptom fluctuation, placebo response, regression to the mean, or a combination of these factors. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Detailed preoperative CP/CPPS-directed medication use and concomitant non-surgical therapies were not systematically collected, limiting our ability to assess their potential contribution to observed symptom changes. Postoperative analgesic use was limited to the immediate perioperative period and was unlikely to have materially influenced symptom assessments at later follow-up timepoints. Symptom changes across instruments should also be interpreted cautiously because NIH-CPSI and IPSS are not entirely independent measures and share overlapping urinary and quality-of-life constructs. Consequently, parallel improvements in these domains may partly reflect the same underlying symptom changes rather than distinct treatment effects. Additionally, follow-up was limited to 6 months, and the long-term durability of symptom relief remains uncertain. Larger, controlled studies with extended follow-up are needed to confirm these preliminary observations and to directly compare Aquablation with TURP and other surgical or non-surgical treatment modalities in patients with BPH and CP/CPPS.

Conclusion

In this prospective pilot study, Aquablation was associated with clinically meaningful improvements in both lower urinary tract symptoms and chronic pelvic pain in men with coexisting benign prostatic hyperplasia and chronic prostatitis/chronic pelvic pain syndrome. These findings provide preliminary, hypothesis-generating evidence that Aquablation may be associated with improvement across both urinary and CP/CPPS-related symptom domains in this selected patient population.

Future studies with larger prospective cohorts and comparator or case-control arms, as well as longer follow-up, are warranted to confirm these findings and to further define the role of Aquablation in the management of men with coexisting BPH and CP/CPPS.
