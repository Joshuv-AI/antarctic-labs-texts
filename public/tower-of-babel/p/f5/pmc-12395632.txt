Concomitant gastric cancer and neuroendocrine tumours in the stomach: a rare case series of 3 patients and a literature review
Authors: Luyu Liu, Weilu Ding, Zhenzhen Wang, Gongning Wang, Limian Er
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConcomitant gastric cancer and neuroendocrine tumours in the stomach are very rare, and their clinical characteristics, pathogenesis and treatment principles remain poorly understood.Case presentationWe present three cases of concomitant gastric cancer and neuroendocrine tumours in the stomach, all of which were pathologically diagnosed through endoscopy and biopsy. One patient received total gastrectomy, while the other two patients underwent endoscopic resection. Moreover, the diagnostic characteristics and treatment of synchronous gastric cancer and neuroendocrine tumours are summarized and analysed in combination with a study of 8 cases.ConclusionsG1 NET may be more likely to be concomitant with gastric cancer. Endoscopic resection is the preferred treatment for patients with synchronous early gastric cancer and NETs.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Gastric NET, Endoscopic treatment

Background

Neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) are a heterogeneous group of neoplasias of peptidergic neurons and neuroendocrine cells that originate in the human body and can occur anywhere in the body, most commonly in the digestive system, such as the stomach, intestine, and pancreas. NENs in the tubular digestive tract (including the pancreas) are known as gastroenteropancreatic neuroendocrine neoplasms (GEP-NENs). At present, the epidemiology of NENs in China has not been reported [1]. Jin-Hu Fan et al. studied the epidemiology of GEP-NENs in China and reported that the most common primary sites of GEP-NENs were the pancreas (31.5%) and rectum (29.6%), followed by the cardia (11.6%) and gastric body (15.4%) [2]. Therefore, the incidence of gastric neuroendocrine tumours is low, and cases of gastric neuroendocrine tumours coexisting with gastric cancer are even rarer. The endoscopic characteristics, pathological characteristics and pathogenesis of this condition remain poorly described. Here, we report three extremely rare cases of gastric cancer coexisting with gastric neuroendocrine tumours and review the relevant literature.

Case presentation

Patient 1. A 65-year-old man presented to our hospital with a history of intermittent abdominal distension for 9 months. An inquiry into the patient’s past medical history revealed a 30-year smoking history. Additionally, the patient had a family history of gastric cancer, and his mother died from cardiac cancer. Laboratory tests revealed no evidence of anaemia or abnormal liver or kidney function. Gastroscopy revealed a whitish, superficial elevated lesion in the greater curvature of the upper gastric body and a shallow ulcerated surface in the broad curvature of the body. The initial endoscopic diagnosis was a gastric neuroendocrine tumour. Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) revealed significant thickening of the gastric wall in the distal antrum with heterogeneous enhancement. Scans of the liver, gallbladder, pancreas, spleen, kidneys, and pelvis were unremarkable. After cardiopulmonary function tests were completed and surgical contraindications were excluded, the patient underwent laparoscopically assisted total gastrectomy and oesophagojejunal Roux-en-Y anastomosis. The pathological findings of the surgical samples revealed moderately differentiated tubular adenocarcinoma at one site and neuroendocrine tumours (G1) at two sites. The patient was eventually diagnosed with synchronous gastric adenocarcinoma (T1bN0M0) and NETs (G1). After the surgery, the patient was intermittently re-examined via gastroscopy. Five years later, high-grade intraepithelial neoplasia of the oesophageal squamous epithelium was found, and further endoscopic submucosal dissection (ESD) treatment was performed.

Patient 2. A 63-year-old male patient, who denied any subjective symptoms, underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy at another hospital as part of his annual health checkup, which unexpectedly revealed a gastric lesion. The patient had been a smoker for 30 years. Oesophagoscopy revealed that the mucosa was slightly rough between the 12:00 and 4:00 positions at the dentate line of the cardia. Additionally, the exam showed evidence of mucosal compression and elevation on the posterior wall of the greater curvature of the gastric body. Pathological examination of the biopsy samples indicated severe dysplasia of the glandular epithelium. Further ultrasound gastroscopy was performed at our hospital (Figure 1), which revealed a large red area of the mucosa at the 11–4:00 position of the cardia, with partial thickening. Narrow band imaging (NBI) and magnifying endoscopy (ME) examination of the cardia revealed clear boundaries and microvascular disorganization that was reticulate or dendritic in appearance. The mucosa of the gastric body was thin, and multiple nodular protuberances measuring 0.2–0.5 cm were observed in the greater curvature and anterior and posterior walls. Exploration of the lesions with a 20 MHz ultrasound microprobe revealed that the first and second layers were hypoechoic and thickened, the third layer was thinner, and some areas could be interrupted. Ultrasonography of the body nodules revealed hypoechoic nodules of approximately 0.3–0.5 cm, and the third layer at the anterior wall was thinner. The following endoscopic diagnosis was made: cardiac intramucosal lesions and multiple NETs in the stomach. No abnormalities were detected on laboratory examination. Chest and abdominal CT revealed local mucosal thickening of the cardiac wall, and an enhanced scan revealed marked enhancement, with a maximum thickness of approximately 1.6 cm. Gastroscopy was repeated one month later (Figure 2). After preoperative tests revealed no contraindications, the patient underwent ESD for cardiac lesions and EMR for multiple NETs (Figure 3). The lesion area was marked and then lifted with a submucosal injection of a mixed solution of methylene blue saline. The tumour was resected without complications. Histopathological examinations revealed that the lesions in the cardia were high-grade squamous intraepithelial neoplasia (HSIN) and high-grade glandular intraepithelial neoplasia (HGAIN). The other five lesions were all NETs (G1). Two weeks later, supplementary treatment after ESD was performed again because of the positive surgical margin of the patient’s postoperative pathology. The patients were followed up regularly for half a year, and no recurrence was observed.

Patient 3. A 54-year-old man was found to have gastric adenocarcinoma on physical examination. He denied any subjective symptoms, and the initial laboratory work-up revealed no abnormalities. Ultrasound gastroscopy was performed at our hospital (Figure 4), which revealed two lesions in the greater curvature of the gastric body. The lesions on the anterior wall were grey and white and approximately 0.6 cm in size. The lesion on the posterior wall was hyperemic and oedematous and was approximately 0.8 cm in size. NBI+ME revealed that the boundary of the two lesions was clear. On the anterior wall, the glands were disordered and thick, showing a staggered distribution, and on the posterior wall side, the glands were of different sizes, and local irregular microvessels were observed. An ultrasound probe revealed that the mucosal layer of the anterior wall of the lesion was slightly thickened, the thickness was approximately 2.2 mm, and the thickness of the adjacent normal mucosa was approximately 1.6 mm. The posterior wall of the lesion contained 1–2 layers of hypoechoic nodules, measuring approximately 4.8 × 7.4 mm, and the posterior submucosa was uneven and continuous. The endoscopic diagnosis was a gastric body lesion (the lesion on the posterior wall was suspected to have submucosal invasion). The depth of tumour invasion detected was estimated to be within the mucosal layer (m) or submucosal layer (sm), with little potential for lymph node involvement. Therefore, ESD combined with EMR was proposed as an alternative to radical surgery, and patient consent was obtained. After delineating the extent of the lesion, a methylene blue-saline mixture was injected into the submucosa of the posterior wall lesion, resulting in adequate elevation of the lesion. Subsequently, the lesion was carefully dissected, and the procedure was successfully completed. The lesion on the anterior wall was successfully resected by a snare. The final pathological results revealed that the lesion in the greater curvature of the upper body was high-grade glandular intraepithelial neoplasia (HGAIN) with focal infiltration of cancer, and the lesion behind the greater curvature was a NET (G1) without vascular invasion or nerve invasion (Figure 5). The patient was followed up regularly for nearly five years, and gastroscopy was re-examined every year, which revealed no signs of recurrence.

Discussion and conclusions

Neuroendocrine tumours are rare tumours that originate from peptiderrgic neurons and neuroendocrine cells and are characterized by neuroendocrine differentiation and the expression of neuroendocrine markers. Gastric neuroendocrine tumours (G-NETs), which originate from enterochromaffin-like cells (ECFs) of the gastric mucosa, constitute less than 2% of all gastric tumours [3]. Modlin et al. conducted an extensive analysis of 13,715 carcinoid tumours over a span of 50 years and reported that only 8.7% of carcinoid tumours located in the gastrointestinal tract were found in the stomach [4]. Gastric neuroendocrine tumours are relatively rare, and the simultaneous presence of gastric NETs alongside gastric cancer is extremely rare in clinical practice; very few cases have been reported.

Early gastric cancer is defined as tumor invasion limited to the mucosa or submucosa, regardless of the presence of regional lymph node metastasis. Intramucosal neoplasia is classified into three broad categories on the basis of histopathology: noninvasive low grade, noninvasive high grade, and cancer with invasion of the lamina propria [5]. Glandular structural atypia (glandular structure disorder, dense crowding, complex branching, papillary structure, significant size difference, etc.) and cellular atypia (pleomorphism, irregular, dark staining, increased nuclear cytoplasmic ratio, frequent nucleoli, and pathological mitotic figures, etc.) are the pathological features of gastric cancer. In terms of immunohistochemical features, Gurzu S et al. suggested that intramural carcinomatosis of the lymph vessels and multifocality could indicate tumor aggressiveness. Microsatellite status, E-cadherin, HER-2, p53, and Ki67 were not significant for the prognosis of early gastric cancer [6]. Pathology is the “gold standard” for diagnosis, and immunohistochemical staining is an irreplaceable method for diagnosis and differential diagnosis of NETs. Synaptophysin and chromogranin A (CgA) are widely recognized as universal markers that need to be detected. Most NETs exhibit diffuse expression of synaptophysin, whereas the expression of CgA varies among NETs depending on their location and degree of differentiation. The immunohistochemical results of NET in the three cases we provided showed Syn (+), CgA (+). The classification of neuroendocrine tumors depends on the determination of tumor cell proliferation activity, which is conventionally evaluated by Ki-67 proliferation index. Specific methods were as follows: Ki-67 proliferation index (%) ≤ 2 was G1, 2 < Ki-67 proliferation index (%) ≤ 20 was G2, Ki-67 proliferation index (%) > 20 was G3 [7]. In this study, both nets in Patient 1 were Ki67 (positive cell count 2%), all five nets in Patient 2 were Ki67 (positive cell count 1%), and one NET in Patient 3 was Ki67 (positive cell count 2%). Consequently, the NETs grade for all three cases presented herein were classified as G1.

From 2007 to 2020, a total of 126 cases of NET were found in the Fourth Hospital of Hebei Medical University, of which only 3 cases of gastric cancer combined with NET(2.4%). A review of the literature revealed that a total of 8 cases of synchronous gastric cancer and NETs have been reported (Table 1) [8–10]. Based on a comprehensive analysis of our three cases in conjunction with the eight previously reported cases, we conclude that the known grades of NETs were all G1. Four patients (36%) had multiple NETs. Among the 11 patients, 8 had accompanying autoimmune gastritis. Synchronous gastric cancers were all in the early stage, well differentiated and confined to the mucosa or submucosa. They can occur in various parts of the stomach, and there is no obvious correlation between their locations. A better understanding of these tumours will facilitate more effective detection and diagnosis of lesions. Below, we discuss several clinical issues, even if patients with these two types of tumours lack specific clinical manifestations. The pathogenesis of gastric cancer complicated with NETs and its relationship with autoimmune gastritis remain unclear. G1 NETs may be more likely to be complicated with gastric cancer. Simultaneous endoscopic resection of both tumours simultaneously is feasible.

Regarding the first clinical issue, we found that patients with gastric cancer complicated with NETs lacked typical clinical symptoms. Most NETs are nonfunctional, and patients lack specific clinical manifestations [11]. Advanced gastric cancer may cause abdominal pain, melena, emaciation, anaemia and other symptoms due to tumour invasion and metastasis. However, gastric cancer often lacks specific clinical manifestations in the early stage When early gastric cancer and NETs were combined, even patients with both cancers lacked typical clinical manifestations. Most lesions are found accidentally by routine physical examination and are very easy to ignore. As a result, many cancers are found at an advanced stage, and the survival time of patients is greatly shortened. Moreover, the quality of life of patients is greatly reduced because of gastrectomy and digestive tract reconstruction. Fortunately, the development and popularization of endoscopic technology have not only increased the detection rate of gastric cancer in the early stage but also significantly increased the diagnostic rate of gastrointestinal NETs (≤ 10 mm) at an earlier stage [12]. Therefore, early gastric cancer screening is an important strategy to achieve early diagnosis and treatment and improve patient prognosis.

We also explored the relationship of autoimmune gastritis with the pathogenesis of gastric cancer with NETs. Gastric NETs are divided into three types according to their pathogenesis. Type 1 is associated with autoimmune chronic atrophic gastritis and accounts for approximately 80% of all gastric NETs. Type 2 is often secondary to Zollinger–Ellison syndrome, and Type 3 is rarer and often presents as sporadic [13]. In our case series, chronic atrophic gastritis was found in 8 patients (73%). Autoimmune atrophic gastritis is a precancerous lesion that can lead to Type 1 NETs and gastric adenocarcinoma. The proposed mechanism suggests that autoimmune gastritis induces the production of antiparietal cell antibodies, resulting in a reduction in parietal cells. This decrease subsequently leads to diminished gastric acid secretion, which in turn stimulates the secretion of gastrin by G cells located in the gastric antrum. Gastrin then promotes the release of histamine, which exerts a trophic effect on ECFs. The proliferation of ECFs may ultimately contribute to the development of NETs [14]. In contrast, gastric adenocarcinoma is a stage in which the gastric mucosa undergoes inflammation, metaplasia, atrophy, and dysplasia and finally becomes cancerous [15]. Autoimmune atrophic gastritis may lead to intestinal metaplasia or long-term hypergastrinemia, which in turn leads to the development of adenocarcinoma [16]. Therefore, patients with atrophic gastritis, who are at high risk of gastric cancer and NETs, should be actively treated and regularly monitored by gastroscopy. Furthermore, it has been proposed that ECFs may significantly contribute to gastric carcinogenesis indirectly through the secretion of Reg proteins. Reg protein can stimulate the proliferation of gastric cells and has the activity of directing cell differentiation into dominant and parietal cell lineages. The proliferation of ECFs, which contributes to the development of NETs and concurrently increases the release of Reg protein, further induces chronic growth stimulation in gastric progenitor cells, potentially elevating the long-term risk of gastric adenocarcinoma development [17]. In addition, patients with current or previous NETs have a 15–25% risk of developing a second malignancy [12], and second primary tumours are usually more aggressive. Therefore, patients usually die from the second tumour [18]. However, the underlying mechanism remains unclear and warrants further investigation.

Third, we found that the NETs associated with gastric cancer were all Grade G1. In 2019, the WHO updated the classification and grading of neuroendocrine neoplasms (NENs) of gastrointestinal, hepatobiliary and pancreatic organs. NENs are divided according to their degree of differentiation into well-differentiated neuroendocrine tumours (G1, G2 and G3), poorly differentiated neuroendocrine carcinomas (NECs) (including small cell types and large cell types), and mixed neuroendocrine nonneuroendocrine neoplasms (MiNENs) [19]. In this cohort of eight patients with established tumour grades, the NETs were classified as G1. This finding may indicate that G1 NETs are more prone to complications associated with secondary gastric malignancies. However, the pathogenesis of gastric cancer and NETs remains unclear, suggesting that patients diagnosed with NETs (G1) should be advised to adhere to regular postoperative gastroscopy to detect second tumours in the early stage. In addition, we identified four patients with multiple NETs. Whether patients with multiple NETs are more likely to have a second tumour remains to be explored. The treatment of multiple NETs is a difficult problem. Specifically, these tumours are difficult to cure with surgery, and regular review is needed, and the possibility of a second primary tumour should be considered during follow-up.

Finally, simultaneous endoscopic resection of both tumours is feasible. Currently, the recommended treatment principles for NETs are as follows: For early gastric NETs, namely, well-differentiated G1 gastric NETs with lesions less than 1 cm in size that neither infiltrate the muscularis propria nor undergo angioinvasion, endoscopic resection is the preferred treatment approach [12]. Endoscopic resection has been very successful in treating early gastric cancer that is limited to the mucosa or superficial submucosa [20]. With the gradual development and improvement of endoscopic technology, ESD or EMR can be properly selected to remove two coexisting lesions at one time if both meet the indications. This treatment approach is characterized by its minimally invasive nature, high patient tolerance, and ability to maintain a superior quality of life postsurgery. It not only effectively eradicates the tumour but also circumvents major surgical interventions while preserving the full functionality of the digestive tract.

In conclusion, synchronous gastric cancer/NET is extremely rare. It is known that autoimmune gastritis is a common risk factor for both Type 1 NET and gastric cancer. Therefore, if a patient is diagnosed with Type 1 NET and has a background of autoimmune gastritis, endoscopists should conduct meticulous screening for early gastric cancer in order to find and treat it in time. Moreover, G1 NET may be more likely to be concomitant with gastric cancer. In terms of treatment, endoscopic resection is the preferred treatment for patients with synchronous early gastric cancer and NETs.
