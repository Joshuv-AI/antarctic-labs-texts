Herpes zoster vaccination and vaccine preferences among persons aged 50–64 years in Australia: Findings from a discrete choice experiment
Authors: Sumitra Shantakumar, John Litt, Robert Booy, Lawrence Vandervoort, Vince Grillo, Emma Bandy, Raunak Parikh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTVaccine-preventable herpes zoster (HZ) poses substantial burden among Australian adults ≥50 years of age (YOA) despite available vaccination. This study aimed to understand the HZ vaccine-related preferences of adults paying out-of-pocket for HZ vaccination, to facilitate targeted recommendations by physicians and ultimately reduce disease burden. A discrete choice experiment was conducted (March–May 2023) to quantify preferences for HZ vaccine attributes among adults 50–64 YOA: HZ-naïve with selected self-reported comorbidities (n = 525; each comorbidity: n = 75), HZ-naïve without comorbidities (n = 150), and current/former HZ patients (n = 150). Each choice task comprised a “no vaccine” option and three hypothetical HZ vaccine profiles characterized by five attributes with varying levels. Attributes and levels were identified through literature review/concept elicitation/cognitive interviews/expert opinion. The attributes that most influenced HZ vaccine choice (measured by relative importance [RI]) were recommendation by government guidelines/medical societies, then HZ lifetime risk reduction, and protection duration. HZ-naïve adults with comorbidities indicated lower RI of recommendation by government guidelines/medical societies and higher RI of HZ lifetime risk reduction than other respondents. Between HZ-naïve adults without comorbidities and HZ patients, there were no significant differences in RI of each attribute. Respondents with comorbidities, whether overall or grouped by comorbidity, shared identical top three attributes. Between HZ-naïve, HZ-vaccinated adults (n = 146) and those without vaccination (n = 529), each top three attribute (recommendation by government guidelines/medical societies, HZ lifetime risk reduction, and protection duration) showed significantly different RI (p <.001). Findings elucidate the motivations underlying HZ vaccine preferences among Australian adults 50–64 YOA, guiding physician-patient conversations about HZ vaccines.KEYWORDS: Discrete choice experiment, herpes zoster, herpes zoster vaccine, intention, patient preference, predictors, vaccination

Introduction

The burden of herpes zoster (HZ), a vaccine-preventable disease,1 on patients in Australia is substantial, especially for those ≥50 years of age (YOA).2 HZ and its complications, particularly post-herpetic neuralgia and herpes zoster ophthalmicus,3,4 can lead to debilitating disease that severely impacts patients’ quality of life.3–5 The adjuvanted recombinant vaccine (RZV), registered for use locally, is funded under the National Immunisation Program (NIP) for adults ≥65 YOA, Aboriginal and/or Torres Strait Islander adults ≥50 YOA, and adults ≥18 YOA with moderate to high HZ risk due to selected underlying conditions and/or immunomodulatory/immunosuppressive treatments.6,7 Although the Australian Technical Advisory Group on Immunization (ATAGI) recommends RZV for adults ≥50 YOA,7 HZ vaccination remains a personal expense for non-Indigenous adults 50–64 YOA.

Based on the Australian Bettering the Evaluation and Care of Health general practitioner database, overall HZ incidence in 2006–2013 was 5.6 per 1,000 persons (95% confidence interval [CI]: 5.2, 6.1). HZ incidence noticeably increased with age beginning at 50 YOA and increased over time, showing a rise of 2.5 per 100,000 patient encounters annually in new HZ cases in April 1998–March 2013.2 As these data were recorded before HZ vaccination was funded under the NIP,8 they may reflect the HZ risk in the Australian population without HZ vaccination or those ineligible for NIP funding.

It has been well established that the risk of HZ infection can be heightened by the presence of certain comorbidities. Of note, a published systematic review and meta-analysis reported statistically significant positive associations between HZ risk and comorbid conditions including asthma, cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD), chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), diabetes, inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), and rheumatoid arthritis (RA).9 These findings are supported by other studies conducted over the past decade that examined the impact of these diseases on HZ risk.10–15 The immunocompromising effects of chronic diseases and immunosuppressive therapies used to manage conditions such as asthma and COPD can impair cell-mediated immunity,12,15,16 leading to the reactivation of latent varicella zoster-virus and resulting in HZ infection.15 Given Australia’s aging population and increasing HZ burden over time, vaccination for older adults warrants consideration.2

As adults ≥50 YOA in Asia-Pacific generally trust physicians for HZ vaccine-related information,17 physician-initiated discussions on HZ vaccination remain key to facilitating patients’ decision-making.18 However, sparse information regarding vaccine attributes that patients value and use to inform vaccination/vaccine choice creates hurdles for physicians to initiate these conversations.18–21 Providing physicians this information may streamline their discussions with patients to facilitate HZ vaccination-related decision-making. In Australia, physician-led discussions with patients 50–64 YOA on HZ vaccination are crucial given the substantial disease burden.

Given that adults 50–64 YOA in Australia can choose to be vaccinated against HZ, we conducted a discrete choice experiment (DCE) to understand their preferences. A DCE was chosen for reasonable external validity and accuracy of findings in reflecting real-world preferences.22,23 We aimed to gauge how different HZ vaccine attributes may inform the local population’s vaccination decisions, including those with selected comorbidities.

Methods

The methods of this DCE study conducted in Australia are similar to those reported in a published manuscript on a DCE study performed in South Korea.24

Overall study design

Similar to the DCE study in South Korea,24 this was a three-phased, cross-sectional study administered to respondents in Australia (Figure 1). Full details of each phase are in Supplementary Appendix 1.

Overall study design. DCE: discrete choice experiment; HZ: herpes zoster; IRB: institutional review board; YOA: years of age.

The DCE was deployed in the quantitative data collection phase (Phase 3) as a self-administered, online survey questionnaire for pre-consented survey panel respondents. All interviews and the online survey were conducted in English. Preferences for key HZ vaccine attributes from the perspective of adults 50–64 YOA were quantified. Results were then used to calculate the relative importance (RI) that respondents placed on HZ vaccine attributes and trade-offs between attributes.

Systematic differences in preferences and trade-offs were evaluated in three cohorts of respondents 50–64 YOA: HZ-naïve general public with selected comorbidities that increased their risk of HZ,9 HZ-naïve general public without selected comorbidities, and current/former HZ patients. All cohorts included individuals with no HZ vaccination history and those who received past vaccination with zoster vaccine live (ZVL).

DCE survey design

Attributes and levels

The set of attributes and levels were finalized using literature review, concept elicitation interviews, cognitive interviews, as well as expert clinician feedback and input (Supplementary Appendices 1 and 2).24

Attributes were clinically relevant, addressed potential concerns of vaccine recommendation decision-makers (including physicians), and were meaningful and salient to respondents. Each attribute had two to four levels, which spanned the clinically relevant range of outcomes that has been or might be seen in clinical trials or real-world practice. It covered the full extent of trade-offs among attributes that respondents were willing to make within the set limits that were defined before they took the DCE survey. Differences in levels encompassed the range of improvements in efficacy outcomes or the range of increases in the risk of toxicities that could be seen in clinical trials or real-world practice.

Vaccine cost and healthcare professionals’ (HCPs) recommendations are generally understood to strongly impact vaccination decision-making.17,18 Hence, these attributes were excluded from the final DCE to focus on the impact of vaccine characteristics and guidelines on perceived attribute importance.

Survey questions

Survey questions related to the primary objectives included a series of eight preference-elicitation questions based on the experimental design.24 Respondents were asked to choose among three hypothetical HZ vaccine profiles (defined by attributes finalized in the previous phases; the attribute lists evaluated in each phase are in Supplementary Appendix 2 and Supplementary Tables S1 and S2) and a “no vaccine” option. Five attributes with two to four levels each (Table 1) were used to estimate a preference model with acceptable CIs for all parameters and avoid respondent fatigue. Experimental design

Study population

Eligible respondents included individuals 50–64 YOA. They could read and understand English, were aware of HZ, and self-reported that they were not hesitant to take vaccines before survey participation. Respondents were recruited via online panels (Kantar Profiles and/or its panel partners). Specialized healthcare recruiters (EKAS and Growman Research and Consulting Pvt Ltd) were used to recruit some of the respondents with comorbidities. The panel participants were from Australian Capital Territory, New South Wales, Northern Territory, Queensland, South Australia, Tasmania, Victoria, and Western Australia.

Respondents were provided a statement of informed consent before survey participation. Panel members of Kantar Profiles and/or its panel partners who completed the survey received panel points equivalent to USD 3–4, based on the Kantar Profiles panel system, as honorarium.

Stratification and sample size

The total sample size was 825, consisting of individuals 50–64 YOA who were (i) members of the general public, naïve of HZ and with selected self-reported comorbidities (n = 525; n = 75 for each comorbidity: asthma, CKD, COPD, CVD, diabetes, IBD, and RA, (ii) members of the general public, naïve of HZ and without self-reported selected comorbidities (n = 150), and (iii) current/former HZ patients (n = 150). Cohorts that comprised members with or without any of the above comorbidities are hereafter referred to as cohorts “with comorbidities” or “without comorbidities,” respectively. Minimum DCE study sample size is typically calculated using significance level, statistical power level, the statistical model used in the DCE analysis, initial belief about the parameter values, and DCE design.25 As initial beliefs about the parameter values could not be determined for this DCE, a variance-covariance matrix was not established for minimum sample size calculation. As published preference studies have sample sizes of 100–300 respondents,26 n = 525 or n = 150 per cohort were considered adequate sample sizes for the intended analysis. The overall sample size (N = 825) ensured a standard error of <0.05 for all attribute levels, meeting the precision criteria recommended for DCE results.27

Cohorts were pooled to analyze the relative preference weights (PWs) for each attribute level, and subgroup comparisons were conducted to understand the differences in preferences among these groups. Data analysis at the pooled response level was based on unweighted data as no weighting was applied to the subgroups.

Subgroups

Bivariate analyses of relative attribute importance were conducted on the following sets of subgroups:24

HZ-naïve general public versus current/former HZ patients.

HZ-naïve general public versus current/former HZ patients.

HZ-naïve general public with one of the selected comorbidities versus those without.

HZ-naïve general public with one of the selected comorbidities versus those without.

HZ-naïve general public who had not received any past HZ vaccination versus those who had received past HZ vaccination with ZVL.

HZ-naïve general public who had not received any past HZ vaccination versus those who had received past HZ vaccination with ZVL.

Respondents who received a vaccine other than a HZ vaccine (hereafter referred to as “non-HZ vaccine”) within the past year versus those who had not.

Respondents who received a vaccine other than a HZ vaccine (hereafter referred to as “non-HZ vaccine”) within the past year versus those who had not.

Endpoints

The primary endpoint was the estimated relative PWs based on the attributes and levels included in the chosen hypothetical HZ vaccine options. The RI of each vaccine attribute over the range of attribute levels and the trade-offs between attributes were calculated using the relative PWs.24

Data analysis

Relative PWs for each attribute level were estimated from respondents’ answers to the preference-elicitation questions. The study sample was described with respect to demographics and general health characteristics, HZ experience, HZ vaccination status, and comorbidities using appropriate statistical methods: categorical variables by counts and percentages, and continuous variables by percentages, means, standard deviations, standard errors, minima, medians, and maxima. Due to the study’s exploratory nature, p-values from statistical tests are descriptive without multiple comparisons adjustment.24

Other data analysis methods used are reported in Supplementary Appendix 5.

Ethics approval

This study received approval from Bellberry Human Research Ethics Committee (application number: 2021–12-1453-A-1) prior to study start.

Across the general public with comorbidities, general public without comorbidities, and current/former HZ patient cohorts, the age distribution was generally similar (Table 2). Among the cohorts, 36.0% (54/150; same proportions in general public without comorbidities and current/former HZ patients) to 49.0% (257/525; general public with comorbidities) were male and 52.7% (79/150; general public without comorbidities) to 68.0% (102/150; current/former HZ patients) had private medical insurance.

Among the general public with comorbidities, 87.0% (457/525) self-reported that they had at least one physician-diagnosed condition from a larger list of conditions (see Table 2 for full list). The three most frequent conditions were diabetes without chronic complications (161/525, 30.7%), hypertension (157/525, 29.9%), and rheumatologic disease (including arthritis and swollen joints; 108/525, 20.6%). Of the general public without comorbidities, 25.3% (38/150) had at least one listed condition that was not among the selected comorbidities; the three most frequently reported were hypertension (31/150; 20.7%), rheumatologic disease (5/150; 3.3%), and prior non-prostate malignancy that was currently in remission (2/150; 1.3%). Among current/former HZ patients, 62.7% (94/150) had at least one listed condition and they self-reported hypertension (60/150, 40.0%), diabetes without chronic complications (33/150, 22.0%), and rheumatologic disease (26/150, 17.3%) as the three most frequently diagnosed.

Approximately a quarter of the general public with comorbidities (140/525, 26.7%) had received a vaccine against HZ, whereas 4.0% (6/150) of those without comorbidities and 22.7% (34/150) of current/former HZ patients had received such a vaccine.

DCE analysis

DCE levels

As an attribute level became more favorable, the frequency that the level was chosen out of the number of times it was presented to the respondents increased in each cohort (Table 4). For example, the option of a 51% reduction in the lifetime risk of contracting HZ was chosen by the general public with comorbidities 18.4% of the time, while a 90% reduction was chosen 46.3% of the time (p <.001). Similarly, among those without comorbidities, 51% reduction and 90% reduction in lifetime risk of HZ were chosen 20.1% and 43.3% of the time (p <.001), respectively. Among current/former HZ patients, these levels were chosen 20.9% and 43.6% of the time, respectively (p <.001). All attributes exhibited this trend, except short-term side effects where the general public with comorbidities chose “one day with flu or mild injection site reaction after each dose” (35.7%) more often than “none” (32.8%; p <.001). When presented with any permutation of the attribute levels for each vaccine option and the choice to opt out of receiving a vaccine for every choice task, >95% of respondents in each cohort chose a vaccine (Table 4).

Relative attribute importance according to past non-HZ vaccination

Among the HZ-naïve general public, those who received non-HZ vaccination within the past year ranked the attributes identically as that of all cohorts collectively.

Similar to all respondents and most other subgroups, respondents who had not received a non-HZ vaccine within the past one year ranked recommendation by government guidelines/medical societies as having greatest influence on HZ vaccine choice (mean RI [95% CI]: 40.7% [33.5%, 47.8%]). However, the other attributes were ranked differently: duration of protection (22.4% [15.4%, 29.4%]), followed by short-term side effects (16.2% [12.3%, 20.1%]), reduction of lifetime risk of getting HZ (12.1% [7.2%, 17.1%]), and prevention of HZ-related complications (8.6% [4.1%, 13.0%]). There was no significant difference between the RI of duration of protection, reduction of lifetime risk of getting HZ, and short-term side effects.

Discussion

This study evaluated preferences for clinically relevant HZ vaccine attributes from the perspectives of the HZ-naïve general public and current/former HZ patients 50–64 YOA in Australia.

Respondents generally shared positive beliefs regarding HZ vaccines, especially those with prior vaccination (against HZ/other diseases) or HZ experience, consistent with previous studies.28,29 In this study, individuals with prior HZ vaccination appeared to recognize the need for HZ prevention and may therefore have viewed HZ vaccination/vaccines positively. Moreover, HZ-experienced individuals may choose vaccination to avoid the pain and negative quality of life impacts of HZ.17 These observations highlight the potential role of HCPs in educating the public on vaccination benefits and available vaccination options.30

Among the total study population, a HZ vaccine recommended by national and international health authorities was chosen almost half of the time that the level was presented as an option. This is corroborated by other survey studies which highlight the importance of recommendations for vaccine implementation.31,32 A HZ vaccine that could decrease the risk of HZ occurrence by 90% was chosen approximately half of the time. A HZ vaccine conferring ≥10 years of protection was chosen more than a third of the time. Moreover, respondents were willing to exchange improved prevention of long-term HZ-related complications and fewer short-term vaccine side effects for greater HZ risk reduction and extended protection duration. These observations suggest that HZ vaccine efficacy may play a larger role than reactogenicity profile in vaccination decision-making. These findings align with observations from a US-based DCE study which concluded that vaccine efficacy was among the attributes with greatest impact on HZ vaccine choice.18 Increasing efforts in relaying scientific evidence and knowledge on efficacy may help improve HZ prevention awareness in the Australian population. Nonetheless, as our study findings are only indicative of the present HZ vaccine-related sentiments of the population based on their order of preferences, HCPs and potential vaccinees should still consider key attributes including vaccine safety in HZ vaccination decision-making.

Overall, all cohorts ranked most attributes similarly based on RI. Unlike the general public without comorbidities and current/former HZ patients, the general public with comorbidities ranked prevention of HZ-related complications higher than short-term side effects. This was potentially due to individuals with comorbidities being more aware and therefore concerned about their increased HZ risk and HZ-related complications such as post-herpetic neuralgia, a sentiment that is usually corroborated by their healthcare providers. Significant differences were noted in the RI of recommendation by government guidelines/medical societies and the duration of protection between the overall general public with comorbidities and the CKD cohort. A higher rate of positivity toward HZ vaccines also was reported among the general public with comorbidities, especially those with CKD. Of note, CKD is an important risk factor for HZ and based on a population-based cohort study conducted in Taiwan, CKD patients suffering from HZ may face increased risk of end-stage renal disease.33

Among all cohorts, there was significant difference between the RI of recommendation by government guidelines/medical societies and both reduction of lifetime risk of getting HZ and protection duration. This trend was also observed within the individual general public with comorbidities, general public without comorbidities, and current/former HZ patient cohorts. Notably, the general public without comorbidities placed higher importance on recommendation by government guidelines/medical societies than those with comorbidities and current/former HZ patients. Individuals considering HZ vaccine options may choose vaccines that are recommended by the authorities as trustworthy, especially when corroborated by HCPs’ recommendations, and may therefore be more willing to receive them.17,29 Hence, it is important for local HCPs to consider prioritizing discussions on HZ vaccines recommended by Australian government bodies (such as the ATAGI and the Australian Government Department of Health and Aged Care) and medical societies in conversations with their patients, including those without comorbidities.

Although doctor’s recommendation was examined in the concept elicitation and cognitive interview phases, this was excluded from the DCE attributes list due to saturation. For this DCE, HCPs, rather than their patients, were presumed to mainly drive HZ vaccination discussions. Moreover, it is generally established that HCP recommendations regarding HZ vaccines strongly impact vaccination decision-making.17,29 Therefore, our analysis focused on elucidating the impact of vaccine characteristics and guidelines on respondents’ perceptions of attribute importance. Furthermore, respondents in the earlier phases (with smaller sample sizes than the DCE phase) prioritized other attributes over doctors’ recommendations (Supplementary Appendices 1 and 2). Nonetheless, as individuals with comorbidities generally interact with their HCPs more frequently than those without, future studies could investigate the degree of influence HCP recommendations may have on this demographic’s HZ vaccine preferences.

Vaccine cost was excluded as an attribute to focus on the impact of differential clinical profiles of HZ vaccines on respondents’ preferences. Nevertheless, the substantial impact of cost on affordability and individuals’ willingness to pay for and receive certain vaccines was acknowledged.18,31,34 Recommended HZ vaccines in Australia are generally out-of-pocket for this study’s target population (adults 50–64 YOA) who may be less willing to receive HZ vaccination than those eligible for NIP-funded vaccination. Further analyses of the impact of vaccine cost on HZ vaccine-related preferences in the Australian public 50–64 YOA should be considered.

Our findings highlight the vital role of Australian HCPs and health authorities in shaping HZ vaccination behaviors among older adults, particularly those who bear the cost of vaccination themselves. Through a better understanding of the preferences and concerns of specific patient groups, Australian HCPs can thus tailor conversations to address attributes pertinent to individual patients and provide them with appropriate recommendations. Prior research has shown the need for improved consumer education about HZ vaccination in Australia.35 Therefore, HCPs’ proactivity in initiating patient-centric dialogue remains crucial to ensuring a high level of patients’ awareness and knowledge of HZ disease, as well as emphasizing the need for prevention through vaccination. Published evidence also suggests that HZ and certain chronic conditions, such as CVD and diabetes, may mutually increase the risks of getting the other disease and exacerbate their associated complications due to underlying pathophysiological connections.36,37 These findings have prompted the call for HZ vaccination as a preventive measure against both HZ and specific chronic comorbidities.36,37 Participation in HZ vaccination advocacy should therefore extend beyond general practitioners and pharmacists in Australia to include HCPs from other specialties that care for patients who may be at higher risk of developing HZ in addition to their existing conditions. This is important as general practitioners may expect specialists managing the patients’ comorbidities to make and follow up on HZ vaccination recommendations.

The insights from this study may support Australian health authorities and advisory bodies in designing HZ vaccination programmes, policies, and evidence-based recommendations to effectively encourage older adults to be vaccinated against the disease, especially those aged 50–64 years and with specific comorbidities that increase their risk of HZ. Through the collective efforts of HCPs and the health authorities, these interventions may reduce the overall disease and economic burden of HZ in Australia, improve patients’ quality of life, and support longer healthy life expectancy among the local population.

Limitations

A limitation of this study was the exclusion of populations that were hesitant in accepting HZ vaccines for themselves or their family members. Additionally, the study population’s education levels and socio-economic statuses were possibly higher than that of the local population. Thus, the findings may not represent the entire Australian population. Future studies of HZ vaccine-related preferences among a larger Australian population may better inform public health strategies to improve vaccine acceptance.

Conclusion

Among attributes studied – and excluding cost considerations or HCP recommendation of a vaccine – vaccine recommendation (national and/or international) by government guidelines or medical societies was the most influential to HZ vaccination decision-making among the Australian population 50–64 YOA, followed by reduction of lifetime risk of getting HZ and protection duration. The general public, including those with comorbidities, were willing to accept nearly identical trade-offs between different attribute levels, apart from minor differences in ranking of certain attributes by importance. By elucidating the motivations underlying HZ vaccine-related preferences, these findings can help guide HCPs in their conversations with patients, especially those with selected comorbidities.
