Deviation control that deteriorates with fixation distance in basic-type intermittent exotropia
Authors: Ryota Takada, Akemi Wakayama, Fumi Tanabe, Shunji Kusaka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/aimsThe clinical assessment of deviation control at near (30 cm or 1/3 m) and far (5–6 m) does not reflect if and how patients’ control changes between these two predetermined test distances. We examine how patients’ control varies at 6 fixation distances between the two clinical test distances of 30 cm and 5 m.MethodsThe medical records of 48 patients with basic intermittent exotropia (IXT) were retrospectively reviewed. Patients who had visited the Kindai University Hospital between October 2021 and March 2022 without a prior history of treatments except spectacle correction were included. Eye position was examined with refractive correction using the cover test, and 6 fixation distances at 30 cm, 1 m, 2 m, 3 m, 4 m, and 5 m were tested in increasing order. Control assessed at each fixation distance was classified using the Kushner’s method (2019). This study rated the control of a poor or fair level as unfavorable and the control of an excellent or good level as favorable. The distance at which the patient’s control deteriorated was analyzed.ResultsOf 48, 38 (79%) patients had a favorable baseline control, and 8 of them could maintain a favorable control at all distances. Of the 30 patients who could not maintain the favorable baseline control, 17 (57%) at 1 m, 2 (7%) at 2 m, 6 (20%) at 3 m, 2 (7%) at 4 m, and 2 (7%) at 5 m were observed with a control deterioration. The incidence of control deterioration at 1 m was significantly higher than the incidence at other distances (between 1 and 2, 4, 5 m, p < 0.01; between 1 and 3 m, p < 0.05).ConclusionsDeviation control in patients with basic IXT was affected by fixation distance and most likely worsened at 1 m. Background

Intermittent exotropia (IXT) is an outward deviation with two states: a latent state of exophoria and a spontaneously manifest exotropia. When IXT is treated surgically, timing for operation is mainly determined based on the magnitude of the exodeviation angle, deterioration of deviation control, deterioration of near or distance stereopsis, and the patient’s quality of life [1]. While exodeviation and stereopsis can be quantified objectively, quantification of control is difficult. By scoring the cover-uncover test (CUT) result, the current methods developed to assess control include the Newcastle Control Score [2], Mayo Clinic’s office-based scale [3], and Look And Cover, then Ten Seconds of Observation Scale for Exotropia (LACTOSE) [4, 5]. In 2019, Kushner classified control into 4 grades: excellent, good, fair, and poor. In cases of poor or fair control deteriorating at either distance or near fixation, whichever is worse, treatment intervention is considered necessary [6].

Deviation is more obvious in distance vision in IXT [7, 8]. Patients’ deviation control first deteriorates at distance, and eventually control at near also becomes poor. Clinically, control is assessed at near (30 cm or 1/3 m) and distance (5–6 m). However, control assessed at near and distance does not reveal if and how patients’ control changes between these two predetermined test distances, or at what distance the control change occurs. Because patients view objects at various distances in daily tasks, such information helps understand patients’ eye position changes in daily visual environments, and early response to patients’ conditions will be possible. To our knowledge, no clinical studies have investigated how patients’ control varies with viewing distance.

By assessing patients’ control at 30 cm, 1 m, 2 m, 3 m, 4 m, and 5 m, this study aimed to examine patients’ control change from near to far between the two clinical test distances of 30 cm and 5 m.

Methods

Patients

Subjects in this retrospective study were 48 patients (26 males; age range, 3 years and 4 months to 33 years and 8 months, mean, 8.2 ± 5.1 years) with basic type IXT [near/distance deviation difference of < 10 prism diopters (PD)], who had visited the Kindai University Hospital between October 2021 and May 2022. Patients with corrected visual acuity of ≥ 1.0 (LogMAR equivalent 0.0), the spherical equivalent of refractive error was − 0.83 ± 2.02 D in the right eye and − 0.94 ± 2.16 D in the left eye. No prior history of treatments except spectacle correction, and under the age of 35 years were included. Additionally, no patients showed obvious signs of fatigue at the time of examination. The exclusion criteria included stereopsis worse than 240″ by the JACO Stereo Test, a history of amblyopia, a vertical deviation angle of ≥ 5 PD, a dissociated vertical deviation, and patients with known systemic diseases.

The protocol of this study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Kindai University Faculty of Medicine and adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all the patients or parents or the legal guardians of under the age of 16.

Test methods

Eye position was examined with refractive correction by the CUT. Six fixation distances at 30 cm, 1 m, 2 m, 3 m, 4 m, and 5 m were tested in increasing order, and each fixation distance was tested by the same examiner with an accommodative target. Control was assessed at each fixation distance using the Kushner’s method [6] (Table 1). This study rated the control of a poor or fair level as unfavorable and the control of an excellent or good level as favorable. In the patients who had shown a control deterioration from favorable to unfavorable, factors associated with control deterioration including age, the near and distance deviation angles, the presence/absence of suppression, and the measurement of the near point of convergence (NPC) were also investigated. Deviation angles were measured at 30 cm and 5 m using the alternate prism cover test (APCT). The presence of suppression was confirmed if diplopia did not occur during the CUT. Using an accommodative target, the NPC measurements of ≤ 10 cm and ≥ 11 cm were respectively classified as good and defective.

Statistical analysis

The distance at which patients’ control deteriorated from favorable to unfavorable was analyzed using the Steel-Dwass multiple comparison test. In addition, an a priori power analysis was conducted using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7, 2020; Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Germany). Assuming α = 0.05, power = 0.90, a correlation among repeated measures of 0.5, and a nonsphericity correction ε = 1, the required sample size was calculated to be 26. The logistic regression analysis was performed to analyze the factors associated with control deterioration at and beyond 1 m. A probability level of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Patients’ control at the 6 fixation distances

Table 2 shows the patients’ control at the 6 fixation distances. Of the 38 patients with a favorable baseline control at 30 cm, 30 patients showed control deteriorating to an unfavorable level while 8 patients maintained a favorable level at all fixation distances. In total, 30 (62%) and 18 (38%, 8 favorable and 10 unfavorable) patients were observed with and without a control change within 5 m, respectively. We further studied the 30 patients with deteriorated control to find out at what distance between 1 m and 5 m their favorable baseline control had deteriorated. The result showed that 17 (56.7%) and 13 (43.3%) patients respectively had control deterioration at 1 m and beyond 1 m, and the number of patients showing deteriorated control at 1 m was significantly larger than those at the other 4 fixation distances (Table 2; p < 0.01 for 2, 4, and 5 m, p < 0.05 for 3 m, the Steel-Dwass test for multiple comparisons).

Discussion

The current results showed that patients’ deviation control was affected by fixation distance in 62% of the patients with basic IXT. Furthermore, significantly more patients showed control deterioration at 1 m than at other fixation distances. These findings suggested that convergence, which is significant for patients’ deviation control, might have considerably reduced beyond 1 m and thus, exotropia was prone to manifest.

While IXT is known to be exotropic at distance, it becomes exophoric at near and the true deviation may not be revealed (i.e., tenacious proximal fusion [9]) owing to convergence. Convergence has four components: tonic, fusional, accommodative, and proximal convergence [10]. Although tonic convergence is a physiological mechanism, the other components of convergence are driven by distinct stimuli: fusional convergence is mediated by retinal disparity, accommodative convergence is driven by accommodation, and proximal convergence is triggered by psychological awareness of nearness. Near fixation involves all three active components of convergence. Among these, proximal convergence accounts for up to 70% of the total convergence response and is characterized by a high response velocity [11]. Therefore, it is considered a major contributor to near deviation control [11, 12]. However, both proximal and accommodative convergence decrease with increasing viewing distance and become minimal at a 3-m distance [13]. Consequently, deviation control at a distance is considered to rely predominantly on fusional convergence. Patients with IXT demonstrate increased disparity-driven fusional convergence at a distance compared with normal subjects [14]. Furthermore, the lateral intraparietal area, which processes disparity cues, has been identified as a potential neural locus involved in IXT [15]. These findings suggest that disparity-induced fusional convergence plays a crucial role in controlling deviation at a distance.

Additionally, studies have reported that the fusion reserve ratio, defined as the convergence reserve divided by the angle of deviation, correlates more strongly with IXT control than compared to the absolute fusional amplitude [16], highlighting the importance of considering the angle of deviation when evaluating convergence capacity. In our study, no significant difference was found in the angle of deviation between the group that showed control deterioration at 1 m and the group that showed deterioration beyond 2 m. Therefore, the decline in control observed at 1 m may be attributed to differences in the relative contributions of the three convergence components rather than to differences in deviation magnitude. Further investigations with larger sample sizes are warranted to clarify this issue.

In this study, none of the five factors investigated were found to be a factor for control deterioration. Gökgöz Özişik G et al. [17] also analyzed the factors (sex, age, family history, refractive error, and deviation angle at distance) for IXT control assessed using the Mayo Clinic’s office-based scale. They concluded that only the deviation angle at distance is a predictor of control, which is different from the current result. We considered that the different objective variables in the two studies might be the reason for the discrepancy between their and our results. It may be possible to use the distance deviation angle to predict patients’ total control at near and far. However, it will be difficult to use patients’ distance or near deviation angle, age, presence of suppression, or NPC measurement to determine if patients’ control will deteriorate at 1 m. Even so, patients view objects at various distances in daily life, and control measured at various distances better reflects patients’ frequency of exotropia in daily life and thus, early detection of deteriorated control will be possible.

Because this study focused on the effect of fixation distance, only patients with basic IXT were included to avoid the influence of the deviation angle difference. Therefore, the current results may not apply to patients with other types of IXT and this is the limitation of this study. A larger number of patients with different types of IXT will be necessary for a future study on the factors for deviation control. Additionally, the Accommodative convergence to accommodation (AC/A) ratio was not measured in this study due to its retrospective nature and the lack of consistent documentation of this parameter. Nonaka F et al. [18] have shown that patients with basic-type IXT generally have a normal AC/A ratio, but future studies could benefit from including this measurement to further explore its role in control.

In conclusion, control of IXT appeared to be influenced by fixation distance, and in our study, it was significantly worsened at 1 m in patients with basic IXT. Assessing control at various fixation distances may provide a more comprehensive understanding of patients’ actual control.
