Randomized controlled trial investigating the effect of a childbirth and parenting booklet intervention on paternal postpartum depression risk
Authors: Yuichi Ichinose, Kevin Y Urayama, Makoto Okamura, Naomi Sugiura, Maiko Suto, Hatoko Sasaki, Kenji Takehara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPaternal postpartum depression (PPPD) remains underrecognized globally, despite prevalence estimates comparable to maternal depression. This study investigated whether a childbirth and parenting booklet distributed to expectant fathers during the prenatal period could reduce the risk of PPPD.MethodsWe performed a parallel-group, randomized controlled trial conducted in an obstetrics clinic in Japan. We recruited expecting fathers and their pregnant partners at 28–32 weeks gestation. In total, the intervention and control groups comprised 236 and 234 couples, respectively. The intervention comprised of a childbirth and parenting booklet that was distributed to the fathers by postal mail at 32 to 34 weeks gestation. The primary outcome was the incidence of paternal depression at 1 or 3 months after delivery. The secondary outcomes measured in this study included various indicators related to participants and childcare.ResultsAt follow-up, signs of depression were observed in 7.2% of fathers in the intervention group and 9.8% in the control group (p = 0.40). Multivariable logistic regression analysis showed a reduced, but non-significant risk of PPPD (odds ratio = 0.70, 95% confidence interval = 0.34–1.42). There were no significant associations in secondary outcomes between the intervention and control group.ConclusionsDistributing a booklet alone may not be sufficient to reduce the risk of PPPD. More personalized, interactive, or accessible interventions may be necessary to support paternal mental health during the perinatal period.Trial registrationUMIN000021475 (15/03/2016).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08200-z.Keywords: Paternal depression, Postpartum depression in father, Interventional study, Randomized controlled trial

Background

Paternal postpartum depression (PPPD) is characterized by depressive symptoms among fathers within the first 12 months after childbirth. PPPD is not an official clinical diagnosis like maternal postpartum depression as recognized in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorder (Fifth Edition) [1]. PPPD has gained increasing attention in recent scientific literature due to its far-reaching implications for families [2–4]. Often overlooked in healthcare settings, PPPD is considered a hidden problem that can negatively impact paternal well-being and overall family dynamics.

Previous studies have reported incidence of PPPD that are as high as maternal postpartum depression [3, 5]. The overall prevalence of PPPD has been reported to be about 10% worldwide; studies from Japan have reported about 11.3–17.0% of fathers experiencing depressive symptoms after childbirth, with rates varying across different cultures and regions [5, 6]. Several risk factors have been identified, including a history of depression, unplanned pregnancy, financial stress, poor sleep quality in infants, lack of social support, and caesarean delivery [7].

The public health significance of PPPD extends beyond the individual father. Research has shown associations between paternal depression and increased risk of child maltreatment, depressive symptoms in children, and impaired parenting behaviours [4, 8, 9]. PPPD often co-occurs with maternal postpartum depression, further amplifying family stress [10]. Furthermore, PPPD has been linked to impaired co-parenting quality, contributing to long-term difficulties in family functioning [11]. Despite the challenges in engaging fathers—who often lack the continuity of care that mothers receive during the perinatal period—early prevention and identification efforts are essential to mitigate the wide-ranging negative consequences of PPPD.

Despite growing recognition, fathers are still under-screened and underserved in perinatal mental health systems. Unlike postpartum depression in mothers, routine screening for PPPD is rarely implemented in clinical practice [12]. This gap highlights the need for father-specific, accessible interventions that can be easily disseminated. Low-cost, low-intensity educational strategies have been proposed as one way to reach a broader population of new fathers who may not seek help through traditional mental health channels. There is growing recognition of the need for targeted, father-friendly interventions that are feasible, scalable, and low-cost.

Taken together, interventional studies for PPPD are needed to expand the body of scientific evidence on this topic. To address the significant gaps in knowledge regarding preventative approaches, we conducted a randomized controlled trial at a Japanese birth clinic to examine whether a childbirth and parenting booklet administered to fathers during pregnancy affects the mental health status and quality of life of the parents after childbirth.

Methods

Study oversight

This study was conducted according to the guidelines of the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments. This study was approved in February 2016 by the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee of the correspondent’s facility (approval number: 1071). This randomized controlled trial was registered on March 15, 2016, on the University hospital Medical Information Network (registry number: UMIN000021475; registration date: 15/03/2016). The protocol has been previously published [13]. This study was supported by Japan Society for the Promotion of Science (JSPS) KAKENHI grants (No. 25862216). This funding source had no role in the design, data collection, analysis, and reporting of this study.

Study design

This two-arm parallel-design, randomized controlled trial was conducted from March 2016 to September 2017. The study was carried out at a local obstetrics clinic in Hekinan City, an industrial area of the Aichi Prefecture of Japan. Many workers from the manufacturing industry live in this area, and the partners of the workers visit the clinic, which performs about 1,000 deliveries annually. No amendments were made to the study protocol, outcome measures, or statistical analysis plan during the study period.

Inclusion criteria

The targeted study participants were expecting fathers and their partners who were visiting the clinic for antenatal checkup starting prior to 28 weeks gestation and who planned to deliver at the clinic. Recruitment was carried out at around 28 to 32 weeks gestation during the clinic visit for their antenatal exam.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria for the study were the following: pregnant women who had a previous mental illness, couples who were not able to write or read Japanese, and couples whom clinic physicians deemed medically ineligible.

Recruitment

The recruitment was conducted by staff members of the clinic. First, the staff of the clinic assessed the eligibility and initiated recruitment of potential participants at the time of antenatal checkup visit. The clinic staff explained the study details and provided the informed consent forms and baseline questionnaire to the couples. Completed informed consent forms and baseline questionnaire were returned by postal mail.

Sample size calculations were performed to determine the target number for enrollment. Based on previous research, we considered the prevalence of fathers who experience PPPD to be about 16% during the first three months post-delivery in the study region [14]. We assumed that the rate of PPPD would reduce to about 8.0% after implementing the intervention. Given a false-positive rate of 0.05 and statistical power of 0.80, we estimated that a sample size of at least 241 fathers in each parallel arm would be required to detect an association. Considering a dropout rate of up to 15%, we targeted recruitment of at least 277 fathers allocated to each group.

Randomization

After receiving informed consent, each couple was randomly allocated to either the intervention or control group (two-arm, 1:1) using the permuted block method with the RAND function in Microsoft Excel. This randomization was performed at the facility in Japan, and all field staff at the study site was blinded from the process. Due to the nature of the intervention, blinding participants to assignment could not be maintained. The participants were requested not to reveal their assignment information, including to other potential participants and clinic staff.

Intervention and control group

Intervention and control group

The intervention comprised of a childbirth and parenting booklet that was distributed to the fathers by postal mail at 32 to 34 weeks gestation. This booklet was developed using various information sources, including insights gained through one-to-one interviews conducted with fathers who had prior experience in childcare. The booklet contained information for expecting fathers before and after childbirth, including the flow of labor and delivery; necessary preparation for fathers; helpful communication tips between parents and child; tips for attending childbirth; potential changes in physical and mental status experienced by parents after childbirth; tips for injury prevention; advantages of male engagement in childcare. Moreover, the booklet was reviewed by three specialists: an epidemiologist, a childcare specialist, and a midwife. Fathers allocated to the intervention group were sent this booklet and were provided with written instructions on how this booklet can be used in their preparation for childbirth and childcare. Those in the control group did not receive the booklet but continued to receive standard care with continued access to usual antenatal and parenting resources offered to all couples.

Data collection

Data collection

Data were collected at three time points. The baseline survey was conducted at recruitment. The second survey was distributed at the clinic one month after the delivery, and the last survey was sent to their homes three months after childbirth by mail. These questionnaires collected basic demographics, covariate data that may be considered potential confounders, and outcome-related measurements. To assess adherence to the intervention, fathers in the intervention group were asked at follow-up whether they had read the booklet and whether they found it helpful. However, no structured follow-up or usage tracking mechanism was implemented, which may have contributed to variability in the actual use and interpretation of the material.

Primary outcome

The main outcome in this study was the incidence of paternal depression at 1 or 3 months after delivery. Paternal depression was assessed by the Edinburgh Postnatal Stress Scale (EPDS), a commonly used measurement tool to detect maternal depression. The EPDS has been validated for use in assessing paternal depression in a Japanese population, in which a score of 8 or higher out of 30 was considered at risk for paternal depression [2]. Sensitivity and specificity for detecting paternal depressive states among Japanese fathers with this cut-off was 81.8% and 94.1%, respectively [2]. EPDS was measured at all the survey periods. EPDS in fathers was assessed separately at one and three months postpartum, as well as across both time points.

Secondary outcomes

Secondary outcomes of interest included participants’ quality of life (QOL) and various related indicators measured using the following scales: quality of life by the World Health Organization Quality of Life-26 (WHOOQL-26), quality of marriage by the Quality of Marriage Index (QMI), work home spillover by the Japanese version of the Survey Work-Home Interaction - NijmeGen (SWING-J), child rearing behavior by the Father’s Child-Rearing Behavior Scale, parent-to-infant bonding by the Mother-to-Infant Bonding Scale in Japanese (MIBS-J), parenting stress by the Parenting Stress Index Short Form (PSI-SF), and child maltreatment by the Child Maltreatment Scale (CMS). Previous studies have evaluated the internal reliability for our secondary measurements and reported Cronbach’s alpha; Japanese version of WHOQOL-26, 0.66–0.84 in Japanese fathers [15]; SWING-J, 0.75–0.86 in Japanese couples [16]; MIBS-J, 0.61 and 0.72 in Japanese fathers [17]; PSI-SF, 0.82 in Japanese mothers [18–20]; CMS, 0.63–0.77 in Japanese mothers [21].

Statistical analysis

The association between PPPD and intervention group assignment was evaluated. The main analysis was conducted based on the intention-to-treat population. Statistical tests were performed to assess potential differences in baseline characteristics between the groups. Chi-square tests were performed for categorical variables, and Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U-test for continuous variables, as appropriate. Additionally, odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) were calculated in multivariable analyses using logistic regression to adjust for baseline variables that showed differences between the intervention and control groups. Based on the level of significance associated with the covariates, three analysis models were used: Model 1, bivariate analysis; Model 2, covariates with p-values < 0.05; Model 3, covariates with p-values < 0.10.

Sensitivity analysis was performed to evaluate the influence of potential misclassification in EPDS on study results and compliance to the intervention. A complete-case analysis was conducted by excluding participants with missing values for EPDS at any of the follow-up periods. Additional sensitivity analysis was performed by classifying non-respondents on EPDS as positive EPDS status assuming that the non-response may be related to mental health issues. A two-sided p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All analyses were performed using R Studio 3.6.1. statistical software.

Results

Participants

Of the 1,376 couples assessed for eligibility by the staff, 1,096 met the criteria and were invited, and 577 agreed to participant in the study (52.6%). The randomization resulted in the assignment of 288 couples to the intervention group and 289 to the control group. A total of 107 couples were considered lost to follow-up either due to delivering at a different clinic or non-response to the follow-up EPDS survey resulting in 236 fathers in the intervention group and 234 in the control group (Fig. 1). No harm or injuries to the participants were observed during the study period.

Flow diagram of the study participants

The baseline characteristics of the study participants are shown in Table 1. The overall mean age of the participants was 32.9 (SD = 5.03) years old and that of partners was 30.7 (SD = 4.27). Most fathers were working full-time at baseline (89.8% in intervention and 85.9% in controls); however, only about half of the partners had been working. The mean working hours were about 9 h in both the intervention and control groups. Both groups had similar baseline characteristics except for partners currently working (p = 0.01), living with extended family (p = 0.03), and history of mental illness in the partners (p = 0.01). Among the intervention group participants, 69.3% reported that they had read the booklet, and 89.4% of them stated that the booklet was helpful.

Association analysis of PPPD

Overall, 8.5% of the fathers were defined as having PPPD (EPDS > = 8) at either one month or three months after delivery. By 3 months, 7.2% of fathers in the intervention group and 9.8% in the control group experienced PPPD; however, this difference was not statistically significant (Table 2). The proportions of PPPD specifically at 1 month and 3 months follow-up were not significantly different between the groups. Multivariable analysis adjusting for the variables that were different between the intervention and control groups showed a reduced risk of PPPD by 3 months after delivery associated with the intervention (OR = 0.70, 95% CI = 0.34–1.42). Although the odds ratio favored the intervention, the result was not statistically significant (Table 3). A sensitivity analysis comprising only participants with EPDS measures at both follow-up periods (complete case analysis) showed that 8.6% and 10.2% of the fathers in the intervention and control groups, respectively, had PPPD (p= 0.40) (Table S1). An analysis where we considered missing EPDS at one month or three months as having PPPD showed that 31.8% of fathers in the intervention and 35.5% in controls may have been affected, a difference that was not statistically significant (Table S2).

Discussion

In this randomized controlled trial utilizing a parallel design to examine the effect of administering an educational booklet to fathers during pregnancy on PPPD risk, we observed no statistically significant difference in PPPD between the intervention and control groups.

Although the results were not statistically significant, the trend suggested that the intervention might hold potential—particularly if further optimized in terms of delivery and engagement. As paternal mental health programs are still emerging, identifying effective low-burden approaches remains a critical goal. This study provides foundational data for future trials testing more interactive or personalized versions of educational support.

We also assessed broader psychosocial outcomes that the booklet might have influenced broader psychosocial outcomes, we examined a range of secondary endpoints, including EPDS in the partners, marital satisfaction, work-life balance, bonding with the baby, father’s involvement in childcare, risk of child maltreatment, and parenting stress. As with PPPD, we did not detect substantial differences between the two groups in these outcomes. These findings suggest that while this low-intensity intervention may provide general psychosocial support to couples. This suggests that a simple educational approach alone is likely insufficient to reduce the risk of PPPD—particularly in the context of working fathers in suburban Japan. The findings of our study align with existing literature that emphasizes the impact of paternal depression on child development and the importance of early identification and intervention. Implementing routine screening for PPPD in primary care settings could facilitate timely support for affected fathers [12].

One possible explanation for the minimal observed effect is that many fathers did not fully engage with the intervention. We acknowledge that approximately 30% of fathers did not read the booklet, and the lack of structured implementation may have affected its impact. Since the intervention consisted solely of booklet distribution, participation in reading the material was entirely voluntary. There was no structured encouragement or follow-up to ensure engagement. Our rationale for choosing this simple and scalable method was to offer educational support in a way that could be easily integrated into standard prenatal care. Future interventions could consider including interactive components or brief engagement confirmation tasks to improve uniformity in exposure.

Our study suggests that PPPD preventive strategies may require more intensive or personalized approaches. PPPD is considered a hidden problem, and health care providers may not notice depressive symptoms in fathers. A meaningful approach to preventing PPPD may be to encourage health care professionals to more actively acknowledge fathers’ involvement and recognize fathers as care receivers similar to the way in which the partner and unborn child receive care. De Montigny et al. [1] and Yogman & Garfield [22] emphasized that health care providers play a key role in promoting childcare involvement of fathers. In addition, fathers’ involvement in childrearing is crucial and beneficial to stabilize family relationships and to enhance mental and social development in their children [23–25]. Previous research suggested that individually modified antenatal education was effective in reducing the risk of maternal depression [26, 27]. Moreover, another study showed that short period of antenatal education improved father’s involvement in childcare [28]. Although the intervention did not show significant effects on PPPD, most participants reported that the childbirth and parenting booklet was a useful tool. Given the observed tendency for an association in our study, it is possible that administering the educational booklet with expanded support in an individualized manner, such as instructing where fathers can contact to, may be helpful.

Within the Japanese context, one factor contributing to the PPPD problem may be related to policies and culture surrounding the working environment. About 75% of fathers in our study reported working overtime beyond Japan’s national average of 40 hours per week [29]. The participants may have had only limited time to spend with their family members as well as to read the booklet. According to the Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare, while 84.1% of Japanese mothers took childcare leave in 2022, only 30.1% of fathers did so [30]. Although the proportion of fathers taking leave has gradually increased, it still remains low. The working conditions may have prevented the fathers from being involved in childcare even though some of them understood the needs or wished to do so. This conflicting reality may contribute to psychological distress among fathers. However, without first nurturing an accommodating work environment, and simply telling fathers with long working hour to spend more time with their family, may lead to additional stress. In this context, workplace reform and broader societal shifts in recognizing paternal roles may be crucial for enabling fatherhood engagement.

Our study had several limitations. First, the prevalence of fathers categorized as PPPD based on the EPDS in the current study was lower than expected; thus, it may have influenced statistical power. Previous studies reported that the proportion of fathers suffering from postpartum depression in Japan ranged from 13.6 to 16.7% [2, 14]. The smaller proportions observed in our study may have been influenced by our exclusion of participants with previous history or current treatment of mental illness at recruitment. While the direction of association was consistent with our hypothesis, there did not appear to be marked differences in proportion of PPPD between the groups. Furthermore, we observed a 18.5% loss to follow-up, which was higher than our expectations of 15%. One possible reason is that the survey was conducted at three different time points and included more than fifty questions per round. Moreover, no follow-up was conducted with non-respondents, but future studies may benefit from reducing the large burden of follow-up questions to maintain participation [31]. Moreover, the study was conducted at only one private clinic in suburban area of Japan. The generalizability of our findings may be limited, as the participants were primarily blue-collar workers employed at nearby factories, possibly leading to an overrepresentation of fathers with irregular work schedules that can influence mental distress in parents and infants[32]. Our study used a score of EPDS greater than or equal to 8 to define participants who had signs of depression. Although previous studies have validated the usage of EPDS in fathers[33], we were not able to confirm the status with a formal clinical diagnosis. Finally, as the booklet was distributed in paper format, its accessibility may have been limited for fathers with demanding work schedules. Future interventions could enhance accessibility and engagement by offering materials in digital formats, such as mobile applications or email-based content Digital delivery may also allow for usage tracking and customized reminders, contributing to improved compliance and effectiveness[34].

Conclusions

The antenatal distribution of a parenting booklet to fathers did not significantly reduce the risk of PPPD in this suburban Japanese population. While feasible, the intervention’s limited impact underscores the need for more tailored and accessible strategies—particularly digital tools. Despite null results, this study provides valuable groundwork for developing scalable approaches to support paternal mental health in the perinatal period.
