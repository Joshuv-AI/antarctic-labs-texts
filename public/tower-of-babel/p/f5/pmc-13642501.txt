Cross-sectional and longitudinal associations of activity diversity with physical function in community-dwelling older adults
Authors: Daijo Shiratsuchi, Shoma Akaida, Yuto Miyake, Kento Tabira, Ryota Kuratsu, Hiroki Nishi, Shoko Atae, Manami Fukumori, Takanori Iko, Hyuma Makizako
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe role of activity diversity across physical, cognitive, and social domains in preserving physical function remains unexplored.AimsTo investigate the associations between multidomain activity diversity and physical function in community-dwelling older adults.MethodsWe analyzed data from 763 participants (mean age: 74.8 ± 6.2 years; 63.8% female) in the 2018 Tarumizu Study. Activity diversity was assessed using 12 items (physical, cognitive, and social activities; four per domain). High engagement was defined as participation in ≥ 3 within a domain. Participants were categorized by the number of highly engaged domains (0–1, 2, and 3). Low physical function was defined as low grip strength (< 28 kg for males; <18 kg for females) and/or slow gait speed (< 1.0 m/s). Logistic regression analyses examined cross-sectional (n = 763) and 5-year longitudinal (n = 175 among those without low physical function at baseline) associations.ResultsCross-sectionally, low physical function occurred in 45.3%, 29.1%, and 21.4% in the 0–1, 2, and 3 domain groups, respectively. Participants engaged in two or three domains had significantly lower odds of low physical function than those engaged in 0–1 domains. Over 5 years, incident low physical function occurred in 35.1%, 16.2%, and 11.4% of the 0–1, 2, and 3 domain groups, respectively, with significantly lower risks in the 2 and 3 domain groups, and a linear trend (p for trend = 0.023).DiscussionEngagement in ≥ 2 activity domains was associated with reduced risk of low physical function in older adults.ConclusionsMaintaining diverse lifestyle activities supports physical function in later life.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1007/s40520-026-03446-9.Keywords: Multidomain engagement, Grip strength, Gait speed, Physical performance

Introduction

Preserving physical function is one of the central goals of healthy aging, as its decline precedes disability, institutionalization, and premature mortality [1]. Physical function is commonly assessed using objective indicators such as gait speed and handgrip strength [2, 3]. Accordingly, physical function is widely used as a clinically relevant and actionable endpoint in epidemiological research on modifiable determinants of aging trajectories.

Accumulating evidence suggests that lifestyle activities across multiple domains may contribute to the maintenance of physical function in later life. Exercise and other forms of structured physical activity are associated with better physical function in older adults, as supported by evidence from intervention studies [4]. Furthermore, social participation and cognitively-stimulating activities have been linked to a lower risk of functional decline or disability, indicating that non-physical activities may also be relevant for maintaining functional capacity in later life [5–7]. Emerging evidence from cross-sectional studies suggests that combined engagement across multiple domains may be associated with better physical function than single-domain engagement alone [8, 9]. However, most studies have examined individual activity domains in isolation or focused primarily on disability-based outcomes rather than explicitly defined endpoints of physical function.

Previous studies focused on the presence or frequency of specific lifestyle activities when examining their associations with health outcomes [10]. However, cognitive aging literature suggests that the breadth or variety of activity engagement may represent a distinct dimension of lifestyle behavior [11]. Engagement across a wider range of activities may reflect greater environmental complexity and a more enriched behavioral repertoire [11]. Related research has suggested that multidomain or combined lifestyle behaviors may be associated with disability and frailty-related outcomes in older adults [12, 13]. These findings support the possibility that broader engagement across lifestyle domains may have relevance beyond any single activity type.

Despite these insights, several important gaps remain. Activity diversity has rarely been operationalized as the breadth of engagement across distinct lifestyle domains using a reproducible and domain-based definition. Moreover, existing observational evidence on multidomain engagement has often focused on disability-based outcomes [14, 15], which may obscure early-stage functional changes identified using objective indicators, such as gait speed and handgrip strength. Consequently, it remains unclear whether broader lifestyle activity engagement across physical, cognitive, and social domains is associated with both the prevalence of low physical function and the risk of its subsequent development. Therefore, this study aimed to examine the cross-sectional and longitudinal associations between activity diversity and low physical function among community-dwelling older adults, allowing us to assess both current functional status and the risk of future functional decline. Our findings will aid the development of strategies for preserving physical function in later life.

Methods

Study design and participants

This study used data from the Tarumizu Study, an ongoing community-based cohort study in Tarumizu City, Kagoshima Prefecture, Japan. This population-based research program investigates factors related to aging, physical function, and health outcomes among community-dwelling older adults and is conducted in collaboration with Kagoshima University, Tarumizu City Hall, and Tarumizu Chuo Hospital.

This analysis included 859 participants aged ≥ 65 years who participated in the baseline assessment of the Tarumizu Study in 2018. Participants were excluded if they had a history of stroke (n = 36), Parkinson’s disease (n = 1), or dementia (n = 8), were certified as requiring long-term care under the Japanese long-term care insurance system (n = 16), or had missing data on key variables (n = 29). After these exclusions, 769 participants were included in the cross-sectional analysis (Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of participant selection for the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses

For the longitudinal analysis, participants with low physical function at baseline were excluded (n = 245). Of the remaining eligible participants, those who did not attend the follow-up assessment conducted in 2023 (n = 345) or had missing follow-up data (n = 3) were excluded. Consequently, 176 participants were included in the longitudinal analysis (mean follow-up period of approximately 5 years; Fig. 1).

All participants provided written informed consent before participation. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee on Epidemiological and Related Studies of Kagoshima University (approval number: 170351) and conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Assessment of activity diversity

Activity diversity was assessed at baseline based on participants’ engagement in multiple lifestyle activities across physical, cognitive, and social domains using items derived from established assessment frameworks applied in the Tarumizu Study [9], including the Japan Science and Technology Agency Index of Competence and the Kihon Checklist [16, 17]. These instruments have been widely used to capture the multidimensional aspects of daily activities among community-dwelling older adults.

A total of 12 lifestyle activities [9], comprising four physical, four cognitive, and four social activities, were evaluated. Physical activities included light exercise or sports, participation in regular exercise or sports, independent use of public transportation (bus or train), and activities involving contact with soil, such as gardening or horticultural work. Cognitive activities included reading newspapers, reading books, enjoying art, films, or music, and watching educational or cultural programs. Social activities included visiting friends, participating in regional festivals or events, involvement in neighborhood or residents’ associations, and engagement in charitable or volunteer activities.

Among these items, light exercise or sports, regular exercise or sports, activities involving contact with soil (gardening or horticultural work), newspaper reading, and book reading were assessed using frequency-based response options. Participants who reported engaging in these activities at least once per week were classified as active. In contrast, using public transportation independently, enjoying art, films, or music, watching educational or cultural programs, visiting friends, participating in regional festivals or events, being a member of neighborhood or residents’ associations, and engaging in charitable or volunteer activities were assessed as dichotomous items, with participants responding “yes” or “no” to engagement. Participants were considered to have sufficient engagement within each domain if they reported participation in at least three of the four activities [18]. This threshold was selected to identify participants with consistent engagement within a domain while minimizing misclassification due to sporadic activity participation.

Participants were categorized into three groups based on the number of domains in which they were rated as active (those active in 0–1 domain, 2 domains, or all 3 domains). This categorization reflects the breadth of lifestyle activity engagement across multiple domains rather than the intensity of individual activities. This operationalization was designed to capture the breadth of engagement across domains rather than the relative contribution of individual activities. For supplementary analyses, we also calculated the total number of activities (range, 0–12).

Assessment of physical function

Physical function was assessed using handgrip strength and usual gait speed, which are indicators of muscle strength and physical performance and are strongly associated with clinically meaningful outcomes [19–21]. Handgrip strength was measured in the standing position using a handheld dynamometer (T.K.K. 5401 Grip-D; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Niigata, Japan), and the maximum grip strength of the dominant hand was used for analyses. Usual gait speed was assessed over a 10-m measurement zone, with 2-m acceleration and deceleration phases provided at the beginning and end of the walkway, respectively. Participants were instructed to walk at their usual pace, and gait speed was measured using an infrared sensor-based measurement system (YW; Yagami Inc., Aichi, Japan). All assessments were performed by trained physiotherapists according to standardized protocols [22].

Low physical function was defined based on established cutoff values for handgrip strength and usual gait speed, according to the criteria proposed by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia and the Japanese version of the Cardiovascular Health Study [23, 24]. Low handgrip strength was defined as < 28 kg for males and < 18 kg for females, and low usual gait speed was defined as < 1.0 m/s. Participants were classified as having low physical function if they met either criterion (handgrip strength and/or gait speed), reflecting impairment in at least one key component of physical function. Physical function (handgrip strength and usual gait speed) was assessed at baseline and reassessed at the 5-year follow-up.

Other variables

All variables described in this section were assessed at baseline. Age (years) and sex (male or female) were recorded. The number of regularly-used medications was assessed as a continuous variable and used as an indicator of overall health status. Information on chronic diseases was collected through structured interviews conducted by trained public health nurses or research staff. Cognitive function was assessed using the Mini-Cog, and cognitive impairment was defined as a score of ≤ 2 [25, 26]. Depressive symptoms were evaluated using the 15-item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS-15), with a score of ≥ 5 indicating the presence of depressive symptoms [27, 28].

Statistical analyses

Participant characteristics are summarized using means and standard deviations for continuous variables and numbers with percentages for categorical variables. Changes in physical function were calculated as follow-up minus baseline values. Differences across groups defined by the number of active lifestyle domains were assessed using one-way analysis of variance for continuous variables and the chi-square test for categorical variables.

Cross-sectional associations between activity diversity and low physical function were examined using logistic regression analysis. Odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p values for trend were calculated, with the 0–1 domain group used as the reference category. The adjusted model included age, sex, number of regularly used medications, and presence of chronic diseases (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus), cognitive impairment, and depressive symptoms, which were selected a priori based on previous studies examining determinants of physical function in older adults [29–31], as well as their potential relevance to both activity engagement and physical function. For the longitudinal analyses, incident low physical function was defined as the new onset of low physical function at the 5-year follow-up among participants without low physical function at baseline. Associations between baseline activity diversity and incident low physical function were examined using logistic regression analysis. The adjusted model additionally included baseline handgrip strength and usual gait speed. As a supplementary analysis, we additionally examined whether the total number of activities was associated with low physical function in both the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses using logistic regression models. To assess potential selection bias due to attrition, we also compared baseline characteristics among participants included in the longitudinal analysis, those lost to follow-up, and those excluded at baseline.

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS software (version 30.0; IBM Japan, Tokyo, Japan). A two-sided p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Participant characteristics

Baseline characteristics of the cross-sectional sample according to the number of active lifestyle domains are shown in Table 1. Participants in the 3-domain group were younger than those in the 0–1 domain and 2-domain groups (73.7 ± 5.9 vs. 75.4 ± 6.7 and 75.1 ± 6.0 years, respectively; p = 0.007), and the 0–1 domain group used more medications than the 3-domain group (3.4 ± 3.0 vs. 2.7 ± 2.7; p = 0.043). Physical function indicators differed across groups, particularly for usual gait speed and low physical function prevalence. Usual gait speed increased in a stepwise manner across the groups (1.18 ± 0.25 m/s in the 0–1 domain, 1.28 ± 0.22 m/s in the 2-domain, and 1.35 ± 0.23 m/s in the 3-domain groups; p < 0.001). As expected, the prevalence of low physical function was highest in the 0–1 domain group (45.3%) and progressively lower in the 2-domain (29.1%) and 3-domain (21.4%) groups (p < 0.001).

Cross-sectional associations between activity diversity and low physical function

In the cross-sectional logistic regression analysis (Table 2), compared with participants in the 0–1 domain group, those in the 2 (adjusted OR = 0.52, 95% CI: 0.34–0.78) and 3 (adjusted OR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.26–0.64) domain groups had significantly lower odds of low physical function after adjusting for age, sex, number of medications, presence of chronic diseases (hypertension, hyperlipidemia, and diabetes mellitus), cognitive impairment, and depressive symptoms. A significant linear trend was observed across increasing number of active domains (p for trend < 0.001).

In supplementary analyses, a higher total number of activities was also associated with lower odds/risk of low physical function in the cross-sectional analysis (adjusted OR = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.78–0.91), whereas the association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant in the longitudinal analysis (adjusted OR = 0.78, 95% CI: 0.60–1.01, p = 0.060) (Online Resource 1, Tables S1 and S2). Baseline and 5-year follow-up values of gait speed and handgrip strength according to activity diversity groups are presented in Online Resource 1, Table S3. Over 5 years, handgrip strength declined by − 3.1 kg in the 0–1, − 2.2 kg in the 2, and − 1.0 kg in the 3 domain groups (p = 0.007). In contrast, the change in gait speed did not differ significantly across groups (− 0.03 ± 0.25, − 0.08 ± 0.18, and − 0.05 ± 0.17 m/s in the 0–1, 2-, and 3-domain groups, respectively; p = 0.349). Baseline characteristics according to inclusion status are presented in Online Resource 1, Table S4. Participants lost to follow-up were older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension, whereas most other characteristics did not differ from those included in the longitudinal analysis.

Discussion

In this community-based study of older adults, engagement across multiple activity domains was consistently associated with a lower likelihood of low physical function. In the cross-sectional analyses, participants who were in a higher number of activity domains exhibited a lower prevalence of low physical function, with evidence of a graded association across increasing domain counts. This cross-sectional association suggests that diverse lifestyle activities may help characterize individuals in community settings who are more likely to have low physical function. The longitudinal analyses further demonstrated that broader engagement in lifestyle activities at baseline was associated with a reduced risk of incident low physical function over a 5-year follow-up period. These associations were observed using objective indicators of physical function, including handgrip strength and usual gait speed, and persisted after adjusting for relevant confounders. In addition, the decline in handgrip strength over 5 years was greater in participants with lower activity diversity (− 3.1 kg in the 0–1 domain group vs. −1.0 kg in the 3 domain group), and the magnitude of decline in the 0–1 domain group exceeded previously reported estimates of the minimal clinically important difference (approximately 2.4–2.7 kg) [32], suggesting potential clinical relevance. In contrast, changes in gait speed did not differ significantly across activity diversity groups, and the slightly greater decline observed in the higher activity diversity groups may partly reflect their higher baseline gait speed values and regression to the mean. These findings suggest that activity diversity is associated with physical function; however, it may partly reflect underlying functional capacity, and the consistency across analyses should be interpreted with caution.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous observational evidence indicating that engagement in lifestyle activities is related to functional health in later life. Previous studies have suggested that participation in multiple activity domains may be particularly relevant. For example, older adults who concurrently engage in physical and mental activities show better physical function, with their gait speed approximately 0.09 m/s faster than that of those with lower or single-domain engagement, a difference that exceed the threshold for clinically meaningful change [8]. Social participation is associated with subsequent functional outcomes, and cohort evidence has suggested that participation across different types of organizations is related to a lower risk of incident functional disability [33]. Similarly, studies examining combined physical and cognitive activity profiles have reported associations with a reduced risk of later disability [6], supporting the premise that non-physical activities may contribute to healthier aging trajectories. However, most previous studies have focused on combinations of two activity domains or single domains and have not explicitly examined the breadth of engagement across physical, cognitive, and social domains. In contrast, the present study operationalized activity diversity across these three domains and demonstrated a graded association with objectively defined low physical function in cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses. The observed trend suggests that broader engagement across multiple domains may be associated with functional health, although it overlaps substantially with overall activity participation. In supplementary analyses, a higher total number of activities was associated with a lower likelihood of low physical function in the cross-sectional analysis, whereas the association was attenuated and no longer statistically significant in the longitudinal analysis. This pattern may suggest that breadth of engagement across different domains and overall activity participation represent closely related but not entirely interchangeable dimensions of lifestyle engagement.

One plausible explanation for these findings is that engagement across multiple lifestyle domains may support physical function through complementary and interrelated pathways. Physical activities contribute directly to the maintenance of muscle strength and mobility, whereas cognitive and social activities may help preserve executive function, motivation, and behavioral regulation, which support sustained physical engagement in daily life. Poorer cognitive performance, particularly in executive processes, is associated with subsequent decline in gait speed, which supports the plausibility of cognitive pathways contributing to mobility trajectories in aging [34]. Executive function plays an important role in the planning, initiation, and adaptation of motor behavior in complex daily environments, and impairments in these processes may reduce the capacity to maintain regular physical activity and mobility. Social participation has been associated with lower extremity strength, balance, and gait speed, suggesting that social engagement may be related to physical function through both behavioral and physiological mechanisms [35–37]. Therefore, broader activity engagement may reflect greater behavioral flexibility and functional reserve, which are consistent with reserve-based frameworks emphasizing the protective role of enriched lifestyle experiences [38, 39]. The observation that these associations were evident cross-sectionally and longitudinally (for incident low physical function over 5 years), even after accounting for baseline handgrip strength and gait speed, suggests that diverse lifestyle activities may capture aspects of physical function beyond contemporaneous performance alone. These findings have potential implications for strategies aimed at preserving physical function in later life. Rather than focusing solely on structured exercise, promoting broader engagement across physical, cognitive, and social domains in daily life may be feasible and scalable approaches that can support functional health among community-dwelling older adults, including those who have difficulty engaging constantly in high-intensity physical activity.

Some study limitations should be acknowledged. The present study focused on the breadth of activity engagement rather than specific domains. Some activities may reflect underlying functional capacity, and domains may overlap in their components. Future studies should examine the contribution and weighting of individual activities. Furthermore, activity breadth and total activity volume were strongly correlated, and collinearity between these variables complicated formal assessment of their independent contributions within a single model. Future studies employing measures of breadth that are mathematically independent from total activity count by design may be better positioned to address this question. Given the observational design, causal relationships cannot be established, and lifestyle activities were assessed using a self-reported measure, which may be subject to recall or reporting bias. Furthermore, the longitudinal analysis was based on a relatively small sample from a single community, which may limit its generalizability. Furthermore, a substantial proportion of participants were not followed up beyond 5 years, and those who remained in the cohort may have been healthier and more functionally independent than those who were lost to follow-up. Comparison of baseline characteristics showed that participants lost to follow-up were older and had a higher prevalence of hypertension, whereas most other characteristics did not differ from those included in the longitudinal analysis. This differential follow-up could have introduced attrition-related selection bias and may have led to underestimation of the true associations between activity diversity and physical function. Although we adjusted for baseline physical function in the longitudinal analysis, reverse causation cannot be fully excluded. The use of dichotomized outcomes based on established cut-offs may have reduced sensitivity to detect subtle changes in physical function. We adjusted for several potential confounders; however, the limited number of events constrained the number of variables that could be included in the models. Important factors such as musculoskeletal conditions (e.g., arthritis), body mass index, and socioeconomic status were not included and may have influenced the observed associations. Therefore, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Nevertheless, the consistency of associations observed across the cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses, using objective indicators of physical function, supports the relevance of activity diversity as a meaningful behavioral correlate of physical function among community-dwelling older adults.

In conclusion, this community-based study demonstrated that engagement in multiple activity domains, including physical, cognitive, and social domains, was associated with a lower likelihood of low physical function among older adults. These associations were observed for prevalent and incident low physical function based on objective measures of handgrip strength and usual gait speed over a 5-year period. The findings suggest that maintaining broader engagement in multiple lifestyle domains may support physical function in later life.
