Phage therapy targeting DNA-encapsulated membrane vesicle-producing intestinal symbiotic Klebsiella pneumoniae ameliorates autoimmune disease
Authors: Bingchun Pu, Rui Wang, Jiang Wang, Linlin Li, Jianhui Li, Mufeng Cai, Yi Zhong, Bo Yan, Lin Lv, Yan Zhang, Ke Dong, Jing Luo, Hanbo Li, Xingyu Chen, Quan Zhou, Chang Liu, Ruqi Tang, Xiong Ma, Xiaokui Guo, Wei Zhao, Jinhong Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAutoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a progressive and currently incurable inflammatory liver disorder. Accumulating evidence suggests that intestinal Klebsiella pneumoniae may contribute to AIH. However, the specific bacterial features underlying its role remain unclear. We isolated 32 strains of K. pneumoniae from both healthy and AIH patients. Genomic and phenotypic analyses identified a unique strain, KpA4-1 from an AIH patient, distinguished by its production of membrane vesicles (MVs) encapsulating plasmid DNA (p2). Administration of p2-containing MVs in mice elevated serum liver enzymes and antinuclear antibodies (ANA), indicating liver injury and autoimmune activation. Oral administration of KpA4-1 in AIH mice exacerbated AIH pathology, evidenced by elevated liver enzymes, increased ANA titers, and enhanced hepatic CD8 + T cell infiltration. Mechanistically, KpA4-1 activated the cGAS–STING signaling pathway that potentiated hepatic inflammation in AIH mice. The pivotal role of the p2 was further validated by attenuated AIH pathology in mice treated with KpA4-1Δp2 or bacteriophage therapy targeting KpA4-1. Collectively, these findings uncover a novel mechanism by which the intestinal K. pneumoniae exacerbates AIH via DNA-encapsulated MVs, highlighting their potential as diagnostic or therapeutic targets.Subject terms: Diseases, Immunology, Microbiology

Introduction

Autoimmune hepatitis (AIH) is a progressive inflammatory liver condition characterized by an autoimmune response targeting hepatocytes, resulting in destruction of the liver parenchyma, with lymphocyte infiltration involving both B and T lymphocytes. The process results in hepatocellular necrosis and apoptosis, and an elevation in serum transaminase levels1. The etiology of AIH is multifactorial and not fully understood, but it is believed to involve a genetic predisposition to autoimmunity with exposure to drug pressures or environmental stimulants that trigger the presentation of autoantigens to the immune system, leading to an aberrant immune response and hepatic injury2. The gut microbiota is increasingly recognized as a significant contributor to autoimmune hepatitis diseases3,4. A proposed mechanism involves microbiota dysbiosis-induced impairment of intestinal barrier permeability, facilitating translocation intestinal microorganisms and their byproducts, such as bacterial DNA and lipopolysaccharide (LPS), into the portal vein5. This translocation can activate the liver’s immune response, potentially inducing inflammation or exacerbating liver injury through the interaction with the liver’s immune cells6.

Microbiome analysis indicates that Klebsiella pneumoniae (K. pneumoniae), a common gut colonizer, has been implicated in the etiology of several chronic liver diseases7, including nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)8, alcoholic liver disease(ALD)9 and primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC)10. It has been reported that patients with AIH exhibit distinct gut microbiota profiles compared to healthy controls, with evidence that the relative abundance of Klebsiella was significantly increased in AIH patients11,12. However, it is still not clear what mechanism, if any, is involved in the contribution of K. pneumoniae to the development of AIH.

Membrane vesicles (MVs) are always secreted from Gram-negative bacteria and implicated in processes such as pathogenesis, immune evasion, inflammation, and immunity. These spherical structures, ranging from 20 to 400 nm in diameter, originate from the lipid membranes of the bacterial cell surface13. MVs are composed of lipids, lipopolysaccharides (LPS), outer membrane proteins, and encapsulated periplasmic content14,15. Theses MV components can act as immunomodulators, eliciting host immune responses. Given their complex nature as versatile carriers of bacterial metabolites, MVs are hypothesized to contribute to both the initiation and progression of AIH pathogenesis.

In this study, we isolated K. pneumoniae strains from fecal samples of both healthy individuals and AIH patients. Among these, we identified a specific strain KpA4-1 that produces DNA-containing MVs. While whether specific DNA/RNA regions influence the immunogenic properties of MVs remains an open question warranting further investigation, our research is aimed at delineating the role of these DNA-encapsulating MVs in AIH pathogenesis, unraveling the underlying mechanisms, and exploring their potential as therapeutic targets for AIH prevention and treatment, with particular emphasis on gut microbiota modulation.

Results

Elevated Klebsiella abundance in the gut microbiota of AIH patients

Fecal samples were collected from 23 AIH patients and 21 healthy controls after obtaining informed consent. 16S rRNA sequencing was performed to determine the composition of the gut microbiota in the two groups. The gut microbiota composition of AIH patients and healthy individuals was different according to the Principal Coordinate Analysis (PCoA) (Fig. 1A). Alpha diversity analysis (Chao1 index) showed significantly reduced microbial diversity in AIH patients compared to healthy individuals (Fig. 1B). Several differentially abundant genera were identified between AIH patients and healthy controls, including Klebsiella, which was significantly enriched in the gut microbiota of AIH patients (Fig. 1C, D). This observation suggests a potential association between Klebsiella enrichment and AIH pathogenesis. Given K. pneumoniae prominence in liver disease, we selected it as a candidate pathobiont for further investigation of its role in AIH development.

Strain-level diversity of K. pneumoniae in the gut microbiota of both healthy and AIH patients

To elucidate the potential involvement of specific K. pneumoniae lineages in AIH pathogenesis, we performed culture-based isolation of K. pneumoniae from fecal samples, successfully obtaining 13 strains from 23 AIH patients and 19 strains from 21 healthy controls (Supplementary Table 1). Whole-genome sequencing was performed on all 32 isolates, followed by pan-genome phylogenetic analysis. However, the phylogenetic tree failed to demonstrate significant clustering segregation between AIH-originated and healthy-originated strains (Supplementary Fig. 1A). Subsequent analyses, including capsular polysaccharide (CPS) types, lipopolysaccharide(LPS), and multilocus sequence typing (MLST) profiles, similarly revealed no significant differences between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 1A). These findings suggest that K. pneumoniae strain variation at the genomic level may not be a primary determinant in AIH development. Notably, parallel isolation from individual specimens yielded multiple phenotypically distinct K. pneumoniae strains in both cohorts (AIH patients: n = 4/23 showing polyclonal colonization; healthy controls: n = 3/21; Supplementary Table 1), underscoring the remarkable strain-level heterogeneity within gut microbiota. Virulence determinants analysis demonstrated that 29 intestinal K. pneumoniae isolates lacked characterized virulence factors (including yersiniabactin, colibactin, aerobactin, salmochelin, hypermucoidy rmpADC, hypermucoidy rmpA2 gene clusters), with only three exceptional strains (KpH9-1, KpA19-1, and KpA20-2) possessing partial virulence-related genes (Supplementary Fig. 1B). These results imply that gut-colonizing K. pneumoniae may mediate pathological effects through virulence-independent mechanisms, potentially involving immunomodulation or metabolites.

Selective encapsulation of plasmid DNA in KpA4-1-derived MVs

Gram-negative bacterial MVs are known to contribute to pathogenesis, immune evasion, inflammation, and immune modulation16. Therefore, we investigated the productions of MVs by these isolated K. pneumoniae strains. It is well documented that the release of MVs is often triggered by environmental stresses, particularly those induced by agent MMC. To identify strains capable of producing MVs, we supplemented cultures of isolates with MMC. Among the 32 isolates, seven exhibited MV production under stress conditions. Four strains (KpH1-5, KpH2-1, KpH12-1, and KpH20-2) of them originated from healthy controls, while three (KpA2-2, KpA4-1, and KpA6-1) from AIH patients (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Comparative genomic analysis of MV-producing strains revealed that KpA4-1, a strain isolated from AIH patients, harbored a plasmid homologous to the previously characterized p2-like plasmid (Fig. 2A)17. Notably, this plasmid has been reported to be encapsulable within MVs. p2-like plasmids demonstrate broad distribution across Enterobacteriaceae, with particularly high prevalence among diverse K. pneumoniae strains (Supplementary Fig. 3A)18,19. Intriguingly, all p2-like plasmid-containing strains in our analysis were exclusively derived from human samples (Supplementary Fig. 3B and Supplementary Table 2).

Phylogenetic tree analyses revealed a close genetic relationship between KpH20-2 (a strain isolated from healthy individuals) and KpA4-1(Supplementary Fig. 1). Therefore, we selected KpA4-1 and KpH20-2 for further functional characterization. Both strains exhibited atypical mucoid characteristics, lacked distinct capsules or biofilms (Supplementary Fig. 4A), and were of low virulence according to virulence gene analysis (Supplementary Fig. 1B). Whole-genome sequencing revealed the presence of five plasmids in the genome of KpA4-1 and six in KpH20-2(Supplementary Fig. 4B).

The morphologies of MVs secreted by both KpH20-2 and KpA4-1, whether under MMC induction or natural culture conditions, showed spherical buds with sizes ranging from 400∼600 nm (Fig. 2B), consistent with typical MV characteristics. The protein components within the MVs of both strains exhibited a similar protein profile that differed from that of the whole cell lysates, with four predominant protein bands in their MVs (Fig. 2C). Proteomic analysis revealed 20 proteins with abundance >0.5% in KpA4-1 MVs and 21 in KpH20-2 MVs, with 16 proteins shared between them (Fig. 2C and Supplementary Table 3). This indicates that the protein composition of KpA4-1 MVs and KpH20-2 MVs is highly similar. Sequencing of their MVs revealed DNA content in KpA4-1 MVs (GenBank No. PP372685), whereas no DNA sequence was detected in KpH20-2 MVs. Subsequent PCR analysis confirmed encapsulation of a specific plasmid, designated p2, within KpA4-1 MVs (Fig. 2D).

p2-containing MVs derived from KpA4-1 induce autoantibody production and liver dysfunction

Foreign DNA could be recognized by DNA sensors which can elicit an immune response as part of host defense against pathogens20. Inappropriate responses to DNA can trigger autoimmunity and contribute to the development of autoimmune diseases21. To investigate the immunostimulatory role of DNA-containing MVs in autoimmune responses, we purified MVs from KpA4-1 (p2-containing) and KpH20-2 (DNA-deficient). Mice were intraperitoneal injected of KpA4-1 MVs, KpH20-2 MVs, or BSA control (Fig. 3A). After treatment, serum analysis revealed significantly elevated ANA levels in KpA4-1 MVs-treated mice (46.12 ± 9.23 pg/ml) compared to both KpH20-2 MVs-treated (4.73 ± 1.00 pg/ml; p < 0.01) and BSA control groups (0.87 ± 0.87 pg/ml; p < 0.01) (Fig. 3B). Furthermore, KpA4-1 MVs administration induced significant increases in liver enzymes, with ALT levels rising to 8.19 ± 0.64 U/L (p < 0.01) and AST to 22.07 ± 1.38 U/L (p < 0.05) versus BSA controls, suggesting potential hepatic injury. Notably, treatment with KpH20-2 MVs also moderately elevated ALT levels (6.44 ± 0.40 U/L; p < 0.05), suggesting that MV components other than DNA may also potentially contribute to hepatic injury.

KpA4-1 exacerbates liver injury in AIH mice through cGAS-STING pathway

To investigate the strain-specific K. pneumoniae involvement in the development of live disease, we established an AIH mouse model via intravenous delivery of adeno-associated virus encoding IL-12 (AAV-IL12). This induces hepatic IL-12 expression and triggers AIH-characteristic liver injury22. Mice were orally gavaged with KpA4-1, KpH20-2 or PBS, starting concomitantly with the initial AAV-IL12 injection (Fig. 4A). After treatment, cecal samples were collected for 16S rRNA sequencing to assess gut microbiota composition. Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA) revealed a significant shift in gut microbiota composition following KpA4-1 treatment compared to the KpH20-2 treatment and control groups. Additionally, the Shannon index indicated that microbial richness and diversity were significantly higher in the KpA4-1 group than in both the KpH20-2 and control groups (Fig. 4B). These findings suggest that the KpA4-1 uniquely alters gut microbiota structure. Furthermore, relative abundance analysis demonstrated a higher proportion of Klebsiella in both the KpA4-1 treated and the KpH20-2 treated group compared with controls.

To assess the hepatic effects of K. pneumoniae strains, we systematically analyzed serum biomarkers and histopathological changes. The KpA4-1 treated group exhibited elevated serum ALT (66.13 ± 10.47 U/L) and AST (175.10 ± 20.07 U/L) levels compared to the PBS control group (ALT: 48.88 ± 5.24 U/L; AST: 149.20 ± 9.66 U/L), indicating substantial liver injury (Fig. 4C). Serum ANA levels, a hallmark of autoimmune hepatitis, were also higher in KpA4-1 treated groups (ANA: 54.86 ± 3.45 U/L) compared to control groups (ANA: 43.79 ± 3.58 U/L), indicating activation of autoimmune response. At the histopathological level, the KpA4-1 treated group displayed pronounced liver injury, involving cytoplasmic vacuolation, loss of nuclear integrity, dense inflammatory cell infiltration surrounding the portal triads with absent hepatocytes. These pathological changes were markedly more severe than those observed in control groups (Fig. 4D), which corroborated biochemical findings. Furthermore, the proportion of hepatic CD8⁺ T cells (CD8⁺/CD45⁺) were significantly increased in KpA4-1 treated group compared to the control group, indicating enhanced liver immune response (Fig. 4E and Supplementary Fig. 5). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed markedly enhanced STING staining in the livers of mice treated with KpA4-1 compared to those treated with KpH20-2 or PBS (Fig. 4F). Consistent with STING activation, levels of IFN-β and IFN-γ were also significantly increased in the KpA4-1 treated group. However, no significant differences were observed in TLR9 or IL-6 levels among the groups (Fig. 4F). These findings suggest that KpA4-1 predominantly activates the cGAS–STING signaling pathway, leading to the production of downstream pro-inflammatory mediators such as IFN-β and IFN-γ, which may contribute to exacerbating hepatic inflammation in AIH.

Minus of the p2 plasmid in KpA4-1 (KpA4-1Δp2) ameliorates hepatic inflammatory responses in the AIH mice

To investigate the role of the p2 plasmid in AIH progression, we generated a p2-minus mutant strain KpA4-1Δp2 from the wild-type strain KpA4-1. Strikingly, KpA4-1Δp2 exhibited defective production of typical MVs, as evidenced by abnormal vesicle size distribution and morphological alterations (Fig. 5A). Proteomics analysis of the KpA4-1Δp2 supernatant further revealed marked differences in protein composition compared to KpA4-1 MVs, which were corroborated by mass spectrometry (Fig. 5A and Supplementary Table 4). These findings demonstrate that p2 plasmid is essential for MV biogenesis in KpA4-1.

To evaluate the functional consequences of p2 deletion, AIH mice were treated with either KpA4-1 or KpA4-1Δp2 for six weeks. Analysis of gut microbiota composition (PCoA) and diversity (Shannon index) revealed significant perturbations in the KpA4-1 treated group, whereas KpA4-1Δp2 administration had no discernible impact on microbial profiles (Fig. 5B), indicating that p2 plasmid is required for KpA4-1 mediated gut microbiota modulation. Consistent with previous observations, KpA4-1 treated mice exhibited elevated serum levels of compared to PBS controls. In contrast, mice treated with KpA4-1Δp2 maintained hepatic injury markers (ALT: 42.20 ± 3.51 U/L; AST: 121.60 ± 4.74 U/L; ANA: 45.87 ± 3.09 U/L) comparable to PBS controls (ALT: 48.88 ± 5.24 U/L; AST: 149.20 ± 9.66 U/L; ANA: 43.79 ± 3.58 U/L) (Fig. 5C). Histopathological analysis further supported these results, demonstrating reduced inflammatory infiltration and milder histological damage in KpA4-1Δp2 treated mice relative to the KpA4-1 treated group (Fig. 5D). Additionally, hepatic CD8⁺ T cell proportions in the KpA4-1Δp2 treated group remained similar to those in PBS controls (Fig. 5E). Immunohistochemical analysis revealed no significant increase in STING, IFN-β, IFN-γ, TLR9, or IL-6 staining in the livers of mice treated with KpA4-1Δp2 (Fig. 5F). These findings suggest that, unlike its parental strain KpA4-1, the deletion of p2 from KpA4-1 abolished its ability to activate the cGAS–STING signaling pathway and exacerbate hepatic inflammation in AIH.

Bacteriophage targeting of KpA4-1 rescues hepatic injury

To further investigate the involvement of KpA4-1 in AIH progression, we employed bacteriophage therapy to specifically reduce its abundance in the intestinal tract. By screening 32 clinical K. pneumoniae isolates as host, we identified a lytic bacteriophage, phiA4-1 (Genbank accession No. PX204642), which specifically targets KpA4-1. phiA4-1 demonstrated the strongest lytic activity against KpA4-1 and exhibited a narrow host range (Supplementary Fig. 6). Based on these properties, phiA4-1 was selected for subsequent experiments. The bacteriophage phiA4-1 formed large, clear plaques on lawn of KpA4-1 (Fig. 6A). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) revealed that phiA4-1 has an icosahedral head and a long non-contracted tail, classifying as a member of the Siphoviridae family (Fig. 6A). At MOI 10 to 0.0001, phiA4-1 could completely inhibit KpA4-1 growth in 3 h (Fig. 6A). Whole-genome sequencing showed that phiA4-1 contains a circular double-stranded DNA genome of 40,808 bp, encoding 46 putative open reading frames (ORFs), with no identifiable antibiotic resistance or virulence genes (Supplementary Table 5).

To determine whether bacteriophage therapy could ameliorate hepatic injury induced by KpA4-1 strains, AIH mice were orally gavaged with 2 × 109 CFU/mL KpA4-1 every 2 days for 6 weeks. Two weeks after the start of KpA4-1 gavage, 2 × 107 PFU/mL bacteriophage phiA4-1 was administered by oral gavage 1 h later following KpA4-1 gavage, while control mice received only PBS (Fig. 6B). Bacteriophage therapy resulted in a gut microbial profile that closely resembled that of control groups (Fig. 6C). ANA (43.89 ± 3.07 U/L) and serum transaminase levels of ALT (45.84 ± 6.28 U/L) and AST (123.40 ± 9.49 U/L) were also declined in bacteriophage treated mice as compared to KpA4-1-treated group (ALT: 66.13 ± 10.47 U/L; AST: 175.10 ± 20.07 U/L; ANA: 54.86 ± 3.45 U/L) (Fig. 6D). Histopathological assessment featured a trend of improvement under bacteriophage treatment, including an alleviated inflammation process and live injury in bacteriophage treated mice as compared to control, such as decreased lymphocyte-predominant inflammatory infiltrate in liver (Fig. 6E). Hepatic CD8⁺ T cell proportions also decreased after bacteriophage treatment (Fig. 6F). These results further supported that strain-specific KpA4-1-induced AIH progress.

Discussion

Autoimmune hepatitis arises from complex interactions between genetic predisposition and environmental triggers. Among these, gut microbiota dysbiosis has emerged as a significant contributing factor. Disorders in the gut microbiota can trigger inflammation that disrupts immune homeostasis, potentially leading to autoimmune diseases23. There is growing evidence that imbalanced gut microbiota can affect the liver through the gut-liver axis, leading to various hepatic diseases, such as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), primary sclerosing cholangitis (PSC), and primary biliary cholangitis (PBC)24.

Previous studies have identified gut microbiota dysbiosis in AIH patients, with particular enrichment of Klebsiella species, implicating their potential involvement in disease pathogenesis. To explore the contribution of K. pneumoniae to AIH development, we performed whole-genome sequencing of isolates obtained from both healthy and AIH patients. Phylogenetic analysis demonstrated no significant sublineage clustering by disease status, reflecting the extensive genomic diversity among gut-colonizing K. pneumoniae populations. Notably, there are hundreds of distinct phylogenetic lineages or clones within the K. pneumoniae population, and even the infectious K. pneumoniae are caused by diverse clones25. Therefore, we speculated that strain-specific bacterial components, rather than clonal lineage, might mediate autoimmune responses in AIH pathogenesis.

MVs, which carry specific cargo, are commonly produced by Gram-negative bacteria and are involved in inflammation and immunomodulation16. It has been reported that MV-associated antigen in the intestinal tract can cross the intestinal mucosal barrier and access the intestinal epithelial cells, initiating inflammation26,27. Our study identified a number of intestinal K. pneumoniae that could produce MVs from both AIH and healthy. Analysis of the composition of these MVs showed that KpA4-1-derived MVs contained DNA component, whereas the other MVs did not. High-throughput sequencing and comparative analysis revealed that the DNA packaged by the KpA4-1-derived MVs was a plasmid homologous to p2 previously reported in strain Kp160417. As reported, p2 in Kp1604 was predicted to encode conserved bacteriophage proteins, but could not be induced as a bacteriophage. Notably, p2 was mainly distributed in Enterobacteriaceae, including Klebsiella pneumoniae, Escherichia coli, Salmonella enterica and Klebsiella oxytoca et al. As most of the p2-like plasmid-carrying strains were virulent and isolated from human samples17, we postulated that they might play a role in the development of disease.

Extracellular DNA, well-characterized for its immunostimulatory properties and association with autoimmune pathogenesis21, originates from multiple sources, including apoptotic cells, NETotic neutrophils, bacterial biofilms, and bacterial MVs. However, the specific contribution of bacterial MV-derived DNA to immune dysregulation remains poorly defined. In our experiments, intraperitoneal administration of DNA-containing MVs from KpA4-1 significantly elevated serum levels of ANA, ALT, and AST in mice. Although this treatment did not induce overt histopathological liver damage, these findings demonstrate the potential pathogenicity of DNA-containing MVs in driving autoimmune responses. More strikingly, in our AIH mouse model, oral administration of KpA4-1 exacerbated disease pathology, manifesting as elevated liver enzymes, increased autoantibody production, and pronounced CD8 + T cell infiltration. The critical role of MV-contained DNA in AIH pathogenesis was further corroborated by the attenuated disease phenotype observed with the p2-deficient KpA4-1 strain (KpA4-1Δp2). Mechanistically, foreign and self-DNA are known to activate pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), including cytosolic DNA sensors (e.g., cGAS–STING) and endosomal receptors (e.g., TLR9)20. Our data demonstrate that KpA4-1 activates the cGAS–STING pathway via its p2-containing MVs, driving the production of pro-inflammatory mediators that amplify hepatic inflammation in AIH.

While our findings support the potential of strain-specific bacteriophage therapy targeting KpA4-1, several important limitations should be carefully considered. Notably, phage resistance emerged rapidly in vitro, within approximately 3 h, and resistance frequencies remained high across different multiplicities of infection (MOIs). These findings underscore an inherent limitation of mono-phage therapy, as the rapid and consistent evolution of resistance may substantially compromise treatment durability. In clinical and experimental settings, phage cocktails have been proposed as a strategy to broaden antibacterial coverage and mitigate resistance development by simultaneously targeting multiple bacterial receptors28. However, whether cocktail-based approaches would confer sustained therapeutic benefit in the context of microbiota dysregulation requires further experimental validation. In addition, in vivo phage efficacy is influenced by host immune clearance, pharmacokinetics, and ecological interactions within the gut microbiome, which could further modulate resistance dynamics and treatment stability. Therefore, further studies incorporating optimized phage combinations and more rigorous preclinical validation will be required before the therapeutic applicability of this approach can be fully established.

In summary, our study identifies a novel mechanism by which DNA-containing MVs of K. pneumoniae strains may contribute to AIH pathogenesis. Future studies should investigate the association between p2-containing K. pneumoniae strains and AIH, with the aim of evaluating their potential as diagnostic or therapeutic targets.

Methods

Human subjects

Twenty-three patients diagnosed with AIH according to the International Autoimmune Hepatitis Group (IAIHG) criteria were recruited from the outpatient department of Renji Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine, between September 2020 and June 202129. The inclusion criteria were based on the presence of autoantibodies, serum IgG levels and histological abnormalities of the liver, with the exception of viral hepatitis. Healthy controls recruited from volunteers participating in the annual health examinations at the health manage center of Renji Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Supplementary Table 1). Fresh fecal samples from all participants were promptly collected and processed for bacterial isolation or frozen at −80 °C within 3 h of sampling. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital, affiliated with Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine (Approval no. 2013–030).

16S rRNA sequencing of fecal microbiota and data processing

Fecal DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Fast DNA Stool Mini Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacture’s instruction. The V3-V4 hypervariable region of the 16S rRNA gene with the forward primer ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCA and the reverse primer TCGGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT, was amplified by PCR and subsequently sequenced on the Illumina platform by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The resulting sequences were processed utilizing the Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology 2(QIIME2) platform30. We then used vsearch plugin31 to cluster sequences into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) at 97% identity and the taxonomy was assigned against the NCBI database. Sequence alignment was performed employing MAFFT32 and phylogenetic tree was constructed with FastTree33. Alpha diversity analysis was conducted to assess the species richness and diversity within individual samples. Beta diversity analysis was applied to discern variations in community composition and structure among different samples.

Isolation of K. pneumoniae and bacteriophage

To isolate K. pneumoniae strains from feces, 1 g of feces was resuspended in 9 ml of Brian Heart Infusion (BHI) medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS). The resulting suspension was subjected to serial dilution, and aliquots were plated onto BHI, Salmonella-Shigella agar (SS), and Columbia Blood agar plates to culture under aerobic conditions at 37 °C for 24 h. The colonies were identified using Matrix-Assisted Laser Desorption/Ionization Time-of-Flight (MALDI-TOF) mass spectrometry. The bacteriophage was isolated from the sewage with the isolated K. pneumoniae strains as host and purified by the method in previous study34. After purification, the isolates were stored at −80 °C until use.

Whole-genome sequencing and analysis of K. pneumoniae isolates

K. pneumoniae isolates was grown in Luria-Bertani (LB) broth to an OD600 of 0.6∼0.8. DNA was extracted and genome sequencing was performed with the PacBio Sequel and Illumina NovaSeq platforms at Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation. The assembly of the whole-genome sequence was carried out by HGAP35 and CANU36. Gene annotation was carried out by online tool RAST(https://rast.nmpdr.org/). Pan-genome analysis and phylogenetic tree analysis were performed using Roary37. Multi locus sequence typing (MLST) were analyzed by BIGSdb-Pasteur web platform (https://bigsdb.pasteur.fr/klebsiella/). K-antigen of extracellular polysaccharide (CPS), O-antigen of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and the virulence genes, including yersiniabactin (ybt); colibactin (clb); aerobactin (iuc); salmochelin (iro); hypermucoidy rmpADC (rmpADC); hypermucoidy rmpA2 (rmpA2), were analyzed by Kleborate38. Multiple alignment of the whole genome were conducted by Progressive Mauve39.

For comparative analysis of the plasmid encapsulated in strain KpA4-1 MVs, BLASTn searches of the sequence (with accession number PP372685 in GenBank database) were performed against the GenBank database at NCBI. Sequences with identity (>95%) and coverage (>85%) were identified as homologous plasmids. The complete genome sequences of K. pneumoniae strains containing homologous plasmids were downloaded (https://github.com/kblin/ncbi-genome-download) and analyzed. The distribution of MLST types, K-antigens, O-antigens and isolation sources for these strains was plotted using Seaborn (https://github.com/mwaskom/seaborn).

Construction of p2 minus strain KpA4-1Δp2

To excise the p2 plasmid in KpA4-1 strain, a two-plasmid CRISPR-Cas system was used as previously described40. Briefly, temperature-sensitive pCasKP-apr was electroporated into KpA4-1. Since the replication of the plasmid depends on its replication gene (rep), we designed the spacer on rep gene for knockout. The CRISPR-Cas9 spacer plasmid was constructed by cloning the rep gene sequence (TAGTCAAGCTCCCGCGCAAAGAGA) from p2 into pSGKP-spec vector. The resulting plasmid was electroporated into the pCasKP-apr-harboring strainKpA4-1. The two-plasmid CRISPR-Cas system was cured as described40. The successful p2 minus strain, designated KpA4-1Δp2, was verified by PCR with primers p2-F (CCTTTCTCTAATCCACGCTTTTGAA) and p2-R (AGCATGACCGAAGAAATGGATAC).

Membrane vesicles (MVs) preparation and purification

K. pneumoniae strains were grown in LB broth at 37 °C with or without the addition of 5 µg/ml of mitomycin C (MMC) at 220 rpm for 6 h. The bacteria cells were removed by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was then passed through a 0.22 µm filter membrane (Millipore, Merck). The filtrate was concentrated via ultrafiltration using a molecular weight cutoff (MWCO) column (Millipore, Merck) of 100 kDa at 4 °C. For MV purification, the concentrate was suspended in 50% (w/v) iodixanol (Optiprep™; Sigma-Aldrich) and subjected to iodixanol density gradient ultracentrifugation at 200,000 × g at 4 °C for 16 h, as previously described41. The MV-containing layer was dialyzed against phosphate buffer saline (PBS) three times for 4 h each time, followed by ultracentrifugation at 140,000 × g for 4 h at 4 °C. The MV pellets were resuspended in PBS and the total protein content of the purified MVs was determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Sangon Biotech). The purified MVs were then stored at −80 °C until further use.

Analysis of the DNA and proteome in MVs

For DNA analysis in MVs, the purified MV samples were treated with DNase I (Takara) at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, DNA was extracted and whole-genome sequence was performed with the PacBio Sequel and Illumina NovaSeq platforms at Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation as described above. To verify DNA in MVs, the primers p2-F (CCTTTCTCTAATCCACGCTTTTGAA) and p2-R (AGCATGACCGAAGAAATGGATAC) were designed to amplify specific sequence fragment in p2 and 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 27 F (AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG) and 1492 R (GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT) as control.

For proteome analysis of MVs, the purified MV sample was subjected to mass spectrometry using a Thermo Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer interfaced with an Easy-nLC 1200 and an Easy Spray source (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA, USA), in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The resulting MS/MS ion spectra were processed and analyzed with PEAKS Studio 10.0 (Bioinformatics Solutions, Waterloo, ON, Canada) for de novo sequencing and database searching to identify the proteome components.

Growth of bacteriophage and bacteria

The in vitro effectiveness of isolated bacteriophages against host bacteria were assessed using growth curves. Briefly, bacteriophages were added to the bacteria culture at OD600 of 0.1, using serial 10-fold dilutions with multiplicity of infection (MOI) ratios ranging from 10 to 0.0001. The OD600 was measured hourly for 5 h. A negative control containing only LB medium was also included. Assays were performed in triplicate and the results were analyzed by GraphPad Prism10.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The purified MVs and bacteriophage was negatively stained with 2% phosphotungstic acid and the morphology was examined by TEM (Tecnai G2 Spirit Biotwin, FEI COMPANY, Hillsboro, QR, United States).

Animal experiment

Female C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old) were obtained from SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The mice were housed in a controlled environment under a 12-h light/dark cycle. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine and conducted in compliance with ethical guidelines.

To treat the mice with MVs, mice (n = 4 ~ 6 per group) were assigned to three experimental groups. 10 μg of purified KpA4-1 MVs (n = 5), KpH20-2 MVs (n = 6) or BSA control (n = 4) were administered via intraperitoneal (i.p.) injection in a 200 μl PBS suspension. The injections were performed twice weekly for six consecutive weeks.

To investigate the role of K. pneumoniae isolates of KpA4-1, KpH20-2 or KpA4-1Δp2 in exacerbating liver disease, C57BL/6 mice (n = 5 per group) were assigned to six experimental groups. AIH mouse model was induced via interleukin-12 (IL-12) administration during bacterial gavage, with modifications as described22,42. The adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8) vector carrying a single-chain IL-12 transgene was generated by Obio Technology (Shanghai, China). The construct was driven by a chimeric albumin promoter to ensure liver-specific IL-12 expression. Five groups of mice received intravenous injections of AAV-IL-12 at a dose of 5 × 10¹⁰ viral genomes/kg, while control mice were administered an equivalent dose of AAV-luciferase. Subsequently, mice were treated with 2 × 109 CFU K. pneumoniae isolates of KpA4-1, KpH20-2, KpA4-1Δp2 or PBS control in 200 μl via oral gavage at two-day intervals over a 6-week period. For bacteriophage therapy, treatment was initiated two weeks after the start of KpA4-1 administration. Mice received 2 × 107 PFU of bacteriophage in 200 μL via oral gavage 1 h later after bacterial KpA4-1 inoculation, with treatment continuing for four weeks.

The mice used in this experiment were anesthetized with sodium pentobarbital, which was administered by intraperitoneal injection. The injection dose was 40–50 mg/kg (2%) intraperitoneally. Blood samples were obtained to evaluate liver function by measuring serum levels of alanine transaminase (ALT), aspartate transaminase (AST), and antinuclear antibodies (ANA). Liver tissues were harvested, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, paraffin-embedded and sectioned. For histopathological examination, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). For immunohistochemistry, sections were incubated with the following antibodies at the indicated dilutions: TLR9 (Absin, abs124402; 1:200), STING (STARTER, S0B0906; 1:500), IFN-β (Absin, abs149274; 1:100), IL-6 (Abcam, ab290735; 1:100), and IFN-γ (Absin, abs146068; 1:100). Cecal contents were collected for 16S rRNA sequencing.

Determination of serum liver enzymes of ALT and AST, autoantibody of ANA

To assess liver enzymes of ALT and AST, and autoantibody of ANA, blood samples were processed by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C, and the serum was collected for subsequent analysis. Serum levels of ALT and AST were analyzed by commercially available assay kits (Jiancheng Institute of Biotechnology, Nanjing) and the autoantibody of ANA were detected by ELISA kits (mlbio).

Flow cytometry

Liver tissues were homogenized, and mononuclear cells were isolated by Percoll density gradient centrifugation. For flow cytometry analysis, cells were stained with fluorescently labeled antibodies for 30 minutes at 4 °C, then analyzed using an LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA). Data were processed using FlowJo software (v10.4; Tree Star, Ashland, OR). Details of antibodies used for flow cytometry analysis are provided in Supplementary Table 6. The markers used for identifying different immune cell subsets were as follows: TCRb+CD4+ for CD4+ T cells; TCRb+CD8+ for CD8+ T cells; CD45⁺CD19+ for B cells; CD11b+Ly6C+ for monocytes; CD11b+F4/80+ for macrophages; CD11c⁺ MHC II⁺ for dendritic cells; CD11b+Ly6G+ for neutrophil; TCRb-NK1.1⁺ for NK cells; TCRb+NK1.1⁺ for NKT cells.

Statistics

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 10 software. Data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences between two groups were evaluated using either a two-tailed Student’s t test or Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Comparison of more than two groups was performed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test. Correlations were tested using the Spearman rank correlation test. The comparison of proportions between two groups was carried out using Fisher’s exact test. A p value of less than 0.05 was set as the threshold for statistical significance.
