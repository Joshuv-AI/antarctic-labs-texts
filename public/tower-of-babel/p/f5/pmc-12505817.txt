Ethical-Linguistic constitution of clinical subjectivities: a Lévinasian perspective
Authors: Carlos Eduardo Pompilio, Mariana de Toledo França
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis article explores the clinical encounter not merely as a site for technical intervention or diagnostic reasoning, but as a complex event where epistemology and ethics converge. Challenging the reduction of medicine to scientific protocols, it argues for a conceptual reorientation grounded in language and human relationality. The encounter between clinician and patient is framed as both an epistemic inquiry and a moral covenant, where understanding a patient’s condition requires access not only to biological data but to their social, cultural, and linguistic lifeworld. While the sciences offer truth about the body, they do not suffice to grasp the full existential dimension of illness. Language thus becomes central—not only as a medium of communication, but as the very space where knowledge and care are shaped and shared. It is in and through language that ethical responsibility toward the patient is enacted.MethodThis article synthesizes a philosophical investigation into the ethical and linguistic foundations of medical practice. Drawing on the work of Wilhelm von Humboldt, Émile Benveniste, Emmanuel Levinas, and decolonial thinkers such as Frantz Fanon and Édouard Glissant, it develops a theoretical framework that helps to clarify how subjectivity, vulnerability, and responsibility emerge in and through language during the clinical encounter. The approach is conceptual and interpretive, grounded in close textual analysis and oriented toward the ethical implications of these philosophical insights within the medical practice.FindingsFrom this analysis emerges a critique of dominant ontological assumptions within Western medicine, particularly its tendency to assimilate the Other into pre-existing categories, thereby enacting a form of epistemic violence. Levinas’s distinction between the Said (le Dit) and the Saying (le Dire) becomes central to this critique. The Said corresponds to propositional knowledge and thematic discourse—typical of clinical reasoning—while the Saying signals a more primordial ethical relation: an act of exposure, vulnerability, and responsibility toward the Other. Proximity, as defined by Levinas, is not a spatial or cognitive closeness but an ethical immediacy—a face-to-face relation where the Other appears as irreducibly singular. Humboldt’s and Benveniste’s linguistic theories reinforce this view by emphasizing that subjectivity is dialogical and relational rather than autonomous and pregiven. In contrast to Habermas’s emphasis on validity claims and rational consensus, Levinas privileges the irreducible alterity of the Other as the foundation of ethical life, a move that reframes the conditions under which medical knowledge and care become possible.DiscussionThese philosophical insights have profound implications for the medical practice. When in a clinical encounter, the patients do not merely present symptoms to be categorized—they bring a world that demands ethical attention. Medical language, far from being neutral, reconfigures how illness is understood and treated. The difference between saying a patient “has diabetes,” “suffers from diabetes,” or “is diabetic” reflects deeper assumptions about identity and embodiment. The ethical quality of care hinges on such linguistic choices. Through the lens of Levinas’s Saying, the patient’s voice is heard not simply as information but as a call to responsibility. Moreover, when placed in conversation with decolonial thinkers, this analysis reveals the extent to which colonial and racialized logics continue to shape medical practice. Fanon’s critique of the objectification of Black bodies, and Glissant’s defense of “opacity” against totalizing knowledge systems, highlight how patients are often forced into identities that obscure their singularity. The ethical demand of the clinical encounter thus resists any framework—biological, social, or racial—that seeks to fully determine the patient in advance of relation.ConclusionThe article concludes that ethical responsibility in medicine arises not from what is known about the patient, but from a willingness to engage with what remains unknown and unknowable—their singularity, vulnerability, and alterity. The clinical encounter is reimagined as a moral space where language becomes the medium through which care is not only delivered but ethically constituted. In this reconfiguration, the practice of medicine moves beyond procedural norms and toward a relational ethics rooted in proximity and attentiveness. By bringing together Levinas’s philosophy of language and responsibility with decolonial critiques of medical rationality, the article calls for a fundamental transformation in how healing is conceived: not as mastery over the body, but as a dialogical and ethical relation between singular beings.Keywords: Emmanuel Levinas, Wilhelm von Humboldt, Intersubjectivity, Linguistic theory, Clinical encounter, Decolonial theory, Ethical proximity, Medical humanities

Introduction

When you talk with the patient, you should listen, first, for what he wants to tell, secondly, for what he does not want to tell, thirdly, for what he cannot tell.

Lawrence J Henderson 1935 [1].

There is an ancient bond between knowledge and ethics that, while not exclusive to medicine, finds in it one of its most sensitive and urgent expressions. The clinical encounter— simultaneously an epistemic inquiry and moral covenant—defies reduction to mere technical protocols or procedural frameworks. This archetypal intersubjective meeting demands recognition as a complex human exchange where scientific understanding meets ethical responsibility. To properly understand its nuances requires a philosophical framework that coherently integrates language, knowledge, and ethics, revealing how diagnosis and treatment decisions are inseparable from the interpretive dialogue between practitioner and patient, and the moral dimensions this dialogue inevitably contains. In that context, physicians and other healthcare professionals need to understand not only the bodily experiences of their patients but also those related to the world they inhabit if the aim is to help them in the best possible way. While the description of bodily properties and functioning finds its incontestable truth in science, “understanding the world,” on the other hand, means having access to the culture, social intimacy, and linguistic idiosyncrasies characteristic of each individual—something considerably more diffuse, to say the least. In the words of Fredrik Svenaeus, “[.] clinical practice, therefore, should not be understood merely as applied science, but also as a type of hermeneutics based on empathic, cultural, and communicative skills“ [2, p. 205]. It is interesting to note, however, that many texts addressing issues in what has become known as “medical humanities” and even the philosophy of medicine itself do not explore this latter characteristic—the communicative one. It has, in turn, been absorbed by theoretical currents originating from literary theory that tend to identify the stories produced by patients during the clinical encounter with the object of study of a Narratology of Propp and Greimas, supplemented by techniques of literary criticism such as close reading [3–7] and by notions originating from social psychology [8–10]. However fruitful such approaches may be, and indeed, many positive developments have been achieved with them, especially the recognition of the importance of communicative skills for adequate medical practice, we understand that the richness of communication and the huge linguistic confrontation that occurs during the clinical encounter necessitate a somewhat more multifaceted and broader theoretical framework.

By the same token, rationality within the medical field is often narrowly conceptualized as strict adherence to evidence-based guidelines, algorithms, and protocols—models that align with what Harold I. Brown termed “classical rationality“ [11], characterized by universality, necessity, and rule-based logic. While this model has brought undeniable scientific progress, it struggles to accommodate the contextual, empathic, and ethical dimensions of real-life clinical care. This has led many to seek alternative forms of rationality—ways of thinking and deciding that integrate moral responsibility and human particularity without abandoning formal reason. Yet what human faculty would be capable of bridging the gap between empirical observation and transcendental reflection? How can theoretical knowledge and ethical responsibility be articulated within a single framework? And, above all, what medium allows for the integration of cognitive structures with lived, embodied experience—especially in moments of vulnerability, such as illness? Michel Foucault explores a closely related idea in The Order of Things, in his discussion of modern humanism [12, pp. 347–351]. According to Foucault, the modern conception of the human being—as formulated by the human sciences—exhibits a hybrid structure: it is empirical, insofar as the human is studied by disciplines such as biology, psychology, and sociology; and transcendental, insofar as the human is also the subject who thinks, reflects, and endows experience with meaning. This dual positioning generates a fundamental paradox: the human being is simultaneously the ground of all knowledge and one of its objects. Foucault identifies this paradox as a source of tension within the human sciences and as a key factor in the destabilization of the subject’s centrality in modern thought, a problem that medicine, in its effort to “humanize” itself, inevitably takes on as well.

We argue that language uniquely inhabits this liminal space between the empirical and the transcendental. It not only appears in observable forms—speech, writing, dialogue—but also underlies our very capacity to think, to mean, and to relate. As such, language is not merely a tool for transmitting clinical information; it is the medium through which knowledge and care, diagnosis and responsibility, illness and disease are rendered mutually intelligible. This dual character makes language a kind of empirical–transcendental hybrid: a phenomenon of the world that also structures our engagement with it. Unlike models centered on consciousness—whether the Cartesian cogito or the Husserlian transcendental ego—language offers a path beyond the antinomies of classical subjectivity. By relocating the foundation of rationality in language, rather than in an isolated subject, we avoid the aporias that haunt philosophies of consciousness: the transcendental “I” estranged from history, and the empirical “I” unable to ground universal knowledge. This line of reasoning became established as the “linguistic turn.”

This theoretical structure takes in consideration the fact that language is not merely a vehicle for transmitting clinical information; it is the very medium through which the ethical dimension of medicine unfolds [13]. It is only through language that theoretical knowledge and moral behavior can be integrated, drawing from a preconceptual structure shaped by human evolution. In the clinical setting, where patients simultaneously embody both the object of medical inquiry and the essential foundation of healthcare knowledge, language alone possesses the unique capacity to navigate this fundamental paradox. The presence of illness, and the presence of the physician, amplify this existential tension and call for a form of communication that can move beyond the objective and toward genuine mutual understanding.

The task, therefore, is to confront this significant challenge by specifically seeking out lines of thought that are structured as cognitive theories culminating in an ethics, or, conversely, as philosophies of intersubjectivity that, through the ethical interaction between individuals, encompass a theory of knowledge. The former must maintain that the idea of morality arises – and is sustained – through dialogue between the involved parties. The latter must demonstrate that morality emerges primarily from the ethical apprehension of the other, and only then does dialogue arise. The first approach outlined above is anchored in Jürgen Habermas’s Theory of Communicative Action [14]; the second finds its articulation in the philosophy of Emmanuel Levinas [15–17]. Both authors aim to attribute ethical properties to language’s communicative dimension in their respective theories. These authors are also proponents of a “linguistic turn,” whose primary characteristic is the extension of its methodological reach to areas beyond traditional epistemology and metaphysics. Habermas and Levinas depart initially from the Humboldtian presupposition (see below) according to which language has (i) a plane of semantic content and (ii) a plane of content being conveyed to someone (pragmatic realm). Habermas calls the latter the illocutionary force of speech acts and the capacity to provoke reactive attitudes in interlocutors. Levinas, in turn, attributes to the Said (le Dit) and the Saying (le Dire). For both, to say something to someone is to direct a meaning, a logical nucleus that needs to be comprehended based on the simple articulations of the Said (or semantic realm), but it is hardly a mere service to one’s own expressive need. Or rather, there is always something more to saying something to someone, and it is precisely at this point that communication emerges as significant. If for Habermas, the pragmatic structure of communication, in which the act of saying something to someone is inserted, involves a context of interaction that presupposes validity claims between interlocutors regarding truth, sincerity, and legitimacy, for Levinas, the ethical dimension transcends the propositional content of the Said and manifests itself in the Saying, the ethical dimension that overflows the propositional content of the Said.

For Levinas, our main focus here, language is not only about what is said (le Dit), but the act of saying (le Dire)—a moment of exposure and responsibility to the Other [16]. This ethical asymmetry, in which the speaker is summoned by the face of the Other, precedes any claim to objectivity or knowledge. In this sense, Levinas offers a radical alternative to classical rationality: one that grounds understanding not in consensus or procedure, but in vulnerability, responsibility, and ethical responsiveness. In confronting the limitations of procedural reason, his philosophy reveals how language can serve as a site of ethical recognition, and how medicine, at its core, is not only a science of bodies but a dialogue between persons. The implications of this are far-reaching. If language precedes and conditions both thought and subjectivity, then clinical ethics must move beyond objectivist models of disease to account for the communicative acts through which illness is narrated, shared, and made intelligible — as Habermas suggests [18, 19]. Conversely, if it is through language that the patient emerges not merely as a body marked by pathology but as a subject who calls for recognition and summons the caregiver to responsibility, then it is ethics that precedes language, as in the Levinasian account. In both views, the clinician’s task is not only diagnostic or technical, but hermeneutic and ethical: to interpret the world disclosed in language and to respond within the horizon of intersubjective meaning. Clinical care thus becomes a form of dialogue—an ethically charged encounter where language is not ancillary, but foundational.

We will begin our discussion with an outline of the linguistic foundation of subjectivity, drawing upon Humboldt and Benveniste. Subsequently, we will delve into the role that Levinas attributes to language within his philosophy, as well as the critique he develops of a certain ontologization of language brought about by the linguistic turn. Next, we will explore a real-world instance experienced by one of the authors (CEP) during their medical residency, using it as an exemplification of the Levinasian notion of proximity. In conclusion, we will engage with the decolonial philosophy of Fanon and Glissant to underscore the intricate nature of applying such perspectives in medicine.

Language and subjectivity

If we accept that the core of medicine is defined by the physician–patient relationship, and that this relationship is essentially linguistic, then we must ask why this “linguistic-clinical” structure has not kept pace with the transformations that language theory has undergone since the 20th century. The linguistic turn in philosophy marked a radical shift: language ceased to be merely a vehicle for ideas and became constitutive of thought itself [20]. Across epistemology, logic, anthropology, and ethics, language became central to how we understand human experience and intersubjective meaning. In this context, the question of valid knowledge shifted toward the conditions for intersubjectively meaningful statements— an orientation that bears directly on medicine, where clinical reasoning depends on how illness is expressed, interpreted, and communicated.

To illustrate, consider three very common and simple statements: (I) “John has diabetes,” (II) “John suffers from diabetes,” and (III) “John is diabetic.” Though all refer to the same condition—an altered glucose metabolism—they differ semantically and ethically. Statement (I) implies detachment: diabetes is something John possesses. Statement (II) expresses impact and suffering, while (III) suggests identity, with the disease intertwined with John’s selfhood. These variations reflect different ontologies of illness, ranging from external object (I), to invasive force (II), to constitutive feature (III). Despite referring to the same clinical entity, each sentence invites a distinct mode of ethical and clinical engagement. This distinction echoes the philosophical differentiation between reference (the object in the world) and sense (the mode of presentation), a move initiated by Gottlob Frege [21] and deepened by the linguistic turn. In medicine, this implies that understanding illness requires attention not only to its referential diagnosis, but to the ways it is discursively framed and lived.

At the same time, we must situate this insight within the broader philosophical landscape. The late 19th and early 20th centuries, after Hegel’s demise, saw a rupture within philosophy itself, producing three main traditions: the hermeneutic-phenomenological school (e.g., Husserl, Heidegger, Gadamer, Levinas, Ricoeur), the analytic-pragmatic school (e.g., Frege,

Vienna Circle, Wittgenstein, Dewey), and the dialectical-materialist tradition (e.g., Marx, Adorno, Althusser) [22]. Despite their divergences, all converged on the critique of positivism and recognized language as foundational [23]. Within this constellation, the German tradition, beginning with Hamann, Herder, and Humboldt, offered a decisive reframing: language is not a mere empirical phenomenon, nor a simple tool of representation—it is the condition of possibility for both cognition and communication.

Wilhelm von Humboldt (1767–1835), in particular, advanced a dual conception of language [13, 24]. On the one hand, language is cognitively constitutive: it is not an object among others, but that which shapes and enables thought. On the other hand, it is pragmatically generative: language is a social praxis that gives rise to intersubjectivity and mutual understanding. This is most evident in his analysis of personal pronouns. For Humboldt, the pronoun “you” (as opposed to “she/he” or “it”) is central to the constitution of the self. The “I” emerges not through opposition to a third-person object, but through dialogical engagement with a second-person interlocutor. In other words, subjectivity arises in relation—not from isolation [13]. This insight has profound consequences for medicine since suggests that the ethical core of clinical practice is not the relation between a knowing subject and an objectified body, but a relation between speaking, suffering subjects—between I and you.

These ideas were further expanded by Émile Benveniste (1902–1976) [25]. He proposed that pronominal analysis emerges as one of the most significant endeavors to comprehend the constitution of subjectivity from the very structure of language. Benveniste views pronouns not as grammatical substitutes but as foundational to subjectivity. “I” and “you” pronouns constitute the subject within the speech act rather than merely expressing pre-existing subjects. The “I” functions as a position realized only when uttered, representing a discursive function rather than a fixed identity. This position inherently requires a “you” as addressee. This approach inverts traditional subject theory insofar the subjectivity emerges through language rather than preceding it. The subject is constituted through discourse in an inherently dialogical relationship. While modern philosophy typically positions the subject as language’s foundation, Benveniste argues the opposite—language founds the subject.

It is within this framework offer by Humboldt and Benveniste that we can understand subjectivity as a product of language. To say “I” is, at the same time, to affirm oneself as a subject and to institute an intersubjective space in which otherness becomes the condition of possibility for the very act of speaking. Language, in enabling this dynamic, accomplishes something more than communication, compassionate care, and data collection. It founds the ethics of discourse by making each subject responsible for what they say before an other who responds or can respond, creating an ethical framework particularly relevant to clinical encounters. When physician and patient meet, they engage in more than information exchange—they participate in a space where two subjects recognize each other in asymmetrical yet interdependent positions structured by language [26]. In the clinic, physicians address a concrete “you” with a life and history, not a generic object of knowledge. Similarly, patients are not merely symptom repositories but subjects who express themselves, even through pain or silence. This reciprocal exchange of speaking and listening forms the ethical foundation of clinical encounters—recognizing that language establishes the bond between subjects.

Language in Levinas and his critique of the linguistic turn

Emmanuel Levinas’s philosophy offers a bold and transformative reorientation of Western thought: a shift from ontology—the inquiry into being—to ethics, understood as the foundational relation between subjects. This inversion arises from Levinas’s critique of a philosophical tradition that, in seeking to understand the world and others, often assimilates the radical alterity of the Other (l’Autre) into the categories of the Same (le Même). For Levinas, ontology’s drive to comprehend and categorize enacts an “epistemological violence,” reducing the Other to the familiar terms of the thinking subject. In doing so, the singularity of the Other is effaced, subsumed under conceptual schemas that reflect the values and assumptions of the “subjective I.”

Against this totalizing impulse, Levinas posits an ethics grounded in the recognition of the Other’s irreducible difference—a difference that eludes conceptual grasp and calls the subject into responsibility. This ethics is not merely theoretical but put into practice, unfolding in what Levinas terms the Saying (le Dire), as distinct from the Said (le Dit). To apprehend this ethical orientation, it is essential to consider Levinas’s diagnosis of Western philosophy’s drive toward totality: the tendency to integrate all alterity into an intelligible whole. In this framework, the Other is not encountered in its singularity but absorbed as an object of knowledge. In contrast, Levinas articulates an ethics of exteriority, wherein the Other confronts the subject in a face-to-face relation, eliciting a responsibility that precedes any act of knowing or categorization. This encounter gestures toward the infinite—toward that which escapes the closure of conceptual totality. Language occupies a central place in this ethical relation. It is not simply a representational medium, as traditionally conceived, but the very locus in which the subject encounters the Other. Levinas’s notions of Saying and Said, developed in Otherwise than Being or Beyond Essence (1974) and the essay “Language and Proximity” (1965), are pivotal here [16, 17].

The Said corresponds to thematic, propositional language—the discourse of explanation, systematization, and control. It is the language of science and logic, indispensable for certain purposes, but ultimately inadequate for engaging the Other in their full alterity. By contrast, the Saying represents the ethical dimension of language. It does not aim to classify or define, but to respond, to expose the self to the presence of the Other. In Totality and Infinity [15] and Otherwise than Being, Levinas insists on the primacy of Saying over Said: ethics precedes ontology. Before we speak about the world, we are summoned by the Other. This idea is further elaborated in the section “Essence and Signification” in Otherwise than Being, where Levinas introduces the notion of a pre-original Saying—a mode of address prior to conceptual language, expressed through responsibility. Here, Saying constitutes an act of openness and vulnerability, a gesture of welcome that does not seek epistemic mastery. For Levinas, recognition is anterior to cognition. The relationship between Saying and Said finds its grounding in Levinas’s concept of proximity, as developed in “Language and Proximity.” He writes:

“One must then admit that there is in speech a relationship with a singularity located outside of the theme of the speech, a singularity that is not thematized by the speech but is approached. Speech and its logical work would then unfold not in knowledge of the interlocutor, but in his proximity… The orientation of the subject upon the object has become a proximity, the intentional has become ethical.” [24, pp. 115–116].

In this passage, Levinas articulates a form of relation not mediated by representation, not governed by objectifying knowledge, but rooted in the ethical immediacy of encounter. Proximity, here, is not spatial closeness or logical apprehension, but the very condition that allows for a relation prior to thematization. It is, in itself, a form of signification—an ethical attunement that precedes and grounds all discourse. Despite Levinas not explicitly citing the clinical encounter in his texts, this excerpt appears so remarkably pertinent to clinical practice that it is difficult to conceive of a more fitting example. In stating that discourse and its logical work do not rest upon knowledge of the interlocutor, but rather upon their proximity, Levinas asserts that its ethical and meaningful dimension lies not in attempting to understand or “know” the other person as an object (such as grasping their characteristics or analyzing them conceptually), but rather in the experience of being proximate to them—in proximity itself.

Yet, while acknowledging the importance of language, Levinas critiques the tendency within the linguistic turn to ontologize language by resisting the idea that being and reality are fundamentally constituted by language. For him, the ethical encounter with the Other reveals a dimension of transcendence and alterity that cannot be fully captured or contained within linguistic frameworks, as the Other is not merely a linguistic construct but a concrete presence that exceeds my grasp. He also challenges the prioritization of language systems, which often emphasizes the internal coherence and structural properties rather than the interpersonal and ethical dimension of language as address. Levinas maintains that the meaning of language is not solely derived from its internal relations but from the ethical relation between speakers. Furthermore, he opposes the reduction of subjectivity to linguistic positions. At this point, his perspective diverges from that of Benveniste (see above). Levinas argues that the ethical subject is constituted by its responsibility for the Other, a responsibility that precedes and grounds linguistic self-identification. The “I” is defined not primarily by its position within language but by its being-for-the-Other. Levinas also warns that focusing primarily on the immanent structures of language risks obscuring the radical alterity and transcendence of the Other, who is not simply another instance of the same, knowable through linguistic categories, but a unique being whose face calls into question my selfsufficiency.

Proximity as the ethical groundwork in clinical practice

In a reasoning concerned with the intersubjective constitution of subjectivity, the abstraction of theoretical language must, at some point, touch ground. The vignette does not illustrate the concept in the trivial sense of “example”; rather, it enacts what the concept itself attempts to articulate: that there is a moment in which language ceases to be thematic and becomes relational, in which theory is exposed to its own ethical implications.

A middle-aged man is admitted for shortness of breath. Labs suggest advanced cancer. The team prepares to discuss options. But the patient is withdrawn, avoids eye contact, and says little. He barely reacts when told the diagnosis. The team begins to speculate: denial?

depression? cultural differences? But one evening, a young resident lingers after rounds. He sits quietly by the bedside. No agenda. No checklist. Just presence. After some silence, the patient says: “You’re the first person who didn’t try to explain everything.”

That moment — quiet, uncertain, unstructured — is proximity. Not based in knowledge of the patient, but in being with him. The resident didn’t thematize the patient — didn’t reduce him to a case or theory — but allowed space for the patient’s singularity to emerge. It didn’t replace medical care. But it made care possible. It created the ethical condition in which treatment could be received as care, not just as intervention. Proximity is not another tool in the doctor’s kit. It’s the space in which medicine becomes ethical — where the “I” and the “you” appear, not as roles, but as persons. And in that space, the practice of medicine becomes a response to the infinite demand of the other. Responsibility for the Other, emerging in the face-to-face encounter, constitutes a Saying that precedes any effort at thematization or conceptualization. For Levinas, proximity is not merely spatial or objective but an ethical relation. It recognizes the Other not as an object to be comprehended but as a subject who calls for responsibility. Even before we conceptualize or categorize, we are already in relation with the Other—a relation that demands responsibility. This idea of proximity is especially pertinent in the clinical encounter. Before any diagnosis, there is the encounter: the face of the patient revealing vulnerability and interpellating the physician. This interpellation calls not only for technical knowledge (Said) but for ethical responsibility (Saying), which precedes and underpins medical practice.

Practical applications: toward a decolonial encounter

The efforts to challenge and ultimately dismantle the enduring effects of colonialism—not just in formal political systems, but in knowledge, culture, language, economics, and subjectivity can be called decolonial encounters. Modern Western medicine still operates under totalizing and colonial logics, often reducing patients to fixed categories such as race, origin, and occupation. These structural biases are especially evident—and devastating— when it comes to Black patients, whose pain is routinely underestimated and whose suffering is frequently dismissed. This persistent failure is a stark illustration of how “bias is what prevents Black persons from being fully seen by medicine” [26, 27]. What emerges is a form of epistemic blindness that perpetuates colonial hierarchies at the core of clinical practice. Édouard Glissant directly challenges this reduction of the Other through his poetics of relation [27]. While colonial medicine relies on root-identities—assumptions such as “Black = less pain” or “Black woman = more resilient body”—Glissant proposes relation-identities: a mode of recognition that affirms the Other’s irreducible opacity. In this view, identity is not pre-given or essentialized but constituted in the ethical space of relation. Within the clinical encounter, the healthcare professional is called not by what they presume to know about a patient’s racial or social identity, but by what remains unknown—by the ethical opacity that the encounter unveils. The clinician is thus summoned into a space where certainty gives way to responsibility. Rather than applying a singular model of care, this approach demands attentiveness to the patient’s cultural and historical singularity. The clinic, then, becomes not a site of epistemic dominance, but a space of relation and translation—where healing arises not from mastery, but from listening.

From a decolonial perspective, Frantz Fanon exposes the structural violence inflicted on racialized bodies and subjectivities, particularly in colonial and postcolonial contexts. In Black Skin, White Masks [28], he reveals how medicine functions as a vehicle of colonial logic, reducing the Black subject to an object—something to be diagnosed, controlled, and often dehumanized. The clinical gaze, for Fanon, is an extension of the “white gaze”: a disciplinary vision that pathologizes Black identity and erases both historical context and subjective complexity. Racism, in this framework, is not merely a matter of overt prejudice; it is embedded in the psychic fabric of the Black subject, who, under the weight of imposed alterity, experiences a profound split between body and consciousness. This psychic division—manifested in alienation, inferiority, and self-fragmentation—reveals how the suffering of racialized patients is inseparable from the enduring legacies of colonial domination, slavery, and exclusion.

For both Fanon and Glissant, the colonial model of medicine—totalizing, reductive, and exclusionary—must be surpassed in favor of an ethical and dialogical clinical encounter. Such a transformation requires recognizing that the suffering of the Black patient is not a personal pathology but a symptom of historical and systemic violence. Fanon critiques the medical institution as complicit in reproducing the white gaze, which objectifies and dehumanizes rather than heals. Against this logic, Fanon and Glissant envision an ethics of encounter that honors the opacity of the Other—refusing to reduce individuals to fixed biological or social identities. They advocate instead for a listening attuned to cultural, historical, and existential singularities, grounding care in relational responsibility rather than domination.

Final considerations

Emmanuel Lévinas’s philosophy offers a compelling critique of the Western philosophical tradition, revealing how its ontological preoccupations often eclipse the ethical dimension of human relations. By distinguishing between the Said and the Saying, Lévinas introduces a language of responsibility and recognition. Saying represents the ethical call of the Other— the irreducible expression of their vulnerability—while the Said refers to the fixed content of discourse. This distinction invites a radical rethinking of both language and ethics, with significant implications for medical practice. In the clinical encounter, the patient’s face embodies fragility and alterity, calling not merely for diagnostic accuracy or technical intervention, but for an ethical response rooted in Saying—a response that recognizes the patient’s singularity and accepts responsibility beyond the confines of objective knowledge.

Viewed through this lens, the clinic becomes more than a space for problem-solving; it emerges as a moral space where the patient’s voice—expressed through Saying—demands empathy, attentiveness, and accountability. The tension between Saying and Said, and the linguistic positioning of those involved, challenges healthcare providers to move beyond procedural norms and engage the relational core of healing. In this encounter, care is not simply delivered but constituted through ethical proximity, where the act of speaking and listening itself enacts responsibility. The practice of medicine, then, becomes inseparable from the lived ethics of intersubjectivity.

A good example of these ideas rests in the decolonial encounters. In this sense, Lévinas, Fanon, and Glissant converge in affirming that genuine ethics does not emerge from what is known about the Other, but from a radical openness to their unknowability. True responsibility arises not from mastery or classification, but from an exposure to alterity and a commitment to encounter. The clinical interaction, therefore, cannot be reduced to technical procedures or diagnostic categories, nor should medicine rely on deterministic models— biological or social—that obscure the patient’s full humanity. Instead, it must overcome the dualism between body and subjectivity, attending to the historical, cultural, ethical, and existential dimensions each patient brings. Medical ethics, in this view, resides not in certainty, but in the willingness to be ethically disturbed by what remains unknown.
