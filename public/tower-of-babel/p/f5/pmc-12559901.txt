Peripheral T‐cell lymphoma with central nervous system involvement and elevated interleukin‐10 levels in the cerebrospinal fluid: A case report
Authors: Xin Wang, Haichen Wei, Dan Liu, Hui Wang, Zengjun Li, Lijie Xing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCentral nervous system (CNS) involvement in peripheral T‐cell lymphoma (PTCL) is rare and is associated with poor prognosis. This report presents two pathologically confirmed PTCL cases. Although cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow cytometry did not detect tumor cells, CSF analysis revealed significantly elevated interleukin (IL)‐10 levels and an increased IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio, suggesting CNS involvement. Following effective chemotherapy, which crossed the blood‐brain barrier, IL‐10 levels and the IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio decreased significantly, with clinical improvement. These findings suggest that IL‐10 is a valuable supplementary diagnostic marker for CNS involvement in PTCL. Whole‐brain radiation therapy, as a consolidative treatment, may be effective for residual or refractory lesions and improve prognosis. We review these patients’ clinical and diagnostic features and discuss the role of radiotherapy in the treatment of CNS involvement in PTCL.

INTRODUCTION

Peripheral T‐cell lymphoma (PTCL) is a rare, aggressive non‐Hodgkin lymphoma subtype with a poor prognosis. Central nervous system (CNS) involvement presents a key challenge, with studies showing a median survival of only 3.4–6.1 months after CNS relapse, underscoring the importance of accurate diagnosis. 1 While open surgery or stereotactic biopsy remains the gold standard for suspected CNS lymphoma, alternative diagnostic methods are crucial when biopsy is not feasible. Indirect methods, such as cytology and flow cytometry, offer high specificity (100%) for detecting lymphoma cells in cerebrospinal fluid (CSF); however, their low sensitivity may result in false negatives. 2 Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) effectively detects CNS lymphoma but lacks specificity, as its imaging features can resemble other brain tumors. Thus, relying solely on imaging may lead to diagnostic errors.

Previous studies have shown that elevated CSF interleukin‐10 (IL10) levels (>5 pg/ml) and an IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio >0.72 hold significant diagnostic value in differentiating primary CNS lymphoma (PCNSL). 3 , 4 However, we observed a comparable phenomenon in secondary CNS lymphoma (SCNSL) in PTCL. This report presents two cases of PTCL with CNS involvement, both showing significantly elevated IL‐10 levels in CSF, emphasizing the diagnostic value of the IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio. Building on existing diagnostic tools, we aimed to offer an additional method for the early diagnosis and monitoring of CNS involvement and recurrence in T‐cell lymphoma.

CASE DESCRIPTION

Case 1

A 59‐year‐old male initially presented with an unexplained fever peaking at 38.3°C, accompanied by lymphadenopathy in the axillary and inguinal regions. Imaging revealed multiple enlarged lymph nodes in the bilateral axillary and mediastinal regions and retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy. After one month of disease progression, the patient sought care at our hospital in September 2023.

Upon admission, the patient exhibited neurological symptoms, including sluggish responses, memory impairment, and slurred speech. Physical examination revealed multiple firm, fixed, and non‐tender superficial lymph nodes in the bilateral neck, axillary, and inguinal regions. Further comprehensive diagnostic workup was performed. Fluorine‐18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (18F‐FDG‐PET) showed increased fluorodeoxyglucose uptake in multiple areas, with the largest lymph node measuring 1.6 cm in diameter and a maximum standardized uptake volume (SUVmax) of 15.9.

Bone marrow flow cytometry detected abnormal mature T‐lymphocytes, constituting 0.1% of the total nucleated cells. These cells expressed CD5, CD4, CD7, CD45, CD2, cCD3, and CD45RO but were negative for CD45RA, CD3, CD8, CD56, and TRBC1, indicating phenotypically abnormal T‐lymphocytes. Bone marrow smear revealed 1.5% atypical lymphocytes. Pathologic biopsy and immunohistochemistry of the right cervical lymph node revealed PD‐1 (+), CD10 (‐), EBER (+), partial Bcl‐6 (+), CD21 (indicating disruption of the follicular dendritic cell network), Ki‐67 (+, 80%), and TIA‐1 (+). Based on these findings, angioimmunoblastic T‐cell lymphoma (AITL) was diagnosed.

To assess the association between the patient's neurological symptoms and CNS involvement, we conducted a comprehensive diagnostic workup. CSF flow cytometry showed no abnormal lymphocytes, while neuroimaging revealed multiple hyperintense lesions on brain MRI, suggesting lymphoma infiltration. CSF analysis revealed significantly elevated interleukin levels, including IL‐10 (341.05 pg/ml) and IL‐6 (59.20 pg/ml), with an IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio of 5.76. Additionally, CSF biochemical analysis showed normal glucose and lactate levels, with total protein at approximately 0.48 g/L, effectively ruling out infectious causes. Given the significant diagnostic value of elevated IL‐10 in PCNSL and considering the patient's neurological symptoms and MRI findings, we speculated that the patient might have CNS involvement. Therefore, we administered targeted CNS therapy for the patient.

We modified the treatment regimen from rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone (R‐CHOP) to R‐THOP (replacing cyclophosphamide with thiotepa) in combination with alternating high‐dose methotrexate (HD‐MTX) to target CNS involvement more effectively. After two treatment cycles, PET‐CT demonstrated partial remission, and the patient's neurological symptoms improved significantly, with no abnormalities found on neurological examination. Lumbar puncture revealed a marked reduction in CSF IL‐6 and IL‐10 levels, with IL‐6 returning to 8.61 pg/ml and IL‐10 decreasing significantly to 40.96 pg/ml. The IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio dropped to 4.76, indicating a favorable therapeutic response. Following four treatment cycles, the patient's CSF showed IL‐10 levels of 31.36 pg/ml, IL‐6 levels of 6.59 pg/ml, and an IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio of 4.75. The progressive decline in CSF IL‐10 and IL‐6 levels aligned with the patient's clinical remission, further supporting the effectiveness of the treatment.

Case 2

A 54‐year‐old female presented with progressively worsening abdominal pain. In March 2023, she underwent gastrointestinal endoscopy and imaging studies at a local hospital. PET‐CT revealed thickening of the proximal jejunal wall with tumor‐like expansion and multiple surrounding nodules, with an SUVmax of 6.5. In addition, localized thickening of the pelvic small intestine with an SUVmax of 12.1 was observed, suggesting a high likelihood of malignant disease. Gastrointestinal endoscopy showed multiple ulcers in the duodenum, jejunum, and colon. Biopsy of the intestinal wall revealed NK/T‐cell lymphoma. Immunohistochemistry results were as follows: CD20 (‐), ALK (‐), TIA (+), GranB (+), CD30 (+, 10%), Ki67 (+, 90%), CD3 (+), CD56 (+), EBER (‐), PD‐L1 (+, 30%), CD5 (‐), CD8 (+), and CD4 (partial+). Bone marrow flow cytometry and biopsy showed no significant abnormalities. However, the patient did not undergo chemotherapy regularly.

Owing to worsening abdominal pain, the patient was referred to our hospital in June 2023. A repeat small bowel endoscopy revealed a jejunal ulcerative mass with signs of necrotic bowel segments and perforation risk. Further biopsy and TCR rearrangement confirmed the diagnosis of monomorphic epitheliotropic intestinal T‐cell lymphoma. Immunohistochemical staining showed the following: CD3 (+), CD4 (‐), CD5 (‐), CD8 (+), CD56 (+), Ki‐67 (+, 80%) (Figure 1A–H), EBERpb (‐), CKpan (epithelial+), CD10 (‐), CD2 (‐), CD20 (‐), CD21 (FDC+), CD30 (‐), CD7 (+), CD79a (‐), TIA‐1 (+), and perforin (+). TCR clonality rearrangement was positive.

Jejunal pathology and brain magnetic resonance imaging in patient 2. In the small intestinal mucosa, a diffuse infiltration of lymphocyte‐like cells is observed, accompanied by evidence of tumor cell epitheliotropism (A, 20×, hematoxylin–eosin staining). Under high‐power microscopy, the cells are small to medium in size (1.5–2 times the size of normal small lymphocytes) with scant cytoplasm. The cells exhibit atypia, and the nuclear morphology is irregular. Some cells have relatively large nuclei, and nucleoli are faintly visible in occasional cells. (B, 40×, hematoxylin–eosin staining). Tumor cells are negative for CD4 (C) and CD5 (D) but positive for CD8 (E), CD3 (F), and CD56 (G). The tumor proliferation index, Ki67, is approximately 80% (H). In axial, coronal, and sagittal planes, patchy and nodular abnormal signals are observed in the left basal ganglia (I).

Considering the patient's poor overall condition and significant bowel necrosis, which contraindicated the use of intensive chemotherapy, we opted to initiate second‐line treatment with a combination of Sintilimab, Chidamide, and Decitabine. The specific regimen consisted of Sintilimab 200 mg on day 0, Chidamide 20 mg biweekly, and Decitabine 10 mg on days 1–5, with a 3‐week interval between cycles. After this treatment, the patient's abdominal pain improved, and her overall condition stabilized. However, on re‐evaluation after two cycles of treatment, follow‐up CT showed poor response to the treatment, and the patient experienced seizures. Neurological examination revealed no significant abnormalities; however, a head MRI detected multiple enlarging abnormal signals, suggesting CNS involvement of PTCL (Figure 1I).

To refine the diagnosis, we conducted CSF flow cytometry, next‐generation sequencing, and lumbar puncture, none of which revealed significant abnormalities. However, similar to that of patient 1, CSF analysis of this patient revealed significantly elevated IL‐10 (106.50 pg/ml) and IL‐6 (126.00 pg/ml) levels, with an IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio of 0.84, exceeding the standard threshold of 0.72. Combined with imaging findings and the elevated CSF IL‐10 level, these results confirmed CNS involvement in this patient.

The patient received one cycle of third‐line treatment with mitoxantrone liposome, Duvelisib, and Thiotepa. Follow‐up imaging assessments showed improvement in the jejunal lymphoma on CT; however, MRI indicated worsening CNS involvement. After evaluating the patient's condition, we initiated a fourth‐line treatment with HD‐MTX. However, the patient's condition rapidly deteriorated. Owing to the lack of effective treatment options, the patient died one week later, and an autopsy was not performed.

DISCUSSION

PTCL is a heterogeneous hematologic malignancy, with common subtypes including PTCL‐NOS, AITL, and ALK‐positive anaplastic large cell lymphoma, with the latter more prone to CNS involvement. 5 Therefore, comprehensive CNS evaluation at diagnosis is essential for effective CNS prophylaxis. Currently, brain biopsy remains the diagnostic gold standard. However, the identification of CNS involvement in aggressive B‐cell lymphomas has significantly advanced. For example, studies indicate that IL‐10 levels in CSF are significantly elevated in PCNSL, making it a reliable diagnostic biomarker. 6 Salmaggi et al. found that the mean CSF IL‐10 concentration in PCNSL was 14.8±23 pg/ml, significantly higher than in other CNS tumors. 7 Additionally, the diagnostic utility of the CSF IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio is well‐established, with cutoff values ranging from 0.72 to 1.6, sensitivities of 66%–95.5%, and specificities of 91%–100%. 8 However, the specificity of these markers has not yet been reported in PTCL. While elevated plasma IL‐10 levels are associated with poor prognosis in patients with PTCL and CNS involvement, 9 research on CSF IL‐10 levels in these patients remains limited.

Diagnosing CNS lymphoma can be challenging owing to its similarity to other CNS tumors and rare inflammatory diseases. 10 However, elevated IL‐10 levels are not typically observed in the CSF of patients with these inflammatory conditions. 4 Additionally, studies have shown that CSF IL‐10 levels are significantly decreased in patients with high‐grade astrocytomas. 11 In contrast, CSF IL‐10 levels were significantly elevated in SCNSL in PTCL, with an IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio >0.72. This finding suggests that monitoring CSF IL‐10 and IL‐6 levels complements traditional CNS diagnostic methods when CNS involvement is suspected in PTCL. For patients with PTCL without CNS involvement, lumbar puncture for CSF analysis or CNS prophylaxis is generally unnecessary; however, no data on CSF IL‐10 levels in this subgroup have been reported. Consistent with findings in patients with diffuse large B‐cell lymphoma (DLBCL), our data showed that CSF IL‐10 levels were normal in patients with PTCL without CNS involvement.

Although IL‐10 elevation in CNS lymphoma has been confirmed, its exact role remains unclear. IL‐10, produced by Th2 cells, monocytes, macrophages, and B cells, plays a key role in regulating inflammation and immune responses. The “seed and soil” hypothesis suggests that macrophages in the tumor microenvironment shift from a pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype to an anti‐inflammatory M2 phenotype, which supports tumor cell proliferation and survival. 12 M2 macrophages secrete immune‐suppressive factors such as IL‐10, which inhibit immune cell activity and promote tumor cell immune evasion. Additionally, IL‐10 enhances the migration and accumulation of M2 macrophages, further amplifying the immunosuppressive environment. Therefore, the elevated IL‐10 levels observed in DLBCL and PTCL are likely derived from tumor‐associated macrophages (a high M2/M1 ratio status), rather than from the tumor cells themselves. However, the precise mechanism by which IL‐10 contributes to this process remains unclear. Clinically, measuring CSF IL‐10 levels offers a minimally invasive and rapid diagnostic tool, especially when brain biopsy is not feasible, such as in older or frail patients or when lesions are in deep brain regions. This approach reduces biopsy risks and aids personalized treatment. While malignant gliomas often require surgery, CNS lymphomas are managed with chemo‐radiotherapy. 13 In cases where brain biopsy is not possible, CSF IL‐10 levels and the IL‐10/IL‐6 ratio, combined with imaging and CSF flow cytometry, can help assess CNS involvement. However, reliance on CSF IL‐10 and IL‐10/IL‐6 ratios carries a risk of misdiagnosis due to potential false positives from immune or inflammatory factors. Large‐scale trials are needed to validate their clinical utility.

Owing to the low incidence of SCNSL, data are insufficient to establish a specific standard treatment regimen for SCNSL in PTCL, and conventional chemotherapy is limited in efficacy. Current treatment strategies typically use regimens similar to those for PCNSL, such as HD‐MTX‐based induction therapy. Several clinical studies on PCNSL have shown that combining HD‐MTX with radiation significantly improves OS, with a median survival of 30–60 months, compared with whole‐brain radiation therapy (WBRT) alone. 14 , 15 However, WBRT‐associated neurotoxicity, particularly in older patients, remains challenging. To reduce neurotoxicity, a phase II study with reduced‐dose WBRT (23.4 Gy) showed an ORR of 95% and a median OS of 6.6 years. 16 These results provide an effective treatment option for PCNSL. Thus, for patients with T‐cell SCNSL in good physical condition, an HD‐MTX‐based regimen followed by reduced‐dose WBRT is a feasible option; however, further research is needed to determine the optimal approach.

CONCLUSION

Our report found significantly elevated IL‐10 levels in CNS infiltration of PTCL. To our knowledge, this is the first report of such findings. Although only two cases were reported, these findings still suggest the importance of CSF IL‐10 in diagnosing central invasion in T‐cell lymphoma. Further validation of these findings by larger clinical studies is required. Furthermore, the role of IL‐10 in the pathogenesis and surveillance of central invasion in B‐cell and T‐cell lymphoma should be investigated.
