Analysis of FDA-Approved Artificial Intelligence and Machine Learning-Enabled Cardiovascular Devices
Authors: Partha Sardar, Elena Dudorova, Saurav Chatterjee, Sahil A. Parikh, Emmanouil S. Brilakis, Jagmeet P. Singh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical Abstract

The U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has cleared an increasing number of artificial intelligence and machine learning (AI/ML)-based medical devices for various health care applications.1, 2, 3 Regulatory approval pathways offer key insights into clinical validation, oversight, and intended use of AI/ML devices, helping clinicians assess their safety, trustworthiness, and real-world applicability in cardiovascular (CV) care. As AI/ML devices are increasingly deployed in clinical settings, it is crucial for end users to assess their applicability within their unique clinical environments.2,3 We conducted an analysis of FDA-approved AI/ML-enabled CV devices and algorithms to examine their characteristics, approval pathways, sociodemographic representation, and clinical applications.What is the clinical question being addressed?What are the characteristics, approval pathways, and clinical applications of FDA-approved AI/ML-enabled cardiovascular devices?What is the main finding?Most cardiovascular AI devices were approved through the 510(k) pathway, with approvals rising notably after 2015. What is the clinical question being addressed?

What are the characteristics, approval pathways, and clinical applications of FDA-approved AI/ML-enabled cardiovascular devices?

What is the main finding?

Most cardiovascular AI devices were approved through the 510(k) pathway, with approvals rising notably after 2015. However, demographic reporting and supporting clinical or prospective studies remained limited. The majority of submissions originated from North America (61.0%), with the most common use case being electrocardiography-based arrhythmia detection (21.1%).

Methods

We reviewed publicly available FDA approval letters for AI/ML-enabled CV devices compiled by the agency through December 2024. The AI/ML-enabled medical device list provides direct access to the FDA’s database entries for each listed medical device incorporating AI/ML. Inclusion criteria encompassed AI-enabled products with potential applications in CV diseases. For all eligible AI/ML-based medical devices, we collected the following characteristics: device name, features, intended use, approval pathway, and other relevant information. For this analysis, each approved device was considered a separate entity, and we examined device characteristics, intended-use patterns, and performed basic descriptive statistics on aggregated data. This study was determined to be exempt by the Institutional Review Board.

Results

A total of 1,016 AI-enabled products were reviewed by the FDA from 1995 to 2024. Among them, 209 were CV devices, with 106 (50.7%) classified as CV and the rest under radiology but intended for CV use. A total of 166 devices were unique, with 43 being updated versions, often for different indications. Several AI tools classified under radiology are designed for CV use—for instance, software that measures ventricular structure and function using modalities such as ultrasound, computed tomography, or magnetic resonance imaging. A total of 161 devices were software-only tools, whereas 48 were hardware-based—integrated into physical platforms such as stethoscopes or imaging systems.

The number of approvals sharply increased since the mid-2010s, with 85% of devices approved in the last 6 years (Figure 1A). Most devices (200, 95.7%) received 510(k) clearance, whereas 9 (4.3%) were approved via the de novo pathway. The most frequent applications included electrocardiography-based arrhythmia detection (21.1%), echocardiography (16.7%), coronary artery disease (CAD) detection and evaluation (16.7%), and hemodynamics and vital signs monitoring (15.8%) (Figure 1B). Arrhythmia detection tools saw their highest approval rates in 2023 (17.8%) and 2022 (15.6%). Hemodynamic monitoring tools peaked in approvals in 2021 (21.9%) and 2019 (18.8%). For echocardiography-based technologies, the leading approval years were 2024 (28.6%) and 2020 (22.9%).

The subspecialty distribution of approved devices was as follows: cardiovascular imaging (25.4%), followed by electrophysiology (24.4%), CAD detection and intervention (16.2%), general cardiology (15.7%), structural and endovascular interventions (13.8%), and heart failure (4.3%). In general cardiology, approved tools include risk prediction models and AI-based murmur analysis. CAD applications feature fractional flow reserve and ischemia detection. Heart failure tools focus on predicting exacerbations and detecting diastolic dysfunction or pulmonary hypertension.

The majority of device requests originated from North America (61.0%), followed by Asia (20.6%) and Europe (16.7%). However, reporting of demographic characteristics was inconsistent. Only 10.0% of approvals reported race/ethnicity, 76.6% did not report the age of study participants, and 78.5% did not report the gender of study participants. In addition, only 56.5% provided comprehensive, detailed performance study results, 9.0% included results from prospective studies, and 5.7% reported clinical trial data supporting the approved device.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrate a rapid increase in FDA-approved AI/ML-enabled medical devices since the mid-2010s, with most cleared via the 510(k) pathway. Given the relatively lenient regulatory process, 510(k) clearance remains the most common approval pathway, accounting for approximately 96.0% of medical devices cleared in the United States.4

Cardiovascular imaging was the most common subspecialty, with applications spanning echocardiography, CAD, and vascular disease detection, followed by electrophysiology. This likely reflects the reliance on digital data—such as imaging and electrocardiograms—and the importance of pattern recognition in cardiovascular care.

However, the availability of clinical performance data was limited. More extensive clinical data would be beneficial for broader application in real-world clinical settings. Despite the growing number of market-approved medical devices, our analysis highlights inconsistent FDA reporting practices, with demographic and socioeconomic characteristics often under-reported. This lack of data may exacerbate the risk of algorithmic bias and health disparities.2,3

Previous studies have shown low rates of demographic reporting, particularly in radiology-focused analyses.2,4 Our specialty-specific analysis found limited reporting of race/ethnicity and clinical trial data—an issue of concern in clinical fields like cardiology, where robust demographic and trial data are essential for guiding decision-making.

A major limitation of this study is that it relied on publicly available FDA approval letters for medical devices classified as “AI/ML-enabled” by the agency. Many additional products may be in use but do not meet the FDA’s definition of a medical device and thus do not require regulatory review. In addition, FDA summaries are not always comprehensive.

The rapid rise in FDA-approved AI/ML cardiovascular devices is encouraging. To improve patient care and expand use, more clinical performance data and clearer demographic reporting are needed. Continued evaluation of their safety, efficacy, and real-world impact will be crucial as these technologies become more deeply integrated into cardiovascular care.
