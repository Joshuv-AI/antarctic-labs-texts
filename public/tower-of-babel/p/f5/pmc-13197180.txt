A critical evaluation of generative query expansion on biomedical literature retrieval
Authors: Yilu Fang, Gongbo Zhang, Fangyi Chen, Yifan Peng, Chunhua Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the effectiveness of generative query expansion for biomedical literature retrieval.Materials and MethodsWe thoroughly examined eight generative query expansion methods using three large language models across five datasets for biomedical literature retrieval. We further performed a quantitative analysis, including performance comparisons, rank transition analysis, and article-type effect analysis. We also conducted a qualitative examination of representative cases, from which we derived an error taxonomy.ResultsOn BioASQ-Y/N, GPT-4o-based query expansion shifts Recall@10 to 0.417-0.512 and nDCG@10 to 0.358-0.479, relative to a baseline of 0.491 and 0.456. For PubMedQA, Precision@1 ranges from 0.764 to 0.876 and nDCG@10 from 0.847 to 0.931, compared with baseline values of 0.893 and 0.935. For 2019-Trec-PM, query expansion yields Recall@100 of 0.217-0.256 and nDCG@100 of 0.272-0.312, versus a baseline of 0.227 and 0.274. Similarly, for 2018-TREC-PM, Recall@100 spans 0.169-0.227 and nDCG@100 spans 0.195-0.250, relative to baseline scores of 0.164 and 0.191. For 2017-TREC-PM, Recall@100 and nDCG@100 fall within 0.111-0.139 and 0.154-0.191 under query expansion, compared with baseline metrics of 0.102 and 0.147. Both general-purpose and domain-specific Llama-based models demonstrate similar performance to GPT-4o.Discussion and ConclusionThe impact of query expansion varies significantly by the expansion methods and type of evidence, but is relatively agnostic to backbone model choice. Notably, query expansion primarily affects article ranking but has a limited impact on the screening stage. Our findings underscore the unique challenges of biomedical literature retrieval and highlight the need to develop domain-specific information retrieval techniques.

Introduction

An effective biomedical literature search is paramount for stakeholders (eg researchers, clinicians, patients, and decision-makers) seeking accurate and relevant information in a limited time. This process is key to systematic reviews and biomedical question-answering (QA). By retrieving relevant studies and clinical trials, stakeholders can conduct thorough, unbiased, and reliable systematic reviews, enable comprehensive evidence synthesis, and identify research gaps.1 Such rigorous analysis informs evidence-based clinical practice and shapes future research directions, ultimately advancing healthcare outcomes and scientific understanding. Additionally, an effective literature search has recently enhanced biomedical QA, which aims to provide accurate, evidence-based answers to healthcare and biological science questions. Incorporating relevant articles from biomedical literature into queries ensures that responses are grounded in verified scientific literature, thereby improving the answer quality and relevance.2

A line of research has focused on developing various dense retrieval methods (eg Log-Augmented DEnse Retrieval (LADER),3 bioMedical Contrastive Pre-trained Transformers (MedCPT),4 BMRERETRIEVER5) to improve biomedical literature search, leveraging learned representations to better capture semantic similarity. Another direction for enhancing retrieval effectiveness is query expansion. It has been widely adopted to boost performance in general-domain information retrieval (IR) by broadening the scope of queries,6,7 which demonstrate improvements on both sparse and dense retrieval systems.

Applying query expansion in the biomedical domain, however, presents unique challenges. (a) The complex and specialized nature of biomedical terminologies.8 A single medical term might be presented by multiple synonyms. For instance, the terms ‘heart attack’ and ‘myocardial infarction’ both refer to the same condition. (b) The rapid evolution of medical language.9,10 Expression of the same concept can evolve over time. For example, earlier publications might use ‘end-stage renal failure’ and ‘end-stage renal disease’, while more recent literature favors ‘end-stage kidney disease’ and ‘stage 5 chronic kidney disease’.11 (c) The contextual nuances that can affect semantic interpretation.12 Polysemy, where words have varying meanings based on context, poses challenges for understanding semantics. The abbreviation ‘AD,’ for example, can stand for ‘Alzheimer’s disease’ or ‘atopic dermatitis,’ depending on the context. To combat the above issues, conventional query expansion techniques have been developed, including pseudo-relevance feedback (PRF), biomedical ontologies, semantic word embeddings, and concept-based expansions from curated domain-specific databases.13–17 However, these approaches face well-known limitations, including noise from irrelevant or overly broad expansion terms, dependency on the coverage and quality of external resources, and manual or heuristic parameter tuning. These challenges highlight the need for more adaptive, context-aware approaches to generate precise and meaningful expansions aligned with the true information need.

The emergence of Large Language Models (LLMs) offers new hope. Various generative query expansion techniques that leverage the extensive parametric knowledge encoded in LLMs have been developed for IR across general domains.18–23 Query expansion methods using LLMs can be broadly grouped into three categories: prompting, supervised fine-tuning, and reinforcement learning.24 Prompting strategies to expand queries include zero-shot prompting (eg, Hypothetical Document Embeddings (HyDE),18 Large language model as Retriever (LameR),22 Mutual Verification method with Large Language model (MILL),19 Generative Relevance Feedback (GRF),21 Corpus-Steered Query Expansion (CSQE),20 few-shot prompting (eg query2doc (Q2D)23), and chain-of-thought (COT).25 Wang et al.26 proposed TrialMind for clinical evidence synthesis, which includes a literature search component with LLM-driven query expansion.

Despite progress in query expansion in general domain IR, generative query expansion techniques remain largely under-researched in the biomedical domain, a representative knowledge-intensive field. To bridge this gap, we conducted a systematic evaluation of eight generative query expansion methods using three LLMs across five biomedical information retrieval datasets. Problem formulation

The generative query expansion problem is formulated as follows: given a query q, the goal is to generate an expanded query q+ by adding additional terms generated by an LLM. These terms are intended to enhance the retrieval of relevant biomedical articles. To achieve this, we employed the technique utilized by Jagerman et al.27 and Wang et al.,23 in which the original query is repeated five times. The prompt formats for these methods are provided in Supplementary Figure S1. Each method was implemented using three LLMs: GPT-4o, Llama-3.1-8b, and Med42-v2-8b.28–30 GPT-4o is a closed-source, general-purpose LLM.28 Llama-3.1-8b is an open-source, general-purpose LLM.29 Med42-v2-8b is an open-source LLM built on the Llama-3 architecture and finetuned on specialized clinical data.30 Together, these models allow us to assess the performance consistency of generative query expansion across both general-purpose and domain-specific LLMs, as well as across closed- and open- source settings. They were assessed on BM25, a sparse retrieval method that ranks articles based on term frequency and inverse document frequency.31

Corpus and datasets

The retrieval corpus contained around 36 million articles from PubMed,4 a widely used and large-scale resource supporting biomedical and life sciences literature search and retrieval.1 The evaluation was conducted using five datasets, including BioASQ-Y/N, PubMedQA*, 2019 TREC-PM, 2018 TREC-PM, and 2017 TREC-PM. For each dataset, we applied a consistent sampling and filtering protocol. Specifically, we selected 5% of the questions and their corresponding target articles as the demonstration set for few-shot learning. We filtered the remaining questions to retain only those with at least one target article in the corpus.

Experimental settings

We used the BM25 implementation provided by Pyserini39 with default parameter settings.

All experiments used the prompt templates described in Supplementary Figure S1. For the few-shot setting, four in-context examples26 were randomly selected from the Demonstration set for the BioASQ-Y/N and PubMedQA* datasets and included in the prompt. For the PM datasets, since there were only 1 or 2 queries in the demonstration set, we considered all of them as the in-context examples.In Q2D, we treated the target articles of a query as its passage. In Q2K, we treated the article’s Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms as its keywords. Given the large number of target articles in the PM datasets, we randomly selected five target articles for each question to remain within the context-window constraint of the LLM. For PRF, we constructed the expansion context using the top three documents retrieved by BM25.

For GPT-4o, the maximum output length was set to 512 tokens, the temperature to 0.1, and the top-p value to 1, with a single response generated per prompt. For Llama-3.1, we used the 8B-parameter version (Ollama identifier: llama3.1:8b). For Med42-v2, we used the Llama3-Med42-8B model (Ollama identifier: thewindmom/llama3-med42-8b), both served locally. For both models, the maximum output length was set to 512 tokens and the temperature to 0.1.

The evaluation metrics include Precision@k, Recall@k, and normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain (nDCG@k) (k∈{1,5,10,15,50,100,1000}). Precision@k and Recall@k do not distinguish whether relevant articles appear early or late within the top-k results, whereas nDCG@k is a rank-aware metric that explicitly rewards relevant articles appearing at higher ranks. Figure 2 presents the metric interpretation and an illustrative example.

Interpretation and an illustrative example of retrieval evaluation metrics. Rank-aware metrics (nDCG@k and MRR@k) capture differences in ranking quality when relevant articles appear at different positions within the top-k results. nDCG: normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain. MRR: Mean Reciprocal Rank.

To ensure the correctness of our reimplementation of the query expansion methods, we conducted a sanity check using the MS MARCO passage ranking dataset40 and compared the results with those from previous studies.22,26 We employed both GPT-4o and Flan-T5-XXL models for query expansion and BM25 for retrieval. Jagerman et al.26 utilized BM25 implemented in Terrier.41 Since BM25 implementations can vary across toolkits and we employed BM25 in Pyserini, we adopted the optimal hyperparameter settings through a grid search (k1 = 0.82, b = 0.68),39 ensuring a fair and accurate comparison. Additionally, we included Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR@10) as an extra evaluation metric.

Results

Main results

We evaluated eight generative query expansion methods across five datasets. BioASQ-Y/N includes a small number of targets per query (median = 3, mean = 5.73, SD = 7.43; N = 586 queries). PubMedQA* contains one relevant document per query (N = 475 queries). The remaining three datasets (ie 2017-2019 TREC PM datasets) all contain large target sets per query and exhibit substantial variability (mean ± SD = 113.48 ± 107.31, 99.9 ± 70.33, and 134.71 ± 116.87, respectively), but have relatively small query sets (N = 29, 48, 38). Since each dataset has a distinct size of target set per query, we customized the primary evaluation metrics per dataset as follows: Recall@10 and nDCG@10 for BioASQ-Y/N, Precision@1 and nDCG@10 for PubMedQA*, and Recall@100 and nDCG@100 for 2017-2019 TREC PM datasets.

Table 2 summarizes the main results of eight generative query expansion methods evaluated with three LLMs across all five datasets using their respective primary metrics. Complete results for all additional metrics are provided in Supplementary Table S1. The impact of generative query expansion on biomedical literature retrieval varied substantially across datasets. Almost all methods yielded consistent improvements on the large-target datasets (ie 2017-2019 TREC PM datasets). In contrast, few methods produced gains on the single-target PubMedQA*, and only some improved the small-target BioASQ-Y/N. The impact of generative query expansion also varied considerably across expansion methods. Q2D_PRF and COT_PRF showed more consistent improvements across different LLMs and datasets, exhibiting robust overall performance, even though they were not always the top-performing method for every dataset.

Validation of the experimental setup

To confirm the validity of our implementation process for query expansion techniques, we reproduced the results on the MS MARCO passage ranking dataset40 reported by Jagerman et al.26 and Wang et al.22 The reproduced values were closely aligned with prior findings (Supplementary Table S2), validating our setup.

Rank transitions and quantitative effects of generative query expansion

As illustrated in Figure 3, the rank transitions for each query-target pair varied substantially across the five datasets.

Rank transition distributions across the five datasets and three large language models. Each stacked bar shows the percentage of relevant articles that improved, degraded, remained stable within the top 1000, or remained unretrieved after query expansion.

BioASQ-Y/N showed extensive reshuffling, with a larger proportion of relevant documents moving downward than upward in rank. On average across eight expansion methods and three LLMs, 38.26% of query-target pairs showed an improved rank, 44.94% showed a worse rank, 7.08% remained unretrieved, and 9.71% remained stable within the top 1000. This reflects its small but highly variable target set.

The single-target dataset PubMedQA* exhibited almost no rank movement, with a slight tendency toward decreases (on average: 82.77% remaining stable within the top 1000, 12.62% decrease, 4.13% increase, and 0.47% unretrieved). This minimal movement is consistent with the negligible or slightly negative performance change observed from this dataset.

The large-target datasets (2017-2019 TREC PM datasets), on the other hand, displayed similar and stable patterns: many relevant articles moved upward, relatively few moved downward, and a substantial portion remained beyond rank 1000 even after expansion. On average, the 2017 TREC PM dataset showed 28.83% of relevant articles moved upward, 18.68% moved downward, 51.96% remained unretrieved, and 0.53% remained stable within the top 1000; 2018 TREC PM dataset showed 34.46%, 21.57%, 43.25%, and 0.72%; and 2019 TREC PM dataset showed 31.57%, 28.40%, 39.31%, and 0.72%. This indicates that query expansion mainly reshuffles documents already within or near the retrievable region. These transition profiles help explain the metric changes observed in the main results, particularly the gains in nDCG@100 and Recall@100, and the modest improvements in Recall@1000.

We further analyzed the article types associated with query-target pairs exhibiting large rank transitions after query expansion (≥40-fold changes) across all datasets, LLMs, and expansion methods. The article type information was retrieved from PubMed metadata in the ‘PublicationType’ field, and one article may have multiple publication types. For example, the article with PMID 19934290 has publication types: ‘Journal Article,’ ‘Multicenter Study,’ ‘Randomized Controlled Trial,’ ‘Research Support, N.I.H., Extramural.’ We excluded broad publication categories, including ‘Clinical Conference’ and ‘Journal Article’, as well as those related to research funding support, such as ‘Research Support, N.I.H., Extramural’, from our analysis. In total, the investigated cases covered 31 publication types. The statistics for these article types are presented in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4. We observed that large rank degradations occurred primarily in case reports and review articles, whereas large rank improvements were also concentrated in review articles.

Qualitative analysis of failure modes in generative query expansion

To better understand the sources of performance degradation observed in the quantitative analysis, we conducted a qualitative examination of error cases. Although rank decreases occurred across multiple datasets, they were frequent in BioASQ-Y/N. We therefore selected the best-performing expansion method, Q2D_PRF, under GPT-4o and analyzed its rank-decrease and unretrieved cases in BioASQ-Y/N as representative examples.

Specifically, we randomly selected 10% of query-target pairs (n = 21) that were unretrieved (ie ranked >1000) before and after expansion. We also selected pairs (n = 38) where the rank declined by more than 40-fold after expansion. Using the Critical Incident Technique,42 we systematically analyzed these 59 extreme cases and identified six key reasons for failure (Table 3). We found that more than one reason applied to multiple cases. We further present representative examples of one successful expansion and six failure modes in Supplementary Figure S3.

Query-article topical misalignment

Query expansion aims to reinforce the primary topic of interest. Therefore, when the articles (n = 29 (49.2%)) did not prioritize the topic in their main discourse, the query expansion method exacerbated the ranking issue.

We found that the target article might have a broad scope, with the question topic only a minor aspect rather than the central focus. For instance, an article discussing the question ‘Is liraglutide effective for weight reduction?’ reviews the broader topic of the long-term efficacy and safety of various anti-obesity drugs, while the drug of interest, ie liraglutide, is only one of many discussed.

Furthermore, the articles could have an entirely different primary scope, with the relevant topic serving merely as background information rather than making it a key subject of discussion. For example, an article addressing the question ‘Is collagen the most abundant human protein?’ primarily focuses on a gelatin detection method, and the evidence that answers the question appears merely as background information.

Query-Article analytical dimension misalignment

Augmented queries might incorporate an analysis of questions from a dimension distinct from that of the articles (n = 17 (28.8%)). Variations in terminology across different analytical dimensions could influence the retriever’s ranking process, potentially prioritizing articles within the same or similar dimensions while deprioritizing those from other dimensions. For example, an article for the question ‘Is treatment with Bacillus Calmette-Guerin used for bladder cancer?’ presents an experimental animal study investigating the protective effects of Bacillus Calmette-Guerin immunotherapy in bladder cancer. In contrast, the augmented query focuses on a different aspect, offering a clinical and mechanistic view of its efficacy.

Lack of rich details in articles

Articles (n = 9 (15.3%)) might lack sufficient details on the topic of the question. This limitation became more pronounced when queries were augmented with additional information. For example, the article with PMID 964994 for the question ‘Is there a genetic cause of craniostenosis?’ is a short case study with only two sentences and offers little supporting information.

Lack of direct evidence in articles and inference incompleteness

In this category, target articles (n = 13 (22.0%)) did not explicitly provide a direct answer to the query and instead required multi-hop reasoning to conclude. While augmented queries might introduce inferential cues, they might still fail to incorporate all necessary reasoning steps for the complete multi-hop inference. For example, an article addressing the question ‘Are interferons defensive proteins?’ provides indirect evidence through the role of an interferon-induced GTP-binding protein in antiviral immunity, but lacks the connection needed to link the specific protein back to the broader function of interferons.

Query-Article studied population or intervention instance mismatch

Augmented queries might highlight specific populations or instances of an intervention based on the retrieved information, but the target articles (n = 4 (6.8%)) might mainly concentrate on different populations or intervention instances. For example, for the question ‘Is Hunter’s disease associated with the X chromosome?’, the target article focuses on adults with type 2 diabetes, whereas the augmented information was generated from case studies of female patients. Another example is an article addressing the question, ‘Are any medications available to prevent COVID-19 following exposure?’ It discusses the role of ivermectin in prevention. However, the augmented information focused on hydroxychloroquine, another potential instance of post-exposure prophylactic medication.

Query-Article acronym and synonym variation

Here, the query and target article (n = 1 (1.7%)) might share conceptual similarities but differed in lexical representation due to the use of acronyms and synonyms. Suppose the query expansion did not incorporate them, perhaps because the retrieved articles used to generate the augmented information lacked them as well. In that case, the augmented query failed to bridge this lexical gap. For example, an article for the question ‘Is MIS-C or Multisystem Inflammatory syndrome in children a complication of Covid-19?’ only mentions ‘Coronavirus 2019.’ Since augmented information was generated from studies that exclusively used ‘Covid-19’, it failed to include ‘Coronavirus 2019’ in the augmented query.

The impact of hit target articles

Our main result showed that Q2D_PRF and COT_PRF exhibited more consistent improvements across various LLMs and datasets. In these two query expansion methods, the top three articles from the initial retrieval were used as context to generate augmented query information. Here, we further explored whether the number of hit target articles would influence retrieval using Q2D_PRF implemented with GPT-4o on BioASQ-Y/N as a representative case. Out of 586 questions analyzed, 250 (42.6%) had more than three target articles, accounting for 2,834 (84.4%) query-target pairs. In these pairs, we observed a negative trend between the number of hit target articles and the proportion of pairs with improved ranks post-expansion (Figure 4). Specifically, the proportion of pairs with improved rankings dropped from 44.1% in the group with no hit target articles to 34.3% in the group with three hit target articles initially. In contrast, those with ranking declines rose from 42.3% to 50.8%. This indicates that augmented information, even when derived directly from a subset of target articles, may not always enhance retrieval effectiveness.

We further examined this phenomenon by analyzing query-target pairs (n = 12) that experienced a rank drop after expansion but initially received three hits. We observed the phenomenon of query-article analytical dimension misalignment, caused by the fact that a biomedical question can be analyzed from various dimensions, with key evidence scattered across studies spanning fundamental biological mechanisms, translational research, clinical investigation, and real-world clinical applications. Consequently, augmented information generated from a subset of target articles at specific research stages might not effectively help retrieve target articles from other stages.

Discussion

In this study, we tested whether prompt-based generative query expansion methods that succeeded in the general domain also enhance knowledge-intensive biomedical literature search. Our results demonstrate that the impact of generative query expansion is strongly shaped by the size of the target set, the article type, and the design of the expansion method, while being relatively agnostic to the LLM used. Additionally, our quantitative analysis highlights that generative query expansion predominantly enhances ranking but has a limited impact on deep-tail retrieval. Our qualitative analysis further reveals six failure modes, and the resulting error taxonomy offers a structured foundation for future improvements in expansion strategies. Our key insights of generative query expansion for biomedical literature retrieval are summarized in Figure 5.

Summary of key insights of generative query expansion for biomedical literature retrieval.

Among all expansion strategies evaluated, PRF-based methods demonstrate relatively greater robustness and consistency in performance gains across datasets and LLMs. PRF-guided expansion improves retrieval by augmenting the original query with evidence-aligned terms extracted from top-ranked documents, such as outcome variables, biomarkers, and language related to statistical associations. For example, the query ‘Is Iron deficiency anemia a common complication of chronic kidney disease?’ was expanded with information ‘…Iron deficiency anemia is indeed a common complication of chronic kidney disease (CKD) … The diagnosis and management are challenging due to the limited predictive ability of routine serum iron markers like ferritin and transferrin saturation…’ Such expansion shifted the query embedding toward the background introduction of the target article (PMID: 25468387), thereby improving rankings. In contrast, expansion based on other strategies, such as ‘… patients with CKD frequently experience iron deficiency due to poor dietary intake, impaired absorption, and increased blood loss …,’ drifted toward a mechanistic explanation. As a result, despite being clinically correct, such expanded queries redirected the query toward review and educational literature clusters and away from the target evidence synthesis, thereby exemplifying the Query-Article Analytical Dimension Misalignment identified in our error taxonomy. Given the relatively consistent performance, PRF-guided expansion warrants further investigation to improve biomedical literature retrieval.

The analysis of article types provides additional insights into the heterogeneous effects of generative query expansion. Query expansion could worsen the retrieval of case reports. Since case reports typically describe unusual observations or novel occurrences in individual patients,43,44 their topical scope is narrow and highly specific. Expansion terms, however, often amplify broader and more generalizable concepts, shifting the semantic focus of the query toward population-level evidence. This mismatch can cause single-patient case reports to appear less relevant under the expanded query, leading to rank degradations. This phenomenon exemplifies the Query-Article Analytical Dimension Misalignment identified in our error taxonomy. Conversely, query expansion appears to enhance the retrieval of review articles. Review articles synthesize existing evidence, offering broad overviews of a topic and drawing terminology from multiple related studies.45 Their content thus tends to align well with the terms introduced during expansion, thereby improving their ranking. However, review articles may also encounter the issue of Query-Article Analytical Dimension Misalignment, which causes the rank degradation we observed. These observations imply that effective query expansion should be responsive to differences in article types and tailored to the specific forms of evidence being targeted.

The value of generative query expansion primarily lies in its ability to refine ranking among documents already within the retrievable range rather than in surfacing deeply buried relevant articles. The capacity distinction between ranking refinement and retrieval coverage has important implications for real-world biomedical search workflows. In settings such as clinical question answering, improving the ordering of the retrievable articles can meaningfully reduce human review time. However, tasks that require comprehensive coverage, such as the screening stage of systematic reviews and evidence mapping, depend heavily on the ability to retrieve relevant articles that reside deep in the ranking tail. Our findings suggest that generative query expansion alone is insufficient for such applications and will likely need to be paired with retrieval approaches that expand the candidate set.

We propose a taxonomy to facilitate error diagnosis in biomedical information retrieval by decomposing query expansion failures into six actionable categories. Each category motivates a distinct algorithmic strategy to improve alignment between the query and the target evidence source. For example, topical correspondence between the query and target evidence can be strengthened through PRF-guided expansion. Analytical dimension misalignment motivates expansion that accounts for study design and types, as well as for the different research stages from biological studies to clinical applications. Population and intervention mismatches call for demographic or treatment-constrained filtering of retrieval results, whereas acronym and synonym variation prompts controlled-vocabulary normalization. Errors due to insufficient detail and indirect evidence motivate the use of multi-hop aggregation strategies. Collectively, this taxonomy transforms qualitative retrieval failures into clear, diagnosable system signals, enabling targeted optimization of biomedical knowledge retrieval.

One limitation of our study is that we did not include expansion methods that incorporate external knowledge sources or employ iterative reasoning. This analysis focuses on one-iteration prompt-based query expansion methods using LLMs for better error attribution. Our primary analysis focuses on sparse retrieval to support interpretability. We did not systematically analyze the impact of generative query expansion across dense or hybrid retrieval paradigms. As a limited additional experiment, we evaluated a representative dense retriever (MedCPT retriever) on the BioASQ-Y/N dataset and observed consistent improvement in retrieval for the best-performing expansion method among the evaluated methods (Q2D_PRF) using GPT-4o. Compared to the baseline, Recall@10 increased from 0.327 to 0.366, and nDCG@10 increased from 0.260 to 0.319 (see Supplementary Table S5 for additional metrics). However, a comprehensive evaluation of dense and hybrid retrieval models remains an important direction for future work. We evaluated both open- and closed-source LLMs, spanning general-purpose and domain-specific models. However, our study does not exhaustively cover the full range of biomedical LLMs or model scales. Nonetheless, our findings underscore the unique challenges of biomedical literature retrieval and call for future efforts to develop and evaluate domain-specific information retrieval techniques. A future research direction is to develop a multi-perspective retrieval framework with knowledge-based indexing to enhance the depth, relevance, and contextual diversity of biomedical literature searches.

Conclusion

In summary, we systematically evaluated the effectiveness of LLM-based generative query expansion for biomedical literature retrieval, a domain characterized by rapidly evolving terminology and high contextual ambiguity. Across eight expansion methods, we observe substantial variability in impact, with PRF-guided approaches yielding relatively consistent, moderate improvements. Through large-scale error analysis, we identify six dominant failure modes, including query-article topical misalignment, analytical dimension misalignment, studied population or intervention mismatch, acronym and synonym variation, lack of rich article details, and lack of direct evidence. We show that biomedical retrieval errors frequently arise from both semantic and evidence misalignment. Our findings highlight the distinctive challenges of biomedical information retrieval, where evidence is often fragmented across multiple stages of biological and clinical research, and underscore the need for domain-specific retrieval strategies that account for study design and types, as well as research stages. While our evaluation primarily focuses on biomedical literature, the identified failure modes and the query expansion analysis are broadly applicable to other knowledge-intensive domains with rapidly evolving terminologies and heterogeneous evidence sources, such as legal information retrieval, financial intelligence, public policy analysis, and cybersecurity. By proposing point-by-point strategies to address the limitations of current IR techniques, our study enhances the robustness of retrieval systems that underpin core biomedical informatics tasks such as evidence synthesis, clinical decision support, and translational research.
