Managing medications together: a qualitative study of collaboration between primary and secondary caregivers of older adults
Authors: Te-Lien Ku, Laura Block, James H Ford II, Shu-Ying Wu, Yen-Ming Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedication management is among the most complex tasks faced by dependent older adults and requires substantial physical and cognitive effort from their family caregivers. Although prior research has described the roles of primary caregivers (PCGs) and secondary caregivers (SCGs) in caring for older adults, limited attention has been paid to how these caregivers collaborate in daily caregiving activities. This study aimed to explore PCGs’ experiences of collaborating with SCGs in managing medications for older adults and to understand the contextual factors that shape this collaboration.MethodsUsing a qualitative descriptive design, this study analyzed 28 semi-structured interview transcripts from PCGs. A combination of deductive and inductive content analysis was used to identify medication management activities performed by PCGs and SCGs, as well as the factors shaping their collaboration. The findings were then integrated to categorize and characterize distinct collaboration styles for medication management.ResultsPrimary caregivers were predominantly adult children or children-in-law, who reported collaborating with SCGs including family members, paid home care aides, or both. Primary caregivers primarily handled medication management activities requiring higher cognitive effort, whereas SCGs often assisted with activities requiring greater physical effort and co-presence with care recipients. Two main categories emerged as shaping collaboration in medication management: (1) conferring decision-making authority and (2) positioning the SCG as an active or passive supporter. Contextual factors influencing collaboration were largely caregiver-related and included kinship ties between PCGs and SCGs, caregivers’ knowledge of the care recipient’s medications, geographic proximity to the care recipient, marital status, and employment status. Four distinct styles of collaboration between PCGs and SCGs were identified and categorized as Partnering, Operating, Bench-Playing, and Intervening.ConclusionsThis study provides valuable insights into how multiple family caregivers collaborate to manage medications for older adults, highlighting differences in task responsibility allocation and degrees of collaboration. Future research should systematically investigate the social and demographic factors shaping these collaborative strategies to inform the development of tailored interventions that better support caregivers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07696-0.Keywords: Collaboration, Medication management, Older adult, Primary caregiver, Secondary caregiver

Background

Nearly all older adults take at least one prescribed medication daily [1, 2], and approximately half take between 5 and 10 medications each day [3–6]. As medication regimens become increasingly complex, many older adults experience challenges in managing them due to age-related declines in memory, executive function, and physical ability [7, 8]. These difficulties can result in inappropriate medication use, nonadherence, medication errors, and adverse drug events, which in turn elevate the risk of emergency department visits and hospitalizations [9–12].

To promote safe and effective medication use, many older adults depend on family members, life partners, friends, or paid home care aides, collectively referred to as family caregivers, who often assume responsibility for medication management [13]. Medication management encompasses a wide range of physical and cognitive tasks and is recognized as a complex, multidimensional process [14–16]. Given this complexity, medication management often involves multiple caregivers: a primary caregiver (PCG) who assumes most of the responsibility and one or more secondary caregivers (SCGs) who provide less frequent or less intensive caregiving support [17–20]. Despite assistance from SCGs, PCGs often encounter challenges in collaborating with them, particularly when it comes to task coordination and communication of care plans [20, 21].

Previous studies have underscored the role of SCG networks in supporting PCGs through direct assistance with care tasks, emotional support, respite provision, and help with household duties [22–24]. As sources of social support, SCGs can help balance caregiving demands, reduce burden, and enhance the coping capacity of PCGs [17, 25, 26]. More recent research has explored collaboration dynamics among multiple caregivers, focusing on access to support networks, coordination of caregiving activities, and contextual factors that shape caregiver work systems [27–30]. Collaboration is further influenced by caregivers’ goals and the nature of task demands, which affect engagement and outcomes such as role satisfaction [31].

Although these studies have advanced understanding of SCG networks and the factors shaping their involvement, critical gaps remain in understanding how PCGs navigate and adapt to these collaborative dynamics in everyday practice, particularly in sharing and coordinating medication management tasks. Prior research has largely emphasized communication between caregivers and care recipients, as well as between caregivers and healthcare professionals, demonstrating its benefits in optimizing medication use and reducing harms [15, 32, 33]. However, caregiver-to-caregiver interactions have received comparatively little attention. Without a deeper understanding of these dynamics, tailored interventions may overlook the complexities of caregiver collaboration and distributed medication management. To address these gaps, this study aimed to understand how PCGs collaborate with SCGs in managing medications for older adults, focusing on PCGs’ perspectives. The specific objectives were to (1) identify and distinguish medication management activities performed by caregivers; (2) explore the factors influencing collaboration; and (3) describe styles of collaboration reported by PCGs.

Methods

Design

This study involved a qualitative analysis nested within a larger study understanding PCGs’ experiences of medication management for community-dwelling older adult care recipients. A qualitative descriptive design was used, as it is well suited to generating rich accounts of PCGs’ lived experiences, actions, and perceptions related to medication management [34, 35]. A comprehensive description of the parent study’s background, design, and study procedures has been reported elsewhere [20].

Participants

Of the 138 PCGs in northern Taiwan who participated in the parent study, 28 residing in urban areas were included in the present study. These caregivers provided care for community-dwelling older adults and met the following inclusion criteria: (1) reporting in the parent study survey that others assisted with daily caregiving, and (2) providing interview data that described interactions with at least one SCG, including family members and/or paid home care aides (i.e., a non-family member hired to assist with daily care), in managing medications for the care recipient. Participants were purposefully selected because their narratives directly addressed the study aim. Although qualitative interview research does not have strict rules for sample size, prior studies suggest that between 9 and 17 participants is generally sufficient to achieve data saturation in well-defined populations [36, 37].

Data collection

Data were collected through surveys and semi-structured interviews. The survey captured participants’ demographic information and caregiving backgrounds, whereas the interviews focused on their medication management experiences, including specific tasks performed and perceived barriers and facilitators. The full interview guide is available in the parent study [20]. Interviews were conducted in quiet, private locations chosen based on participants’ preferences to ensure confidentiality and comfort. The first author, a licensed pharmacist with formal training in qualitative research, performed all interviews, which were audio-recorded and transcribed verbatim in Mandarin Chinese. Among the 28 participants included in this analysis, interviews averaged 61 min in length (SD = 21 min).

Data analysis

Interview transcripts were managed using NVivo 14 (Lumivero, LLC, Denver, CO) and analyzed through a combination of deductive and inductive qualitative content analysis [38]. This analytic approach aligned with the qualitative descriptive design, as it emphasizes interpreting meaning directly from textual data within a naturalistic paradigm [35, 39]. Analysis followed the three phases of qualitative content analysis: preparation, organizing, and reporting [38]. All analyses were conducted using the original Mandarin transcripts to preserve linguistic and contextual meaning. The first and corresponding authors independently reviewed and coded the transcripts at the level of meaning units (i.e., one or more sentences). Both are native Mandarin-speaking qualitative researchers fluent in English, with expertise in pharmacy practice, aging, and caregiving.

Deductive coding was guided by Look and Stone’s [15] classification of medication management activities, distinguishing between direct (physically demanding) and indirect (cognitively demanding) tasks (Table 1). Codes were quantified by activity and caregiver role, and bar charts were used to visualize the distribution of activities across caregivers. Inductive coding was then used to explore factors shaping collaboration between PCGs and SCGs [38]. Two researchers performed open coding, generating notes and preliminary headings through close reading the transcripts. Initial codes were subsequently grouped into higher-order categories through comparison of similarities and differences. The coding scheme was refined iteratively through team discussions and cross-comparisons. Finally, related categories were abstracted into main domains, each labeled and defined to capture underlying meanings and patterns across the dataset.

Selected codes and illustrative excerpts were translated into English by the first author and confirmed by the corresponding author. The second author, a native English speaker, reviewed translations and participated in interpreting findings to ensure clarity and consistency of meaning. Translation accuracy and analytic interpretations were refined through iterative team discussions and clarifications. Throughout the process, the research team continually assessed the emergence of new codes and categories. Analysis of interviews from the 28 participants indicated no additional themes, and consensus was reached that data saturation had been achieved.

To synthesize findings, the same three researchers integrated the deductive and inductive results to identify collaborative styles that characterize how PCGs and SCGs jointly manage medications. This synthesis involved iterative discussions based on independently coded data and consensus-based refinement. These collaborative styles were developed by comparing how caregiving and sociodemographic factors influenced collaborative processes and shaped the division of medication management activities between caregivers. This integrative approach moved beyond isolated coding categories to illustrate how multiple contextual factors interact to form real-world caregiving dynamics. The synthesis resulted in higher-order categories that reflect contextually grounded patterns of caregiver collaboration [35, 38].

Rigor

Several strategies were applied during data collection, analysis, and reporting to ensure the study’s trustworthiness. Credibility was enhanced through both investigator and methodological triangulation, involving multiple researchers and complementary data sources during analysis [40]. Confirmability and interpretive rigor were supported by including direct participant quotations in the final report and by using a multilingual analytic process. This process involved team-based translation review and two rounds of external peer debriefing with multidisciplinary researchers outside the study team, who were native speakers of Mandarin or English, serving as verification strategies to preserve meaning across languages [41]. Transferability was facilitated by providing rich, contextualized descriptions of participants’ demographic characteristics and caregiving backgrounds, enabling readers to understand how participants’ experiences were embedded in their everyday caregiving practices [42]. Dependability was strengthened through transparent documentation of study procedures and analytic processes, complemented by verbatim quotes to demonstrate that interpretations were grounded in participants’ accounts [41, 43]. Finally, the study followed the Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research to ensure comprehensive and systematic reporting of design, methodology, and findings (Additional file 1) [44].

Results

Participant information

The demographic characteristics and caregiving backgrounds of PCGs and care recipients are presented in Tables 2 and 3. On average, PCGs were 53 years old (SD = 9.7), and the majority were female (n = 22, 78.6%). The mean duration of caregiving was 5.3 years (SD = 4.1). Nearly all PCGs (n = 27, 96.4%) cared for a parent or parent-in-law, and only one cared for a grandparent. Most PCGs (n = 22, 78.6%) had attained at least a college degree. Twelve PCGs (43.0%) were employed at the time of the study, and 19 (67.9%) lived with the care recipient. PCGs reported managing a mean of 8 medications per day (SD = 4.6) for the care recipient. Regarding SCG involvement, 11 PCGs (39.3%) received support exclusively from family members, 8 (28.6%) exclusively from non-family members (i.e., paid home care aides), and 9 (32.1%) from both family members and non-family members.

Care recipients were older adults with varying levels of caregiving needs. Approximately 75% had at least one chronic condition, and 67.9% had dementia. Most care recipients required at least moderate assistance with activities of daily living, as measured using the Traditional Chinese version of the Barthel Index (Table 3) [45, 46]. Many also experienced cognitive and physical limitations that reduced their capacity for independent medication management. Consequently, caregivers frequently assumed primary responsibility for medication-related tasks and decision-making, although care recipients continued to participate in medication-taking routines and communicate treatment-related concerns when able.

Part 1. Distribution of caregiver involvement in medication management activities

Figure 1 summarizes the frequency and distribution of coded medication management activities performed by PCGs and SCGs. All identified activities aligned with the classification framework of Look and Stone [15], and no new activity categories emerged from the analysis. A recurring feature across both activity types was whether the task required co-presence with the care recipient (i.e., being physically present in the same space), which often influenced how responsibilities were divided between caregivers. Overall, PCGs demonstrated greater involvement in most activities, particularly cognitively demanding indirect tasks. In contrast, SCGs were generally less engaged, except for physically demanding direct activities, such as medication administration, where their participation occasionally exceeded that of PCGs. Direct activities

Primary caregivers typically handled direct tasks that could be performed independently of the care recipient, such as preparing pillboxes or refilling prescriptions. In contrast, SCGs were more often involved in direct activities that required interaction with the care recipient, such as assisting with medication administration. The following excerpt illustrates this coordination: the PCG prepared medications in advance, while the SCG administered them via nasogastric (NG) feeding.

“She [care recipient] needs [nasogastric] feeding. I usually grind the medications, mix them with water, and prepare them in a cup. Because she [care recipient] typically takes her medication after meals, I prepare everything in advance, and she [secondary caregiver] does the feeding.” (ID09).

Indirect activities

Primary caregivers were more actively engaged in indirect activities than SCGs, highlighting their central role in managing cognitively demanding tasks. Secondary caregivers’ participation in indirect activities was generally limited to those requiring co-presence with the care recipient, such as ensuring medications were taken on time and monitoring for side effects. The following excerpt illustrates how a PCG relied on an SCG to support the care recipient’s medication adherence in her absence.

“I asked my brother, who lives with our parents, to help make sure my mother takes her medications on time in the morning and evening.” (ID16).

Conferring decision-making authority

The extent to which PCGs shared medication-related decision-making authority with SCGs varied substantially, ranging from PCGs maintaining full control to partially or fully involving SCGs in the decision-making process. These collaborative arrangements were primarily influenced by kinship relationships and the SCG’s familiarity with the care recipient’s medication regimen, both of which contributed to the level of trust established between caregivers. Increased involvement of SCGs in decision-making was also associated with greater participation in indirect or cognitively demanding medication management tasks.

In this study, shared decision-making was observed exclusively among family-member SCGs. Two distinct patterns emerged. In some cases, the SCG assumed full decision-making authority for medication management, whereas in others, decision-making authority was shared between SCGs and PCGs for the same medication management tasks. For example, one participant described how his sister, who alternated caregiving responsibilities with him for their mother, assumed full control over medication decisions when their mother stayed with her.

“When my mother stayed at my sister’s place, my sister was always worried that she (mother) might take her medications too many times by mistake. To prevent this, my sister managed her (mother) medications, gave them to her after breakfast and again after dinner.” (ID13).

In contrast, when decision-making was shared between the PCG and SCG, medication management involved collaborative deliberation rather than unilateral control. One participant described how she and her sisters jointly made decisions regarding medication adjustments for their mother.

“When it comes to medication, we often hesitate about what to do. For example, there were three nights in a row when her blood pressure went up to 190 [mmHg], and the three of us (sisters) discussed how to handle it together. No single person made the decision.” (ID19).

When SCGs were non-family members, such as paid home care aides, PCGs consistently retained primary medication-related decision-making authority. Nevertheless, these SCGs played an important supportive role by monitoring the care recipient’s condition and reporting their observations to the PCG, who made the final medication-related decisions. This arrangement reflected a clear hierarchy in decision-making authority. As one participant described:

“Yes, the care aide reports to me. For example, she lets me know if my mother’s blood pressure is high that day, or she checks it again later since she records it daily. This requires some training. She might think my mother has high blood pressure and report it to me. If I see that the readings have been consistently high for two or three days and her condition isn’t improving, I adjust the medication myself. If it still doesn’t improve, I bring the blood pressure records to the next doctor’s visit and ask the doctor to adjust the medication or run further tests.” (ID24).

When SCGs were family members but lacked a comparable understanding of the care recipient’s health condition or medication regimen, they often deferred decision-making to the PCG. This deference reflected both their limited understanding and the trust they placed in the PCG’s experience and familiarity with the care recipient’s medications. One participant with a medical background explained how his sister relied on him to address medication-related problems, illustrating how perceived competence shaped confidence in decision-making:

“Sometimes, if my sister missed a dose or gave my father’s medication late, she wouldn’t handle it on her own and would need to ask me what to do and how to do it.” (ID07).

Positioning the SCG as an active or passive supporter

Primary caregivers perceived SCGs as either active or passive supporters, depending on the consistency and nature of their involvement in medication management. Active support was characterized by regular and structured participation, where responsibilities were either shared with the PCG or clearly delineated between caregivers. In contrast, passive support was typically intermittent or situational, provided on an as-needed basis for specific tasks. These roles did not always correspond directly to the type of medication management activity or level of decision-making authority. For instance, an SCG could be considered an active supporter even when performing relatively simple indirect tasks, provided their involvement was consistent and predictable. Conversely, an SCG might be regarded as providing passive support even when occasionally involved in decision-making, if their participation was irregular or temporary.

Active support also encompassed arrangements in which responsibilities overlapped between caregivers, requiring similar levels of cognitive or physical effort. One participant described how she and her brother alternated the physically demanding task of medication preparation, reflecting an equitable and coordinated approach to caregiving.

“We bought a grinder. We had to grind the pills and be extremely careful. Just preparing one week’s supply took a lot of time. In the beginning, it took us more than two hours each week to get everything ready. It was exhausting. My brother and I took turns.” (ID11).

In other cases, responsibilities were clearly divided, with each caregiver assuming primary responsibility for specific aspects of medication management. This structured division of labor ensured continuity of care and optimized available resources. For example, access to transportation often determined who handled prescription refills or accompanied the care recipient to medical appointments:

“For doctor visits, my sister-in-law and brother-in-law take [care recipient] to the hospital every month. They drive her to see the doctor and pick up her medications. They come back every week, but when it comes to [medication management] tasks at home, that is my responsibility.” (ID01).

Secondary caregivers’ participation was frequently constrained by employment obligations or geographic distance from the care recipient, limiting their role to passive support. In such cases, SCGs stepped in temporarily when PCGs were unavailable, such as during respite periods or competing family demands, offering short-term assistance rather than consistent involvement. These task-shifting arrangements commonly occurred within kinship networks, with PCGs delegating specific duties while retaining overall responsibility. One participant explained how family members occasionally provided respite support without altering the broader caregiving structure:

“My mother has colon cancer, and I’m the one who always takes her to the hospital for chemotherapy and stays with her during treatments. Sometimes someone else watches her so I can go home and rest, but most of the time, I stay with her in the hospital.” (ID03).

Because of their limited involvement, SCGs in passive roles often lacked familiarity with the care recipient’s medication regimen and needs. To reduce potential errors, PCGs typically handled cognitively demanding tasks in advance, such as organizing medications and managing schedules, while assigning simpler direct activities to SCGs. Although this division of labor aimed to safeguard medication accuracy, PCGs continued to express concerns about whether delegated tasks were performed correctly, underscoring the ongoing burden of oversight. One participant reflected:

“I usually handle all her [care recipient] medications. But if I am not around, I ask my siblings to help. Still, I worry that they may not fully understand what I’ve prepared, and sometimes my mom takes the medications without them noticing.” (ID18).

Four distinct styles of collaboration between PCGs and SCGs emerged from the integration of the two preceding categories that influenced collaboration. These styles, Partnering, Operating, Bench-Playing, and Intervening, reflect how PCGs perceived SCGs’ roles, the ways in which they engaged in medication management, the types of activities performed, and the contextual factors shaping these arrangements. Partnering

The Partnering style describes accounts in which PCGs perceived SCGs as active collaborators who were consistently involved in both direct and indirect medication management activities and engaged in shared decision-making. Primary caregivers emphasized that these SCGs provided structured and reliable assistance, either by assuming distinct responsibilities or by jointly performing overlapping tasks. Such arrangements were often negotiated through mutual discussion and influenced by each caregiver’s expertise and available resources. For instance, SCGs with access to transportation often managed medical appointments and prescription refills, whereas those with greater digital literacy handled online searches for medication-related information.

Decision-making within the Partnering style was typically viewed as a shared responsibility, grounded in mutual trust and a comparable understanding of the care recipient’s health and medications. Primary caregivers described decision-making as collective and reciprocal, reflecting both a willingness and, at times, a necessity to involve SCGs. Collaboration patterns varied by living arrangement. When care recipients alternated between households, medication-related decisions were often made independently within each caregiving context. In contrast, caregivers who lived together were more likely to make decisions jointly. Across both contexts, PCGs underscored that SCGs’ comparable knowledge of the care recipient’s condition strengthened confidence in shared decision-making.

Operating

The Operating style involved SCGs serving as task-focused executors who consistently performed medication-related activities but had limited participation in decision-making. This style was most commonly associated with paid home care aides. Primary caregivers described these SCGs as dependable in carrying out assigned tasks and adhering to routines, although they were not expected to participate in higher-level planning or problem-solving. Their continuous physical presence allowed PCGs to focus on indirect or cognitively demanding caregiving responsibilities. Tasks assigned to SCGs in this style were typically predefined, procedural, and closely supervised, including administering preorganized medications, monitoring adherence, and reporting daily observations.

Decision-making authority was retained with the PCG, who served as the central coordinator for medication management. Primary caregivers attributed this hierarchical arrangement to SCGs’ limited understanding of the care recipient’s medical condition or the rationale behind prescribed medications. Reliability and adherence to instructions were therefore valued over independent judgment. Nonetheless, PCGs acknowledged that SCGs contributed to decision-making processes by observing and reporting clinical changes. When SCGs were non-family members, PCGs emphasized a formal hierarchy, often noting that SCGs were not permitted to make autonomous decisions about medication management.

Bench-Playing

The Bench-Playing style referred to SCGs who acted as peripheral supporters, participating in medication management only intermittently. Their involvement was generally ad hoc and unstructured, stepping in when PCGs were unavailable due to other responsibilities, health issues, or the need for respite. Primary caregivers described this sporadic participation as shaped by situational factors, such as employment, geographic distance, or family obligations. In this arrangement, decision-making was retained by the PCG, who served as the primary point of coordination and oversight. Although Bench-Playing SCGs provided short-term assistance, their limited understanding or knowledge of the care recipient’s medications required ongoing supervision. Their contributions offered temporary relief rather than sustained collaboration or shared decision-making.

Intervening

The Intervening style, although less common, was notable for the tension it introduced into caregiving dynamics. This style involved SCGs who had minimal or no involvement in medication management but periodically challenged or attempted to override the PCG’s decisions, resulting in conflict, emotional distress, and perceptions of undermined autonomy in decision-making among PCGs. For example, one PCG described a family member SCG who neither lived with the care recipient nor participated in caregiving tasks. Despite this limited involvement, the SCG criticized the PCG following an emergency hospital admission, attributing the event to inadequate medication management, and attempted to influence prescribing decisions by communicating directly with physicians while excluding the PCG from the process. This pattern illustrates how limited involvement combined with perceived interference can disrupt caregiving relationships and exacerbate stress among PCGs.

Discussion

This study explored how PCGs and members of their SCG networks collaborate to manage medications for community-dwelling older adults. Building on prior research that has identified key factors shaping SCG involvement, such as geographic proximity, available resources, kinship ties, and family role expectations [28], this study extends existing knowledge by showing how these factors play out in day-to-day medication management. By integrating shared medication management activities with contextual factors shaping collaboration, the findings provide a more detailed understanding of how caregiving responsibilities are negotiated, distributed, and coordinated in practice.

The demographic composition of the sample reflects the broader caregiving context in Taiwan and provides important context for interpreting the findings. Nearly all PCGs were adult children or children-in-law of the care recipient, which is consistent with common caregiving arrangements in Taiwan, where older adults often live with or near their adult children [47]. Cultural expectations related to filial piety may also contribute to adult children or children-in-law assuming primary caregiving roles, even when the care recipient has a living spouse [20, 48]. Within this context, PCGs often assumed primary responsibility for medication management and medication-related decision-making, while SCGs more commonly provided supportive or task-specific assistance.

Although this study focused primarily on collaboration between PCGs and SCGs, care recipients remained involved in medication management to varying extents and were not entirely excluded from medication-related activities. Many care recipients experienced substantial cognitive or functional limitations, which influenced the degree of caregiver involvement and responsibility. Nevertheless, care recipients continued to participate in medication management when able, including communicating symptoms, expressing treatment preferences, and engaging in medication-taking routines when possible. These findings suggest that medication management should be understood as a dynamic and relational process shaped not only by caregiver collaboration but also by the care recipient’s functional capacity and level of participation. Future research should further investigate how care recipient capacity and participation influence caregiver collaboration and medication-related decision-making.

A major contribution of this study is the identification and characterization of four distinct collaboration styles: Partnering, Operating, Bench-Playing, and Intervening. These styles reflect how medication management responsibilities and decision-making are negotiated between PCGs and SCGs across diverse caregiving contexts. Rather than presenting collaboration as a set of isolated themes, this typology offers an analytically integrative framework that connects caregiver roles, authority relationships, and task interdependence to distinct patterns of collaboration in everyday medication management [49]. Grounded in the lived experiences of PCGs, these styles represent analytical configurations that illustrate how similar medication-related tasks may be accomplished through fundamentally different relational and organizational processes [50, 51]. By highlighting variation in the composition of SCG networks and the ways SCGs are involved, the typology facilitates comparisons across caregiving arrangements and provides insight into why collaboration may differ even under comparable caregiving demands.

Importantly, this typology has implications for both practice and research. In practice, it may serve as a useful framework for assessing caregiving networks and tailoring support strategies according to patterns of caregiver involvement. For example, healthcare providers may proactively offer medication-related education and support to SCGs in Operating or Bench-Playing roles, while anticipating potential conflict and role ambiguity among SCGs involved in Intervening roles. From a research perspective, this typology provides a conceptual basis for classifying caregiving patterns and understanding how interactions among multiple caregivers influence caregiver burden, medication safety, and care recipient outcomes. Together, these findings advance a more relational and contextually grounded understanding of family-based medication management.

This study also demonstrated that PCGs adopted flexible and adaptive approaches when collaborating with SCGs. Primary caregivers adjusted caregiving strategies based on available resources, considering factors such as the SCG’s availability (e.g., work schedules, geographic proximity), caregiving experience, medication-related knowledge, and relationship to the care recipient (e.g., family member versus paid home care aide) when assigning responsibilities. These arrangements reflected deliberate coordination and integration of available resources to optimize caregiving efficiency. Conceptually, this resembles the economic principle of comparative advantage, in which individuals assume tasks aligned with their relative strengths and lower opportunity costs to improve overall efficiency [52, 53]. Although originating from economic theory, this task-specialization logic is consistent with qualitative caregiving research showing that responsibilities are adaptively distributed according to caregivers’ capacities, availability, and contextual constraints [30]. For example, co-residing SCGs commonly undertook direct, physically demanding tasks; individuals with greater digital literacy searched for medication-related information, and those with access to transportation managed prescription refills or accompanied care recipients to medical appointments. Collectively, these patterns underscore the adaptability, complementarity, and resourcefulness embedded within collaborative medication management.

Collaboration between PCGs and SCGs took diverse and context-dependent forms, encompassing both shared responsibilities and hierarchical dynamics. Primary caregivers commonly viewed SCGs as valuable sources of social support who contributed to physical caregiving tasks and medication-related decision-making, thereby helping to distribute caregiving demands. Within caregiving networks involving multiple SCGs, collaboration styles varied across individual dyadic relationships and were shaped particularly by whether the SCG was a family member or a paid home care aide. These collaboration styles were not mutually exclusive within a caregiving network. For example, a PCG might adopt a Partnering style with family-member SCGs while simultaneously engaging in an Operating style with non-family-member SCGs. Collaboration also occurred among SCGs themselves. Family-member SCGs who collaborated as partners with the PCG could, in turn, assume more hierarchical roles when interacting with non-family-member SCGs.

The predominance of female PCGs in this study also reflects broader gendered patterns in family caregiving reported internationally [54, 55]. Women frequently assume central caregiving responsibilities while simultaneously managing multiple family and social roles, including responsibilities as daughters, spouses, mothers, and employees. These intersecting obligations may partially explain why collaboration with SCGs was influenced by factors such as employment demands, marital responsibilities, and geographic distance. The findings therefore suggest that medication management collaboration is shaped not only by caregiving demands but also by broader gendered expectations surrounding family care. Future interventions should incorporate gender-sensitive approaches to caregiver support, including flexible respite services, caregiver education, workplace accommodations, and strategies that facilitate more equitable sharing of caregiving responsibilities across family networks. Future research should also use quantitative caregiver surveys to further examine how gender-related roles and responsibilities influence the adoption of different collaboration styles in medication management.

Recurring barriers to effective caregiver collaboration emerged across the Operating, Bench-Playing, and Intervening styles. Limited medication knowledge among SCGs was a major concern for PCGs, particularly when direct supervision was not possible [56]. Language barriers posed additional challenges, especially with foreign care aides. Difficulties in communication, task delegation, and trust often led PCGs to maintain control, leading to overmonitoring and slower transitions [20, 57]. The Intervening style illustrated how unequal distribution of responsibilities and contested decision-making could escalate conflicts. Prior research has identified family conflict as a major source of caregiver stress, particularly when expectations are perceived as inequitable or care decisions are disrupted [58, 59]. Future research should investigate how practical and relational barriers interact and identify strategies, such as structured communication protocols, AI-assisted translation tools, and technological or non-technological telecaregiving supports, to promote equitable task sharing, reduce conflict, and improve medication safety [60–62].

This study focused on collaborative processes and relational work in medication management rather than outcomes for caregivers or care recipients. Therefore, the proposed collaboration style framework is not intended to imply that any style is inherently more effective than another. Nevertheless, the physical, cognitive, and relational demands of medication management may contribute to caregiver stress. Previous research has emphasized the mediating role of SCGs as social support that can help PCGs cope with complex caregiving demands [17, 63, 64]. Our findings suggest moving beyond a binary view of informal support (i.e., its presence or absence) toward a dynamic understanding of caregiving networks, recognizing that collaboration itself requires effort and that certain caregivers may inadvertently increase stress. Future studies should investigate how these collaboration styles affect caregiver stress, wellbeing, and care outcomes. While certain styles may appear in other caregiving contexts, this study was intentionally grounded in medication management due to its prevalence, cognitive demands, and implications for care quality and safety. Further research could investigate whether these collaboration styles extend to caregiving activities beyond medication management.

This study has several limitations. First, as a sub-analysis of interviews focused on PCGs’ experiences, SCG perspectives were not directly captured. Interview questions were aligned with the aims of the parent study and not specifically designed to probe caregiver collaboration, which may have limited the exploration of some relevant dynamics. Detailed sociodemographic information about SCGs, such as age, education, and caregiving intensity, was not systematically collected, restricting analysis of how SCG characteristics shape collaboration. In addition, most participants were female adult children or children-in-law of care recipients. Therefore, the findings may not fully represent the experiences of male caregivers, spouses, or other caregiving relationships. Future studies should incorporate SCG perspectives, collect comprehensive data on both PCGs and SCGs, and recruit diverse caregiving populations to better capture variations in collaboration strategies across kinship and caregiving roles.

Conclusions

This study clarifies how PCGs and SCGs collaborate in managing medications for older adults. By identifying specific caregiving tasks, categorizing collaborative strategies, and exploring contextual influences, this study reveals the dynamic distribution of responsibilities and decision-making within caregiving networks. These insights provide a foundation for developing targeted, context-sensitive interventions that enhance collaboration, safety, and efficiency. The typology of caregiver collaboration offered here serves as a practical framework for designing and evaluating interventions that reflect the unique dynamics of diverse caregiver networks rather than assuming that family practices are uniform.
