Attitudes toward returning pathogenic KCNQ1 variant and uptake of cascade screening in the Amish
Authors: Elizabeth A Streeten, Yue Guan, Kathleen A Ryan, Melanie Daue, Toni I Pollin, Kristen CR Wood, Kristin A Maloney, Alan R Shuldiner, Braxton D Mitchell, Devyani Chowdhury, Amber L Beitelshees
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess the experience of Amish regarding return of results (RoR) of long QT syndrome founder variant KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met), information sharing, and uptake of beta-blockers, and variant testing in at-risk family members (cascade testing).MethodsProbands (n = 88) with KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met), previously identified by exome sequencing, after RoR, were mailed: (1) survey on attitudes toward RoR and (2) response card indicating interest in free, in-home cascade testing of first-degree relatives, indicating contact preference by study team (direct contact) or proband. Participant recall of beta blocker recommendation was compared with notes from RoR counseling.ResultsSurveys were received from 33 of 88 (37.5%) participants. Positive perceptions of the utility of genetic testing were reported (mean = 32.1, SD = 3.4, range 25-38). Of 23 respondents recalling that a beta blocker was recommended, 4 (17.4%) were taking one. Cascade testing interest responses were received from 69 of 88 (78%) participants; 25 expressed interest in cascade testing of children or information for family members. Most participants chose direct contact (90%). Seventy-nine family members (63 children, 16 siblings) from 18 families were tested, all through direct contact.ConclusionThe RoR process was well received, although uptake of beta blockers was low. Cascade testing interest was modest; however, most followed through on testing. These results help to inform strategies to improve cascade testing in this population.Keywords: Cascade screening, KCNQ1, Long QT syndrome, Old Order Amish, Return of results

Introduction

Cascade screening (or cascade testing) is defined as the process of offering genetic testing to at-risk family members of someone who has been identified to have a specific genetic variant.1 A gap exists in knowledge of the most effective implementation strategies of cascade testing for actionable genetic disorders, particularly in founder populations. The Old Order Amish (Amish) population of Lancaster County, PA, was established by around 400 founders who emigrated from Europe (most from Switzerland) in the early 1700s.2 As of 2024, there were approximately 43,000 Amish in Lancaster County Amish.3 The Amish are primarily farmers, use horse/buggy for transportation and do not use electricity. They are conservative in both ideology and manner of dressing and receive education through the 8th grade in Amish schools. Although many have phones, they are used primarily to receive and return messages, and they do not use computers. These lifestyle characteristics are important when designing research with this population. Amish individuals marry only within the community, leading to a genetic bottleneck whereby some genetic variants are enriched in the population.

Among studies in the general population, commonly cited barriers to cascade testing include low understanding and limited communication skills among probands.4 Additional barriers in the Amish include cultural and religious issues, in addition to the costs of tests and travel.5,6 The Amish have lower health literacy than the general population, have more interest in testing/treatment for symptomatic problems than in preventive care and genetic testing is a culturally sensitive issue.6

The current practice in cascade testing is for probands to make the initial contact with at-risk relatives to avoid privacy concerns. However, a recent review found studies conducted outside the United States with direct contact of relatives by health care providers yielded higher uptake rates.7 There is a significant amount of information available on participant experience of genetic counseling in many populations.8 However, there is a paucity of information on participant experience and uptake of cascade testing in relatively isolated populations such as the Amish.

Long QT syndrome (LQTS) is a group of disorders that increases the risk of syncope and sudden cardiac death (SCD) due to arrhythmias (including Torsade de pointes).9 LQTS is characterized by prolongation of the QTc interval (QT corrected for heart rate) on an electrocardiogram (ECG) and may be acquired (eg, medication exposure)10 or hereditary.9 Hereditary LQTS (also referred to as congenital LQTS, includes several types, of which the most common are caused by pathogenic monoallelic loss of function variants in 2 cardiac potassium channel genes, KCNQ1 (HGNC:6294) (long QT syndrome type 1, LQT1 [MIM 192500]) and KCNH2 (HGNC:6251) (long QT syndrome type 2, LQT2 [MIM 613688]) and a gain-of-function variant in the sodium channel gene SCN5A (HGNC:10593) (long QT syndrome type 3, LQT3 [603796])11: LQT1, LQT2 and LQT3 are autosomal dominant in inheritance. Biallelic pathogenic variants in KCNQ1 result in Jervell and Lange-Nielsen syndrome, characterized by risk of SCD and hearing loss. Further discussion of LQTS will be restricted to LQT1, the subject of this study. In LQT1, treatment with beta blocker therapy reduces the risk of SCD by 62% to 91%.12

We previously identified a pathogenic missense variant in KCNQ1 NC_000011.10:g.2571391C>T NM_000218.3:c.671C>T p.(Thr224Met), that is highly enriched in the Lancaster Amish due to a founder effect (MAF = 0.012, carrier frequency 1 in 45)13 and causes LQT1. In our previous report, we described our reclassification of this variant from a variant of unknown significance to pathogenic, using American College of Medical Genetics and Association for Molecular Pathology criteria.13 In contrast, in the general population of European descent, the prevalence of LQTS is estimated at 1/2500, with LQT1 being the most common type.14 In terms of penetrance of the p.(Thr224Met) variant, the phenotypes of abnormal ECG versus symptoms (eg, syncope, SCD) need to be considered separately. In our study of 88 Amish participants with KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) versus 54 without the variant, we found that 83 versus 18% had an abnormal QTc on ECG (maximal QTc) (P < .0001), 32 versus 17% had at least 1 syncopal episode (P = .02), and 4% versus 0 had a first-degree relative with SCD under age 30 (P = .026).13 Using the Heart Rhythm Society (HRS) guidelines for treatment with a beta blocker for LQT1,15 80 of 88 (93%) met criteria for beta-blocker use.13

The penetrance of abnormal QTc and prevalence of syncope that we found for the p.(Thr224Met) variant were similar to what has been reported for other KCNQ1 variants in LQT1.16 LQT1 is included on the American College of Medical Genetics list of reportable actionable secondary findings in clinical exome and genome sequencing.17 Cascade testing of family members of patients with LQT1 (for the variant found in the proband) is recommended by the HRS and the European Heart Rhythm Association.18

The purpose of this study was 2-fold: (1) to assess the response to genetic counseling and medical management recommendations among Amish participants with KCNQ1 founder variant p.(Thr224Met) and (2) to develop and pilot test an intervention to improve the uptake of cascade variant testing of first-degree family members.

Materials and Methods

Overview

A total of 88 participants who had previously undergone RoR for the variant KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) between 9/2017 and 3/201813 were invited to participate in the study. The study included a survey assessing their response to genetic counseling and medical management recommendations from the RoR process and an invitation to offer variant cascade testing for the KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) variant to first-degree family members. The pilot test intervention was designed to improve the uptake of cascade variant testing of first-degree family members. Participants could choose either study team contact of family members (direct contact) or participant contact (proband contact) of family members. Variant testing was offered free and in-home.

Participants

The participants described here were among >5500 Amish participants recruited through multiple protocols performed between 1995 and 2017 as part of the University of Maryland Amish Research Program.19, 20, 21, 22, 23 As previously reported,13 124 individuals were identified by research exome sequencing performed by Regeneron Genetics Center with the variant KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met), and 88 opted to receive results following confirmation of the variant in a “clinical,” Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendment-certified laboratory at the University of Maryland. As previously reported, these 88 probands were provided RoR by a clinical geneticist, including recommendations for beta blockers and cascade testing of first-degree relatives.13 Recommendations for beta-blocker use provided during RoR used HRS guidelines for long QT syndrome type 1.15 Based on these guidelines, of the 88 probands published previously, 93% were recommended to take beta-blockers. The study was approved by The University of Maryland Baltimore Institutional Review Board. Participants signed informed consent to participate in cascade screening. Information on sex/gender were collected by self-report during genetic counseling performed during RoR.13

Survey

Study invitation letters including a survey (Supplemental Information 1) and a response card to indicate interest in cascade testing of family members (Supplemental Information 2, see below) were mailed in March 2019, 12 to 18 months after RoR. Surveys were received until July 2019. Up to 3 attempts were made to visit probands’ homes to collect cascade testing response cards and/or surveys. The survey assessed understanding of and attitudes toward RoR, visits to health care providers, beta blocker use, lifestyle modification, and result sharing with family members. Amish women who have worked with our research clinic as liaisons reviewed the surveys to assess for clarity and understanding. Surveys and other correspondence were written for an 8th-grade reading level. Our study team developed the survey, with the exception of the questions regarding perceived utility of genetic testing and decisional regret which came from a validated survey. A 5 dollar bill was included with the mailing.

Evaluation of survey responses

We assessed 2 dimensions of probands’ attitudes on the RoR process (Supplemental Information 3): (1) perceived utility of genetic testing results: measured by an 8-item 5-Likert scale adapted from previous studies.24 Responses were summed to create a composite measure (range: 8-40). Higher scores indicate greater perceived utility. (2) Decision regret regarding genetic testing: assessed using the 5-item Decision Regret Scale.25 A sum score was calculated and converted to a 0 to 100 scale, in which a higher score indicates greater regret. A sum score >50 typically indicates overall regret.

Participants were asked whether a beta blocker was recommended during RoR and whether it was being taken. Responses were compared with notes from counseling provided by a clinical geneticist approximately 12 to 18 months earlier,13 to assess recall accuracy. In addition, they were asked whether lifestyle modifications had been recommended and had been made.

Cascade testing response card

A study invitation letter included a response card (Supplemental Information 1) to be filled out by the proband, to indicate interest in free in-home testing for KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) of minor children and offering the variant testing to other adult first-degree family members. Those interested in testing of adult family members could choose direct contact (by the study team) or proband contact. If direct contact was requested, the proband provided addresses of their adult family member (including adult children) and the study team mailed letters (Supplemental Information 2) to these first-degree family members offering cascade testing, including LQT1 information and instructions on scheduling testing. For proband contact, letters were provided to probands to share with family members, including information about LQT1 and KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met).

Cascade testing

In relatives requesting KCNQ1 testing, at home pretest genetic counseling was conducted in person. Because the Amish do not use electricity, they do not have computers that could be used for virtual counseling. In addition, although most Amish do have telephones, they prefer in-person contact for discussions about research. Therefore, it has been our practice to perform pretest and positive posttest counseling in person. Pretest genetic counseling was performed by the study nurse, and saliva was collected for study-funded cascade testing for KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met). The study nurse received training on performing pretest counseling by the study clinical geneticist and was provided a talking cue sheet. Testing was performed by the Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendment-certified laboratory at the Clinic for Special Children (CSC, Strasburg, PA). Relatives testing positive for the KCNQ1 variant received genetic counseling and were referred for clinical care to area providers (cardiologists and primary care providers) with experience with LQT1 and in treating the Amish. An ECG was offered to family members testing positive for p.(Thr224Met) to aid in clinical follow-up. Those who tested negative for the KCNQ1 variant were mailed a letter stating these results. To estimate the uptake of cascade screening, we divided the number of tested first-degree relatives of the 88 probands by the total number of at-risk first-degree relatives.

Results

Participants

Respondents to both the survey and interest in cascade testing were representative of the 88 proband group, with no difference in age, sex, symptoms, or family history of first-degree relatives with SCD < age 30 (Supplemental Information 4, Supplemental Tables 1 and 2).

Survey

Of 88 probands invited to participate in this study, 33 (37.5%) returned the survey. The mean age of the survey respondents was 51 ± 18 years, and 48% were female. Not all participants answered all questions.

Perceived utility of genetic testing results

Most participants felt they made the right decision in getting results, were confident in result accuracy and understood the counseling and written informational materials given. Participants (n = 22) reported positive perceptions of the utility of genetic testing and satisfaction with information provided (mean = 32.1, SD = 3.4, range: 25-38).

Decision regret regarding genetic testing

Overall, participants (n = 24) felt low regret about their decision to receive KCNQ1 genetic testing results (mean = 38.8, SD = 8.4, range: 20-55).

Beta blocker recommendations, uptake of medical care, and lifestyle changes

Thirty probands answered questions regarding beta blockers: 23 reported a beta blocker was recommended during RoR, 5 reported it was not recommended, and 2 did not remember. Of 23 who recalled a beta blocker recommendation, 4 (4/23, 17.4%) reported taking a beta blocker, including 1 participant who was prescribed a beta blocker for another problem before RoR. Recall was good regarding whether a beta blocker was recommended (n = 23) or not (n = 3) during RoR, with 26 of 30 (87%) recalling correctly based on counseling documentation. An additional 2 individuals, who did not return the survey, stated that they were on a beta blocker during a home visit, for a total of 6 on a beta blocker. Nine (9/32, 28%) participants reported seeing a primary care provider (n = 8) or cardiologist (n = 1) after RoR. Regarding lifestyle modification for LQTS recommended during counseling, of 30 respondents, 9 recalled receiving this information. Of 16 who responded to whether or not they had made lifestyle changes, 2 indicated that they had. Anecdotally, during RoR, many of the 88 probands voiced that they would be unable to limit physical activity because of their active jobs and home duties. These physical demands include farming using horse drawn plows and carts for men, washing clothes by hand for women, and hitching horses to buggies and driving buggies for both.

Information sharing

Regarding information shared by probands with family members, 17 of 29 (58.6%) probands who answered this question shared test results with a family member (Figure 1), most commonly with mothers, sisters, and adult children. The most common information shared was the positive KCNQ1 result, followed by feelings about this result and personal risk of SCD (Figure 1). Regarding personal risk (of the proband) for SCD, only 9 of 25 (36%) respondents for this question shared this risk with family member(s). Regarding family member’s risk of developing a heart problem, 8 of 25 (32%) respondents shared this information with family member(s). The most common reasons reported for not sharing genetic test results with family were not knowing how to explain genetic risk, followed by not knowing enough about LQT1, not feeling that it was their duty to discuss disease risk with family, not wanting to burden family, and not wanting to share their health information.

Cascade testing response cards and uptake of cascade variant testing

Response cards regarding interest in cascade testing were received from 69 of 88 (78.4%) probands with 25 of 69 (36.2%) indicating either a desire for cascade testing of minor children or offering testing to other adult first-degree family members or both (Figure 2). In 18 of 20 (90%) interested in offering cascade testing to family members, direct contact was requested rather than proband contact. From these responses, 79 participants received KCNQ1 testing, including 16 siblings and 63 offspring of the probands (19 adults and 44 minors). The 79 participants tested were family members of 18 of 25 probands requesting cascade testing. When evaluating all 88 probands, testing was completed on 16 of 388 (4.1%) of siblings and 63 of 421 (15.0%) of offspring at risk, totaling 79 of 809 (9.8%) of all at-risk first-degree relatives. Throughout the course of the study, an additional 57 participants who were not first-degree relatives of the original 88 probands but were first-degree relatives of newly identified participants with the KCNQ1 variant received cascade genetic testing, bringing the total number of participants tested to 131.

Cascade variant testing results

Testing was positive in 56 of 131 (42.7%) family members. All 56 received results and genetic counseling. Because of COVID-19 restrictions, some results were disclosed by phone. All were offered ECGs and referred for medical follow-up to cardiologists and primary care providers familiar with LQT1 and care in the Amish.

Discussion

RoR for KCNQ1, p.(Thr224Met) was well received by study participants, uptake of beta blockers was low and interest in cascade testing was modest. Among participants who completed surveys, positive perceptions of the utility of genetic testing, satisfaction with information provided and low levels of regret were reported. No one indicated feeling harmed by receiving results, although it cannot be ruled out that some of the 62% not returning the surveys felt harmed. This positive response to our RoR process is reassuring that our methods are aligned with expectations in the population. Perhaps not surprising given this population’s generally low usage of prescription medications, particularly when asymptomatic,26 uptake of beta blockers was low. Recall of whether a beta blocker was recommended in RoR was high in participants, but only 17.4% were taking a beta blocker. Interest in offering cascade testing to first-degree family members was modest, with most testing occurring in offspring of probands rather than siblings or parents.

Over half (58.6%) of survey participants shared positive KCNQ1 results with family, most often with mothers, sisters, and adult offspring, as has also been reported in other populations.27 The most common reason for not sharing results was not knowing how to explain the results, which has been reported as a barrier to cascade testing for LQTS in another study.28 Although over half shared the positive genetic test result with a family member, only a third shared the disease risk associated with the variant, specifically a possible risk of SCD in themselves or risk of a cardiac problem in family members. Participants felt ill-equipped to discuss specific disease risk with family members, which lends support for the concept of trained professionals providing this information to relatives.

Interest in offering cascade testing to family members was modest among responding participants, with 25 of 69 respondents (36.2%) requesting information for cascade testing of family members. We speculate that this modest interest may be due in part to the complexities of offering cascade testing through a research study in which we mailed letters to probands 12-18 months after they received their RoR. It is possible that offering cascade testing in person during RoR counseling could improve uptake. Other reasons for declining cascade testing are likely. Several participants who declined cascade testing but responded to the survey wrote in comments about why they declined cascade testing. Some of these comments were specific, including that they were not interested because they (probands) were asymptomatic and that they did not have time. However, a few indicated that they simply were “not interested at this time.” Others have commented that the reasons for Amish declining preventive health services are unclear.26

Cascade testing was performed in 79 first-degree relatives in 18 of these 25 families, with lack of follow through in the remaining 7 families. Although this cascade testing yield is suboptimal, before this study, only 2 individuals had testing for the KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) variant through a clinic that offers inexpensive genetic testing to the community (CSC), suggesting that our study greatly amplified the extent of testing for KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) in the community. We cannot rule out that testing was performed in another laboratory. However, this seems unlikely because the cost for genetic testing at CSC is much lower than in commercial genetic testing laboratories, an important factor in this population without health insurance.

We are unaware of any statistics on the number of Amish with traditional health insurance. However, our anecdotal experience over the past 25 years with Amish research participants and Amish women who serve as liaisons in our research is that very few have commercial insurance and that this creates a barrier to cascade testing. The Amish have the opportunity to participate in “Amish Aid,” a policy that helps to cover health care costs, but this only applies to inpatient hospital care. It is estimated that 30% of Amish in Lancaster County, PA, participate in “Amish Aid.”29 Our results lend support to the concept that removal of the cost/travel barrier improves uptake of cascade testing in this population.

In our study, nearly all (90%) who were interested in cascade testing being offered to family requested direct study team contact of family members. Others have shown a higher uptake of cascade screening in familial hypercholesterolemia when physician contact of family members is offered.7 Uptake of testing and alignment with ethical principles in the Amish with direct versus indirect (proband) contact of family members is being assessed in a larger ongoing study.

We are unaware of any previous data from founder populations on the uptake of cascade testing. However, comparing our data with a study of cascade testing in pediatric LQTS and hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, done in 6 US tertiary cardiology clinics, interest in cascade testing was higher than in our respondents at 75% (of 315 probands).30 In the tertiary cardiology clinic study, declining testing and lack of family follow through were the most common reasons for cascade testing not being performed, as in our results presented here. One factor in the greater uptake of cascade testing in the tertiary cardiology clinic study30 compared with ours could be the difference in ascertainment of probands, symptomatic disease versus community research exome sequencing.

Strengths of this study include the use of standardized methods for interpreting survey results and study of a well-phenotyped participant group. Using a founder population such as the Amish, with a relatively homogeneous lifestyle, lends both strengths and limitations to the study: strengths include similar cultural beliefs and occupations, as well as consistent educational background (8th grade), characteristics that would vary greatly among individuals in an outbred population. In addition, we have detailed knowledge of the pedigrees in this Amish population. Limitations include reduced potential generalizability to other populations and inability to utilize electronic forms of communication (not allowed in Amish culture). In addition, our results pertain specifically to Amish individuals who initially agreed to participate in population-based genetics research and subsequent RoR. Thus, the extent to which these results apply to Amish individuals who did not participate in such research is not known. The results are also specific to KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) and may not be applicable to other variants within the Amish community or to other non-Amish populations. Although our findings are specific to the Amish, the Amish do share some characteristics with rural and/or conservative communities. Thus, it is possible that our findings could translate to other cultures or communities; however, this would need to be tested directly. Regarding consent to contact relatives directly outside the scope of a research study, the laws vary by state and have been summarized in a previously published scoping review.4 Another limitation is that in this study, we did not ask about over-the-counter supplement use. In our previous study, we found that 77% of Amish take supplements, including 62% who take an herbal supplement.31 Because some herbal preparations may prolong QT, we cannot rule out that supplement use affected QT in this study.

Conclusion

Returning results of KCNQ1 p.(Thr224Met) obtained from research exome sequencing and confirmed clinically was well received in the Amish. Although interest in cascade testing was modest, it far exceeded the testing done before the in-home, free testing in this study. For cascade testing, nearly all participants requested study team contact of family members. Preference for direct contact of family members to initiate cascade testing will need confirmation in a larger study. These results help to inform strategies to improve cascade testing in the Amish and may be applicable to other founder or underserved populations.
