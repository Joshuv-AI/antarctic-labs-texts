Evaluating the impact of mesenchymal stem cell therapy on visual acuity and retinal nerve fiber layer thickness in optic neuropathy patients: a comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Samira Chaibakhsh, Fatemeh Azimi, Alireza Shoae-Hassani, Parvin Niknam, Ali Ghamari, Samaneh Dehghan, Naveed Nilforushan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStem cell therapy has emerged as a potential therapeutic avenue for optic neuropathy patients. To assess its safety and efficacy, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis, focusing on the latest evidence pertaining to the improvement of visual acuity (VA) through stem cell therapy.MethodsWe analyzed Each database from its inception until June 2024. PubMed, Scopus, and Google Scholar were systematically searched to identify the included studies. Data were extracted regarding the year of publication, the name of the first author, sample size, VA (Log Mar), and Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer (RNFL) thickness. PRISMA protocol was used as a guide to perform this meta-analysis. STATA 16 was used for statistical analysis.ResultsA total of 66 eyes were examined in seven papers. Based on the meta-analysis, the mean VA (Log MAR) of patients with optic neuropathy improved from 0.90 to 0.65 following stem cell therapy intervention (p-value = 0.001). The thickness of the RNFLs did not demonstrate a significant change (p-value was 0.174).ConclusionAccording to this systematic review and meta-analysis, stem cell therapy may improve the visual acuity of patients with optic neuropathy. Aside from the traditional therapy that can be provided to patients with optic neuropathy, stem cell therapy may also be beneficial.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12886-024-03588-2.Keywords: Meta-analysis, Stem cell therapy, Optic neuropathy

Background

Visual signals are transmitted from the retina to the cerebral cortex through the optic nerve. Damage to the optic nerve prevents it from transmitting signals to the brain. The optic nerve diseases, including optic neuropathy, also known as optic nerve atrophy, can cause partial or complete vision loss as a result of chronic and degenerative conditions of the optic nerve. There are different kinds of optic neuropathy such as glaucomatous, ischemic, inflammatory, compression, toxic, hereditary, traumatic, and tumor-induced. For instance, it is estimated that 111.8 million people will suffer from glaucoma in the year 2040. Specifically, glaucoma is characterized by the destruction of the retinal ganglion cells (RGCs, ) whose axons form the optic nerve. In the event of an injury, the cells of the RGCs degenerate. Glaucoma is one of the leading causes of RGC degeneration worldwide, and more than 8 million people suffer irreversible bilateral blindness as a result [1–4].

Since the central nervous system (CNS) is incapable of regeneration, any damage to this system results in permanent neurological deficits [5, 6]. Unfortunately, there is currently no effective therapeutic option for the survival and regeneration of RGCs. Several reasons limit RGCs to regenerate their axons and induce the process of apoptosis, including intrinsic factors and an inhibitory CNS environment [1, 2, 6–9]. It has been suggested that regeneration can be improved by shifting the inhibitory environment of the CNS into a permissive one, in addition to stimulating RGCs’ intrinsic regeneration programs [7].

As a promising and rapidly developing area of medicine, cell therapy appears among diverse treatment approaches that encourage neuronal survival and axonal extension. In this way, scientists can develop treatments for various nervous system diseases. Numerous presently incurable degenerative conditions, including glaucoma, may be treatable with stem cell transplantation [7, 10].

Researchers have been exploring different approaches to use cell therapy for this disease, including:

Stem cell transplantation: (a) Stem cells can be differentiated into RGCs and then transplanted into the eye to replace damaged cells and restore vision [11]. (b) Neuroprotective cell therapy: Certain types of cells can be used to protect existing RGCs from further damage, slowing the progression of glaucoma [1, 12]. (c) Gene therapy: Cells can be genetically modified to produce neuroprotective factors or enhance RGC survival [13]. (d) Drug delivery using cells: Modified cells can be used to deliver therapeutic agents directly to the eye, reducing side effects and improving treatment effectiveness [14, 15].

The main effects of cell therapy are to restore the function of the immune system, to replace dead cells, as paracrine-mediated therapies, and to improve tissue tropism. As a treatment for degenerative eye diseases, stem cells (SCs) appear to improve eyesight, the field of vision, and color discrimination capacity in patients with optic nerve injuries or neuropathy. Despite these promising approaches, they cannot easily be translated into the clinic [7].

Several preclinical and clinical studies have investigated the use of embryonic stem cells (ESCs) and induced pluripotent stem cells (iPSCs) to replace dead or diseased retinal cells. However, these cells did not effectively restore retinal connections despite expressing RGC markers [16–20]. Among different cell therapy options for optic nerve disorders, mature stromal cells, such as mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are becoming increasingly popular [20, 21]. There is a wide variety of adult tissues that contain these cells, including blood, skin, dental pulp, bone marrow, adipose tissue, and tissues derived from the umbilical cord [7, 20, 22, 23].

Mesenchymal stem cell transplant has been the most suitable stem cell transplant type for neurological disorders and optic neuropathies because it is immunomodulatory and produces multiple growth factors that can lead to substitution, activate the body’s regenerative properties, and promote cell survival. As well as being free of antigens associated with transplant rejection, such as the major histocompatibility complex, MSCs also lack oncogenic factors [21, 24, 25]. The ability of MSCs to regenerate new neurons and glial cells in the optic nerve and retina makes them beneficial for treating diseases of these organs. There are no ethical concerns about obtaining MSCs as an alternative to ESCs [1, 26, 27].

The visual acuity test which determines the clarity of the patient’s vision is one of the components of a comprehensive ophthalmologic examination. Non-invasive, non-contact optical coherence tomography (OCT) provides real-time, cross-sectional images of the retina and measures the thickness of the Retinal Nerve Fiber Layer (RNFL) Thickness. This test can be used to diagnose and help to manage several retinal and optic nerve diseases. New generations of OCTs can measure not only the thickness of RNFLs, which include RGC axons, blood vessels, and glial tissue but also the thickness of RGCs. As all types of optic neuropathies progress, the thickness of RGCs and their axons decreases, and OCT machines can detect such changes from the early stages of the disease. In optical coherence tomography (OCT), the thickness of the RNFL is determined by analyzing the temporal delay of backscattered light from tissue structures. Low-coherence interferometry determines the echo delay time of backscattered light from retinal layers.

[28, 29].

Currently, no systematic reviews or meta-analyses have been conducted on the impact of stem cell therapy on visual acuity and RNFL thickness in patients with optic neuropathy. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to summarize the evidence available on these outcomes. Based on the results of comparative studies available in the current literature, this systematic review and meta-analysis examined whether SCs implantation improved visual acuity in patients with optic neuropathy (Fig. 1). The paper included seven studies, which demonstrated that MSCs implantation improved visual acuity in patients with optic neuropathy. Our findings suggest that MSCs are beneficial in the treatment of optic neuropathy patients.

Methods

PRISMA protocol was used as a guide to perform this meta-analysis [30]. PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) is a set of evidence-based minimum items that systematic reviewers can use to help them report why systematic reviews were conducted, what the authors did, and what they found in systematic reviews and meta-analyses in a transparent manner [31, 32].

Search strategy

PubMed, google scholar, and Scopus were sought by JUNE 2024. The used keywords were Mesh terms or text words utilizing the following syntax (” cell therapy” OR “stem cell” OR “cell treatment”) AND (glaucoma OR “optic nerve” OR “optic neuropathy” OR “optic nerve injury”).

Eligibility criteria ad extracting data

Criteria for including studies were as follows: (a) patients diagnosed with optic neuropathy; (b) eyes treated with stem cells; (c) visual acuity or RNFL thickness were reported; (d) was published in English. We evaluated the abstracts of studies and investigated their full texts if they were eligible. Afterward, if they were eligible, important variables were extracted. EndNote X9 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) was used to remove duplicate records.

The year of publication, the name of the first author, the sample size, the VA (Log Mar), and the thickness of the RNFL were extracted. Two independent researchers (SD and FA) conducted the searches and extracted data, and disagreements were resolved by the research committee of authors. To improve the accuracy of data extraction, a checklist consisting of the most important variables was developed.

Outcome and effect size

As a result of this study, two outcomes were examined: visual acuity and the thickness of the RNFL. We calculated the effect size based on the mean change in VA and RNFL thickness from baseline to after cell therapy.

Risk of bias and quality control

As part of the quality assessment process, the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) was used [33, 34]. The quality of the papers was moderate because some of the important variables, such as the number of cells, were not stated in the papers.

Statistical methods

We used the statistical package SPSS24 (Statistical Package for Social Science) for the statistical analysis of our data. To describe continuous variables, mean values (SDs) were used while for qualitative variables, frequency values (%) were used. In order to conduct the analysis, each eye was considered as a sample. In order to control the correlation between the subjects of each study, Generalized Estimating Equations (GEEs) were applied. According to this method, the samples (eyes) of each study were considered as a cluster with a specific correlation between them. A statistically significant result was defined as a P-value < 0.05.

In seven papers that met the inclusion criteria (Fig 2), 62 eyes were analyzed. A total of 94 eyes and 66 patients underwent cell therapy.

Discussion

The purpose of this study was to determine whether stem cell therapy could improve visual acuity in patients suffering from optic neuropathy. There have been no systematic reviews or meta-analyses of stem cell therapy for optic neuropathy patients. Based on this meta-analysis, stem cell therapy was found to improve VA values in patients. Seven published studies [21, 35–40] have reported VA changes before and after stem cell therapy, and four published studies [21, 38–40] have reported that there was no difference in RNFL thickness before and after stem cell therapy. After cell therapy, there was no statistically significant change in the RNFL mean thickness. In the VA mean, a change from 0.88 before intervention to 0.64 after cell therapy was observed. VA changes were statistically significant (p-value < 0.001).

Compared with baseline values, patients with optic neuropathy experienced improvements in VA after stem cell therapy. Our meta-analysis shows that stem cell therapy improves visual acuity regardless of age or gender. VA and RNFL thickness were not significantly correlated with age (regression coefficient = 0.01(0.02), p-value = 0.991), regression coefficient = 0.54(0.40), p-value = 0.174). A significant difference was not observed between the mean change in VA between males and females (regression coefficient = 0.26(0.28), p = 0.345). Additionally, the mean change in RNFL thickness did not differ significantly between males and females (regression coefficient = 1.73(4.92), p-value = 0.725). It is not clear whether the improvements in visual acuity reported following stem cell therapy are a direct result of stem cell therapy or whether this process involves more than just the proliferation and differentiation of MSCs. Despite the fact that MSCs are nutritionally beneficial for preventing retinal degeneration and immunosuppression, these cells have low rates of migration and differentiation [23].

While VA improved, RNFL thickness remained unchanged, possibly due to different OCT recording devices. It is important to note, however, that several factors can affect the results, such as the type of cells, their number, and the method of administration. Due to the limited amount of research and the limited number of patient reports regarding stem cell therapy for optic neuropathy, it is not possible to provide information regarding these parameters.

There are many types of ocular disorders that can lead to optic nerve damage, resulting in declines in visual acuity, field defects, and even blindness. The term optic neuropathy is used to describe a wide range of such disorders [36]. Several factors can lead to optic neuropathy, including glaucoma, hereditary neuronal and mitochondrial disorders, inflammation, infection, ischemia, trauma, drug toxicity, and mechanical compression caused by orbital or intracranial lesions [4, 20–22, 39, 43, 44].

Currently, available treatments relieve signs and symptoms of optic neuropathy and are not enough to prevent neurodegenerative processes. Therefore, high-quality rectification by different strategies such as retinal prostheses, and gene and stem cell therapies are urgently needed to promote axonal growth and neuronal survival [9, 21].

Stem cell therapy can replace damaged cells directly by implanting exogenous cells into the affected area. These exogenous SCs are also capable to promote the proliferation, differentiation, and restoration of tissue-resident cells by modifying their microenvironments through the activation of precursor cells [17, 45]. Several studies have shown that SCs protect and regenerate the optic nerve in some pathology cases [46]. Replacement of lost neurons with ESCs, iPSCs, or MSCs has been used to regrowth axons and regenerate neurons [3, 7, 26, 47, 48].

Mesenchymal stem cells, which are mature stromal cells, are becoming increasingly popular as a treatment option for optic nerve disorders [20, 21]. They can restore visual performance (VP) in different ocular degenerative disorders through various therapeutic pathways [49–51]. For example, Hu et al. [52] found that intravitreally transplanted bone marrow MSCs (BMSCs) prevented RGC death in an optic nerve injury model. It is believed that BMMSCs release mRNA, microRNA, proteins, mitochondrial components, and ribosomes through their extracellular vesicles and exosomes. As a consequence, MSCs are able to communicate with niches surrounding them and can activate naive stem cells to proliferate and differentiate [9, 37, 53].

Additionally, adipose MSCs were found to be effective in restoring vision following optic nerve injury, as well as in suppressing inflammation [39]. In 2008, the first steps were taken toward stem cell therapy for neurodegenerative eye diseases. There have been some publications on the treatment of optic neuropathies with stem cells in clinical trials; however, there have only been a relatively small number of patients who have undergone this treatment. The conclusions cannot be validated. During the mentioned studies, MSCs were used to evaluate their safety when injected intravitreally. Intravitreal injections were the most common method of injection. While intravitreal treatments, which follow anti-VEGF therapy procedures, are simple and easy to administer, they are not suitable for inserting cellular materials into the vitreous because the vitreous must remain transparent. By utilizing the MSCs’ secretomes and their active constituents, transplant techniques may be improved, which may help prevent complications related to intravitreal injections of cells, as well as procedures that require retinal interventions [29].

In some trials, bone marrow aspiration is used to obtain autologous cells, which results in a very small amount of MSCs (0.1% of toral harvested cells) [54]; Even so, the fact that only two clinical trials have utilized MSCs derived from adipose tissue [55], is surprising due to the ease of obtaining and the higher concentration of these cells. Only one report has utilized allogenic MSCs from umbilical cords. Adipose tissue-derived MSCs have significant advantages over bone marrow MSCs in terms of the ease of harvesting them from donors, the speed at which they grow, the number of proteins they secrete, and the ability to regulate the immune system [23]. In clinical applications, these characteristics are important. So, ADSCs are more convenient in an outpatient setting and have a higher success rate than alternatives.

There is, however, no information in this report regarding immunosuppressive therapy. In different studies, different cell doses have been used, and these doses differ greatly from one study to the next [56]. No adverse events were reported in these studies after SC therapy for optic neuropathy. These preliminary results suggest that SC therapy is a relatively safe and promising treatment for optic neuropathy; most likely due to the method of implanting the cells. It should be noted, however, that different SC types may require different administration routes.

A consensus has not been reached on how to calculate the number of cells used for intravitreal injections. A mononuclear cell aspirate is typically administered at high doses in clinical studies, whereas enriched purified MSCs administered at much lower doses. There is, however, no information regarding the calculation of cell doses or the process by which cells are manufactured in international clinical trial registries [57].

Further research is required on the effects of various stem cell types, administration methods, and immunosuppression strategies used in this context before the clinical application can be made. Although, Clinical trials are currently underway for some stem cell-based therapies. There have been no reports of adverse proliferation, rejection, or serious ocular or systemic safety concerns [10, 20, 21, 58], but concerns remain about teratoma formation, immune reaction, and tumorigenicity in the long run.

In 2020, Kahramanet et al. examined 29 eyes of 23 patients for optic atrophy following suprachoroidal UC-MSC implantation. A 12-month follow-up showed that suprachoroidal UC-MSC implantation improved BCVA and VF results; however, RNFL thickness measurements did not change significantly [21]. Vilela et al., (2021) report electroretinographic (ERG) results after injecting BMMSCs into each eye of two patients with advanced glaucoma. Neither visual acuity nor visual field were improved after treatment. A patient with proliferative vitreoretinopathy was removed from their study due to retinal detachment [40].

A study conducted by Limoli et al., (2020) included 25 patients (35 eyes) with Glaucomatous optic neuropathy (GON) in progressive disease conditions. Researchers found that both visual performance (VP) and visual acuity (VA) improved significantly after 6 months. BCVA and sensitivity, as well as residual close-up vision, did not significantly increase in the controls (p > 0.5) [37].

In a phase 1 and 2 study, Ayse Oner et al. implanted ADMSCs suprachoroidal in four eyes of four patients who suffer from optic atrophy for a variety of reasons. According to their report, all patients had improved visual acuity, visual field, and mfERG recordings after six months of follow-up. The OCTs of all patients also revealed choroidal thickening. Improvements were still encouraging despite a small sample size. Their study demonstrated that suprachoroidal delivery of ADMSCs can effectively treat optic nerve diseases that lack curative treatment currently [39].

In a Stem Cell Ophthalmology Treatment Study (SCOTS), Jeffrey N. Weiss (2015) presented the results of a patient with relapsing optic neuropathy treated with autologous BMSC. BMSCs in the right eye were injected into the optic nerve, and in the left eye, they were injected retrobulbar, subtenonally, and intravitreally. After 15 months of SCOTS, the patient’s left and right visual acuities improved to 20/20 and 20/150, respectively. Bilateral visual fields showed significant improvement. At 3 and 6 months after SCOTS, the thickness of the macular and fast retinal nerve fiber layers was maximally improved. In addition, the patient reduced her mycophenylate dose from 1,500 mg to 500 mg per day and did not require steroid pulse therapy [35]. In another report by Weiss (2015), they presented the results of one patient with optic neuropathy treated within the SCOTS, which used BMSCs to treat optic nerve diseases. Following four months of treatment in SCOTS, the central visual acuity had improved to 20/100 OD and 20/40 OS for both eyes [36].

Ten patients with sequential non-arthritic ischemic optic neuropathy were treated with BMSCs in the SCOTS study conducted by Weiss et al. in 2017. After SCOTS, 80% of patients improved in VA (P = 0.029) while 20% remained stable; 73.6% of eyes treated gained vision (P = 0.019), while 15.9% remained stable. Improvements typically appear six months after surgery. BMSCs could secrete proteins and hormones via paracrine mechanisms, mitochondrial transfer, messenger RNA release via exosomes, or transdifferentiate into neurons via exosomes [38].

Limitation

It is important to note that this study had some limitations. Some clinical trials using stem cells, including MSCs, are currently recruiting patients or are in the early stages of development. It is imperative to note that Clinicaltrials.gov sometimes does not provide information regarding how cells are obtained or dosages used in clinical trials. In addition, there are insufficient published studies demonstrating the effectiveness of these cells in treating retinal and optic nerve diseases [18]. There were few studies included in this meta-analysis, with small sample sizes, including patients who had poor vision and were legally blind, which made it difficult to measure visual function accurately, increasing the risk of bias. There was no comparison of stem cell types in the meta-analysis. Further studies are required regarding the types of stem cells, the number of cells injected, the methods of administration, and immunosuppression strategies in this context. Among the limitations of this study was the lack of information on the exact OCT device used for the test in the included studies. There were only three studies that mentioned the name of the OCT device ((Heidelberg Engineering, Heidelberg, Germany) [39, 40]) and ((Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany) [37]). In light of this, we were unable to determine precisely why there was no significant change in the RNFL thickness in the retina. It is important to note that our study did not include additional parameters such as ERG (Electroretinogram), OCT (Optical Coherence Tomography), IOP (Intraocular Pressure), and choroidal thickness. These parameters are crucial for a comprehensive assessment of stem cell therapy efficacy. Future studies should aim to incorporate these parameters to provide a more complete analysis of treatment outcomes.

There have been very few studies and patient reports on stem cell therapy for optic neuropathy, so we cannot provide information on these parameters at this time. There were no reported ocular or systemic adverse events, indicating that more research is needed to determine whether stem cell therapy is safe; since long-term outcomes were not available, the study did not assess them.

Conclusion

The development of SC therapy trials is essential to understand its beneficial clinical practice. Up to now, treatments based on stem cells have shown promising results in optic nerve diseases for which there are currently no curative treatment options. The VA outcomes of patients with optic neuropathy may be improved by stem cell therapy, according to the data. We believe that stem cell therapies will play an increasing role in the treatment of degenerative retinal and optic nerve diseases in the near future. It will, however, take a considerable amount of effort to reach phases III and IV of clinical trials. In order for the successful implementation of SCs more evidence and strict guidelines are needed. To move forward, several factors need to be addressed, including unified dose criteria. The second recommendation is to use allogeneic MSCs in cryopreservation processes more frequently since MSCs are immune-privileged and do not reject other cells. Further studies are needed to determine long-term effects, the method of administration, and the approach to personalized treatment. In the future, studies should be conducted to examine stem cell therapy mechanisms, compare the efficacy and safety of stem cell types, and test stem cell-derived exosomes for optic neuropathy treatment.
