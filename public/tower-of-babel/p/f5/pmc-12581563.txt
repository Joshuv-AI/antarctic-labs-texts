The role of dynamic larynx CT in the investigation of persistent hoarseness following viral infections
Authors: Yong Wang, Ziying Lin, Guanbin Zhu, Hua Zhong, Dongjian Xiao, Yanli Ma, Peiyun Zhuang, Dairong Cao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo preliminarily investigate the value of the dynamic larynx CT manifestations in predicting the laryngeal nerve damage and the treatment effectiveness, in patients experiencing hoarseness following viral infection.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, consecutive participants were enrolled between September 2020 and December 2024. All participants with hoarseness following viral infection underwent primary laryngoscopy and dynamic larynx CT scans. Based on classification criteria that assess morphological and positional changes of the vocal fold on both the maximum open and minimum closed phase images, vocal fold deformation types were categorized into three types (I-III). LEMG with interval less than 2 days was performed to determine the laryngeal nerve damage. Non-parametric tests and chi-square tests were used for univariate analyses. Kappa tests were used for consistency analyses. Linear regression was used to fit the data.ResultsA total of 42 participants (Median age, 47.5 years; 17 men) were included. There was a moderate consistency between Type III and posterior cricoarytenoid muscle (PCA) injury (k = 0.508, p < 0.001) or multiple nerve branch injuries (k = 0.646, p < 0.001). Type II vocal fold deformation patients had significantly shorter recovery time than type III (2.0 vs. 5.0 months, p < 0.001). Recovery time greater than 3 months was more common in type III than type II (15.0 vs. 3.0 months, p = 0.003).ConclusionIn patients experiencing hoarseness following viral infection, the Classification of vocal fold deformation based on dynamic CT may have preliminary potential value in predicting the laryngeal nerve damage and the treatment effect, but larger multi-center studies are needed to confirm these findings.Trial registrationIRB-2020-019, 25-Mar-2020.Keywords: Virus, CT Cine, Three-dimensional reconstruction, Vocal fold paralysis

Background

Various viral infection is still a global health problem. For example, according to the report of the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention, the weekly positive rate of SARS-CoV-2 ranged from 1.3% to 21.1% in 2024 [1]. Previous reports have shown that the incidence of dysphonia in viral infection patients can be as high as 79% [2]. Dysphonia following viral infection may have multiple causes including intubation injury, sensory neuropathy, and postviral neuropathy [3]. Vocal fold dyskinesia, as one of the most important kind of the voice disease, there are different therapeutic directions according to different causes. Vocal fold paralysis (including viral infection) is one of the main causes of unilateral vocal fold dyskinesia, often manifested clinically as hoarseness.

At present, primary laryngoscopy was the initial measure and laryngeal electromyography (LEMG) was the main clinical method for the diagnosis of vocal fold dyskinesia but LEMG hold numerous contraindications (such as Coagulation disorders, ‌Implanted electronic devices, Local infections or skin lesions, et al. ) and are invasive, which shall not be conducted many times [4, 5]. LEMG was not available in all clinics and countries. Meanwhile, dynamic laryngeal CT may be promising in this field, yet its diagnostic value for laryngeal nerve injuries has not been adequately investigated [6–10]. Dynamic laryngeal CT can record the spatial characteristics of laryngeal structures during phonation and enhance the diagnostic rate compared to strobe laryngoscopy. CT scanning with cine mode combined with three-dimensional (3D) reconstruction can display the changes of larynx structures in vocal fold dyskinesia, and can be used for the differential diagnosis of arytenoid cartilage subluxation and vocal fold paralysis.

The purpose of this study is to explore the value of CT classification of vocal fold deformation in hoarseness patients after various viral infection using spectrum CT Cine mode scanning combined with 3D reconstruction images.

Methods

Participants

General Information: In our one prospective study, a total of 604 consecutive participants diagnosed with suspicious vocal fold dyskinesia and underwent dynamic CT examination of the larynx were enrolled between April 2019 and December 2024. The aim was to evaluate the imaging differences and characteristics of unilateral vocal fold dyskinesia caused by different etiologies. This study selected the patients among them who suffer from various viral infection as the etiology. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are shown in Fig. 1. Finally, 42 participants with hoarseness and 1 patient with foreign body sensation in the throat after various viral infection were collected from September 2020 to December 2024. All participants underwent dynamic larynx CT examination and LEMG at our hospital.

Flowchart of participants’ inclusion and exclusion criteria

CT scanning

CT acquisitions with cine mode on Revolution CT scanner (GE, Healthcare) were performed when the patients were asked to breathe deeply and then make “Yi” sound continuously. The following parameters were used: scanning time 5.6 s, tube voltage 70 kV, rotation time 0.28 s, reconstruction image thickness 1.25 mm, scanning range 80 mm (from 2nd vertebral body to 6th vertebral body). The effective radiation dose was about 2 to 3 mSv.

Post-processing and image evaluation

CT images of all participants were transferred to an AW4.7 workstation (GE Healthcare). The data were used to generate the 20-phase images, and the images at a maximum glottis-opening phase and a minimum glottis-closing phase were selected for further processing. The generated images included the following: a multiplanar reformation (MPR) image of an oblique horizontal position on the glottal plane, an MPR image of an oblique coronal position at the 1/3 junction of the anterior and middle vocal folds, a thin maximum intensity projection (MIP) image of the cricoarytenoid joint in oblique coronal and sagittal positions, a volume rendering (VR) image of the cricoarytenoid joint, and a virtual endoscopy (VE) image of the glottis. Furthermore, the following parameters were measured for the differential diagnosis of vocal fold dyskinesia in all 604 participants: the length of the vocal fold, the width of the glottis, and the subglottal convergence angle, the anteversion angle, the elevation angle, and the valgus angle, as well as the varus angle of the cricoarytenoid joints. Meanwhile, based on the purpose of this sub study, the oblique coronal MPR images of the 1/3 junction of the anterior and middle vocal fold were mainly selected for further evaluation. According to the morphological and positional changes of the bilateral vocal folds on these images, the types of vocal fold deformation were classified into Type I, Type II and Type III [9].

Two radiologists, Z.L. and Y.W., with over 5 and 17 years of experience, respectively, independently evaluated the CT images while blinded to the clinical diagnosis. They conducted measurements and classified the cases into Type I, Type II, and Type III vocal fold deformation types using the criteria outlined below. Any discrepancies in classification between the two radiologists were resolved by a third chief radiologist, D.C., who has over 30 years of experience in head and neck radiology.

Classification criteria

Type I vocal fold deformation is characterized by the sectional vocal fold resembling an inclined triangle or vertical line during the opening phase in oblique coronal images, while exhibiting deformation—in the form of thickening or a shape resembling an inverted trapezoid—during the closed phase. This type maintains a normal pattern of glottal opening and closing.

Type II vocal fold deformation is defined by a fixed position of the vocal fold, with no obvious movement observed during the opening and closed phases; however, the sectional vocal fold shows deformation in the closed phase.

Type III vocal fold deformation is defined by a fixed position of the vocal fold, with no obvious movement observed during the opening and closed phases, and the sectional vocal fold exhibits no obvious deformation during the closed phase (Fig. 2).

a. On the oblique coronal image of the glottis opening phase, the cross-sectional shape of the vocal folds appears as an oblique triangle or vertical line. On the closed phase, there is deformation in the vocal fold section (thickening of the vocal fold thickness or inverted trapezoid shape), and the glottis opening and closing are normal, with bilateral vocal fold deformation of type I; b. In the opening and closed phases, the position of the left vocal fold is fixed or there is no significant movement, but in the closed phase, the left vocal fold section undergoes the above-mentioned deformation. The left vocal fold is the affected side, with type II deformation. The right vocal fold is the healthy side. c. In the opening and closed phases, the position of the right vocal fold is fixed or shows no significant movement, and there is no obvious deformation in the closed phase. The right vocal fold is the affected side, with type III deformation. The left vocal fold is the healthy side

LEMG examination and clinical treatment

All participants were suggested to perform LEMG examination for three key laryngeal muscles bilaterally: Cricothyroid muscle (CT) - innervated by the external branch of superior laryngeal nerve (eSLN); Thyroarytenoid (TA) and posterior cricoarytenoid (PCA) muscles - receiving dominant innervation from the anterior and posterior branch of recurrent laryngeal nerve (‌Abr-RLN and Pbr-RLN), respectively. The location and number of injured nerve branch, pre-treatment hoarseness time and voice recovery time after voice training and neuronutrition treatment of all participants were recorded. The recovery time was defined by patient-reported or clinician-assessed. The adjusted recovery time, calculated by adding or subtracting pre-treatment hoarseness time.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0.1.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and STATA version 15. Continuous variables were summarized as median (interquartile range), and categorical variables were summarized as counts (percentages). Non-parametric tests and chi-square tests were used, as appropriate, for univariate analyses. Kappa tests were used for consistency analyses. Linear regression was used to fit the data. Violin plots and Sankey diagram were used for data visualization. A two-sided p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Due to this being a preliminary exploratory study and the inability to mandate throat CT scans for patients with hoarseness after viral infection, the sample size was not calculated.

Results

Figure 1 presents the flowchart of study participants. During the enrollment period, totally 604 patients with suspected vocal dyskinesia and dynamic CT examination of the larynx were enrolled between April 2019 and December 2024. Exclusions included 187 individuals with a benign or malignant lesion of larynx or head and neck regions, 135 individuals with arytenoid subluxation, 91 individuals with a benign or malignant lesion of thoracic aorta or lung and mediastinum regions. Of the remaining patients, 62 individuals were excluded due to without vocal fold dyskinesia, 86 with undetermined etiology.

Finally, we enrolled 43 study participants who experienced hoarseness following various viral infection between September 2020 and December 2024. Among these 43 patients in this group, 42 (median age, 47.5 years [IQR, 39]; 17 females, 25 males) developed hoarseness due to viral infection, while another 1 patient reported a foreign body sensation in the throat. A total of 40 patients underwent LEMG examination within 2 days post-CT examination, and of these, 36 cases were diagnosed with varying degrees of nerve damage as confirmed by LEMG, including the one patient with foreign body sensation in the throat. Patient characteristics are presented in the Table 1. The duration of hoarseness prior to seeking medical attention after viral infection ranged from 3 days to 120 months, with an average of 7.7 months (median, 2 months [IQR, 4.25]).

One case exhibited vocal fold deformation type I, with no significant vocal fold dyskinesia observed on imaging and no obvious abnormalities in glottal opening and closing; Clinically, the patient reported only a foreign body sensation in the throat. LEMG revealed PCA injury on the affected side. Additionally, there were 18 cases of vocal fold deformation type II and 24 cases of type III within this group (Table 1).

The recovery time were longer for patients with vocal fold deformation type III compared to type II generally. However, there was no statistically significant difference in pre-treatment hoarseness duration between type II and type III patients (2.0 vs. 2.5 months, p = 0.908). For the vocal fold deformation type II group, the recovery time of hoarseness symptoms after treatment ranged from 10 days to 6 months, with an average of 2.2 months (median, 2.0 months [IQR, 2.1]). In contrast, the recovery time in type III group ranged from 15 day to 12 months, with an average of 4.9 months (median, 5.0 months [IQR, 3.0]). Patients with type II vocal fold deformation had significantly shorter recovery time compared to those with type III (2.0 vs. 5.0 months, p < 0.001). Moreover, recovery times exceeding 3 months were more prevalent in type III patients than in type II patients (15.0 vs. 3.0 months, p = 0.003). Linear regression indicates that, for vocal fold deformation types II and III with the same pre-treatment hoarseness duration, the recovery time for type III is longer than that for type II. The adjusted recovery time, calculated by adding or subtracting pre-treatment hoarseness time, was also longer in type III patients compared to type II patients (6.0 vs. 3.8 months, p = 0.019 or 1.0 vs. -1.1 months, p = 0.015, respectively) (Tables 1 and 2; Figs. 3 and 4).

Discussion

At present, primary laryngoscopy was the initial measure and LEMG was the only method that can truly provide evidence and the extent of nerve damage for the diagnosis of vocal fold dyskinesia. However, factors such as invasiveness, several relative complications, and expert experience limit the widespread application of LEMG. In contrast, with the benefits of easy for examiners, reproducible, fast, non-invasive for patients, exclusion of tumors in the course of vagal nerve in the same examination, dynamic laryngeal CT has emerged as a promising diagnostic tool. However, besides of radiation exposure and patient compliance, its diagnostic criteria and clinical value remain inadequately explored. Our study preliminarily indicates that among patients experiencing hoarseness after various viral infection, there was a moderate consistency between Type III vocal fold deformation and multiple nerve branch injuries (k = 0.646, p < 0.001). Furthermore, patients with Type II deformation exhibited a significantly shorter recovery time compared to those with type III deformation (2.0 vs. 5.0 months, p < 0.001). The adjusted recovery time, calculated by adding or subtracting pre-treatment hoarseness time, was also longer in type III patients compared to type II patients (6.0 vs. 3.8 months, p = 0.019 or 1.0 vs. -1.1 months, p = 0.015, respectively).

Recovery time add pre-treatment hoarseness time was calculated for evaluating whole duration of the disease, in other words, the longer the recovery time add pre-treatment hoarseness time is, the longer the patient suffers from hoarseness. Recovery time subtract pre-treatment hoarseness time was calculated as adjusted recovery time, so as to diminish the influence of pre-treatment hoarseness time on recovery time and better depict the recovery process. Given the same recovery time, the adjusted recovery time is shorter when pre-treatment hoarseness time is longer, suggesting faster improvement.

With the development of Voice Medicine in recent years, patients with hoarseness following viral infection have received increasing attention. Idiopathic vocal fold paralysis is usually attributed to neuropathy resulting from viral infections [11–14]. Increasing evidences suggests that neurotropic is a common feature associated with coronaviruses [15–20].

Previous studies have classified unilateral vocal fold paralysis into three types (A, B, and C) based on the morphological characteristics of the vocal folds [21]. However, these three types share a common trait: that is no thickening of the affected vocal folds during the vocal phase. An earlier investigation proposed various methods for classifying vocal fold deformations and applied these classifications to differentiate between vocal fold paralysis and arytenoid cartilage subluxation [9]. Vocal fold deformation type I is typically observed in the healthy vocal folds, while Type III is more commonly found on the affected side in cases of vocal fold paralysis. Patients with arytenoid subluxation often exhibit vocal fold deformation type II on the affected side; however, our ongoing research has revealed that some patients with nerve injury may also exhibit type II deformation.

In this study, Type II individuals often present with damage to a single nerve branch, while type III patients typically exhibit damage to multiple branches. Rapoport et al. [14] reported that among the four patients with vocal fold paralysis due to SARS-CoV-2 infection, the superior and recurrent laryngeal nerve were involved at the same time. Krasnodębska et al. [22] found that a moderate correlation between the damage degree of CT muscle and the severity of speech disorders (r = 0.46). Theoretically, virus-related injury could may affect various segments of the vagal nerve, leading to varying degrees of damage to both the SLN and RLN. The RLN contains not only motor fibers, but also sensory fibers [23]. Following injury, regenerated motor fibers may be connected to non-motor fibers and only a limited number of regenerated nerve fibers successfully reach the peripheral myelin sheath side [24]. Furthermore, even after nerve reinnervation, muscle contraction may not fully recover [25]. Therefore, the hoarseness duration and the timeliness of treatment can have a certain impact on the speed of nerve injury recovery. Our findings indicate that patients with type II and a pre-treatment hoarseness duration less than 3 months had a markedly shorter recovery period post-treatment compared to those with type III and a hoarseness duration exceeding 3 months. Previous animal studies have also suggested [26–29] that the therapeutic effect diminish with prolonged treatment time.

Sunderland classified peripheral nerve injury into five grades (I-V) [30]. As the severity of peripheral nerve damage increases, the extent of nerve injury progresses from mild to severe. The vocal fold deformation types II and III that we have identified may present the external manifestations of this range of nerve damage. Yang et al. [31] found that in a mouse model of nerve injury caused by viral infection, demyelination predominates in the early stages, followed by axonal injury. Based on previous findings and the results of this study, we propose two possible mechanisms for type II vocal fold deformation resulting from nerve injury: (a). partial injury to the RLN, where residual nerve impulses are insufficient for normal vocal fold movements; and (b). predominant damage to the myelin sheath of the nerve, resulting in slowed conduction velocity, reduced action potential amplitude, and abnormal vocal fold movements. Patients with Type II deformation and LEMG (-) who exhibited shorter disease course may experience predominantly simple myelin sheath injury, resulting in a significant therapeutic recovery.

Limitations: Firstly, the small sample size and its impact on statistical power and generalizability, larger sample study should be further performed for generalization and sub-group analysis. Secondly, potential selection bias due to the single-center design and exclusion criteria. Thirdly, the incomplete LEMG data (3 missing cases) and its potential effect on the consistency analysis. Fourthly, The broad range of pre-treatment hoarseness duration (3 days to 120 months), which may introduce confounding factors in recovery time analysis.

Dynamic CT, along with the classification of vocal fold deformation, has diagnostic potential in predicting the extent of laryngeal nerve damage and the effectiveness of treatment in patients experiencing vocal fold dyskinesia following viral infection, but larger multi-center studies are needed to confirm these findings.
