Renal Impairment Does Not Affect Pharmacokinetics, Safety or Tolerability of Zenagamtide
Authors: Lykke Ida Kaas Oldenburg, Sine Pfeiffer Haugaard, Tobias Karlsson, Victoria Kegel‐Hübner, Mahera Parwez, Christoffer Wiingaard, Anne Flint
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimsZenagamtide is a novel, unimolecular glucagon‐like peptide‐1 and amylin receptor agonist in development for weight management and Type 2 diabetes. This study investigated the pharmacokinetic (PK) properties, safety and tolerability of zenagamtide in participants with various degrees of renal impairment versus participants with normal renal function.Materials and MethodsAdults with body mass index 20.0–39.9 kg/m2 were categorised based on baseline renal status using the CKD‐EPI Collaboration creatinine equation (2021); normal function, mild impairment, moderate impairment, severe impairment and end‐stage renal disease (ESRD). Participants received a single subcutaneous dose of zenagamtide 0.3 mg, followed by a 4‐week follow‐up period. The primary endpoint was area under the zenagamtide plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity (AUC0–∞). Additional endpoints included maximum observed plasma zenagamtide concentration (C max) and number of treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs).ResultsIn total, 42 participants were included (n = 14, normal renal function group; n = 7 per renal impairment group). The range (geometric mean) of zenagamtide AUC0–∞ and C max across renal impairment groups were 520–715 h × nmol/L and 2.8–3.5 nmol versus 543 h × nmol/L and 3.2 nmol/L for the normal renal function group, respectively. Overall, TEAEs were reported in 30 (71.4%) participants. TEAEs were non‐serious, none were severe and the majority were mild and most frequently gastrointestinal in nature.ConclusionsRenal impairment did not appear to have a clinically relevant impact on the PK or safety profile of zenagamtide, suggesting that dose adjustment of zenagamtide is not warranted in this population. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT06559527.Keywords: pharmacokinetics, renal impairment, safety, zenagamtide

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic metabolic disease that significantly increases the risk of developing obesity‐related complications such as Type 2 diabetes (T2D), cardiovascular (CV) disease and osteoarthritis [1, 2]. Many individuals living with obesity and/or T2D either currently have, or may experience, progression to different stages of renal impairment [3, 4, 5]. Both obesity and T2D are key risk factors that can independently, or synergistically, work to accelerate the progression of various stages of chronic kidney disease (CKD) [3, 4, 5].

Regulatory authorities recommend assessing how renal impairment affects the pharmacokinetic (PK) properties of a new drug molecule as this may indicate the need for dose adjustment in these populations [6, 7]. The effects of renal impairment can impact not only renal excretion but may also alter PK processes including metabolism and active transport of a drug in the kidney, gut or liver and may further affect absorption, tissue distribution and plasma protein binding [6, 7]. Decline in renal function may be associated with decreased drug clearance resulting in increased free drug exposure, leading to an increased risk of toxicity and adverse drug reactions. Therapies may also become less effective when renal function is impaired or dose reductions are excessive [8].

Glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists (GLP‐1RAs) and amylin receptor (AMYR) agonists offer significant beneficial effects in obesity and T2D, including substantial weight loss, improving glucose control, and reducing CV disease, heart failure and CKD [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15]. Previous studies investigating the GLP‐1RA and AMYR agonist drug classes revealed no clinically relevant differences in PK between participants with normal renal function compared with participants with renal impairment following treatment with the AMYR agonists pramlintide [16] and cagrilintide [17], the GLP‐1RA semaglutide [18] or the dual glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide receptor agonist (GIP‐RA) and GLP‐1RA tirzepatide [19]. In fact, GLP‐1RAs have demonstrated renoprotective effects in large clinical trials, including reductions in albuminuria, a lower risk of new‐onset albuminuria [20] and a reduction in the rate of estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline [21, 22]. Furthermore, once‐weekly semaglutide treatment in the SELECT and FLOW trials revealed consistent benefits on major kidney outcomes, including a reduction in the rate of eGFR decline, major adverse cardiovascular events and all‐cause mortality in participants with overweight/obesity and established cardiovascular disease without diabetes [10, 23], and T2D and CKD, respectively [12, 24]. In addition, a reduced rate of eGFR decline and reduced albuminuria have also been observed in individuals with T2D and high CV risk, who received the dual GIP and GLP‐1 receptor agonist tirzepatide [13].

Zenagamtide, previously known as amycretin (NNC0487‐0111), is a long‐acting unimolecular GLP‐1RA and AMYR agonist, designed to leverage the effects of the two core biologies [25, 26]. Oral and subcutaneous (s.c.) formulations of zenagamtide are currently under investigation for weight management and T2D. In a first‐in‐human phase 1 clinical study, daily oral administration of zenagamtide in participants with overweight or obesity appeared safe and tolerable, with treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs) that were predominantly mild or moderate in severity and gastrointestinal in nature [26], in line with the GLP‐1RA and AMYR agonist drug classes [15, 27, 28, 29]. Oral zenagamtide led to a mean reduction in body weight of up to 13.1% (2 × 50 mg zenagamtide) at 12 weeks, compared with 1.2% with placebo [26]. In a phase 1b/2a study, once‐weekly, s.c. administration of up to zenagamtide 60 mg in participants with overweight or obesity also appeared safe and tolerable, consistent with GLP‐1RAs and AMYR agonists, with no new safety signals observed and led to significant, dose‐dependent reductions in body weight of up to 24.3% compared with 1.1% with placebo, which seemed to have not reached a nadir after 36 weeks of treatment [25]. These results supported continued investigation of zenagamtide in relevant study populations.

Here, we investigate the PK properties, safety and tolerability of zenagamtide in participants with various degrees of renal impairment compared with participants with normal renal function and assess whether renal impairment necessitates dose adjustment.

Materials and Methods

An interventional, single‐centre, single‐dose, open‐label, parallel‐group study investigated the PK, safety and tolerability of a single s.c. dose of zenagamtide 0.3 mg in participants with varying degrees of renal impairment compared with participants with normal renal function (NCT06559527). The study was conducted at a single centre at Charité Research Organization GmbH, Berlin, Germany between 20 August 2024 and 24 February 2025. The zenagamtide peptide is acylated with a C18 diacid‐based sidechain at the C‐terminus of the GLP‐1 part of the molecule at position K37, enabling reversible albumin binding and providing a long systemic half‐life [26]. Study design is shown in Figure S1. The study protocol was approved by the relevant institutional review board/independent ethics committee prior to initiation. All participants provided written, informed consent prior to any study‐related activities. The study was conducted in accordance with applicable laws and regulations, and international guidelines including the Declaration of Helsinki and applicable International Conference on Harmonisation Good Clinical Practice Guideline.

Study Population

Male or female participants aged 18–80 years, with a body mass index 20.0–39.9 kg/m2 and a body weight > 60.0 kg were eligible for inclusion. Participants with normal renal function were eligible if considered healthy based on the medical history, physical examination and results of vital signs, electrocardiogram (ECG) and clinical laboratory tests performed during the screening visit, as judged by the investigator. Participants with impaired renal function were excluded if they had previously undergone renal transplantation or had unstable renal function or acute exacerbation of renal disease within 14 days of baseline, as indicated by recent history or worsening of clinical or laboratory signs. Participants requiring dialysis treatment were on haemodialysis and able to undergo scheduled treatment according to the standard clinical regimen throughout the study period. Diabetes is the most common cause of end‐stage renal disease (ESRD), with an estimated 40% of people living with T2D also presenting diabetic kidney disease [30]. Participants with diabetes were permitted for inclusion in the renal impairment groups as representative of the real‐world patient population. A glycated haemoglobin ≥ 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) at screening was an exclusion criteria specific to the normal renal function group only, to provide a healthy reference population.

Participants were allocated into groups according to renal function using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration creatinine equation (2021) [31], adjusted for estimated individual body surface area [32]: normal renal function (eGFR ≥ 90 mL/min), mild renal impairment (eGFR 60–89 mL/min), moderate renal impairment (eGFR 30–59 mL/min), severe renal impairment (eGFR < 30 mL/min, not requiring dialysis) and ESRD (requiring dialysis). As per U.S. Food and Drug Administration guidelines [7], renal impairment groups (mild, moderate and severe) were matched to the extent possible with the reference population (participants with normal renal function) with respect to age, sex, race and body weight. Recruitment of participants with renal impairment was initiated prior to participants with normal renal function. For participants with ESRD, administration of zenagamtide occurred at least 24 h prior to the next planned dialysis session. To eliminate potential variation between different types of dialysis membrane, only high‐flux polysulfone membranes were permitted.

Study Design

The study consisted of a screening visit performed up to 28 days prior to the in‐house visit which consisted of a 5‐night in‐house period (Day −1 to Day 5) with a single drug administration of s.c. zenagamtide 0.3 mg at site on Day 1. Zenagamtide was administered in a skinfold in the abdomen using a NovoPen 4 by qualified staff at the clinic. Drug administration was followed by a 4‐week follow‐up period, which included zenagamtide PK blood sampling and safety assessments, including six ambulatory follow‐up visits (Days 7 to 22) and a single end‐of‐study (EOS) visit (Day 28). PK samples were collected at six timepoints on Day 1 (from 09:00 to 22:00), three timepoints on Day 2 (from 02:00 to 22:00), two timepoints on Day 3 (10:00 and 22:00) and a single timepoint for all remaining visit days (10:00). Laboratory assessments were carried out at screening, Days −1, 1, 2, 3, 4 and EOS visit. Vital signs were collected at screening and on Days −1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9, 11, 15, 22 and EOS visit. ECG was conducted at screening and on Days −1, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 11 and EOS visit. Physical examination was performed at screening, Day −1 and EOS visit. For visits requiring fasting, participants were required to fast for at least 8 h prior to the assessment in question, with water intake allowed.

Endpoints and Statistical Analysis

The primary and secondary objectives, respectively, were to compare the PK properties and assess the safety and tolerability of zenagamtide 0.3 mg in participants with various degrees of renal impairment and normal renal function. The primary study endpoint was area under the zenagamtide plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity after a single dose (AUC0–∞). Supportive secondary endpoints were maximum observed plasma zenagamtide concentration (C max), time to C max (t max), terminal half‐life of zenagamtide (t 1/2), apparent volume of distribution of zenagamtide (V z /F) and apparent clearance of zenagamtide (CL/F), all after a single dose. The number of TEAEs was a supportive endpoint. All endpoints were measured from baseline (Day 1, pre‐dose) until completion of the EOS visit (Day 28), except the number of TEAEs, which was measured from the time of trial‐product administration (Day 1) until completion of the EOS visit. Additional safety information, including laboratory assessments, ECGs and vital signs, was also collected.

The full analysis set and safety analysis set comprised all participants who were exposed to study intervention and were used to analyse endpoints related to the PK objectives and assessments related to safety, respectively. Adverse events were coded using the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 27.1. For statistical comparison of normal renal function versus each renal impairment group, AUC0–∞ and C max were log‐transformed and analysed using a normal linear model with renal function group and sex as categorical factors and age and log‐body weight at baseline as continuous covariates. Estimated treatment ratios (ETRs) for renal impairment groups versus normal renal function group were estimated with 90% confidence intervals (CIs). TEAEs and other secondary endpoints are summarised descriptively by renal function group. The sample size was selected to have at least 12 study completers with normal renal function and six completers in each of the renal impairment groups. Sample size was not based on a formal power calculation but is within the standard range of participants in studies investigating PK in participants with renal impairment [7], per regulatory guidance [6], and was considered adequate to support the primary objective of the study. The study was not powered for statistical comparison of PK parameters between normal renal function and renal impairment groups.

Plasma Bioanalysis

The PK assessment of zenagamtide plasma samples was performed by Celerion Switzerland AG (Fehraltorf, Switzerland). Venous blood samples were collected in K2‐EDTA Vacutainer tubes, processed and stored at −20°C until analysis. The concentration of zenagamtide in human plasma (EDTA) samples was determined by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry by Celerion Switzerland AG, in accordance with the Swiss Ordinance relating to Good Laboratory Practice.

Results

In total, 42 participants were exposed to treatment (full analysis set and safety analysis set: normal renal function, n = 14; mild renal impairment, n = 7; moderate renal impairment, n = 7; severe renal impairment, n = 7; ESRD, n = 7). All 42 participants completed the study. Demographics and baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Participants were predominantly White (97.6%) and non‐Hispanic or Latino (97.6%) and higher proportions of male participants were observed in the severe renal impairment (71.4%) and ESRD (85.7%) groups. Five individuals in the renal impairment groups presented with T2D at screening (mild renal impairment: n = 1, moderate renal impairment: n = 4). Baseline characteristics were generally similar amongst the renal impairment groups, except eGFR, which decreased with increasing renal impairment.

Pharmacokinetics

Geometric mean concentration–time profiles of plasma zenagamtide are shown in Figure 1. Mean observed AUC0–∞ ranged from 520 to 715 nmol × h/L across all groups. Participants with impaired renal function (moderate, severe and ESRD groups) presented higher observed values for total zenagamtide exposure (AUC0–∞) compared to participants with normal renal function (Figure 2A). For estimated values, there was a trend towards higher geometric mean AUC0–∞ with increasing renal impairment across mild, moderate and severe renal impairment groups; the ETRs (90% CI) of the mean AUC0–∞ of zenagamtide in each of the renal impairment groups compared to the normal renal function group were 1.13 (1.01, 1.26) for the mild renal impairment group, 1.32 (1.12, 1.56) for the moderate renal impairment group, 1.35 (1.19, 1.53) for the severe renal impairment group and 1.27 (1.08, 1.49) for the ESRD group (Table 2). Geometric mean observed C max ranged from 2.8 to 3.5 nmol across all groups. ETRs indicated an increased C max in the renal impairment groups versus the normal renal function group, with no consistent pattern across renal impairment groups (Table 2, Figure 2B). Median t max was approximately 48 h in the normal renal function and mild, moderate and severe renal impairment groups compared with 36 h in the ESRD group. Geometric mean t 1/2 tended to be longer in the renal impairment groups compared to normal renal function (range in renal impairment groups: 94.0–112.8 h versus normal renal function: 88.1 h). CL/F and V z /F showed no consistent pattern across groups (Table 2).

Safety

A total of 81 TEAEs were reported in 30 (71.4%) participants; 34 events in 10 participants with normal renal function (71.4%), nine events in four participants with mild renal impairment (57.1%), nine events in five participants with moderate renal impairment (71.4%), eight events in five participants with severe renal impairment (71.4%) and 21 events in six participants with ESRD (85.7%). All TEAEs were mild or moderate in severity, with the majority presenting as mild. No serious TEAEs, TEAEs leading to study withdrawal or deaths were reported (Table 3). The most frequent TEAEs were within the System Organ Class (SOC) gastrointestinal disorders, with 38 TEAEs reported in 18 (42.9%) participants, driven by nausea and vomiting both with nine events reported in seven (16.7%) participants and the SOC metabolism and nutrition disorders with 17 events of decreased appetite reported in 17 participants (40.5%) (Table 3). Nine events of vomiting were reported in seven participants (normal renal function: four events in three participants; ESRD: five events in four participants), with no events observed in the mild, moderate and severe renal impairment groups. Overall, the reporting of TEAEs was generally similar with no clear pattern across the renal impairment groups and participants with normal renal function. No clinically relevant findings were observed for laboratory parameters, ECG or vital signs.

Discussion

The results of this study showed a general trend of higher AUC with more severe renal impairment compared to normal renal function, and an increase in C max in renal impairment groups compared to the normal renal function group. However, the magnitude of these differences was deemed not to be of clinical significance. Furthermore, no safety or tolerability issues were identified regarding the use of a single dose of zenagamtide 0.3 mg in participants with renal impairment. Within the context of previous data demonstrating dose proportionality of zenagamtide across a wide dose range [25], these results suggest that dose adjustment of zenagamtide is not warranted in people with renal impairment.

Renal impairment has been shown to significantly impact renal clearance and alter the bioavailability and absorption of drugs predominantly metabolised by the kidney, and also drugs metabolised by the liver and intestine [6, 7]. Impaired renal function is also associated with changes in the distribution of drugs [7]. Based upon the molecular structure of zenagamtide, metabolism of the peptide in humans is expected to take place via proteolytic cleavage of the peptide backbone and beta‐oxidation of the fatty diacid chain across the body, as demonstrated for related peptides such as semaglutide [33] and the GIP/GLP1‐agonist tirzepatide [34]. From a mechanistic perspective, both GLP‐1R and AMYR agonists exhibit limited renal clearance of intact peptide and are primarily eliminated via proteolytic degradation [17, 33], thereby reducing the likelihood of clinically meaningful PK alterations in the setting of renal impairment. For fatty‐acid modified peptides, potential changes in protein binding in people with impaired renal function could theoretically influence drug distribution; however, given the large excess of circulating albumin relative to zenagamtide exposure, such effects are not expected to be clinically significant. Whilst PK alterations are therefore not anticipated, clinical experience with amylin receptor agonists in advanced CKD and ESRD remains limited.

In this study, over a 28‐day observation period following a single s.c. dose (0.3 mg) of zenagamtide, geometric mean observed AUC0–∞ for plasma zenagamtide ranged from 520 to 715 nmol × h/L in renal impairment groups and was 543 nmol × h/L in participants with normal renal function. A trend towards higher ETRs was observed with increasing renal impairment in mild, moderate and severe impairment groups after adjusting for sex, age and body weight. Observed geometric mean C max or plasma zenagamtide ranged from 2.8 to 3.5 nmol/L in renal impairment groups, and was 3.2 nmol/L in the normal renal function group. ETRs indicated an increased C max in the renal impairment groups with no consistent pattern observed in these groups. Median t max for plasma zenagamtide ranged from 36 to 48 h. The reduced t max observed in the ESRD group may be due to ESRD specific features, such as uremia‐induced changes in drug absorption and volume of distribution in this population [35]. However, the ranges of t max across renal impairment groups do not indicate a significantly shorter t max, and may reflect a chance finding due to the low sample size, and PK sampling time points. The geometric mean t 1/2 tended to be longer with higher degrees of renal impairment. This slight prolongation of t 1/2 remains appropriate for once‐weekly dosing. No consistent pattern in CL/F and V z /F was observed across groups. Overall, the differences observed across groups were not considered clinically relevant, suggesting dose adjustment of zenagamtide is not warranted in people with renal impairment.

Zenagamtide is the first unimolecular GLP‐1RA and AMYR agonist with oral and s.c. formulations currently under investigation for weight management and T2D. This study represents the first assessment of zenagamtide PK parameters in participants with renal impairment. In previous studies, increases in AUC and C max were observed with increasing s.c. and oral doses of zenagamtide, consistent with dose proportionality and with a clear relationship observed between PK and pharmacodynamic results [25, 26]. The results of this study are consistent with previous PK studies investigating the two drug classes (GLP‐1RA and AMYR agonists) separately. No clinically relevant differences in PK were previously reported between participants with normal renal function and renal impairment following a single s.c. administration of the GLP‐1RA semaglutide (0.5 mg) [18], or s.c. administration of the dual GIP‐RA and GLP‐1RA tirzepatide (5 mg) [19]. In addition, no statistically significant differences were observed in total (AUC0–∞) or peak (C max) exposure after administration of a single 60 μg dose of the AMYR agonist pramlintide in participants with Type 1 diabetes with renal impairment compared to those with normal renal function [16]. Similarly, investigation of a single s.c. dose of the AMYR agonist cagrilintide (0.6 mg) found no clinically relevant differences in PK between participants with normal renal function and those with renal impairment [17]. It should be noted that both of these AMYR agonist studies did not assess participants with ESRD, as included here. Clinical pharmacology studies have demonstrated that ESRD requiring dialysis does not impact the PK of GLP‐1RAs [36], whilst available data on amylin analogue use in patients with advanced CKD, and those on dialysis, is limited primarily due to a lack of dedicated clinical trials.

Safety data with zenagamtide in this study were consistent with available data for zenagamtide [25, 26], and GLP‐1RAs and AMYR agonists [9, 14, 15, 29], with no new safety or tolerability issues observed. The most frequent TEAEs were decreased appetite, nausea and vomiting. Nine events of vomiting were observed in seven participants (normal renal function: four events in three participants; ESRD: five events in four participants), with no events observed in the mild, moderate and severe renal impairment groups. A 0.3 mg dose of zenagamtide may be sufficient to induce vomiting in participants with normal renal function, with instances of this gastrointestinal TEAE previously reported during initial treatment at this dose [25]. It should be noted that people with ESRD often experience a range of gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea and vomiting, associated with electrolyte and fluid imbalance, toxin accretion, uremia and the effects of dialysis [37]. TEAEs were non‐serious and mild or moderate in severity, with the majority presenting as mild. Whilst 16 moderate TEAEs were reported in 13 participants, no clear pattern was evident across groups. No severe TEAEs were reported, and there was no indication of increased frequency or severity of TEAEs with zenagamtide with increasing renal impairment.

Potential limitations of this trial include the small sample size and the evaluation of a 0.3 mg dose, which is lower than the maintenance doses currently being evaluated in clinical development [25], and is inherent to the single‐dose study design. For GLP 1–RA containing compounds, dose escalation is typically required to ensure tolerability [25]. The potentially poor health status of participants in this study, notably in the ESRD group, meant that multiple dosing with escalation to a higher dose was considered inappropriate. However, the dose used here is sufficient to characterise PK and to conclude that the increase observed in PK properties for renal impairment groups are within the general variability of PK and therefore are not to be considered clinically relevant. Participants with renal impairment will be included in the phase 3 programme for zenagamtide to allow for characterisation of longer‐term safety at higher doses in this population. The inclusion of five individuals with T2D across the renal impairment groups (mild: n = 1, moderate: n = 4) could have potentially impacted PK and tolerability [38]. Additional limitations include the racial and ethnic homogeneity (97.6% White and 97.6% non‐Latino participants).

In conclusion, single‐dose s.c. zenagamtide 0.3 mg appeared safe and tolerable with no new safety findings and no increase in the number of TEAEs with increasing renal impairment and presented a safety profile consistent with early phase studies of GLP‐1RAs and AMYR agonists. Renal impairment (mild, moderate, severe and ESRD) did not appear to have a clinically relevant impact on the PK properties or safety profile of a single s.c. dose of zenagamtide. Taken within the context of previous data demonstrating dose proportionality of zenagamtide across a wide dose range [25], these results suggest that dose adjustment of zenagamtide is not warranted in people with renal impairment, including people undergoing dialysis.
