Umbilical venous blood flow and its association with placental pathology in pregnancies complicated by gestational diabetes mellitus
Authors: Chadakarn Phaloprakarn, Petcharat Jenkumwong, Sasiwan Suthasmalee, Chutima Chavanisakun, Siriwan Tangjitgamol
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUmbilical venous blood flow (UV‐Q) reflects fetoplacental circulation and contributes to an understanding of fetal physiology. It may also serve as a basis for developing new diagnostic tools to assess fetal wellbeing. This study aimed to investigate UV‐Q patterns in pregnancies complicated by gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) and to examine the associations between absolute and fetal weight‐normalized UV‐Q and placental size and histologic features.Material and MethodsThis prospective study was conducted at a university hospital in Bangkok, Thailand, between December 2021 and May 2024. A total of 200 singleton pregnancies complicated by GDM underwent ultrasound assessment between 35 and 36 weeks of gestation. Measurements included estimated fetal weight, umbilical vein diameter, and Doppler‐derived flow velocity. Absolute and normalized UV‐Q values were calculated and categorized into three groups: <10th, 10th–90th (reference), and >90th percentiles. After delivery, placental morphometry (weight, diameter, circumference, and volume) and histologic features—including maternal and fetal vascular malperfusion, delayed villous maturation (DVM), and chorangiosis—were evaluated. Associations between UV‐Q and placental size were assessed using one‐way analysis of covariance, adjusting for parity, insulin use, gestational age at delivery, birthweight, and infant sex. These covariates were also included in the multivariable logistic regression models to examine associations between UV‐Q and histologic findings.ResultsHigher absolute UV‐Q was significantly associated with greater placental weight, diameter, circumference, and volume (p = 0.018–0.049). Additionally, in multivariable analysis, pregnancies with absolute UV‐Q >90th percentile had a significantly increased risk of DVM (adjusted odds ratio 2.75, 95% confidence interval 1.02–7.86). In contrast, normalized UV‐Q showed no significant associations with placental morphometric features or DVM. Furthermore, neither absolute nor normalized UV‐Q was significantly associated with other histologic placental abnormalities, including maternal or fetal vascular malperfusion or chorangiosis.ConclusionsIn pregnancies complicated by GDM, elevated absolute UV‐Q was associated with increased placental size and a higher risk of DVM. These findings suggest that absolute UV‐Q may serve as a noninvasive indicator of placental structural adaptation and function in diabetic pregnancies. Further research is needed to clarify the clinical significance and underlying mechanisms of these associations.

INTRODUCTION

Advancements in Doppler ultrasound have significantly improved the assessment of umbilical vessel hemodynamics in pregnancy. 1 Most studies have primarily focused on analyzing flow velocity waveforms, particularly in the umbilical arteries, to monitor placental function and fetal well‐being. However, few investigations have explored blood flow volume—particularly blood flow in the umbilical vein (UV)—largely due to concerns regarding measurement accuracy and reproducibility. 2

Building on these hemodynamic assessments, researchers have sought to correlate Doppler findings with underlying placental pathology. Several studies have examined the relationship between umbilical artery Doppler velocimetry and placental histopathological findings, establishing associations with conditions such as maternal vascular malperfusion (MVM) and fetal growth restriction. 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 However, evidence linking umbilical venous blood flow (UV‐Q) to placental pathology remains limited. Given the UV's critical role in directly transporting oxygen and nutrients from the placenta to the fetus, 7 UV‐Q may serve as a useful indicator of fetoplacental circulation. To date, only a single case report has described low UV‐Q and reduced venous velocity in a fetus with fetal vascular malperfusion (FVM), 8 and one study has reported an association between UV‐Q and placental size 9 —highlighting a gap in current research.

This gap is particularly relevant in the context of gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), a condition known to affect placental development and function. 10 , 11 GDM has been associated with a range of gross and microscopic placental changes, including increased placental weight, delayed villous maturation (DVM), MVM, and FVM. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 These structural abnormalities may influence fetoplacental blood flow, including UV‐Q. Although previous studies have evaluated UV‐Q in low‐risk and diabetic pregnancies, 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 22 its relationship with placental pathology specifically in GDM remains unexamined. Understanding this relationship could provide valuable insights into the complex pathophysiology of GDM‐affected pregnancies and potentially identify new monitoring strategies for this high‐risk population.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to investigate the association between UV‐Q and placental histopathological features in pregnancies complicated by GDM.

MATERIAL AND METHODS

Study design and participants

The placenta in gestational diabetes mellitus (PL‐GDM) study was a prospective cohort study comprising multiple subprojects designed to investigate various aspects of placental pathology in pregnancies complicated by GDM. The main PL‐GDM study was conducted at the Faculty of Medicine Vajira Hospital, Navamindradhiraj University, Bangkok, Thailand, between December 2021 and May 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Vajira Institutional Review Board (approval no. 215/2564; approved on October 29, 2021) and adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.

This subproject was conducted under the umbrella of the PL‐GDM study, focusing on a cohort of pregnant women diagnosed with GDM according to the Carpenter and Coustan criteria. 23 Participants were consecutively recruited during routine antenatal clinic visits. All eligible individuals were informed about the study and provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. Inclusion criteria were a singleton pregnancy, a gestational age (GA) of less than 35 weeks at the time of recruitment, and the absence of major fetal anomalies. Exclusion criteria included pre‐existing diabetes mellitus, chronic hypertension, HIV or syphilis infection, absence of a three‐vessel umbilical cord, preterm delivery before 37 weeks of gestation, or unavailable placental tissue for analysis.

The sample size was calculated based on data from a pilot study of 30 GDM cases that underwent UV‐Q measurement between 35 and 36 weeks of gestation and had placental collection at term delivery (>37 weeks of gestation). The study found mean placental weights of 449 ± 60 g, 500 ± 79 g, and 582 ± 80 g in pregnancies with absolute UV‐Q values below the 10th percentile, between the 10th and 90th percentiles, and above the 90th percentile, respectively. Assuming a significance level of 0.05 and a power of 80%, a minimum of 150 participants was required. Since the main PL‐GDM study enrolled 200 participants with GDM, this subproject utilized the same cohort.

Ultrasound assessment

All participants underwent ultrasound assessment of fetal biometry (biparietal diameter, head circumference, abdominal circumference, and femur length), UV diameter, and Doppler studies for UV flow velocity between 35 and 36 weeks of gestation. These scans were conducted using a Voluson E10 ultrasound machine (GE Healthcare, Chicago, IL, USA) equipped with a 2–9 MHz curvilinear transducer, with participants positioned semi‐recumbently throughout.

Fetal biometry was performed to calculate the estimated fetal weight (EFW) using the formula of Hadlock et al. 24 The UV diameter was measured on a free‐floating loop of the cord using a perpendicular ultrasound beam relative to the vessel's long axis, as previously described. 25 Measurements were obtained from inner wall to inner wall using B‐mode imaging at maximum magnification (Figure 1). Three separate recordings were taken, and their average was used as the final value. All measurements were conducted during fetal quiescence to minimize movement and improve accuracy.

Sonographic measurement of the umbilical vein diameter in a free‐floating loop of the umbilical cord using B‐mode imaging, with a perpendicular ultrasound beam and inner‐to‐inner wall assessment.

UV flow velocity was assessed under similar conditions, using a longitudinal view of the free‐floating UV loop at the site where the diameter was measured. Color‐flow imaging was used for visualization, and pulsed‐wave Doppler was applied, with the sample volume positioned centrally within the vessel lumen (Figure 2). The time‐averaged maximum velocity was calculated from three consecutive measurements, obtained at an insonation angle as close to 0° as possible and not exceeding 30°. The mean velocity of the UV was estimated as half of the time‐averaged maximum velocity (0.5 × time‐averaged maximum velocity). 25 Absolute UV‐Q, expressed in mL/min, was then calculated using the following formula 26 : Absolute UV‐Q = UV mean velocity (cm/s) × π × (UV diameter/2)2 (cm2) × 60. This formula integrates both vessel diameter and flow velocity to estimate volumetric flow. To account for fetal size, Absolute UV‐Q was further normalized by the EFW, yielding weight‐adjusted flow (normalized UV‐Q), expressed in mL/min/kg. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21

Sonographic assessment of umbilical venous flow velocity using color‐flow imaging and pulsed‐wave Doppler, with the sample volume centered in the vessel lumen and an insonation angle ≤30°.

Ultrasound assessments were performed by three experienced sonographers (C.P., P.J., and S.S.), who were blinded to participants' clinical information. Reliability testing demonstrated excellent consistency for UV diameter measurements, with intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.93 for intraobserver and 0.90 for interobserver measurements. For UV flow velocity measurements, reliability was good, with intraclass correlation coefficients of 0.78 for intraobserver and 0.75 for interobserver measurements.

Placental evaluation

Placentas and attached umbilical cords were collected and promptly fixed in 10% buffered formalin after delivery. The examining pathologist (C.C.), who performed the gross examination, trimmed the membranes and umbilical cord before weighing the placenta using a calibrated digital scale (PULISITE model PTX‐5102, Provision Scientific Company Limited, Bangkok, Thailand). Placental morphological features were recorded, including diameter and circumference (measured with a flexible measuring tape), as well as volume (calculated from placental dimensions).

Placental specimens were processed for histologic evaluation in accordance with standardized protocols outlined in the Amsterdam placental workshop group statement. 27 In brief, sections were collected from the umbilical cord, membranes, and three full‐thickness placental sites: the central disc, near the cord insertion, and the disc margin. Additional samples were obtained from any grossly abnormal areas. Tissues were paraffin‐embedded, sectioned at 4 μm, stained with hematoxylin and eosin, and independently reviewed twice by a blinded pathologist (C.C.). Any diagnostic discrepancies were resolved through consensus with a second pathologist (S.T.).

Histologic evaluation was conducted according to the Amsterdam criteria, 27 with a focus on identifying MVM and FVM. Additional placental lesions, such as DVM and chorangiosis, were also assessed. The diagnostic criteria for these lesions followed standard definitions described elsewhere. 27 , 28

Data collection and statistical analysis

Maternal demographic data, including age and parity, were collected at the time of enrollment. Pregnancy‐related information, such as GA at GDM diagnosis, treatment modality (diet or insulin), GA at delivery, birthweight and its corresponding percentile category, 29 and infant sex, was also recorded.

Absolute and EFW‐normalized UV‐Q values were categorized into three groups based on their distribution: below the 10th percentile, between the 10th and 90th percentiles (reference group), and above the 90th percentile. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate, and compared across groups using one‐way analysis of variance followed by post hoc least significant difference tests, or the Kruskal–Wallis H test when applicable. Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, with group comparisons performed using the chi‐squared test or Fisher's exact test, as appropriate.

Differences in placental size among the three absolute or normalized UV‐Q groups were analyzed using one‐way analysis of covariance (ANCOVA), controlling for parity, insulin use, GA at delivery, birthweight, and infant sex. These covariates were also included in multivariable logistic regression models to assess associations between absolute or normalized UV‐Q groups and placental histologic findings, with results reported as adjusted odds ratios (aORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Correlations between placental weight and its diameter, circumference, and volume were assessed using Pearson's correlation coefficient. The correlation between placental weight and birthweight was also evaluated. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 28.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

A total of 200 women with GDM were stratified into three groups according to the percentiles of absolute and EFW‐normalized UV‐Q: <10th percentile, 10th–90th percentile, and >90th percentile. The 10th, 50th (median), and 90th percentiles for absolute UV‐Q values were 240, 340, and 510 mL/min, respectively, and 85, 125, and 190 mL/min/kg for normalized UV‐Q. Maternal age, parity, GA at GDM diagnosis, insulin use, GA at the time of UV‐Q measurement, GA at delivery, and infant sex did not differ significantly across groups categorized by either absolute or normalized UV‐Q (all p > 0.05) (Table 1).

In the absolute UV‐Q groups, mean placental weight was 463.9 ± 61.0 g in the <10th percentile group (p = 0.012 vs. 10th–90th percentile), 505.5 ± 95.8 g in the 10th–90th percentile group, and 574.4 ± 101.1 g in the >90th percentile group (p = 0.008 vs. 10th–90th percentile; p for trend <0.001). Beyond placental weight, other placental morphometric features also varied across UV‐Q categories. Differences in placental diameter (p = 0.497 for <10th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p = 0.002 for >90th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p for trend = 0.010), circumference (p = 0.860 for <10th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p = 0.041 for >90th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p for trend = 0.017), and volume (p = 0.303 for <10th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p = 0.005 for >90th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p for trend = 0.007) were observed across the absolute UV‐Q groups, with the largest measurements found in the >90th percentile group (Table 2). Among the normalized UV‐Q groups, only circumference showed a significant difference (p = 0.034 for <10th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p = 0.101 for >90th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p for trend = 0.047), whereas differences in diameter and volume did not reach statistical significance.

These morphometric variations were further supported by correlation analyses. Placental weight showed positive correlations with placental diameter (r = 0.297; p < 0.001), circumference (r = 0.359; p < 0.001), and volume (r = 0.834; p < 0.001), with the strongest correlation observed for placental volume. In addition, a moderate positive correlation was found between placental weight and birthweight (r = 0.584; p < 0.001).

Regarding histologic findings, the prevalence of MVM, FVM, and chorangiosis did not differ significantly among groups. However, the frequency of DVM increased progressively across both absolute (p = 0.184 for <10th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p = 0.041 for >90th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p for trend = 0.035) and normalized (p = 0.046 for <10th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p = 0.051 for >90th vs. 10th–90th percentile; p for trend = 0.013) UV‐Q groups (Table 2). Notably, in the normalized UV‐Q stratification, DVM was absent in the <10th percentile group but present in 16.9% and 35.0% of the 10th–90th and >90th percentile groups, respectively.

ANCOVA and multivariable logistic regression analyses

Results from ANCOVA indicated significant associations between absolute UV‐Q and placental weight, diameter, circumference, and volume (p = 0.047, 0.018, 0.049, and 0.042, respectively), after adjusting for parity, insulin use, GA at delivery, birthweight, and infant sex. Conversely, an additional ANCOVA for normalized UV‐Q did not revealed significant associations with these four placental parameters (p = 0.128, 0.361, 0.100, and 0.272, respectively).

Multivariable logistic regression analysis demonstrated a significant association between absolute UV‐Q groups and the presence of DVM (Table 3). After adjusting for parity, insulin use, GA at delivery, birthweight, and infant sex, women in the >90th percentile absolute UV‐Q group had higher odds of DVM compared to the reference group (10th–90th percentile) (aOR 2.75, 95% CI 1.02–7.86). In contrast, women in the <10th percentile group had lower odds of DVM, although the association was not statistically significant (aOR 0.27, 95% CI 0.03–2.17).

DISCUSSION

In this subset analysis of the PL‐GDM study, which focused on pregnancies complicated by GDM, we found that higher absolute UV‐Q values were significantly associated with greater placental weight, diameter, circumference, and volume. Additionally, pregnancies with absolute UV‐Q values above the 90th percentile had significantly higher odds of DVM. In contrast, normalized UV‐Q values were not significantly associated with placental morphometric features or DVM. Furthermore, neither absolute nor normalized UV‐Q was significantly associated with other histologic placental abnormalities, including MVM, FVM, or chorangiosis.

These findings build upon the early body of evidence by demonstrating that both reduced and increased UV‐Q may carry clinical significance, particularly in the context of placental abnormalities related to GDM. Specifically, our results reinforce the association between UV‐Q and placental size, especially in late gestation. The only prior study to examine this relationship—conducted by Flo et al. 9 —used magnetic resonance imaging to assess placental volume at 27 and 37 weeks of gestation, reporting a significant correlation with UV‐Q only at 37 weeks. Similarly, we found that absolute UV‐Q measured at 35–36 weeks was significantly associated with greater placental weight, diameter, circumference, and volume.

This GA‐dependent pattern likely reflects the increasing physiological demands of the fetus and the placenta's adaptive capacity as pregnancy advances. By late gestation, the placenta attains a high level of functional maturity to support enhanced oxygen and nutrient exchange. 30 Structural adaptations—such as increased villous branching and expansion of the vascular bed—serve to improve fetoplacental blood flow, including UV‐Q. 30 This developmental trajectory may help explain why the association with placental size becomes more pronounced in late gestation, as opposed to earlier stages when placental architecture remains more plastic and less functionally mature.

The strength of these associations also depends on how UV‐Q is measured—specifically, whether absolute or normalized values offer greater physiological relevance. Normalized UV‐Q, typically expressed as mL/min/kg of EFW, is commonly used to adjust for fetal size and facilitate comparisons across GAs or populations. 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 However, in the context of GDM, absolute UV‐Q may be more informative. In our study, absolute UV‐Q was significantly associated with placental size and DVM—parameters that likely reflect structural adaptations to increased metabolic demands. 31 These findings suggest that absolute flow measurements may more accurately capture the extent of placental compensation in GDM. In contrast, normalization by EFW could obscure such physiologic adaptations, particularly in pregnancies where both fetal and placental overgrowth are common and closely linked.

Beyond placental size, we also investigated the associations between UV‐Q and placental histopathology. While a previous case report described reduced UV‐Q in a fetus with FVM, growth restriction, and a single umbilical artery, 8 our findings in a GDM cohort with structurally normal three‐vessel cords revealed a contrasting pattern. Rather than diminished flow, we found that elevated absolute UV‐Q—particularly values above the 90th percentile—was significantly associated with DVM. This finding may reflect characteristic pathophysiologic changes in diabetic pregnancies. 31 In a hyperglycemic intrauterine environment, fetal hyperinsulinemia promotes accelerated growth and increased metabolic demand. 32 The placenta compensates through structural adaptations, 10 , 11 including villous immaturity, 13 , 14 often characterized by enlarged terminal villi. 33 These changes may enhance nutrient transfer and venous return, 31 thereby contributing to elevated UV‐Q.

Although birthweight was not the primary outcome of our study, an important secondary finding was that pregnancies with absolute UV‐Q values above the 90th percentile were significantly associated with higher birthweights. This observation aligns with previous studies reporting a positive correlation between elevated UV‐Q and fetal overgrowth. 34 , 35 Several physiological mechanisms may explain this association. Higher UV‐Q likely reflects more efficient transplacental transfer of oxygen and nutrients, 30 which in turn supports greater fetal growth. In pregnancies complicated by GDM, maternal hyperglycemia stimulates fetal hyperinsulinemia—a potent anabolic stimulus that contributes to both placental hypertrophy and excessive fetal growth. 32 This mechanism is further supported by our finding of a positive correlation between placental weight and birthweight. Taken together, these results suggest that UV‐Q may serve as a physiological marker of enhanced fetoplacental exchange and increased fetal growth potential.

An important consideration when evaluating UV‐Q is the technical challenge inherent in Doppler‐based measurements. Accurate estimation of volume flow requires precise determination of venous diameter and time‐averaged maximum velocity, both of which can be affected by fetal movements, transducer angle, and operator experience. 2 , 36 These limitations are particularly pronounced in longitudinal studies, where repeated measurements are difficult to standardize—especially when using the free‐floating loop of the UV, which may change position in response to fetal activity. Although the intra‐abdominal portion of the UV provides greater anatomical consistency, its visualization becomes increasingly challenging in late pregnancy due to the growing fetus and shifting fetal position. 37

Given these technical considerations, our cross‐sectional design offered practical advantages by avoiding the variability inherent in repeated imaging while remaining logistically feasible. We conducted measurements at 35–36 weeks of gestation to capture UV‐Q during a period of advanced placental development and peak functional activity. For optimal measurement conditions, we selected the free‐floating UV loop, which provides clear visualization, enables simultaneous assessment of vessel diameter and flow, permits optimal Doppler angle correction (<30 degrees), and minimizes interference from adjacent fetal structures. 37 These features make this approach particularly well suited for third‐trimester assessment, when imaging becomes more challenging. 37

Building on this methodological framework, our approach yielded median absolute and normalized UV‐Q values of 340 mL/min and 125 mL/min/kg, respectively—values notably higher than those reported in low‐risk pregnancies (247–258 mL/min and 94–98 mL/min/kg). 37 These elevated values likely reflect enhanced fetoplacental perfusion in GDM, potentially due to compensatory physiological adaptations such as increased transplacental nutrient transfer and altered placental hemodynamics. 30 The rigorous standardization of our protocol—including measures to ensure fetal quiescence, optimize image magnification, and maintain low insonation angles—may have further contributed to these higher values, given that such refinements are especially critical for accurate assessment using the free‐floating UV loop.

Importantly, the reliability and consistency of our UV‐Q measurements—demonstrated by high intraclass correlation coefficients for both vessel diameter and flow velocity—underscore the potential of this Doppler‐based approach as a clinically relevant biomarker of placental development and histopathologic changes in GDM pregnancies. Recognition of the association between elevated UV‐Q, DVM, and increased birthweight may enhance fetal surveillance and improve the interpretation of growth trajectories, particularly in pregnancies complicated by suspected overgrowth or abnormal Doppler findings. Prospective longitudinal studies are warranted to characterize the dynamic evolution of UV‐Q across gestation and to clarify its mechanistic relationships with placental angiogenesis, villous maturation, and neonatal outcomes in diabetic pregnancies.

A major strength of this study is its prospective design within a well‐characterized cohort of pregnancies complicated by GDM, allowing detailed evaluation of UV‐Q alongside comprehensive assessment of both gross and histologic placental parameters. The use of standardized Doppler protocols and blinding of pathologists to clinical and Doppler data minimized measurement and classification bias. Notably, both UV diameter and UV‐Q were measured at the same anatomical site—within the free‐floating cord loop—ensuring consistency and reducing the risk of error in volume flow calculations. The inclusion of both absolute and normalized UV‐Q values enabled a nuanced analysis, demonstrating that absolute values more accurately reflected placental blood flow patterns than normalized values. Finally, adjustment for key covariates—including parity, insulin use, GA at delivery, birthweight, and infant sex—via ANCOVA and multivariable logistic regression further strengthens the validity of the observed associations.

However, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the single time point of UV‐Q measurement precluded evaluation of temporal changes in placental hemodynamics and limited causal inference between UV‐Q alterations and placental pathology. Second, although we adjusted for multiple maternal and fetal covariates, residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Third, the relatively small sample size in the extreme UV‐Q groups (<10th and >90th percentiles) may have limited statistical power and the precision of subgroup analyses. Fourth, insulin use in this study was defined as any use during pregnancy; however, we did not collect data on insulin type, dosage, or glycemic control, which could influence placental blood flow and fetal outcomes. Lastly, as the cohort was restricted to pregnancies complicated by GDM, the generalizability of our findings to other populations is limited.

CONCLUSION

In pregnancies complicated by GDM, high absolute UV‐Q (>90th percentile) was independently associated with larger placental size and increased odds of DVM, while low absolute UV‐Q showed a nonsignificant inverse trend. These findings suggest that abnormal UV‐Q patterns may reflect or contribute to distinct placental pathophysiologic states. Incorporating UV‐Q assessment into clinical evaluation could provide additional insights into fetoplacental health. Further research is warranted to validate these findings, clarify underlying mechanisms, and determine the prognostic significance of UV‐Q‐related placental changes.
