Real-world evidence of sacituzumab govitecan in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer: an updated multicenter analysis

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Trop-2-directed antibody–drug conjugate sacituzumab govitecan (SG) has shown significant benefit in metastatic triple-negative breast cancer (mTNBC) in clinical trials. However, evidence about its effectiveness and safety in real-world heterogeneous patient populations is limited. This multicenter study, including the largest European and second-largest worldwide real-world cohort of SG-treated patients with mTNBC, offers valuable insights for its use in routine clinical practice.Materials and methodsThis analysis included 256 women with mTNBC treated with SG across 17 Italian oncology centers, consisting of 217 retrospectively identified patients and 39 prospectively enrolled patients. Treatment activity and safety were assessed according to RECIST v1.1 and Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events del National Cancer Institute (NCI-CTCAE) v5.0 criteria.ResultsMedian patient age was 54 years (range 26-80), and 70.7% had TNBC at initial diagnosis. The median interval from metastatic diagnosis to SG initiation was 16 months (range 0-115), with a median of two prior treatment lines (range 0-7). Frequent metastatic sites were lymph nodes (60.9%), bone (43.0%), lung (42.6%), liver (33.2%), and brain (26.2%). Overall, 37.5% achieved complete or partial response, 27.7% had stable disease, and 31.2% experienced progression. At a median follow-up of 17.4 months, 202 patients had progressed, and 139 had died. Median real-world progression-free survival was 6.4 months [95% confidence interval (CI) 5.3-7.3] and median real-world overall survival was 13.6 months (95% CI 11.5-15.5). The most common treatment-related adverse events were alopecia (69.1%), neutropenia (57.0%), nausea (49.6%), anemia (46.9%), and diarrhea (36.7%). Grade 3-4 neutropenia occurred in 18.0% and 5.9%, respectively. Dose reductions were required in 36.5%, and 2.7% discontinued treatment.ConclusionsSG demonstrated durable clinical benefit and manageable toxicity in patients with mTNBC, highlighting the importance of optimized adverse-event management. The size and heterogeneity of the study population, including the largest reported cohort of patients with central nervous system metastases, support the robustness of these findings.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) accounts for ∼15%-20% of all breast cancer (BC) subtypes. TNBC lacks estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PgR) expression and does not overexpress the human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2).1 This highly aggressive disease is linked to early relapse, high metastatic potential, and limited treatment options, leading to poor clinical outcomes.1, 2, 3 In the metastatic setting, standard treatment has been sequential chemotherapy. However, this approach causes substantial toxicity and limited clinical benefit, with a median progression-free survival (PFS) of 2-3 months and a median overall survival (OS) of ∼10 months.2,4 Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP) inhibitors have recently expanded therapeutic options for biomarker-selected patients. Yet, many individuals with TNBC lack effective targeted therapies.

The phase III ASCENT trial showed that sacituzumab govitecan (SG) significantly improved outcomes in heavily pretreated patients with metastatic TNBC (mTNBC).5,6 SG is a novel antibody–drug conjugate (ADC) that combines targeted delivery with potent cytotoxic efficacy.7 SG consists of a humanized monoclonal antibody targeting trophoblast cell-surface antigen 2 (Trop-2), a transmembrane glycoprotein highly expressed in >90% of TNBC tumors.8,9 This antibody is conjugated via a hydrolysable linker to SN-38, the active metabolite of irinotecan and a potent topoisomerase I inhibitor. The structural design of SG facilitates the delivery of high concentrations of cytotoxic payload directly to tumor cells and introduces a bystander effect within the tumor microenvironment. Additionally, the high drug-to-antibody ratio (∼7.6:1) achieved through this linker enhances the antitumor efficacy of SG.10

According to the ASCENT trial, compared with chemotherapy, SG increased median PFS from 1.7 to 4.8 months, median OS from 6.9 to 11.8 months, and objective response rate (ORR) from 5% to 35%.5,6 The benefits were seen across clinical subgroups, including those with an initial TNBC diagnosis, brain metastases, older age, and prior exposure to ICIs.5,6,11,12 These results led to regulatory approval of SG by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the European Medicines Agency (EMA), and several national health authorities. However, the generalizability of trial outcomes to clinical practice remains uncertain because patients with poor Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status (≥2), central nervous system (CNS) metastases, substantial comorbidities, or other high-risk features are often underrepresented in clinical trials. In this context, real-world studies are essential. Recent evidence from the United States, Europe, and the United Kingdom supports the activity and manageable safety profile of SG in real-world settings, although differences in patient characteristics, treatment sequencing, and health care practices continue to influence outcomes.13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20

Our previous multicenter real-world analysis of 57 patients with mTNBC treated with SG across seven Italian oncology centers demonstrated meaningful efficacy and manageable toxicity.17 As Italian institutions were not included in the pivotal ASCENT trial, our study provided an initial opportunity to independently validate SG performance within the Italian health care setting. Building on these findings, we conducted an expanded, multicenter, real-world analysis involving 256 patients treated across 17 Italian oncology centers, comprising 217 retrospectively identified and 39 prospectively enrolled patients. This cohort extends our prior Italian real-world series of 57 patients, all of whom are incorporated into the current analysis. This cohort constitutes the largest real-world group of SG-treated patients with mTNBC reported to date in Europe and the second-largest worldwide, allowing for a robust evaluation of treatment outcomes in routine clinical practice. The study population reflects current treatment patterns and includes patient groups frequently underrepresented in randomized trials. Importantly, this analysis includes the largest real-world cohort of patients with CNS metastases treated with SG reported to date. These data provide clinically relevant insights in a setting that remains insufficiently characterized and highlight the need for further dedicated investigations in patients with brain metastases. In addition to assessing SG effectiveness, the study offers a comprehensive characterization of safety profiles, treatment modifications, and supportive-care strategies. The findings are interpreted in the context of pivotal clinical trials and other real-world studies, thereby informing the optimal use of SG in the routine management of mTNBC.

Materials and Methods

Study objectives, data source, and design

This analysis was conducted within the GIM14-BIOMETA study (NCT02284581), a large multicenter observational cohort that included both retrospectively and prospectively enrolled patients with metastatic BC, regardless of tumor biological subtype. A total of 5045 patients were included in the GIM14-BIOMETA cohort across participating centers. Patient selection for our analysis followed a stepwise process (Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108251). Firstly, individuals with a confirmed diagnosis of mTNBC were identified in the overall GIM14-BIOMETA population. Secondly, among these, those who received SG were selected. Metastatic TNBC status was determined using the most recent biopsy and defined according to the American Society of Clinical Oncology and College of American Pathologists guidelines: ER <1%, PgR <1%, HER2 scored as 0 or 1+, or 2+ with nonamplified FISH. Metastatic disease was assessed per RECIST v1.1. Using this approach, 256 patients treated at 17 Italian oncology centers were included in the final study population: 217 retrospectively identified and 39 prospectively enrolled. All patients met the core eligibility requirements of the GIM14-BIOMETA study, including age ≥18 years, histologically confirmed metastatic disease, and availability of relevant clinical, efficacy, and safety data. Individuals who received SG in randomized clinical trials were excluded from this analysis.

Patients received SG as an intravenous infusion at 10 mg/kg on days 1 and 8 of a 21-day cycle, according to the EMA Summary of Product Characteristics (SmPC). Treatment continued until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity. Dose modifications, including delays or reductions, were made as needed for infusion-related reactions or adverse events (AEs), following SmPC recommendations.

Effectiveness

Real-world PFS (rwPFS) was defined as the time from the first dose of SG to disease progression or death from any cause, whichever came first. Real-world OS (rwOS) was defined as the time from the first dose to death from any cause. ORR was assessed using the RECIST v1.1 criteria. Disease-free interval (DFI) was defined as the time from the initial diagnosis to recurrent or metastatic disease. Patients without progression or death at last follow-up were censored for PFS. Those alive at last follow-up were censored for OS. AEs related to SG were recorded and graded using the National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (NCI-CTCAE) version 5.0.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics summarized the variables of interest. Continuous variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median with range, depending on their distribution. Categorical variables were summarized using absolute and relative frequencies.

rwPFS and rwOS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method; median survival times and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were provided. Median follow-up duration was calculated with the inverse Kaplan–Meier approach. Subgroup analyses by ECOG performance status, presence of visceral disease, brain metastases, DFI, baseline TN status, line of SG therapy, prior ICI exposure, prior trastuzumab-deruxtecan (T-DXd) exposure, and BRCA1/2 mutation status were prespecified in the statistical analysis plan as clinically relevant variables. However, given the observational and real-world nature of the study, all subgroup analyses should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating.

AEs related to treatment were coded using Preferred Terms and assigned to the primary System Organ Class as defined by the Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities (MedDRA), version 23.0. For events reported multiple times in the same patient, the highest NCI-CTCAE grade was retained.

All patients in the cohort were included in the analyses. Statistical significance was set at a two-sided P value <0.05. Given the observational nature of the dataset, all P values were interpreted descriptively. Statistical analyses were carried out using R statistical software (version 4.4.3, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Baseline patient characteristics

Our analysis included 256 patients who started SG treatment at 17 Italian oncology centers between December 2021 and June 2025. Of these, 217 were retrospectively identified, and 39 were prospectively enrolled. The median age at treatment start was 54 years (range 26-80; Table 1). Approximately 95.6% of individuals had an ECOG 0-1 performance status, whereas 4.4% had ECOG ≥2. Among patients with available biomarker data, 12.5% had germline BRCA1/2 mutations. At initial diagnosis, 70.7% had TNBC, 27.3% had HER2-low status, and 6.2% were classified as ER-low. De novo metastatic disease was observed in 18.4% of cases. Among the 70 patients with HER2-low status at initial diagnosis, only 14 received T-DXd in the metastatic setting before SG, while 50 received T-DXd after SG. The DFI distribution was ≤12 months in 7.8%, and >12 months in 72.7% of patients. The median time from diagnosis of metastatic disease to SG initiation was 16 months (range 0-115). The most frequent metastatic sites were lymph nodes (60.9%), bone (43.0%), lung (42.6%), liver (33.2%), and brain (26.2%). Among the 67 patients with brain metastases, 60 underwent radiotherapy. More than half of the cohort (50.4%) had three or more metastatic sites, 37.1% had two, and 12.5% had a single site. Prior exposure to PARP inhibitors or programmed cell death protein 1/programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-1/PD-L1) inhibitors was reported in 8.2% and 28.9% of cases, respectively. Patients had received a median of two prior systemic therapy lines for metastatic disease (range 0-7). Seven patients received SG as first-line metastatic therapy due to early relapse within 12 months after completion of (neo)adjuvant therapy. To better reflect real-world clinical practice and minimize selection bias, these patients were included in the analysis. Supportive care and premedication practices

As summarized in Table 2, premedication and preventive treatments were routinely used to manage treatment-related toxicities. Steroid premedication was administered in 207 (80.9%) patients. Dose reductions, temporary interruptions, and treatment discontinuations in the ASCENT and TROPiCS-02 trials were mainly associated with neutropenia and diarrhea,5,6,21 so prophylactic granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) and loperamide were also commonly implemented. Overall, 146 patients (57.0%) received prophylactic G-CSF; 58 (22.7%) received it as primary prophylaxis at baseline, and 88 (34.4%) as secondary prophylaxis after developing neutropenia. Among patients receiving secondary prophylaxis, neutropenia grades at onset were as follows: 25 (28.4%) grade 1, 24 (27.3%) grade 2, 27 (30.7%) grade 3, and 12 (13.6%) grade 4 events. Prophylactic loperamide was given to 16 patients (6.1%) to prevent treatment-induced diarrhea. Antiemetic strategies varied: at baseline, 42.1% of patients received a 5-hydroxytryptamine 3 receptor antagonist alone, whereas 51.8% received a combination of 5-hydroxytryptamine 3 and neurokinin-1 receptor antagonists before the first treatment cycle. An additional 6.6% were switched to the combination regimen after side effects began. Hematologic toxicities were the predominant grade ≥3 events. Neutropenia occurred in 57.0% of patients, including 18.0% with grade 3 and 5.9% with grade 4; no febrile episodes were recorded. Anemia (46.9%) was mainly low grade, with only 1.2% grade 3, and thrombocytopenia (10.2%) did not exceed grade 2 severity.

Gastrointestinal events were common but manageable. Nausea affected nearly half of patients (49.6%), with only one grade 3 event. Diarrhea occurred in 36.7% (10 grade 3 and no grade 4 events). Vomiting (10.9%), constipation (3.9%), abdominal pain (3.1%), and stomach pain (1.6%) were mostly mild. Erosive gastritis, dyspepsia, and epigastric pain were each reported in 0.4%.

General and constitutional symptoms included asthenia (27.3%; two grade 3) and fatigue (6.3%; two grade 3). Skin and mucosal disorders were usually mild, with alopecia most frequent (69.1%). Mucositis (4.3%), rash (2.0%), pruritus (2.0%), erythema (0.8%), dry skin (0.4%), and hyperpigmentation (0.4%) occurred sporadically.

Neurologic, psychiatric, and metabolic events were rare and limited to grades 1 and 2, including cholinergic syndrome (3.1%), dysgeusia (1.2%), neuropathy (0.8%), headache (0.8%), insomnia (0.4%), and hyperglycemia (1.6%). Musculoskeletal symptoms (myalgia and arthralgia, 0.4% each) were isolated findings.

Laboratory abnormalities included alanine aminotransferase/aspartate aminotransferase elevation in 13.3% of patients (grade ≥3 in 3.9%) and bilirubin increase in 2.7%, all low grade. Isolated lipase and amylase elevations (0.4% each) were also observed.

Treatment modifications were limited: 7 patients (2.7%) required temporary interruption due to toxicity, and 92 (36.5%) underwent dose reduction.

Efficacy outcomes

The clinical outcomes showed meaningful disease control in a substantial subset of individuals. The ORR was 37.5%, with almost all responses partial and only one complete response. Another 27.7% of patients achieved stable disease, whereas 31.2% experienced progressive disease (Table 4). At a median follow-up of 17.4 months (95% CI 15.7-19.3), the median rwPFS was 6.4 months (95% CI 5.3-7.3; Table 4; Figure 1A). The median rwOS was 13.6 months (95% CI 11.5-15.5; Table 4; Figure 1B). Subgroup analyses of rwPFS and rwOS identified ECOG performance status ≥2 as the variable most strongly associated with poorer outcomes, showing the highest hazard ratios for both rwPFS (HR 3.16; P < 0.001) and rwOS (HR 3.13; P = 0.003). However, as this subgroup included only 11 patients, these results should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than conclusive. Visceral disease was significantly associated with shorter rwPFS (HR 1.40; P = 0.03), whereas brain metastases and DFI ≤ 12 months were the key prognostic factors for rwOS (HR 1.67; P = 0.006 and HR 2.15; P = 0.001, respectively). Discussion

Our multicenter, real-world study expands previous clinical trial data and real-world analyses on the clinical activity and safety profile of SG in patients with mTNBC. SG demonstrated significant clinical activity and maintained a manageable safety profile in routine clinical practice. Survival outcomes were consistent with those observed in ASCENT, even though the present study included a less selected real-world population with poorer performance status.5,6 In our study, subgroup analysis indicated that an ECOG score of 2 or higher was associated with poorer rwPFS and rwOS. However, as the ECOG ≥2 subgroup included only 11 patients (4.4%), these findings should be interpreted with caution. The presence of visceral disease was associated with shorter rwPFS, whereas brain metastases and DFI ≤12 months were correlated with worse rwOS. Response rates further confirmed the antitumor activity of SG across clinically relevant subgroups. Our cohort included patients often underrepresented in trials, such as those with brain metastases and prior treatment with PD-1/PD-L1 or PARP inhibitors. The distribution of metastatic sites in our population was broadly comparable to that in the ASCENT trial, with some variations, such as a higher proportion of bone and nodal disease, that might reflect differences in patient characteristics and real-world patterns of metastatic spread. Importantly, this study also included the largest real-world group of SG-treated patients with brain metastases to date, offering valuable insights into this high-risk group. Given the poor prognosis associated with CNS metastases, the rwPFS and rwOS in our analysis appear particularly encouraging. These findings align with previous real-world data and highlight the need for prospective studies to clarify the role of SG in this setting.14

To our knowledge, this study represents the largest real-world SG-treated mTNBC cohort in Europe and the second-largest worldwide, reinforcing the clinical relevance of our findings. Compared with our previous Italian analysis (n = 57),17 clinical outcomes improved. These gains are noteworthy since the current cohort included 26.2% of patients with brain metastases. Such improvements may reflect broader patient selection, better supportive care, and greater physician experience with SG in real-world settings, further supporting its effectiveness and tolerability. Consistent findings were also reported by Nanda et al.20 in the largest real-world dataset for mTNBC so far, which included 381 patients treated with SG in the second or later line. The population was heterogeneous, with 17% of patients having ECOG ≥2 and 23% with brain metastases. Despite these unfavorable features, median rwOS was 11.3 months, and time to next treatment or death (TTNTD, used as a proxy for PFS) was 5.6 months. Similarly, Kalinsky et al.13 analyzed 230 US patients from the ConcertAI Patient360™ database (Cambridge, Massachusetts, USA), reporting a median PFS of 3.8 months and OS of 10.0 months across all treatment lines, with OS extending to 13.9 months in the second-line setting. While our study lacks the racial diversity of the cohorts by Nanda and Kalinsky, it showed longer PFS and comparable OS, reinforcing the consistency of SG’s clinical benefit across different populations and health care systems. Among other US-based studies, Alaklabi et al.18 (n = 115) observed median PFS and OS of 4.8 and 9.6 months, respectively, and ORR of 27.8%. In Europe, smaller real-world studies reported variable results. De Moura et al.16 (n = 99) described median PFS and OS of 3.9 and 8.6 months, respectively, with an ORR of 29%. In a German single-center analysis, Reinisch et al.15 (n = 43) reported PFS of 5.0 months and OS of 13.1 months. In the UK multicenter study by Hanna et al.19 (n = 132), the median PFS was 5.2 months and OS 8.7 months. Taken together, these findings place our study at the upper range of real-world outcomes for SG, both in response and survival.

The relatively favorable safety outcomes observed in our study may reflect optimization in SG administration, earlier integration within treatment sequencing, and the implementation of systematic supportive-care protocols. To contextualize our findings, we compared the incidence and management of SG-related AEs in our cohort with those reported in the ASCENT trial, other contemporary real-world studies, and the PRIMED phase II trial, which prospectively assessed the impact of mandatory primary prophylaxis with G-CSF and loperamide on the safety of SG in HER2-negative BC (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108251).5,6,13,15,17, 18, 19, 20,22 In ASCENT, neutropenia was the most common AE (63% overall; grade ≥3 in 52%), with febrile neutropenia in 6% and leukopenia in 11% of patients.5,6 Hematologic events were generally manageable with reactive G-CSF support, transient dose interruptions, and 22% dose reductions, resulting in ≤5% treatment discontinuations. In our cohort, severe neutropenia was less frequent and no febrile episodes were observed. Treatment discontinuation due to toxicity was uncommon, supporting the feasibility of proactive management strategies. These results likely reflect broader early use of G-CSF prophylaxis (22.7% primary) and align with the PRIMED trial, which showed that mandatory G-CSF prophylaxis significantly reduced grade ≥3 neutropenia to 16%, eliminated febrile episodes, and resulted in manageable dose reductions (22%) and discontinuations (8%) at extended follow-up.22 Comparable benefits were seen in the real-world study by Nanda et al.,20 in which G-CSF primary prophylaxis (48%) corresponded to grade 3/4 neutropenia in 27% of patients, with 31% dose reduction and 3.4% neutropenia-related discontinuations. In line with these findings, a real-world analysis by Pieniążek et al.23 including 83 patients reported primary G-CSF prophylaxis in 42.2% of cases. Neutropenia was observed in 63.9% of patients, with grade ≥3 events in 44.6%, but only one case of febrile neutropenia. Treatment discontinuation due to complications was reported in 3.4% of patients.24 Similarly, Kalinsky et al.13 reported broad G-CSF adoption concomitantly with SG (58%), 33% cases of neutropenia (grade not specified), and a 7% discontinuation rate, further supporting the role of structured prophylaxis. Studies with lower G-CSF adoption or earlier clinical experience generally reported greater hematologic toxicity and discontinuation rates. For example, Alaklabi et al.18 reported grade ≥3 neutropenia in 35.7%, dose reduction in 51.3%, and discontinuation in 13.2%, whereas Reinisch et al.15 described 27.9% grade ≥3 neutropenia and 39.5% dose reductions. Overall, despite potential differences due to patient heterogeneity and supportive-care practices, our results align with and reinforce existing evidence that early and flexible integration of G-CSF prophylaxis in routine care can help mitigate hematologic toxicity, reduce treatment discontinuations, and ensure safe and sustained exposure to SG in clinical practice. Regarding gastrointestinal toxicities, in ASCENT, diarrhea occurred in 64% of patients (grade ≥3 in 12%), typically early in treatment, and was effectively managed with loperamide and dose adjustment (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2026.108251).5,6 These events rarely led to permanent discontinuation, with dose reductions in 22% of patients. In our cohort, gastrointestinal events were mostly mild and manageable, with a low incidence of high-grade diarrhea. Despite limited use of formal loperamide prophylaxis (6.1%), early symptom recognition, patient education, and timely dose modification were effective in preventing severe events. The PRIMED trial similarly reported any-grade diarrhea in 34% and grade ≥3 diarrhea in 4% of patients, confirming that structured prophylaxis and early intervention can prevent treatment-limiting toxicity.22 In real-world cohorts reported by Pieniążek, Kalinsky, Alaklabi, and Reinisch, grade ≥3 diarrhea ranged from ∼4% to ∼19%. However, none of these analyses provided detailed information on prophylactic loperamide use, suggesting gastrointestinal toxicity was mainly managed through reactive interventions. These data indicate that SG-related diarrhea is predictable and rarely dose-limiting when managed proactively. Collectively, the findings indicate that implementing adequate prophylactic measures and structured early-reactive management are essential to minimize SG-associated toxicity. Early administration of G-CSF prophylaxis and timely intervention for gastrointestinal symptoms reduce the incidence of high-grade AEs and decrease the rate of treatment discontinuations.

Among other gastrointestinal side effects observed in this study, nausea and vomiting were infrequent and generally mild, with only one case each of grade 3 severity. This favorable outcome likely reflects the proactive administration of antiemetic regimens consistent with established guidelines. Only a few patients needed the addition of an neurokinin-1 receptor antagonist after symptoms began for secondary prevention. These findings highlight the importance of flexible dosing and consistent supportive care in optimizing patient outcomes. Of note, in real-world practice, reports of subjective side effects may be incomplete due to gaps in clinical documentation.25,26 Also, since clinical practice is less strictly regulated than clinical trials, dose reductions often occur earlier and more frequently. This may contribute to a lower incidence of reported side effects in routine care compared with clinical trials.26

The main strengths of our study are its multicenter design and large sample size, which provide solid statistical power to assess treatment outcomes and AEs. Comprehensive data collection using rigorous real-world standards further enhances internal validity. The study also included patient groups often excluded or underrepresented in clinical trials, such as those with CNS metastases. Nevertheless, the restriction to an exclusively Italian population may limit the generalizability of these findings to other health care systems. The cohort included some of the earliest patients treated with SG in Italy after regulatory approval, many of whom had a higher disease burden and poorer prognosis. This may underestimate outcomes compared with later-treated populations. As with all retrospective real-world analyses, the study is subject to selection bias, missing data, and heterogeneity in clinical practice and reporting across centers, despite standardized data collection. Finally, subgroup analyses were prespecified as clinically relevant but should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating given the observational design, real-world heterogeneity, and potential for multiple comparisons.

While our findings confirm the real-world applicability and consistent efficacy of SG in patients with mTNBC, future studies with patient-reported outcomes and health-economic data are needed. Such research will refine treatment sequencing, identify predictive biomarkers for efficacy and toxicity, and guide personalized therapy. Recent evidence has begun to identify potential clinical predictors of SG benefit. In a multicenter analysis by Pieniążek et al.23 including 83 patients with mTNBC, baseline hemoglobin level and neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) emerged as independent prognostic factors for survival. Lower hemoglobin levels were associated with shorter PFS (HR 0.82, P = 0.03), whereas an elevated baseline NLR predicted worse OS (HR 1.18, P = 0.03). These routine hematologic and clinical variables may support prognostic stratification and treatment optimization in real-world practice. Findings from Morganti et al.24 suggest that DNADX subclasses may also help predict response to SG. DNADX is a plasma assay that uses low-pass whole-genome sequencing of circulating tumor DNA to classify cancers into five biological subtypes: TF-low, with low tumor DNA levels and less aggressive disease; CNA-flat, indicating genetically stable tumors; luminal-high regulated, representing hormone-driven cancers; basal-related, associated with aggressive, basal-like tumors; and proliferative, marked by rapidly dividing, highly aggressive tumors. In this retrospective study of 184 patients with metastatic BC, real-world outcomes were consistent with prior clinical evidence. For patients with TNBC, the median TTNT was 4.3 months and the median OS was 10.4 months. For those with HR-positive/HER2-negative disease, TTNT was 4.2 months and OS was 10.2 months.24 Among the 74 patients with available plasma samples, survival varied by DNADX subtype: TF-low tumors had the longest TTNT (4.3 months) and OS (11.3 months), whereas basal-related and proliferative tumors had the poorest outcomes, with OS as low as 4.5 months.24 Despite limitations such as sample size and variability in sampling, the findings support DNADX as a promising noninvasive biomarker, with ongoing trials expected to further assess its predictive value for SG treatment (SATEEN study: NCT06100874; SERIES study: NCT06263543). Prospective validation and integration with molecular or genomic biomarkers could further enhance patient selection and inform individualized ADC-based treatment strategies.

Conclusions

This multicenter real-world study confirms that SG is effective and safe for treating patients with mTNBC representative of daily oncology practice. With 256 individuals, this cohort is the largest European and the second-largest global real-world group reported so far. These findings support and extend those from clinical trials and earlier real-world studies. By including patients with ECOG ≥1 and brain metastases, the study shows that SG can be used for a broad range of challenging and often underrepresented groups.

Beyond treatment outcomes, this study also shows that carefully managing side effects is key to improving the tolerability of SG and keeping patients on therapy in real-world settings. The low rates of serious hematologic and gastrointestinal AEs, absence of febrile neutropenia, and limited treatment discontinuations likely reflect proactive and flexible supportive care, such as early or preventive use of G-CSF, individualized antiemetic plans, and timely dose changes. These findings align with emerging evidence from other studies and highlight the importance of preventing and quickly managing side effects to keep SG treatment safe and ongoing.20,22

These results highlight the importance of real-world evidence in complementing randomized trial data, informing clinical decision making, and improving treatment strategies in routine practice. Future research should prioritize long-term results, biomarker identification for outcome prediction, and optimal management for high-risk groups, such as older patients and those with CNS involvement.
