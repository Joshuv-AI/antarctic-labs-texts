Perception of neonatal outcomes: lessons learned from positive health
Authors: Mary Lauren Neel, Kristen L Benninger
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraditional measures of neurodevelopmental outcomes in preterm children are deficit focused and not informed by parent and family perspectives. Parents care less about medical classification of severity of disability and more about the child’s ability to function and participate. Positive health encompasses a broad and dynamic perspective on health that goes beyond the absence of disease and focuses on physical, mental, and social well-being, life-satisfaction, and goal attainment. Measuring positive health as an outcome in children at high risk of disability is challenging but possible. Further exploration of positive health as an outcome may meet the needs of both families and researchers.Keywords: Neurodevelopment, Positive health, Well-being, Newborn, Infant, Prematurity

Traditional neurodevelopmental impairment-focused outcomes

Studies of preterm infant outcomes have historically included mortality and “moderate-severe disability.” Moderate-severe disability in these studies is typically defined by moderate-severe cognitive impairment, severe motor impairment, moderate-severe cerebral palsy (CP), blindness, or deafness.1–5 However, medical advancements such as cochlear implants or hearing amplification continue to change the landscape of “severe” disabilities such as deafness. These adaptations begin to challenge our assumptions of severity.

Not only has the functional impact of some of these “severe” impairments changed through medical advancements, as longer-term follow-up has become more common, recognition of other domain-specific challenges faced by preterm-born children has increased.6 These include diagnoses such as learning disabilities, attentional deficits, executive function challenges, and autism spectrum disorders, that were previously excluded from definitions of moderate-severe disability.2,7,8 Although describing the full scope of neurodevelopment challenges after preterm birth is an important step forward, it is notable that these additional outcomes are commonly described as “minor” or “low severity morbidities.”2,6

Descriptions such as “minor” may distinguish these more prevalent outcomes from those classically included in moderate-severe disability definitions. However, severity of disability is a matter of perspective, and this classification is not universally accepted.5,9,10 Disabilities that are severely impactful to one person and family may be less impactful to another and vice versa. Broad classification of disability as major, minor, or severe implies a universal standard of severity that simply does not exist.

Parent perspectives

In most studies, the severity classification of disabilities in preterm infants reflects perspectives and expert consensus of researchers and clinicians on outcomes that are most impactful to children and families.11 However, more recent studies show that parents, families, and children themselves view their outcomes more positively than medical professionals.9,12–14 A recent study compared medical categorization of preterm neurodevelopment with parental classification (no impairment, mild/moderate impairment, severe impairment) at their child’s 18–21 month corrected age (CA) clinic visit and found that agreement between medical and parental classification was poor (kappa = 0.3). Parents generally classified their child’s development as normal or less impaired than the medical classification. Seventeen percent of children met the medical categorization for “severe impairment”, while only 2 % of parents classified their child as having severe impairment.9 It is important to note that these parents and children are not denying the fact that their child has a medical condition. Parents of preterm-born toddlers express concern over their child’s health and development,4,9,13 while still perceiving their child as less impaired than medical classifications would suggest.9 Similarly, preterm-born young adults report more functional limitations than their term-born counterparts, but do not report differences in quality of life.12

This discordance between medical and family perspectives may stem from differences between professional and personal values. Parents of preterm-born toddlers value domains not often included in medical definitions of neurodevelopmental impairment, specifically feeding, language, behavior, and function.9,15 Parents desire a strength-based approach to reporting, emphasizing what their child can do with the right supports rather than what their child cannot do.4 Parents express that their child’s ability to function and participate in individual, family, and community settings is more impactful than their diagnoses.4,9

This parental emphasis on function is consistent with the World Health Organization’s International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) framework,16 an overall structure for the classification of human health and disability. The six “F-words” of child development and neurodisability have stemmed from this ICF framework. These include: function, family, fitness, fun, friends, and future (Fig. 1).17 The “F-words” suggest outcomes and goals for all children, including those born preterm with and without disabilities, that families may find more meaningful than neonatal follow-up’s traditional categorization of impairment. The “F-words” seek to capture the child as a whole person and to support their ability to participate and thrive in their environment. The future is a major concern for many parents of preterm children – even when their children are currently doing well.14 In contrast to definitions of severity of neurodevelopmental impairment, the “F-words” framework promotes a positive health perspective by emphasizing each child’s unique strengths and interests, guiding families and clinicians to focus on capabilities and potential rather than limitations. This, in turn, allows parents to envision and promote the best future for their child.

Positive health

The ICF framework dovetails nicely with some models of positive health outcomes. The concept of positive health was initially recognized by large public health organizations in the 1940s when the World Health Organization (WHO) defined health as a “state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity.”18 Continued emphasis on the importance of positive health is reflected in the most recent version of the Healthy People initiative, Healthy People 2030, which states an overarching goal for people to “attain healthy, thriving lives and well-being free of preventable disease, disability, injury, and premature death.”19 However, no universal definition exists for positive health and some studies still define positive health only as the absence of disease.20 This definition does not work well for children born preterm and predisposed to lifelong disabilities. Obviously, the absence of disease and other morbidities is enviable, but in some cases, this cannot be achieved.21 What then, does positive health look like for these children and families?

Positive health in children

Positive health encompasses a broad and dynamic perspective on health that goes beyond the absence of disease and focuses on physical, mental, and social well-being, life-satisfaction, and goal attainment.21–24 Pediatric positive health incorporates critical developmental processes and developmental plasticity.25–27 A child’s trajectory leading to positive health reflects the accumulation of personal and environmental strengths and interactions between a child and their environment over time—a concept effectively illustrated by the ICF framework and emphasized in the “F-words.”17,28 Thus, early childhood represents a critical time for fostering positive assets (both personal and environmental) and integrated functions (e.g., physical activity, balanced diet, achievement, self-regulation) to promote positive health.22,25–27 Beyond lack of disease, current perspectives attempt to identify ‘health assets’ or features leading to positive health.22 An important determinant of positive health outcomes in children is the overall well-being of their parents and the broader family system29–31—reflected in ‘Family’, one of the “F-words” in the ICF framework.17,28 Positive, nurturing and stimulating early relationships, parent mental health, parenting self-efficacy, social support, and healthy and supportive family environments are especially critical in early childhood to build the foundation for long-term relational and mental health.29–31

Measuring the more nuanced outcome of positive health is challenging.23,32 As compared to presence or absence of disease, measurement tools are not yet available to capture the full scope of positive health.23 Quantification of positive health in young children is further challenged because assessments of life satisfaction, meaning and purpose require self-reflection and a sense of identity that is just emerging in early childhood.27 Many existing measures of positive health in children target adolescents and are not appropriate for younger children.27

More specific instruments have been proposed to quantify aspects of positive health in young children. These include the Healthy Pathways Child-Report Scales, which assesses subjective well-being, comfort, and resilience.33 However, the Healthy Pathways Child-Report Scales also include some aspects that may promote positive health, like school engagement or balanced nutrition, rather than serve as a positive health outcome measure per se.33 Other measures, such as the Child Flourishing Index (CFI), have sought to quantify aspects of child flourishing, including curiosity, persistence, and self-regulation.34 While the CFI is unique in that it can be used in children as young as 6 months, flourishing is only one aspect of positive health.35 As described above, positive health is a broad construct including all aspects of health: mental, social, emotional, and physical while flourishing refers to mental well-being.34 A recent scoping review sought to collate current definitions and measures of child thriving.36 This review identified gaps in the current literature, including a dearth of measures of thriving in young children and diverse populations,36 including children born preterm and/or with disabilities. It also highlights the need for a clear definition of child thriving.

Resilience is a component of positive health that has been studied in high-risk infants.27,37,38 To date, there is not a standard definition of “resilience” in formerly preterm infants. Resilience in this population is often defined as performing in the average or above average range on standardized developmental assessments, despite the developmental risks presented by prematurity.37,39–41 Other authors have proposed more nuanced definitions of resilience, including “better-than-expected” outcomes based on individual perinatal risk factors.37 Regardless of the definition of resilience, positive adaptation in the face of risk or adversity promotes resilience and, in many cases, positive health.37,40,42 However, as historically marginalized groups have highlighted, the necessity of resilience can be a burden to those who have no choice but to be resilient.43 Thus for some people, the expectation of resilience can, in fact, undermine positive health.

Studies assessing positive health in children

Though measuring positive health as an outcome in children is challenging, it is possible to achieve. Studies are beginning to include measurement of positive health, even in children at high risk of disability. In a study of 10-year-olds born at <28 weeks gestation, researchers developed the Positive Child Health Index (PCHI).20,44 The PCHI includes 11 chronic health conditions, such as asthma, intellectual disability, and anxiety. Child scores on the PCHI were positively associated with parent-reported quality of life, measured with the validated Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory.20,44,45 While this study seeks to quantify positive health in former preterm children, the definition and tool used does not include all aspects of positive health, instead defining positive health as the presence or absence of chronic conditions.

In contrast, the Environmental influences on Child Health Outcomes (ECHO) Program supplements measures indicating presence or absence of disease with a ‘Positive Health Outcome Measure’ comprised of global health, life satisfaction, and meaning and purpose (Fig. 2).22 Each component of the Positive Health Outcome Measure is assessed with an age-specific NIH Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System (PROMIS) instrument (Table 1).46–48 The ECHO program, involving longitudinal assessment of >50,000 children, will provide important information about how positive health develops during childhood; the social, family, and physical environments that influence positive health; and if specific sensitive periods exist when some factors have disproportionate effects on outcomes. One of the first ECHO cohort studies to use aspects of the Positive Health Outcome Measure found that while young children with chronic illness had lower scores for general health measures, their life-satisfaction on parent-report measures was comparable to children without chronic illness.49 This relationship holds true in older children and adolescents with chronic illnesses self-reporting similar levels of positive health as peers without chronic illnesses except in children with chronic pain or depression.50 In the first study to focus these positive health measures in former preterm infants, Logan et al. examined the impact of neonatal morbidities and neurodevelopmental impairment on positive health in three longitudinal cohorts of preterm infants from the ECHO program.51 While bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), intraventricular hemorrhage (IVH), and cerebral palsy (CP) were associated with lower parent-reported positive health scores during early childhood, none were associated with lower child-reported positive health scores during adolescence.51 This study provides evidence that perception of well-being or life-satisfaction may evolve over time as children grow and develop.

To facilitate practical integration of positive health into pediatric care, a research team from the Netherlands and a focus group of children used qualitative research methods to co-design the ‘My Positive Health’ (MPH) dialogue tool.52,53 In this study, children’s self-described, broad view of health included ‘feeling good about yourself’ and ‘being able to participate in activities’. The MPH dialogue tool for children contains 6 dimensions of health (Fig. 3): 1) my body, 2) my feelings and thoughts, 3) feeling good about yourself, 4) participation, 5) now and in the future, and 6) daily life. Using this tool, the group aims to help children share what is important to their health. In 2025, the group published a mixed-methods study sharing facilitators and barriers to implementation of the MPH dialogue tool for children with a chronic condition.53 Despite some logistical and organizational challenges inherent to a busy outpatient pediatric clinic, participating healthcare providers and children reported overall positive experiences using the tool. Children reported the tool helped facilitate personalized and meaningful conversations with their healthcare provider and enabled them to reflect and discuss their overall health status, function, and goals.53 The domains identified by this focus group are similar to those identified by interviews with other youth about positive health priorities.54,55

Conclusion

Positive health is more than absence of disease. Thus, many aspects of positive health are attainable even for individuals at risk for or with disabilities. Positive health includes all aspects of health, including physical, mental and social domains. One challenge of such a broad and comprehensive construct is measurement. Researchers studying positive health outcomes must be clear in their constructs and tools.

Although the importance of re-focusing measurement and reporting of neonatal outcomes to include priorities of children and families cannot be understated, an objective, standardized, assessment of neurodevelopmental outcome with strong psychometric properties remains crucial. Additionally, it is important to have measures that can be used across large clinical and research networks and across cultures. When understanding the long-term developmental impact of a new medication or procedure, neither child nor parent review of function or assessment of neurodevelopmental impairment alone are adequate to quantify the full impact on child outcome.

Even with measurement challenges, positive health outcomes offer clinicians and researchers the opportunity to better align with families and to discuss outcomes that are important to them. Studies have shown that aspects of positive health can be modified by positive childhood experiences,56–61 family or personal factors.33,37 The outcome of positive health as assessed by standardized, validated, age-specific measures and evaluated in large, longitudinal cohort studies such as ECHO could provide the foundation for incorporating measures of positive health into assessment of outcomes in former preterm infants. Once clinicians and researchers are aligned with our patients and families about meaningful outcomes, we can work to measure these outcomes and to design and test interventions to improve outcomes for all high-risk infants. A truly comprehensive evaluation of neonatal outcomes should include assessment of function and quality of life, physical, mental, and social well-being, alongside measures of cognitive, language and motor function.
