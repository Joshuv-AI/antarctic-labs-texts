Harnessing the potential of Alcaligenes faecalis for optimum biodegradation of norfloxacin spiked wastewater using response surface methodology
Authors: Akhtar Ali, Muhammad Farhan, Aziz ur Rehman, Amina Kanwal, Faiza Sharif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of antibiotics is increasing and they enter water untreated. These antibiotics tendency to bioaccumulate and contribute to bacterial resistance. This study is designed to explore and optimize the potential of resistant bacteria for the treatment of norfloxacin (NOR) antibiotic spiked wastewater. The Alcaligenes faecalis ABR-14 was isolated from wastewater and sludge samples and was identified using 16S rRNA sequencing (Macrogen Inc. Geumchen-gu, South Korea). The 1515 bp was submitted in NCBI Genbank and the accession number of ‘PV364597.1’ was obtained. The results demonstrated that Alcaligenes faecalis ABR-14 efficiently broke down NOR reaching 96% degradation at 100 mg/L in 10 days under ideal circumstances such as 35 °C, pH 6.5, sodium acetate, NH4Cl and 105 CFU/mL. Two significate parameters (pH and Nitrogen (N) source), one less-significant parameter (inoculum density) and 3 non-significant parameters (antibiotic concentration, temperature and Carbon (C) source) based on Central Composite Design (CCD) and Response Surface Methodology (RSM) were revealed. The Hanes plot gives straight line, where R2 is 0.99, Vmax = 200.21, Km = 133.4. The plot between experimental and predicted values represent the best fit of the values based on straightness (R2 = 0.91). ANOVA for CCD present the p value below 0.05 which represents the significance and critically important factors. This study is among the first highlights the potential application of Alcaligenes faecalis ABR-14 for norfloxacin biodegradation. The generation of non-toxic metabolites makes this eco-friendly and sustainable. Introduction

An increasing use of fluoroquinolone antibiotics in humans and veterinary is creating a serious ecological and health hazards. These wastewater (containing antibiotics) are entering into the natural water streams without treatment, which is creating resistance in bacteria1. Norfloxacin (NOR) is one of the widely used antibiotics in human and agricultural practices. The extensive usage, their bio-accumulative nature and environmental persistence of this antibiotic have raised concerns in recent years. Antibiotics taken by humans or animals are released via faeces and urine as parent molecule and metabolites2. As a result, comparatively large levels of these antibiotic have been found not only in wastewater, but also in soils and groundwater3. They are released from a variety of sources, such as hospitals, pharmaceutical companies, municipalities, industrial effluents, animal farms and exerts deleterious effects on non-target creatures4. Being persistent they pose a major threat to human health and the ecosystem health5.

Norfloxacin may lead to the development of antibiotic resistance as it degrades slowly6. In dry and semi-arid areas, untreated wastewater is often used for irrigation due to a lack of freshwater7. Large doses of antibiotics are introduced into the soil by irrigation with such effluent and the use of manure in fields. It has been shown that many antibiotics contained in soil are phytotoxic, which lowers the output of significant crops8. Manure is also known as a potent source of antibiotics, it is often incorporated into the soil prior to seed planting. Through this it becomes one of the leading sources of exposing crops to antibiotics, either directly or indirectly. Although seeds have protective coverings, still these pollutants obstruct the seed germination9. Antibiotics do enter agricultural land through various pathways, including: (i) pharmaceutical companies wastewater, (ii) faeces and urine of grazing animals10, (iv) use of wastewater for irrigation, and (v) use of biosolids as compost and manure11. In middle and low income countries like Pakistan, hospital wastewater is not treated and is released into the rivers and water bodies. These eventually results in the build-up of antibiotics in agricultural soils12.

Bioremediation is the way to remove antibiotics from environment in natural, economical and sustainable way. In situ bioremediation, which takes place at the location where pollution has occurred and ex situ bioremediation, in which polluted soil or water is removed and treated in a laboratory are the two forms of bioremediation that are currently present13. Ex situ bioremediation is more costly than in situ procedures because it needs excavation, but it is also quicker and simpler to regulate than in situ approaches14. A mixed bacterial consortium consisting of Proteobacteria and Bacteroidia was used by researchers to investigate the degradation and metabolic routes of CIP15. Researchers are more interested in the microbial strains that are more resistant against antibiotics and have the high capabilities of degradation.

The use of resistant microbes for the antibiotic biodegradation is reported in number of studies. Such as, sulfadiazine and sulfamethoxazole16, trimethoprim, clindamycin17, ciprofloxacin18, Vancomycin19,20, β-lactamase (Muraleedharan et al. 30), norfloxacin21. The biodegradation results in the non-toxic metabolites and this is evident due to their low antimicrobial activities22. This research is unique in respect that it is trying to use notorious (problematic) NOR resistant microbes to fix antibiotic pollution in wastewaters. This is a new way of looking into the environmental problem, where we can use the negative character (resistance) of one microbe for our benefit (environmental clean-up). The present research is intending to i) isolate and identify bacteria from wastewater drains that is capable to degrade NOR, ii) degradation of NOR, iii) optimization of parameters using Central Composite Design and Response Surface Methodology.

Materials and methods

Chemical preparation, media preparation and sterilization

The chemicals used in this study were purchased from Sigma Aldrich (UK), Biochem Chemopharma (France) and Fischer Scientific (UK). All glassware, culture loops, needles and growth media were sterilized before the experiment to prevent contamination. NOR infusion was purchased from pharmacy in Lahore, Pakistan. Standard and stock solution were prepared by dissolving NOR in methanol. The solutions were stored in amber glass at low temperature to maintain stability. Nutritional agar for bioremediation experiment was prepared by dissolving 28 g in 1000 mL of distilled water. The mixture was homogenized, boiled and autoclaved at 121 °C for 15 min. Nutrient broth (NB) was prepared using similar method by dissolving 13 g in 1000 mL of distilled water and autoclaved.

Bacterial strain isolation and identification

Standard protocols were used to collect sludge and wastewater samples in sterile glass bottles from sewage drains located across Lahore. To reduce physiochemical change, these samples were kept at 4 °C and transported in ice boxes. Using MSM (minimal salt medium) with a pH of 6.8–7.0, bacterial strains that resist NOR were isolated from wastewater and sludge samples. In a 250 mL flask, 150 mL of sterilized MSM, 5 mg/L of NOR and 5 mL of wastewater sample was mixed. The microbes were cultured for 10 days at 37 °C on a rotating shaker at 100 rpm. Afterwards, culture (10 mL) were moved to new medium (MSM) each week and the concentration of antibiotic was progressively raised to 100 mg/L (maximum). After the isolation, 150 µL of the diluted culture was distributed onto petri plates carefully with 100 mg/L of NOR along with the nutritional agar after serial dilutions had been made. The streak plate technique was used carefully to purify bacterial colonies that showed best growth. 16S rRNA sequencing was used to identify the bacterial isolates, using universal forward (5ʹGGATTAGATACCCTGGTA3ʹ) and reverse primers (5ʹCCGTCAATTCMTTTRAGTTT3ʹ) by Macrogen (Macrogen Inc. Geumchen-gu, South Korea). Identified strains were sub- cultured after every two weeks to ensure viability after being moved on nutrient agar slants for storage.

Experimental setup and analysis

Inoculum was prepared by transferring a 10 mL loop of separated microbes to 100 mL of sterile NB and incubating it for 24 h at 37 °C on shaking incubator. A haemocytometer was used for measuring the number of cells present in this solution which served as the inoculum for further degradation tests23. The shake flask technique was used to evaluate the bacterial strains capacity for degradation. Each flask had inoculum along with 30 mL of MSM, and a specified concentration of NOR. The flasks were then shacked at 100 rpm and incubated at 37 °C. Additionally, control flasks with the same NOR concentration but no inoculum was set up. Every 24 h, samples (5 mL each) were taken out in order to analyse the remaining antibiotics. Oasis HLB cartridges were used for solid phase extraction (SPE) with preconditioning of Milli-Q water (10 mL) and methanol (10 mL) having flowrate of 10 mL/min. The analytes were rinsed with 10 mL of the cartridge’s ethanol concentrated with a mild N2-stream and then re-dissolved in 1 mL of methanol. The final extract was filtered using nylon syringe filters (0.2 mm) into a 2 mL amber glass vial and kept at 18 °C until analysis. The HPLC–MS Agilent Liquid Chromatography 1100 series24 was used to perform the NOR analysis with limit of quantification is 0.13 μg/g.

Results and discussion

Antibacterial resistance has become a critical issue globally, as it has created a challenge that enhances bacterial resistance to antibiotics27. The severity of this event has led WHO to rank antibiotic resistance as the 3rd most urgent health issue in the twenty-first century28. One of the primary cause of antibiotic resistant bacteria in various environmental compartments is faecal waste29. It has been reported that gram-negative bacteria usually exhibit multiple-antibiotic resistance30. The massive misuse of antibiotics is speeding up the frequency of prevalence and transmission of resistance in most water bodies31. This is because that the bacteria naturally attain antibiotic resistance as part of their survival tactic against competitors32. One of the potential way to remove antibiotic in sustainable and eco-friendly way is to use these resistant bacteria and harness their potential22,33.

Selection and identification of isolates

In the current research, a number of different samples were checked on the resistant isolates, of which 22 are reported in Fig. 1. The results obtained by this set of twenty-two isolates were initially negative but were screened in the upper concentration of NOR (100 mg/L). The process of screening was done based on the capacity of the isolates to grow in the presence of an elevated level of norfloxacin. There was very good growth of the isolate ABR-14 in the largest concentration, i.e., 100 mg/L. Other isolates were not as resistant, such as ABR-12 and ABR-16, which did not survive even after 80 mg/L. Equally, ABR-18, ABR-44, ABR-45, and ABR-66 did not exhibit very good growth at concentrations above 40 mg/L. Good growth at NOR will be the indicator of resistance, and poor growth at NOR will be the indicator of sensitivity (non-resistance). ABR-14 was one of the isolates that was selected to continue experimentation and was determined via 16S rRNA sequencing. The 1515 bp was submitted to NCBI Genbank and obtained an accession number of PV364591.1. The strain ABR-14 was identified as Alcaligenes faecalis strain ABR14, and a phylogenetic tree was provided in Fig. 2 to demonstrate the relationship with other related strains of bacterial species. As established by the BLAST analysis (Table S1), the protein-coding DNA was 99.7%, 99.1%, and 98.9% similar to Alcaligenes faecalis strain NBRC 13,111 (NR_113606.1), Alcaligenes faecalis strain IAM 12,369 (NR_043445.1), and NR_043445.1 (NR_104977.1), respectively. Isolation of bacteria is a suitable and environmentally friendly method to eliminate antibiotics in the environment. The bacteria should also have adequate resistance to be able to survive in harsh settings. Prior studies have examined a variety of different resistant bacterial isolates, including E. coli34, Enterococcus35, Acinetobacter species36, E. faecium37, and Aeromonas hydrophila sp. N215-138, E. faecium and E. coli39, Thiobacillus22 as well as Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes and Proteobacteria34.

Influence of various conditions on biodegradation of Norfloxacin

Previous research has shown that several resistant bacterial isolates were used to effectively degrade antibiotics such as clindamycin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole, and trimethoprim17, vancomycin20 and norfloxacin22. The difference in antibiotic’s physicochemical properties could be the reason of their wide degradation patterns by bacterial strains40. Microbial cellular mechanisms help the degradation of antibiotics in various environment11. Antibiotics can be degraded with the help of abiotic processes (reduction, photolysis, sorption, and hydrolysis) and biotic processes (which include microorganisms)22,41.

The degradation of norfloxacin was up to 87% in 2 5 mg/L (low concentrations) whereas, only 35% degradation was observed in 100 mg/L (high concentration). At 100 mg/L, the bacterial growth shows longer lag phase compared to the short lag phase at low concentration (Fig. 3a). The degradation of norfloxacin was low at 25 mg/L on days 2–6, then it began to increase gradually till the 14th day. The degradation did not increase around day 14–18. The stationary phase begins after day 18, and subsequently, the decline phase begins. Maximum degradation of 87%, 69%, 51%, and 35% was observed at 25, 50, 75, and 100 mg/L, respectively, on the 18th day. The temperature change has been illustrated in Fig. 3b. At 45 °C, the least degradation (32%) was characterized. Whereas, the maximum degradation (47%) was at 35 °C. In the experiment on temperature, the concentration was maintained at 100 mg/L. By the 18th day, the maximum degradation observed was 39, 47, 35, 42 and 32% at 30, 35, 37, 40, and 45 °C, respectively (Fig. 3b). At pH6.5, the maximum degradation of 63% was recorded, but comparatively less degradation was recorded at pH8 (43%) (Fig. 3c). The lag phase was reduced to the 4th day, and the log phase started that lasting 18 days.

These carbon source provide energy (in the form of high energy electrons) and nutrients to the bacterial cells. Carbon sources can also act as a stimulus or co-metabolite for bacterial growth, and they may cause a significant decrease in the log phase. The capacity of A. faecalis ABR-14 to degrade norfloxacin in the presence of glucose, yeast extract, and sodium acetate was tested (Fig. 3d). The degradation in the presence of sodium acetate, yeast extract, and glucose was 72, 61, and 53%, respectively, which suggests their role in co-metabolic enhancement. In co-metabolism, the carbon source catalyses the reaction or enzymes that are required for norfloxacin degradation. The co-substrates may cause slow degradation and thus bacteria do not participate in the degradation of complex molecules (antibiotic). In addition, nitrogen is also a very significant nutrient that stimulates and boosts the bacterial growth and biodegradation process. The effects of sodium nitrate and ammonium chloride were also tested to increase the norfloxacin biodegradation (Fig. 3e). Similar to C-source, the promotion of degradation by nitrogen may relate to the involvement of nitrogen in enzyme and protein synthesis42. The greatest degradation that was noticed was 76 and 68% in the presence of NH4CI and NaNO3, respectively. To increase the degradation of norfloxacin, an optimum nitrogen source is necessary so as to obtain maximum cellular activity. This also indicates that excess or limited nitrogen supply may cause the destruction of the bacterial metabolism43. The inoculum density is the another important factor that aids in the rapid biodegradation. With a rise in inoculum density the degradation of norfloxacin, increased up to 96% in 10 days at 105 CFU (Fig. 3f), under ideal circumstances such as 35 °C, pH 6.5, sodium acetate, NH4Cl and 105 CFU/mL.

For optimum degradation of norfloxacin the both biotic and abiotic factors need to be set. Qian et al.22 reported co-metabolism of norfloxacin and nitrate using Thiobacillus in batch experiment and the maximum removal efficiency was 85.2%. Dlamini et al.44 reported the 95.1% removal efficiency of NOR with the use of laccase immobilized on zeolitic imidazolate framework. The use of glucose and the amino acid supplements do not enhance norfloxacin degradation in significant manner. However, if these are used in combination of metal ions (Mn2+, Fe3+) then substantial increase in degradation happens, which is due to the synergistic interaction45. According to Mohy-u-Din et al.39, 108 CFU/mL is needed for 97% and 90% removal of CIP was recorded by E. faecium and E. coli, respectively. Similarly, other strains of bacteria, like Lactobacillus gasseri, Enterobacter and Bacillus showed the degradation efficiency of 100%, 96% and 75%, respectively46. However, Aeromonas hydrophila sp. N215-1 only showed the degradation of 25% of NOR within 24 h (Chen et al. 38). Thiobacillus demonstrated the 85.2% NOR through co-metabolism in the presence of nitrate22.

Degradation kinetics

In the kinetic study, a straight line was produced when the rate of reaction and substrate concentration were plotted using a single substrate. In most cases, they assume a hyperbolic curve when an enzyme-catalysed reaction takes place. At low concentration of substrate, there was an increase in the rate of reaction, this is because there are free active sites at which the reactions occur in the enzyme. Such vacant sites enable additional substrate to attach to them47. Compared to that, at high substrate concentration, the enzyme was saturated and therefore led to a constant rate of the reaction. When this reaches a point, all the active sites are converted, and the reaction rate is at peak. This is the maximum rate of reaction at the saturation point, which is denoted as Vmax. The affinity between an enzyme and its target also comes into play, this is the correlation between a reaction rate as well as the concentration of substances in question. This is a constant called ‘Michaelis constant (Km)’ which is inversely proportional to enzyme affinity. In practice, to be a substitute for the term ‘Km’ the term ‘Ks’ in most cases is utilized, and it indicates the concentration of substrate at which enzyme attains 50% of Vmax (Mohy-u-Din et al. 39). Ks and Vmax are significant parameters to obtain the rate of reaction and, in particular, the product formation (Fig. 4A). It also assists in forecasting whether the availability of the substrate is a restriction to the rate of reaction or not. When assayed in MSM, E. faecium had a Vmax of 5.99 compared to E. coli with a Vmax of 8.25, confirming the previously established kinetics involved in the degradation of CIP24. The first exposure of antibiotics to microbes determines the outcomes of the microbial breakdown, and inhibiting outcomes are evident at high concentrations48. Dlamini et al.44 reported Km (39.1) and Vmax (0.92) of norfloxacin degradation with laccase immobilization method. Yang et al.45 reported that the key enzymes involved in degradation of norfloxacin are laccase and cytochrome P450. The laccase activity boost in presence of carbon sources and may reach up to 2.7 fold enhancement. However, laccase activity may increase by 9.3 fold with Fe+3. The rate of CIP degradation that was attained by Tran et al.49 is 91.4%. Pan and Chu24 recorded the influence of temperature and its impact on the degradation of antibiotics in various temperature ranges. 98% NOR degradation of E. coli was measured at 20 °C starting concentration of 30 mg/L. Chen et al.50 provided the kinetics of analysis and estimated the semi-saturated (Ks), inhibitory constant (Ki), and specific growth rate of 1.9 mg/L, 23.9 mg/L, and 0.2/h, respectively. Mohy-u-Din et al.39 noted the maximum degradation was on the 18th day at 40 °C in the case of E. faecium and 37 °C in the case of E. coli. There is an excellent correlation between the experimental results and kinetic models since kinetic analysis had an R2 value of 0.94. The low Henry’s law constants i.e. KH = 8.8 × 10–13 Pa.m3mmol-1 and high molecular weight during norfloxacin degradation, is the indicator that hydrolysis or volatilization has not occurred and the major route is the biodegradation22.

Optimization using central composite design

The contribution of six discrete factors to the degradation of norfloxacin was explored with the help of central composite design (CCD). The experimental design (Table 1) contains the number of runs, experimental, and predicted values. The graph that was plotted between predicted and experimental values shows its straightness (R2 = 0.91), which is the best fitting of values (Fig. 4b). Table 2 shows the ANOVA table of CCD. When p value is less than 0.05, it means the factor is highly critical and significant. Equation of the dependent and the independent factors is shown in Eq. 4. Three factors were found to be non-significant: NOR concentration, temperature and carbon source (Table 2, Fig. 5a-c). Inoculum density was less significant factor while pH and nitrogen source were noted as significant factors. The combined effect of the two factors on degradation of norfloxacin is shown in Fig. 6 (a-f). It was noted in the contour plot that degradation showed an upward trend with the rise in pH, carbon source, nitrogen source, and inoculum density but there will come a point when it will have reached its peak (Fig. 7a-f). At this stage (maximum), no additional increase was observed, and the degradation curve goes into the stationary phase. This can also be attributed to the provision of balance between metabolites and the parent molecule. The study by Samuel et al.51 implemented response surface methodology and concluded that Certocystis paradoxa MSR2 can be used as a decent bio-sorbent. The magnitude of its effectiveness is determined by initial concentration, pH, and temperature. Similarly, Dlamini et al.44 optimized the biodegradation of norfloxacin but instead on using microbial culture they used the laccase enzyme and immobilized it on to the zeolitic imidazolate framework. The model proved 95.1% removal efficiency in 8 h with the pH of 6.4, laccase of 10 mg and initial concentration of 10.5 mg/L.

In the same manner, Ungureanu et al.52 stated the efficiency and relevance of inoculant density in the process of biodegrading clofibric acid through the design of an experiment. Similarly, Chidambaram53 has also optimized three variables, such as contact time, dose, and original concentration of the biosorbent during chromium biosorption. In the same way, Chen et al.50 optimized the batch mode biodegradation of NOR with surface response methodology. The curve identified that the optimal parameter was used in an 85L AAO (Anaerobic-anoxic–oxic) reactor and inoculated with Aeromonas hydrophila sp. N215-1. The NOR degradation was enhanced from 56 to 81% with the optimization and bioaugmentation. Optimization of the parameters through response surface methodology has resulted in 73% of cefadroxil degradation, 77% of ciprofloxacin degradation, and 54% of amoxicillin degradation in another experiment41. Adsorptive removal of ciprofloxacin with chitosan composites was achieved with the optimization of central composite design. The pH was the most important parameter in the removal of the composite54.

Antibacterial assay of residues

Antibacterial assay is necessary to evaluate the physio-chemical and toxicity properties of the NOR metabolite. The reduction in antibacterial activity may be due to the production of less toxic metabolites. Metabolite undergo modification with altered structure and changes in the functional group also reduce the antibacterial potency of the degraded products (Fig. 8). The biodegradation of NOR was tested and the antimicrobial activity of its metabolites towards S. aureus and E. coli. The metabolites produced at the end of the experiment were less toxic compared to that of NOR. The products that were formed at the beginning of the experimentation were highly toxic and possessed extensive zones of inhibition, and later the zone of inhibition diminished greatly (p < 0.05), which denotes the minimal antibacterial properties. The zone of inhibition for E. coli before the degradation began was 32 mm. As the experiment (degradation) proceeds, the zone of inhibition begins to decline and it diminished to 28 mm on the 4th day. The decline in zone of inhibition was sharp between the 4th day and to 10th day, after which the fall was not sharp. The zone of inhibition at the end of the experiment (12th day) was 20 mm, this corresponds to a 31% reduction as compared to the control. In a similar manner, the zone of inhibition against S. aureus was 37 mm at the beginning of the experiment, where degradation had not been started yet (Fig. 8). Then the size of the inhibition zone gradually decreased, it was 34 mm on the 4th day of incubation. Later, with the start of rapid degradation the zone of inhibition reduced to 25 mm at 10th day. At the end of the experiment, the zone of inhibition was recorded as 21 mm at day 12, this is 36% lower than that of the control. Throughout this experiment, the capabilities of Alcaligenes faecalis ABR-14 for norfloxacin degradation were proved and supported. Exiguobacterium indicum and Stutzerimonas stutzeri degraded the ciprofloxacin in 10 days, and the metabolites exhibit 17% and 23% less antibacterial activities then the parent molecule26. Similarly, the decline in antibacterial potency is also reported using the fungal stain Pleurotus ostreatus. This strain degraded the ciprofloxacin in 14 days, and the metabolites showed 26% less antibacterial properties than the control55.

Conclusions

The antibiotic pollution in wastewater has increased many folds, and this can be tackled by using resistance microbes. This study provides the clear evidence that Alcaligenes faecalis ABR-14 can degrade NOR with high efficacy and do generate environmentally friendly by-products. The NOR resistance ability of Alcaligenes faecalis ABR-14 can be a potent tool for cleaning environment. The findings indicated that degradation efficacy depends on different parameters such as concentration of antibiotic, temperature, pH, source of C, source of N, as well as inoculum density. Alcaligenes faecalis ABR-14 shows the NOR degradation of 96%. Central composite design and response surface methodology were used to determine the optimum growth parameters. pH and N source were proved to be critical and significant parameters. The following were ideal conditions: NOR concentration = 100 mg/L, temperature = 35 °C, pH = 6.5, C source = sodium acetate, N source = ammonium chloride, and inoculum density of 105 CFU. The research indicates that there is a practical potential mitigation strategy in the treatment of wastewater or in an aquatic environment using strong microbes to reduce any water pollution by antibiotics. Further investigations are needed to focus on developing these findings to use in real-life applications in ecological and wastewater treatment systems. The finding layout the groundwork for future development of NOR-contaminated wastewater treatment system. The study also demonstrates the tangible and novel solution to a pressing environmental problem.
