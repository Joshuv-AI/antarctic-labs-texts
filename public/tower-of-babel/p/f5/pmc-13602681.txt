Research Ethics Committees (RECs) perspectives on large language models and AI ethics review: a South African case
Authors: Adetayo Emmanuel Obasa, Siti Kabanda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe rapid integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into health research presents a dual promise: to serve as an epistemic equalizer by lowering barriers to scientific participation, and a significant governance challenge, risking a “validity gap” where output volume outpaces integrity. Research Ethics Committees (RECs), tasked with safeguarding research, face a critical “purview weakness” in evaluating these opaque technologies. This tension is most acute in African contexts, where the need for innovation intersects with vulnerability to algorithmic colonization and where empirical data on REC preparedness is absent.MethodsA qualitative study employing in-depth, semi-structured interviews was conducted with a purposively sampled cohort of 14 REC chairs, members, and research ethics office staff from health science institutions across South Africa. Data were collected between January and July 2024, transcribed verbatim, and analysed using inductive thematic analysis.ResultsAnalysis yielded five central themes namely: (1) understanding of LLMs, (2) perceived benefits of LLMs use in health research, (3) perceived challenges and concerns in the use of LLMs in health research, (4) human oversight – augmentation, not automation, and (5) fragmented AI literacy within the research. Respondents recognized significant benefits in administrative efficiency, research lifecycle support, and democratizing writing skills. Crucially, there was unanimous consensus that LLMs must only augment, not automate, ethics review. Human oversight was deemed irreplaceable for contextual understanding, empathy, and complex moral deliberation qualities respondents implicitly aligned with relational, Ubuntu-informed ethics.ConclusionSouth African ethics gatekeepers perceive LLMs as powerful but risky tools. Their insistence on human-centric governance, rooted in contextual and communal values, provides a vital counter-narrative to purely technocratic oversight models. These findings provide an urgent empirical foundation for developing context-specific AI governance guidelines in African and other LMIC health research systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12910-026-01471-x.Keywords: Large Language Models (LLMs), Generative Articifical Intelligence (GenAI), Research Ethics Committees (RECs), Africa

Introduction

Large Language Models (LLMs) are a subset of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI) [1, 2]. LLMs refer to Transformer-based neural networks with billions of parameters, trained on vast text corpora via self‑supervised objectives (e.g., next‑token prediction) to perform diverse language tasks [3]. GenAI is the broader class of AI systems that generate novel content, text, images, audio, video, or code, by learning underlying patterns from training data [2]. The rapid integration of Generative Artificial Intelligence (GenAI), particularly Language Models (LLMs) into the health research ecosystem presents a paradigm shift with ambiguous implications for global scientific equity.

This capability has democratized scientific production by lowering barriers to content generation across disciplines [2]. However, LLMs intersect with distinct opportunities, such as amplifying research output from underrepresented institutions—and challenges related to data representativeness, infrastructure, and epistemic equity [4–7]. For researchers in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), these tools hold allure as potential “epistemic equalizers,” capable of reducing the linguistic and technical barriers that have historically stratified participation in high-impact publishing. However, this rapid technological diffusion has critically outpaced the development of ethical governance, creating a precarious “validity gap” where the ease of generating persuasive scientific text obscures significant risks to research integrity, including hallucination, bias, and the erosion of critical scholarship [8, 9].

At the centre of this tension stand Research Ethics Committees (RECs), the traditional gatekeepers of research integrity. Charged with protecting human participants, RECs now face the daunting task of auditing “black box” algorithms whose mechanisms are often opaque. This challenge exposes a structural vulnerability described as “purview weakness"a state where oversight bodies lack the specific technical competence to evaluate the novel risks of emerging technologies [10]. Recent empirical audits underscore the urgency of this gap. The fundamental tendency of LLMs to “hallucinate” or fabricate plausible-sounding references is a well-documented technical flaw that critically undermines their reliability as unsupervised research tools [11]. The psychological phenomenon of “automation bias,” where experts over-rely on algorithmic outputs, risks embedding such errors into decision-making processes, a concern validated in clinical settings beyond AI [12, 13].

This governance challenge is most acute and consequential in the African context. African health research occupies a unique ethical locus, balancing the urgent need for innovation and capacity-building against the profound risks of “algorithmic colonization”, where local data is extracted to train externally owned models without reciprocal benefit or local oversight. While global frameworks like the UNESCO Recommendation on the Ethics of AI emphasize principles of transparency and fairness, their operationalization in resource-constrained settings remains critically underexplored [14, 15]. Although the African Union’s Continental Artificial Intelligence Strategy, 2024 and nascent national policies advocate for AI governance rooted in local values, there is a stark deficiency of empirical data on how African ethics gatekeepers perceive and navigate these technologies [16].

To date, limited studies in Africa have explored RECs’ perspectives on AI in health research [17, 18]. Crucially, no empirical research has focused on the perspectives of REC members and Research Ethics Office (REO) staff in South Africa regarding the integration of LLMs. This study directly addresses this pivotal gap. By synthesizing the views of a diverse cohort ranging from early-career administrators to veteran academics, we aim to answer a pivotal question: How do the gatekeepers of African health research perceive the trade-offs between the efficiency of GenAI and the imperatives of ethical oversight? This study therefore aims to explore how South African REC members and REO staff perceive (1) the use of LLMs in health research protocols (AI as the object of ethical review) and (2) the integration of LLMs into the research ethics review workflow itself (AI as an instrument to support review).

Methods

Study design

This was a qualitative study using a thematic analysis approach. In-depth interviews were conducted with REC members and Research Ethics Office (REO) staff from institutions across South Africa.

Sampling

A purposive sampling approach was used to identify REC members and REO staff from health science institutions across South Africa, ensuring diverse representation from different institutional contexts. Additional respondents were identified through snowball sampling. Inclusion criteria required that respondents be health REC chairs, members, or REO staff, with REC members having served for a minimum of six months prior to study commencement. Respondents who had not participated in REC activities or attended REC meetings for the past two years were excluded. Respondents were recruited through professional networks and institutional websites, and approached via email for voluntary participation. A total of 17 respondents accepted the invitation and were interviewed; however, three interviews were incomplete and therefore excluded from the analysis.

Data collection

Fourteen in-depth interviews were conducted between January and July 2024. Once the respondents accepted the invitation, they were requested a convenient appointment to participate in the interview. The interview was conducted in English by EO using semi-structured interview guide that was developed based on literature on LLMs and ethics [19–21]. The interview guide (appendix-1) was piloted on three RECs members and modified for readability and clarity. The interviews were conducted online via Microsoft Teams Meeting platform within a private space. All the interviews were audio recorded with the permission of the respondents. The interviews lasted between 30 and 45 min. Data collection and analysis proceeded concurrently. Data saturation was reached with the 11th respondent from the total of the 17 that were interviewed. However, EO continued with the interview even when no new information emerged.

Data saturation was operationalized as the point at which no new codes or themes emerged from successive interviews [22, 23]. Saturation was assessed concurrently with data collection through analytic memos prepared after each interview and reviewed weekly by both authors. Formal saturation was confirmed following the 11th interview, after independent review of the cumulative codebook and memos showed no new thematic content across the preceding three interviews. Data collection continued for three additional interviews to verify thematic stability and ensure adequate representation across respondent categories; these yielded no new codes, corroborating the initial assessment. Of 17 respondents interviewed, three were excluded due to premature termination (n = 1) or audio recording failure resulting in incomplete transcripts (n = 2). These exclusions occurred prior to the saturation assessment, which was based exclusively on the 14 complete transcripts comprising the final analytic dataset.

Reflectivity statement

Given both authors’ professional roles within the South African research ethics governance system, we acknowledge that our experiences and prior engagement with the topic may have influenced participant recruitment, data interpretation, and the analytical framing of findings. We engaged in ongoing critical reflection throughout the research process to mitigate potential bias and ensure that our professional positioning informed rather than constrained the analysis.

Rigor and trustworthiness

Methodological rigor adhered to established trustworthiness criteria [24]. Credibility was strengthened through prolonged engagement over the seven-month data collection period, weekly team debriefings to interrogate emergent interpretations, and iterative interview guide refinement during piloting. Transferability was supported by thick description of the study context and respondent characteristics (Table 1). Dependability and confirmability were ensured by carefully contextualizing our work and providing clear, detailed documentation of study methods. This transparency allows others to replicate or review the study. Furthermore, we engaged in an in-depth discussion of the themes, keeping respondent perspectives’ central to the analytic narrative, with quotations selected to reflect the full range of respondent views.

Analysis

Audio recordings were transcribed verbatim by trained transcriber. The transcripts were imported into and managed using Atlas.ti software (version 25). The data were systematically coded, organized into subthemes, and subsequently developed into major themes. These themes were extensively discussed and deliberated between the two authors to ensure consensus was reached. The data were analysed thematically [25] using an inductive approach, and illustrative quotations were selected for each theme to narrate the study’s findings. Disagreements were resolved through discussion until consensus was reached. The framework was then applied to remaining transcripts, with codes iteratively refined through meetings. Themes were generated by grouping related codes and reviewed against the dataset to ensure they accurately represented the data. The final thematic structure was agreed upon by both authors. Rigor was enhanced through iterative team discussions during analysis, where emerging themes were critically interrogated and alternative interpretations considered until consensus was reached. This reflexive practice was used to strengthen the credibility of our findings. In addition, by discussing the themes in depth, we ensured that respondents’ voices remained central, which supported the confirmability of the results. Finally, by thoroughly contextualizing our work and clearly outlining our methods, we enhanced the dependability of the study.

Results

A total of 14 respondents—comprising REC members, REC chairs, and Research Ethics Office (REO) staff—were included in the final analysis. All respondents were affiliated with institutional health research ethics committees across South Africa. Table 1 presents the demographic and professional characteristics of the respondent cohort.

Thematic analysis of the semi-structured interview data yielded five overarching themes concerning respondents’ perspectives on LLMs and their integration into health research and ethics review processes: (1) understanding of LLMs; (2) perceived benefits of LLM use in health research; (3) perceived challenges and concerns regarding LLM use; (4) human oversight as augmentation rather than automation; and (5) fragmented AI literacy within the research ecosystem. Theme 1: understanding of LLMs

Most of the respondents were familiar with LLMs, with some mentioning that they got to know more about the LLMs through attending webinars and other platforms.

“…Like I have heard about it, like I have attended webinars.” (Respondent 011).

“I have been exposed to it before it kind of gains traction. I think it was just before COVID I had to prepare an abstract regarding the future of healthcare and how artificial intelligence poses a significant threat and danger to the way healthcare research, is not only access but also communication in terms of equity and fairness.” (Respondent 10).

Although respondents were familiar with LLMs, they acknowledged to having only a limited understanding of how these models function. For some, this lack of understanding contributed to their hesitation in using LLMs in their own research or daily work activities.

“I know about them, but my biggest thing is ignorance. I don’t know how they work; I don’t know the details about how generative language models…I don’t know they operate. I mean I have got the gist of it, but I don’t know the nitty gritty…I am familiar with them, but I don’t know nearly enough to really understand them…I think a lot of my hesitancy like I said multiple times because I don’t understand it.” (Respondent 001).

“Wouldn’t say like I am that confident in my knowledge about it… I think for me, the only time like I have ever used it like in my work. It’s just like I’ll have maybe ChatGPT open even though I don’t trust it that much” (Respondent 011).

However, some of the respondents recognized that there is a need for understanding LLMs.

“Yeah, I think this is where I need to learn a bit more and I think you know, it’s one of the things we kind of hear about and I’m always trying to kind of read. So, I have been reading articles as I have seen them pop up around the use of. I would say more in the news media, but the use of LLM when it comes to research ethics.” (Respondent 002).

“We have to have an understanding on how to use it and we have to have an understanding of its limitations.” (Respondent 012).

Theme 2: perceived benefits of LLMs use in health research

Most respondents expressed the numerous benefits associated with the use of LLMs, this included administrative efficiency, support across the research lifecycle, and the enhancement of capacity on how AI can impacts the research ecosystem.

Administrative efficiency

Some of the respondents perceived LLMs to be quite efficiency particularly with aspect of assisting in managing time effectively, allowing researchers and reviewers to navigate bureaucratic bottlenecks that typically slow down scientific progress.

“We all have got extremely limited time. So, I asked…. we did a study a while ago on tropidine and in patients…because we often find that people would just take the couple of days for their preparations. And I asked because I kind of know what the literature says and so on, and I asked JENNI for an outline of a literature review to discuss tropidine level in patients…and it gave me a beautiful outline. It provided a structured and logical outline…then…go and fill in the gaps. So, I think in terms of time saving, there is a fantastic benefit…” (Respondent 001).

“ I needed to work on the protocol in a quick turnaround time and was worried about not being able to produce it in time. So, took the…we sort of had like a concept note and I took that, and I took the survey, and I asked ChatGPT to generate a protocol kind of using specific headings. As a starting point. So, I found it quite beneficial because it just gave me some of the like meat that I needed, but obviously I still had to do a lot more work on it so it was more like a time saver” (Respondent 006).

Others perceived use of LLMs tends to enhance productivity and speeds up the work process.

“It’s speed up the a lot of things, there’s a lot of benefit which can come up with it” (Respondent 004).

Support across the scientific lifecycle

Respondents viewed LLMs as valuable across various stages of the research cycle, with some noting their usefulness in tasks such as conceptualizing stage of research or developing proposals, and supporting data collection

“…in the conceptualization stage, I would say that there, there is definitely benefit there. And I’ve actually started using it a little bit. So, for my own research. So as an example. I have to very quickly put together a research study protocol and that kind of have the broad topic and we had to actually already had the draft survey questions…I could have done that myself and set and like written everything out and taken from existing protocols that I have, but this just did that first step for me and then I could go back and refine it and flesh things out, and so from that perspective and in kind of the conceptualization stage, I think it’s very helpful…for the conceptualization proposal writing, I think it can be really helpful, you know.” (Respondent 006).

“I know some people have used Scopus AI and it’s assisted them in developing the proposals as well”. (Respondent 008)

Some respondents also extended this utility to the empirical phase, noting that LLMs are seen as efficient for data collection, suggesting a comprehensive role for AI as a “research assistant” throughout the protocol’s life.

“it can give you a quick way of collecting whatever you want to collect in terms of data” (Respondent 004).

Democratizing skills and knowledge

Respondents highlighted that LLMs assist in improving writing skills, a factor particularly relevant in diverse linguistic contexts where excellent science may be obscured by language barriers.

“learning writing skills, because again, the people that we are dealing with, the students, the clinicians that are writing protocols and writing papers, are not language experts. They are from Nigeria or they are from Kenya or they are from Germany or wherever that English is not the first language, so it can help. And how is Chad GPT giving me an outline and fixing my language and my grammar? How is that different from you sending me your protocol and editing it and you learn from that? So, I think you can have as much as a learning experience if that is what you are aiming to do. ” (Respondent 001).

“And I think also in terms of, for research, is just to help them learn how to write, I mean you can use chat to ChatGPT for that to upgrade your language. So, we don’t have a lot of first language English speakers, some are second, third. So, I think in writing it would help, but they’ll also need to learn how to paraphrase the stuff that they do get from the AI assistant” (Respondent 005).

One of respondents saw the tool was valued for its ability to assist in understanding other research disciplines. Especially, if it involves complex methodologies outside a reviewer’s primary expertise, LLMs were seen as tools that empower generalist committees to evaluate specialized protocols with greater confidence.

“But yeah, I think usually for that just to extract key points so that I understand the research better because obviously the research that comes to RECs is from different disciplines and I won’t always understand everything. I will just understand what I’ve been exposed to in my training and also just you know, what you know, we obviously try to read continuously and you know, know as much as possible. But it’s really difficult to know everything. So I think in that regard it does help to summarize points much, much quickly so that I can review it much quicker” (Respondent 011).

Theme 3: perceived challenges and concerns in the use of LLMs in health research

Overreliance on LLMs

The respondents highlighted how some individuals have now become overreliance on LLMs use and as result, this could impact their critical thinking.

“Exactly the problem for me is reliability. You know, we mustn’t rely on it. You know, because I mean, there will be times which will force you to think on your toes and write something…” (Respondent 007).

(Respondent 007) further adds how being over reliant on LLM use promotes laziness:

It must not promote laziness. It must not promote…even in the workplace now it’s one of the things I’ve picked up in my colleagues that when they type their emails, when they write their emails, you pick up those blank additionally in like, OK, this is AI, so this person is no longer typing their own emails. This person is just taking….and it just like it. You don’t know how to address that because I don’t know how to discourage them. They mean well, but they must not create laziness where they can’t think on their toes and be able to respond to emails. So, one of the issues that you are facing is that it might promote that and even in research.

Some of the respondents mentioned on how being over reliant on LLMs use affects students in that they miss the core learning outcomes of their degrees, such as developing a proposal or conceptualizing a research problem.

“So, AI doesn’t just do the writing for you. I think it also helps you to do the thinking…But as a student coming in using that platform? Generating whatever topic, the person was because you can just make a command. It gives you the topic and of course it gives you the different structure. So, these basically are things that are, I mean that’s that student needs to be tested on their ability to be able to conceptualize a problem and a topic and also to research on that. That is part of their academic programme. But if you depend on a machine to do that for you then you are losing that aspect as a student. So that’s why I feel it’s one of the disadvantages of the AI in terms of research because the students basically at the end of the day will not be able to have that skill of being able to conceptualize a problem and research on that problem and come up with a final output.” (Respondent 013).

Expertise dependent on LLM use

Some of the respondents highlighted the challenge that effective use of LLMs requires individuals have adequate knowledge in the relevant area of research or content, as this is essential for framing good prompts that give high‑quality outcomes.

“…if you’re not a content expert in what you are asking ChatGPT, you might get inadequate information and not know that. So, for example, I asked ChatGPT to, I think it was copilot I can’t remember, but I was looking for a cardiovascular screen for the community. So, I was looking for a cardiovascular screen that was not including any biometric measures such as blood pressure or cholesterol. And it obviously it just kept on giving me the same answer and incorrect answer. But because I’m an expert in the field, I could pick it up. So, the concern is that if you’re not an expert in the field…if you are not familiar with what you are looking for information. You’re not able to differentiate that it is not actually correct or it’s inadequate. The other thing is this very generic…if we really want to use it effectively, we have to actually really do some prompt engineering kind of training. How to write proper prompts to get useful information? But in fact, most people don’t know how to write good prompts anyway, they don’t put the context in the focus and what they’re asking, and then they get very generic information.”(Respondent 012).

“When you show prompts, you actually have to have a very good background already, and it’s to help you to make some intelligent interaction with the large language model” (Respondent 014).

Cautious engagement with LLMs

Some respondents indicated engaging with LLMs cautiously, reflecting their concerns regarding their validity. Several adopted a reserved attitude toward their use, with some displaying ambivalence.

“Personally, it’s more of a double-edged sword. If you don’t know how to use it, you can either injure someone or you can help someone. Like I said, there is no harm in engaging with these AI tools for certain answers, but it still needs to be some form of scrutinizing what gets put out because of certain things that may be overlooked in receiving an answer.” (Respondent 010).

“We still use it but with caution” (Respondent 005).

“So, I definitely am not…. I would just say I wouldd have to…… I wouldd want to be balanced and careful, and I think that the right studies need to be done to validate things before we just put systems in place and use new systems” (Respondent 009).

Theme 4: human oversight – augmentation, not automation

The respondents provided their perspectives on LLM use to support ethics review of research proposals. There was a unanimous consensus that LLM should function as a supportive tool (administrative related tasks such as initial screening of applications) rather than a decision-making agent. The idea is that this approach was perceived as a way to allow human reviewers to focus on complex ethical dilemmas.

“they can do initial screening, but I do not believe that they can do everything. Yeah, at the start. Because they are programmed the same as what ChatGPT to look at certain perspectives all right, and not the holistic. And I think where a human have that holistic picture and that’s why I would say no. They can take or play a role in the initial screening. I believe that all right, but in depth and deeper there you will need the warm bodies to do that.” (Respondent 003).

“The human side is very much needed when it comes to RECs as well.” (Respondent 010).

“Yeah, I think that human oversight is very important…You know those deliberations that we have those discussions and debates are very important and I don’t think that a model alone can do it alone and think of all these different perspectives that we come from.” (Respondent 011).

Respondents also rejected the notion of “automated review,” emphasizing that ethical deliberation requires human empathy, context sensitivity, and moral reasoning—qualities they believed AI inherently lacks.

“…they don’t have those human elements, they’re not emotional. So I think for certain questions…it might…not be that deep, but as a person you look at it and you think this might evoke some sort of emotion in someone, you know, even though it is straightforward and the study is probably just a low to medium risk, but when you think about it as a person, you realize that you know this might aggravate someone or make them feel a certain way, but AI might not pick that up, or they might not see it that way because you know they just sort of like emotionless or neutral.” (Respondent 011).

“I think that as long as humans are involved in health care setups, you need human judgment, and you need that discussion in the committee.let’s say for example, there’s a teenage mother that comes into study, and now because she is under 18, she cannot get a benefit from, you know, the investigational drug because her parents need to give consent for her to be included in a clinical trial. But they don’t know that she’s pregnant, so that is now a very complex human social situation. So, you need to wait up so many things and a lot of it is human judgment based on the contextual realities in the environment where you are that you know well.” (Respondent 001).

Nearly, all the respondents expressed that RECs cannot be replaced by AI and that emphasised that the human element is still essential in the process.

“Unfortunately, I don’t think so, because we are still very far from eliminating human interference in AI… So, you can use AI to assist, but not completely eliminating it.” (Respondent 013).

“I think it won’t…because how ethics is viewed and how the role of ethics committees is viewed. Ethics committees run the whole review process at the cornerstone of an ethics committee’s existence is discussion and getting each and everyone’s viewpoint about this application. That’s the cornerstone of an Ethics Committee existence. So, if you take that away,.The whole nuance of the discussion, deliberation and coming to a consensus is not going to be there.” (Respondent 007).

Theme 5 : fragmented AI literacy within the research

Research participants’ limited understanding of AI

Respondents emphasized the need of fostering AI literacy among research participants to enhance their understanding of AI and LLMs. Some indicated it might take time but suggested this could be achieved by simplifying the informed consent process and ensuring that researchers devote sufficient time to clearly explain and contextualizing key concepts when introducing the study.

“I think it would take a longer time, right? Like the process of informed consent…I think like you would need to take careful steps to try and break it down like very simply what’s actually happening. But at the same time, if you’re talking to someone who has, like, no, just doesn’t know about large language models, I think it’s going to be difficult for them to grasp, right? Like, but what do you mean? Like the data goes in there. What do you mean like this model learns?.In our research projects, we often spend a lot of time just explaining to people. So, I think. I think we will get there. I think we’re going to have to get there because more and more people are using it. Yeah, but it’ll take, I think, time to try and explain it properly” (Respondent 006).

Another respondent mentioned d that researchers could draw on established disciplines, such as biobanks, to communicate effectively with research participants. This approach would help participants better AI, for instance by clarifying that their data may be processed using LLMs.

“I think when it comes to really understanding and conveying, you know which what is still a quite a complex concept, I think we need to really find the right tools, language and otherwise to convey to participants what this actually means. Umm, you know, I think potentially we borrow from other disciplines. I’m thinking about like data repositories or biobanks you know where you’re talking to people about their genetic data which might be used…your data might be going into this much larger thing, your personal data, your genetic data. So potentially there’s similar types of language that we can borrow.people don’t really know. I think people are a little bit scared of AI, right?…it it’s already kind of challenging for people of different literacy levels to grasp, even quite straightforward research. And that makes sense because, you know, researchers its own thing, and it’s its own language. But when it comes to more complex research, I think we really need to think about our duty to participants to really inform them, to make sure that they understand, to allow them to ask questions and to devote enough time to that consent process.” (Respondent 002).

Need for AI training and expertise among RECs

Respondents reported that RECs require training on AI as most members lack expertise in this field. This need is underscored by the fact that some REC members have already reviewed AI-related studies. The challenges they encounter include determining whether research proposals have been generated with the assistance of LLMs, particularly when such use has not been acknowledged, as well as detecting risks (ethical issues) that may arise from integrating LLMs into research practices.

“I think we need to have some sort of AI training done with the HREC members. I mean that’s the only way so that maybe all of us are being equipped to actually review ethics applications which deal with AI…so yeah. I think training” (Respondent 005).

“we need training on how to be able to detect that kind of risk…Well, no risk if there’s no human participants, minimal, medium, minimal, low, medium and high risk. But we’re looking at a subject, we’re looking at humans when we think AI, there’s almost that distance. There’s a computer between humans and the researcher or the person who puts the input, and I think we need to see that distance move closer and to understand that it’s not a computer, it’s not just input on a large language model platform. This could have direct benefits to someone in the end, right? And I think information and data as almost a, because I’m security inclined as a risk, is something that I think needs to be incorporated in how we assess risk.” (Respondent 008).

One REC member noted that a lack of basic understanding or knowledge of AI may hinder the ability of REC members to adequately review studies involving AI.

“Yes, I will say big yes to that because I mean if you don’t know about AI, for instance, and what AI does and the limitations of AI, then you as a HREC member might not be able to adequately review a protocol that deals with AI. You need to know the cons and the pros of the use of AI to be able to do that. So, I will say yes…HREC members to be to be capacitated in terms of reviewing …AI related protocols” (Respondent 013).

This is particularly important especially the fact that the field of AI is ever changing rapidly:

“Have someone come and give like bite size information on the, you know, bite size information? Not the information to completely understand how it works, but enough. And that’s updated regularly because these things change so rapidly and that someone else needs to take responsibility to upskill the committee with what is needed” (Respondent 001).

Discussion

The first empirical study in South Africa to explore how Research Ethics Committees (RECs) and Research Ethics Office (REO) staff perceive the integration of Large Language Models (LLMs) into health research, revealing a governance model rooted in Ubuntu ethics that insists on human augmentation over automation. Considering the expanding utilization of Gen AI in health research ecosystem, these findings address a pivotal literature gap by offering empirical evidence to guide the development of context-specific and pragmatic approaches to these technologies.

Our study shows that most respondents were familiar with LLMs (e.g. ChatGPT) but admitted that they only have superficial understanding of how these model’s function. The interpretation of these findings is structured around a foundational analytical distinction. The difference between (1) RECs’ role in reviewing research that employs AI or LLM methodologies (i.e., AI as the object of review) and (2) RECs/REOs and researchers’ use of LLM tools within the research and review workflow (i.e., AI as the instrument of review). While these dimensions intersect, they raise distinct ethical considerations. The former concerns participant protection in AI-driven studies, algorithmic transparency, and informed consent for AI data practices. The latter concerns professional integrity, automation bias, and the boundaries of acceptable delegation in knowledge production. Respondents’ responses often moved between these domains without explicit demarcation; in what follows, we attend to each dimension separately while acknowledging their interdependence in practice. This finding aligns with prior studies from Uganda and Nigeria, where ethics committee members demonstrated limited understanding of AI, though our study differed by incorporating both REC members and REO staff [17, 26]. Our study extends this work by incorporating perspectives from both REC members and REO staff.

Studies have similarly reported a limited understanding of AI among groups such as faculty members, research staff, and students [27, 28]. In our study, respondents had limited technical literacy engendered hesitancy and low trust in LLM outputs. This hesitancy is not merely anecdotal; it reflects a structural as ‘purview weakness.’ where ethics committees lack the technical competence to audit algorithmic risks [29]. This study confirms that even experienced REC members and REO staff feel this knowledge gap, reinforcing calls for capacity-building in AI ethics. Prior studies have proposed various approaches to improve individuals’ familiarity, confidence, and ethical literacy in engaging with AI, through workshops and hands-on sessions [28, 30].

LLM services are typically external infrastructures which are often owned by large tech companies outside Africa. The respondents expressed wariness toward “black box” AI models that is partly rooted in this externality, they are unsure how the model works and where the data are stored. This resonates with African data sovereignty and decolonial critiques in the AI ethics discourse [31–34]. The African Union’s Continental Strategy for AI (2024) prioritizes localization and control of AI systems and data, aiming to reduce reliance on dominant global players. Studies have argued that dependence on foreign AI platforms without local oversight can amount to “algorithmic colonization”, imposing outside values and eroding local agency [35–38] The concern around black box is an ongoing issue that requires a collaborative solution from all stakeholders.

Most respondents identified LLM as a supportive tool to streamline labour-intensive tasks, with several tangible benefits of how LLM can be integrated into the research process [39]. These perceived advantages can accelerate early-stage tasks and lighten researchers’ workload across board which augment human capabilities [40, 41]. In effect, LLMs were seen as “research assistants” that can accelerate early-stage tasks and lighten researchers’ workload across design, execution, and analysis. Respondents also perceived that LLMs can help accelerate writing, and this has been observed in other studies as LLMs allowing scientists to allocate more time to brainstorm and conceptualised ideas [42, 43]. Our respondents’ view of LLMs as ‘equalizers’ is empirically supported by Dalaman et al. 2025, whose analysis demonstrated that LLM usage has led to a convergence in lexical diversity between native and non-native English speakers. This confirms that LLMs are effectively democratizing access to high-impact journals [44]. Kendro et al. 2026, similarly found that while LLM-generated texts differ markedly from human writing in lexical diversity, human writers themselves showed no significant variation across language background or education level underscoring that LLMs produce a distinct, rather than representative, form of language [45]. Our respondents also caution that this efficiency may come at the cost of ‘linguistic homogenization,’ potentially erasing unique African rhetorical styles and enforcing a ‘standardized’ Western academic voice.”

This is particularly salient for African researchers and students for whom English is not a first language, as their scientific writing can be substantively impeded by language proficiency constraints. Parallel findings have been documented in other studies, which indicate that AI-assisted writing tools not only provide effective support during the academic writing process but may also contribute to the longitudinal development of scholarly writing competencies [46, 47].

Although respondents appreciated the advantages of LLMs, their attitudes remained cautious, citing uncertainty regarding both the data fed into the models and the quality of the outputs produced. The ambivalence captured in our findings what we initially characterized as “cautious engagement” is perhaps better understood not as a discrete theme but as the underlying affective and epistemic orientation that conditions how participants approach both the benefits and risks of LLMs. Rather than reflecting indecision, this guarded posture signals a mature recognition that technological integration in ethics governance must proceed incrementally, with continuous evaluation of consequences. Similarly in a study conducted by Mulaudzi and Hamilton [48] which explored lecturers’ perspectives on the role of artificial intelligence (AI) in personalised learning within higher education, the authors recommended a balanced and cautious approach and resonates with broader calls for “human-in-the-loop” verification in AI-assisted research processes. Therefore, adopting a caution approach is important when considering the reliability and accuracy of the AI output [49].

Respondents warned that over reliance on AI might undermine critical thinking skills. The fear that researchers might uncritically accept AI outputs aligns with the psychological phenomenon of ‘automation bias. Furthermore, the concern regarding ‘hallucinations’ is well-founded; Magesh and Surani (2025) found that even specialized AI research tools hallucinate legal and medical citations between 17% and 33% of the time. This suggests that without rigorous ‘human-in-the-loop’ verification, the integration of LLMs could compromise the epistemic integrity of health research [50]. In another study, Geng et al., 2025 expressed concerns for students over dependence on AI tools, which they fear may hinder their problem-solving skills [47]. Another aspect of over-reliance is that the student misses the process of conceptualizing a problem and designing an approach, which are core learning outcomes in academic development [51, 52]. Several studies highlighted the challenges associated with over-reliance on LLMs has also been globally reported [26, 47, 53]. This emphasizes the need to ensure that there is a process that encourages transparency and prompt engineering to prevent the risk of overreliance on LLMs, while simultaneously maximizing their benefits. Such processes should emphasise that human intellectual engagement must remain central. The principle of beneficence (ensuring net benefit), the respondents implied that any efficiency gained from LLMs should not come at the cost of cultivating individual’ analytical skills. AI should augment but never replace human reasoning in making research decisions [51, 52]. Studies have shown that many users lack the prompt engineering skills or background knowledge to ensure high-quality outputs. This challenge is amplified in resource-constrained settings: fragmented AI literacy means some users may take LLM output at face value [54–56].

Respondents also highlighted that the review of ethics applications should not be conducted as a fully “AI-driven” process. LLMs should augment human ethics review, not automate or replace it. Respondents’ insistence on human irreplaceability in ethics review warrants critical examination, particularly given their self-acknowledged limited familiarity with AI capabilities. Their position may also reflect scepticism about the accuracy and reliability of AI systems, which may contribute to a lack of trust and consequently a negative perception of AI as a potential substitute for human ethics review. This raises important questions about whether such positions reflect informed assessment or institutional caution in response to technological uncertainty. Respondents themselves acknowledged only “superficial understanding” of LLM functioning [20–23]; their assertions that AI “cannot understand context” or “lacks empathy” may therefore derive less from familiarity with current AI capabilities than from normative commitments about what ethics review ought to be. Recent scholarship complicates this binary. Studies demonstrate that AI can detect patterns of bias humans miss [55] and that human reviewers exhibit measurable inconsistencies in protocol assessment [56].

This is not to argue for AI replacement, which our respondents rightly reject, but to suggest that the choice is not between “human wisdom” and “flawed machine.” Rather, the challenge is designing hybrid systems that leverage AI’s consistency and scalability while preserving human judgment for the irreducibly relational dimensions of ethics review. Ubuntu ethics, with its emphasis on communal deliberation and context-sensitive moral reasoning, provides resources for thinking about such hybridity: technology can serve the community without replacing its moral agents [50, 51]. The pertinent question is not whether humans remain central they must but under what conditions AI might enhance human consistency, flag oversights, and surface considerations reviewers might miss. Answering this requires moving beyond defensive boundary-work toward empirical investigation of where human review succeeds and fails, and where AI might genuinely augment rather than undermine ethical deliberation.

Furthermore, respondents envision LLMs as supportive tools for routine tasks, with human committee members retaining full responsibility for deliberation and final decisions in complex ethical judgments remain. An automated system cannot weigh the ‘relational’ impact of a study on a community’s social fabric a nuance our respondents correctly identified as the ‘cornerstone’ of ethical deliberation. This perspective resonates with the African philosophy of Ubuntu. Unlike Western frameworks that prioritize individual autonomy and ‘privacy,’ Ubuntu ethics emphasizes ‘Communalism’ the understanding that an individual’s data is inextricably linked to their community [37, 57, 58]. The respondents echoed this, asserting that LLMs lack the empathy, contextual awareness, and moral intuition that humans bring. An AI reviewing a protocol might miss culturally specific concerns or the emotional weight of a scenario that human members would immediately recognize. By maintaining human-led ethics deliberation, RECs are upholding these principles and the spirit of Ubuntu, ensuring technology does not erode human dignity and agency in research governance.

Respondents’ suggestion to use AI for clerical screening is consistent with emerging global proposals. Our findings highlighted that AI might be useful for handling the growing volume of research applications, acting as a triage or advisory system Porsdam Mann et al. 2025, recently proposed the development of ‘Application-Specific LLMs’ (e.g., Chat-IRB) designed explicitly to triage protocols for completeness, thereby reducing administrative burdens without supplanting ethical judgment [53]. Within an African context, this aligns with the new guidelines developed in Uganda by Nabukenya et al. [17], which explicitly framework how RECs can audit AI systems for social value and safety. Not only did respondents stress keeping “warm bodies” in the review process, but they also highlighted areas where AI simply cannot substitute for human qualities: empathy, contextual interpretation, and moral reasoning. These are precisely the attributes that frameworks like Ubuntu ethics champion in pursuit of inclusive and compassionate decision-making [59].

Finally, our study uncovered issues of fragmented AI literacy, both among research participants (the public) and within RECs themselves. Respondents were concerned that uneven understanding of AI across society could lead to inequities and ethical blind spots. They emphasized the need for community engagement strategies to bridge these gaps. In South Africa, applying Ubuntu’s relational ethos would mean treating participants as partners who deserve clear explanations and the opportunity to ask questions. Our data show RECs are aware of this duty; they feel a duty of care to insist on contextualized consent practices. This might translate into RECs asking investigators: “How will you explain the AI component to participants of varying literacy levels?” and requiring that consent forms are written in plain language. As AI literacy in the general population is currently low and uneven, such vigilant oversight by RECs is crucial to maintain trust. Notably, respondents acknowledged it “will take time” for widespread understanding to develop. As such, the adoption of community engagement with the participants approach could be ideal, as it is in line with African community engagement principles, which value dialogue and shared understanding between researchers and communities [60].

Regardless of technological sophistication, responsible deployment will require informed human oversight. Fragmented literacy can be addressed through education and international cooperation, ensuring that African ethics committees are not left behind in the AI era but instead are leading with contextually informed oversight. Responsible AI deployment in LMICs has become challenging due to scientific and regulatory constraints, as well as limited capacity to evaluate the safety and effectiveness of new technologies [18, 61, 62]. There is a pressing need for more empirical research in LMICs, particularly in Africa to explore RECs’ perspectives on AI and its use in reviewing research protocols.

Study limitations

This study has certain limitations. Since it was conducted in South Africa, some findings may not be broadly applicable in other contexts. Another limitation is that most respondents came from institutions with established RECs, which may restrict the representation of REC and REO staff from a wider range of institutions across the country. The study sample comprised REC members exclusively from academic backgrounds. Although this was not a deliberate inclusion criterion, it reflects the predominant membership profile of participating institutions’ health RECs. The absence of non-academic members such as community representatives, legal experts, or healthcare practitioners from non-academic settings means that their perspectives on LLMs are not captured, representing a limitation of this study. Future research should aim to include community representatives, legal experts, or healthcare practitioners from non-academic settings to ensure broader representation. Despite these limitations, our findings provide valuable insights into the perspectives of REC members and REO staff on the use of LLMs and the review of AI-related applications.

Conclusion

South African REC members and REO staff approach LLMs with a balanced perspective: they see clear opportunities to enhance research efficiency and equity, yet they remain vigilant about the ethical pitfalls and insist on keeping humans at the center of the decision-making process. This outlook is well-aligned with both African ethical values (notably Ubuntu’s emphasis on community, accountability, and human dignity) and global AI ethics frameworks (which stress human rights, fairness, and transparency in AI deployment [14, 15]. The study findings reinforce the importance of developing context-specific governance guidelines for AI in health research guidelines that incorporate African perspectives on relational ethics, data sovereignty, and justice. Encouraging steps are already being taken Uganda has developed a guide for AI ethics review emphasizing social value, autonomy, and data responsibility [60]. The successful and ethical integration of LLMs into African health research will depend on continuous human oversight, robust ethics training, and engagement with local values and communities. By heeding the voices of RECs and other local stakeholders, we can ensure that AI is used as a tool to advance health and social justice, rather than as a force that exacerbates existing inequalities or undermines human agency.
