Understanding Ukraine’s immunization supply chain resilience in the face of war: a mixed-method study
Authors: Stanislav Gaievskyi, Yevgenii Grechukha, Oleg Khomenko, Oleksandra Karkishechenko, Denys Deineha, Oleg Benes, Sharif Ismail, Jarno Habicht
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe full-scale invasion of Ukraine in 2022 created unprecedented pressures on the national immunization supply chain (ISC). Despite large-scale energy disruptions, population displacement, and insecurity, vaccine availability and storage conditions were largely maintained. This study aimed to examine how Ukraine’s ISC has adapted to war-related disruptions and to identify key enablers, barriers, and priorities for strengthening its resilience.MethodsA mixed-methods approach combined 15 semi-structured interviews with stakeholders from national, oblast, and facility levels, a review of literature, regulatory documents, and programme data. Data were analyzed using thematic analysis and interpreted using the Blanchet et al. health system resilience framework.ResultsAnalysis found that the digitalization of the vaccine stock management system and the wide availability of training strengthened the knowledge capacity of the ISC. Large-scale investments by international partners in WHO-prequalified cold chain equipment during the COVID-19, complemented by the deployment of backup power solutions, significantly improved the system’s ability to manage uncertainty related to electricity outages. The centralization of ISC function enhanced governance and coordination, contributing to legitimacy capacity. Strong collaboration across government institutions and with international partners also helped sustain ISC in the face of the war disruptions, demonstrating the importance of interdependence capacity. However, several barriers persist, including gaps in population data for forecasting vaccine needs and coverage monitoring, unequal distribution of new cold chain equipment, and continued reliance on donor support.ConclusionsDespite repeated attacks, Ukraine’s ISC has continued to function. The findings highlight key enablers of resilience and remaining bottlenecks and provide actionable recommendations. Ukraine’s experience offers transferable lessons for strengthening ISC resilience in other conflict settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13031-026-00806-0.

Introduction

Vaccines are among the most effective public-health interventions [1]. However, national immunization programmes (NIPs) continue to face persistent challenges in optimizing vaccination uptake, sustainably closing immunization coverage gaps, particularly in conflict settings [2]. Central to many of these challenges is the immunization supply chain (ISC), which is defined as the system of equipment, staff, and procedures used to forecast, procure, store, and distribute vaccines from the manufacturer to the point of administration [3]. Inadequate national ISC capacity or performance can disrupt vaccine distribution and result in product damage, high wastage rates, and limited vaccine availability. Supply chain incidents ultimately risk undermining public trust in NIPs [4]. Strengthening the ISC is therefore essential to expanding effective immunization coverage.

Since the start of the Russian Federation’s full-scale invasion in 2022, Ukraine’s NIP has faced unprecedented challenges. These include direct attacks on health-care infrastructure, with 2,914 attacks recorded as of March 2026; large-scale population displacement, with 3.7 million internally displaced and 5.7 million refugees; repeated strikes on energy infrastructure, resulting in the destruction or occupation of over two thirds of national energy-generation capacity with power outages lasting multiple days by late 2025; and sustained disruptions to logistics routes, including the continued closure of national airspace [5–8]. Despite these shocks, Ukraine’s NIP has demonstrated remarkable resilience, largely maintaining ISC and sustaining the provision of immunization services. This is reflected in the recovery of vaccination coverage for key antigens to pre-invasion levels (Fig. 1) [9].

The conceptualization of health system resilience proposed by Blanchet and colleagues provides a useful analytical framework for examining which system responses have contributed to the resilience observed in Ukraine’s ISC [10]. Blanchet et al. conceptualize health system resilience as comprising four interrelated capacities: (1) the ability to generate, integrate, and analyze different forms of knowledge and information; (2) the capacity to anticipate and respond to uncertainty and unexpected events; (3) the ability to manage interdependence by engaging effectively with multiple actors and navigating cross-scale dynamics and feedbacks; and (4) the capacity to establish and sustain legitimate institutions that are socially accepted and adapted to the local context. Applying this analytical lens to the Ukrainian ISC allows examination of the factors that enabled or constrained these resilience capacities during the ongoing crisis.

The full-scale invasion is not the only crisis that the country’s NIP has faced in recent years; its capacity to respond to various shocks has been built gradually over the years. Ukraine had to deal with the consequences of the conflict, starting from the initial Russian aggression in 2014, with parts of the territory being occupied and the population displaced [11]. In parallel, Ukraine was affected by multiple measles (2017–2019) and polio outbreaks (2015 and 2021), as well as COVID-19 [12–14]. Despite being constantly tasked with emergency response, the Government of Ukraine continued to implement system reforms, demonstrating strong transformative capacity, to further expand and strengthen its NIP.

Over the years, the Government’s efforts have been supported by international partners that provided extensive assistance to the NIP, addressing both immediate emergency response needs and sustaining implementation of system reforms. Substantial investments in cold-chain equipment (CCE), initially driven by the COVID-19 vaccine response and largely financed through Gavi, the Vaccine Alliance, World Bank, and European Commission, enabled the replacement of the majority of legacy, non-specialized equipment with WHO-prequalified CCE across all levels of the system [15–17]. In parallel, large-scale training initiatives implemented by the WHO and United Nations Children’s Fund (UNICEF) strengthened workforce capacity to operate the new CCE [18, 19]. Furthermore, in 2025, Ukraine received additional support from Gavi under its Fragility policy, securing uninterrupted annual supplies of routine vaccines for the first time since the beginning of the full-scale war, thus reducing fiscal pressure on the national budget during the ongoing war [20]. System responses seen in Ukraine broadly align with evidence on strengthening ISC generated in other conflict settings, from which it is known that investments in WHO-prequalified CCE and context-specific backup power arrangements, strengthening workforce capacity, as well as improving governance and information systems to enable timely decision-making is critical to build system capacity to respond to conflict-related shocks [21].

Further development of the programme remains a clear priority of the Government, with the human papillomavirus (HPV) vaccine introduced to the NIP in 2026 [22]. However, there is a notable lack of up-to-date evidence on the ISC performance to guide further system-strengthening and resilience efforts to underpin a strong NIP. The last comprehensive national assessment of the ISC, using the Effective Vaccine Management (EVM) Assessment methodology, was conducted in 2017. That assessment led to the approval of a national effective vaccine management action plan; however, despite numerous subsequent system reforms, implementation of this plan has not yet been formally assessed [23].

This study aims to generate evidence to inform the future strengthening of Ukraine’s ISC by assessing how previous system reforms and responses have shaped resilience in the context of war-related shocks. Drawing on key informant interviews (KIIs), document review, and secondary data analysis, the study maps the current structure and functions of the ISC and identifies key enablers that have supported system resilience, examines persistent performance barriers, and highlights priority areas for future improvement, using Blanchet’s theoretical framework of health system resilience. Documenting Ukraine’s experience in adapting and developing its ISC amid unprecedented threats provides essential evidence to support not only national decision-making but also contributes to a limited body of evidence, particularly in the European context, on ISC resilience in the face of war-related shocks.

Methods

Study design

This study used a mixed-methods approach with two data sources: (1) KIIs conducted with stakeholders from different levels of the ISC, and (2) a literature and document review, along with secondary data obtained from informants. This approach enabled mapping of the current ISC structure, functions, and implemented responses, primarily informed by the document review, while the interviews provided a more granular understanding of system characteristics underpinning resilience, remaining barriers, and potential areas for improvement.

Data collection

National key informants were purposively selected from the main institutions and international partners involved in ISC management, with the most senior representative responsible for immunization-related functions invited for an interview. At the oblast level, heads of immunization departments at oblast Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (oCDCs) were interviewed, with oblasts purposively selected to capture variation in conflict exposure and geography. Key informants at the service delivery level were identified through snowball sampling during interviews with oCDC representatives, who were asked to nominate several health care facilities (HCFs) within their oblast. From these, we aimed to include HCFs representing diverse sizes and forms of ownership (public and private). This approach aimed to recruit informants to capture diverse perspectives on the ISC performance, recent reforms, challenges, and opportunities for improvement. To reduce the risk of recruiting informants with limited system knowledge due to high staff turnover, an inclusion criterion of at least one year in the current position was applied. Interviews were conducted during July-August 2025, online via MS Teams, each lasting 45–60 min.

In parallel, a review of peer-reviewed and gray literature was undertaken in both Ukrainian and English. Peer-reviewed literature searches were conducted in Google Scholar using the terms Ukraine AND (immunization OR vaccination) AND cold chain. Grey literature was identified through general Google searches using the same terms and through targeted searches using the operator site: to identify relevant documents from domains of key organizations involved in ISC support (WHO, UNICEF, Gavi, European Union, and other partners). Additional policy documents, guidelines, and operational materials referenced during KIIs were also included. Secondary data shared by KIIs, such as inventories, distribution reports, regulatory documents, and standard operating procedures (SOPs), were reviewed and used to contextualize findings from interviews and documents.

Data analysis

Data from KIIs and literature were analyzed in parallel and integrated at the interpretation stage [24]. All interview recordings were machine-transcribed verbatim and subsequently verified prior to coding. Initial coding of transcripts and literature was inductive: the research team iteratively read the data, identified relevant text segments, and transferred them to Excel, assigning open codes. Codes were continuously revised and refined, grouping those with similar meaning [25]. The KIIs transcripts underwent subsequent deductive coding, when inductive codes were further categorized by the predefined codes guided by the resilience capacities categories suggested by Blanchet et al., namely knowledge, uncertainty, legitimacy, and interdependence [10]. The codes were further organized into types of findings (enabler, barrier, suggested solution). Integrating data from literature and KIIs at the interpretation stage enabled a more comprehensive contextual understanding of the system and helped identify differences between how processes were intended to function according to legislation and project documents and how they were implemented in practice according to informant’s perspectives.

Results

To achieve the study aim, we combined a review of national legislation, technical guidelines, published and grey literature, and available secondary data with 15 KIIs conducted across national, oblast, and service-delivery levels. We first present a description of the ISC structure and functions to provide better context, and then the identified ISC resilience enablers, barriers, and improvement solutions. Current immunization supply chain system description

Ukraine’s ISC is structured across four levels: (1) the national level, comprising two publicly owned central stores and a number of subcontracted private storage and distribution providers; (2) 24 subnational (oblast) stores managed by oCDCs; (3) 128 sub-oblast hubs; and (4) 2,597 service-delivery providers delivering vaccination services across 7,452 locations nationwide (Fig. 2) [36].

In 2018, Ukraine introduced a three-year vaccine forecasting cycle based on the WHO–UNICEF methodology, which estimates vaccine needs according to defined target population groups [49]. However, following the onset of the full-scale invasion and the resulting large-scale population movements, the Government suspended the official publication of population estimates. As a consequence, vaccine demand forecasting has increasingly relied on historic consumption data rather than updated demographic projections (national, #4).

National

Ukraine supplies vaccines for the NIP through two main routes: (1) procurement via state funds and (2) donations. In 2025–2026, Gavi, as part of its Fragility policy, has supported Ukraine by supplying a substantial share of its annual vaccine stock requirements [20]. In the past year, deliveries through both routes were split into 1 to 3 shipments per year per antigen (national, #4). The Medical Procurement of Ukraine (MPU) is a central procurement agency established in 2018 as part of the health reform, responsible for the procurement, storage, and distribution of medicines and other health commodities, including vaccine [34]. Recently, the MPU merged with Ukrmedpostach and acquired its own storage and distribution capacity. However, MPU outsources most vaccine handling to other for-profit Good Distribution Practice (GDP)-certified stores due to a lack of storage space (national #2).

Donated vaccines are usually stored and distributed by one of the for-profit GDP-certified distributors, which include privately owned companies and state-owned enterprises such as Ukrvaktsina. Ukrvaktsina has substantial storage and distribution capacity; however, although it is government-owned, in the current system setup, it is mostly not engaged in the distribution of vaccines procured by state funds.

Oblast and sub-oblast stores

National stores deliver vaccines to 24 oCDCs via a push approach. The frequency of deliveries from national to oblast stores usually depends on whether the vaccine was procured via state funds or donated. With vaccines procured with state funds, the entire shipment received by the national store is usually immediately distributed to oblast stores, primarily due to a lack of funds for national-level buffer storage and multiple rounds of distribution (national#4). For donated vaccines, when partners cover logistics costs, deliveries from the national to oblast stores are split across multiple shipments, based on quantities requested by oblasts, with buffer stock held at the national level.

Oblast CDCs, which were tasked with NIP management at the oblast level in 2023, reported directly to the MoH; they replaced the previous fragmented system managed through oblast governments [50]. With support from international partners, such as Gavi and UNICEF, as well as the World Bank loan to Ukraine, most of the oblasts have been equipped with WHO-prequalified WICRs and ice-lined refrigerators (ILRs), and specialized refrigerated trucks for vaccine logistics [15]. Distribution from oblast stores to the lower levels is organized differently across different oblasts (national, #3). Some oblasts deliver vaccines directly to each HCF, while others deliver vaccines to sub-oblast hubs, where HCFs pick them up using their own non-specialized transport and passive cold chain equipment. Usually, the sub-oblast hubs are used only for short-term storage, where vaccines are picked up by HCFs within 1 to 2 days of arrival.

Local level

Deliveries from oblast/sub-oblast stores are mostly based on facility requests and are done every few months using a mix of pull and push approaches. Need-based resupply and redistribution are reported to occur often at this level to prevent stockouts (national, #4; oblast, #3; HCF, #2). The majority of vaccination services are delivered at the primary health care (PHC) level. As of December 2025, a total of 2,597 PHC providers, which can be public or privately owned legal entities, were contracted by the National Health Service of Ukraine (NHSU) to provide PHC services [51]. It is typical for PHC providers to deliver services across multiple service delivery points, which range from small single-physician PHC practices to large PHC centers with up to 60 PHC physicians. In 2025, PHC services were provided across 7,452 such points across Ukraine. Public providers operated a substantially larger number of service delivery points (5,700 vs. 1,749), and employed significantly more PHC physicians (19,077 vs. 5,203). Correspondingly, public PHC providers delivered 84.2% (n = 7,295,297) of all vaccine doses administered in 2025. Public PHC providers have benefited from the support of international partners and largely replaced legacy domestic CCE with WHO-prequalified items. However, it was difficult to determine the precise share of service delivery points equipped with prequalified CCE and those still using legacy equipment due to a lack of data suitable for such analysis.

Financing

National storage and distribution of donated vaccines, such as those supported by Gavi, are typically financed separately through contracts with for-profit logistics providers (national, #4). National-level logistics costs for government-procured vaccines are typically covered as a share of the MPU-managed procurement budget (national, #2). At the oblast level, storage and distribution of vaccines are financed through the budgets of oCDCs, which are allocated annually by the MoH (national, #3). Both national- and oblast-level logistics are further supported by international partners through equipment donations and by covering selected operational costs, such as fuel. The costs of pull-based logistics, conducted by PHC providers from the oblast/sub-oblast hub and then distributed to service delivery locations, are typically covered by providers themselves. PHC providers, both public and private, are financed by the NHSU based on the number of registered patients.

Information management

Ukraine relies on a legacy NIP information management system developed in 1999, Ukrvaс, for vaccine stock and coverage tracking, in which each facility reports estimated target population groups for each antigen together with vaccine utilization data [29]. A more robust and up-to-date system for vaccine stock management, MedData, operated by the MPU, was developed with support from partners, initially to manage COVID-19 vaccines and related commodities and later expanded to include routine vaccines [31]. This system provides more detailed and actionable data on vaccine stocks across different levels of the supply chain. However, MedData was designed primarily for stock management and does not include data on target populations; therefore, Ukrvac continues to be used in parallel for coverage monitoring. At the same time, the eHealth system established as part of the country’s health financing reform to enable performance-based payments by NHSU, has gradually expanded to include a growing number of patient records, including vaccination data [30]. This development made it possible to calculate vaccination coverage using patient-level data (number of doses administered and the size of the target population registered in eHealth), as opposed to facility-level summaries available in Ukrvac, mitigating the lack of official population estimates since the onset of the war. Despite the potential advantages of this approach for coverage estimation, the Government has not yet approved its official use due to concerns regarding data completeness and potential differences in coverage estimates compared with those previously reported through the Ukrvaс system.

Knowledge

Two key themes emerged from the KIIs that appeared to enhance the knowledge capacity of the ISC. First, the use of the MedData electronic stock management system for routine vaccines was highlighted by multiple stakeholders across different system levels as instrumental to improving visibility of vaccine stocks and facilitating its management. Respondents at the service delivery level emphasized that the system is significantly easier to use than the previous paper-based reporting, allowing facilities to compensate for the loss of nursing staff displaced by the war, as fewer personnel are required to maintain stock records. Second, stakeholders reported numerous training opportunities for immunization and logistics staff, often supported by international partners, which helped strengthen health workers’ knowledge of effective vaccine management in the context of war-related disruptions.

Despite these improvements, important knowledge gaps persisted, hindering ISC resilience. Respondents reported a lack of reliable population figures due to large-scale displacement and the suspension of official demographic statistics, which complicated forecasting vaccine needs and monitoring immunization coverage. Lack of usable CCE mapping has hindered the effective distribution of newly supplied CCE. Leading to some facilities receiving more equipment than they could utilize, leaving units unused, while others received too few and continued relying on domestic refrigerators. Oblast-level respondents reported critical challenges with remote temperature monitoring systems (RTMS) installed together with WICRs. The most common issue was the software configuration for sending alarm notifications, which in some cases arrived only after four hours of continuous temperature excursion. Furthermore, despite an abundance of training opportunities, because these opportunities are supported by partners on a one-off project basis and due to high staff turnover, some staff remain untrained on ISC management functions.

To strengthen knowledge capacity, respondents proposed exploring the use of eHealth data for vaccine needs forecasting and monitoring immunization coverage. Expanding the functionality of the MedData system to include CCE inventory and potentially temperature monitoring records was also suggested, which could improve visibility of equipment availability, functionality, and utilization across different levels of the system. Respondents further recommended engaging with manufacturers of the RTMS to address issues related to temperature-excursion notifications. Finally, the development of standardized, low-cost online training modules on ISC management was proposed as a way to make training opportunities more sustainable and accessible for healthcare workers.

Uncertainty

The large-scale deployment of WHO-prequalified CCE, initiated during the COVID-19 pandemic, was identified by respondents as a key enabler of the ISC’s capacity to manage uncertainty, particularly that arising from frequent and prolonged power outages. WHO-prequalified CCE has substantially longer holdover times than non-specialized or domestic equipment, allowing vaccines to remain within recommended temperature ranges during electricity disruptions. Respondents also highlighted the rapid expansion of backup power solutions across the system since the start of the invasion as another critical factor enabling the system to cope with unstable power supply. In addition, the flexibility of vaccine suppliers to adjust delivery schedules in response to power outages at the national store helped reduce the risk of disruptions to vaccine receipt and storage at the national level. Finally, the availability of buffer stock at the national level and the resources required for multiple distribution rounds of donated vaccines helped prevent stockouts at the oblast level. The ability of oCDCs to rapidly resupply and redistribute vaccines within oblasts further contributed to maintaining an uninterrupted vaccine supply to the service delivery level in the face of war-related shocks.

Several barriers were also identified that hinder the system’s ability to manage uncertainty. Respondents at the oblast level noted that while buffer stocks existed at the national level for partner-donated vaccines, there was no national-level buffer stock for vaccines procured through the government budget due to limited storage space and funding, sometimes leading to stockouts and overstocking at lower levels. In addition, respondents at the service delivery level highlighted that the large ILRs (242 L), the most frequently supplied type, may not be the most appropriate for small clinics, many of which handle relatively low vaccine volumes. As a result, large ILRs are often underutilized, while smaller WHO-prequalified refrigerators could be more efficient for such facilities. Respondents also reported reliability issues with one type of newly supplied ILR, describing a high number of malfunctions shortly after deployment. Shortages of passive containers for vaccine transport further limited the ability to safely redistribute vaccines between facilities.

Proposed solutions to strengthen the ISC’s capacity to manage uncertainty included redistribution of already supplied CCE based on updated equipment mapping, as well as additional procurement of smaller ILRs and passive containers. Strengthening the national store’s own storage capacity, along with establishing the necessary financial mechanisms to enable buffer stock for government-procured vaccines and multiple rounds of redistribution, was also identified as a critical action.

Legitimacy

Centralization of ISC management functions at the oblast level under oCDCs, supported by technical assistance, equipment, and coverage of some logistics costs by partners, such as fuel, was perceived by stakeholders as an important enabler of ISC legitimacy. This arrangement helped clarify responsibilities for vaccine management and coordination at the oblast level.

At the same time, significant variability in the organization of ISC systems across oblasts was identified as a barrier to legitimacy capacity. Respondents noted that this variability was largely due to the lack of clear guidance from the national level on how oblast-level systems should be structured. Furthermore, confusion regarding regulatory requirements for the use of CTMDs led to delays in their introduction and, in some cases, non-compliance with national legislation. High reliance on individual staff knowledge and the lack of actionable SOPs for various ISC functions, particularly for responding to cold chain emergencies, were also identified as important barriers. Respondents noted that some existing SOPs were developed primarily to comply with formal requirements rather than to provide practical operational guidance for ISC procedures. In addition, stakeholders reported that some ISC training delivered by partners, while based on the best global practices, sometimes does not sufficiently reflect national legislation, which may also lead to compliance issues.

Several respondents at the national level highlighted the lack of clear regulations on how to assess vaccine viability following minor temperature excursions as another barrier. In the context of the war, minor temperature deviations were reported as largely unavoidable. In the absence of regulatory guidance aligned with international standards for assessing whether vaccines remain usable after minor temperature excursions, such incidents lead to distribution delays and, ultimately, unnecessary vaccine wastage.

Among the proposed solutions to strengthen the legitimacy capacity of the ISC was the development of tailored national guidance for establishing and organizing ISC systems at the oblast level. Respondents also suggested making greater use of the WHO-prequalification mechanism to streamline regulatory approvals for CTMDs. The development of a national competency framework for ISC staff was proposed to help align partner-supported training activities and ensure consistency with national regulations. Finally, the development of national guidance on how to assess the viability of vaccines following temperature excursions, aligned with international practices, was also suggested.

Interdependence

Interdependence across institutions played an important role in sustaining the ISC during the war. Respondents highlighted strong intersectoral coordination through the Vaccine-Preventable Diseases Task Force of the MoH (VPD TF), which facilitated information sharing and joint problem-solving among government institutions and international partners. Another important element of interdependence was the role of international partners, such as Gavi, which supported the government by financing the supply of routine vaccines in light of the extreme fiscal pressure on the national budget caused by the war.

However, several barriers associated with interdependence also emerged. First, respondents noted the limited availability of service companies capable of maintaining newly supplied CCE, which slowed repair and maintenance processes. Furthermore, budget expenditure restrictions introduced under martial law affected the ability of ISC actors to procure necessary equipment and reduced operational flexibility. Finally, stakeholders highlighted that the ISC remained highly dependent on donor support for vaccines, CCE, CTMDs, and some operational costs, leaving the system particularly vulnerable in the context of shrinking development budgets.

To address these challenges, respondents and the analysis highlighted the need for a sustainable long-term strategy for the procurement of vaccines, CCE, and CTMDs that would gradually reduce reliance on external partners. In addition, engaging with manufacturers and maintenance service providers for CCE was suggested as an important step to ensure the availability of repair and servicing capacity.

Discussion

The results of this study, combining a review of existing literature with insights from key informants across system levels, provide a comprehensive overview of Ukraine’s ISC structure and functions and the factors shaping its resilience during the war. Interpreting these findings through Blanchet’s health system resilience framework highlights how different system responses influenced four key resilience capacities: knowledge, uncertainty management, legitimacy, and interdependence.

Digitalization and training of ISC staff emerged as key contributors to the knowledge capacity of the ISC. The introduction of the MedData electronic stock-management system improved visibility of vaccine stocks and simplified reporting processes, which became particularly important in the context of workforce shortages caused by war-related displacement. Training opportunities for immunization and logistics staff, often supported by international partners, were also described as an important contributor to system resilience. Insufficient cold chain management skills are widely documented as a major contributor to vaccine wastage, particularly in conflict settings [52]. However, several knowledge gaps remain. The absence of reliable population denominators following large-scale displacement complicates vaccine needs forecasting and coverage monitoring, a common challenge across conflict settings [53]. Informants also highlighted gaps in CCE mapping and technical issues with new RTMS. Expanding the functionality of MedData to include CCE inventories and potentially temperature-monitoring records could be leveraged to target corrective measures. Furthermore, informants also suggested exploring the use of patient registries within the national eHealth system as a proxy for population estimates for forecasting and coverage monitoring. Investment in the digitalization of ISC’s information management systems has been identified as an effective intervention in several literature reviews investigating how to strengthen ISCs in humanitarian settings [54–56]. At the same time, when developing such systems with support from partners, decision-makers should also consider the long-term maintenance and operational costs, which can be substantial, and identify mechanisms through which the government can cover them on its own to ensure sustainability.

Infrastructure improvements appear to have been central to the ISC’s capacity to manage uncertainty during the war. Informants consistently highlighted the large-scale deployment of WHO-prequalified CCE and the rapid expansion of backup power, supported by partners such as Gavi, the World Bank, UNICEF, and WHO, as one of the key factors contributing to the demonstrated resilience of the ISC. This represents a major improvement compared with the findings of the EVM assessment, which identified reliance on non-specialized equipment as a key weakness of the system. As such equipment typically lacks adequate temperature control, freeze protection, and holdover capacity, increasing the risk of vaccine damage [4]. Temperature-related vaccine damage is widely documented globally and is often associated with equipment-related failures [57]. Nevertheless, several infrastructure challenges remain. Unequal distribution of newly supplied equipment limits its system-wide benefits, while shortages of passive transport containers and CTMDs persist. It was suggested that conducting targeted redistribution of available CCE based on updated CCE mapping could further increase the effectiveness of recent investments.

Governance arrangements also appear to have contributed to the legitimacy capacity of the ISC resilience. The centralization of ISC functions under oCDCs reportedly strengthened coordination, and along with comprehensive support by partners with equipment and coverage of some logistics costs, has enabled the system to better respond to war-related shocks. Strong governance and coordination mechanisms have been repeatedly identified as key determinants of ISC resilience in conflict [54]. Likely, Ukraine’s past experiences in responding to health crises may have also contributed to improved coordination during the current war [12]. At the same time, several governance challenges persist. Informants reported variability in the organization of oblast-level ISC and the absence of actionable SOPs for ISC procedures, hindering a consistent response to war-related disruptions. The lack of standardized guidance on assessing vaccine viability following temperature excursions may also contribute to unnecessary vaccine wastage. Similar procedural gaps have been reported in other settings [4]. Developing clear national guidance on vaccine distribution practices and ISC incident management could therefore strengthen operational consistency across the system.

Ukraine’s ISC interdependence capacity between national institutions and international partners played a critical role in sustaining the ISC during the war. External partners supported vaccine procurement, CCE, training activities, and operational costs at a time when the national budget faced severe fiscal pressure [58]. Such partnerships are common in conflict settings and can help maintain immunization services during crises [59]. However, reliance on partner support also represents a critical vulnerability, a common problem across conflict settings [60]. In the context of shrinking development budgets, strengthening domestic financing mechanisms and developing a long-term strategy for procurement and maintenance of supply chain infrastructure will be important for ensuring sustainability. Furthermore, engaging with equipment manufacturers and service providers could also help address the limited availability of maintenance services for newly supplied cold chain equipment.

More broadly, the findings indicate that the enablers of the demonstrated resilience capacities of the ISC were underpinned by a combination of extrinsic and intrinsic factors. Extrinsically, international partners supported these capacities through substantial investments in donations of ISC equipment and vaccines, coverage of selected logistics costs, and staff training. Intrinsically, national reforms aimed at centralizing ISC functions, strong coordination mechanisms, and pre-war digitalization were key factors supporting the observed capacities. These findings provide transferable lessons for international partners and affected governments on which capacities can be strengthened, and through what mechanisms, to sustain ISC functions and, in turn, immunization coverage in countries affected by war.

Limitations

This study has several strengths and limitations. First, as with any qualitative study, the findings do not aim to quantify the prevalence, magnitude, or severity of the identified challenges or practices; rather, they reflect the range of experiences and issues perceived across the system. However, given the high degree of regional heterogeneity, some experiences and issues specific to regions not included in the sample may have been omitted. Second, the WHO affiliation of the interviewers may have influenced responses, either by encouraging interviewees to portray the situation more positively than it actually was in order to receive acknowledgment, or conversely by emphasizing challenges to justify the need for additional support. Lastly, the official coverage estimates presented in the introduction should be interpreted with caution given the lack of updated population estimates. To mitigate potential bias, the study employed triangulation of data obtained from a diverse group of stakeholders representing national, oblast, and facility levels, with findings cross-validated across stakeholders and against the existing literature. This approach increased confidence in the credibility of the results and reduced the influence of individual viewpoints. Despite these limitations, the study provides valuable insights into the key factors that contributed to system resilience and identifies priority challenges warranting the attention of decision-makers.

Conclusions

Ukraine’s ISC has demonstrated substantial resilience during the ongoing war, supported by investments in WHO-prequalified CCE and backup power, digitalization of stock management, strengthened coordination, and extensive partner support. At the same time, persistent gaps in information systems, equipment distribution, regulatory guidance, and financial sustainability continue to constrain system performance. Addressing these bottlenecks will be critical not only for sustaining routine immunization services in Ukraine but also for strengthening preparedness for future shocks. Ukraine’s experience offers important lessons for other conflict-affected settings seeking to build more resilient ISC.
