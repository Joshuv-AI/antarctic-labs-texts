Effects of different orthodontic retainers on dental arch stability and periodontal health: a systematic review with pairwise meta-analysis and network meta-analysis
Authors: Xiaochen Zhang, Ying Li, Zhen Zhang, Jianwei Liu, Qinfeng Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveOrthodontic retainers play a crucial role in maintaining dental arch stability and periodontal health; however, the relative advantages of different retainer types remain inconsistent. This study aimed to systematically evaluate the comparative effectiveness of different retainers by integrating both direct and indirect evidence.MethodsA systematic search of PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library was conducted up to January 2026. Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing different types of retainers were included. Pairwise meta-analysis was performed to compare fixed retainers and removable retainers. A Bayesian framework–based network meta-analysis (NMA) was further conducted to explore comparative patterns among conventional fixed retainers (FRs), computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing fixed retainers (CAD/CAM FRs), vacuum-formed retainers (VFRs), and Hawley retainers (HRs).ResultsA total of 18 studies (n = 1,471) were included. Pairwise meta-analysis showed no statistically significant differences between the two types of retainers in periodontal indices, including gingival index (GI) and plaque index (PI), as well as in most stability outcomes. However, removable retainers were associated with greater increases in arch length (AL), while the observed reduction in intercanine distance (ICD) was statistically borderline and clinically small. The NMA similarly revealed no statistically significant differences. Ranking probabilities suggested exploratory, outcome-specific patterns: FRs ranked highest for AL, CAD/CAM FRs for Little’s irregularity index (LII), HRs for ICD, and VFRs for interpremolar distance (IPD); however, these rankings should be interpreted cautiously because most network estimates were imprecise and statistically non-significant.ConclusionOverall, the available evidence did not demonstrate definitive superiority of any retainer type. Ranking results suggested possible outcome-specific patterns, but these findings remain exploratory because of sparse networks, imprecise estimates, and limited certainty of evidence. Further well-designed randomized trials are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12903-026-09123-7.

Introduction

Orthodontic treatment is widely used to improve dental alignment, esthetics, and occlusal function, but post-treatment relapse remains a common clinical concern. Relapse is associated with periodontal tissue remodeling, occlusal forces, and interindividual anatomical differences [1]. Therefore, the retention phase is essential for maintaining dental arch stability and preserving long-term treatment outcomes [2]. Common retention options include fixed and removable retainers [3, 4]. Fixed retainers, including conventional fixed retainers (FRs) and computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing fixed retainers (CAD/CAM FRs), provide continuous retention without relying on patient compliance [5]. Removable retainers, including Hawley retainers (HRs) and vacuum-formed retainers (VFRs), are easier to clean and may be more favorable for periodontal maintenance, but their effectiveness depends on patient adherence [6].

Although previous reviews have compared fixed and removable retention protocols, several evidence gaps remain [7]. First, comparative evidence for specific retainer categories, particularly CAD/CAM FRs, is still limited. Second, studies vary substantially in follow-up duration, retention protocols, patient characteristics, and outcome definitions, making direct comparison difficult [8–12]. Third, many outcomes are supported by a small number of randomized controlled trials (RCTs), limiting the strength of quantitative synthesis and the reliability of treatment rankings [13, 14]. As a result, the relative effects of different retainers on both dental arch stability and periodontal health remain uncertain.

This systematic review aimed to update and organize the available randomized evidence on orthodontic retainers using a hierarchical analytical approach. Pairwise meta-analysis was used to summarize direct evidence comparing fixed and removable retainers, whereas Bayesian network meta-analysis (NMA) was used as an exploratory framework to compare FRs, CAD/CAM FRs, VFRs, and HRs when the evidence network allowed. By evaluating dental arch stability, periodontal outcomes, and complications, this review aimed to provide a cautious evidence summary to inform clinical retainer selection and identify priorities for future comparative trials.

Methods

This study strictly follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15] and is registered with the PROSPERO international prospective systematic review database Registration number: CRD420261369586.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library. Boolean operators were used to construct the search strategy to comprehensively identify relevant studies published up to January 17, 2026. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms, primarily focusing on keywords such as “orthodontics” and “orthodontic retainers.” The complete search strategy is provided in Supplementary Material 1.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The review question was structured according to the PICO framework. The population (P) was patients who had completed active orthodontic treatment and required orthodontic retention. The interventions (I) were different types of orthodontic retainers, including conventional fixed retainers, CAD/CAM fixed retainers, vacuum-formed retainers, and Hawley retainers. The comparators (C) were other eligible retainer types within the same evidence network or fixed versus removable retainers in pairwise comparisons. The outcomes (O) included dental arch stability, periodontal health indicators, and retainer-related complications.

The inclusion criteria for this systematic review and NMA were as follows: (1) participants were orthodontic patients who had completed fixed orthodontic treatment and entered the retention phase, with no restriction on sex, and including adolescents or adults; (2) interventions involved orthodontic retainers, with comparisons between at least two different types of retainers, such as FRs (conventional fixed retainers), CAD/CAM FRs, VFRs, and HRs; (3) studies reported at least one predefined outcome of interest, including periodontal health indicators—gingival index (GI) and plaque index (PI)—or dental arch stability indicators, including arch length (AL), Little’s irregularity index (LII), intercanine distance (ICD), interpremolar distance (IPD), and related complications; (4) study design was randomized controlled trials (RCTs).

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) case reports, case series, reviews, systematic reviews, meta-analyses, conference abstracts, theses, trial registry records without available outcome data, unpublished studies, and studies not published in English; (2) studies involving only the active orthodontic treatment phase without entering the retention phase; (3) studies that did not clearly specify the type of retainer, or those in which multiple retainers were used without separable data; (4) studies that did not report any of the predefined outcomes (GI, PI, AL, LII, ICD, IPD, or complications); (5) studies with missing key data that prevented extraction or calculation of effect sizes; (6) duplicate publications from the same study population, in which case only the study with the largest sample size or longest follow-up duration was included.

Literature screening

All retrieved records were imported into EndNote X9.3.3 (Clarivate Analytics, Philadelphia, PA, USA) for management. Duplicate records were removed through a combination of automatic detection and manual verification. Two reviewers independently conducted a two-stage screening process. In the first stage, studies were screened based on titles and abstracts. In the second stage, full texts of potentially eligible studies were assessed for final inclusion. Disagreements between the two reviewers were resolved through discussion; if consensus could not be reached, a third reviewer made the final decision. The study selection process was illustrated using a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Data extraction

Data extraction was independently performed by two reviewers using a predesigned and pilot-tested data extraction form. The extracted information included first author, publication year, country or region, sample size, participant age and sex distribution, type and material of retainers, and follow-up duration. Regarding outcomes, predefined indicators related to oral and periodontal health, as well as dental alignment and occlusion, were extracted. For continuous outcomes, means and standard deviations were preferentially extracted; for dichotomous outcomes, the number of events and total sample size were extracted. Discrepancies during data extraction were resolved through discussion, with arbitration by a third reviewer if necessary. For studies with incomplete or unclear data, attempts were made to contact the original authors for additional information. When multiple publications reported the same study population at different follow-up durations, the longest follow-up report was retained for quantitative synthesis to avoid double counting.

Risk of bias and certainty of evidence assessment

The risk of bias of included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Cochrane Collaboration–recommended Risk of Bias 2 tool (RoB 2) for randomized controlled trials [16]. The assessment covered domains including the randomization process, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, measurement of outcomes, and selective reporting of results. Each study was rated as “low risk,” “some concerns,” or “high risk” for each domain, leading to an overall risk of bias judgment. Disagreements were resolved through discussion, or by consultation with a third reviewer if necessary. The results of the risk of bias assessment were presented graphically. The certainty of evidence was assessed separately for pairwise and network evidence. For pairwise meta-analysis, the GRADE [17] framework was used to evaluate risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias. For network meta-analysis, confidence in network estimates was assessed using the CINeMA [18] framework, considering within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity, and incoherence.

Statistical analysis

A hierarchical analytical strategy combining pairwise meta-analysis and network meta-analysis (NMA) was adopted. Pairwise meta-analysis was first performed to summarize direct evidence comparing fixed and removable retainers and to provide a direct-evidence baseline for the broader NMA. For continuous outcomes, mean difference (MD) or standardized mean difference (SMD) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs) was calculated according to the consistency of measurement scales across studies. For dichotomous outcomes, risk ratios (RRs) with 95% CIs were calculated. Statistical heterogeneity in pairwise meta-analyses was assessed using the I² statistic; an I² value ≥ 50% was considered to indicate substantial heterogeneity, in which case a random-effects model was applied, otherwise a fixed-effect model was used. Pairwise meta-analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.0. Clinical heterogeneity was assessed by comparing key study-level characteristics across included trials, including follow-up duration, participant age, retention protocol, retainer design/material, outcome definitions, and risk of bias. Because orthodontic relapse is time-dependent, follow-up duration was considered an exploratory stratification factor (< 12 months vs. ≥12 months). Subgroup analyses were performed only when sufficient studies or connected networks were available within each stratum; when quantitative subgroup analysis was not feasible because of sparse data, disconnected networks, or only one contributing study, the findings were summarized narratively and interpreted cautiously. When a pairwise comparison for a given outcome was informed by only one study, the effect estimate was presented descriptively rather than interpreted as pooled meta-analytic evidence. Pairwise syntheses based on two studies were retained for completeness but were interpreted cautiously because between-study variability and robustness could not be adequately assessed. Subsequently, Bayesian NMA was conducted to compare different types of orthodontic retainers. For each outcome, the evidence network was constructed after data extraction, and its geometry was examined before interpreting the network estimates. Network connectivity, the number of available direct comparisons, and the presence of closed loops were assessed to determine whether inconsistency could be meaningfully evaluated. Before interpreting indirect comparisons, the transitivity assumption was assessed qualitatively by comparing potential effect modifiers across treatment comparisons, including participant age, follow-up duration, retention protocol, retainer material and design, outcome definition, and risk of bias. When these characteristics varied substantially, or when the evidence network was sparse or lacked closed loops, indirect comparisons and ranking probabilities were interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. The NMA was performed in R software version 4.5.0 using the gemtc package within a Bayesian framework based on Markov chain Monte Carlo simulation. For continuous outcomes, a normal likelihood with an identity link was applied, and effect sizes were expressed as MD or SMD. For dichotomous outcomes, a binomial likelihood with a log link was used, and effect sizes were expressed as RR. A random-effects model was applied in all NMAs. Four Markov chains were run with 50,000 iterations, including 20,000 burn-in iterations and a thinning interval of 1. Model fit was evaluated using the deviance information criterion (DIC). Both consistency and inconsistency models were fitted and compared. If the difference in DIC (ΔDIC) was > 5, potential inconsistency within the network was considered; if ΔDIC was ≤ 5, the consistency model was used for the main analysis. However, DIC-based model comparison was not interpreted as definitive evidence that inconsistency was absent, particularly in sparse networks with limited or no closed loops. Posterior distributions were used to calculate ranking probabilities after model convergence was confirmed. Ranking probabilities were interpreted as exploratory summaries and were considered together with effect estimates, credible intervals, and network geometry. Sensitivity analyses were performed by excluding studies judged as having high overall risk of bias. For the NMA, each outcome-specific network was checked after exclusion, and sensitivity analyses were performed only when the network remained connected. Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed using comparison-adjusted funnel plots for all NMA outcomes. For outcomes with 5–9 studies, funnel plots were interpreted descriptively; for outcomes with ≥ 10 studies, an Egger-type regression test was additionally applied. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata version 16.0 and RStudio version 4.5.0.

Results

Study selection

A total of 3,083 potentially relevant records were identified through the systematic search. After removing duplicates using EndNote X9.3.3, 2,060 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 1,982 were excluded for clearly not meeting the inclusion criteria. Subsequently, 78 articles were assessed through full-text review, and 18 studies were finally included in the quantitative analysis [19–36]. The detailed reasons for exclusion are presented in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1).

Study characteristics

A total of 18 RCTs [19–36], published between 2006 and 2025, were included after removing overlapping reports from the same trial populations. These studies involved 1,471 patients who had completed orthodontic treatment and entered the retention phase, from multiple countries including Germany, Malaysia, Iraq, Croatia, Norway, the United States, Sweden, Denmark, Turkey, the United Kingdom, Belgium, and Egypt. The follow-up duration ranged from 3 to 24 months. Most studies compared two types of retainers, while a few were three-arm trials. Detailed characteristics are presented in Table 1. Clinical heterogeneity was observed across the included studies. Follow-up duration, participant characteristics, retention protocols, retainer materials/designs, and outcome assessment methods varied across trials. Because orthodontic relapse may progress over time, these differences were considered important contextual factors when interpreting both direct and network estimates.

Quality assessment

The risk-of-bias assessment is shown in Fig. 2. Among the 18 included studies, 8 studies (44.4%) were judged as having low overall risk of bias, 4 studies (22.2%) as having some concerns, and 6 studies (33.3%) as having high overall risk of bias. High overall risk of bias was mainly related to concerns in measurement of the outcome and deviations from intended interventions. Therefore, the risk-of-bias profile was mixed rather than predominantly low risk, and the findings should be interpreted with appropriate caution.

Pairwise meta-analysis

Several pairwise comparisons were based on only one or two studies; therefore, these estimates were retained to provide a complete summary of the available direct evidence but were interpreted cautiously. Comparisons based on a single study were treated as descriptive findings rather than pooled meta-analytic evidence. In the pairwise meta-analysis comparing fixed retainers and removable retainers, two studies [22, 30] reported periodontal health outcomes, with no significant differences observed. Specifically, there were no statistically significant differences in GI (MD = 0.04, 95% CI: -0.03 to 0.12, P = 0.236) or PI (MD = -0.04, 95% CI: -0.15 to 0.06, P = 0.402), and no heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0%). Regarding dental arch stability outcomes, no significant difference was observed in LII [20, 23, 31, 33] (MD = 0.24, 95% CI: -0.14 to 0.62, P = 0.215; I² = 61.1%). For ICD [20, 22, 31], removable retainers showed a statistically borderline reduction compared with fixed retainers (MD = -0.11 mm, 95% CI: -0.22 to -0.00, P = 0.045; I² = 0%). Given the very small absolute magnitude and the confidence interval approaching the null, this finding should be interpreted cautiously and may not represent a clinically meaningful difference. In addition, AL [20, 31] was significantly increased in the removable retainers group (MD = 0.42, 95% CI: 0.24 to 0.60, P < 0.001; I² = 0%), suggesting less favorable maintenance of arch length compared with fixed retainers. One study [20] reported no significant difference in IPD (MD = -0.22, 95% CI: -2.23 to 1.79, P = 0.830). In terms of safety, only one study [29] reported complications, and no significant difference was observed between groups (RR = 1.00, 95% CI: 0.21 to 4.73). Detailed results are presented in Table 2. Because orthodontic relapse is time-dependent, we explored whether follow-up duration could explain part of the clinical heterogeneity in the direct fixed-versus-removable comparisons. After stratification by follow-up duration (< 12 months vs. ≥12 months), one or both follow-up strata contained fewer than two direct studies for each outcome; therefore, a reliable formal subgroup meta-analysis could not be performed. Follow-up duration was instead treated as an important source of clinical heterogeneity, and pooled estimates combining different follow-up durations were interpreted cautiously because short-term and longer-term relapse outcomes may not be directly comparable. Sensitivity analyses excluding studies at high overall risk of bias were also conducted for the pairwise meta-analysis. The results for GI and PI were unchanged after exclusion. For AL and IPD, no direct fixed-versus-removable comparison remained and sensitivity analysis was therefore not estimable. For LII, the sensitivity estimate was MD = 0.47 (95% CI: 0.11 to 0.82), while for ICD and complications only one direct study remained and the estimates were treated descriptively rather than as pooled meta-analytic results. These results are shown in Table 2.

Network meta-analysis

Network geometry

Based on the available comparative evidence for different outcomes, separate evidence networks were constructed. Although most interventions formed connected networks, several outcome-specific networks were sparse and included few or no closed loops. Direct evidence was mainly available for comparisons between FRs and VFRs and between FRs and CAD/CAM FRs, followed by comparisons between FRs and HRs. Because several networks contained few direct comparisons and limited or no closed loops, local inconsistency could not be reliably evaluated for all outcomes. Therefore, the network estimates should be interpreted primarily as structured summaries of the available comparative evidence rather than as robust indirect comparative evidence. Consistency assessment and model selection

Both consistency and inconsistency models were fitted and compared using the DIC. For all outcomes, the ΔDIC between the consistency and inconsistency models was ≤ 5; therefore, the consistency model was used for the primary analysis. However, this model-selection result should not be interpreted as strong evidence that inconsistency was absent. In sparse networks with few or no closed loops, DIC-based model comparison has limited ability to detect inconsistency. Therefore, the NMA findings, particularly ranking probabilities, were interpreted as exploratory rather than confirmatory. Detailed results are provided in Supplementary Material 2.

Periodontal outcomes (GI and PI)

For periodontal health-related outcomes (Table 3), the NMA did not identify any significant differences among retainers in GI or PI. For GI, compared with CAD/CAM FRs, the MD was − 0.08 (95% CrI: -1.26 to 1.20) for FRs, -0.02 (95% CrI: -2.02 to 2.05) for HRs, and − 0.03 (95% CrI: -1.72 to 1.74) for VFRs. Similarly, for PI, compared with CAD/CAM FRs, the MD was 0.00 (95% CrI: -0.24 to 0.23) for FRs, -0.06 (95% CrI: -0.43 to 0.31) for HRs, and − 0.04 (95% CrI: -0.35 to 0.26) for VFRs. Across both GI and PI networks, all comparisons crossed the null, indicating no clear periodontal advantage of any specific retainer type. However, the credible intervals for several GI comparisons were wide, indicating substantial imprecision. Therefore, ranking probabilities for GI and PI should be interpreted only as exploratory summaries rather than evidence of superiority.

For complication rates (Table 5), the NMA did not identify significant differences among different retainers. Exploratory ranking summaries

Ranking probabilities were reported only as exploratory summaries of relative probabilities. Because most network estimates were imprecise, statistically non-significant, and in several cases based on sparse networks with limited closed loops, these rankings should not be used to infer definitive superiority of any retainer type. Therefore, the ranking results were interpreted together with the corresponding effect estimates, credible intervals, and network geometry. Possible outcome-specific ranking patterns were observed: FRs ranked first for GI, AL, and complications; HRs ranked first for PI and ICD; CAD/CAM FRs ranked first for LII; and VFRs ranked first for IPD. However, these rankings should not be interpreted as evidence that one retainer type is superior, particularly for outcomes with sparse networks, wide credible intervals, or limited closed loops. Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses excluding studies at high overall risk of bias were conducted for both pairwise and network meta-analyses. In the pairwise meta-analysis, the results for GI and PI were unchanged after exclusion of high-risk studies, with GI remaining non-significant (MD = 0.04, 95% CI: −0.03 to 0.12, P = 0.236) and PI also remaining non-significant (MD = − 0.04, 95% CI: −0.15 to 0.06, P = 0.402). For LII, the sensitivity estimate became statistically significant after exclusion of high-risk studies (MD = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.11 to 0.82, P = 0.011; I² = 0%), but this result was based on only three studies and should therefore be interpreted cautiously. For ICD and complications, only one direct study remained after exclusion, so the estimates were presented descriptively rather than as pooled results. For AL and IPD, no direct fixed-versus-removable comparison remained, and sensitivity analysis was not estimable. For the Bayesian NMA, the GI and PI networks were unchanged because no contributing studies were rated as high overall risk of bias. After excluding high-risk studies, the LII, ICD, and complications networks remained connected and were re-analysed, whereas the AL and IPD networks became disconnected and were therefore not estimable. Overall, the sensitivity analyses did not materially alter the main conclusions: most comparisons remained statistically non-significant, and ranking results remained uncertain. Detailed NMA sensitivity league tables and SUCRA rankings are provided in Supplementary Material 4.

Exploratory subgroup analysis by follow-up duration

An exploratory subgroup analysis by follow-up duration was conducted because orthodontic relapse is time-dependent. Bayesian NMA was performed within each follow-up stratum (< 12 months vs. ≥12 months) only when the within-stratum network remained connected and estimable. For GI and PI, both short-term and longer-term networks were estimable, but all credible intervals crossed the null, indicating no clear periodontal advantage of any retainer type within either follow-up stratum. For AL, the < 12-month stratum included only one study and was therefore treated descriptively; no formal league table or SUCRA ranking was interpreted. In the ≥ 12-month stratum, all network comparisons crossed the null, although exploratory rankings favored FRs. For LII and ICD, the < 12-month networks were disconnected and were not quantitatively analyzed. In the ≥ 12-month strata, the estimated comparisons remained imprecise and all credible intervals crossed the null, despite exploratory ranking patterns favoring CAD/CAM FRs for LII and HRs for ICD. For IPD, the < 12-month stratum included only one study and was treated descriptively rather than as a formal network estimate; in the ≥ 12-month stratum, no statistically clear difference was observed among retainers. For complications, both follow-up strata were estimable but sparse, and the relative risk estimates had wide credible intervals crossing 1, indicating no clear difference in complication risk. Overall, the follow-up-stratified analyses did not provide robust evidence that relative retainer effects differed between short-term and longer-term follow-up. These analyses were limited by sparse within-stratum networks, disconnected comparisons for some outcomes, single-study strata, and imprecise estimates. Therefore, the subgroup findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating only. Detailed feasibility assessment and stratified results are provided in Supplementary Material 5.

Publication bias

Comparison-adjusted funnel plots were generated for all seven NMA outcomes and are provided in Supplementary Material 6. For outcomes with 5–9 studies, funnel plots were interpreted descriptively because formal regression-based tests are unreliable with sparse evidence. For outcomes with ≥ 10 studies, an Egger-type regression test was additionally applied; possible asymmetry was observed for LII, whereas no evidence of asymmetry was observed for ICD, as summarized in Supplementary Material 7.

Certainty of evidence

The GRADE assessment showed moderate certainty for GI and PI, low certainty for AL, ICD, and complications, and very low certainty for LII and IPD in the pairwise meta-analysis. The main reasons for downgrading were risk-of-bias concerns, imprecision, and the limited number of studies contributing to several outcomes. The CINeMA assessment indicated very low confidence across all network comparisons. This was mainly attributable to sparse networks, imprecision, limited ability to assess incoherence, and risk-of-bias concerns. Detailed GRADE and CINeMA assessments are provided in Supplementary Materials 8 and 9, respectively.

Discussion

This study systematically evaluated the comparative effectiveness of different orthodontic retainers on dental arch stability and periodontal health based on pairwise meta-analysis and Bayesian NMA. No clear statistically significant differences were observed between fixed and removable retainers across most outcomes. Ranking probabilities suggested possible outcome-specific patterns; however, these patterns should be interpreted cautiously because the evidence networks were sparse, several outcomes lacked closed loops, and most effect estimates were imprecise. Overall, the current evidence does not provide robust support for the definitive superiority of any specific retainer type. The ranking findings should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

From a mechanistic perspective, the findings are consistent with the established distinction between passive biomechanical retention and compliance-dependent removable retention. Fixed retainers maintain tooth position through continuous mechanical constraint and may help resist anterior tooth movement. However, any apparent advantages in LII or AL should be interpreted cautiously because several estimates were based on limited evidence and the certainty of evidence was low or very low. Removable retainers require regular wear, and previous studies have suggested that inadequate wear time may increase the risk of relapse [37, 38]. Nevertheless, compliance was not consistently or objectively assessed in the trials included in this review. Therefore, adherence-related explanations should be regarded as clinical context and hypothesis-generating interpretation rather than direct evidence from the present analysis.

Furthermore, this study highlights that dental arch stability is not a single endpoint but a multidimensional structural outcome [39]. AL and LII primarily reflect anteroposterior stability and individual tooth alignment [7, 40, 41], whereas ICD and IPD represent the maintenance of transverse arch dimensions [41]. Although the pairwise meta-analysis suggested a borderline reduction in ICD with removable retainers, the absolute difference was only 0.11 mm and is unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Moreover, the corresponding NMA estimates for transverse stability outcomes had wide credible intervals crossing the null, reinforcing the need for cautious interpretation. The observed ranking patterns may partly reflect differences in retainer design and retention coverage, but these explanations remain speculative because most network estimates were imprecise and statistically non-significant. Future adequately powered trials are needed to confirm whether specific retainer designs provide meaningful advantages for particular stability outcomes.

In terms of periodontal health, no statistically significant differences were observed among retainer types. Although ranking probabilities suggested possible differences across retainers, these rankings should be interpreted with considerable caution because the NMA estimates were imprecise and the credible intervals crossed the null. Therefore, the current evidence does not establish superiority of HRs or any other retainer type for periodontal outcomes. Possible differences related to oral hygiene access, retainer structure, or plaque retention remain plausible clinical considerations, but they were not directly demonstrated by the present analysis.

Importantly, the findings do not support a simplistic dichotomy such as fixed retainers being superior to removable retainers, or removable retainers being more favorable for periodontal health. Instead, they suggest that retainer selection should remain individualized and should consider relapse risk, periodontal status, patient preference, expected adherence, retainer design, and clinician judgment. However, these clinical considerations should not be interpreted as evidence that any retainer type is definitively superior. Regarding safety, no significant differences in complication rates were observed among the retainer types included in the complication network. The network did not include HRs and was based on limited evidence with wide CrIs; therefore, complication rankings should be regarded as incomplete and insufficient for drawing definitive comparative safety conclusions across all retainer types.

The strengths of this study include the use of a hierarchical analytical strategy integrating both direct and indirect evidence, as well as the simultaneous evaluation of dental arch stability and periodontal health. The added value of this approach lies in updating and organizing the available randomized evidence across specific retainer categories, including CAD/CAM FRs, within a single framework. However, because the available evidence network was sparse, the NMA findings should be regarded as exploratory and should not be interpreted as establishing definitive superiority among retainer types. Several methodological limitations should be considered. First, although potentially overlapping reports were rechecked and overlapping datasets were removed where identified, residual overlap or differences in reporting across follow-up publications cannot be completely excluded. Second, the validity of indirect comparisons depends on the transitivity assumption; although potential effect modifiers such as age, follow-up duration, retention protocol, retainer material/design, outcome definition, and risk of bias were qualitatively examined, these factors varied across studies and may still have affected network estimates. Third, clinical heterogeneity should also be considered when interpreting the findings. The included trials differed in follow-up duration, participant characteristics, retention protocols, retainer design/material, and outcome definitions. Follow-up duration is particularly important because orthodontic relapse is time-dependent. Although we conducted exploratory follow-up–stratified analyses where feasible, formal subgroup comparisons were often limited by sparse evidence, disconnected within-stratum networks, or single-study strata. Therefore, the available data were insufficient to determine whether the comparative effects of different retainers varied according to follow-up duration or other clinical characteristics. Fourth, sensitivity analyses excluding studies at high risk of bias were performed where feasible, but some outcome networks became disconnected after exclusion, limiting interpretability. Fifth, although GRADE and CINeMA assessments were added, the certainty/confidence of evidence remained limited, mainly because of sparse evidence, imprecision, risk-of-bias concerns, and limited ability to assess incoherence. Sixth, patient compliance was not consistently or objectively measured, limiting interpretation of adherence-dependent removable retainer outcomes. Finally, grey literature sources, including conference proceedings, theses, trial registries, unpublished studies, and non-English publications, were not systematically included, which may have led to omission of potentially relevant evidence.

In summary, this study suggests that the clinical effectiveness of orthodontic retainers is multidimensional and subject to individual variability. Future research should incorporate longer follow-up periods and assessments of patient compliance to develop more precise and individualized retention strategies, thereby optimizing long-term orthodontic outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, the available randomized evidence did not demonstrate robust or consistent superiority of any specific orthodontic retainer type across dental arch stability, periodontal health, or complication outcomes. The network meta-analysis provided exploratory ranking summaries, but these should be interpreted cautiously because of sparse networks, limited closed loops, imprecise estimates, and low confidence in much of the network evidence. Future well-designed randomized trials with standardized outcome definitions and longer follow-up are needed to provide more reliable comparative evidence.
