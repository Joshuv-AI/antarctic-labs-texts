Predictive Value of Perilesional Fundus Autofluorescence Patterns and OCT-Based Ellipsoid Zone/Retinal Pigment Epithelium Loss Ratios in Geographic Atrophy Growth Rates
Authors: Julia Mai, Gregor S Reiter, Sophie Riedl, Wolf-Dieter Vogl, Amir Sadeghipour, Alex McKeown, Emma Foos, Roy Schwartz, Stefan Sacu, Hrvoje Bogunovic, Ursula Schmidt-Erfurth
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the association between perilesional fundus autofluorescence (FAF) patterns and ellipsoid zone (EZ)/retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) loss ratio on OCT in geographic atrophy (GA) and their predictive value on disease progression and therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan.DesignPost hoc analysis of 2 prospective randomized controlled phase III clinical trials.SubjectsPatients with GA secondary to age-related macular degeneration.MethodsOCT and FAF images from the OAKS (NCT03525613) and DERBY (NCT03525600) trials, 2 phase III, randomized controlled clinical trials of intravitreal pegcetacoplan for the treatment of GA secondary to age-related macular degeneration, were analyzed. Retinal pigment epithelium and EZ loss were measured on OCT by previously validated deep learning–based algorithms. Perilesional FAF patterns were graded at a centralized reading center from the trials. Pooled study eyes and fellow eyes that met the inclusion/exclusion criteria were divided into 4 quartiles based on baseline EZ/RPE loss ratios on OCT. The distribution of FAF patterns between the quartiles was analyzed. The association between baseline FAF pattern and disease progression and therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan was investigated.Main Outcome MeasuresDistribution of FAF patterns between EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles, mean change in FAF-based GA growth, RPE, and EZ loss on OCT.ResultsFundus autofluorescence and OCT gradings were available for 1317 study and fellow eyes from both trials pooled together. The focal pattern showed smaller ratios (P < 0.001), whereas the diffuse-trickling pattern was present in higher ratios (P = 0.005) at baseline. All other patterns showed no correlation with EZ/RPE loss ratios. There was an association of the focal and the diffuse-trickling pattern with FAF-based GA progression and OCT-based RPE loss, but not with OCT-based EZ loss. A greater treatment effect was identified in eyes with the diffuse-trickling pattern, which was present in a small number of eyes.ConclusionsThe focal and diffuse-trickling FAF patterns were associated with RPE loss; however, perilesional FAF patterns were not associated with photoreceptor degeneration. There was a greater treatment effect in eyes with the diffuse-trickling pattern, presenting with higher EZ/RPE loss ratios on OCT in a small subgroup.Financial DisclosuresProprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Age-related macular degeneration, EZ/RPE loss ratio, Fundus autofluorescence, Geographic atrophy, Optical coherence tomography

Geographic atrophy (GA) secondary to age-related macular degeneration (AMD) is a chronic degenerative disease that leads to progressive and irreversible loss of the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), photoreceptors, choriocapillaris, and visual function.1, 2, 3 Since 2023, the first 2 substances in the form of intravitreal complement inhibitors, namely pegcetacoplan and avacincaptad pegol, which lead to an overall slower progression of GA, have been available to treat the disease in the United States.4,5 Different imaging modalities can be used to monitor GA progression and therapeutic response. The results of the clinical trials are based on fundus autofluorescence (FAF), which is a 2-dimensional imaging method that measures GA lesions at the level of the RPE. Missing fluorophores because of absent RPE cells in GA appear as sharply demarcated hypofluorescent areas on FAF.6 Natural history studies, based on FAF hypoautofluorescence, demonstrated that GA underlies a substantial interindividual variability of progression rates.7 Previous studies investigated the role of hyperfluorescent areas in the junctional zone of GA as a potential marker for disease progression stratification. These perilesional FAF patterns were classified into different categories and were suggested to be correlated with different progression speeds.8 The so-called banded and diffuse patterns were associated with faster GA progression than eyes with the focal or an absent perilesional FAF pattern.9 The diffuse pattern was further subdivided, from which the diffuse-trickling pattern exhibited the highest progression speed.10 Clinical trials therefore used the presence of hyperfluorescent areas in the junctional zone on FAF as an inclusion criterion to ensure disease activity in GA.4

However, distinct retinal layers cannot be visualized on FAF. Other limitations of FAF include the difficulty in evaluating foveal involvement with blue-light FAF in GA, patient discomfort, and requirement for specific camera settings.6 In contrast, OCT provides high-resolution visualization of different retinal layers and has emerged as the standard imaging tool in clinical routine to monitor AMD progression and treatment.11 Several studies have shown the impact of photoreceptor status, which can be visualized on OCT, on future GA progression and therapeutic effect.12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17 In particular, the area of ellipsoid zone (EZ) loss outside of areas with RPE loss, i.e., the EZ/RPE loss ratio, demonstrated an association with GA growth and therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan as assessed by automated OCT analysis.18 Eyes with a high EZ/RPE loss ratio at baseline had significantly faster disease progression and enhanced therapeutic effect than eyes with a low EZ/RPE loss ratio.18 However, the association between perilesional FAF patterns and EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles on OCT as well as the impact of FAF patterns on the therapeutic response in GA has not been investigated so far.

The purpose of this study was therefore to analyze the distribution of perilesional FAF patterns between OCT-based EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles. Moreover, the impact of FAF patterns on disease activity and therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan was investigated using the data from the prospective phase III OAKS and DERBY trials.

Methods

Study Population

This study used FAF and OCT data from the 2 prospective, multicenter, randomized, sham-controlled phase III trials OAKS (NCT03525613) and DERBY (NCT03525600) investigating pegcetacoplan, an intravitreal administered C3 and C3b inhibitor for the treatment of GA secondary to AMD. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria have been published previously.4 In brief, patients had to be aged >60 years with a diagnosis of GA secondary to AMD as confirmed by FAF imaging by experts at a centralized reading center. Patients had to present with a best-corrected visual acuity of at least 24 letters on the ETDRS chart. The eye with the worse best-corrected visual acuity was defined as the study eye if both eyes were eligible. Geographic atrophy area measurements of 2.5 to 17.5 mm2, based on FAF imaging, and at least 1 GA lesion >1.25 mm2 in the case of multifocal lesions were included. All study eyes had to present a mandatory perilesional hyperautofluorescence on FAF for inclusion. Exclusion criteria were history or current evidence of any active macular neovascularization (MNV), defined as exudative MNV showing retinal fluid, GA secondary to other conditions, as well as retinal diseases other than AMD. Eyes with nonexudative MNV as well as those with MNV in the fellow eye were not excluded. Both subfoveal and nonsubfoveal GA lesions were eligible for inclusion in the trials.

Patients were randomized 2:2:1:1 in the trials to receive 15 mg/0.1 mL pegcetacoplan monthly (PM), pegcetacoplan every other month (PEOM), sham monthly, or sham every other month. The primary end point was the change in GA lesion growth at month 12, measured on FAF. Patients were followed up over 24 months, and FAF and OCT imaging were performed at every study visit. During the first 12 months, all patients received monthly study visits, whereas in the second year, patients in every-other-month treatment arms were scheduled for bimonthly study visits.

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before any study-related procedures, and both studies adhered to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. This post hoc analysis was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Medical University of Vienna (EK Nr: 1246/2016).

Image Protocol and Analysis

Patients imaged with a Spectralis HRA+OCT device (Heidelberg Engineering) were included in this analysis. The imaging protocol of the OCT volumes consisted of a macula-centered volumetric scan, covering the 20° × 20° central field of view. The OCT volumes contained 49 B-scans with 120-μm spacing, each containing 512 A-scans with automatic real-time tracking set to 16. The OCT images were acquired with the follow-up function of the device to accurately measure the growth rates over time. Fundus autofluorescence images were acquired using a confocal scanning laser ophthalmoscope (Heidelberg Engineering), with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm and a field of view of 30° × 30°, centered on the fovea. The FAF and OCT imaging data collected over the 24-month period of the trials were used for this analysis.

Fundus autofluorescence images were graded manually at a centralized reading center for diagnosis, lesion growth, and perilesional FAF patterns of the GA lesions. Eyes with the focal, banded, patchy, and diffuse-trickling FAF patterns were included in this analysis. A template of the 4 perilesional FAF patterns that were graded in this study is shown in Figure S1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

OCT volumes were analyzed by 2 complementary, previously validated deep learning–based algorithms for the measurement of RPE loss and EZ loss. The algorithm for the segmentation of RPE loss is based on a specialized convolutional neural network, which segments the entire 3-dimensional OCT volume as a 2-dimensional en face map of RPE loss, reflecting the clinical GA lesion and benefiting from rich spatial context.19 The algorithm for the segmentation of EZ loss consists of a composite convolutional neural network for pixel-level segmentation that delineates the layer between the inner boundary of the EZ and the outer boundary of the interdigitation zone (the inner boundary of the RPE).20 The algorithm provides a thickness value for every A-scan, and an EZ thickness of 4 μm or less was defined as EZ loss. An en face map of EZ loss was generated and brought into correspondence with the RPE loss map. The generated areas of RPE loss and EZ loss within the 20° × 20° field of view of the OCT were calculated and expressed in mm2.

Statistical Analysis

Patients were divided into quartiles of the baseline ratio of EZ loss to RPE loss measured in mm2, with quartile 1 representing small ratios and quartile 4 high ratios, represented by a larger EZ loss area outside of RPE loss. For the analysis of the distribution of FAF patterns between OCT-based EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles, both study eyes and fellow eyes that met the inclusion criteria were included. A χ2 test was calculated to evaluate the difference in the occurrence of perilesional FAF patterns between EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles.

To evaluate the association of perilesional FAF patterns on future disease progression and therapeutic response, data from the 2 studies, OAKS and DERBY, were combined, and eyes from the monthly and every-other-month sham groups were pooled (henceforth called sham).

To investigate the impact of perilesional FAF patterns on disease activity, the pooled sham eyes and eligible fellow eyes from both OAKS and DERBY combined were included, reflecting the natural history course of the disease without any treatment effect.21 The GA growth rate measured manually on FAF as well as the automated OCT-based measurements for RPE loss and EZ loss at month 24 were compared between FAF patterns using a mixed-effect model for repeated measures.

A modified intent-to-treat population was used to perform all treatment efficacy analyses, defined as all patients who had received at least 1 pegcetacoplan or sham injection and had a baseline and at least 1 follow-up visit with a GA lesion measurement by FAF, and that was used for primary efficacy analysis in the OAKS and DERBY studies. Moreover, at least 1 EZ loss and RPE loss measurement from the OCT image had to be available. Therapeutic response of FAF-based GA growth and RPE loss and EZ loss between the perilesional FAF patterns was calculated with a mixed-effect model for repeated measures.

Results

Distribution of FAF Patterns between EZ/RPE Loss Ratio Quartiles

For the evaluation of the distribution of perilesional FAF patterns between EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles on OCT at baseline, 1317 study and eligible fellow eyes were included. The distribution of perilesional FAF patterns between EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles on OCT is summarized in Table 1. Although there was overall high variability in the distribution of FAF patterns between the OCT ratio quartiles, the focal pattern was more often present in quartile 1 (P < 0.001), indicating slower disease progression. The diffuse-trickling pattern, on the contrary, was more often present in the higher OCT ratio quartiles (P = 0.005), indicating fast disease progression. However, the diffuse-trickling pattern was only present in a small number of eyes (n = 52), representing about 4% of the study population.

Impact of FAF Patterns on Disease Progression

For the analysis of the impact of perilesional FAF patterns on future disease progression, both for GA growth on FAF as well as RPE and EZ loss on OCT, 604 combined study eyes from the pooled sham cohort and eligible fellow eyes were included. In total, 282 (46.7%) eyes presented with the focal pattern, 173 (28.6%) with the banded pattern, 125 (20.7%) with the patchy pattern, and 24 (4.0%) with the diffuse-trickling pattern.

Regarding FAF-based GA growth, there was moderately slower progression in eyes with the focal pattern than in those with the banded (–14.1%, P = 0.01) and patchy (–22.7%, P < 0.0001) patterns. Only the diffuse-trickling (–42.5%, P < 0.0001) pattern at month 24 showed greater progression over the long term than the focal pattern. The banded and the patchy patterns demonstrated slower GA progression on FAF than the diffuse-trickling pattern at month 24 (–33.1%, P < 0.0001 and –25.7%, P = 0.001, respectively). There was no significant difference in FAF-based GA growth rates between the banded and the patchy FAF patterns (10% slower progression in banded vs. patchy pattern, P = 0.08). For OCT-based RPE loss, slower progression was present in eyes with the focal pattern than in those with the patchy pattern (–20%, P = 0.0001) and the diffuse-trickling pattern (–37.6%, P < 0.0001) at month 24. Impact of FAF Patterns on Therapeutic Response

To evaluate the impact of perilesional FAF patterns on the therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan, 888 study eyes from OAKS and DERBY combined were included: 272 from the PM group, 311 from the PEOM group, and 305 from the sham group. In the PM group, 140 (51%) eyes with the focal, 54 (20%) with the banded, 67 (25%) with the patchy, and 11 (4%) with the diffuse-trickling pattern were included in the analysis. In the PEOM group, 144 (46%) eyes with the focal, 73 (23%) eyes with the banded, 79 (25%) eyes with the patchy, and 15 (5%) eyes with the diffuse-trickling pattern were included. In the sham group, 146 (48%) eyes had the focal, 79 (26%) the banded, 67 (22%) the patchy, and 13 (4%) the diffuse-trickling pattern.

For FAF-based GA growth, slower progression was observed in eyes with the focal pattern in the PM group compared with sham (–19.7%, P = 0.0009) and in the PEOM group compared with sham (–16.6%, P = 0.005) at month 24. There was a slower progression in FAF-based GA growth in eyes with the patchy pattern in the PM group compared with sham (–16.8%, P = 0.023) and in the PEOM group compared with sham, which did not reach significance (–10.6%, P = 0.13). Eyes with the diffuse-trickling pattern presented a more pronounced treatment effect with significantly slower GA progression on FAF in PM compared with sham (–33.8%, P = 0.007) and PEOM compared with sham (–27.9%, P = 0.016). Eyes presenting with the banded pattern did not show a significant difference in treatment effects for PM compared with sham (–12.6%, P = 0.098) and PEOM compared with sham (–12.9%, P = 0.065). Discussion

Perilesional FAF patterns were originally described to represent distinct phenotypes in GA that exhibit different progression rates and thus could be used to stratify patients into fast and slow progressors.9,22 More recently, automated image analysis of OCT revealed a more objective and quantifiable biomarker that had significant associations with future GA progression rates, namely the EZ/RPE loss ratio.18 The rationale behind this study was to investigate the distribution of perilesional FAF patterns between EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles on OCT and the association of perilesional FAF patterns on future disease progression of GA growth measured on FAF as well as on RPE loss and EZ loss measured on OCT. Moreover, the present study addresses the previously unexplored impact of FAF patterns on the therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan, based on data from the 2 largest successful phase III trials in GA to date.4

The association between OCT-based EZ/RPE loss ratios and perilesional FAF patterns at baseline was primarily very variable. A significant difference was found in the distribution of the focal and the diffuse-trickling patterns between EZ/RPE loss ratios. The focal pattern was more prominent in quartile 1 with the smallest EZ/RPE loss ratio, indicating slow disease progression, whereas the diffuse-trickling pattern was more present in higher EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles, indicating faster progression speed. However, only a very small number, 4% of eyes, was graded as the diffuse-trickling pattern, which is in line with previous studies.9 The banded and the patchy patterns did not show a significant difference in the distribution between the EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles. Because the perilesional FAF patterns are based on manual grading, they underlie substantial interreader variability, as shown previously.23 On the other hand, the EZ/RPE loss ratio is based on automated image segmentation and therefore represents an objective and reproducible biomarker.

Previous studies hypothesized that GA progression speed measured on FAF is associated with perilesional FAF patterns.2,9,24,25 This study showed that the focal and the diffuse-trickling perilesional FAF patterns were associated with future FAF-based GA growth and OCT-based RPE loss. A significant difference in the growth rate became evident for the focal pattern, which showed slower progression than the other patterns, and the diffuse-trickling pattern, which showed faster progression than the other patterns, both for FAF-based GA growth and RPE loss on OCT. However, there was no significant association between perilesional FAF patterns and future EZ loss, reflecting the most detrimental path in GA on OCT. Originally, it was postulated that the hyperautofluorescence of perilesional FAF patterns derives from accumulated lipofuscin in the junctional zone of GA.26 Histologic autofluorescence, however, revealed that higher autofluorescent intensity was associated with vertically superimposed RPE cells and areas with advanced RPE alterations.27,28 These studies suggested, therefore, that hyperautofluorescent FAF patterns do not necessarily predict subsequent cell death and do not support the predisposition of excess RPE lipofuscin to atrophy expansion. A study investigating quantitative FAF in the junctional zone around the GA lesion did not find overall increased FAF intensities in patients with unifocal GA29 and a steady decrease of quantitative FAF in patients with intermediate AMD that progress to GA,30 supporting the theory of lipofuscin loss rather than its accumulation. Other studies found that the baseline hyperautofluorescence correlated with baseline GA area, implicating that FAF patterns do reflect disease stages rather than distinct phenotypes in GA.26,31 It was shown that eyes with the banded and diffuse patterns had larger baseline GA areas, and eyes with low hyperautofluorescence (or no focal pattern) were associated with smaller baseline areas of GA. They also hypothesized that the enlargement of GA induces changes in FAF signals and that FAF patterns are the consequence and not the cause of GA progression.26 Another study that used digital image analysis of serial FAF images found that increased FAF is not a strong risk factor for the development or the expansion of GA and hypothesized that other mechanisms not measured by increased FAF are driving the development of GA.32 A recent histopathologic analysis found that photoreceptor disintegrity leads to a reduction of incoming light absorption and might therefore be a contributor to hyperautofluorescence over drusen on FAF. Moreover, photoreceptor disintegrity leads to more incoming light reaching the RPE, which is usually dysmorphic and thickened, and therefore possibly contributes to hyperautofluorescence in the outer junctional zone, suggesting that EZ loss predates RPE loss.33 Automated OCT image analysis revealed a significant impact of the photoreceptor layer, in particular the EZ layer, on future disease progression in GA, both globally and locally.12, 13, 14 In our study, no significant association was found between perilesional FAF patterns and future EZ loss on OCT. This finding suggests that perilesional FAF patterns do not reflect disease activity and adds to the understanding of disease pathogenesis in GA. With the acknowledgment by the US Food and Drug Administration of EZ loss as an outcome marker for GA trials, EZ loss has emerged as an important biomarker in GA monitoring.34 Our study also shows a more pronounced treatment effect of pegcetacoplan on future EZ loss than on RPE loss, indicating that EZ loss may be a more powerful biomarker of atrophy progression. This has also been shown in other studies.15, 16, 17, 18 Because perilesional FAF patterns reflect variability in morphologic alterations of impaired RPE, no association was found between FAF patterns and the status of the photoreceptor layers. However, a study showed that early GA lesions are associated with larger EZ/RPE loss ratios and higher disease activity, whereas more advanced GA lesions present with lower EZ/RPE loss ratios, underlining the role of the EZ/RPE loss ratio for determining disease activity in GA.35 Another recent study using data from a clinical setting in a smaller cohort of eyes demonstrated that eyes with the diffuse and diffuse-trickling pattern were more likely to present with a higher EZ/RPE loss ratio. By evaluating the simultaneous impact of the baseline GA lesion size, perilesional FAF patterns, and EZ/RPE loss ratio on future GA progression, the authors showed that none of the patterns had a significant impact, whereas the EZ/RPE loss ratio at baseline contributed significantly to the slope of GA progression.36

This study evaluated the association of perilesional FAF patterns on therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan. There was no difference between FAF patterns on therapeutic response except for the patchy vs. the diffuse-trickling pattern on OCT-based RPE loss and the patchy vs. the diffuse-trickling and focal vs. the diffuse-trickling patterns on OCT-based EZ loss. However, only a small number of eyes were graded with the diffuse-trickling pattern in our study; therefore, these results should be interpreted with caution. The diffuse-trickling pattern was originally described to exhibit the highest GA progression rates of the FAF patterns.9 A strong association was found between the diffuse-trickling pattern and the presence of subretinal drusenoid deposits (SDDs), which are a known risk factor for disease progression in AMD and faster progression rates in GA.12,37, 38, 39 Moreover, patients presenting with the diffuse-trickling pattern were reported to have a younger age at onset, showed a thin choroid, and had a higher prevalence of systemic comorbidities.10 This is in line with the hypothesis that the presence of SDDs reflects a modified pathologic pathway to GA compared with drusen-related pathogenesis, in which SDDs are more linked to systemic cardiovascular disease and choroidal factors such as hypoperfusion.40,41

A solid risk/benefit ratio needs to be considered before implementing intravitreal treatment for GA. Once started, it is a long-term treatment with possible side effects and interindividual differences in treatment response. Therefore, a robust biomarker for patient stratification would be helpful to identify patients that benefit most from treatment. Post hoc analyses by means of automated image analysis of OCT from the OAKS and DERBY trials demonstrated that the EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles were associated with treatment efficacy.18 Treatment response increased from EZ/RPE loss ratio quartile 1 over quartile 2 and quartile 3 and was most pronounced in quartile 4, whereas in this study, only eyes with the diffuse-trickling pattern were associated with superior treatment effects, represented by only a small number of eyes. Therefore, the use of automated and objective measurements of EZ/RPE loss ratios on OCT might represent a reliable and robust biomarker for risk stratification in patients with GA.

Limitations of this study include the retrospective design of a post hoc analysis, which could lead to a potential selection bias. Only eyes that were imaged with a Spectralis device were included in this analysis, because the device-specific algorithms need to be trained and validated. However, the Spectralis device was used in the majority of the images obtained in the OAKS and DERBY trials. Moreover, most study eyes included in the trials were already advanced with a large GA lesion at baseline. Furthermore, the included numbers in the statistical model are limited because of the splitting of the study cohort in the treatment groups and between the perilesional FAF patterns. Prospective, longitudinal, studies from clinical settings are needed to evaluate these findings. Another limitation is that eyes with the diffuse pattern apart from the diffuse-trickling were not included in this study, and that FAF patterns are based on manual grading and are therefore prone to interreader variability. However, the grading was performed at a reading center in a standardized manner.

In conclusion, perilesional FAF patterns were associated with baseline EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles on OCT only in a variable manner. Eyes with the focal pattern were more common in smaller quartiles, and eyes with the diffuse-trickling pattern, although only a small number of eyes, were more common in higher quartiles. Fundus autofluorescence patterns were associated with future FAF-based GA progression and OCT-based RPE loss but not with OCT-based EZ loss, highlighting the limited ability of FAF to reflect the pathologic mechanisms involved in GA. This work provides novel insights into the association between perilesional FAF patterns and the therapeutic response to pegcetacoplan in GA. A more pronounced treatment effect was observed in the diffuse-trickling pattern, which was only present in <5% of eyes. Because eyes with the diffuse-trickling pattern showed higher EZ/RPE loss ratio quartiles on OCT, the use of automated and objective measurements of EZ/RPE loss ratios on OCT might be a reliable biomarker for risk stratification in patients with GA.
