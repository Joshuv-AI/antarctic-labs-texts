Thyroid autoimmunity is strongly associated with embryo quality in infertile euthyroid women receiving IVF/ICSI, especially patients with TSH values above 2.5 uIU/mL and poor ovarian response
Authors: Yimeng Lu, Shanshan Wu, Xudong Zhang, Xu Zhang, Xinyao Li, Jichun Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMaternal thyroid autoimmune condition on reproductive health is a topic worthy of attention. Current studies of different groups have produced conflicting results regarding the precise relationship between thyroid autoimmunity (TAI) and IVF/ICSI outcomes.MethodsA retrospective cohort study included 3197 infertile euthyroid women undergoing their first fresh cycle of IVF/ICSI in our center between January 2015 to May 2024. Participants were split into two groups: the TAI group (n = 965) and the control group (n = 2232) based on the thyroid autoantibody status. Adjusted multivariate logistic regression models were employed to assess the association between TAI and embryo quality as well as pregnancy outcomes. The subgroup and stratified analyses were performed to evaluate the role of thyroid autoantibody types and titers. The subgroup analysis was conducted based on serum TSH levels and ovarian response.ResultsThe TAI was negatively related to embryos with two-pronucleus (2PN), available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos, no matter which type of thyroid autoantibody is positive. Stratification analysis of thyroid autoantibody titers showed the association was greater as the thyroglobulin antibodies (TGAb) and thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAb) titers increased, respectively. The subgroup analysis showed that TAI patients with high normal thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH) levels (≥ 2.5uIU/mL) and poor ovarian response (POR) were more likely to have fewer 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos. However, no association between TAI and pregnancy outcomes was found in all models.ConclusionThis study provides comprehensive evidence for the adverse effects of TAI on embryo quality during IVF/ICSI. It is necessary to closely monitor the thyroid hormones and autoantibody levels during treatment, especially in TAI women with TSH ≥ 2.5 uIU/mL and POR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12884-025-08145-3.

Introduction

Thyroid autoimmunity (TAI) is one of the most common causes of thyroid dysfunction among women of childbearing age and is mainly characterized by the presence of thyroglobulin antibodies (TGAb) and/or thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPOAb) [1]. It is proven that the ovary and the thyroid gland share multiple antigens [2–4], thus, the existence of thyroid autoantibodies may theoretically cause damage to the ovaries. It may explain that euthyroid women with TAI were more prone to be infertile [5] and have a higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes [6, 7].

In vitro fertilization (IVF)/Intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) is currently considered one of the most effective treatment strategies for infertility [8, 9]. Previous studies have shown that thyroid autoantibody as an independent factor may be associated with failure of IVF/ICSI, such as recurrent implantation failure, and embryo maldevelopment [10].

Thyroid dysfunction affects not only maternal reproductive health but also fetal intelligence development [11, 12]. The influence of maternal thyroid autoimmune condition on reproductive health is a topic worthy of attention. However, the findings have remained the subject of substantial disagreement among studies due to variable study populations and research designs. The specific mechanism of thyroid autoantibodies on reproductive health still needs further exploration.

This study aimed to investigate the exact influence of maternal TAI on IVF/ICSI outcomes and further clarify which condition is mainly responsible by subgroup and stratification analysis.

Methods

Study design and participants

A total of 3197 participants undergoing their first fresh cycle of IVF/ICSI in the Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University were screened for eligibility and approved by the Ethics Committee. All patients received routine thyroid function tests, including free triiodothyronine (FT3), free thyroxine (FT4), thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH), TGAb, and TPOAb, which were measured with the automated immunochemiluminescent assay (ICMA) kits (Abbott, IL, USA) [13]. The thyroid function test within one year before controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) of participants was included in the statistical analysis in the present study.

Patients aged 20–40 years old with normal thyroid function were included: TSH = 0.3–4.8 uIU/mL; FT3 = 2.63–5.71 pmol/l; FT4 = 9.01–19.05 pmol/l. The exclusion criteria included: (1) the history of thyroid disease, autoimmune disease, X chromosome abnormalities; (2) the history of thyroid hormone/anti-thyroid drug use, thyroid surgery, ovarian treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or surgery); (3) the use of donor oocytes, pre-implantation genetic testing; (4) male partner with semen and endocrine abnormalities. The TAI group (n = 965) was further divided into the TPOAb single-positive group (> 5.61 IU/ml, n = 411), the TGAb single-positive group (> 4.11 IU/ml, n = 118), and both TPOAb and TGAb positive group (n = 436). The control group (n = 2232) was patients with both TPOAb and TGAb within the normal limits.

Archive files and raw data between January 2015 to May 2024 were anonymously obtained from the computerized medical chart database of the Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University.

Treatment protocols

According to the different conditions of the patients, the appropriate COS regimen was selected: gonadotropin-releasing hormone (GnRH) antagonist, GnRH agonist, microdose flare-up, and other protocols. Ovarian response was monitored by hormonal and ultrasonographic measurements to adjust doses. Normally, when three or more follicles reached a mean diameter of 18 mm, human chorionic gonadotropin (hCG) was injected to induce ovulation, and oocytes were retrieved by ovarian puncture under transvaginal ultrasonography (TVU) after 34-36 h. The oocyte and embryo morphological assessments were determined by senior embryologists blinded to the study. The retrieved oocytes were fertilized using IVF or ICSI based on sperm quality and clinical indications. The ICSI procedure was routinely performed for all retrieved metaphase II oocytes for male infertility. Embryo transfer (ET) was performed under transabdominal ultrasound guidance 3rd/5th days after oocyte retrieval if high-quality embryos exist and there are no other contraindications. Using the Peter system to evaluate Day 3 cleavage embryo quality [14, 15], embryos with 6–10 cells and anucleate fragmentation < 20% were categorized as high-quality cleavage embryos. Using the Gardner system to evaluate Day 5 blastocyst quality [16], a high-quality blastocyst was defined as having a grade ≥ 3BB on day 5 or ≥ 4BB on day 6. All embryo quality assessments were performed by dual independent grading to ensure high intra- and inter-rater reliabilities, that is, two fixed senior embryologists (> 5 years of experience) using standard operating procedure. Biochemical pregnancy was determined by serum hCG > 5 mIU/mL at 2 weeks after ET. Clinical pregnancy was considered as the ultrasound-confirmed observation of a gestational sac 28 days after ET. Live birth was defined as the delivery of at least one viable fetus after 28 weeks of gestation. Miscarriage was defined as a fetal loss within 20 weeks of gestation.

Measurements and outcomes

Archive files and the database of patient records were examined for age, body mass index (BMI), basal follicle-stimulation hormone (bFSH), anti-Mullerian hormone (AMH), and thyroid function tests. Follicles between 2–9 mm in diameter during the early follicular phase by TVU were recorded as basal antral follicle counts (AFC). The cycle data, including COS protocols, starting dose of gonadotropin (Gn), endometrial thickness on the day of hCG, the number of oocytes retrieved, embryo grade, number, stage, and quality of embryos transferred, as well as pregnancy outcomes, were collected.

The main outcomes were the embryo outcomes, including two-pronucleus (2PN) embryos, available cleavage embryos, high-quality cleavage embryos, abnormal fertilized embryos, blastocyst formation, and high-quality blastocysts. The abnormal fertilized embryos meant non-2PN fertilized embryos, including embryos with no PN, embryos with one PN, and embryos with multiple PN. In this study, the rate of 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, high-quality cleavage embryos, and abnormal fertilized embryos was calculated among the patients retrieving oocytes ≥ 1.

Pregnancy outcomes were the subordinate outcomes, including biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, miscarriage, and live birth. In this study, the rate of biochemical pregnancy, clinical pregnancy, miscarriage, and live birth were calculated based on patients receiving fresh ET.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to describe participants' demographic, cycle, and clinical features. The normality of continuous variables using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and continuous variables with non-normality were compared with the Mann–Whitney U test, shown as [median (IQR)]. The categorical variable was presented as [number (percentage)] using Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact test. Embryo outcomes and pregnancy outcomes were considered binary outcomes. Of embryo outcomes, 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, high-quality cleavage embryos, and abnormal fertilized embryos were calculated based on per oocytes retrieved, while blastocyst formation and high-quality blastocysts were based on per embryo receiving blastocyst culture. Multivariate logistic regression analysis was conducted to assess the effect of TAI on embryo outcomes and pregnancy outcomes. The potential confounders included factors that had been reported to be related to study outcomes that were adjusted in the final models [17, 18], including age, BMI, infertility duration, history of ≥ 2 first-trimester miscarriages, smoking status, infertility type, infertility cause, bFSH, AMH, AFC, FT3, FT4, TSH, COS protocols, fertilization method, starting dose of Gn. All models applied the same set of covariates. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess the multicollinearity of variables. When VIF < 5, we considered that there was no multicollinearity.

According to the status of thyroid autoantibody, the TAI group was divided into three subgroups (TPOAb single-positive group, TGAb single-positive group, and both TPOAb and TGAb positive group) for further analysis. Stratified analysis according to thyroid autoantibody titers. The negative population was regarded as a reference, TGAb- or TPOAb-positive women were divided into four groups according to the quartiles of the antibody titers, respectively.

The serum TSH levels have been reported to be associated with embryo implantation and pregnancy outcomes [19, 20]. A TSH concentration of 2.5 uIU/mL was deemed as the cut point according to the recommendation of the 2015 American Society for Reproductive Medicine Guideline (ASRM) [20]. Thus, the subgroup analysis was performed based on the categories of serum TSH levels (< 2.5 uIU/mL and ≥ 2.5 uIU/mL) in the two groups, respectively. In addition, considering the possible relationship between TAI and ovarian reserve [21–23], another subgroup analysis was performed based on the ovarian response concerning the Bologna criteria (10): poor ovarian response (POR) patients (≤ 3 oocytes retrieved after COS, n = 1261) and normal ovarian response (NOR) patients (10–15 oocytes retrieved after COS, n = 1283).

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 26.0). A 2-tailed value of p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

After adjustment for potential confounders which were reported to affect the embryos and pregnancy, the adjusted multivariate logistic regression analysis revealed a significant decline in chances of patients with TAI to achieve 2PN embryos [OR 0.962 (95% CI 0.946–0.978), p < 0.001], available cleavage embryos [OR 0.938 (95% CI 0.918–0.958), p < 0.001], high-quality cleavage embryos [OR 0.915 (95% CI 0.882–0.950), p < 0.001], and high-quality blastocysts [OR 0.932 (95% CI 0.880–0.988), p = 0.018]. Additionally, abnormal fertilized embryos were significantly positively associated with the TAI [OR 1.125 (95% CI 1.019–1.243), p = 0.020]. After correcting for relative confounders, the association of TAI with pregnancy outcomes is still not found.

Subgroup analysis according to thyroid autoantibody type

To further figure out which autoantibody matters, we divided the TAI group into three subgroups according to the status of thyroid autoantibody type: TPOAb-positive group, TGAb-positive group, and both TPOAb and TGAb-positive group. The relationship of these three subgroups with embryo and pregnancy outcomes is presented in Table 4. Either patients in the TPOAb-positive group or both TPOAb and TGAb-positive groups were significantly negatively associated with 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos. Discussion

Multiple studies have shown that TAI may influence various stages of IVF/ICSI, from follicular and embryo growth to pregnancy [22, 24]. The infertile women who had a higher prevalence of TAI can also prove the existence of a connection between TAI and impaired IVF/ICSI outcomes from the side [25]. However, the underlying mechanisms are still not clear. Now, many studies have reported the association between TAI and IVF/ICSI outcomes, but due to the heterogeneity of participants, different study designs and sample sizes, and differences in ethnicity and life habits, the results remain controversial.

In the present study, the TAI was significantly negatively associated with 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos, and such association was stronger as the thyroid autoantibody titers increased. Especially in the high normal TSH patients (TSH ≥ 2.5uIU/mL) and POR patients, such a relationship is more significant statistically, but no associations are found in the low normal TSH patients and NOR patients. In addition, our results did not express an association in terms of pregnancy outcomes.

As for the precise relationship between the TAI and the embryo quality, our results have shown a significantly negative effect of TAI in terms of 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos. Table 2 presents TAI women have a lower fresh ET rate, which may be attributed to the absence of available cleavage embryos. While the inadequate endometrium is another cause, since previous research indicated that TAI would compromise endometrial receptivity [26]. Accumulative evidence has proven that TAI significantly affected embryo quality in euthyroid women undergoing assisted reproduction technology [27, 28], which is consistent with the results of our study. A retrospective study published by Monteleone et.al had proven that TAI was related to lower fertilization rate, lower embryo quality, and lower pregnancy rate, as well as higher early miscarriage rate [29]. We supposed that embryo quality is impaired due to the presence of thyroid autoantibodies. Monteleone et.al demonstrated that TGAb and TPOAb could be measured in ovarian follicular fluid in TAI patients [29]. It had been known that the ovary and the thyroid gland share multiple antigens [2–4], thus the presence of thyroid autoantibodies may exert an infiltration to impair oocytes, thereby reducing the quality and fertilization potential, which was also confirmed by our results. Our results seem to show that the TPOAb-positive group, as well as both the TPOAb and TGAb-positive group, are more responsible. However, the sample size of the TGAb-positive group was small, and its role needs further investigation. In addition, our results provide evidence that increasing TPOAb and TGAb levels can reduce the probability of obtaining 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos. It was also consistent with most of the previous studies [30–32].

Cause studies of different groups have produced conflicting results, we restricted the study participants by subgroup analysis to reduce the heterogeneity. Most studies proposed that preconception TSH value is an independent risk factor of fertility outcome [33–35]. One study in 1993 proved that the presence of thyroid hormones in female follicular fluid and the thyroid hormone receptors on the surface of female granulosa cells [36] suggest thyroid hormones may impact female follicle development by influencing granulosa cells. It also explains the fewer retrieved oocytes in the TAI group in our study (Table 1). The guideline from the ASRM [20] and the American Thyroid Association (ATA) [37] recommended that serum TSH levels should be below 2.5 uIU/mL for TAI women before activating IVF/ICSI, and that is supported by our study as well. As shown in Table 5, TAI patients with high normal TSH levels (≥ 2.5 uIU/mL) have a lower possibility of obtaining 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos.

Furthermore, in the present results, the AMH values, the number of AFC, and retrieved oocytes were significantly lower, as well as the bFSH value was significantly higher in the TAI group, indicating that decreased ovarian function exists in TAI patients. Pathophysiology suggests alterations in thyroid hormone production may disrupt the hypothalamic-pituitary-ovarian axis to impair folliculogenesis [38]. POR is a manifestation of decreased ovarian reserve. In the present study, we found that POR patients with TAI were more Likely to have fewer 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos than POR patients with non-TAI, no matter which thyroid autoantibody type is positive. Meanwhile, in the NOR group, TAI did not affect embryo quality. New research conducted by Yongjie Zhang et.al had similar results [39]. They explored the association of TAI with ART outcomes in 1172 euthyroid women with diminished ovarian reserve and showed that TAI was associated with reductions in the number of oocytes retrieved and embryo quality. The thyroid function screening in women with premature ovarian insufficiency and diminished ovarian reserve is best performed following the recommendations made by the 2021 European Thyroid Association (ETA) [40] and the European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) in 2016 [41]. Such a recommendation above also should be applied to women with POR.

As early as 1996, Geva et.al first reported a negative correlation between TAI and pregnancy rate per cycle in IVF [42]. A more accepted hypothesis is that TAI, as an immune disorder, could hinder the establishment of immune tolerance and further influence pregnancy and live births [43, 44]. Another study proposes that TAI patients have a subtle deficiency of thyroid function, although the clinical examinations are normal, since TAI may increase the risk of developing subclinical and clinical hypothyroidism [45–47]. The same is true of our results. Although the thyroid function of all participants was normal in our study, as shown in Table 1, the TAI group had a relatively lower FT3 value and higher TSH value. Therefore, thyroid-related hormones may not meet the demand during the COS process and early pregnancy in women with TAI, leading to miscarriage eventually. Likewise, a meta-analysis including 4 studies has shown that TAI patients had a higher miscarriage rate, but no relationship between clinical pregnancy and live birth [10, 48]. Another meta-analysis including 12 studies found such an association between TAI and a higher miscarriage rate and a lower live birth rate [10]. Still, some other studies showed no association between TAI and pregnancy outcomes of IVF/ICSI, potentially due to a relatively small sample size that cannot show the association [49–51]. Luckazuk et.al showed no significant differences in terms of fertilization rate, implantation, pregnancy, Live birth, and miscarriage between 114 TPOAb-positive patients and 495 non-TAI infertile patients receiving IVF/ICSI [49]. Also, Sakar et.al and Tan et.al showed comparable pregnancy and miscarriage rates between TAI and non-TAI patients with 251 samples and 835 samples, respectively [50, 51]. Therefore, further studies with large sample sizes are needed to investigate the relationship between TAI and pregnancy outcomes in IVF/ICSI. Our results have not found any association between TAI and pregnancy outcomes, which may be due to the preferential selection of high-quality embryos for ET in clinical practice. Cumulative pregnancy outcomes were not investigated due to only first-fresh ET in the present study. From Table 2, the number, stage, and quality of embryos transferred were comparable between the two groups, which greatly affected pregnancy outcomes.

Finally, the choice of fertilization method is also worth noting, although our data did not show differences. ETA guidelines released in 2021 [40] suggested ICSI was the preferred fertilization method for TAI women. ICSI may improve pregnancy outcomes in TAI women by mitigating the adverse effects of abnormal follicular micro-environment [29, 52]. However, some studies have reported that ICSI did not receive better ART outcomes compared to IVF in TAI women [53–55]. Further clinical trials are needed to demonstrate this point.

Taken together, as shown in Fig. 3, the present study underscores the need for careful attention to embryo quality in TAI women with high-risk factors undergoing IVF/ICSI, that is when accompanied by POR, high normal TSH levels, or elevated thyroid autoantibody titers. Toward them, appropriate interventions can be implemented in clinical practice, such as controlling thyroid hormone levels, individualizing COS protocol, and using ICSI preferentially. For TAI women without high-risk factors above, conventional IVF can still be the preferred treatment, and excessive thyroid function intervention is unnecessary. Preconception maternal thyroid overtreatment may cause potential risk factors. We included only patients’ first fresh cycle, which may reduce the impact of multiple failure cycles in IVF/ICSI on the titer of thyroid antibodies [57]. Besides, we excluded patients who had a history of thyroid treatment and some common comorbidities with TAI as much as possible to eliminate confounders. However, as a single-center retrospective study, this study had several limitations. First, access to pertinent data was limited, and the sample size was small, which may affect the precision of effect estimates. Second, although embryo assessment was performed by experienced permanent embryologists using validated criteria in our center, inherent biological variability and subjective interpretation may affect grading reproducibility. Last, a homogeneous Asian population limited the applicability for other ethnic groups. Thus, future multi-center studies with cross-validation would need to strengthen these findings.

Conclusion

In summary, we demonstrated that the TAI reduces the probability of 2PN embryos, available cleavage embryos, and high-quality cleavage embryos among euthyroid women receiving IVF/ICSI, especially those women with TSH values above 2.5 uIU/mL or poor ovarian response. It is necessary to focus on the TSH level and ovarian response for infertile patients with TAI receiving IVF/ICSI in clinical practice.
