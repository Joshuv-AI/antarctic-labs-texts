Independent and joint relationships of physical activity and pulmonary function with the risk of all-cause mortality
Authors: Jingxin Ma, Can Shen, Yu Jia, Rong Yang, Shanye Yi, Yiheng Zhou, Rui Zeng, Zhi Wan, Qian Zhao, Yi Lei, Xiaoyang Liao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPhysical activity (PA) plays a crucial protective role in the overall health outcomes of individuals with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. However, accurate recommendations for amount and categories of PA are still unclear for individuals with impaired pulmonary function (PF) in community environments. Thus, this study aims to investigate the independent and joint relationships of PA and PF with mortality.MethodsIn a prospective UK Biobank cohort study, PA was collected by touchscreen questionnaires, and metabolic equivalents of task (MET)-minutes/week of total exercise was calculated by walking plus moderate-intensity PA plus vigorous-intensity PA. According to the standard of the Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (GOLD) 2024, GOLD stage 1, GOLD stage 2 and GOLD stages 3–4 were classified as mild, medium and severe PF impairment, respectively. Cox regression was established to analyse the association between PA, PF and the risk of all-cause mortality.ResultsA total of 354 572 participants were included with mean age of 56.0 years. Compared with lowest quintile of PA, highest quintile of PA was associated with lower risk of mortality in individuals with normal PF (HR=0.828), mild (HR=0.768), medium (HR=0.697) and severe PF impairment (HR=0.588). Generally, PF impairment related to mortality, and this relationship was independent of PA (p<0.05), except for individuals adhering to third quintile (1378–2340 MET-minutes/week) of PA (p>0.05). In joint analysis, more severe PF impairment and lower PA had a dose-response relationship with mortality. Moreover, highest quintile of walking/total exercise was associated with elevated mortality, while adhering to high level of vigorous-intensity PA/total exercise ratio achieved lowest risk of mortality, regardless of individuals with or without PF impairment.ConclusionsThere were significant independent and joint relationships of lower PA and PF impairment with the elevated risk of mortality. However, engaging in appropriate total exercise weekly can mitigate adverse effects associated with mild PF decline.

Introduction

Individuals with impaired pulmonary function (PF) encounter numerous challenges, including clinical symptoms such as dyspnoea, wheezing and chest tightness, and they are at an elevated risk of all-cause mortality (ACM).1 2 Given the growing prevalence of smoking and an ageing demographic, the mortality rate among those with impaired PF is anticipated to escalate, further exacerbating the health burden.1 3 Consequently, it is imperative to implement strategies to mitigate the adverse impacts of declining PF.

While pharmacological treatments are the most common intervention and can alleviate clinical symptoms, their efficacy in enhancing physical function or halting disease progression is limited.4 5 Non-pharmacological interventions, particularly physical activity (PA), have been shown to significantly influence the prognosis of pulmonary disease.5 6 Robust evidence supports the notion that regular PA can positively affect the overall health outcomes of patients with chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), including slowing the decline in PF and improving quality of life.5 7 8 However, no prior research has investigated the impact of PA on individuals with or without impaired PF within a community context. More importantly, many prior studies have categorised PA intensity solely based on the frequency or type of exercise, while a more accurate approach is to quantify PA using metabolic equivalents of task (MET) and categorise it into light-intensity physical activity (LPA), moderate-intensity physical activity (MPA) and vigorous-intensity physical activity (VPA) based on MET values. Given the potential of PA to protect PF, it is imperative to investigate which specific levels and forms of PA can counteract the detrimental impacts of diminished PF. Addressing these questions is crucial for guiding the specific exercise in individuals with impaired PF.

Therefore, this study aims to: (1) investigate the independent and joint association of PA amount and PF in relation to ACM; (2) investigate the association of PA categories and PF with ACM in community-based study cohort.

Methods

Patient and public involvement

This study was conducted on UK Biobank database;9 patients and public were not involved in the development of study design or recruitment of participants.

Study participants

Our analysis was based on data from the UK Biobank, a large-scale prospective cohort study that recruited 502 655 participants aged 38 to 73 years between March 2006 and July 2010. The UK Biobank obtained ethical approval from the Northwest Centre for Research Ethics Committee. Participants underwent comprehensive assessments that included physical examinations by trained staff, completion of touchscreen questionnaires and participation in a verbal interview.10

In this study, we included a total of 354 572 individuals after excluding participants who had withdrawn from the UK Biobank (n=242), those without valid pulmonary function data (n=49 388), those without valid exercise data (n=86 084) and those with missing covariate data exceeding 5% (n=12 369).

Assessment of PA

PA was quantified using the modified short-form International Physical Activity Questionnaire, which assessed the duration and frequency of PA. All data pertaining to PA were collected between March 2006 and October 2010. PA was quantified using the weekly total MET values, which were derived by multiplying the MET value of each activity by its frequency (times per week) and duration (minutes per session). This study primarily examined moderate-to-vigorous intensity physical activity (MVPA) and total exercise. MVPA encompasses both MPA and VPA, while total exercise is the aggregate of walking and MVPA. The PA pattern, including walking pattern, MPA pattern and VPA pattern, quantifies the proportion of a specific exercise type to total exercise. This index is calculated by dividing the METs of a given PA (eg, walking, MPA or VPA) by the METs of total exercise, which reflects the relative engagement in each type of PA. Individuals’ dominant PA patterns were then classified as walking-dominant, MPA-dominant or VPA-dominant based on which type of PA constituted the largest proportion of their total exercise. For instance, consider an individual with a total weekly exercise volume of 1000 MET-minutes, comprising 200 MET-minutes from walking (walking pattern: 20.0%), 200 MET-minutes from MPA (MPA pattern: 20.0%) and 400 MET-minutes from VPA (VPA pattern: 40.0%). This person’s PA would be classified as VPA-dominant pattern.

The following PA data were stratified into quintiles (Q1 to Q5) based on the number of participants. MVPA was categorised as follows: <160, 160–600, 601–1320, 1321–2640 and >2640 MET-minutes/week. Total exercise was categorised as: <667, 667–1377, 1378–2340, 2341–4212 and >4212 MET-minutes/week. Walking pattern was categorised into quintiles as well: <21.1%, 21.1%–35.4%, 35.5%–52.3%, 52.4%–78.7% and >78.7%. MPA pattern was classified into: <9.3%, 9.3%–22.6%, 22.7%–33.8%, 33.9%–50.3% and >50.3%. VPA pattern was categorised into: 0.0%, 0.1%–16.4%, 16.5%–31.0%, 31.1%–48.4% and >48.4%.

Assessment of PF

PF data were obtained by using a Vitalograph Pneumotrac 6800; the participants were asked to record two to three blows (lasting for at least 6 s) within a period of about 6 min. All participants completed the spirometry between March 2006 and October 2010. The Global Initiative for Chronic Obstructive Pulmonary Disease (GOLD) 2024 report1 classifies individuals with impaired PF into four categories based on the post-bronchodilator forced expiratory volume in 1 second (FEV1)/forced vital capacity (FVC) ratio and value of FEV1 (% reference): GOLD stage 1 (characterised by FEV1/FVC <0.7 and FEV1 ≥80% predicted), GOLD stage 2 (characterised by FEV1/FVC <0.7 and 50%≤FEV1<80% predicted), GOLD stage 3 (characterised by FEV1/FVC <0.7 and 30%≤FEV1<50% predicted) and GOLD stage 4 (characterised by FEV1/FVC <0.7 and FEV1 <30% predicted). Due to the limited number of participants in the GOLD stages 3 and 4 (<1% of the total sample), we categorised PF into four distinct groups: normal, mild impairment (GOLD stage 1), medium impairment (GOLD stage 2) and those with severe impairment (GOLD stages 3 and 4). The analysis used the highest recorded values of FEV1 and FVC from multiple PF tests conducted among the participants. The predicted FEV1 values were determined using the Global Lung Function Initiative global 2022 equations.11

Assessment of outcome

The primary outcome was ACM, ascertained according to the International Classification of Diseases edition 10. Participants were followed up through May 2024, with ACM obtained through linkage to Hospital Episode Statistics and the UK Biobank Cause of Death Registry.12

Covariates

Potential confounding factors encompassed sex, age, the Townsend Deprivation Index (a composite measure of material deprivation derived from four census data, namely unemployment, household overcrowding, non-car ownership and non-home ownership), Body Mass Index (BMI), education level, race, smoking status and alcohol consumption status. All analytical variables were derived from participants’ initial comprehensive assessment at enrolment. For missing data among covariates, median values were imputed for continuous variables, while mode values were used for categorical variables.13

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were presented as n (%) for categorical variables and mean (SD) for those with a normal distribution. Both the independent and joint association between PA and PF with ACM were analysed using Cox proportional hazards regression models. The impact of PA pattern on ACM among participants with impaired PF was also investigated. These models were mostly adjusted for sex, age, the Townsend Deprivation Index, BMI, education level, race, smoking status and alcohol consumption status.

A combined variable was constructed to explore the joint effects of PA and PF, dividing the sample into 20 groups, where the reference group was defined by normal PF and the highest levels of PA. The results were reported as HRs with their corresponding 95% CIs. Statistical significance was set at a p value of <0.05 for all analyses. The statistical software used was SPSS, version 29.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Independent association of PA and PF with risk of ACM

In figure 1a and online supplemental table S2, a gradual decrease of ACM risk was observed as PA levels increased from Q1 to Q3 in individuals with normal PF (Q3 vs Q1, HR=0.828, 95% CI: 0.788, 0.871) and mildly impaired PF (Q3 vs Q1, HR=0.768, 95% CI: 0.673, 0.875). Moreover, a negative dose-response relationship was observed between total exercise and ACM in individuals with medium PF impairment (Q5 vs Q1, HR=0.697, 95% CI: 0.612, 0.793) and severe PF impairment (Q5 vs Q1, HR=0.588, 95% CI: 0.449, 0.771). Similar results were noted in MVPA (figure 1b and online supplemental table S3).

Joint associations of PA and PF with risk of ACM

In the joint analysis of total exercise and PF, as shown in figure 3a and online supplemental table S6, a discernible trend emerged regarding the risk of ACM: compared with individuals with normal PF at the Q5 level of total exercise, the risk of ACM incrementally escalated as PA levels diminished and PF worsened. Individuals with severe PF impairment exhibited the highest increase in the risk of ACM, within a total exercise volume of less than 667 MET-minutes per week (HR=3.746, 95% CI: 3.218, 4.360). This trend, however, was not evident among individuals with normal PF at Q3 and Q4 levels of total exercise, nor among those with mild PF impairment at the Q3 level of total exercise. Similar results were obtained in the joint analysis of MVPA and PF (figure 3b and online supplemental table S7).

Discussion

This is the first community-based study to present a comprehensive analysis of relationship between PA, PF and ACM. Our research indicates that PA can significantly reduce the risk of ACM by up to 23.2%, 30.3% and 41.2% for those with mild, medium and severe PF impairment, respectively. Findings also indicate that decreased PF and reduced PA are linked to a heightened risk of ACM, except for those with only mild PF impairment who engaged in appropriate exercise (weekly total exercise of 1378–2340 MET-minutes). Furthermore, the proportion of walking activity exceeding 78.7% was associated with an increased ACM, while appropriate ratio of MPA (9.3 to 33.8%) and VPA (>0.0%) can result in a reduction of ACM risk. Subsequent analyses also find that the risk of ACM is significantly reduced in individuals with impaired PF within VPA-dominant pattern.

Previous research has demonstrated that PA offers a protective effect for patients with COPD, including symptom relief, improved quality of life and reduction of ACM.14 15 For example, a prospective cohort study involving 2386 individuals with COPD revealed that those with higher levels of PA exhibited a reduced risk of ACM compared with those with the lowest levels of PA (HR=0.76).16 Another prospective cohort study revealed that among patients with COPD, each 0.14 increase in PA level—measured using a multisensory armband and calculated by dividing the total daily energy expenditure by whole-night sleeping energy expenditure—was associated with reduced ACM (HR=0.46).17 However, most of the previous studies have been confined to patients diagnosed with COPD, while our study extends this scope by examining individuals with PF impairment within a community-based context.

Existing literature indicates that long-term engagement in aerobic and resistance exercise training confers health benefits to patients with COPD. However, these studies have not specified the precise amount of exercise necessary to confer health benefits in patients with COPD.1820 To address this issue, we undertook independent and joint analyses, which indicate that engaging in weekly total exercise with 1378 to 2340 MET-minutes does not lead to an increased risk of ACM despite a mild reduction in PF. This finding aligns with the recommended weekly PA levels for adults as outlined in the WHO 2020 guidelines on physical activity and sedentary behaviour,21 which suggest 150–300 min of MPA or 75–150 min of VPA, or an equivalent combination of MVPA per week, where the thresholds correspond to a total exercise of at least 450 to 1800 MET-minutes per week.

These advantages of PA for individuals with mild PF impairment are comparable to those observed in the general population, for instance, a cohort analysis has demonstrated that higher levels of PA were linked to greater gains in life expectancy in the general population, with a gain of 4.5 years at the highest level (1350+ MET minutes/week).22 These findings align with established literature demonstrating that PA confers significant health benefits, particularly through improvements in cardiorespiratory fitness and muscle strength, which can consequently improve disease prognosis across multiple conditions. This mechanistic relationship explains why prior studies report an inverse association between cardiorespiratory fitness, muscle strength (robust indicators of PA) and ACM.2329 However, this beneficial effect of PA is not observed among participants with medium and severe PF impairment, compared with individuals with normal PF, even when engaging in various levels of PA, the ACM risk of individuals with medium and severe PF impairment remains elevated. This lack of effect is hypothesised that individuals with such impairments frequently encounter difficulties in sustaining exercise due to lung hyperinflation.19 Several studies even found that ACM can increase in such individuals following a biweekly high-intensity rehabilitation programme.30 Consequently, Fiorentino et al suggest interval training as a suitable alternative for patients with a more severe disease and higher symptom burden of illness.4 In summary, the health benefits of PA may not suffice to counteract the detrimental effects of medium and severe PF impairment, but it can delay the increase in the risk of ACM. Our analyses, both independent and joint, indicate that among individuals with medium and severe PF impairment, the risk of ACM is minimally increased when total exercise exceeds 4212 MET-minute per week, compared with those with normal PF.

Previous research indicates that walking can diminish the risk of mortality in the general population.31 32 However, there is a dearth of studies examining the relationship between walking and the risk of ACM among individuals with PF impairment. Our study addresses this gap, yet we fail to observe the same protective effect observed in the general population. On the contrary, our research indicates that the risk of ACM escalates significantly when the ratio of walking to total exercise surpasses 78.7%, while when the ratio is less than 78.7%, no significant health impact is detected in individuals with impaired PF. These findings are congruent with a prior study by Vetrovsky et al,33 which reported that a simple lifestyle walking intervention did not improve cardiac function. Furthermore, even downhill walking, a form of exercise that combines endurance with aerobic training and typically demands less oxygen and ventilation than flat walking, did not result in significant symptom improvement in COPD patients.4 34 35

Unlike walking, in individuals with impaired PF, the ACM risk is significantly reduced when MPA constituted between 9.3% and 33.8% of total exercise, as well as when any amount of VPA was engaged in. Moreover, we also observe that individuals conducting VPA-dominant PA pattern have a significant reduction in risk of ACM. Consequently, we posit that walking may not represent an optimal strategy for preventing disease progression and improving long-term outcomes in individuals with impaired PF. Instead, we advocate for VPA as the principal form of exercise, complemented by MPA.

The protective effect of PA on individuals with PF impairment may stem from its capacity to enhance respiratory muscle function, ventilatory regulation and lung diffusion efficiency.3638 More importantly, PA may delay the decline in PF through improvements in muscle strength, body composition and fat distribution.3941 Such physiological adaptations caused by PA enable individuals with PF impairment to tolerate a higher magnitude of activity without limiting dyspnoea.30 42 These protective mechanisms of PA parallel the obesity paradox observed in patients with COPD. Specifically, individuals with COPD who exhibit higher fat-free mass and BMI demonstrate reduced ACM, which may be attributed to factors such as increased muscle mass and greater metabolic reserves.43 44 Therefore, among those with mild PF impairment, we even observed PA could counteract the elevated risk of ACM associated with declining PF. Nevertheless, in cases of medium and severe PF impairment and main engagement in LPA, the detrimental effects of impaired PF may exceed the mitigating capacity of PA, which could explain the absence of a counteracting effect from PA observed in individuals with medium and severe PF impairment and the lack of protective benefits from walking.

Strengths and limitations

A key strength of this study lies in its substantial sample size and long-term follow-up period. However, this study has several limitations. First, PA data were self-reported, and we used walking as a proxy for LPA due to the absence of LPA data in the UK Biobank, which may introduce inaccuracies. Second, the PF data used does not originate from post-bronchodilator inhalation tests, and the maximum rather than the optimal values were chosen for calculations, potentially affecting the differentiation of PF categories. Third, more than 90% of participants were white, suggesting that caution should be exercised when generalising our findings to other populations. Fourth, individuals with impaired PF are usually forced to reduce their PA levels because of symptoms like dyspnoea,45 which may introduce reverse causality within our study. However, both sensitivity analyses we conducted yielded results consistent with the primary findings, which mitigates such reverse causation concerns to a certain extent. Fifth, in theory, individuals classified as GOLD stages 3 and 4 may find it challenging to engage in VPA. However, a subset of these individuals can accumulate enough PA through multiple short-duration sessions. Consequently, our study finds that VPA may enhance health outcomes for this demographic. Nonetheless, caution is advised when recommending VPA to this group due to their specific health considerations.

Conclusion

In this study, there were significant independent and joint relationships of lower PA and PF impairment with the elevated risk of mortality. However, engaging in appropriate total exercise weekly can mitigate adverse effects associated with mild PF decline. Our findings underscore the potential of PA as an intervention target for individuals with PF impairment, which may inform future PA recommendations and pulmonary disease therapies. It is worth emphasising that the protective impact of PA on PF impairment could be overstated, particularly with respect to the efficacy of VPA on populations with GOLD stages 3 and 4, because the limited sample size and the deterioration in PF potentially impedes the capacity to exercise. Further research is warranted to ascertain the protective role of VPA in these groups.
