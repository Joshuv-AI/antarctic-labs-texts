The role of objective testing in urban adolescents with asthma symptoms in an African centre
Authors: REM Mphahlele, G Mosler, V Oyenuga, J Grigg, R Masekela
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUrban African adolescents suffer from undiagnosed and uncontrolled asthma symptoms. The purpose of this pilot study was to identify factors related to poorly controlled asthma symptoms and evaluate whether the objective measures suggested by the European Respiratory Society task force (ERS-TF) diagnostic algorithm for children assisted with asthma diagnosis.MethodsBetween July 2019 and November 2021, we conducted a cross-sectional cohort study of urban school-going adolescents between 12 and 14 years as part of the Achieving Control of Asthma in Children in Africa (ACACIA) project. The study comprised two stages; (a) screening for asthma symptoms and diagnosis and (b) asthma control test (ACT), pre and post-bronchodilator spirometry, and FeNO measurements in those who were screen-positive.ResultsOf 2093 adolescents screened, 180 were included, and 56% were female. Most participants had severe asthma symptoms (n = 128; 71%) and uncontrolled asthma (n = 157; 87%). Half (n = 90) had a previous asthma diagnosis, and were more likely to have uncontrolled asthma, p = 0.04. A significantly higher number of adolescents with uncontrolled asthma were exposed to traffic near their home (58% vs. 35%, p value < 0.01). Those who were exposed to kerosene lamps and insect sprays were more likely to have airway inflammation; p = 0.02 and p = 0.01; respectively. The prevalence of abnormal spirometry and bronchodilator response (BDR) was 12%. By performing the BDR test in those with normal spirometry, we increased the diagnostic yield of the ERS-TF algorithm by 50%.ConclusionIn this at-risk population, asthma symptoms are common, uncontrolled and related to environmental factors. In over half of those who undergo screening procedures such as spirometry, BDR and FeNO, the likelihood of a diagnosis remains low. Additional research involving non symptomatic control groups and longitudinal assessment is required to validate these results.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-026-07017-9.

Introduction

Asthma affects at least 269 million people globally [1] and, according to the Global Burden of Disease study, caused 461 000 deaths across the world in 2019 [2]. Undiagnosed and uncontrolled asthma poses a heavy financial and societal burden. There is a considerable impact on the family and the broader community due to missed school and work days, emergency care visits, doctor visits, hospital admissions and costs of medicines [1]. According to the current European Respiratory Guidelines for diagnosis of asthma in children, asthma diagnosis is based on demonstration of classical clinical features coupled with objective measures with spirometry or fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) [3].

Several validated tools are available for the assessment of asthma control. The Asthma Control Test (ACT) is a validated tool recommended by the National Institute of Health (NIH) and provides a reliable quantitative assessment of asthma control. Although asthma control studies in Africa are limited [4], surveys from South Africa show that asthma control has improved, but assessment differs between physician and patient [5, 6]. However, lung function, particularly FEV1, may provide additional information on degree of airway obstruction and expiratory airflow limitation [7, 8]. Studies have shown a weak relationship between ACT scores with FEV1 and no correlation with fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), indicating a lack of concordance between patient symptoms and perceptions or a delayed change in objective markers [5, 8].

FeNO has been shown to be increased in asthma and regarded as an indirect marker of eosinophilic airway inflammation [3, 9]. However FeNO is a complex metric which can be elevated or suppressed by multiple factors including atopy, viral infections, recent inhaled corticosteroid use, environmental exposures and dietary nitrate intake. FeNO should therefore be used in combination with other biomarkers to improve phenotypic resolution [3]. One of the asthma phenotypes is the eosinophilic phenotype, and systemic eosinophils and FeNO which have been shown to correlate are reliable markers to identify the phenotype [3, 10, 11]. A recent study found that the asthma phenotype in African children may be distinct from children in other parts of the world, with fewer children with an eosinophilic asthma phenotype [11]. A multicentre randomised controlled trial found that addition of FeNO in chronic asthma follow-up did not improve control [12]. Instead, it appears that the role of FeNO may be to rule in rather than rule out asthma [3]. The limited availability of studies addressing these diagnostic strategies, particularly in resource-constrained, high-burden settings, underscores the need for targeted research. This study aims to address these knowledge gaps and provide evidence that may inform further research, as well as clinical practice and public health policies for asthma management in African adolescents.

With this cross-sectional study, we aimed to identify factors related to poor asthma control in a cohort of urban adolescents, with as secondary objective being to evaluate whether the addition of objective markers assisted in the European Respiratory Society-supported diagnostic algorithm [3] for asthma diagnosis.

Methodology

Study design

This was a cross-sectional cohort study of urban school-going adolescents between the ages of 12 and 14 years with asthma symptoms, conducted as part of a multinational Sub-Saharan country collaboration: The Achieving Control of Asthma in Children in Africa (ACACIA) study [13]. This study comprised two stages; screening for asthma symptoms and core data collection, including completing a questionnaire developed for ACACIA (Breathing Survey) and objective measurements in those who were screen positive with pre- and post-bronchodilator spirometry, and exhaled breath nitric oxide (FeNO) measurements.

Setting

Participants were recruited in peri-urban and urban public schools in Ethekwini Metropolitan district, Durban, South Africa, between July 2019 and November 2021. Ethekwini is the third most populous city in South Africa, with a total population of 4,239,901 and a population density exceeding 1,000 individuals per square kilometre. Notably, 21% of the population comprises children aged 0 to 14 years [14]. Public schools in Ethekwini which still have a legacy of spatial segregation where historically Black African areas have nearly homogenous learner populations, while schools in formerly White/Indian suburbs may still reflect legacy demographics. Furthermore, schools are stratified according to a quintile system where quintile 1–3 (non-fee paying) are predominantly attended by Black African learners and quintile 4–5 are attended by Black African, White and Indian learners. This may lead to ethnic clustering that diverges from the general population [15].

Recruitment

Schools were randomly selected from the Department of Basic Education (DoBE) school database, and a cluster randomised approach was taken to select schools [16]. The identified schools were approached by telephone, email or visit. Grades with adolescents aged 12–14 were used to recruit participants.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Adolescents who had asthma symptoms in the last 12 months or asthma diagnosis according to Global Asthma Network (GAN) criteria were included [17]. These participants were identified using a screening survey (Breathing Survey) and recruited as part of the ACACIA study [13]. Only adolescents with informed consent from their parents/guardians and signed assent were included. Adolescents with contraindications, including infectious respiratory illness, were excluded from performing spirometry and fractional exhaled breath nitric oxide (FeNO). Those who opted not to use a bronchodilator did not perform a bronchodilator response test and were excluded as incomplete or missing data. Adolescents who exhibited abnormal findings consistent with asthma were provided with information and advised to seek evaluation at their nearest clinic.

Screening for asthma

Screening questions in the form of a “Breathing Survey” (BS) were developed from the validated GAN questionnaire using its demographic questions and questions 2 to 7 about breathing [17]. The Breathing Survey identified adolescents with ongoing symptoms or self-reported asthma diagnoses. Participants who answered “yes” to any of the following questions were defined as a positive screen: “Have you had wheezing or whistling in the chest in the past 12 months?” and “Have you ever had asthma?”

Core data collection

ACACIA questionnaire and Asthma Control Test (ACT)

The ACACIA online questionnaire is adapted from a version of a London survey of young people with asthma [18]. The questionnaire was available in digital (online and offline) and hardcopy format and filled in by participants with support from investigators. The questionnaire included questions on personal information, including ethnicity, gender, previous asthma diagnosis, medications, parental allergy history and comorbidities, i.e. eczema and rhinitis. The questionnaire included the National Institutes of Health (NIH) and clinically validated ACT [19].

Fractional exhaled nitric oxide

FeNO measurement was performed using the NIOX VERO following the American Thoracic Society and European Respiratory Society (ATS/ERS) standards [9]. Nitric oxide values were measured and expressed as parts per billion (ppb, equivalent to nanolitres per litre).

Spirometry

Trained field workers measured lung function using the NDD Easy on-PC following the ATS/ERS guidelines [20]. Weight and height were measured according to the GAN protocol using Safeway Digital Weight Scale and SECA 213 Stadiometer Portable Height Measurement [17]. Pre- and post-bronchodilator spirometry was performed using ATS/ERS guidelines [20].

Outcomes

Self-reported symptoms

Severe asthma symptoms

Severe asthma symptoms were defined according to GAN criteria [17]. A positive response to any of the following reported symptoms in the past 12 months: four or more attacks of wheezing, sleep disturbed due to wheezing one or more nights a week and wheezing severe enough to limit speech.

Asthma control

Asthma control was measured using the Asthma control test (ACT). An ACT score ≤ 19 indicated uncontrolled asthma [19].

Objective measures

Airway inflammation

A FeNO cutoff of ≥ 25 ppb was used to classify participants as having airway inflammation as per ATS/ERS guidelines for children [21].

Lung function

Abnormal spirometry was classified as a forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) or FEV1/forced vital capacity (FVC) less than the lower limit of normal (LLN) and/or < 80% predicted and/or FEV1/FVC z score ≤ -1,64. All participants’ spirometry results underwent severity grading based on the pre-bronchodilator FEV1 z-score. Bronchodilator response was defined as an increase of ≥ 12% and 200 mL from baseline in the highest FEV1. Spirometry parameters used for analysis included: FEV1 in litres, percent predicted and z-score. FVC, in litres, percent predicted and z-score. FEV1/FVC as a ratio and LLN, Forced Expiratory Flow (FEF) 25–75 in litres/second and percent predicted [22]. GLI2012 reference equation ‘Other’ was used for data analysis [23].

Exposures

Environmental exposures, including smoking, biomass fuel, chemical and traffic pollution exposure, and primary lighting and cooking sources, were assessed using a self-reported survey.

European Respiratory Society task force (ERS-TF) diagnostic algorithm outcomes

Asthma confirmed was defined as the presence of asthma symptoms—such as variable wheeze, cough, or breathlessness—and have objective evidence of variable airflow limitation demonstrated through positive bronchodilator reversibility on spirometry and elevated fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO),

Probable asthma was defined as the presence of asthma symptoms—such as variable wheeze, cough, or breathlessness, no observed variable airflow limitation but elevated fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO), According to ERS-TF diagnostic algorithm these cases often warrant a therapeutic trial with follow-up and repeat testing to clarify the diagnosis.

Asthma unlikely was defined as the presence of asthma symptoms without and objective tests to support the diagnosis—for example, normal spirometry without reversibility and low FeNO,

Statistical analysis

As this study was exploratory, no sample size calculation was done. Descriptive statistics (frequencies and percentages) were used to summarize categorical variables, and differences were determined using the chi-square test. Median and IQR were calculated for continuous variables, and a comparison of lung function between those with uncontrolled and severe asthma symptoms was made using the Mann-Whitney U test. Sensitivity and specificity analysis was used to assess the diagnostic performance of spirometry for the outcome asthma confirmed. Where appropriate, results with a p-value of ≤ 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Ethics

The study was reviewed by the Biomedical Research Ethics Committee (BREC) of the University of KwaZulu Natal BREC number: BF002/19, the DoBE KZN, and the Department of Health KZN.

Results

A total of 2093 participants completed the ‘breathing survey’; 114 participants were excluded due to age outside of 12–14; therefore, 1979 were included in the breathing survey (BS) analysis, Fig. 1. From 1979 included in the BS, 546 participants had asthma symptoms as defined by GAN or an asthma diagnosis and were eligible to undergo core data collection. As a result, the prevalence of asthma in this population was 27.6%. Of these (n = 564), 384 were excluded due to missing or incomplete data; therefore, 180 were included for core data analysis. The majority of losses were attributable to incomplete data capture at subsequent study stages; including screening assessments, incomplete questionnaire responses, or insufficient FeNO and spirometry data; which collectively precluded reliable outcome classification according to our predefined criteria. From the 180 included participants, n = 109 had reproducible and acceptable results and were used for spirometry analysis; Fig. 1.

Asthma diagnosis by symptoms

Of the 180 eligible participants, 90 (50%) had lifetime asthma and the majority (n = 128; 71.1%), had severe asthma symptoms. Of those with doctor-diagnosed asthma (n = 53; 29.4%), over half (52.8%) were taking medication for asthma. A tenth, (n = 20; 11.1%), reported previous treatment for tuberculosis (TB). The prevalence of eczema and rhinitis was 10.0% and 15.6%, respectively. More adolescents reported that their parents had asthma (22.8%) compared to other allergies (6.7%) and eczema (3.3%), Table 1.

ACT, FeNO and spirometry

According to the ACT score, the prevalence of uncontrolled asthma was high, (n = 157/180; 87.2%). Almost half had airway inflammation based on a FeNO ≥ 25ppb (n = 74/180; 41.1%), a relatively larger proportion of these had severe rather than non-severe asthma symptoms (45.3% vs. 30.8%), however this was not statistically significant, p = 0.07; Supplementary Table A. Few participants (n = 13/109; 11,9%) had abnormal spirometry and BDR (n = 13/109; 11.9%). Of the thirteen participants with bronchodilator responsiveness, twelve had uncontrolled asthma although this was not statistically significant using Fischer’s Exact test, p = 0.69. According to the ERS-TF diagnostic algorithm, 44.9% (n = 49/109) with spirometry results had either confirmed or probable asthma, Table 1.

Medications

Only 50 (27.8%) adolescents in the study reported using asthma medication for wheezing; the majority were adolescents with severe asthma symptoms (n = 41; 82.0%); Supplementary Table B. The most commonly used medications were short acting beta-agonists (SABA), 50% of the adolescents on asthma medication used salbutamol inhalers. ICS and combination treatment were less widely used; beclomethasone (6.0%) vs. fluticasone and salmeterol (4.0%), while cough syrup (4.0%) and prednisolone (6.0%) were similarly used. Few participants used spacers (n = 10; 20.0%). Commercial spacers were more commonly used than homemade spacers (16.0% vs. 4.0%). The use of medication was not different between those with controlled and uncontrolled asthma as well as severe and non-severe asthma symptoms; Supplementary Table B.

Spirometry

The mean weight and height ± SD of the 109 participants with acceptable spirometry were 51.5 kg ± 14.0 and 152.8 cm ± 7.9, respectively. The population median FEV1/FVC ratio, LLN and z score were 88.96, 78.80 and − 0.12 respectively. Median FEV1 (litres), 2.31 (2.02–2.60) was higher than median FEV1 LLN, 1.85 (1.68–1.98) with a FEV1 z score median of -0.07 (-0.71–0.65). However, those with airway inflammation had significantly lower FEV1/FVC ratio (86.6 vs. 89.9); p value < 0.05. Z-scores for FEV1 and FEV1/FVC were also lower in those with airway inflammation (-0.41 vs. 0.18 and − 0.45 vs. 0.01); respectively. Those with airway inflammation were more likely to have BDR (21.7% vs. 4.8%, p < 0.01), Table 2. There were no differences in spirometry measurements discriminating asthma control and severe symptoms, Supplementary Table C and D.

Diagnostic algorithm flow

Of the 180 who performed spirometry, 109 participants had reproducible results and were included in the analysis. Abnormal spirometry was found in only 13 adolescents (11.9%), Fig. 3. Of the 13 only 4 (30.7%) had a positive BDR. Four adolescents with abnormal spirometry had a negative BDR and a FeNO < 25 ppb; with 56 others with normal spirometry and FeNO < 25 ppb, therefore 60 (55.0%) were unlikely to have asthma. In addition to the 13 adolescents with BDR, five with a negative BDR had a FeNO ≥ 25 ppb, confirming asthma in 18 (16.5%) of adolescents. Asthma diagnosis by spirometry

The sensitivity for diagnosing asthma with spirometry using the LLN or a fixed cut-off for FEV1 and/or FEV1/FVC of < 80 was 50.0%, 95%CI (26.0–73.9) and specificity was 95.6% 95%CI (89.1–98.8). The positive likelihood ratio was 11.4 (3.9–32.9) and negative likelihood ratio 0.5 (0.3–0.8).

Discussion

In this at risk population, the proportion of adolescents with symptoms or a diagnosis of asthma was high (27.6%). Of these participants, 87.2% had poorly controlled asthma symptoms, and only 27.6% were on asthma medication. Uncontrolled asthma prevalence is comparable to that found in adults across Africa [24, 25]. While studies assessing asthma control in children and adolescents are limited, these show that those who are followed up have better control [4, 5, 7, 26, 27]. Limited access to asthma treatment is the primary, modifiable factor in this cohort. Improving availability and utilization of asthma therapies, particularly inhaled corticosteroids, is in line with current recommendations and is a central strategy to mitigate uncontrolled asthma. The prevalence of atopy in the form of eczema (10%) and hay fever (15.6%) is lower than an urban community study of asthmatic adolescents in Uganda where 21.3% had hay fever [28]. Adolescents who were exposed to environmental factors including traffic near home and chemical irritants like mosquito coils were more likely to have uncontrolled asthma. Lung function was significantly lower in those with airway inflammation and no different across asthma control and severity groups.

A significant proportion of adolescents in our study (71.1%) had severe asthma symptoms. The majority who reported taking medication for wheeze were those with severe asthma symptoms. Severe asthma symptoms are highly prevalent in children and adolescents in Sub-Saharan Africa and thought to be driven by urbanization, poor air quality and poverty [4, 29, 30]. The implications of asthma are most debilitating in those with severe asthma symptoms as the burden of costs for asthma care is higher and quality of life is poorer in this population [31]. In a study among asthmatic children across 3 LMIC centres (Brazil, Ecuador and Uganda), the proportion of severe asthma ranged from 45 to 66% [11]. In contrast, to what was previously thought, only about a third of asthmatics in the LMIC centres had eosinophilic asthma (EA) as measured by sputum eosinophils, however this phenotype was associated with severe asthma symptoms [11]. Based on FeNO, a little under half (41%) of our cohort had airway inflammation. While the majority had severe asthma symptoms, no relationship was found between airway inflammation and severe asthma symptoms, probably due to our small sample size. Notably, airway inflammation was more likely in those who used kerosene and insecticides. A comparable South African cohort residing in peri-urban informal settlements also found the majority with airway inflammation reported kerosene exposure. Kerosene, a common source of energy used for cooking and heating was significantly associated with rhinitis and asthma [32].

Overall, the adolescents in this study showed evidence of obstructive lung disease with a median FEV1/FVC LLN (78.8%). In comparison, a prospective population-based birth cohort of 630 adolescents with asthma symptoms in the United Kingdom had a slightly lower mean LLN (76%) [33]. Lung function interpretation has generally assumed a difference in lung function between people of African compared to European ancestry, and attributed this to chest size [23, 34]. However, FEV1/FVC and LLN remains independent of ethnic group [23] and remains a better choice for diagnosis of obstruction. Moreover, recent literature argues against using ethnic-specific equations as this may perpetuate health disparities and contribute to biased medical care [35]. No significant differences in lung function measures could be used to identify those with severe or uncontrolled asthma. However, multiple lung function parameters showed a significant difference between those with airway inflammation and no airway inflammation. FEV1 Z score, FEV1/FVC ratio and Z score were lower in participants with airway inflammation compared to those without. Although these differences were statistically significant, their clinical relevance is uncertain. Persistent inflammation has been associated with exacerbations and lung function decline, however, there is weak evidence that airway inflammation is related to airflow obstruction [36, 37]. Gao et al., showed that children with FeNO ≥ 35.5 ppb were more likely to have sputum eosinophilia and showed greater BDR [10]. Similarly, in our study those with airway inflammation were more likely to have a BDR (21% vs. 4%), p < 0.01. Noticeably and perhaps indicating its robustness, there were no differences in the LLN of any lung parameter between groups.

Studies using a fixed cut off FEV1/FVC < 80% [34, 35]; Grzelewski et al., and de Jong et al. to diagnose asthma in children showed high specificity (90–91%), and low sensitivity (12–46%) [38]. Using the FEV1/FVC below the LLN or < 80%, or an FEV1 < LLN or < 80% predicted showed low sensitivity (50.0%) and high specificity (95.6%) in our population. Low sensitivity and high specificity indicate that spirometry may not be useful as a screening test in this population, and is insufficient for detecting most cases. However, should abnormal spirometry be found in this population it is likely a true case. In this study 50% with abnormal spirometry had confirmed asthma on following through the ERS-TF algorithm.

A small number of adolescents had abnormal spirometry (11,9%). Performing BDR on all the study participants increased our diagnostic yield by 50%. Furthermore, baseline spirometry values were within the normal range for the majority (69%) of those with BDR. This in contrast with findings described by Coverstone et al., and Yancey et al., where baseline FEV1% predicted was inversely related to bronchodilator reversibility [39, 40]. However, a retrospective chart review of a total number of 8586 spirometry tests performed for asthma, Dufetell et al., showed 7750 (90.3%) had post-BDR documented and 5238 (67.6% with BDR) showed baseline spirometry values within the normal range [41]. It would be interesting to assess bronchodilator dose response in this cohort as done by Gruwell et al. where children and adolescents with poor bronchodilator responsiveness did not have increased current symptoms or impaired quality of life but were twice as likely to experience exacerbations or require ED visit and had a 3-fold increased odds of hospitalization by 12 months, independent of airflow obstruction [42]. Lastly addition of FeNO in the diagnostic pathway increased in almost a third of children being identified as ‘probable asthma’ who require further confirmatory testing with peak flow variability or challenge testing [3]. Taken together, these findings support the proposed ERS algorithm on the performance of BDR in symptomatic patients with normal spirometry as well as performing a combination of objective tests in confirming asthma in a population of at risk adolescents [3]. These findings are particularly relevant to asthma diagnosis in African children, given the ongoing barriers to spirometry access and the active pursuit of context-appropriate solutions [43].

Strengths and limitations.

Our study is one of a few studies using a combination of subjective and objective measures, including FeNO and spirometry, to examine asthma epidemiology in Southern Africa. As a pilot in an urban population, the sample size of the study was small and due to high attrition rates has implications for selective bias and generalizability, particularly for children residing in rural areas. The higher symptom burden in our population may largely reflect insufficient access to or use of controller medications rather than an inherently more severe asthma phenotype. The application of standard severity criteria—such as those from GAN—may overestimate intrinsic disease severity by conflating it with the effects of undertreatment. A cross-sectional approach, while valuable for capturing a snapshot of asthma phenotypes within our cohort, inherently limits causal inference and the ability to track changes over time. The outcomes—‘asthma confirmed,’ ‘asthma unlikely,’ and ‘asthma probable’ while not validated were operationalized based on available resources or rather lack thereof. Additional validation, such as longitudinal follow-up or external benchmarking, would strengthen the reliability and generalizability of our findings. Despite these constraints, our findings highlight the urgent need for targeted interventions to improve asthma management and underscore the importance of screening strategies that go beyond baseline spirometry alone.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complexity of asthma diagnostics and management in adolescents in Southern Africa, where environmental exposures, limited access to controller therapies, and diagnostic resource constraints converge. A multi-pronged diagnostic approach may not only improve identification of true asthma cases but also distinguish phenotypes, which have implications for treatment. Addressing the modifiable barrier of medication access, particularly inhaled corticosteroids, as well as environmental exposures is an urgent public health need.

Longitudinal research involving larger cohorts and asymptomatic control groups focusing on refining diagnostic strategies that contextually appropriate across diverse African populations are needed to validate the findings of this study and enhance clinical utility.
