Dynapenic Abdominal Obesity and the Risk of Non-Neoplastic Digestive System Diseases in Postmenopausal Women: A Prospective Cohort Study
Authors: Xuetao Dong, Yan Zhang, Chunxiao Zhang, Guodong Huang, Yandi Liu, Muran Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-neoplastic digestive system diseases (NNDSD) are highly prevalent in older adults and impose a substantial health burden, particularly among postmenopausal women. Dynapenic abdominal obesity (DAO), characterized by the coexistence of reduced muscle strength and abdominal obesity, has been associated with a range of chronic diseases. However, its association with incident NNDSD in postmenopausal women remains insufficiently explored. This study aimed to explore the prospective association between DAO and incident NNDSD in postmenopausal women.MethodsThis prospective cohort study included 2115 postmenopausal women drawn from five waves of the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study. Participants were classified into four mutually exclusive groups: non-dynapenic and non-abdominally obese (ND/NAO), abdominal obesity alone (ND/AO), dynapenia alone (D/NAO), and concurrent dynapenia and abdominal obesity (D/AO). Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were used to evaluate the association between DAO and incident NNDSD, defined based on self-reported physician diagnoses obtained through standardized questionnaires.ResultsOver a 9-year follow-up, 446 participants developed NNDSD. The incidence of NNDSD was highest among participants in the D/AO group. The D/AO group showed a significantly higher risk of NNDSD compared to the ND/NAO group (HR: 1.892, 95% CI: 1.256–2.849). Subgroup and sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these findings.ConclusionDAO is independently associated with an increased risk of NNDSD in postmenopausal women. Given the observational nature of this study, the findings should be interpreted with caution and do not imply causality. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to confirm these associations and to evaluate their clinical and preventive implications.Keywords: non-neoplastic digestive system diseases, dynapenic abdominal obesity, postmenopausal women, public health

Introduction

The global population is aging at an unprecedented pace, and this rapid demographic transition has led to a growing prevalence of age-related chronic diseases, among which digestive system disorders constitute a major global health burden.1 Epidemiological studies suggest that digestive system disorders account for more than one-third of all cases and nearly one-fifth of new diagnoses.2 Non-neoplastic digestive system diseases (NNDSD) represent a substantial and often underestimated health burden worldwide, encompassing a broad spectrum of functional and organic disorders such as gastroesophageal reflux disease, peptic ulcer disease, and inflammatory bowel disease.3,4 The global burden of NNDSD remains substantial. Although age-standardized mortality rates have declined over time, incidence rates have remained relatively stable Notably, NNDSD is estimated to account for approximately 70%–80% of total healthcare expenditures associated with digestive diseases.4 Emerging evidence suggests the existence of a pathogenic continuum linking specific NNDSD to the development of gastrointestinal malignancies.5,6 These conditions contribute substantially to the rising disease burden, impaired quality of life, and increasing healthcare utilization.7,8

Abdominal obesity, defined by the excessive accumulation of visceral adipose tissue, is a well-established contributor to systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and metabolic syndrome.9,10 Abdominal obesity has long been recognized as a key determinant of digestive health and is closely associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease, functional dyspepsia, and other digestive disorders.11–13 However, obesity rarely occurs in isolation in older adults. With advancing age, excess adiposity frequently coexists with progressive declines in muscle strength.14,15 Traditionally, sarcopenia has been defined primarily by a decline in skeletal muscle mass.16 However, accumulating evidence indicates that muscle function, specifically strength and power, collectively termed dynapenia, may represent a more sensitive and earlier predictor of functional decline, disability, and mortality than muscle mass alone.17,18 The coexistence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity delineates a distinct clinical phenotype, referred to as dynapenic abdominal obesity (DAO).19 This phenotype embodies a “double burden,” whereby obesity-related metabolic dysregulation amplifies the functional impairments and metabolic inefficiency associated with reduced muscle strength, thereby perpetuating a vicious cycle that predisposes individuals to a broad spectrum of adverse health outcomes.20,21 Compared with abdominal obesity or dynapenia alone, this combined condition may exert synergistic effects through amplified inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, and diminished physiological reserve.22,23 Although associations between DAO and adverse cardiometabolic outcomes, frailty progression, and all-cause mortality are increasingly recognized,19,24 its potential relationship with NNDSD remains insufficiently explored. Several biologically plausible pathophysiological mechanisms may underlie the association between DAO and NNDSD. Importantly, current epidemiological evidence directly linking DAO to digestive diseases remains limited, and the proposed mechanisms are primarily hypothesis-driven and extrapolated from studies on abdominal obesity and dynapenia separately. First, chronic low-grade systemic inflammation and oxidative stress associated with excess visceral adiposity may compromise gastrointestinal mucosal integrity and disrupt gut microbiota homeostasis.25–27 This proinflammatory milieu may be further exacerbated by reduced levels of muscle-derived anti-inflammatory myokines in individuals with dynapenia, suggesting a potential synergistic effect in DAO. Second, dynapenia involving the core and respiratory musculature may lead to diminished esophageal clearance, impaired gastrointestinal motility, and elevated intra-abdominal pressure,28–30 all of which are key contributors to the pathogenesis of NNDSD. Such neuromuscular impairments, when coexisting with abdominal obesity, may further exacerbate gastrointestinal dysfunction through both mechanical and functional pathways. Despite these biologically plausible hypotheses, no large-scale epidemiological studies have yet examined whether DAO functions as an integrated risk factor for NNDSD, particularly among postmenopausal women.

Alterations in body composition following menopause are dual-faceted. Postmenopausal women experience a marked shift in body composition driven by declining estrogen levels, characterized by pronounced visceral fat accumulation and accelerated loss of skeletal muscle mass.31–33 These changes not only predispose individuals to DAO but may also adversely affect gastrointestinal physiology through mechanisms such as increased intra-abdominal pressure, altered gut motility, and low-grade systemic inflammation.28–30 Consequently, postmenopausal women may be particularly susceptible to NNDSD.

Using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative cohort of middle-aged and older adults, we aimed to investigate the association between DAO and the risk of NNDSD among postmenopausal women. We hypothesized that DAO would be associated with a higher risk of NNDSD than abdominal obesity or dynapenia alone. By integrating measures of muscle strength and central adiposity, this study seeks to provide novel insights into body composition phenotypes relevant to digestive health and to inform more precise risk stratification and preventive strategies for postmenopausal women.

Methods

Study Population

Data for this cohort study were obtained from five waves of the CHARLS conducted between 2011 and 2020. CHARLS targets Chinese residents aged 45 years and older, designed to investigate population aging and monitoring longitudinal changes in health status among middle-aged and older adults. The present analysis used de-identified, publicly available CHARLS data, which had received prior ethical approval from the Biomedical Ethics Review Committee of Peking University (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided written informed consent. Following review by the Ethics Committee of the Tianjin Union Medical Center, this study was determined to meet the criteria outlined in the Ethical Review of Life Science and Medical Research Involving Human Subjects (effective February 18, 2023), Article 32, items (1) and (2). Detailed information on the CHARLS study is available at https://charls.pku.edu.cn/index.htm. Participants were classified as postmenopausal if they answered “yes” to the direct question: “Have you started menopause?” In accordance with previous studies,34,35 participants with unknown or missing data were excluded from the analyses. A total of 6,091 postmenopausal women were included at baseline. After exclusion of individuals with incomplete or missing data, the final analytical cohort comprised 2,115 participants (Figure 1).

Flow chart of participants selection.

Assessment of Dynapenia, Abdominal Obesity, and Dynapenic Abdominal Obesity

Handgrip strength (HGS) was measured by trained personnel using a calibrated, standardized dynamometer.36 Dynapenia was defined as HGS < 18 kg in women, consistent with the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) consensus.37 Abdominal obesity was defined as waist circumference (WC) ≥ 85 cm in women according to the 2024 Chinese Medical Association Guidelines for the Prevention and Control of Obesity.38 Participants were classified into four mutually exclusive groups based on the presence or absence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity: (1) no dynapenia and no abdominal obesity (ND/NAO), (2) abdominal obesity alone (ND/AO), (3) dynapenia alone (D/NAO), and (4) concurrent dynapenia and abdominal obesity (D/AO).

Definition of NNDSD

Consistent with previous studies,2,4 diagnoses of NNDSD was established based on physician-confirmed diagnoses reported by participants via structured survey interviews. Specifically, incident NNDSD was identified by the question: “Have you been diagnosed by a physician with a gastric or other non-neoplastic digestive system disease?” Given the scope of the CHARLS dataset, NNDSD was defined as a composite outcome encompassing a broad spectrum of non-malignant digestive conditions. This approach has been widely adopted in large-scale epidemiological studies to capture the overall burden of digestive morbidity when disease-specific diagnoses are not uniformly available. The follow-up period spanned 2011 to 2020, with a median duration of 9 years. The onset of NNDSD was defined as the midpoint between the follow-up wave in which the diagnosis was reported and the participant’s most recent preceding follow-up.35,39

Covariates

Covariates were selected a priori based on existing literature,2,4 clinical relevance, and their established or plausible associations with both the exposure and the outcome, including age, place of residence (urban or rural), educational attainment (less than high school versus high school or higher), marital status (married versus other), body mass index (BMI), alcohol consumption (current, former, or never), smoking status (current, former, or never), C-reactive protein (CRP), uric acid (UA), and history of hypertension and diabetes. BMI was included to account for overall adiposity independent of central fat distribution and was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters (kg/m2). Diabetes and hypertension were defined based on self-reported physician diagnosis or the use of corresponding medications.40

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or Kruskal–Wallis tests as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were presented as percentages and evaluated using the chi-square test. The analytical framework was guided by a conceptual causal model in which DAO was specified as the primary exposure and NNDSD as the outcome, with covariates selected as potential confounders. Multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed to assess the association between dynapenic abdominal obesity and incident endpoint event, with results expressed as hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Model 1 was unadjusted for covariates. Model 2 was adjusted for age, residence, educational attainment, and marital status. Model 3 additionally adjusted for alcohol consumption, smoking status, and BMI. The proportional hazards assumption was assessed using Schoenfeld residuals, and no violations were detected. Subgroup and interaction analyses were performed to examine potential effect modification by various key covariates, such as age, BMI drinking status, and smoking status. Potential multicollinearity among covariates was assessed, and no evidence of significant collinearity was identified (Table S1). To ensure the robustness of the results, three sensitivity analyses were conducted. Sensitivity analysis 1 was adjusted for Model 3 with additional adjustment for a history of hypertension and diabetes, both of which are common risk factors for multiple chronic diseases. Sensitivity analysis 2 was adjusted for Model 3 with additional adjustment for CRP and UA. CRP and UA are widely acknowledged surrogate indicates for systematic inflammation and oxidative stress levels, respectively.41,42 Sensitivity analysis 3 was conducted using a multiple imputation approach. Sensitivity analysis 4 was performed by excluding incident events occurring during the first follow-up wave to reduce the potential for reverse causation. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.5. Two-sided P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The proportions of participants in the ND/NAO, ND/AO, D/NAO, and D/AO groups were 41.32%, 49.60%, 4.30%, and 4.78%, respectively. Participants in the D/AO group were generally older, had higher levels of BMI, CRP, and UA, and exhibited higher prevalences of hypertension and diabetes than those in the ND/NAO group.

Associations Between Dynapenic Abdominal Obesity and New-Onset NNDSD

During the 9-year follow-up, a total of 446 participants developed NNDSD. Incidence rates of new-onset NNDSD, stratified by dynapenia and abdominal obesity status, are presented in Figure 2. The incidence of NNDSD was highest in the D/AO group compared with other groups. Table 2 and Figure 3 present the association between DAO and incident NNDSD. Compared with the reference group (ND/NAO), participants in the ND/AO (HR: 1.150, 95% CI: 0.908–1.455) and D/NAO (HR: 1.057, 95% CI: 0.661–1.689) groups exhibited higher risks of NNDSD, although these associations were not statistically significant after full adjustment. In contrast, participants in the D/AO group exhibited a significantly increased risk of NNDSD (HR: 1.892, 95% CI: 1.256–2.849). Subgroup and interaction analyses indicated that age, BMI, drinking status, and smoking status did not significantly modify the association between DAO and the risk of new-onset NNDSD (all P for interaction > 0.05; Figure 4). The robustness of the primary findings was further assessed through multiple sensitivity analyses. First, additional adjustment for hypertension and diabetes did not materially alter the association between DAO and incident NNDSD. Second, further adjustment for inflammatory and oxidative stress biomarkers yielded similar results. Third, analyses based on multiple imputation for missing data produced consistent estimates. Finally, exclusion of incident events occurring during the first follow-up wave did not materially change the results, with hazard ratios remaining consistent in both magnitude and statistical significance compared with the primary models. Across all sensitivity analyses, the association between DAO and the increased risk of NNDSD remained stable and statistically significant (Table 3 and Figure 5).

Discussion

In this large prospective cohort study of postmenopausal women, we found that the co-occurrence of dynapenia and abdominal obesity, a condition termed DAO, was robustly and independently associated with a significantly increased risk of incident NNDSD. Individuals with the DAO phenotype faced an approximately 89% higher risk of developing NNDSD than those with neither condition, even after comprehensive adjustment for a broad range of potential confounders, including sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, metabolic comorbidities, and biomarkers of inflammation and oxidative stress. Notably, neither abdominal obesity nor dynapenia alone conferred a risk increase comparable to that observed for DAO, underscoring the importance of considering composite body composition phenotypes rather than isolated components. Collectively, these findings extend current understanding of the relationship between body composition and digestive health.

Our study extends previous research linking obesity to digestive system disorders by highlighting the role of muscle strength. Abdominal obesity has been consistently associated with gastroesophageal reflux disease, dyspeptic symptoms, and other non-neoplastic gastrointestinal conditions via mechanisms involving visceral fat–driven inflammation, insulin resistance, and altered gut motility.11–13 However, these mechanisms may be further amplified in the presence of dynapenia. Results from our study indicated that DAO was robustly and independently associated with a markedly increased risk of incident NNDSD. Given the heterogeneous nature of NNDSD, several plausible but non-specific biological pathways may explain the observed association between DAO and NNDSD. First, excess visceral adiposity promotes a chronic proinflammatory state characterized by elevated circulating cytokines and oxidative stress, which may compromise gastrointestinal mucosal integrity and disrupt gut barrier function.25–27 Second, dynapenia may adversely affect the function of skeletal muscles involved in swallowing, respiration, and trunk stabilization. Weakness of these muscle groups has been linked to impaired esophageal motility, delayed gastric emptying, and abnormal intestinal peristalsis, all recognized contributors to non-neoplastic digestive pathology.28–30 Third, the coexistence of abdominal obesity and dynapenia may reflect more advanced metabolic dysfunction, modulating insulin resistance and altered myokine secretion, further influencing gut microbiota composition and host–microbe interactions.15,23,43,44 These proposed mechanisms are biologically plausible but should be interpreted with caution, as causal relationships cannot be established given the observational design of this study. Accordingly, the mechanistic interpretations presented here should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Notably, the definition of NNDSD in this study was based on a single self-reported physician diagnosis, encompassing a heterogeneous group of conditions, including both functional and organic gastrointestinal disorders. This broad classification may mask disease-specific associations and introduce heterogeneity in the underlying pathophysiological mechanisms. Accordingly, the biological pathways proposed should be interpreted with caution, as different digestive diseases may involve distinct or only partially overlapping mechanisms. The use of a composite outcome facilitates the capture of the overall burden of digestive morbidity in large-scale epidemiological settings. Future research are warranted to disentangle the associations between DAO and specific digestive disease entities to better elucidate disease-specific mechanisms and clinical implications.

It is important to emphasize that our findings should be interpreted within the unique endocrine context of postmenopause. The pronounced postmenopausal decline in estrogen represents a key mechanistic link, promoting both ectopic fat deposition in visceral abdominal depots and the accelerated loss of skeletal muscle mass and function, thereby establishing a physiological milieu conducive to the development of DAO and its downstream metabolic and functional consequences.31,45,46 Therefore, DAO can be considered a tangible clinical manifestation of the adverse body composition changes characteristic of the menopausal transition, rendering it a highly relevant risk marker for this population.

From a clinical and public health perspective, these findings carry important implications. Current clinical practice often relies on body mass index (BMI), which does not distinguish between fat and muscle mass and may misclassify individuals with normal weight but elevated body fat and reduced muscle strength as low risk.47,48 Reliance on BMI alone may underestimate the risk of digestive system disorders in older women with preserved body weight but reduced muscle strength and increased central adiposity. Our findings suggest that simple, feasible measures such as HGS and WC may have potential value in improving risk stratification for digestive system disorders among postmenopausal women. However, given the observational nature of this study and the use of a composite outcome, these findings should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to validate these associations and clarify their clinical utility.

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, NNDSD was ascertained using self-reported physician diagnoses, which may be susceptible to recall bias and misclassification. However, previous validation studies support the reliability of these self-reported events, thereby reinforcing their credibility.49,50 Second, information on specific digestive disease subtypes was unavailable, precluding analyses of disease-specific associations. The observed associations should be interpreted as reflecting overall susceptibility to digestive disorders rather than specific disease entities. Third, although extensive covariate adjustment and sensitivity analyses were conducted, residual confounding cannot be entirely excluded. Fourth, a substantial proportion of participants were excluded due to missing data, which may introduce selection bias. Finally, as this study was conducted predominantly in a Chinese population, the generalizability of the findings warrants validation in other ethnic and cultural contexts.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that DAO was independently associated with a significantly increased risk of NNDSD. Simple assessments of HGS and WC may help identify individuals at increased risk. Given the reliance on self-reported outcomes and the potential for residual confounding, the findings should be interpreted with caution. Further prospective and interventional studies are warranted to confirm these associations and to evaluate their clinical and preventive implications.
