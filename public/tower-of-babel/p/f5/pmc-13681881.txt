Electrical impedance tomography in neonatal respiratory diseases: a clinical review of applications and evidence
Authors: Yifan Wang, Yaping Song, Caiju Xing, Feng Fu, Xun Jiang, Yan Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neonatal respiratory diseases remain a leading cause of morbidity and mortality in intensive care settings, with management complicated by the limitations of current monitoring tools. Chest radiography provides only static snapshots and cannot capture the dynamic evolution of lung aeration, while lung ultrasound, though portable, remains operator-dependent and incapable of quantifying regional ventilation. Electrical Impedance Tomography (EIT) has emerged as a noninvasive, radiation free imaging modality that addresses these gaps by enabling real time, continuous visualization of regional lung function at the bedside. This review synthesises evidence from studies published between 2011 and 2026, examining how EIT informs the management of neonatal respiratory distress syndrome, acute respiratory distress syndrome, bronchopulmonary dysplasia, pneumothorax, and the transitions inherent to mechanical ventilation. Across these disease contexts, EIT reveals regional ventilation heterogeneity invisible to conventional monitors, provides immediate physiological feedback during therapeutic interventions, and enables early detection of complications before clinical deterioration. We identify key barriers to clinical adoption, including technical limitations, lack of standardised protocols, and the need for outcome driven trials, and propose a research agenda to accelerate translation. By reframing EIT not merely as an imaging tool but as a dynamic decision support system integrated into clinical workflows, we aim to provide clinicians with a framework for leveraging this technology to improve outcomes in the most vulnerable patients.

Background

The transition from placental to pulmonary gas exchange at birth represents one of the most profound physiological adaptations in human life [1], a process that is particularly precarious in preterm and critically ill neonates whose structurally immature lungs are uniquely vulnerable to injury. Despite advances in respiratory support over recent decades, bronchopulmonary dysplasia continues to affect up to 40% of extremely preterm infants [2, 3], and life-threatening complications such as pneumothorax remain common, with delayed diagnosis contributing to adverse outcomes [4]. These persistent challenges reflect a fundamental limitation in how we monitor respiratory function: conventional tools provide only global, static snapshots that cannot capture the dynamic, regional nature of lung pathophysiology.

Chest radiography, the most widely available imaging modality, offers a single moment in time and cannot visualise the evolution of aeration during the respiratory cycle [5]. Moreover, cumulative radiation exposure poses particular risks to neonates, whose developing tissues are highly radiosensitive [6]. Lung ultrasound provides bedside portability but suffers from operator dependence and cannot quantify regional ventilation distribution [7]. Ventilator derived measurements such as tidal volume and respiratory system compliance reflect global lung function and may mask clinically significant regional heterogeneity that drives ventilator induced lung injury [8]. As a result, the absence of suitable bedside monitoring tools leaves several critical clinical questions unanswered. Effective management of neonatal respiratory support requires specific knowledge: the distribution of ventilation between dependent and non-dependent lung regions; the balance between recruitment and overdistension following adjustments in positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) or continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP); the maintenance of end-expiratory lung volume during procedures such as surfactant administration, endotracheal suctioning, apnoea, and extubation; the improvement in regional ventilation homogeneity after an intervention; and the earliest detectable signs of complications such as pneumothorax before clinical deterioration. These information needs are particularly urgent during non-invasive respiratory support, where interface leaks frequently render ventilator-derived tidal volume, minute ventilation and breathing pattern unreliable.

Electrical impedance tomography (EIT) has emerged as a technology uniquely positioned to address these gaps. By measuring thoracic impedance changes during ventilation [9], EIT generates real time, tomographic images of regional lung function at the bedside, without radiation [10]. The past decade has witnessed exponential growth in EIT research, with studies progressively moving from feasibility demonstrations to clinical applications that directly inform patient management. This review systematically summarizes the existing evidence on the use of EIT in neonatal respiratory diseases. The patient populations involved mainly include preterm infants (gestational age > 22 weeks) and term infants with respiratory complications. Respiratory support settings vary across studies and mainly include conventional mechanical ventilation, high-frequency oscillatory ventilation, and non-invasive respiratory support, such as continuous positive airway pressure, bilevel positive airway pressure, and high-flow nasal cannula oxygen therapy. In these clinical settings, EIT is primarily used to assess regional ventilation distribution, changes in end-expiratory lung impedance, ventilation homogeneity, regional lung recruitment and derecruitment, overdistension and collapse during PEEP titration, and dynamic lung volume changes during surfactant therapy or extubation. Relevant clinical endpoints include successful extubation without reintubation within 48–72 h, early detection of pneumothorax before physiological deterioration, and the need for supplemental oxygen at 36 weeks corrected gestational age.

This review does not aim to provide a comprehensive catalogue of all neonatal EIT studies. Rather, it focuses on clarifying how EIT-derived parameters provide physiological evidence for clinical judgment, assist bedside monitoring, and support individualized respiratory management. We then further discuss the key barriers limiting the widespread application of EIT and propose future research directions to promote the translation of this promising technology into routine neonatal intensive care practice.

Principles and parameters for clinical practice

Technical principles in clinical context

A basic understanding of EIT principles is essential for clinicians seeking to integrate this technology into practice. During EIT examination, a small alternating current is applied through electrodes placed around the chest, and the resulting voltages are measured to reconstruct cross sectional images of impedance change [8]. Because air is a poor electrical conductor while fluid and tissue conduct more readily, lung impedance increases during inspiration as alveolar air content rises and falls during exhalation [11]. Each pixel in the resulting image represents the local tidal volume, with temporal resolution of 20 to 50 frames per second enabling breath by breath analysis [12]. EIT for absolute or semi-quantitative lung volume estimation relies on patient-specific calibration (based on tidal volume or other reference measurements), but calibration coefficients are affected by electrode placement, chest geometry, lung disease and breathing mode, and exhibit significant inter-individual variability.

Operational considerations for neonates

Successful EIT application in neonates requires attention to several practical details. Skin preparation with warm saline removes vernix and amniotic fluid to optimise electrode contact. In neonatal pulmonary EIT, 16 electrodes are typically placed circumferentially around the chest at the nipple line (Fig. 1). This standardized placement ensures that measured impedance changes reliably reflect fluctuations in end-expiratory lung volume (EELV) and tidal volume (TV) [13]. Clinically, the smallest chest circumference currently monitored in newborns is approximately 240 mm (To enable monitoring in even smaller infants, we are developing miniaturized electrode pads and have filed a patent application). Key parameters and their clinical interpretation

In clinical practice, regional division methods are commonly employed for the quantitative analysis of EIT images to assess local pulmonary ventilation function. Frequently used division approaches include the four-quadrant method and the four-stratification approaches (Fig. 2). If the region of interest (ROI) is divided along the ventral-dorsal axis into ROI1-ROI4, gravitational effects result in physiological differences in ventilation distribution between these zones, thereby influencing both local and global lung ventilation homogeneity [14]. Key indices derived from EIT include the Global Inhomogeneity Index (GI) [15], Center of Ventilation (CoV), change in end expiratory lung impedance (ΔEELI) [16], and tidal impedance variation (TIV), silent spaces (SS), median oscillation of recruited region (MOR) [17], the inspiratory time to expiratory time ratio (Ti/Te) and regional time constant (TCreg). These parameters translate raw impedance data into clinically actionable information. Clinical applications in neonatal respiratory diseases

Optimizing surfactant therapy in respiratory distress syndrome

Exogenous surfactant replacement remains the cornerstone of neonatal respiratory distress syndrome management [18], yet clinicians have historically lacked real time feedback on its physiological effects. The invisibility of lung volume changes during administration has meant that post surfactant ventilator adjustments remain largely empirical, guided by global oxygenation rather than regional recruitment patterns.

EIT has transformed this landscape by enabling continuous monitoring of regional lung volume changes during and after surfactant administration [19]. In a seminal study of 15 preterm infants receiving high frequency oscillatory ventilation, Miedema et al. [20] observed that surfactant rapidly increased lung volume and compliance, with preferential recruitment of dependent lung regions. This finding carries direct clinical implications: the gravity dependent increase in dorsal lung aeration suggests that positive end expiratory pressure should be reduced promptly after surfactant to prevent overdistension of nondependent zones that were already well aerated before administration. Subsequent work has confirmed that surfactant administration alters regional ventilation distribution, promoting more uniform air distribution across the lungs. Furthermore, the value of EIT becomes particularly evident during less invasive surfactant administration, a technique increasingly used to avoid the hemodynamic instability associated with intubation. Gonçalves Ferri et al. [21] reported a case of an extremely preterm infant monitored with EIT during less-invasive surfactant application (LISA), revealing pathophysiology invisible to conventional monitoring. Before surfactant administration, they observed rapid post inspiratory lung volume decline and pendelluft, a phenomenon in which gas moves from nondependent to dependent zones during inspiration without contributing to tidal volume, indicating severe regional heterogeneity. After surfactant administration, spontaneous sigh breaths triggered progressive lung recruitment, eliminated pendelluft, and stabilized tidal volume. This case illustrates that EIT can document not only the fact of therapeutic response but also its underlying mechanism, providing insights that could inform individualised post treatment ventilator strategies. Significant changes in EIT parameters reflected an overall improvement in lung function. Chatziioannidis et al. [22] utilized EIT to monitor lung function changes before and after exogenous pulmonary surfactant (PS) administration in 17 preterm infants. They found that PS administration could alter regional ventilation distribution, promoting more uniform air distribution.

Beyond immediate physiological monitoring, EIT may also contribute to noninvasive tidal volume estimation in infants with uncuffed endotracheal tubes, where conventional ventilator measurements may be inaccurate. Kim et al. [23] validated in an animal model that surfactant treatment enhances the correlation between EIT derived and ventilator measured tidal volumes, supporting the feasibility of this approach. Together, these studies establish EIT as a valuable tool for optimizing surfactant therapy, enabling real time assessment of regional recruitment that can guidepost treatment ventilator adjustments and potentially reduce the risk of volutrauma.

Individualizing positive end expiratory pressure in acute respiratory distress syndrome

Positive end expiratory pressure titration in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) involves a fundamental tradeoff that has challenged clinicians for decades. Insufficient PEEP allows cyclic alveolar collapse and atelectrauma, while excessive PEEP causes overdistension and dead space ventilation. Conventional PEEP selection based on oxygenation or global respiratory system compliance may not optimise this balance, particularly when lung injury is heterogeneous and the effects of PEEP vary across regions.

EIT addresses this challenge by enabling visualization of regional responses to PEEP changes. During a decremental PEEP trial (Fig. 3), regional compliance changes measured by EIT are used to calculate the percentage of alveolar overdistension (OD%) and collapse (CL%) across the lung at each PEEP level. Costa et al. [24] established a bedside EIT-guided approach to quantify regional lung collapse and local overdistension in adults with ARDS, in which the intersection of alveolar overdistension and collapse curves derived from EIT defines the optimal PEEP level that minimizes both conditions, thereby providing a physiological framework for individualized PEEP titration. More recently, Soltész et al. [25] extended this approach to 6 neonates and 20 children with ARDS, demonstrating its feasibility and safety in both populations. Notably, the EIT derived optimal PEEP was consistently lower than clinically set values, suggesting that conventional practice may systematically overinflate the lungs. This finding raises the possibility that EIT guided PEEP titration could reduce ventilator induced lung injury, though outcome studies are needed to confirm this hypothesis. An important question is whether EIT adds value beyond global measures of respiratory mechanics. Ren et al. [26] addressed this by comparing PEEP selected by global respiratory system compliance with that selected by EIT regional compliance in children with moderate to severe ARDS. While the two methods yielded clinically equivalent PEEP levels, EIT revealed spatial heterogeneity, as quantified by the GI and CoV. This finding suggests that EIT does not replace global measures but rather complements them by providing information about regional distribution that global measures cannot capture. For the clinician, this means that EIT guided PEEP titration may be particularly valuable when lung injury is suspected to be heterogeneous, such as in focal ARDS or when conventional PEEP selection yields ambiguous results. From prediction to personalized support in bronchopulmonary dysplasia

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia represents the most common morbidity of extreme preterm birth [27], yet its prediction remains imprecise, and its management lacks objective guidance. The pathology of BPD is characterized by regional heterogeneity [28], with areas of overdistension adjacent to atelectasis and fibrosis, yet conventional monitoring provides only global measures that cannot capture this complexity [29].

Early studies explored whether EIT could enhance BPD prediction by quantifying ventilation distribution in the first days of life. Gaertner et al. [30] prospectively monitored 32 extremely preterm infants and found that a lower percentage of ventilated lung volume and a higher ventilation homogeneity ratio predicted the need for supplemental oxygen at 28 days. However, these EIT parameters did not outperform clinical variables such as gestational age and birth weight in predicting moderate to severe BPD at 36 weeks, and they failed to predict early intubation within 24 h of birth. This negative finding is important because it suggests that early EIT measures may reflect current physiological status but cannot capture the complex, multifactorial trajectory to chronic lung disease that involves inflammation, nutrition, and genetic factors beyond initial lung mechanics. The implication for clinical practice is that EIT should not be used as a standalone predictor of BPD risk but may complement existing clinical assessments.

For infants with established severe BPD, the role of EIT shifts from prediction to guiding ongoing respiratory support. Shui et al. [31] evaluated EIT guided PEEP titration in 4 infants with severe BPD, successfully identifying sustainable PEEP levels in 4 of 5 assessments and reducing PEEP by up to 4 cmH₂O without adverse events. This proof-of-concept study suggests that even in chronically injured lungs, EIT can detect regional overdistension and guide interventions to minimize it.

Perhaps most importantly, EIT may enable characterisation of individual disease phenotypes in severe BPD. Enzer et al. [32] recently reported the use of EIT to monitor an infant with severe BPD during long term respiratory support, documenting regional ventilation heterogeneity and ventilation perfusion mismatch that persisted despite stable global measures. This case illustrates that infants with similar global physiology may have markedly different regional disease patterns, potentially requiring different management strategies. Therefore, by characterizing regional ventilation heterogeneity and ventilation–perfusion mismatch, EIT may provide physiological correlates of this endpoint and serve as a complementary assessment tool for BPD severity. While this concept remains exploratory, it points toward a future in which EIT enables truly personalized respiratory support based on individual disease phenotype rather than population averages.

Early detection and real time diagnosis of pneumothorax

Pneumothorax remains a life-threatening emergency in neonatal intensive care [33], with diagnosis often delayed because clinical signs are nonspecific and radiographic confirmation requires transport or portable imaging. To clarify the clinical burden of this issue, Bhatia et al. [34] delineated the incidence and high-risk predictors of pneumothorax among very preterm infants, providing fundamental baseline clinical references for investigations into EIT-based early pneumothorax detection. Each hour of delayed treatment increases the risk of tension physiology and may prolong respiratory compromise, yet the tools for early detection have remained limited.

The physical principles underlying EIT make it uniquely suited for pneumothorax detection. Air in the pleural space dramatically increases local impedance, creating a static region of low ventilation that contrasts with the dynamic impedance changes of surrounding aerated lung tissue throughout the respiratory cycle [8]. This characteristic pattern enables real time identification of pneumothorax at the bedside. Kallio et al. [35] reported continuous EIT monitoring in a preterm infant who developed right sided tension pneumothorax during Neurally adjusted ventilatory assist (NAVA), documenting a marked and instantaneous rise in end expiratory lung impedance on the affected side accompanied by heterogeneous ventilation changes. Critically, EIT detected these changes in real time, enabling immediate clinical response before physiological deterioration.

Even more importantly, EIT may detect pneumothorax before clinical deterioration occurs. Miedema et al. [36] monitored 6 preterm lambs and found that all spontaneous pneumothoraxes occurred in nondependent lung zones. A distinct phase angle delay between nondependent and dependent lungs appeared 30 and 15 min before the event, localising to the affected quadrant one minute prior to pneumothorax occurrence. This pattern suggests that EIT could provide early warning, enabling preemptive intervention before tension physiology develops. In a surfactant depleted piglet model, Bhatia et al. [37] confirmed that EIT can accurately detect and localize small pneumothoraces before physiological deterioration occurs, and can also monitor their resolution during treatment.

For the clinician, these findings have direct implications. Continuous EIT monitoring in infants at high risk for pneumothorax, such as those receiving high pressure ventilation or those with evolving air leak syndromes, could enable earlier detection and intervention. The characteristic impedance pattern is readily identifiable, and evolving phase angle delays may identify infants requiring increased vigilance. While prospective studies are needed to confirm that EIT guided management improves outcomes, the physiological rationale and animal data are compelling.

Guiding mechanical ventilation and critical transitions

Decisions about when to extubate and how to transition between ventilation modes remain among the most challenging in neonatal intensive care. Extubation failure rates of 20 to 30% in preterm infants highlight the limitations of current assessment tools [38, 39], which rely on global stability rather than regional lung function. Similarly, transitions from invasive to noninvasive support or between noninvasive modes are managed empirically, without real time feedback on their physiological consequences. EIT has begun to illuminate these transitions by revealing regional changes invisible to conventional monitors. Virsilas et al. [40] compared invasive versus noninvasive respiratory support in 72 preterm infants and found that while invasive support provided higher end expiratory lung volume and lower heterogeneity, spontaneous breathing better engaged dependent lung regions. This tradeoff between stability and physiological engagement may inform mode selection for individual patients. For an infant with severe lung disease, the stability of invasive support may outweigh the benefits of dependent lung engagement; for an infant weaning from ventilation, promoting dependent lung ventilation may accelerate recovery.

During mode transitions, EIT reveals which infants tolerate changes well and which experience lung volume loss requiring more gradual weaning. Rub et al. [41] monitored 15 preterm infants transitioning from continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) to high flow nasal cannula and found that the transition did not consistently reduce end expiratory lung volume, challenging conventional assumptions that high flow necessarily provides less support. However, Buchler et al. [42] confirmed that some infants do experience volume loss during this transition, suggesting that EIT monitoring could identify those requiring more gradual weaning or alternative modes. Extubation represents the most critical transition from invasive to noninvasive support. Wisse et al. [43] monitored 24 extremely preterm infants before and after extubation, finding that silent spaces decreased in nondependent zones but increased in dependent zones after extubation. Critically, the reintubation group exhibited higher post extubation lung inhomogeneity, suggesting that the Global Inhomogeneity Index may predict extubation failure. Plastina et al. [44] found that despite lung volume loss during extubation, clinical stability before extubation facilitated partial recovery, supporting the value of optimizing pre extubation status. Bhatia et al. [45] dynamically monitored 30 neonates throughout extubation from synchronized intermittent positive pressure ventilation to nasal CPAP, demonstrating that EIT clearly depicts lung volume loss and compensatory increases in dorsal lung ventilation during this period. Even in the delivery room, EIT is providing new insights into respiratory transitions. Belting et al. [46] monitored 73 term and late preterm infants immediately after cesarean delivery and found that the inspiratory to expiratory time ratio, oxygen saturation, and heart rate within the first 10 min predicted respiratory failure. Early CPAP rapidly improved end expiratory lung volume, and nasopharyngeal tube insertion in nonresponsive extremely preterm infants significantly increased end expiratory lung impedance, confirming effective rescue. These findings suggest that EIT could guide delivery room interventions by providing immediate feedback on their effectiveness.

Emerging applications in special populations

Beyond the common conditions discussed above, EIT is finding applications in several specialized populations that illustrate its versatility. In congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH), Douglas et al. [47] monitored 15 neonates preoperatively, postoperatively, and before discharge, finding that ventilation remained predominantly in the contralateral lung throughout with ipsilateral ventilation increasing from 35% preoperatively to 42% at discharge. EIT thus documents the natural history of regional lung function in CDH and could guide decisions about surgical timing and postoperative support. Ventilation perfusion matching, a key determinant of gas exchange efficiency, has traditionally required complex imaging modalities unsuitable for bedside use. Tingay et al. [48] used EIT to identify unilateral ventilation and perfusion defects in a neonate with pulmonary artery thrombosis, demonstrating the potential for combined ventilation perfusion EIT to assess gas exchange at the bedside. While still investigational, the lung pulsatility method has been validated for the qualitative assessment of pulmonary perfusion, providing crucial reference for related research [49]. Using EIT for ventilation/perfusion (V/Q) assessment remains an emerging research application (Fig. 4), not an established routine clinical tool in neonates. Our preliminary (unpublished) case studies show that there is a potential association between EIT-derived indicators and arterial blood gas parameters. These findings indicate that evaluating these indicators may help stratify treatment strategies, including ventilator titration, hemodynamic optimization and anticoagulation considerations, and depicts a promising frontier for precision critical care. However, compared with the relatively mature EIT ventilation monitoring, EIT-based V/Q assessment is more complex in methodology and currently relies on technical approaches such as cardiac cycle-related impedance changes or exogenous contrast agents. Therefore, EIT for V/Q assessment cannot yet be regarded as a routine clinical diagnostic tool, and its diagnostic accuracy, calibration requirements, repeatability and impact on clinical outcomes still need further verification in the neonatal population. In apnea of prematurity, Miedema et al. [50] monitored 15 preterm infants during apneic episodes and found significant decreases in end expiratory lung impedance followed by compensatory post apnea tidal volume increases that restored lung volume. This compensatory mechanism may explain why most apneic episodes are well tolerated, but its failure could identify infants at risk for desaturation. Finally, Janulionis et al. Barriers to clinical adoption and a path forward

Despite the compelling evidence summarized above, EIT has not yet achieved widespread adoption in neonatal intensive care. Understanding the barriers to translation is essential for developing strategies to overcome them.

Technical limitations remain the most fundamental. Current electrode belts, designed primarily for older children and adults, may not optimally fit extremely low birth weight infants whose chest circumferences fall below commercial specifications. Miniaturized, flexible electrodes with improved biocompatibility are needed to accommodate the smallest patients while maintaining signal quality. Neonatal motion and ventilator induced chest wall movement create artifact that can obscure physiological signals, requiring advanced signal processing algorithms to distinguish true impedance changes from noise. Image reconstruction is also influenced by the assumed thoracic geometry. Simplified cylindrical or adult-derived chest models may introduce reconstruction errors in neonates because of their small size, compliant chest wall and age-dependent thoracic shape; therefore, neonatal-specific thoracic models and age-appropriate finite-element meshes should be incorporated when available. Most current devices use time-difference imaging, which shows changes from baseline but cannot directly measure absolute lung volume. Absolute or semi-quantitative lung volume estimation therefore requires validated algorithms and patient-specific calibration, and the validity of calibration coefficients should be considered when measurements are compared across patients, devices or clinical conditions.

Clinical barriers are equally important and perhaps more challenging to overcome. No universally accepted protocols exist for EIT data acquisition, parameter calculation, or interpretation. EIT interpretation requires understanding of both the technology and lung pathophysiology, yet this expertise is not yet integrated into fellowship training or continuing medical education. Adding EIT monitoring to already complex NICU workflows require careful consideration of how data will be displayed, who will interpret it, and how it will influence decision making. Most critically, the majority of studies to date have focused on physiological endpoints rather than patient important outcomes. Whether EIT guided management reduces bronchopulmonary dysplasia, shortens ventilation duration, or improves neurodevelopmental outcomes remains unknown, for randomised controlled trials have not yet been conducted.

Conclusion

Electrical impedance tomography addresses limitations of conventional monitoring that have persisted for decades, providing dynamic, regional, real-time information that responds directly to three core clinical challenges: the invisibility of regional ventilation heterogeneity, the lack of immediate feedback during respiratory support adjustments, and the delayed recognition of complications. Across the spectrum of neonatal respiratory disease, EIT reveals regional surfactant effects that guide post treatment ventilator adjustments in respiratory distress syndrome, enables PEEP titration that balances collapse against overdistension in acute respiratory distress syndrome, detects regional heterogeneity enabling targeted interventions in bronchopulmonary dysplasia, offers both real time diagnosis and potential early warning for pneumothorax, and identifies infants likely to fail extubation during weaning. Yet these capabilities have not translated into widespread adoption, for technical refinement of electrodes and algorithms, standardisation of protocols and training, and rigorous outcome studies remain necessary precursors to routine use. Emerging directions including artificial intelligence enhanced image reconstruction, multifrequency EIT for tissue characterisation, and perfusion imaging for ventilation perfusion assessment promise to further expand clinical utility, moving the field toward individualised, physiology driven management guided by real time visualization of regional lung function.
