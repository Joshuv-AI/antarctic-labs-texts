Effect of Tonsillar Retractor on Intracranial and Intraocular Pressure in Children Undergoing Adenotonsillectomy Surgeries
Authors: Ayse Bozkurt Oflaz, Meltem Isık, Merih Onal, Mete Kaan Bozkurt, Ozkan Onal, Banu Bozkurt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe tonsillar retractor used during adenotonsillectomy, one of the most common pediatric surgeries, may increase sympathetic activity. This study aimed to evaluate the effect of tonsillar retractor insertion on intracranial pressure (ICP) and intraocular pressure (IOP) in children undergoing adenotonsillectomy.MethodsThis prospective observational study included 24 pediatric patients undergoing adenotonsillectomy and 20 age‐matched controls undergoing other surgeries. Bilateral optic nerve sheath diameters (ONSD) were measured by ultrasonography, and IOP was assessed by tonometry before and immediately after tonsillar retractor insertion. Hemodynamic parameters were also recorded at the same time points and compared between groups. Pressure changes were analyzed using the Generalized Estimating Equation method, and effect sizes were calculated with Cohen's d.ResultsInsertion of the tonsillar retractor caused a significant increase in mean ONSD from 4.66 ± 0.58 mm to 5.61 ± 0.52 mm (p < 0.001, d = 2.36). Mean IOP rose significantly from 13.7 ± 2.8 mmHg to 18.2 ± 5.3 mmHg (p < 0.001, d = 0.97). Systolic blood pressure increased from 100.4 ± 10.1 mmHg to 108.5 ± 10.8 mmHg (p = 0.0008), whereas diastolic pressure changes were not significant (p = 0.097). No significant correlation was found between changes in ONSD and IOP (r = −0.22, p = 0.30).ConclusionTonsillar retractor use during adenotonsillectomy results in a rapid, marked rise in ICP and IOP. These findings should be considered by otolaryngologists and anesthesiologists managing pediatric patients at risk of sympathetic surges.Level of Evidence2.Keywords: adenotonsillectomy, children, complication, intraocular pressure, optic nerve sheath diameter, tonsillar retractor

Introduction

Adenotonsillectomy remains the most prevalent surgical procedure performed on pediatric patients globally [1]. The methodology surrounding adenotonsillectomy has seen limited evolution since the foundational research conducted by Crowe and his colleagues in the early 20th century. The Crowe‐Davis tonsil retractor was developed to facilitate mouth opening and tongue depression, thereby enhancing the efficacy of surgical interventions. Subsequently, Boyle modified this instrument to be compatible with tracheal tubes, leading to the introduction of the Boyle‐Davis mouth gag, also known as the tonsillar retractor [2]. The Boyle‐Davis tonsillar retractor is equipped with a mechanism that provides the surgeon with an unobstructed view of the oropharynx during surgical procedures, while also ensuring the mouth remains open, and it features a groove intended to accommodate the endotracheal intubation tube securely. Nevertheless, the application of this device is not without inherent risks.

The use of a tonsillar retractor during pediatric adenotonsillectomy elicits a substantial sympathetic response, akin to that observed during laryngoscopy and intubation. This reflex may result in elevated intracranial pressure (ICP) [3]. While the existing literature presents limited data on alterations in intraocular pressure (IOP) attributable to the use of a tonsillar retractor, it is acknowledged that IOP varies in pediatric patients who have undergone tracheal intubation and supraglottic airway device insertion [4, 5].

The impact of the tonsillar retractor on IOP and its potential relationship with ICP remains unclear. This study seeks to bridge the knowledge gap by examining how the tonsillar retractor during pediatric adenotonsillectomy surgeries affects the optic nerve sheath diameter (ONSD)—a marker of ICP—as well as IOP and hemodynamic parameters.

The primary outcome of this research is the effect of sympathetic activation induced by the application of a tonsillar retractor on IOP and ICP in pediatric patients undergoing adenotonsillectomy surgeries.

Our primary hypothesis is that the use of a tonsillar retractor elevates IOP and ICP via sympathetic activation.

Materials and Methods

This prospective, observational, single‐center study was meticulously designed and conducted in collaboration with the Anesthesiology, Ophthalmology, and Otorhinolaryngology clinics of a university hospital. Approval from the Ethics Committee of the same university hospital was granted on May 21, 2024, under decision number 2024/266. Informed consent was obtained from the parents of all pediatric patients participating in the study. The principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki were rigorously adhered to throughout the study.

Participating patients were aged 2 to 17 years and underwent elective surgical procedures at the otolaryngology clinic between June 2024 and January 2025. The study population was categorized into two distinct groups: the study group and the control group. The study group consisted of patients scheduled to undergo adenotonsillectomy and who would use tonsillar retractors during the procedure (Figure 1). The control group comprised patients of the same age range who would undergo elective surgeries requiring general anesthesia, excluding adenotonsillectomy surgery.

The superior view (a) and the lateral view (b) of the head of the patient with a tonsillar retractor.

The study involved patients with a best‐corrected visual acuity of 20/20 in both eyes and no ocular disease. Subjects were excluded if they had an IOP exceeding 21 mmHg during the preoperative eye exam, had cataract, glaucoma, or retinal issues, or exhibited a high refractive error greater than 3 D, including myopia, astigmatism, or hyperopia. Additionally, those with orbital trauma or a history of orbital or ocular surgery were also excluded.

Measurements

Demographic data, anesthesia details, and surgical information for all patients were documented.

Intraocular Pressure (IOP)

The IOP was measured with a non‐contact, portable tonometer by the same experienced ophthalmologist, who had previously performed at least 20 measurements (Figure 2a). The average of two IOP readings per eye was used for analysis.

Intraocular pressure measured with Tono‐Pen (a), optic nerve sheath diameter evaluated by ultrasonography (b).

In the study group, the first IOP measurement was taken immediately after endotracheal intubation but before the placement of the tonsillar retractor. The second measurement was taken immediately after tonsillar retractor placement, before the initiation of adenotonsillectomy. In the control group, the initial IOP measurement was performed immediately after endotracheal intubation, before the surgical procedure. The second measurement was obtained under deep anesthesia at the end of the surgical procedure, before extubation. The timing of measurements in the control group was chosen to reflect a comparable deep‐anesthesia state without oropharyngeal stimulation.

Optic Nerve Sheath Diameter (ONSD)

The ONSD measurements were obtained using transorbital ultrasonography (USG) by the same experienced anesthesiologist, who had previously performed at least 20 measurements (Figure 2b). The mean of two measurements per eye was used for analysis.

In the study group, the initial measurement was taken immediately after endotracheal intubation but before the placement of the tonsillar retractor. The subsequent measurement was recorded after the tonsillar retractor was positioned, but before the commencement of adenotonsillectomy. In the control group, the initial measurement was conducted immediately following endotracheal intubation. The second measurement was performed after the surgical procedure under deep anesthesia, before extubation.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using SPSS v21.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The normality of the distribution for continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Repeated‐measures ANOVA was used for normally distributed data, and Friedman's test was used for non‐normally distributed data. Bonferroni‐corrected post hoc analyses were performed when significant.

The significance level was 5% (α = 0.05) and the statistical power at 95% (1‐β = 0.95). The study included two eyes per individual, and a dependent data structure was used due to interocular measurement correlation. Therefore, the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) method was employed in the statistical analyses to control for interocular correlation and maintain the validity of the analyses at the individual level. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 44 patients participated in the study. The study group included 24 patients, and the control group included 20. The overall mean age was 6.89 ± 3.33 years; the control group's mean age was 7.21 ± 3.13 years, and the study group's was 6.5 ± 3.59 years. In the study group, 13 patients (54.2%) were male, whereas 11 (65%) were male in the control group. The average height was 119.42 ± 22.91 cm for the study group and 124.85 ± 19.89 cm for the control group. The mean weight was 29.5 ± 17.35 kg in the study group and 24.90 ± 13.89 kg in the control group (see Table 1).

All patients, except for two in the study group and three in the control group, were classified as ASA 1 (see Table 1). One patient in the study group had asthma, another had thalassemia, while in the control group, one patient had a thyroid nodule, another had an atrial septal defect, and a different patient had a hypoplastic kidney. In the study group, 10 patients underwent adenoidectomy, 9 underwent tonsillectomy, and 5 underwent adenotonsillectomy. Meanwhile, patients in the control group underwent septoplasty (n = 10) or tympanoplasty (n = 10), which are otorhinolaryngological procedures other than adenotonsillectomy.

When comparing hemodynamic parameters between the two groups, no significant differences were found at baseline (see Table 1). The average heart rate before insertion of the tonsillar retractor was 105.38 ± 16.23 bpm in the study group, compared with 111.95 ± 17.94 bpm in the control group before surgery. Blood pressure measurements also showed no significant differences: the study group's systolic blood pressure was 100.42 ± 10.10 mmHg and diastolic was 56.71 ± 13.02 mmHg, while the control group's systolic was 94.5 ± 10.6 mmHg and diastolic was 51.30 ± 9.18 mmHg.

There was no significant difference between the ONSD and IOP values measured prior to any surgical procedure following endotracheal intubation in both groups (see Table 1). The initial IOP measurements before the placement of the tonsillar retractor in the study group averaged 13.70 ± 2.77 mmHg, whereas the mean IOP value before the surgical procedure in the control group was 14.48 ± 4.94 mmHg (p = 0.5352). The mean ONSD value before the placement of the tonsillar retractor was 4.66 ± 0.58 mm in the study group, while the ONSD value before the surgical procedure was 4.29 ± 0.63 mm in the control group (p = 0.0510).

In the study group, the mean systolic blood pressure increased significantly from 100.42 ± 10.10 mmHg to 108.5 ± 10.81 mmHg following the insertion of the tonsillar retractor (p = 0.0008, Cohen's d = 0.785). Concurrently, the mean diastolic blood pressure increased from 56.7 ± 13.02 mmHg at baseline to 62.04 ± 12.71 mmHg (p = 0.0973, d = 0.353). Additionally, the heart rate rose from 105.38 ± 16.23 bpm to 105.75 ± 21.14 bpm after retractor insertion (p = 0.9039, d = 0.025).

The IOP values demonstrated a clinically and statistically significant increase from 13.70 ± 2.77 mmHg to 18.17 ± 5.29 mmHg after the insertion (p < 0.001, d = 0.969). Similarly, the ONSD values showed a statistically and clinically significant rise from 4.66 ± 0.58 mm to 5.61 ± 0.52 mm (p < 0.001, d = 2.363, Table 2).

The correlations between the variables measured after tonsillar retractor insertion were examined using correlation analysis. No significant relationship was identified between the alterations in IOP and changes in ONSD (r = −0.220, p = 0.3021). Likewise, no statistically significant correlation was observed between the difference in systolic blood pressure and IOP (r = 0.093, p = 0.6647), nor between the difference in systolic blood pressure and ONSD (r = 0.185, p = 0.3859).

Discussion

Tonsillectomy and adenotonsillectomy are among the most frequently performed surgical procedures worldwide in pediatric patients. The utilization of tonsillar retractors has become a standard practice to enhance the surgeon's visualization during these procedures [6]. During the application of a tonsillar retractor, a reflex sympathetic response is elicited, resembling but more sustained than that observed during endotracheal intubation. This reflex sympathetic response may result in elevations in IOP and ONSD. Both IOP and ONSD are reliable parameters that serve as sensitive indicators of alterations in intracranial pressure (ICP) [7, 8, 9, 10].

Although existing research has examined the effects of tonsillar retractors on ONSD or IOP independently, their concurrent assessment within the same patient cohort remains limited [11]. This study investigated the impact of tonsillar retractors on ONSD and IOP in pediatric patients undergoing adenotonsillectomy, examining their correlation and capacity to reflect ICP.

Our investigation revealed a 33% increase in mean IOP and a 20% increase in ONSD following the placement of a tonsillar retractor. These findings align with those of studies by Altıparmak et al. [3] and Karali et al. [12], which demonstrated that the use of tonsillar retractors during adenotonsillectomy significantly elevates ONSD. Furthermore, noteworthy increases in ONSD values were observed during suspension laryngoscopy when employing a suspension spoon in place of a tonsillar retractor. This outcome indicates that the ONSD is a parameter responsive to sympathetic activation during various upper airway procedures [13]. The overarching conclusion from these studies is that increases in ONSD values occur even with brief upper‐airway manipulations and serve as a reliable surrogate marker of ICP [13].

In our study, in addition to ONSD values, which are regarded as subjective due to potential variability among clinicians performing the measurements, we also observed an increase in IOP values measured with a tonometer, which are considered more objective. In a randomized controlled trial comparing I‐Gel placement with tracheal intubation in pediatric strabismus surgery, Deepu Antony et al. [5] demonstrated that the intubation procedure significantly elevated IOP values. This finding indicates that upper airway manipulations substantially influence IOP in pediatric patients, suggesting that the IOP increase observed following tonsillar retractor placement in our study may have been attributable to this physiological mechanism.

The underlying pathophysiology of increased IOP and ICP resulting from the use of a tonsillar retractor can be explained by stimulation of the oropharynx, a region characterized by a rich neural network and sensitive receptors that can evoke sympathetic reflexes [14]. When the tonsillar retractor is placed in the oral cavity, the epiglottis, base of the tongue, and jaw muscles are stretched, triggering sympathetic discharge and subsequent catecholamine release [3]. Research shows that hypertension induced by the sympathetic nervous system can elevate intraocular venous pressure, which in turn increases IOP and choroidal blood volume, thereby increasing ICP [15, 16, 17]. Consequently, the simultaneous elevation of IOP and ICP means that the orbital and cranial compartments are simultaneously affected. High ICP reduces cerebral perfusion pressure, reducing blood flow to the brain, and prolonged elevated ICP levels can cause neurological damage [4]. Although physiological mechanisms can tolerate short‐term increases in ICP in healthy children, irreversible consequences may occur in pediatric patients with risk factors such as cleft palate repair, obstructive sleep apnea, or intraoral tumor resection, who will be exposed to sympathetic stimulation due to the need for long‐term tonsillar retractor placement [18, 19].

Nevertheless, our correlation analyses demonstrated that the increases in IOP and ONSD observed after tonsillar retractor placement were not directly associated with systemic hemodynamic parameters. Notably, the lack of a significant correlation between changes in IOP and ONSD suggests that these measurements reflect distinct physiological mechanisms underlying intracranial pressure dynamics. This interpretation is further supported by magnetic resonance imaging studies in healthy adults, which similarly reported no significant relationship between ONSD and IOP, suggesting that ONSD is predominantly influenced by intracranial rather than intraocular factors [20]. Likewise, in a study involving patients in the prone position during lumbar disc surgery, both ONSD and IOP increased significantly, yet no significant correlation between them was observed [11]. These findings indicate that ONSD mainly indicates changes in subarachnoid pressure, whereas IOP is influenced by physiological processes such as ocular venous return and choroidal volume. Accordingly, ONSD values are reliable for assessing ICP‐related changes, independently of IOP. The absence of a significant correlation between IOP, ONSD, and systolic blood pressure in our study supports the notion that local regulatory mechanisms controlling ocular and cerebral perfusion function independently of systemic pressure. In conclusion, although current research confirms that ONSD values are reliable for assessing IOP in children, our findings indicate that relying solely on systemic measurements may not accurately predict intracranial or ocular pressure changes. ONSD, as an ultrasound‐based indicator, can be influenced by operator experience, whereas IOP measurement uses objective methods. This supports the idea that combining both provides a comprehensive view of ICP dynamics. This result could offer valuable insights for the clinical monitoring of pediatric patients, especially those at risk for neurological complications. Unlike previous studies in the literature which examined ONSD or IOP separately, our study makes a unique contribution by simultaneously assessing both indicators within the same patient group.

Although our study has notable strengths, it is essential to recognize some limitations. The study represents a single‐center experience with a relatively small sample size and primarily included ASA I pediatric patients, which may limit the generalizability of our findings, particularly to higher‐risk pediatric groups. Additionally, the average duration of use of the tonsillar retractor was 20 min, preventing definitive conclusions about pressure changes during surgeries in which the retractor might be used for longer periods. Another limitation is that the duration of surgical procedures in the control group may have been longer than that of adenotonsillectomy, which could have influenced ONSD and IOP measurements due to prolonged exposure to anesthesia and surgical stress. Methodologically, ONSD measurements were performed using USG, which can vary among operators [21, 22, 23]. Although IOP was measured objectively by tonometry, rapid fluctuations might have been missed because measurements were taken only at specific times. It is important to note that the pressure‐lowering effects of anesthetic agents influence intraoperative IOP values [24]. Measurements obtained under anesthesia might not accurately reflect IOP levels in awake children; specifically, baseline IOP values recorded after induction could underestimate the accurate baseline IOP, leading to an overestimation of post‐retractor increases [24, 25, 26]. However, this limitation arises from the challenge of conducting such measurements in awake pediatric patients. Nonetheless, using post‐induction values as a reference can be regarded as an internal control. However, as all measurements were performed under identical anesthetic conditions, intra‐individual comparisons remain valid.

Additionally, the long‐term effects of intraoperative changes on postoperative neurological or ophthalmological functions were not assessed. In children, cognitive functions were assessed only through clinical observation, with no standardized neuropsychological tests. Lastly, despite all patients following a standard anesthesia protocol, the influence of individual pharmacodynamic differences on hemodynamic responses cannot be ruled out.

Conclusion

This study showed that the tonsillar retractor used in pediatric adenotonsillectomy is not merely a mechanical device that improves surgical visibility, but also acts as a stimulus that significantly increases IOP and surrogate markers of intracranial pressure, namely ONSD. Although these rises continued during retractor use, they were clinically manageable in healthy ASA I children. However, the possible risks of these temporary increases should not be ignored in pediatric patients with underlying neurological or systemic health issues or during surgeries involving prolonged retractor use. Therefore, anesthesiologists and surgeons should be cognizant of these physiological responses, endeavor to minimize the duration of retractor deployment, and implement meticulous hemodynamic and neurological monitoring strategies to ensure safe surgical procedures and preserve cognitive integrity in pediatric patients.
