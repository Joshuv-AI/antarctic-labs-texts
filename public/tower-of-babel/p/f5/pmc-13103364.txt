A crystal graph to vector approach for predicting magnetic properties
Authors: Sandeep Singh, Abhishek Sharma, Arti Kashyap
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe introduce CG-Vec, a crystal graph-to-vector framework that replaces iterative message passing with compact, interpretable descriptors coupled to conventional machine learning. Across diverse datasets, CG-Vec matches the accuracy of deep graph networks on large datasets but substantially outperforms them in data-scarce regimes. The advantage is most pronounced for magnetic properties, where existing approaches have struggled: CG-Vec delivers reliable predictions of magnetization in both ferromagnetic and ferrimagnetic systems and of Curie temperature. Beyond magnetism, CG-Vec performs competitively for formation energy and band gap, demonstrating broad applicability. These results establish vectorized representations as a practical and scalable alternative to deep architectures, enabling efficient and interpretable modeling of complex material properties.Subject terms: Materials science, Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

Magnetization is a fundamental property of materials that plays an important role in data storage, energy systems, spintronics, microelectronics, aerospace applications, and quantum computing and in driving innovation across multiple high-tech industries1–4. Magnetism originates from the intrinsic spin and orbital magnetic moments of electrons5. The net magnetic moment of an atom depends on the number and configuration of unpaired electrons, which are determined by the Pauli exclusion principle and Hund’s rule6,7. The alignment of individual magnetic moments is governed by interactions between neighboring atoms primarily through exchange interactions but with additional contributions from spin-orbit coupling and local crystal fields8,9. These interactions operate on various length scales and and are highly sensitive to bonding geometry and structural distortions. As a result, predicting magnetic behavior in complex materials remains a significant challenge both theoretically, computationally as well as experimentally10–13.

Given the complexity of magnetic interactions, the discovery of new magnetic materials has historically relied on experimental synthesis and characterization. Further, the experiments are often guided by chemical intuition and incremental exploration of known compounds14,15. In recent years, density functional theory (DFT) based computational methods have enabled prediction of material stability and properties before experimental synthesis16,17. Spin-polarized DFT serve as the primary tool to model various crystal structures, evaluate their thermodynamic stability and predict key magnetic properties such as local moments, exchange interactions, and magnetic anisotropies18,19. To improve accuracy, methods such as DFT+U, hybrid functionals, and spin-dynamics simulations are used, but they require much higher computational cost20–22. Based on these predictions, a few promising materials are chosen and synthesized using standard experimental techniques23–26. Their structure and magnetic properties are then measured to test the theory. This process is repeated because experiments often differ from calculations due to defects and temperature effects27,28. While this method has led to important discoveries, it is slow and costly, which has driven the adoption of high-throughput computing and machine-learning–based screening29,30.

Achieving rapid and reliable discovery of magnetic materials through machine learning requires: (i) physically meaningful descriptors that capture the electronic and structural features governing magnetism and (ii) machine-learning models capable of mapping these descriptors onto accurate predictions of magnetic properties31,32. Together, these approaches allow large chemical spaces to be screened quickly and at much lower cost than traditional first-principles methods. The models can predict stable, high-magnetization materials before synthesis and help experiments to be focused on the most promising candidates33–38.

Graph-based deep learning models have become a widely used tools in materials informatics by representing crystals and molecules as networks of atoms and bonds39. A key example is the Crystal Graph Convolutional Neural Network (CGCNN)40, which predicts material properties directly from atomic connections without using hand-made features.Building on this foundation, several derivative architectures were developed to address specific limitations. For example, iCGCNN41 introduced edge updates and incorporated three-body interactions via Voronoi neighbors to better capture multi-body correlations42. OGCNN43 extended the framework by adding orbital–orbital interactions and spherical harmonics, enabling improved treatment of angular dependencies in systems with strongly correlated d- and f-electrons. MEGNet44 further advanced the paradigm by introducing global state vectors (e.g., temperature, pressure), thereby unifying crystalline and molecular predictions and extending applicability to multi-fidelity datasets. Subsequent developments further diversified the design space. GATGNN45 applied multi-head graph attention and global pooling to identify critical atomic interactions, improving predictions of phonon spectra, dielectric constants, and electronic density of states. ALIGNN46 redefined bonds as nodes in a line graph to explicitly encode bond angles and dihedral geometries, achieving higher accuracy for properties such as piezoelectricity and thermoelectricity. BonDNet47 integrated atom, bond and global-level features to predict bond dissociation energies and chemical reactivity. Many architectures are implemented within the Materials Graph Library (MatGL)48 and have further extended these capabilities. M3GNet49 incorporates three-body interactions to capture angular dependencies, while CHGNet50 introduces a magnetic moment-informed message passing to model systems where spin states strongly influence material properties. TensorNet51 and SO(3)Net52 adopt rotationally equivariant frameworks, enabling accurate prediction of tensorial quantities such as forces, stresses, and phonon spectra. Despite these advances, current GNN-based approaches face important limitations. Achieving high predictive accuracy often requires large, diverse datasets, which are scarce for niche material classes such as correlated magnets or metastable phases53. Models trained on narrowly defined datasets can struggle to generalize. Furthermore, training and inference for large systems remain computationally demanding. These challenges demand a continuous need for next-generation models that balance accuracy with data efficiency and computational scalability54. In addition to data and scalability constraints, most existing GNN architectures are fundamentally limited by their locality bias55, making them inadequate for collective phenomena such as magnetism, Curie and Néel temperatures. These properties depend on long-range exchange interactions and temperature-dependent spin ordering, which are often absent from conventional GNN formulations56,57. Even with recent advances in global attention and equivariant message passing, performance gaps remain for quantum mechanical properties53. These gaps motivate the development of approaches that explicitly incorporate long-range physics while retaining the efficiency of graph-based learning54,58.

Magnetic materials are difficult to model because many datasets are small and incomplete, as magnetic DFT calculations are costly and often do not converge easily. Large neural networks tend to overfit in this setting. A compact representation is useful because it reduces the number of model parameters and makes training more stable when data is limited. It also allows us to see which atomic and structural features influence the prediction, which helps us understand the magnetic behavior of the material.

To overcome the limitations of conventional GNNs-particularly their locality bias, computational overhead, and limited interpretability-we propose decoupling the crystal graph representation from deep message-passing architectures. Instead of end-to-end propagation, our framework transforms crystal structures into fixed-length (CG-Vec), interpretable vector descriptors through explicit feature construction. Each node is assigned physically meaningful attributes, such as atomic number, mass, electron affinity, and theoretical spin moment, enabling a representation that directly encodes the chemical and magnetic characteristics most relevant to long-range behavior58,59. Electronic-structure effects are captured through a compact orbital-field-matrix (OFM)34,61 inspired descriptor, which encodes the number of unpaired valence-shell electrons in a reduced 32-dimensional one-hot representation. This provides a physically grounded measure of magnetic contributions while remaining computationally efficient. These features are statistically aggregated over the crystal graph (via mean and variance) to yield a fixed-length, interpretable vector that captures both local chemical environments and global compositional trends. The resulting representation preserves multi-scale information relevant to short-range bonding and long-range interaction, while remaining computationally efficient and physically interpretable40,46. By integrating these descriptors into a single vector representation, our CG-Vec framework enables a smooth use with classical machine learning algorithms such as gradient boosting and random forests62,63. This design avoids the computational overhead of deep GNN training, performs effectively in low data regimes, and provides transparent structure–property relationships. We demonstrate the utility of CG-Vec for predicting magnetization per unit volume along with formation energy, band gap, and Curie temperature64–66. In the following, we describe our methodology, including data curation, graph construction, feature engineering, and model training.

Methodology

This section presents the schematic of the proposed framework (Fig. 1), which integrates data preparation, graph construction, feature engineering, vectorization, and evaluation metrics. Each component is designed to produce descriptors that are both physically interpretable and computationally efficient, enabling robust and scalable predictions of key magnetic material properties.

Feature-enhanced graph-to-vector workflow for magnetic property prediction. Crystal structures from NEMAD, C2DB, and MP are converted into crystal graphs enriched with atomic spin moments, OFM descriptors, and interatomic distances. Two complementary approaches are benchmarked: (1) graph convolutional networks with fully connected layers, and (2) vectorized graph descriptors used with a Random Forest regressor. This dual-path design highlights the balance between accuracy and computational efficiency in discovering candidate magnetic materials.

Dataset curation and preprocessing

Three datasets were curated to evaluate our graph-to-vec framework: (i) MP-TM, containing formation energy, band gap and magnetization of magnetic compounds from the Materials Project (MP)67 (v2021.11.10) restricted to compositions with transition-metal (TM) elements68. This dataset was further categorized into MP-FM and MP-FiM subsets, containing ferromagnetic (FM) and ferrimagnetic (FiM) compounds, respectively; (ii) C2DB-2D, comprising formation energy and magnetization data for two-dimensional materials from the Computational 2D Materials Database69; and (iii) NEMAD-EXP, containing experimentally measured formation energies and Curie temperatures downloaded from the NEMAD database70.

Separately, an element-specific FiM dataset was constructed from MP. Eight transition metals (Ti, V, Cr, Mn, Fe, Co, Ni, and Cu) were chosen, and for each element a dataset was generated containing all compounds which include the selected element. This results in eight element-specific datasets. Our primary objective is to evaluate model performance in small-data regimes, where extracting transferable structure–property relationships is especially challenging. Later, we show that while feature-enhanced CGCNN struggles under such conditions, our CG-Vec approach remains robust and achieves superior performance.

All datasets were preprocessed using a uniform protocol: entries with energies above the convex hull were removed, duplicate material IDs discarded, and compounds missing target properties excluded. Structural information was extracted for each compound to construct crystal graphs for modeling. This process produced high-quality, internally consistent datasets suitable for rigorous evaluation, with target properties selected to capture both magnetic performance and stability: magnetization quantifies magnetic strength, Curie temperature captures thermal stability of magnetic ordering, formation energy ensures thermodynamic stability, and band gap reflects electronic behavior relevant to spintronic and multifunctional applications. Collectively, these datasets and preprocessing steps enable systematic evaluation of the models’ performance across the target properties over a diverse set of compounds.

Crystal graph construction and feature engineering

To construct crystal graphs, we extract structural information from the datasets. For MP, the crystal structure is directly available in a Crystallographic Information File (CIF)-like format, while for other databases, lattice constants and atomic coordinates are converted into the same format. Using these structures, we create Pymatgen.structure objects to capture the periodic arrangement of atoms. For crystal graphs, we treat these atoms as nodes and bonds or proximity-based interactions as edges. The nearest neighbors are identified within a fixed cutoff radius of 8–12 Å, producing neighbor indices and pairwise distances. To maintain consistency across samples, up to 12–16 nearest neighbors are retained, with padding applied when fewer are present. This produces a discrete graph where adjacency is defined by neighbor index mapping, and each node is connected to a local subgraph representing its coordination environment.

For node features, we construct node embeddings that encode spin, electronic, and elemental characteristics. These include the theoretical atomic spin magnetic moment, one-hot representation of the OFM, and basic atomic properties such as mass, atomic number, radius, and electron affinity. Edge features are obtained by expanding interatomic distances with Gaussian basis functions, providing a smooth, high-dimensional representation of local geometry. Together, these yield three tensors: node features, edge features, and neighbor indices, which fully define the crystal graph.

This uniform descriptor ensures that crystal graphs of varying sizes and topologies can be compared directly, while preserving both the average trends and the variability in atomic and bonding environments.

Building on feature-enhanced crystal graphs, the vectorization step replaces the need for computationally intensive convolutions on large graphs. While convolution-based models such as CGCNN are effective in capturing local atomic environments, they often struggle to represent a global structural context. Statistical pooling overcomes this limitation by condensing both atomic and bonding information into compact fixed-length vectors that preserve local detail while embedding global variability. We hypothesize that this representation can achieve performance comparable to and superior to CGCNN and its variants, while offering improved efficiency, scalability, interpretability, and reduced dependence on large datasets.

Evaluation metrics

For performance evaluations, we divide the datasets into 60% for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing. The hyperparameters of the model were tuned by random search, and robustness was ensured by 5-fold cross-validation, which reduces performance variance and prevents overfitting. The quality of the model was assessed using three complementary metrics: mean absolute error (MAE), coefficient of determination (R2), and correlation coefficient (CC). MAE offers an intuitive measure of prediction error, particularly relevant for low-magnitude targets such as volume normalized magnetization. R2 indicates how well the model evaluates the variance in the data, while CC evaluates the linear agreement between predicted and observed values. Together, this setup balances generalizability and reliability, allowing the transfer of learned physical principles across diverse material datasets72–74.

Results and discussion

The choice of structural representation plays a critical role in the success of machine learning models for material property prediction. While deep graph networks such as CGCNN have become the standard for crystal-based learning tasks, their reliance on local message passing may not always capture the global electronic or magnetic signatures relevant to certain properties, particularly in data-scarce regimes. In this work, we perform a systematic comparison between a state-of-the-art graph convolutional framework and a simple but physically motivated mean–std pooling approach (CG-Vec) across a diverse range of material datasets and target properties. By combining dimensionality reduction analyses with extensive benchmarking on formation energy, band gap, magnetization, and Curie temperature, we identify the conditions under which each representation excels, and provide physical insight into their respective strengths and limitations.

Across the four benchmark properties–formation energy, band gap, magnetization, and Curie temperature–distinct yet property-dependent performance patterns emerge. Properties dominated by short-range bonding physics, such as formation energy and band gap, are consistently well captured by CGCNN. In contrast, properties that reflect collective or long-range electronic effects, such as magnetization and Curie temperature, are better resolved by CG-Vec, particularly in the FiM compounds and data-scarce regimes. These contrasting outcomes motivate a systematic analysis of each property, not only to benchmark accuracy across representations but also to question the prevailing assumption that deep graph networks are universally superior for materials prediction.

To systematically evaluate the impact of representation choice on FiM magnetization prediction (Table-1), we benchmarked three approaches: the baseline Conventional CGCNN, a feature-enhanced CGCNN (enhanced CGCNN) that incorporates atomic spin moments and OFM in node features, and a vector representation derived via mean–std pooling of spin moments (CG-Vec). Eight element-specific FiM datasets spanning Ti to Cu were analyzed, and the results reveal a clear performance hierarchy. Conventional CGCNN exhibits limited predictive ability across most datasets (, 0.1-0.3; CC < 0.6), indicating that structural information alone does not sufficiently capture the complex spin compensation phenomena inherent to FiM systems. Incorporating atomic spin moments and OFM markedly improves CGCNN performance, with average gains of 0.2–0.3 and MAE reductions of 20–30%, confirming that magnetization prediction is strongly tied to elemental spin contributions rather than purely geometric descriptors.

Consequently, mean–std pooling methods capture the dominant physics more effectively than deep convolutional message passing. This challenges the assumption that deeper graph models are inherently superior for all properties and positions. Comparative analysis of enhanced CGCNN and CG-to-Vec representations

To assess computational efficiency, we measured the runtime, memory usage, and inference cost of CG-Vec and CGCNN on the same cobalt-based FM (Co-FM) dataset of 2,500 materials using CPU-only execution. CG-Vec separates into a one-time crystal graph–to–vector construction step (61.7 seconds for the full dataset) followed by model training on fixed-length descriptors. Training the Random Forest model takes 3.6 seconds, uses 45 MB of peak CPU memory, and achieves an inference time of seconds per sample, with an estimated floating-point operations per sample. In contrast, CGCNN requires 455 seconds of total training time, uses 1.42 GB of peak CPU memory, and has an inference time of seconds per sample on the same dataset and hardware. This higher computational cost arises from the repeated message-passing operations and feature updates performed during each training epoch. Overall, these results demonstrate that CG-Vec is substantially more computationally efficient than CGCNN while achieving comparable or better performance.

To obtain a qualitative view of how the two models organise materials in their internal feature spaces, dimensionality reduction techniques, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) and t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding (t-SNE), were employed. These visualisations are not used for training or evaluation, but serve as diagnostic tools. PCA and t-SNE of the CGCNN embeddings (Fig. A1 and Fig. A2) revealed that although the variance distribution improved across successive convolutional layers (from the first layer, “conv-1”, through the fourth, “conv-4”), the resulting projections exhibited diffuse clusters and weak alignment with magnetization trends. For CG-Vec (Fig. A3), the projections display smoother colour variation and more compact grouping. These observations are consistent with the fact that CGCNN builds its representation from local environments, while CG-Vec aggregates material information into a single vector. Detailed visualizations and interpretation are provided in the supplementary information.

For formation energy, CGCNN achieved the strongest performance on the large MP dataset. This is consistent with the fact that formation energy is dominated by short-range bonding and local coordination environments, which CGCNN can represent effectively through message passing along atomic bonds. On smaller datasets, such as C2DB-2D and NEMAD-EXP, both models produced similar overall accuracy, with CG-Vec occasionally matching or slightly surpassing CGCNN. This suggests that the simpler, compact representation of CG-Vec can be advantageous when training data are limited, reducing the risk of overfitting.

For the prediction of the band gap, CGCNN retained a modest advantage. Band gaps depend strongly on the local atomic environment and orbital overlap, which benefit from the fine-grained structural encoding of the graph-based approach. In contrast, CG-Vec’s global representation, while efficient, may lose some of the subtle structural details critical to capturing variations in electronic structure.

In magnetization prediction, performance trends depended on the type of magnetic ordering and dataset size. For well-sampled FM systems, CGCNN again performed best. However, in FiM systems and smaller datasets such as C2DB-2D and MP-FiM, CG-Vec outperformed CGCNN. This reversal likely reflects the fact that FiM properties arise from imbalances between multiple sublattices, and when training data is scarce, a compact global descriptor can generalize better by focusing on chemical and compositional patterns rather than dataset-specific structural details.

For the Curie temperature, CG-Vec showed a clear advantage. Curie temperature is a complex emergent property influenced by long-range exchange interactions, spin–lattice coupling, and collective effects. The ability of CG-Vec to encode stable, system-level information in a compact form appears to be particularly beneficial for such multi-scale targets, especially when the available data are limited.

Overall, the results reveal complementary strengths. Enhanced CGCNN is most effective for large datasets and properties governed by local atomic-scale physics, such as formation energy and band gap. CG-Vec excels in small or noisy datasets and in predicting properties that integrate information over multiple length scales, such as FiM magnetization and Curie temperature. These findings indicate that model choice should be guided by both the size of the dataset and the physical nature of the target property. In practical materials discovery workflows, combining the two approaches-either through property-specific model selection or through ensemble methods-offers a route to maximizing predictive reliability and efficiency, especially in high-throughput or data-constrained scenarios (Fig. 3).

Additionally, to assess the transferability of CG-Vec beyond random train–test splits, we performed a leave-one-element-out evaluation on both the 20,000-entry FM dataset and the 3,000-entry FiM dataset. For each transition metal (Co, Fe, Mn, Cr, Ni, V, Ti, Cu), all compounds containing that element were excluded from the training set and used only for testing. CG-Vec retained good predictive accuracy across all held-out elements, including in the FiM dataset, indicating that its performance is not driven solely by element-specific correlations. We additionally evaluated CG-Vec on external datasets (C2DB and NEMAD); in these cases, prediction accuracy decreased, consistent with the substantial differences in dimensionality and data origin relative to the training data.

To check whether the difference between the FM and FiM results comes only from dataset size, we also trained both models on reduced FM subsets of about 3,000 compounds for Fe-, Co-, Mo-, and Cr-based materials. CGCNN still showed lower errors than CG-Vec on the Fe- and Co-based sets, while both models had very similar accuracy on the Mo- and Cr-based sets. This behavior is reasonable: magnetism in Fe and Co compounds is strongly influenced by local atomic moments, so the detailed local environments captured by CGCNN are especially helpful. In contrast, magnetic moments in Mo and Cr compounds are weaker and depend more on overall composition, which CG-Vec already represents well through its pooled features.

For benchmarking, CHGNet was evaluated on volume normalized magnetization prediction using approximately 2000 FM and 1000 FiM materials. On the FM set of samples, CHGNet yielded an CC of 0.41 and MAE of 0.021. On the FiM set of samples, the CC was 0.59 with MAE of 0.026. This indicates that CHGNet cannot make reliable and accurate magnetization prediction. Negative values were found for both cases, indicating poor variance capture, likely due to weak sensitivity to small magnetization variations and distribution imbalance in the evaluated data.

Discussion

Our results show that there is no universally optimal representation for all material properties. Enhanced CGCNN excels in large datasets and for targets governed primarily by local bonding environments, whereas CG-Vec offers superior accuracy and generalization in sparse datasets and for emergent properties that integrate information over multiple length scales. This complementarity supports an adaptive, property-aware modeling strategy, where the choice of representation is guided by both data availability and the underlying physics of the target. Such targeted selection can not only improve predictive accuracy but also reduce computational overhead, accelerating high-throughput screening and enabling more reliable discovery of functional materials where experimental data are scarce or costly.

A striking case arises in FiM systems, where magnetization trends are dictated less by fine structural detail and more by global electronic configurations and spin population imbalances. Incorporating spin-aware features improves both CGCNN and CG-Vec, yet the pooled vector descriptors consistently deliver higher accuracy, robustness, and transferability across diverse material classes. This suggests that for magnetic properties dominated by collective electronic behavior, physics-informed statistical descriptors can outperform deep graph architectures, particularly under data-limited conditions. By capturing the magnetic signatures without overfitting to fine structural variations, such approaches provide a powerful and efficient alternative for magnetism-driven materials discovery.

Conclusion

This work introduces CG-Vec, a vectorized crystal graph representation that reduces computational cost while preserving interpretability. By replacing convolutional graph updates with fixed vector descriptors, CG-Vec overcomes CGCNN’s limitation of capturing only local environments, enabling better integration of local and global structural information. Across diverse datasets and properties-formation energy, band gap, magnetization, and Curie temperature-CG-Vec achieves comparable or superior to CGCNN, particularly excelling in magnetic property prediction. This framework demonstrates a scalable and transferable approach for property prediction in materials informatics.

The results highlight that the CG-Vec pipeline not only reduces the need for large datasets and high computational resources, but also maintains strong predictive accuracy across distinct material classes. The approach captures atomic and bonding characteristics without iterative graph convolutions, leading to significant efficiency gains. In magnetic systems, where property determination is inherently complex due to coupled local and global interactions, CG-Vec’s balanced feature representation shows clear advantages over graph convolution–based methods.

Looking ahead, the CG-Vec framework can be extended to incorporate richer descriptors derived from electronic structure, thermodynamic stability metrics, or multi-property joint training. Its lightweight architecture makes it suitable for large-scale screening of candidate materials in high-throughput settings, and for integration with generative models for inverse design. By bridging performance with efficiency, CG-Vec offers a practical path toward accelerating the discovery of novel materials with targeted functionalities.
