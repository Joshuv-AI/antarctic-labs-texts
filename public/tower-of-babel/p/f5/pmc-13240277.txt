Mid‐ and late‐life depression increases risk of Alzheimer's disease and related dementias in veterans
Authors: Lay Kodama, Elizabeth Valocchi, Catherine Chanfreau‐Coffinier, Victoria C. Merritt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONUsing 20‐year data from the Veterans Health Administration's electronic health record, we explored associations between mid‐ and late‐life depression with incident Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD).METHODSParticipants included Veterans with (n = 272,739) and without (n = 435,965) depression matched on sociodemographic and healthcare utilization factors. International Classification of Diseases codes were used to define medical conditions. We performed a time‐to‐event analysis using Fine‐Gray models with death as a competing event.RESULTSDepression was associated with ADRD (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.36; 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.35–1.38), with higher risk for mid‐life depression (age at index date <60: HR = 2.56; 95% CI = 2.44–2.69) compared to late‐life depression (age ≥60: HR = 1.70; 95% CI = 1.67–1.72). This association persisted regardless of traumatic brain injury (TBI) and posttraumatic stress disorder status, but was especially strong for those with TBI prior to depression diagnosis.DISCUSSIONFindings emphasize the need to screen for cognitive changes in Veterans diagnosed with depression, especially those with mid‐life depression.

Background

Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) are highly prevalent conditions among older adults, causing burden to patients, caregivers, and the health system. 1 At this time, there are few effective pharmacological intervention options available, making identification of modifiable risk factors crucial to decrease risk of future ADRD diagnosis. One such risk factor is depression. 2

Prior studies suggest a bidirectional association between depression and ADRD. 2 Depressive symptoms may be an early symptom of ADRD itself, a psychological response to disease and cognitive changes, and/or an independent risk factor due to sharing of common neuropathological pathways (e.g., dysregulation of neurotransmitters, increased neuroinflammation and stress hormones, and downstream accumulation of proteinopathies). 3 It is also possible that depressive symptoms may lead to behaviors that increase risk for other independent risk factors associated with ADRD (e.g., social isolation, physical inactivity). 4 , 5 To parse these mechanisms, it is important to study the temporal dynamics of depression diagnosis relative to ADRD diagnosis. In line with this, studies have demonstrated a strong association between late‐life depression and ADRD. 5 , 6 , 7 Moreover, some recent large‐scale electronic health record (EHR)‐based studies have shown that early‐, mid‐, and late‐life depression are all associated with elevated ADRD risk. 8 , 9

Fewer studies, however, have assessed the association between depression and ADRD among military Veterans, despite their increased susceptibility to developing ADRD due to deployment‐related exposure 10 , 11 as well as higher rates of depression compared to the general population. 12 Veterans also experience higher rates of traumatic brain injury (TBI), as well as significant mental health (e.g., posttraumatic stress disorder [PTSD]) comorbidity burdens—all of which have been linked to an increased risk of ADRD and often co‐occur with depression. 13 , 14 Understanding the relationship between depression and ADRD risk among Veterans, as well as how common comorbidities such as TBI and PTSD may modulate this relationship, is crucial for the development of time‐ and risk factor‐specific interventions to lower risk of future ADRD.

In this study, we used 20‐year longitudinal data collected from the Veterans Health Administration's (VHA's) EHR to understand the association between mid‐ and late‐life depression with incident ADRD among Veterans receiving their healthcare through the Department of Veterans Affairs (VA). Our hypothesis was that both mid‐ and late‐life depression increases risk for future ADRD in Veterans, independent of other psychiatric comorbidities, pointing to depression being not only an early symptom of ADRD but also an independent risk factor for future ADRD.

METHODS

Procedures

We received study approval by our local VA Institutional Review Board, as well as the US Army Medical Research and Development Command, Office of Human and Animal Research Oversight, Office of Human Research Oversight, in 2023. All data for this study originated from the VA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure (VINCI), a VA resource that contains the Corporate Data Warehouse (CDW), which encompasses EHR data for all VHA‐enrolled patients. 15 , 16 Informed consent for this study was waived, as the project was classified as exempt under Category 4 given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of secondary data.

Study design and cohort selection

This was a retrospective cohort study using existing data obtained from the VHA EHR between 10/01/1999 and 05/05/2025. We first identified Veterans with a depression diagnosis (n = 1,269,982), defined by having at least one inpatient or two distinct outpatient visits (on different dates) with an International Classification of Diseases (ICD)‐9/10 code for depression listed in Table S1. These codes were informed by a previously published work. 9 Depression onset was defined as the date of the first documented depression diagnosis (i.e., ICD code) in the EHR. We did not take into account recurrence of diagnoses or symptom severity in this study, as our intent was to evaluate a broad depressive phenotype and its association with future ADRD risk. All other Veterans not defined as having depression by this definition were considered the “No Depression” group (n = 3,168,555).

The sampling strategy is summarized in Figure 1. All Veterans were assigned an index date. For Veterans with depression, the index date was defined as the date of the first documented depression diagnosis in the EHR; for Veterans without depression, the index date was selected randomly within the documented observation period. Veterans with an ADRD diagnosis prior to index date were excluded. Due to computational limitations, 1,000,000 Veterans from the pool of eligible Veterans were randomly selected for matching (Depression group n = 282,503; No Depression group n = 717,497). We applied propensity score (PS) matching (MatchIt in R 17 ) to select two Veterans without depression for each Veteran with depression using a generalized linear model, employing the nearest‐neighbor method without replacement and the following matching criteria: exact matching on sex, race, and ethnicity; age at index date (± 3 years); first VHA visit year (± 3 years); and total observation period (i.e., duration of VHA care; ± 1 year). The final cohort included 272,739 Veterans with depression and 435,965 Veterans without depression.

Other pertinent variables included TBI, PTSD, and baseline cardiovascular risk factors. Veterans with a TBI history were defined by at least one documentation of a TBI ICD code listed in Table S2, with time of TBI diagnosis defined relative to depression diagnosis date (before or after). TBI severity was classified as mild, moderate/severe/penetrating, or unclassified, derived from the Armed Forces Health Surveillance Branch of the Military Health System. 18 For Veterans with TBI ICD codes spanning multiple severity levels, the most severe level was chosen for classification. Veterans with a PTSD history were defined by ICD codes (ICD‐9: 309.81; ICD‐10: F43.10, F43.11, F43.12) documented for at least one inpatient or two distinct outpatient visits, with time of PTSD diagnosis defined relative to depression diagnosis date (before or after). Both TBI and PTSD diagnoses needed to have occurred prior to an event (ADRD diagnosis or death) or the end of the observation period. Baseline cardiovascular risk factors were classified as either present or absent prior to index date, with presence defined as having at least one inpatient or two outpatient ICD codes for that particular condition. See Table S3 for the list of cardiovascular risk factors and corresponding ICD codes.

The primary outcome of interest for this study was “clinical ADRD.” A validated algorithm derived within the VHA EHR was used to define ADRD status. 19 An ADRD diagnosis was defined by documentation of at least two ICD codes (on different dates) from a pre‐determined set of codes related to ADRD (i.e., ICD codes for AD, non‐specific dementia, or another related dementia). A full list of the ADRD ICD codes can be found in Table S4. We did not incorporate information regarding AD‐related biomarkers (i.e., cases include clinical cases of diagnosed AD, non‐specific dementia, or another related dementia). ADRD diagnosis needed to be documented between the ages of 65 and 100 years old. If an ADRD diagnosis occurred outside of this age range, it was not included as an event.

Statistical analysis

We performed a time‐to‐event analysis of the association of depression and ADRD. We used a Fine‐Gray model with death as a competing event using the R package Survival 20 and ggsurvfit. 21 Death dates were collected from the EHR. We performed population‐stratified analyses by age at index date (< 60 years old; ≥60 years old), sex, TBI status, and PTSD status relative to time of depression diagnosis, and time since index date (≤10 years; > 10 years). The age cut off for “mid‐life” and “late‐life” was defined according to previous studies. 9 , 22 Sensitivity analyses were done with Fine‐Gray models adjusted for all baseline cardiovascular risk factors or the log of the total number of documented visits. Additional sensitivity analyses were carried out to examine the impact of removing those with less than 1 year of follow‐up data. See Figure 1 for a graphical representation of these analyses. The proportional hazards assumption was satisfied based on visual inspection of the complementary log‐log plot (Supplementary Figure). All analyses were conducted using R Statistical Software version 4.21 (R Core Team 2024), 23 and plots were generated using R package ggplot2. 24

Data availability

The data and code used to generate these results are accessible to VA‐credentialed staff who are granted access to study‐specific data (along with tools for analysis and reporting) in a secure, virtual working environment (i.e., VINCI). Under current VA policy, VINCI is only accessible to VA‐credentialed staff; thus, the datasets generated and/or analyzed during the current study are not publicly available.

RESULTS

We identified 272,739 Veterans with a diagnosis of depression (i.e., the “Depression” group) and 435,965 Veterans without a depression diagnosis (i.e., the “No Depression” group), using propensity score matching on sociodemographic and healthcare utilization factors at index date (Figure 1). The majority of Veterans were male (96%) and white, non‐Hispanic (91%). Veterans with depression were, on average, 63.2 years of age (SD = 10.1) at index date, and Veterans without depression were, on average, 66.3 years of age (SD = 9.2) at index date. The average duration of VHA care was 19.6 years (SD = 5.4; Min = 2.0; Max = 25.6) among Veterans with depression and 18.7 years (SD = 5.5; Min = 2.0; Max = 25.6) among Veterans without depression. Follow‐up from index date was, on average, 11.2 years (SD = 6.8; Min < 1; Max = 25.6). Veterans were generally well‐matched on the parameters used in matching (Table 1).

The prevalence of ADRD was 14.8% (n = 40,277) in Veterans with depression and 9.8% (n = 42,770) in Veterans without depression (Table 1), with the average time between index date to ADRD diagnosis being 7.1 years (SD = 5.5; Min < 1; Max = 25.5). With regard to TBI history, 8.7% of Veterans with depression compared to 4.0% of Veterans without depression had a history of TBI; 2.2% received a TBI diagnosis prior to depression diagnosis, whereas 6.5% received a TBI diagnosis after their depression diagnosis (Table 1). For those with a TBI diagnosis, documented severity levels included mild, moderate/severe/penetrating, and unclassified, with distributions being similar across all three classifications for both No Depression and Depression groups (Table S5). As for PTSD, 38.8% of Veterans with depression compared to 7.2% of Veterans without depression had a history of PTSD; 20.6% received a PTSD diagnosis prior to depression diagnosis, while 18.2% received the PTSD diagnosis after their depression diagnosis (Table 1). Finally, 18.2% (n = 49,520) with depression and 14.6% (n = 63,498) without depression died prior to the end of the follow‐up period (Table 1).

Using a Cox proportional hazard model and accounting for the competing risk of death using the Fine‐Gray method, the hazard ratio (HR) of developing ADRD following a depression diagnosis was 1.36 (95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.35–1.38), with this risk being similar between sexes (Figure 2; Table 2). This association persisted even after removing those with less than 1 year of follow‐up data (n = 33,335 Veterans removed; n = 675,369 Veterans included; HR = 1.29; 95% CI = 1.27–1.31) and regardless of follow‐up time period (i.e., time from index date ≤10 years: HR = 1.64; 95% CI = 1.61–1.66; > 10 years: HR = 1.43; 95% CI = 1.40–1.47; Table 2). In the age‐stratified analyses, a higher risk of ADRD was observed for those diagnosed with depression in mid‐life years (i.e., age at index date < 60: HR = 2.56; 95% CI = 2.44–2.69) compared to those diagnosed with depression in late‐life (i.e., age ≥60: HR = 1.70; 95% CI = 1.67–1.72; Figure 2; Table 2). These associations persisted regardless of adjusting for baseline cardiovascular risk factors (overall: HR = 1.30; 95% CI = 1.28–1.31; age at index date < 60: HR = 2.40; 95% CI = 2.28–2.52; age ≥60: HR = 1.66; 95% CI = 1.63–1.68) or adjusting for total number of visits (overall: HR = 1.46; 95% CI = 1.43–1.48).

We evaluated the impact of TBI and PTSD as potential modifying factors. When TBI was considered, the association of depression and ADRD was strongest for Veterans with a TBI diagnosis prior to depression diagnosis (HR = 1.80; 95% CI = 1.62–2.00) compared to those without a history of TBI (HR = 1.38; 95% CI = 1.36–1.40) and those with a TBI diagnosis after depression diagnosis (HR = 1.15; 95% CI = 1.08–1.23; Figure 2; Table 2). When focusing on those with a TBI diagnosis after depression diagnosis (n = 28,329), even after removing those with less than 1 year of follow‐up data after a TBI diagnosis (n = 4,517 Veterans removed), this pattern persisted (HR = 1.15; 95% CI = 1.06–1.25). When PTSD was considered, the association of depression and ADRD was strongest for Veterans without PTSD (HR = 1.65; 95% CI = 1.62–1.67) and in those with a PTSD diagnosis after depression diagnosis (HR = 1.57; 95% CI = 1.46–1.69), while those with a PTSD diagnosis prior to depression had a lower risk compared to these two groups (HR = 1.36; 95% CI = 1.28–1.45; Figure 2; Table 2).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use a nationwide sample of Veterans to examine associations between mid‐ and late‐life depression and incident ADRD. Our findings showed that depression is associated with an increased risk of developing ADRD in Veterans, and that the association is significantly stronger in Veterans diagnosed with depression during mid‐life. Although sex is a well‐known modifier of ADRD risk, 25 , 26 our sex‐stratified analysis showed that the overall risk of depression on future ADRD was similar between male and female Veterans, consistent with a prior large study in Veterans. 27 These associations persisted even after accounting for baseline cardiovascular risk factors and healthcare utilization (assessed as the total number of documented visits). Moreover, the association between depression and ADRD was observed independently of TBI or PTSD diagnosis status, suggesting that depression may be an independent risk factor for ADRD years (and possibly decades) before the onset of prodromal symptoms of the disease.

Our findings are consistent with previous literature examining the association of depression and ADRD. A meta‐analysis including 33 studies of depression and future dementia risk among civilians showed that depression was associated with increased risk of dementia in 30 studies, though there was considerable heterogeneity across studies in terms of the level of risk reported (pooled relative risk [RR]: 1.96, 95% CI: 1.59–2.43 for all‐cause dementia; RR: 1.90, 95% CI: 1.52–2.38 for AD). 22 Within the same meta‐analysis, subgroup analysis showed significant associations between late‐onset depression (defined as > 60 or 65 years old) and dementia (pooled RR: 1.92, 95% CI: 1.13–3.24) but not early‐ or mid‐life depression and dementia (pooled RR: 1.17, 95% CI: 0.80–1.73). 22 A more recent Danish cohort study looking at 246,499 individuals with depression and 1,190,302 matched individuals without depression found depression was associated with a 2.41 hazard of developing dementia (95% CI: 2.35–2.47); the association between depression and dementia remained elevated regardless of depression onset (i.e., early, mid, or late‐life depression) or time from index date. 9 A study conducted in Veterans aged ≥55 years found that Veterans with dysthymia or depression were twice as likely to develop dementia compared to those without dysthymia or depression, though no age‐stratified analyses were conducted to examine timing of mood disorder diagnosis. 7 Our study results and the hazard ratios reported are consistent with the ranges documented in these previous studies. Our findings are also aligned with two recent studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow‐up durations that have found mid‐life depression is associated with elevated risk for dementia. 8 , 9 Moreover, our study is the first to comprehensively study mid‐ and late‐life depression in the Veteran population.

Why mid‐life depression confers higher risk for future ADRD remains unclear, though this finding points to the likelihood of depression being an independent risk factor for future ADRD. One possibility is depression leads to behaviors that increase risk for other independent risk factors associated with ADRD, though a recent study looking at specific mid‐life depression symptoms adjusted for baseline factors such as physical inactivity, hearing loss, and excessive alcohol consumption, and found depression symptoms were independently associated with increased risk for dementia. 28 Other possibilities include shared biological pathways between depression and ADRD. Depression is known to lead to dysregulation of neurotransmitters, increased neuroinflammation and stress hormones, and increased permeability of the blood–brain barrier, all of which could lead to increased vulnerability to ADRD pathogenesis. 29 , 30

Veterans experience significant comorbidities that impact mental health, making it important to understand how common comorbidities may modulate the risk between depression and ADRD. We focused on TBI and PTSD in our study as both are known risk factors of ADRD and are more prevalent in Veterans compared to civilian populations. 31 , 32 Prior studies have identified TBI as an independent risk factor for future dementia. 2 , 13 , 33 Many of these studies, however, include depression and other mental health comorbidities as a covariate for adjustment of their models, and therefore it is unclear whether there is differential risk depending on the relative timing of depression and TBI diagnoses. We observed that the association of depression and future ADRD was strongest for those with a TBI diagnosis prior to depression diagnosis, which may point to the added risk of depressive symptoms from the sequelae of TBI itself. 34 , 35

We also found the association between depression and ADRD risk was elevated regardless of PTSD diagnosis. Of note, PTSD was more prevalent in Veterans with depression compared to those without depression (Table 1), which is aligned with the literature. There was high overlap between the diagnoses of depression and PTSD, especially in the military population, 36 with one meta‐analysis finding 52% (95% CI: 48–56) of individuals with current PTSD having co‐occurring major depressive disorder. 37 PTSD has also been found to be an independent risk factor for dementia in both civilians and Veterans, 22 , 38 , 39 and therefore our findings could be partially explained through trauma‐related mood symptoms, rather than depression itself, being associated with ADRD risk. Yet we found significant association between depression and ADRD even in those without a PTSD diagnosis, supporting that depression specifically may act as an independent risk factor, regardless of past trauma. Whether or not PTSD treatment, which has high overlap with depression treatment options, may have modulated the risk of depression on future ADRD, remains a future interest for exploration.

There are several limitations to this study that need to be taken into account when interpreting these findings. First, use of EHR data has inherent limitations due to its retrospective nature and existing biases in clinical judgement, documentation, and recognition of symptoms. This is true for all conditions of interest in this study, as the first documented diagnosis date may not accurately reflect the actual onset of the disease. We also did not have data relating to education status and other environmental factors such as socioeconomic status or neighborhood information, which are known modulators of ADRD risk. 40 Though we did a sex‐stratified analysis, the sample size of female Veterans was considerably smaller than male Veterans due to Veteran demographics; thus, further studies need to be done in larger female cohorts. Finally, we did not incorporate information on the treatment status, chronicity, or severity of the conditions of interest (e.g. recurrence of depressive symptoms, concurrent psychiatric symptoms such as psychosis), nor did we include information on other psychiatric comorbidities, which are important factors to consider in the future as they have been associated with differential risk of dementia. 13 , 41

Overall, our findings support that depression is a risk factor for future ADRD in Veterans with the highest risk (> 2.5×) observed in Veterans with depression onset during mid‐life. Importantly, depression was associated with ADRD independent of TBI and PTSD diagnosis, although our results suggest that there may be a differential risk depending on the relative timing of each diagnosis. Currently there is no clinical recommendation to screen all older patients for cognitive changes according to the U.S. Preventive Services Task Force. 42 This study, however, adds support to consider cognitive screening in older patients who have had a prior diagnosis of depression, especially mid‐life depression, as they are at higher risk of ADRD, with attention to potential aggravating factors such as TBI and PTSD history and their timing relative to depression symptoms.
