Multiplexed sensitivity encoding diffusion-weighted imaging in the assessment of meningiomas: improving the image quality, grading accuracy, and proliferative activity evaluation
Authors: Danjie Lin, Sihui Liu, Xiaodan Chen, Ye Li, Yichao Zhang, Yizhu Chen, Xiaofang Zhou, Hui Zheng, Lingmin Zheng, Peirong Jiang, Tianjin Zhong, Jialu Zhang, Yunjing Xue, Lin Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMeningiomas often occur near the skull, where tumor imaging is prone to distortion and susceptibility artifacts, resulting in an unsatisfactory image quality of conventional single-shot echo-planar diffusion-weighted imaging (ssEPI-DWI). Our aim in this study was to evaluate the image quality of multiplexed sensitivity encoding diffusion-weighted imaging (MUSE-DWI) in meningiomas and the accuracy of the apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) histogram parameters for tumor grading and proliferative activity evaluation based on MUSE-DWI and ssEPI-DWI.MethodsA total of 73 consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed meningiomas were included in this prospective study. Three neuroradiologists independently assessed the image quality using a 5-point Likert scale, whereas two other neuroradiologists quantitatively calculated the signal-to-noise ratio of lesion (SNRlesion), signal-to-noise ratio of the normal white matter (SNRnormal), and lesion to brain contrast-to-noise ratio (CNR). Inter-rater agreement was assessed using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC). Image quality scores, SNR values, and CNR values of the two diffusion techniques were compared using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Histogram metrics of ADC values were extracted from the whole tumor. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of single histogram parameter and combined models for tumor grading. Spearman rank correlation was applied to assess the correlation between histogram parameters and the Ki-67 labelling index (LI).ResultsSharpness, distortion, artifact, lesion conspicuity, and overall image quality were significantly better in MUSE-DWI than those in ssEPI-DWI, with good agreement among the three raters (P<0.05; ICC: 0.83–0.90), whereas there was no significant difference in diagnostic confidence between the two DWI sequences (P>0.05; ICC: 0.80–0.82). The values of SNRlesion, SNRnormal, and CNR were all significantly higher in MUSE-DWI than they were in ssEPI-DWI with good agreement between the two raters (P<0.05; ICC: 0.82–0.92). In both diffusion techniques, high-grade meningiomas showed significantly lower ADC 10th percentiles (C10) and ADC median values (P=0.005–0.039), and higher ADC interquartile range (IQR) values (P=0.005–0.025), compared to low-grade meningiomas. After false discovery rate (FDR) correction, only the ADC C10 (q=0.025) and ADC IQR (q=0.025) values derived from MUSE-DWI remained significantly different between high- and low-grade meningiomas. The best diagnostic accuracy was obtained by combining the ADC C10 and ADC IQR from MUSE-DWI [area under the curve (AUC) =0.774]. After nested cross-validation, the model still showed an AUC of 0.769. For MUSE-DWI, ADC IQR (P=0.007) and variance (P=0.040) were significantly positively correlated with Ki-67 LI. For ssEPI-DWI, only ADC IQR (P=0.047) was significantly positively correlated with Ki-67 LI. However, after FDR correction, no ADC histogram parameter remained significantly associated with Ki-67 LI (all q values >0.05).ConclusionsCompared with ssEPI-DWI, MUSE-DWI improves the diffusion image quality of meningiomas, enhancing the accuracy of meningioma grading and proliferative activity evaluation.

Introduction

According to the recent statistics from The Central Brain Tumor Registry of the United States, meningiomas are one of the most common primary intracranial tumors, accounting for 40.8% of all primary brain tumors (1). In the 2021 World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Central Nervous System (CNS) Tumors, WHO grade 1 meningiomas are low-grade meningiomas (LGMs), whereas WHO grade 2 and grade 3 meningiomas are high-grade meningiomas (HGMs) (2). HGMs exhibit a poorer prognosis compared to LGMs, with increased mortality, higher recurrence rates, and more complex treatment plans (3). Ki-67 is a nuclear antigen and a marker for tumor cell proliferation. Elevated levels of Ki-67 expression are typically associated with the rapid proliferation of tumor cells (4). Previous studies have indicated a correlation between the expression level of Ki-67 and the prognosis of meningiomas (5,6). Therefore, preoperative evaluation of the grade and proliferative activity of meningiomas plays a significant role in formulating surgical strategies, predicting recurrence, and assessing prognosis.

Diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI) provides insights into the motion of water molecules within tissues (7,8). High-grade brain tumors often exhibit increased cellular proliferation and dense cellular arrangements, thus limiting the diffusion of water molecules within the tumor (9,10). Prior studies have indicated the significant values of diffusion techniques and diffusion-based integrated models in evaluating biological behaviors of meningiomas, including grading, proliferative activity, growth pattern, and tumor recurrence (6,11-15).

However, meningiomas often occur near the skull and even at the base of the skull, where tumor diffusion imaging is prone to distortion and susceptibility artifact (16-18). In the brain, susceptibility artifacts of DWI particularly occur in areas between air-containing cranial bones and brain tissue (19,20). At present, the single-shot echo-planar imaging (ssEPI) technique is most commonly used in intracranial diffusion imaging, but it is associated with relatively large susceptibility artifacts and geometric distortions (21). The distortion and insufficient clarity of ssEPI-DWI not only compromise the quality of images, but also further impact the accuracy of measured apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) values. Inaccurate ADC values adversely impact the grading diagnosis and assessment of proliferative activity in meningiomas.

Multiplexed sensitivity encoding (MUSE) is a recent novel technology focused on reducing the distortion of diffusion images and improving image quality (22). MUSE-DWI is based on multi-shot echo-planar imaging (EPI) to reduce image distortion, enabling high spatial resolution imaging (22,23). Previous studies have indicated that MUSE-DWI can improve image quality and increase diagnostic accuracy in conditions including pediatric brain, hippocampal sclerosis, Crohn’s disease, breast cancer, liver cancer, and female pelvis (19,24-28). However, to date, there has been no study of MUSE-DWI on brain tumors.

Given the advantages of MUSE, we hypothesized that the diffusion imaging of meningiomas could be improved, potentially enhancing diagnostic accuracy and providing additional value in tumor evaluation. Therefore, the purpose of this study was to qualitatively and quantitatively compare the image quality of MUSE-DWI with that of ssEPI-DWI in the brain. Additionally, we aimed to assess the performance of these two techniques in the evaluation of meningioma grading and tumor proliferative activity using whole-tumor histogram analyses. We present this article in accordance with the STARD reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2024-2893/rc).

Methods

Patients

This prospective study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital (No. 2020WSJK036). Written informed consent was provided by all patients. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. A total of 105 consecutive suspected meningioma patients were prospectively enrolled in our hospital from April 2022 to June 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) acquired preoperative magnetic resonance (MR) imaging, including MUSE-DWI and ssEPI-DWI; (II) underwent surgical treatment at our hospital within 10 days after MR examination; and (III) postoperative pathology confirmed the diagnosis of meningiomas according to the 2021 WHO classification of CNS tumors. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) history of cranial radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or neurosurgical intervention before the surgery; (II) severe motion artifacts; and (III) diffuse and extensive hemorrhagic foci within the entire tumor. Finally, 81 patients were pathologically confirmed as having meningioma. Of the excluded patients, 2 had received preoperative cranial radiotherapy, 4 had severe motion artifacts, and 2 presented diffuse and extensive hemorrhagic foci. The final cohort consisted of 73 meningioma patients. The patient flowchart is presented in Figure 1.

MR examination

All images were preoperatively acquired with a 3-T MR scanner (Signa Premier, GE Healthcare, Milwaukee, WI, USA) using a 48-channel receiver head coil.

The conventional imaging protocols included pre-contrast axial T1-weighted fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images, axial T2-weighted PROPELLER images and axial T2-weighted FLAIR images. Before contrast agent injection, both axial MUSE-DWI and ssEPI-DWI were acquired with b values of 0 and 1,000 s/mm2. Compared with ssEPI-DWI, MUSE-DWI was acquired with the same scanning parameters, except for the utilization of multi-shot (2-shot) acquisition to deduce distortion, repetition time/echo time (TR/TE) =2,500/57 ms, and a scanning duration of 39 seconds. ADC maps were generated from DWI with the b-value of 1,000 s/mm². Post-contrast images were acquired after intravenous injection of the gadolinium contrast agent (0.1 mmol/kg, Gadobutrol, Bayer Schering, Berlin, Germany) at a rate of 2 mL/s. Qualitative image analysis

Three observers (with 5, 10, and 20 years of experience in neuroradiology, respectively) independently performed visual assessments of image quality for the two DWI sequences at b=1,000 s/mm2. They were blinded to patient information and DWI methods. Corresponding T2WI and contrast-enhanced T1WI were provided as anatomical reference standards for the cases. The observers rated image quality at lesion sites for both DWI sequences using a 5-point Likert scale (Table S1) in terms of sharpness, geometry distortion, susceptibility artifacts, lesion conspicuity, diagnostic confidence, and overall image quality, as described in previous relevant literature (24,28).

Quantitative image analysis

Two observers (with 5 and 10 years of experience in neuroradiology, respectively) independently conducted quantitative analyses of lesions and normal brain white matter on the diffusion images with a b-value of 1,000 mm/s2. In each patient, the region of interest (ROI) was manually outlined at the slice of the maximum lesion extent for measurement of signal intensity within the lesion (SIlesion). ROIs with an area of 1.0 cm2 were placed on the contralateral white matter to obtain the signal intensity of normal brain tissue (SInormal) (29,30). As parallel imaging could affect the measurement of background noise levels (31,32), we assessed local noise using standard deviation of normal brain tissue (SDnormal). For the two DWI sequences, ROIs were placed at the same level and position, with identical sizes.

MR image processing and histogram analysis

For each patient, two radiologists (with 10 and 20 years of experience in neuroradiology, respectively) independently delineated the volume of interest (VOI) of the solid portion of the whole tumor on two sets of ADC maps. They were blinded to the types of DWI sequences, ADC maps, and pathological results. With reference to structural MR and corresponding DW images, the whole-tumor VOI was obtained by using ITK-SNAP program (version 4.0.1, www.itksnap.org), avoiding necrosis, cystic changes, calcifications, and hemorrhage.

The histogram data within the VOIs were assessed using an open-source software package FeAture Explorer (FAE; https://github.com/salan668/FAE). The three-dimensional (3D) information from all voxels was calculated to generate the corresponding histograms of the two different ADC maps. Then, the following histogram parameters were extracted: minimum, 10th percentile (C10), mean, median, 90th percentile (C90), maximum, IQR, kurtosis, variance, and skewness.

Pathological analysis

The pathological diagnosis of meningiomas was conducted by a neuropathologist (with 10 years of experience in neuropathology). Tumor grades were determined in accordance with the 2021 WHO classification of CNS tumors. The tumor proliferative index was estimated as the percentage of positive tumor cell nuclei labeled by the Ki-67 monoclonal antibody. The area with the highest density of positive tumor nuclei was selected for counting.

Nested cross-validation and model evaluation

To evaluate model performance while minimizing overfitting, we employed a nested cross-validation framework. The outer layer used fivefold stratified cross-validation, in which the dataset was split into five folds. In each iteration, one fold served as the test set and the remaining four were used for training.

Within each outer training set, an inner leave-one-out cross-validation (LOOCV) was performed for both feature selection and hyperparameter tuning. In each inner iteration, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to assess feature significance. Features with P values <0.1 were recorded, and those selected in over 50% of the inner folds were retained. A least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO)-regularized logistic regression model was then trained, with the regularization parameter optimized based on area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC) using the inner LOOCV.

The optimized model was retrained on the full outer training fold and tested on the held-out outer test fold. This procedure was repeated across all five outer folds. Final model performance was assessed using AUC, and ROC curves were plotted for each fold as well as for overall performance.

This nested approach ensured that feature selection and hyperparameter tuning were confined to the training data, thus avoiding information leakage and producing unbiased performance estimates.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using the software SPSS 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA), R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and MedCalc (version 20.022; MedCalc Software, Ostend, Belgium). After assessing the normality distribution of SNR and CNR through Shapiro-Wilk test, image quality scores, SNR, and CNR of the two diffusion techniques were compared using the paired Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Inter-rater agreements in image quality scores, SNR, CNR, and histogram parameters were evaluated using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC) with a two-way random-effects model and 95% confidence intervals (CIs): 0.00–0.20, poor correlation; 0.21–0.40, fair correlation; 0.41–0.60, moderate correlation; 0.61–0.80, good correlation; and 0.81–1.00, excellent correlation (11,33,34).

For MUSE-DWI, Mann-Whitney U test was used to compare the differences in histogram parameters between LGMs and HGMs. False discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to control the false positive rate in multiple comparisons. For all histogram parameters with significant differences, ROC curves were generated to evaluate their discriminative diagnostic efficacy. Thereafter, the binary logistic regression analysis was used to construct a combined model. The diagnostic efficacy of the combined model in discriminating between LGMs and HGMs was assessed using ROC curves. The corresponding AUCs with the 95% CI were calculated to determine the optimal cut-off value for each histogram metric and the combined model in meningioma grading. The diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of the value were calculated, and the optimal threshold was determined by the maximum Youden’s index. The mean AUC based on the nested cross-validation was used to evaluate the robustness of the model. Applying the same methodology, the aforementioned statistical analyses were performed on ssEPI-DWI.

The Spearman rank correlation method was applied to assess the correlation between histogram parameters and the Ki-67 index. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

Ultimately, 73 patients were included. Among them, 14 cases were classified as HGMs, comprising 11 cases of atypical meningiomas, 2 cases of anaplastic meningiomas, and 1 case of meningothelial meningioma with brain invasion; 59 cases were classified as LGMs. Patient characteristics are summarized in Table S2. HGMs and LGMs exhibited no significant differences in terms of gender, age, tumor size, and location. The proportion of peritumoral edema and Ki-67 labeling index (LI) in HGMs was significantly elevated compared to LGMs (with P=0.002 and P<0.001, respectively).

The ROC analysis results are presented in Table 5 and Figure 4. The AUC of the single ADC histogram parameter of MUSE-DWI for grading meningiomas ranged from 0.743 to 0.744.

Discussion

This study demonstrated that MUSE-DWI exhibited superior image sharpness, fewer geometric distortions, fewer susceptibility artifacts, better lesion conspicuity, and higher overall image quality in evaluating the meningioma compared to ssEPI-DWI. When quantitatively assessing image quality, MUSE-DWI exhibited higher SNR and CNR compared to ssEPI-DWI. In grading accuracy, the MUSE-DWI combined model demonstrated a certain level of diagnostic efficiency, whereas the ssEPI-DWI failed to construct a combined model due to the absence of ADC histogram parameters that remained statistically significant for meningioma grading after FDR correction.

This study conducted a comprehensive comparison of image quality between MUSE-DWI and ssEPI-DWI for meningioma, including a 5-point Likert scale and quantitative comparisons of SNR and CNR values. The results consistently indicated superior image quality with MUSE-DWI. This is consistent with previous studies where MUSE-DWI had superior qualitative image quality over ssEPI-DWI (19,24,28). Previous studies regarding the pancreas, liver, and breast demonstrated that MUSE-DWI had higher SNR and CNR compared to ssEPI-DWI (26,27,35), consistent with the findings of this study. The superior image quality of MUSE over ssEPI can be explained as follows: ssEPI is susceptible to geometric distortion due to local variations in eddy currents and magnetic field, and T2* blurring due to prolonged readout (21); MUSE collects k-space data over multiple excitations and utilizes self-navigation to correct motion-induced phase errors between excitations, significantly reducing distortion artifacts (22,36). To our knowledge, the current study is the first to apply MUSE-DWI to evaluate meningiomas.

In this study, the ADC C10 values of HGMs in MUSE-DWI were significantly lower than those of LGMs, consistent with the findings of previous studies (11,37). This may be attributed to the higher nucleus-to-cytoplasm ratio and smaller extracellular space observed in most HGMs, resulting in pronounced restriction of water molecule diffusion. This study demonstrated that the ADC IQR values of HGMs in MUSE-DWI were significantly higher than those of LGMs. This may be attributed to the ability of IQR to reflect the range of diffusion changes within the tumor. A larger IQR indicates a wider range of diffusion changes within the tissue, possibly indicating greater tissue heterogeneity. In addition, this study revealed that the minimum and maximum values of ADC values did not exhibit significant differences between HGMs and LGMs, contrary to the previous findings (11). This inconsistency may be attributed to the influence of magnetic susceptibility artifacts on the extreme values.

It is worth noting that the MUSE-DWI combined model achieved clinically meaningful grading performance, whereas the ssEPI-DWI failed to construct a combined model. One possible reason is that susceptibility artifacts might lead to less accurate ADC values measured by ssEPI-DWI, thereby impacting the grading diagnostic efficacy of meningiomas. The whole-tumor histogram analysis based on 3D-VOI can be used to assess tumor heterogeneity (38), but it is greatly affected by artifacts. MUSE-DWI can reduce susceptibility artifacts, thus providing a better reflection of tumor heterogeneity. The MUSE-DWI combined model exhibited the best diagnostic performance, with an AUC of 0.774. After nested cross-validation, the model still showed a mean AUC of 0.769. The model still exhibited a reasonable level of predictive capability, indicating that it retains useful predictive power even under a more rigorous validation strategy. Similar to our studies, MUSE-DWI was also demonstrated to enhance the diagnostic accuracy of various diseases, including hippocampal sclerosis, rectal cancer, and malignant liver lesions (25,27,39).

Regarding the correlation with the Ki-67 proliferation index, both diffusion techniques’ ADC IQR and MUSE-DWI’s ADC variance values exhibited significant positive correlations with Ki-67, which was similar to the previous research findings (11). This may be attributed to the ability of IQR and variance to reflect tumor heterogeneity. Thus, tumors with higher proliferative activity tend to display greater heterogeneity, resulting in elevated IQR and variance values. Of note, the correlation between the histogram parameter IQR of MUSE-DWI and the Ki-67 index was stronger than that of ssEPI-DWI, and the variance value of MUSE-DWI showed a significant positive correlation with the Ki-67 LI, whereas the variance value of ssEPI-DWI did not exhibit statistically significant correlation with the Ki-67 LI. This may be attributed to the fact that MUSE-DWI provided more accurate true IQR and variance values for the entire tumor. However, after FDR correction, no ADC histogram parameter remained significantly associated with Ki-67 LI. Nevertheless, the ADC IQR and variance values still exhibited a trend toward correlation with Ki-67 LI.

The use of ADC for prediction remains a topic of debate. In macroadenomas, the initial hypothesis suggested that high ADC values indicate reduced cellularity, leading to increased extracellular space, a condition commonly observed in fibrous adenomas. Although most studies do not find a statistically significant correlation or association (40,41), those that do report the following ADC values: >(1.363±0.259)×10−3 mm2/s (42), >0.60×10−3 mm2/s (43), and >0.63×10−3 mm2/s (44). Some works have noted low ADC values in tumors with high consistency, suggesting that fibrous adenomas may exhibit increased cellularity at the expense of a higher nuclear–cytoplasmic ratio. However, the literature has not yet established the relationship between hardened consistency and increased cellularity (45). In meningiomas, the fibrous components may also impact the ADC values.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, it was a single-center study, and the number of cases included was relatively small. Secondly, although MUSE-DWI can reduce geometric deformation, there is still room for improvement in enhancing image quality. Thirdly, ADC values are influenced not only by true diffusion but also heavily by T2 relaxation, which may affect interpretation (46,47). Additionally, the TR/TE settings differed slightly between MUSE-DWI and ssEPI-DWI. Future studies with consistent parameters will help to validate the current findings. Finally, although MUSE-DWI has improved diagnostic efficacy of meningioma grading compared to ssEPI-DWI to a certain extent, its diagnostic performance still requires further improvement. This is mainly limited by the application of a single diffusion technique. It is foreseeable that the combination of MUSE-DWI with other advanced diffusion techniques could further enhance the grading diagnostic efficacy of meningiomas.

Conclusions

MUSE-DWI improves the image quality of meningioma imaging within an acceptable short acquisition time. Through histogram analysis, MUSE-DWI outperformed ssEPI-DWI in the grading diagnosis and proliferative activity evaluation of meningiomas. Therefore, incorporating MUSE-DWI into routine clinical imaging protocols for meningiomas is feasible and advantageous.
