A bioenergetic understanding of “aerobic glycolysis”
Authors: Valerie L Wasem, Shona A Mookerjee
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract“Aerobic glycolysis” is a widely used term whose current meaning has drifted from its original usage in a way that has created confusion and inaccuracy. This drift has weakened “aerobic glycolysis” as a hypothesis-testing framework, despite the critical importance of glycolysis in understanding cellular bioenergetic behavior. Here, we examine the historical and contemporary uses of “aerobic glycolysis” and the related “Warburg effect”. We argue that “aerobic glycolysis” as originally investigated was essentially a bioenergetic phenomenon. We review the bioenergetic model of glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration as ATP supply pathways operating together to meet cellular ATP demand. A bioenergetic view of aerobic glycolysis clarifies that it is not a less desirable contingency or indicator of pathology, but rather a part of a kinetically regulated system of cellular energy supply. On this basis, the operation of glycolysis under many different physiological and pathological conditions can be better interrogated and understood.Subject terms: Biochemistry, Cancer metabolism, Energy metabolism, Control theory

Introduction

On its face, “aerobic glycolysis” describes glycolysis that takes place in the presence of oxygen. Under this interpretation nearly all glycolytic activity in aerobic organisms would qualify. Yet across the metabolism literature of the last several decades, “aerobic glycolysis” generally implies an unusual, pathological, or otherwise distinct metabolic state. Despite its common use, “aerobic glycolysis” currently lacks a clear and consistent definition. It has been described as elevated lactate production or extracellular acidification, increased glucose uptake relative to respiration, a decreased stoichiometric ATP yield per mole glucose consumed, or an asserted cellular “preference” for glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation (Table 1). These definitions are not equivalent, nor do they converge on a coherent mechanism.

Historically, however, “aerobic glycolysis” did have a specific experimental meaning. Warburg and others used the term to describe glycolytic flux to lactate measured under controlled aerobic conditions, relative to the same flux measured in the absence of oxygen1. Crucially, Warburg did not propose that glycolysis and respiration were mutually exclusive, as some interpretations imply, nor did he suggest that aerobic glycolysis was primarily a function of cancer cells. The “Warburg effect” as we understand it today was, in fact, only put forward decades later2.

Current investigation of aerobic glycolysis often frames glycolysis as a paradoxical or less desirable energetic alternative to mitochondrial respiration. This obscures the fact that glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation are bioenergetically linked pathways that operate together to meet cellular energy demand. Glycolysis is the sole entry point for sugars into central carbon metabolism, yielding pyruvate that may be reduced to lactate or oxidized in the TCA cycle. From this perspective, glycolytic flux to both lactate and the TCA cycle under aerobic conditions is not paradoxical, but predictable.

This Perspective has three aims. First, we revisit how the term “aerobic glycolysis” was originally developed as a quantitative comparison between specific conditions. Second, we examine how contemporary usage has largely abandoned the quantitative aspect of the original definition, resulting in inconsistent and sometimes contradictory interpretations of glycolytic activity. Third, we argue that the mechanisms and regulation of aerobic glycolysis, at least over the acute timeframe of seconds to minutes, are better understood using the system modeling approach of metabolic control analysis, where glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation are linked branches of total cellular ATP production, rather than opposing or hierarchical pathways.

Historical origins

As currently described in textbooks, the ten-step pathway of glycolysis has two separate phases, the ATP consumption or “investment” phase (reactions 1–5) and the ATP production or “earnings” phase (reactions 6–10)3. Glycolysis converts sugars into pyruvate. Pyruvate is oxidized in the TCA cycle, yielding the electrons that power oxidative phosphorylation. Alternatively, pyruvate is reduced in the cytosol by lactate dehydrogenase, oxidizing NAD and yielding lactate. Factors that drive pyruvate toward either oxidation or reduction include transport into the mitochondria4,5 the availability of oxygen, the abundance of intracellular ATP6, and the redox status of the cytosolic NAD pool7. Whether pyruvate is oxidized or reduced, the prior steps of glycolysis remain identical. Alternative to the generally accepted endpoint of pyruvate, other modern definitions of glycolysis align more with the traditional definition of fermentation, ending with the reduction of pyruvate to lactate8.

Aerobic, anaerobic, and the Pasteur, Warburg, and Crabtree effects

The terms “aerobic” and “anaerobic” were developed by Louis Pasteur around 1861, who identified that fermentation occurs in the absence of “air” (as later refined, oxygen) and slows in its presence. These experiments were critical in showing that fermentation (as well as “putrefaction”) was a process carried out by biological organisms, classified as either “air-living”, aerobic, or “without-air-living”, anaerobic (refs. 9,10, as described in ref. 11).

In The Pasteur Effect and Its Mechanism, Dixon later describes the “Pasteur effect” as inhibition of fermentation by oxygen, observable by the “action of oxygen in diminishing carbohydrate destruction and in suppressing or decreasing the accumulation of the products of anaerobic metabolism”, in which “the fundamental method of demonstrating the Pasteur effect in any tissue is to show that the rate of carbohydrate destruction in that tissue is less in the presence of oxygen than under anaerobic conditions”1. Later efforts by Buchner, followed by Harden and Young, then Embden, Meyerhof, Parnas, and others elucidated the reactions of “carbohydrate destruction” that are now familiar to us as glycolysis12. Put simply, the Pasteur effect is the phenomenon of fermentation running more slowly in the presence of oxygen. Extending this observation to assume that oxygen should suppress most or all fermentative glycolysis is misleading, but it seems to contribute to why “aerobic glycolysis” is assumed to be aberrant.

Warburg extends the Pasteur effect into cancer metabolism

Otto Warburg is now perhaps the most widely remembered of many scientists focused on the clinical relevance of the glucose-consuming pathway1. Warburg himself developed the term “glycolysis”, describing the “splitting” of glucose into lactate13, perhaps before a wider recognition of Meyerhof’s work demonstrating that microbial fermentation and mammalian glycolysis were the same pathway14. Additionally, because Warburg observed glycolysis largely in the context of tumor samples, in sometimes marked contrast to normal tissue samples, “aerobic glycolysis” became associated with cancer cell metabolism15, despite abundant evidence of aerobic glycolysis in non-cancer models16. Moreover, the tumor samples Warburg reported on happened to lack functional mitochondria, as he reported himself15,17. The failure of these samples to exhibit the Pasteur effect, and instead continue to rely on glycolysis as their sole source of ATP production, was caused by the lack of mitochondrial respiration and not cancer per se18,19. Despite this, the misconception that cancer cells are mostly or entirely glycolysis-reliant persists (Table 1).

Warburg’s new terms “aerobic glycolysis” and “anaerobic glycolysis” were nomenclature used to compare glucose flux to lactate in the absence and presence of atmospheric O2. In later work, he wrote, “We assume it is understood that tumor cells obtain the energy required for their existence in two ways: by respiration and by fermentation. In respiration, they burn organic materials to carbon dioxide and water; in fermentation, they split glucose to lactic acid”15. Use of “aerobic glycolysis” to compare rates of glycolysis measured under these specific conditions persisted well into the 1960s20. “Aerobic glycolysis” was not, as is often asserted, coined to describe the glycolysis displayed by tumor cells. This error remains common and perpetuates the idea that glycolysis in the presence of O2 is aberrant. Of note, the “Warburg effect” as put forth by Racker2 is a much broader set of assumptions than “aerobic glycolysis” and deserves its own, separate, conceptual interrogation21.

Historically, and as still used, “fermentation” ends with lactate (or in yeast, ethanol and sometimes acetate), while current textbook definitions of glycolysis end in pyruvate3. As used by Warburg, the ambiguity of the pathway endpoint in the term “aerobic glycolysis” was addressed by defining lactate as the endpoint. However, without a clear definition, the endpoint ambiguity becomes a problem as lactate and pyruvate have different relationships with substrate oxidation in the TCA cycle14. Lactate exits substrate oxidation, while pyruvate feeds it.

Crabtree inverts the Pasteur effect

Around the same time as Warburg’s seminal work, Herbert Crabtree is credited with demonstrating the “Crabtree effect”, the effective inverse of the Pasteur effect. Crabtree observed that respiration by mammalian tumor slices slowed when glucose was introduced to the incubation solution17. This finding has been recapitulated using current methods by many investigators, including us22–24. Interestingly, if the Pasteur effect can be (misleadingly) interpreted as a “preference” by cells for respiration, the same logic applies equally to the Crabtree effect. This would lead to the opposite interpretation of a “preference” for glycolysis, as cells readily switch part of their ATP production from respiration to glycolysis if a fermentable sugar is introduced.

Crabtree also found that in addition to glycolysis, many tumors exhibited substantial rates of respiration, with high variability across samples25. This finding has been extensively supported26–31, even in cells described as exhibiting the “Warburg effect”32,33. These findings further weaken assertions that glycolysis operating concurrently with respiration is unusual, or that transformed cells do not rely, sometimes substantially, on oxidative phosphorylation.

Confusion in the modern meaning and application of “aerobic glycolysis”

Table 1 helps to illustrate that while definitions of “aerobic glycolysis” are almost unique to each paper, they generally assert “preferred” or “rapid” glycolysis despite the presence of readily available oxygen. Different pathway endpoints (lactate, pyruvate, or other upstream intermediates) and subjective assertions about “preferred” and “rapid” confound a consistent definition (Fig. 1 and Table 1) and underlie many, often conflicting, models34–41.

Most definitions of “aerobic glycolysis” still suggest that any substantial rate of glycolysis in the presence of readily available oxygen is paradoxical or aberrant16. This view carries several unsupportable assumptions. First, that cells “prefer” to meet ATP demand through respiration when oxygen is available, per the Pasteur effect. Second, glycolysis is intrinsically “less efficient” because the stoichiometric ATP yield per glucose is lower. Third, that glycolysis operating under aerobic conditions is biologically unfavorable, presumably because of this stoichiometric inefficiency. Lastly, normal cells in aerobic conditions suppress glycolysis unless respiration is impaired.

Aerobic glycolysis is normal

As early as 1929, Crabtree noted that “aerobic glycolysis is not a specific feature of tumor tissue”17. Glycolysis in the presence of O2 has been more recently demonstrated both in vitro and in vivo, in both normal and transformed samples and cell lines33,42–45. Different models propose aerobic glycolysis as a feature of cancer metabolism, including decreased expression of mitochondrial H+-ATP synthase46, increased demand for biosynthetic intermediates to support cellular proliferation47, and demand for oxidized NAD35. However, these and other models48 coalesce not around transformed cells, but simply high energy demand.

Rapid proliferation is associated with high energy demand

Glycolysis, both as a source of ATP and of carbon intermediates, is proposed as a requirement or at least substantial facilitator of rapid proliferation in both normal and transformed cells41,44,49,50. While it may be that glycolysis is noticeable in these conditions, it is rarely shown that the proportion of glycolytic ATP production is greater than before the demand was imposed. Without that, there is no evidence for a“preference” at all, but could simply be increased ATP demand driving both respiration and glycolysis faster. Other proposed drivers of aerobic glycolysis include diffusion-limited oxidative ATP to cytosolic ATP consumers, which then require glycolytic ATP to operate42,49,51.

Glycolysis-dependent oxidative phosphorylation is routine in neural tissue

Glycolysis in neurons is particularly poorly suited to the assumptions of “aerobic glycolysis”, where glucose is a major source of pyruvate for the TCA cycle and oxidative phosphorylation52. In addition, lactate is a significant substrate, and is shuttled between astrocytes and neurons53, violating the assumptions of aerobic glycolysis54. Lactate itself has emerged as an important metabolic intermediate rather than a waste product55–57. For these and other reasons, describing neuronal metabolism as “aerobic glycolysis” has been criticized58–61.

Different measurement techniques capture different groups of reactions in the glycolysis pathway

Measurements associated with glycolysis do not all have the same relationship to the pathway (Fig. 1A). Broadly, measuring the input of glucose uptake captures all downstream fluxes, while measuring the fermentative output of lactate only captures fermentation and misses all fluxes of intermediates that exit glycolysis upstream of lactate. Collapsing these different measurements all into “glycolysis” is therefore another source of confusion.

Extracellular metabolites: H+ and lactate

The Warburg manometer is a specialized instrument designed to measure gas partial pressures related to biological activities. Glycolysis could be inferred by the Crabtree effect17. To couple fermentative glycolysis more directly to a gas partial pressure, bicarbonate was added, which was converted to CO2 proportional to the medium acidification by lactic acid-derived H+17,62,63. Using this system, Crabtree defined aerobic glycolysis as “deducting the measured respiration from the measured total CO2 evolved through the operation of the combined respiratory and glycolytic processes. The assumption has been made by Warburg and others that the respiratory quotient is unity, under the conditions of the manometric experiment”17,64. In other words, this method detected glycolysis by acidification, linked H+ to CO2 for manometric measurement, and subtracted respiratory CO2 from the total, defining glycolytic flux to lactate. We note the essential recapitulation of this approach performed using the Seahorse XF Analyzer24, highlighting the applicability of this long-standing conceptual framework to modern bioenergetic analysis. In this system, pH-sensitive and O2-sensitive fluorescent probes are combined, allowing simultaneous detection of H+ and O2 fluxes30 that can be used to estimate ATP production rates24. The simultaneous measurement of these fluxes represents a substantial advance in the ability to interpret changes to measured rates associated with cellular energy turnover, because it allows distinction of changes to total cellular energy production. All glucose that runs to lactate will be measured by this approach. Glycolytic fluxes to pentose phosphate and TCA cycles are not directly measured by this method. For definitions of aerobic glycolysis that propose high carbon flux to the pentose phosphate pathway, these methods would not be suitable without careful design to isolate this flux.

Lactate derived from glucose fermentation can also be readily measured. In cell culture, it stably accumulates in conditioned sample medium, and quantitative measurement is easily made using colorimetric or luminescent detection of NADH65. Like H+ detection using the Warburg manometer, measuring lactate to monitor glycolysis omits any fluxes of glycolysis intermediates to other pathways upstream of lactate dehydrogenase.

Glucose uptake

Glucose uptake into cultured cells can be monitored using metabolites like 2-deoxy-D-glucose (2-DG) and -2-deoxy-2-18F fluoro-D-glucose (18F-FDG). These species are readily transported into the cell and phosphorylated by hexokinase. The absence of the 2-hydroxyl group prevents conversion by phosphoglucoisomerase (PGI) and promotes PGI inhibition66. The phosphorylated intermediates are poor substrates for both glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase and phosphoglucomutase67. Thus, they are trapped in the cell by phosphorylation, accumulate with little to no downstream conversion, and can be readily monitored using, e.g., spectrophotometry or positron emission tomography (PET)68. Energetically, the initial phosphorylation step consumes ATP without any downstream ATP production, acting as a transient source of energy demand until PGI is fully inhibited (or, in theory, until intracellular phosphate is depleted69).

Many tumors display increased glucose uptake relative to surrounding tissue, making PET an effective diagnostic tool for cancer detection in vivo. In fact, PET-based cancer screening is a likely factor in the renewed interest in the “Warburg effect” as a marker of cancer metabolism70, which the detection of “increased glycolysis” appears to validate. However, as addressed above, this finding alone cannot distinguish between high energy demand overall, and a shift in the partitioning of ATP production toward glycolysis. This ambiguity leads to many tumors being described by the “Warburg effect” without evidence for a “preference for” or “shift toward” glycolysis.

Metabolic control unifies the rates of glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation

Glycolysis is one of two shared pathways of total cellular ATP production, and the only one that converts sugars into pyruvate. ATP production is tightly regulated in most cells. Understood this way, glycolysis operating under conditions of abundant O2 is not pathological or paradoxical but simply a branch of ATP generation (Fig. 1B). It operates when glycolysis substrates are available, and is co-regulated with oxidative phosphorylation to match the rate of existing ATP demand71,72. The Pasteur and Crabtree effects taken together support this shared operation by showing that when each pathway of ATP production runs alone to meet a given demand, its rate is faster than when both pathways share production (Fig. 1C)24,73. How ATP production is a function of their sensitivity to intracellular ATP, and whose control is distributed across the elements of this branched pathway71.

What ultimately controls the rate of glycolysis? On the timescale of seconds to minutes, the mechanisms of control are primarily kinetic, and metabolic control analysis (MCA) can therefore be used to address this question74,75. Whether ATP turnover, carbon flux, or redox homeostasis, MCA allows restatement of this question to determine how much each of these processes controls glycolysis under given conditions. Recognizing that control of glycolysis is distributed across the ATP-generating system, and quantifying this control, could resolve persistent misconceptions about the operation of glycolysis in the presence of O2.

On timescales longer than these, glycolytic metabolites, particularly lactate, have been shown to act as signals that influence transcription, translation, and cellular remodeling76,77. These findings underscore that accumulation of lactate can have downstream effects over prolonged timescales, independent of how the operation of glycolysis is defined.

Conclusion

The concept of “aerobic glycolysis” as currently used is confounded by ambiguity and contradictory assumptions. Its current use departs from the original experimental meaning of quantitatively comparing rates of glycolysis with and without concurrent respiration. Like others before us60, we conclude that “aerobic glycolysis” as currently used is often misleading and ambiguous, limiting our conceptual power and imagination. These weaknesses can be overcome by using metabolic control principles to interrogate the rate of glycolysis as a function of its energy, redox, and carbon flows. In this way, we can move beyond ambiguous labels toward a quantitative, integrated understanding of glycolysis as the central pathway through which sugars are transformed into metabolites, energy, and signals that sustain cellular functions and shape responses to both acute and long-term environmental changes.
