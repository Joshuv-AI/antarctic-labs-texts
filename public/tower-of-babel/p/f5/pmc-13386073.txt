Case of Aicardi-Goutières syndrome diagnosed in adulthood on whole-genome sequencing
Authors: Lewis Cobb, Jayant Kumar, Amit Roy, Sarah Walsh, Una-Marie Sheerin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground: Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (AGS) is a neurogenetic leukoencephalopathy that typically manifests within the first 6 months of life. Classically, AGS presents with an early encephalopathic episode characterised by feeding difficulties, irritability and psychomotor regression or delay. Cutaneous lesions affecting the extremities and epilepsy are common, with symptoms usually evolving over weeks to months before stabilising. Milder phenotypes have been described, often demonstrating relative preservation of language and cognitive function.Case presentation: Here, we report a case of a 31-year-old woman with compound heterozygous variants in the ADAR gene: (GRCh38) NM_001111.4:c.577C>G p.(Pro193Ala) and (GRCh38) NM_001111.4:c.1111_1112del p.(Ser371Cysfs*3), consistent with AGS. The developmental history revealed normal early motor milestones. At age 3, she developed progressive ataxia and hypotonia. Over time, she exhibited spasticity, dystonia and learning difficulties, along with significant cardiac valvular calcification.Conclusions: The patient was recently referred for consideration of treatment with baricitinib, a selective JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor. Introduction

Aicardi-Goutières syndrome (AGS) is a genetically heterogeneous, monogenic type I interferonopathy caused by pathogenic variants across multiple genes, including TREX1, RNASEH2B, ADAR, IFIH1 and others, involved in nucleic acid metabolism and sensing pathways, resulting in inappropriate activation of innate immune signalling and chronic upregulation of interferon-stimulated genes. It is most commonly inherited in an autosomal recessive pattern, although autosomal dominant variants have been reported.1

AGS typically presents within the first year of life, often following a brief period of normal development. A cardinal encephalopathic episode may include feeding difficulties, irritability and psychomotor regression. Common clinical features in paediatric cases include chilblain-like skin lesions, spasticity, dystonia, impaired motor skills and intellectual disability. Typical neuroimaging findings include basal ganglia calcifications, most reliably detected on brain CT, white matter signal abnormalities on MRI and elevated interferon alpha (IFN-α) levels and lymphocytosis in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF). While classical AGS typically presents in infancy, late-onset cases are increasingly recognised and are defined by neurological symptoms after the age of 1 year. These individuals may exhibit spastic paraparesis or dystonia but with relatively preserved cognition and communication as well as subtle abnormalities or normal brain imaging. Given the variability in presentation, effective management strategies are crucial, particularly targeted immunomodulation.

The management of AGS is multidisciplinary, combining supportive care tailored to individual symptoms. Targeted immunomodulation should be considered where appropriate. JAK inhibitors (eg, Baricitinib) are used in AGS as it is a type I interferonopathy. In this condition, chronic, inappropriate interferon signalling drives inflammation, a process mediated through the JAK–STAT pathway. By inhibiting JAKs, these drugs aim to reduce the interferon-stimulated gene response, thereby reducing interferon-mediated clinical features. The literature to date suggests benefit is often more apparent for systemic and cutaneous manifestations, whereas reversal of established neurological impairment is less consistent, likely reflecting irreversible early injury and treatment timing.2

Case report

A 31-year-old woman was referred to the adult neurology clinic for consideration of a unifying diagnosis of severe cardiac valvular calcification and neurological features of spasticity and dystonia. Our patient was born at term and displayed normal early developmental milestones until the age of 3 when she developed an ataxic gait and generalised hypotonia. At 11 years of age, she developed dyspnoea with presyncope; on examination she was noted to have a heart murmur and echocardiogram revealed severe calcified mitral stenosis, aortic stenosis and moderate mitral valve regurgitation requiring aortic valve, mitral valve and aortic root replacement. At the age of 12 years, she required surgery for a hip subluxation. A dual-chamber pacemaker was inserted in adulthood due to the development of complete heart block. Her parents were not consanguineous and there is no history of neurological disorders in her wider family. Despite extensive investigation in childhood, no unifying diagnosis was made. The only abnormal finding was a nerve conduction study showing an axonal sensory neuropathy. No clinical improvement was observed with L-DOPA challenge or pyridoxine trial.

Examination in adulthood revealed normal eye movements with no nystagmus, dystonic facies and dysarthric speech. Upper limb examination revealed a bilateral spastic catch, normal power, brisk reflexes and normal sensation. There was asymmetrical finger-nose dysmetria and dystonic posturing of the arms. In the lower limbs, there was increased tone bilaterally, mild pyramidal weakness, normal reflexes and bilateral heel-shin ataxia. There was dystonia of the right foot with intortion during walking. She required a wheelchair to mobilise. Cutaneous findings were reviewed by a consultant dermatologist and revealed perniotic, erythematous plaques on the dorsal aspect of both hands, overlying the metacarpophalangeal, proximal interphalangeal and distal interphalangeal joints. There was no epidermal change. There were no other cutaneous stigmata of dermatomyositis. While there are differential diagnoses for this presentation, the clinical features were consistent with cutaneous findings of AGS (figure 1).

Brain MRI was performed when the patient was 31 years of age, which was re-reviewed once the diagnosis of AGS was being considered. Although not reported initially, it confirmed subtle presumed calcification in the basal ganglia (online supplemental figure 1).

Genetic testing using whole-genome sequencing was performed (Genomics England panel R55 childhood-onset hereditary ataxia and cerebellar anomalies panel) with the suspicion that the patient may have AGS. This showed compound heterozygous variants in the ADAR gene: (GRCh38) NM_001111.4:c.577C>G p.(Pro193Ala) and (GRCh38) NM_001111.4:c.1111_1112del p.(Ser371Cysfs*3). Genetic phase was established by parental genetic testing, confirming these variants were in trans, with each parent being a heterozygous carrier. Genetic test results confirmed a molecular diagnosis of AGS. CSF and plasma interferon testing were not required because of the positive genetic test results. Following molecular diagnosis, the patient has been referred for consideration of starting Baricitinib, a JAK1/JAK2 inhibitor.

Discussion

This case represents a late-onset spastic–dystonic presentation of ADAR-related AGS. Although ADAR variants are typically associated with a bilateral striatal necrosis spectrum, there was no radiological evidence of striatal necrosis in this patient.

Cerebral calcification is the most common radiological finding in AGS and is typically considered a diagnostic criterion (excluding genetic diagnosis). In a study of 110 patients with AGS, 90.9% had brain calcifications.3 CT is the most sensitive imaging modality for detecting calcification. In patients who have not had a CT brain, MRI sequences sensitive to detection of mineralisation, for example, susceptibility-weighted images and GRE sequences should be performed and reviewed carefully to detect mineralisation. A literature search revealed two patients with AGS without intracerebral calcification—one 3-year-old female with a RNASEH2B pathogenic variant: homozygous NM_024570.4:c.529G>A (p.Ala177Thr)4 and a 6-year-old male with a biallelic TREX1 pathogenic variant: NM_033629.5: c.868_885del: Pro290_Ala295del.5 Two other cases (with RNASEH2B pathogenic variants) have been recorded but developed cerebral calcification later in childhood.6

Interestingly, while cerebral calcification is a hallmark of the disorder, our patient presented with severe cardiac valvular calcification, an extracerebral manifestation that further expands the phenotypic spectrum of AGS. Crow et al reported three individuals with biallelic ADAR variants and progressive valvular calcification and recommended screening echocardiography to facilitate early detection of cardiac involvement. This case provides further support for incorporating baseline and longitudinal echocardiographic surveillance into follow-up, as valvular calcification may progress to haemodynamically significant stenosis and/or regurgitation with implications for counselling and prognosis, including risk of heart failure. The pathophysiological basis remains incompletely understood but is thought to reflect chronic type I interferon-mediated inflammation and tissue remodelling, predisposing to dystrophic mineralisation of valve tissue over time.7

This case report highlights the importance of considering AGS in individuals who may not have been considered to have AGS due to an atypical phenotype as well as those lacking obvious brain calcification. It has been hypothesised that the ADAR phenotype may have a later onset presentation with milder features and a more stable course,8 our case gives further credence to this.

This case exemplifies the diagnostic power of whole-genome sequencing in uncovering rare, atypical presentations of genetic disorders, especially when traditional diagnostic pathways have been exhausted. The hypothesis-free approach of WGS allowed for a unifying molecular diagnosis years after symptom onset, highlighting its utility in complex neurogenetic conditions and has opened the option for a new therapeutic drug.
