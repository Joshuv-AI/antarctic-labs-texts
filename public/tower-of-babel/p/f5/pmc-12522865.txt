The neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio: a superior inflammatory marker predicting poor prognosis in IgA nephropathy
Authors: Youxia Liu, Huyan Yu, Yi Luo, Hongfen Li, Fanghao Wang, Yue Xing, Zhanfei Wu, Wenying Li, Junya Jia, Tiekun Yan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveImmunoglobulin A nephropathy (IgAN) is a chronic immune-mediated inflammatory disorder, and a substantial body of research indicates that the activation of inflammatory responses plays a pivotal role in its onset and progression. We conducted a comprehensive assessment of the influence of systemic inflammatory markers, specifically the neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR), and the systemic immune-inflammation (SII), on the phenotypic manifestations and disease progression of IgAN.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted, involving 559 patients who underwent regular follow-up evaluations spanning a minimum of one year. The utility of these inflammatory indicators in predicting IgAN progression was quantified using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC). The kidney progression event was defined as a 30% estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) decline or end-stage kidney disease or death. A prognostic model was developed using Cox proportional hazards regression. Circulating levels of galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), factor B, and mannose-binding lectin (MBL) were quantitatively determined through enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA).ResultsAmong the investigated markers, NLR demonstrated the highest prognostic utility, with an AUROC of 0.622 (p < 0.001) at a cutoff value of 2.11. NMR and SII followed closely, exhibiting AUROCs of 0.611 (p < 0.001) and 0.581 (p = 0.006) respectively, at their respective cutoff points of 8.25 and 560.11. Conversely, LMR, PLR, and PAR yielded AUROCs below the statistical significance threshold (0.473, 0.532, and 0.556, respectively; all p > 0.05). Univariate Cox regression analysis revealed that elevated neutrophil counts, NMR, NLR, SII, urinary leukocyte levels, and severe Oxford C score were predictive of IgAN progression. Notably, NMR emerged as an independent risk factor for adverse renal outcomes in IgAN patients, even after adjusting for established risk factors, whereas NLR and SII lost statistical significance. Patients with high NMR displayed more pronounced clinical-pathological features and elevated Gd-IgA1 and factor B levels compared to those with low NMR, whereas no such correlation was observed between Gd-IgA1 and either NLR or SII.ConclusionsOur findings highlight the crucial role of NMR as an independent predictor of disease progression in IgAN, underscoring its utility in risk stratification for patients.Keywords: IgA nephropathy, Neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio, Gd-IgA1, Factor B, Composite kidney endpoint

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN), a globally prevalent primary glomerular disease [1], is characterized by a slowly progressive course, with approximately 30–40% of patients progressing to end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) within two decades of diagnosis [2]. Thus, the identification of IgAN patients at high risk of disease progression is of paramount importance.

Central to the pathogenesis of IgAN lies galactose-deficient IgA1 (Gd-IgA1), which acts as a pivotal initiator. In this intricate process, inflammatory responses and immune reactions play a crucial role in both the onset and progression of IgAN [3, 4]. Recently, attention has converged on novel inflammatory markers derived from routine hemogram tests, which offer a promising avenue for disease monitoring. Cellular components such as neutrophils, monocytes, lymphocytes, and platelets are well-established contributors to inflammatory processes and are integral to both innate and adaptive immune systems [5–8]. Consequently, ratios derived from these cell types, including the neutrophil-to-monocyte ratio (NMR), lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), platelet-to-lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and platelet-to-albumin ratio (PAR), have emerged as simple, cost-effective, and non-invasive inflammatory markers across various disease landscapes, including rheumatoid arthritis [9], chronic kidney disease [10–12], and acute kidney injury [13–15]. The Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII), a composite marker formulated by Hu et al. in 2014 that integrates lymphocyte, neutrophil, and platelet counts for a comprehensive assessment of systemic inflammation, was originally proposed in oncology [16]. Similar to the PLR and NLR, the SII is also an indicator obtained from routine blood counts and has subsequently been applied in nephrology, demonstrating its effectiveness as a prognostic factor in diseases driven by inflammation, such as anti-neutrophil cytoplasmic antibodies associated vasculitis (AAV) [17, 18]. Recent studies have reported promising findings, indicating that NLR, PAR, and PLR could serve as valuable predictors of disease severity and progression in IgAN [19–21]. However, many of these studies have been limited by relatively small sample sizes, leading to only modest performance in general research. Furthermore, none have directly compared the efficacy of these markers in predicting the progression of IgAN.

Given the need for cost-effective, highly sensitive, and easily reproducible biomarkers that can accurately predict outcomes and track disease progression in IgAN patients, this study aims to assess the utility of novel inflammatory markers-NMR, NLR, SII, LMR, PLR, and PAR-by assessing their associations with disease activity and progression in IgAN. To our knowledge, this is the first comprehensive study to directly compare the predictive value of these inflammatory markers in IgAN, thereby facilitating the identification of optimal biomarkers for disease monitoring and risk stratification.

Methods

Study patients

We systematically evaluated a cohort of patients diagnosed with IgAN in Tianjin Medical University General Hospital. Between May 2010 and April 2020, our investigation encompassed 728 individuals with biopsy-proven primary IgAN who had undergone a follow-up period exceeding 12 months. Diagnosis of IgAN was on the basis of the presence of dominant of IgA deposition in the mesangial area by immunofluorescence (IF) microscopy. Participants with missing follow-up data or incomplete blood count test data were excluded.

A comprehensive set of demographic and clinical parameters were collected at the time of kidney biopsy, including sex, age, diabetic status, hypertension status, systolic (SBP) and diastolic (DBP) blood pressures, hematological indices (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, platelets, hemoglobin), inflammatory markers (erythrocyte sedimentation rate, C-reactive protein, anti-streptolysin O), coagulation factors (partial thromboplastin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, fibrinogen, D-Dimer), hepatic and renal function indices (albumin, serum creatinine, eGFR, uric acid), lipid profiles (total cholesterol, triglycerides, high density lipoprotein, low density lipoprotein), fasting plasma glucose, and serum immunoglobulin levels (IgA, IgG, IgM, IgE) along with complement factors (C3, C4). Additionally, 24-hour urine protein excretion, urinary red and white blood cell counts were recorded.

The MEST-C scoring system (M for mesangial hypercellularity, E for endocapillary hypercellularity, S for segmental glomerulosclerosis, T for tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis, and C for crescents) under the Oxford classification was utilized to assess pathological lesions in IgAN [22]. The eGFR was calculated via the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) formula [23]

Definitions

The SII at the time of diagnosis was determined utilizing the formula: (Platelet Count × Neutrophil Count)/Lymphocyte Count, where all components were measured at the diagnosis point. Immunosuppressive therapy, defined as the utilization of steroids and/or immunosuppressive agents such as cyclophosphamide, cyclosporine, or mycophenolate mofetil, was initiated subsequent to the biopsy. The composite kidney endpoint was comprehensively defined to encompass any of the following events: a 30% decrease in the eGFR or the onset of end-stage kidney disease (ESKD) characterized by an eGFR below 15 ml/min per 1.73 m2, or death.

Gd-IgA1, factor B and MBL detection

EDTA-anticoagulated plasma samples were meticulously collected and promptly aliquoted for storage at −180 °C on the day of renal biopsy. To investigate the immune-mediated mechanisms underlying IgAN, we quantified the circulating levels of Gd-IgA1, as well as the complement system components B factor and mannose-binding lectin (MBL), in a cohort of 144 IgAN patients. This was achieved through the utilization of an enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) with commercial kits sourced from IBL (Fujioka, Japan) for Gd-IgA1 detection and Abcam (Cambridge, UK) for B factor and MBL measurements. All assays were conducted in strict adherence to the manufacturers’ protocols, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the obtained results.

Statistical analysis

To establish optimal cut-off values for NMR, NLR, SII, LMR, PLR, and PAR, the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC) was calculated. The optimal cutoff points to maximize the sensitivity and specificity of the test were calculated using Youden Index. For normally distributed continuous variables, mean values with their respective standard deviations (SD) were reported and compared using the Student’s t-test. Non-normally distributed data were expressed as medians with interquartile ranges and analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Dichotomous variables were presented as counts and percentages, with comparisons made using the Chi-squared (χ2) test. Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated to assess the predictive value of these biomarkers for survival free from the composite kidney endpoint, and differences in survival distributions were evaluated using the log-rank test. To further investigate the independent prognostic significance of the identified biomarkers, both univariable and multivariable Cox proportional hazards models were employed. Data processing and analysis were executed using SPSS software version 25.0. The consistency of treatment effect with respect to the composite kidney endpoint was evaluated across subgroups defined according to gender, age, hypertension, anemia, albumin, baseline proteinuria, CKD stage, and Oxford Classification using R 3.6.3. The figures were generated using GraphPad Prism version 8.0 and R 3.6.3. A two-tailed p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Predictive value of NMR, NLR, SII, LMR, PLR and PAR for IgAN progression

Rigorous exclusion criteria were applied to exclude patients with insufficient clinical records (N = 169), resulting in a final cohort of 559 patients for subsequent analysis (depicted in Fig. 1). The baseline characteristics of these patients were shown in Table 1. We performed an analysis of the AUROC to rigorously determine the predictive ability of relevant indicators for the composite renal endpoint (Fig. 2). NLR cutoff value 2.11 yielded the highest AUROC (0.622, 95% CI: 0.564 to 0.680, p < 0.001). Followed by NMR and SII, these two markers cutoff values of 8.25 and 560.11 resulted in AUROCs of 0.611 (0.554 to 0.668, p < 0.001) and 0.581 (0.521 to 0.641, p = 0.006), respectively. Additionally, LMR, PLR, and PAR yielded AUROCs of 0.473, 0.532, and 0.556, respectively. These three markers revealed no statistically significant superiority in their predictive performance (all p > 0.05). Furthermore, previous studies have collectively demonstrated that these three markers-NLR, NMR, and SII-are associated with the progression of IgAN. Consequently, these markers were included in the subsequent analysis.

Correlation between NMR with IgAN progression

To more clearly illustrate the complex relationships between inflammatory markers and prognosis in IgAN, we conducted a comprehensive assessment of the prognostic impact of various inflammatory and hematological markers. We employed univariate Cox proportional hazards models to evaluate the individual effects of leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, urine leukocytes, and derived ratios (NMR, NLR, PLR), as well as the presence of pathological E1 lesions and C1–2 lesions (Table 2). The results indicated that elevated neutrophils, NMR, NLR, SII, urine leukocytes, and C1–2 were associated with the progression of IgAN. NMR, NLR, and SII were linked to kidney outcomes whether treated as continuous or categorical variables.

Correlation between NMR with key clinicopathological indicators

In exploring the intricate correlations between NMR and IgAN disease manifestations, we divided IgAN patients into two distinct cohorts: one defined by elevated NMR levels (high NMR group) and the other by relatively lower NMR levels (low NMR group). The threshold for defining NMR, rigorously established using the maximally selected log-rank statistical method, was set at 8.25 (Table 4). In the high NMR group, there was a higher incidence of female patients (55.4% vs. 43.77%, p = 0.008) and hypertension (55.76% vs. 39.86%, p < 0.001). Additionally, this group exhibited elevated leukocyte counts (7.613 ± 2.03410^9/L vs. 6.615 ± 1.83310^9 /L, p < 0.001), neutrophil counts (4.986 ± 1.59310^9/L vs. 3.661 ± 1.26710^9/L, p < 0.001), ESR (24 mm/h vs. 17 mm/h, p = 0.016), serum creatinine (87.000 μmol/L vs. 83.000 μmol/L, p = 0.016), serum C3 levels (94.516 ± 20.952 mg/dL vs. 90.345 ± 18.860 mg/dL, p = 0.017), serum C4 levels [23.900 (19.050, 27.450) mg/dL vs. 22.000 (18.775, 26.125) mg/dL, p = 0.022], and UTP [1508.800 (750.000, 2930.000) mg/24 h vs. 1184.700 (693.600, 2128.000) mg/24 h, p = 0.012]. Conversely, the high NMR group displayed lower lymphocyte counts (1.989 ± 0.67410^9/L vs. 2.170 ± 0.66610^9/L, p = 0.002), monocyte counts (0.455 ± 0.16010^9/L vs. 0.635 ± 0.63710^9/L, p < 0.0001) and hemoglobin levels (128.127 ± 19.245 g/L vs. 132.206 ± 20.478 g/L, p = 0.016). These patients also had a lower eGFR (86.534 ml/min per 1.73 m2 vs. 94.396 ml/min per 1.73 m2, p = 0.003) and lower IgG levels (1018.9 ± 256.605 mg/dL vs. 1071.657 ± 274.546 mg/dL, p = 0.027). Regarding the Oxford classification of renal pathology, the high NMR group had a significantly higher percentage of M1 (p = 0.002), S1 (p < 0.001), and T2 (p = 0.023) scores compared to the low NMR group. There were no difference in age (p = 0.647), presence of DM (p = 0.447), PT (p = 0.213), APTT (p = 0.073), FIB (p = 0.070), D-D (p = 0.051), albumin (p = 0.350), uric acid (p = 0.071), FPG (p = 0.075), TC (p = 0.262), TG (p = 0.248), HDL (p = 0.765), LDL (p = 0.177), serum IgA (p = 0.828), IgM (p = 0.334), IgE (p = 0.310), CRP (p = 0.666), ASO (p = 0.565), URBC (p = 0.125) and UWBC (p = 0.178), respectively. Additionally, there were no significant differences in the utilization rate of RAASi (p = 0.225) and immunosuppressive agents (p = 0.778). Notably, the incidence of the composite renal outcome was significantly higher in the high NMR group compared to the other group (28.78% vs. 14.95%, p < 0.001). All details are depicted in Table 5.

Correlation between NMR with Gd-IgA1, B levels and MBL

Given the correlation between elevated inflammatory markers and poor kidney outcomes, we proceeded to compare the circulating levels of Gd-IgA1, factor B, and MBL between groups with high and low levels of NMR, NLR, and SII (Table 6). Our findings revealed that patients with high NMR levels exhibited significantly higher Gd-IgA1 levels (13.705 ± 7.113 ug/ml vs. 11.182 ± 5.833 ug/ml, p = 0.041) and factor B levels (281.436 ± 139.386 pg/ml vs. 228.027 ± 98.411 pg/ml, p = 0.008) compared to those with low NMR levels. Additionally, higher levels of factor B were observed in patients with high NLR (288.030 ± 155.220 pg/ml vs. 227.632 ± 85.452 pg/ml, p = 0.003) and high SII (294.320 ± 160.808 pg/ml vs. 227.566 ± 84.088 pg/ml, p = 0.001), while the levels of Gd-IgA1 remained similar between these groups. Regarding complement MBL, patients in both high and low NMR, NLR, and SII groups exhibited similar levels. The association between NMR and Gd-IgA1 (R = 0.209, p = 0.012) and factor B (R = 0.169, p = 0.043) was displayed in Fig. 5.

Discussion

This study employed the AUROC curve and Cox regression models to compare established inflammatory markers. Among these markers, the NMR emerged as a pivotal independent prognostic factor, significantly predicting the progression of IgAN towards adverse outcome events. This discovery underscores the clinical utility of NMR in risk stratification and disease management strategies for IgAN patients.

Indeed, in recent years, there has been a burgeoning interest in elucidating the intricate interplay between inflammatory markers and the pathogenesis of diverse kidney diseases [25, 26]. In this study, we initially compared the performance of several markers NMR, NLR, SII, LMR, PLR, and PAR in predicting IgAN progression. We found that only NMR, NLR, and SII demonstrated good performance, with higher AUC values for predicting progression in IgAN. Furthermore, Furthermore, after statistically adjusting for the influence of well-established risk factors for kidney disease progression, only NMR remained significantly associated with IgAN progression. Additionally, we accounted for the influence of corticosteroids and other immunosuppressants, which possess potent anti-inflammatory effects. Previous studies have often highlighted NLR as the most effective marker for diagnosing and tracking the progression of IgAN [19, 24, 27]. In this study, after adjustments for such therapies, NLR and SII lost their significance, while NMR continued to stand out. This oversight suggests that NMR might be an undervalued yet critical biomarker for evaluating IgAN progression, even in the setting of immunosuppressive treatment.

Given the multifactorial nature of IgAN, it is plausible that multiple factors contribute to the phenotypic diversity and disease progression. Nevertheless, our subgroup analyses have consistently shown that elevated NMR levels are associated with the kidney disease progression across diverse IgAN patient subgroups. This correlation underscores the utility of NMR, which can be easily obtained through routine blood tests, highlighting its broad applicability and potential as a prognostic biomarker in IgAN.

In our study population, NE levels were clearly associated with disease progression, while MO and LY levels did not show such associations. Additionally, the use of NMR showed a significant improvement over NE alone, suggesting its potential as a more comprehensive biomarker in predicting disease progression. The migration of neutrophils from the peripheral blood to the kidneys represents a pivotal event in the pathogenesis of numerous renal diseases, with IgAN being no exception. In particular, the accumulation of neutrophils within crescentic glomeruli, a pathological hallmark of IgAN, has been implicated in the acceleration of disease progression [28]. Their ability to exacerbate kidney injury through the release of reactive oxygen species (ROS), myeloperoxidase (MPO), and proteolytic enzymes underscores their significant contribution to tissue damage and renal dysfunction [29–31]. In the context of IgAN, activated neutrophils have been shown to promote cell death and the production of endothelin-1 (ET-1) in glomerular mesangial cells. This process is mediated, in part, by the release of superoxide, a highly reactive form of oxygen that can cause oxidative stress and damage to cellular components [32]. Monocytes, the precursors of dendritic cells and tissue macrophages, can differentiate into alternatively activated macrophages [33]. These macrophages can mitigate inflammatory responses and promote tissue repair through the production of interleukin-10 (IL-10) and transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-β1) [34]. The dynamic between lymphocytes and monocytes often reflects antagonistic roles in various inflammatory contexts, suggesting that NMR might capture an imbalance among these immune cells that correlates with exacerbated inflammation and poorer prognosis in IgAN patients.

The clinical characteristics of IgAN patients vary significantly with different NMR levels. Our analysis reveals a clear pattern: patients with higher NMR levels exhibit a distinct clinical profile characterized by older age, female preponderance, higher prevalence of hypertension, elevated mean blood pressure, and more severe renal pathological lesions. Additionally, we have uncovered a novel aspect of this association: patients with high NMR levels also exhibited significantly elevated levels of Gd-IgA1 and factor B. However, no significant correlation was observed between Gd-IgA1 levels and either NLR or SII. These findings indicate a potential association between innate immune cells, adaptive immunity, and the activation of the alternative complement pathway. Neutrophils and monocytes, as key components of the innate immune system, are capable of secreting various cytokines and chemokines that can shape the adaptive immune response. Infections and inflammation of the upper respiratory or digestive tracts consistently increase interleukin-6 (IL-6) production. In turn, elevated IL-6 levels enhance the production of Gd-IgA1 in IgA1-secreting cell lines derived from patients with IgAN. Furthermore, the formation of immune complexes by Gd-IgA1 activates the complement system. Some evidence suggests that B cell activating factors, APRIL and BAFF, are crucially involved in the production of Gd-IgA in IgAN. However, the exact stimuli that trigger the release of APRIL and BAFF in IgAN have not been extensively investigated. Neutrophils are known to be a source of these factors and are key players in the immune response, involved in various inflammatory processes. Their activation and subsequent release of cytokines can exert significant effects on the surrounding microenvironment. Therefore, blocking APRIL production or activity presents a potential therapeutic approach to reduce circulating levels of Gd-IgA1 and associated immune complexes.

This study demonstrates two notable strengths: first, it incorporates a comprehensive set of markers, providing a multidimensional perspective; second, the subgroup analysis reveals the absence of significant interactions, which enhances the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Indeed, there are some limitations. Firstly, the retrospective design and single-center nature of the study limit the generalizability of the findings. The relatively small sample size may also have impacted the statistical power of the analysis. Furthermore, the mean follow-up time of 50 months, while providing some insight into the short- to medium-term outcomes of IgAN patients, is indeed relatively short for a disease known to progress slowly over time. Longer follow-up periods would allow for a more comprehensive assessment of the relationship between NMR levels and IgAN disease progression. Given these limitations, it is imperative that future studies address these issues by adopting a prospective design, enrolling larger patient cohorts from multiple centers, and implementing longer follow-up periods.

Conclusion

In summary, our findings highlight the critical role of NMR as a significant and independent risk factor for disease progression in IgAN patients. This revelation underscores the importance of considering NMR levels in the assessment and management of IgAN, potentially serving as a useful biomarker for disease severity and prognosis.
