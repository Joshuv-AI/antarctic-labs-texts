Knowledge, practice, and attitude of basic life support among schoolteachers in Saudi Arabia
Authors: Ziyad M. Alshahrani, Majed A. Alkahtani, Mohammed A. Almeshari, Faisal A. Turkestani, Mazen M. Homoud, Sultan A. Alshehri, Gokul G. Krishna, Layan Fahad Saaty, Jumana Rekan Aleasawi, Shuhd Qaid Mudhsh, Tulin Hamzah Rizq, Molok Muaamar Almekhlafi, Malak Mohammed Ghunaim, Ayedh D. Alahmari
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:Basic life support (BLS) is the critical lifesaving skill delivered in a case of cardiac arrest or respiratory depression. However, its awareness and practice among schoolteachers, often first responders in schools, remain underexplored. In Saudi Arabia, there is a growing recognition for the need for BLS training in educational institutions to ensure the safety of students and staff. The Saudi Ministry of Education is dedicated to ensuring every school has a safe environment and has set an objective to minimize school accidents.Method:A cross-sectional questionnaire was conducted to assess the knowledge, skills, and attitudes of BLS, among schoolteachers. Inclusion criteria were active teachers in any type of non-specialized schools (Governmental, Private, and International) in Saudi Arabia, aged 23 years or older.Results:The study comprised 587 participants, of which were 84 males (14.3%) and 503 females (85.7%). The age distribution revealed that the majority were aged 41–50 years (48.9%). A significant proportion, 89.4%, were employed in governmental schools, and 77.2% held a bachelor’s degree.Conclusion:The study revealed that schoolteachers in Saudi Arabia demonstrated inadequate knowledge of BLS, with 88.8% classified as having poor or very poor knowledge. Awareness of the BLS term abbreviation was significantly higher among trained participants (85.3%). In first response to an unresponsive person, BLS-trained participants (39.2%) performed significantly better than untrained ones (26.1%) (P = 0.001). The Ministry of Education should evaluate policy reforms to mandate BLS training as a compulsory component of a teacher’s certification.

Introduction

Basic Life Support (BLS) is an essential practice to save the lives of individuals. The first recorded successful application of BLS practice was in 1744 by Tossach.[1] The survival rates of out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA) ranges between 2% and 20% with 67 to 170 per 100,000 individuals annually.[2] Immediate delivery of effective cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) increases the survival rate and decreases morbidity among victims of cardiac arrest in emergency situations.[3] There are three essential steps to improve survival rates among OHCA victims, which are 1) early detection of cardiopulmonary depression, 2) prompt intervention with resuscitation, 3) and early use of defibrillation.[4] To implement these steps successfully, the first responder must be highly competent and acquires sufficient comprehension in providing such tasks.[5]

In public schools in Saudi Arabia, the first responders to medical emergencies are usually teachers. Thus, teachers must be skilled in performing BLS.[6] The health and well-being of public-school students are a top priority for the Saudi Ministry of Education. The ministry is dedicated to ensuring every school has a safe environment and has set an objective to minimize school accidents. According to the Saudi General Authority for Statistics, approximately 6.3 million students were enrolled in Saudi Arabian schools as of 2019, distributed across 43,233 nationwide. This significant student population presents considerable challenges in ensuring their health and safety. Many students are at risk of sudden cardiac arrest, a concern highlighted by Al Enizi.[7] During a typical school day, students may encounter both direct and indirect causes of cardiac arrest, including conditions such as cardiomyopathies, congenital heart disease, heart valve disease, myocarditis, heart attacks, hypoxia, and drug overdoses.[8] Food choking is deemed to be one of leading causes to cardiopulmonary arrest among school children.[9] In 2020, according to WHO, accidents and injuries incremented to 8.4 million among children and youth.[10] Therefore, there is a huge demand for competent, skillful, and well-trained teachers in each school to provide BLS to increase the survival rate of OHCA and decrease accidents and injuries in schools.[11]

Pervious, studies highlighted that teachers agreed with the statement that excellent first aid and CPR training are necessary to avoid school accidents.[671011]

The study aimed to investigate schoolteachers’ knowledge, attitude, and practice toward BLS across Saudi Arabia. Additionally, to assess socio-demographic factors that influence knowledge and skills among teachers.

Methods

Study design and recruitment

A cross-sectional study was conducted involving 587 schoolteachers aged 23 years and older, who were actively employed in governmental, private, or international schools across Saudi Arabia. Teachers who were retired, not actively teaching, or residing outside Saudi Arabia were excluded from the study.

Study tool

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire referenced from cross-sectional study research conducted in the Al-Qassim region[12]; and delivered online. The questionnaire comprised 23 questions, divided into three domains: knowledge domain assessing of first aid and CPR techniques, including familiarity with BLS protocols (8 questions). Practice (13 questions) practice of CPR techniques. Knowledge and practice were assessed with 23 questions; and scoring were as follow “Very poor” score <30; “Poor” >30 to 45%; “Average” >45% to 55%; “Good” >55% to 65%, “Very good” >65% to 75%, and “Excellent” >75%. Attitude toward the need for mandatory BLS training in schools and the perceived importance of first aid preparedness. The questionnaire was created using the Google Forms and distributed electronically via social media platforms to schoolteachers in Saudi Arabia.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for the study was granted from the institutional review board (RES-2024-0054). Participants filled out the questionnaire anonymously to maintain privacy. Electronic consent forms have been provided for participants to sign prior to filling out the survey. No identifiable data has been collected, and all data was stored on an encrypted and password-protected computer.

Data analysis

Data were presented as numbers and percentages for categorical variables, while medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) were used for continuous variables. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test, which indicated that the data were not normally distributed. Therefore, non-parametric tests, including the Mann-Whitney U test and Kruskal-Wallis’s test, were employed for group comparisons of continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. All statistical analyses were conducted using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software, version 28 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Results

The study included 587 participants, of which were 84 males (14.3%) and 503 females (85.7%). The age distribution revealed that the majority were aged 41–50 years (48.9%), followed by 31–40 years (27.3%). Most participants reported more than 11 years of experience 431 (73.4%), while 36 (6.1%) had less than 2 years of experience. A significant proportion 525 (89.4%), were employed in government schools, and 453 (77.2%) held at least a bachelor’s degree. Regarding BLS training, 382 (65.2%) participants reported having no previous training. Of those who received training, 106 (52%) did so in school, and 69 (33.8%) through the Saudi Red Crescent Authority. Additionally, 62 (30.4%) had their last BLS training within the past 6 months of issuing, and 98 (48%) had more than 2 years, respectively, [Table 1].

The majority of participants had limited knowledge in BLS, with 29.3% rated as “Very poor” and 59.5% as “Poor.” Only a small portion demonstrated higher levels of understanding, with 6.8% having “Average” knowledge and less than 5% rated as “Good,” “Very good,” or “Excellent” [Figure 1].

Shows the knowledge and practice of schoolteachers

A substantial proportion (28.8%) have not undergone BLS training due to uncertainty regarding training locations, while 26.9% cited time constraints as a barrier. Nevertheless, the majority (86.5%) expressed interest in additional training, with 57.4% motivated by a desire to prevent avoidable fatalities. Most participants (91.3%) indicated willingness to engage in a complimentary CPR course, and 62.2% supported the implementation of mandatory CPR training for teacher certification. Furthermore, 81.8% advocated for the presence of an AED in schools [Table 3].

Discussion

This study highlighted significant gaps in the knowledge, practice, and attitudes toward BLS among schoolteachers in Saudi Arabia. The findings underlined the substantial proportion of participants, who lack adequate training and awareness of BLS. Only 5% of participants demonstrated “Good” to “Excellent” knowledge, while the majority were rated as “Very poor” or “Poor.” 88.8% Regarding pervious training, 65.2% of teachers had no prior BLS training, underscored the lack of preparedness for emergencies and the urgent need for structured, mandatory training programs for schoolteachers.

Despite the overall low scores, the participants who had undergone prior BLS training had better scores in several domains, including the recognition of BLS abbreviations (85.3% vs. 73.6%, P = 0.001) and initial actions for unresponsive people (39.2% vs. 26.1%, P = 0.001). Nevertheless, even among trained individuals, there were still gaps in knowledge, and many failed to answer questions correctly, such as AED operation and chest compression. This emphasized the need for excellent and recurrent BLS training courses.

In a study conducted among Saudi population, the result highlighted that the knowledge of BLS efficacy is decreased significantly among individuals, who are not healthcare practitioners.[13] This align is consistent with prior researches conducted among schoolteachers in Saudi Arabia, which showed a lack of improvement in teachers’ BLS knowledge and skills deficiencies. In Al-Qassim, a study revealed that the BLS knowledge was low, as in the present study, with only a few participants showing good knowledge.[12] A questionnaire-based study conducted in Al-Madinah also suggested that the teachers were deficient in some of the vital BLS skills and had course-training disparities caused the absence of these skills in the teachers’ population.[6] Another study conducted in Al-Madinah revealed that the knowledge about pediatric first aid and management was also low.[14] A study in Taif City reported that while some BLS awareness existed, the actual physical application was still suboptimal,[15] as it is in the presented study. Furthermore, research conducted among nine Arab countries, including Saudi Arabia, showed the same training deficiencies, suggesting that the problem is not unique but widespread throughout the region.[16] These results support the necessity of providing extensive BLS awareness and training to the general population as recommended in Alshamrani et al.[17] Schools and geographic influences on BLS attitudes and training varied across Saudi Arabia due to the availability of the programs and the policies for different schools.

Most teachers had a positive attitude towards BLS training. Indeed, 86.5% showed interest in BLS, and 62.2% supported mandatory certification for teachers. However, the challenges continued to emerge. The most cited factors were time pressure (26.9%) and knowledge on where to get training (28.8%). However, the willingness to undergo free training (91.3%) shows that the participants support more BLS education which aligned with study conducted In Al-Qassim where teachers had a positive attitude toward BLS training, but the availability of training programs was a challenge to participate in Alahmed et al.[18] In a study conducted Al-Madinah, reported that there is no policy on the training of BLS in schools, which resulted in variation in the level of skills among educators.[19] In Taif, cultural barriers, including reluctance to attend co-sexual training sessions, affected training attendance.[20] Another study conducted across Arab countries revealed that cultural beliefs and resource constraints influenced the training program accessibility and BLS competency.[5] Teachers must complete compulsory BLS training to guarantee adequate emergency action in schools; this is evident from this study as knowledge deficiency was discovered. BLS skills among teachers diminishment persists over time, requiring refresher and periodic courses whereby teachers can maintain mastery of BLS.[21] Mandatory training would help schools achieve the same level of readiness across the educational system; those schools with the most vulnerable students would benefit from having educators who can confidently deal with emergencies and, therefore, possibly save lives.

The Saudi Ministry of Education could improve BLS training by implementing the regulation that requires schoolteachers to have BLS certification and regular training courses in the curriculum. Training options could be made available through cooperation with a health organization like the Saudi Red Crescent Authority to ensure a uniform training density across the regions.[22] These partnerships could assist in AED placement in schools and ongoing refresher courses for instructors on up-to-date lifesaving skills and knowledge.

With the current increased deployment of AEDs, particularly in learning institutions, there would be a huge improvement in handling cardiac arrest incidences given that AEDs may provide an instant lifesaving for first responders. The percentage obtained regarding the support of teachers for AED availability was 81.8%, which highlighted the recognition of teachers for this equipment on campus.[23] With staff education, AEDs may improve the number of students who survive sudden cardiac arrests in schools by increasing the likelihood of receiving appropriate care before the arrival of emergency health services.

Limitations to the study

Study limitations included non-probability convenience sampling, which may affect the findings since participants are selected from different regions of Saudi Arabia. Female participants were significantly higher than male; thus, the BLS knowledge, practice, and attitudes among male schoolteachers were not adequately examined. Such limitations highlighted the need for future research to obtain a larger, more randomized sample is needed to provide a precise reflection of the population. This approach will enhance the generalizability of the findings and provide an additional comprehensive understanding of the subject matter.

Conclusion

The study concluded that most schoolteachers possess poor or very poor knowledge and practice regarding BLS, despite expressing positive attitudes toward BLS training. While teachers with prior training exhibited better knowledge and skills, notable gaps persist among both trained and untrained groups. These findings underscored the necessity for mandatory, and continuous BLS training for schoolteachers, as well as the urgent need to enhance awareness and preparedness for medical emergencies within the educational sector.
