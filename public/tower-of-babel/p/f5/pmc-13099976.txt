The effects of human activity and snow cover on the distribution of mammals and terrestrial birds in the Altai Mountains under climate change
Authors: Xiaqiu Tao, Xuan Liu, Shaopeng Cui, Songkai Guo, Chunwang Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Altai Mountains, a complex mountain system of Central Asia, is particularly sensitive to global change. Under increasing human activities and continuing climate change, the range of animals may show expansion or contraction. In this study, we evaluated and predicted the distribution dynamics of 27 animal species and the resulted change of species richness in the Altai Mountains by using MaxEnt model in the current and future periods. The results show that most species are predicted to mainly distribute in the northwest of the Altai Mountains under current conditions. In the future, habitats located in the central region may be largely lost. Most species tend to shift their ranges towards higher altitudes or latitudes. Human activities, snow cover and precipitation of coldest quarter are the most important predictors explaining the potential distributions of most species. As global climate change continues to alter potentially suitable habitats, we recommend to establish a transboundary protected area across the four countries (China, Kazakhstan, Mongolia and Russia) in the central region of the Altai Mountains. Additionally, we suggest reducing potential anthropogenic impacts on wildlife and their habitats by regulating human activities.Subject terms: Conservation biology, Climate-change ecology

Introduction

Climate change has been recognized as one of the major threats to biodiversity and ecosystem1. It severely affects the geographical distribution, population size, and behavior of wild animals2, especially in high mountain ecosystems3. These steep landscapes are particularly sensitive to changes in temperature and precipitation due to their extreme relief and steep gradients4. Furthermore, mountain animals exhibit higher rates of endemism compared to those in other regions, making them more vulnerable to shifting climatic conditions5.

It was suggested that snow cover plays an important role in alpine and subalpine environments6,7. The depth, duration, and spatial distribution of snow determine winter microclimates, access to forage and exposure to predators, and thus influence species distributions, taxonomic richness, and functional diversity of mammals and terrestrial birds8,9. Snow can create thermally buffered subnivian refugia10, but it can also impose movement costs11,12, alter predator-prey interactions13,14, and generate camouflage mismatches15,16 when snow duration declines. Recent studies have shown that the number of snow cover days also has a significant impact on species distribution patterns17,18.

With the development of economy and society, regional human activities have increased, leading to growing concerns about their ecological consequences19. Over the past few centuries, human activities have been a major driver of altering species distribution patterns and leading to the extinction of species in many regions20. In Central Asia, livestock grazing, a common human activity, has drawn increasing attention due to a dramatic increase in livestock and its potential impact on wild animal communities21. The detrimental effects of livestock encroachment and competition on wildlife have been documented22,23. Endangered species are particularly vulnerable to human activities because they are more sensitive to environmental changes than common species24.

The Altai Mountains are a typical transboundary region in Central Asia, spanning the borders of China, Kazakhstan, Mongolia, and Russia. It is listed as a “Global 200” priority ecological ecoregion for conservation, which has representative and diverse ecosystems and excels in providing ecosystem services such as biodiversity maintenance25. As a complex mountain system of Central Asia, the Altai Mountains are particularly sensitive to climate change26, with one of the highest vertical isotherm shift velocities among mountain regions27. Snow cover is a dominant landscape feature in the Altai Mountains from November to April28,29. Previous studies have shown that since the 1960s, climate change has led to a significant decrease in the number of snow cover days, while snow depth (SD) has shown a significant increase30,31. High-mountain regions experience accelerated physical and biological change due to cryosphere loss and elevation-dependent warming, with cascading impacts on biodiversity and resource use3,32. However, administrative borders act as static barriers that may fail to account for species shifting their ranges in response to climate change33. Current conservation strategies often lack a predictive understanding of where transboundary biodiversity hotspots will shift under future warming. Therefore, in transboundary mountain ranges such as the Altai, these sensitivities intersect with heterogeneous national land-use and protection regimes, underscoring the need for coordinated cross-border management.

The Maximum Entropy (MaxEnt) model is a correlative machine learning model with a wide range of applications in ecology and suitability studies, which can model data from presence-only records34. The results of the MaxEnt model are easy to interpret and serve as indicators of species vulnerability caused by distribution change35. Due to its advantages, such as ease of use, the ability to generate clear maps, and adjustable parameters, the MaxEnt model is widely applied in predicting the potential distribution of invasive alien species, suitable habitats for endangered species, and the potential effects of climate change on species distribution36,37.

In this study, we use the MaxEnt model to predict the habitat distribution of mammals and terrestrial birds in the Altai Mountains under current environmental conditions and under future climate scenarios, and to identify transboundary conservation priorities that can inform coordinated management across China, Kazakhstan, Mongolia, and Russia. Our results show that main habitats of most animals are located in the northwest of the Mountains, human activities, snow cover and precipitation of coldest quarter are drivers of habitat suitability, most species tend to shift their ranges towards higher altitudes or latitudes under climate change and the central area of the Altay Mountains is the key protected areas for the Altai Mountains’ wildlife based on predictive scenarios. These results provide a scientific basis for the effective conservation and management of animals in the Altai Mountains and the planning of transboundary protected areas.

Suitable habitat of species in the current period

The current distribution maps showed that the main habitats of most animals are located in the northwest of the Mountains, such as the wolverine (Gulo gulo), the Asian badger (Meles leucurus), and the western capercaillie (Tetrao urogallus). While the main habitats of some species are in the central part, such as the stoat (Mustela erminea), the Pallas’s cat (Otocolobus manul) and the snow leopard (Fig. 1). There were no species whose primary habitats are located in the southeastern part of the Altai Mountains. In terms of habitat area, the moose (Alces alces) had a relatively small range, while the snow leopard was generally widely distributed. In addition, some endangered species, such as the snow leopard and the Siberian ibex (Capra sibirica), are also widely distributed in the Altai Mountains (Table 2).

The changes of species’ habitat under climate change

It can be observed that the distribution ranges of almost all species will lose part of their current suitable habitats and gain new suitable habitats through migration, resulting in a shift in the spatial distribution of these suitable habitats. The suitable habitat areas for 11 species in the Altai Mountains have increased. These species include common quail (Coturnix coturnix), mountain hare (Lepus timidus), Eurasian lynx, black grouse (Lyrurus tetrix), alpine pika (Ochotona alpina), grey partridge (Perdix perdix), western capercaillie, hazel grouse (Tetrastes bonasia), brown bear (Ursus arctos), and red fox (Vulpes vulpes) (Fig. 2). Among these, hazel grouse exhibited the highest percentage increase in suitable habitat area, with an expansion of 169.91% compared to the current conditions. In contrast, the suitable habitat areas for common quail increase by only approximately 5.42%. The suitable habitat areas for the remaining 17 species have decreased by 2070s in the Altai Mountains. These species include moose, gray wolf (Canis lupus), Siberian ibex, Siberian roe deer (Capreolus pygargus), red deer (Cervus elaphus), wolverine, willow ptarmigan (Lagopus lagopus), rock ptarmigan (Lagopus muta), Tolai hare (Lepus tolai), gray marmot (Marmota baibacina), sable (Martes zibellina), Asian badger, stoat, Pallas’s cat, snow leopard, Daurian partridge (Perdix dauurica), Altai snowcock (Tetraogallus altaicus). Among these species, stoat, Tolai hare, and Pallas’s cat exhibited higher percentage loss in suitable habitat area, with contraction of 99.86%, 98.53%, and 92.07% respectively, compared to the current conditions. In contrast, the suitable habitat area for Asian badger decreased by only approximately 0.70%.

Environmental variables that affected the distribution

HII (human impact index) was the most significant environmental factor contributing to the MaxEnt model simulations in the Altai Mountains, and was identified as an important environmental variable influencing the distribution for 18 species (Fig. 5). Among these 18 species, moose, gray wolf, sable, and brown bear showed a negative correlation with the human impact index. In contrast, red deer, common quail, wolverine, willow ptarmigan, mountain hare, Tolai hare, stoat, Daurian partridge, grey partridge, western capercaillie, Altai snowcock, hazel grouse, and red fox showed a positive correlation with the human impact index. The presence probability of the black grouse initially increases and then decreases as the human impact index rises (Supplementary Fig. 1).

Following HII, Bio19 (precipitation of the coldest quarter) was also identified as an important environmental variable influencing the distribution of 12 species (Fig. 5). The Asian badger showed a negative correlation with precipitation. In contrast, the mountain hare, sable, and alpine pika showed a positive correlation with precipitation. The presence probabilities of the moose, red deer, gray marmot, Daurian partridge, grey partridge, Altai snowcock, and brown bear initially increase and then decrease as the precipitation rises. However, the Siberian roe deer exhibited a bimodal pattern, with its presence probability increasing, then decreasing, increasing again, and finally declining (Supplementary Fig. 2).

SCD (the number of snow cover days) had a moderate contribution to the MaxEnt model simulations, affecting the distribution of 8 species (Fig. 5). Regarding the number of snow cover days, the Asian badger and western capercaillie showed a positive correlation. The Siberian ibex, wolverine, and Pallas’s cat exhibited a trend of initially decreasing and then increasing. In contrast, the mountain hare, sable, and brown bear displayed a trend of initially increasing and then decreasing (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Discussion

Factors constraining the geographical distribution of animals

In Central Asia, some animals, such as the snow leopard, western capercaillie, Asian badger, Eurasian lynx, wolverine, and stoat, are widely distributed around the Altai Mountains. Although the geographical distribution of some of them is still limited by current climatic constraints, according to our study, the human impact index, precipitation of coldest quarter, and the number of snow cover days were the three most important factors that constrained the distribution of the current potential suitable habitat for mammals and birds in the Altai Mountains. Among anthropogenic pressures, grazing is the primary human disturbance factor in the Altai Mountains38. The number of livestock in this area has increased excessively in recent years, which has aggravated the degradation of ecosystem39. In our data, large and medium-sized mammals such as moose and gray wolves showed negative relationships with intensively used areas, whereas smaller mammals and terrestrial birds, including red fox, Tolai hare, Daurian partridge, and grey partridge, were positively associated with areas of moderate human activity. This pattern is consistent with findings from other ecosystems. Grazing and associated land uses can create more open vegetation and occasional food subsidies, which benefit small mammals and terrestrial birds40,41, sometimes in conjunction with mesopredator release42. In contrast, large and medium-sized mammals typically avoid high human presence and show reduced movement under strong human impact due to perceived risk and disturbance43. Similarly, Salvatori44 found a positive correlation in the occurrence between small mammals (such as red fox, marmot, ground squirrel, and hare) and livestock, and a negative correlation between large mammals (such as snow leopard and Siberian ibex) with livestock44. In our study, precipitation of the coldest quarter and the number of snow cover days are as the most important climate factors. Given that in the coldest quarter, precipitation in the Altai Mountains is mainly driven by water vapor sourced from the Atlantic and Arctic Oceans45, it primarily occurs as snowfall from October through April46. This indicates that the snow variables play a crucial role in the simulation of potential habitat of animals in the Altai Mountains. This finding is consistent with previous studies on polecats in Sweden, wild yaks on the Tibetan Plateau, tundra, and other alpine regions19,47. Generally, for small mammals, snow cover can provide protective subnivean environments, while for medium- and large-sized mammals, snow cover affects foraging efficiency and winter range use10,48,49. Nevertheless, across taxa, snow cover did not uniformly exceed min temperature of coldest month, NDVI, or altitude in explanatory power in our models. Altitude frequently ranked around the third position, indicating a supporting role rather than a single dominant driver. In terms of mechanism, winter temperature and snow represent complementary winter constraints-the former primarily reflecting energetic stress and thermal limitation, and the latter constraining mobility and subnivium buffering-so their relative importance can vary among species and regions8,50. Similarly, NDVI and altitude proxy resource phenology and topo-climate, respectively; after collinearity filtering, variance can be distributed among correlated winter variables, yielding similar contributions rather than a single standout predictor51–53.

Effects of global change on the species richness

Our study showed that, excluding the impacts of human disturbances, the climate limits the current distribution of mammals and terrestrial birds in the Altai Mountains. Thus, we infer that global climate change will have a more direct impact on these animals in the future. In our projections, suitable habitat shows clear spatial heterogeneity under future climate scenarios: north-western ridges and high-elevation areas at the four-country junction tend to retain or gain suitability, while many mid-elevation landscapes in the central Altai lose suitable habitat. This pattern is consistent with previous studies, indicating variations in species-specific responses to climate change. For instance, Luo54 predicted the impact of climate change on 22 ungulate species, finding that the ranges of six species are expected to expand, while those of 16 species are expected to contract in the future54.

The centroid analyses further highlight that range shifts in the Altai are strongly directional within an alpine context. Most species’ distribution centroids move towards higher altitudes or higher latitudes, with ten species shifting by more than 90 km. Notably, the Eurasian lynx, wolverine, red deer, and alpine pika show pronounced movements towards more northerly and elevated parts of the mountain range. These shifts are consistent with the expectation that many species will shift their range northward (particularly in the Northern Hemisphere) and upward in elevation in response to elevation-dependent warming55,56. However, there is extremely large variation in the rates57. For example, Walther58 observed that some animal species shifted their distribution to higher altitudes in response to rising temperature58. This distributional shift occurs as species follow the shifts in their preferred climatic conditions, such as temperature and precipitation59 or respond to non-climatic factors correlated with habitat suitability, such as vegetation patterns60. However, our results also indicate that a few species shift downslope or toward lower latitudes, underscoring that local topography, snow-cover dynamics, and habitat configuration can generate complex redistribution pathways in mountain regions39,61.

Furthermore, our data on the distribution pattern of species richness reveal a distinct spatial gradient across the Altai Mountains, characterized by the highest species richness in the northwestern region and a progressive decline toward the southeast. Under future climate scenarios, species richness remains high in the transboundary high-mountain core but declines in parts of the central Altai, where many species lose suitable habitat. Similar richness gradients have been reported in other mountain regions62. The anticipated persistence of a biodiversity hotspot located near the four-country border in the Altai Mountains provides further justification for implementing alpine-focused transboundary conservation initiatives in the region.

It should be noted that future projections were based solely on climatic variables due to the lack of high-resolution data on human impact and snow cover. Therefore, the estimated range changes mainly represent climate-driven potential shifts rather than fully integrated environmental responses. Despite this limitation, the two-period comparison remains informative about climatic sensitivity and redistribution trends.

The need for the establishment of transboundary nature reserves

The Altai Mountains are a high-elevation, cryosphere-influenced mountain system that is particularly sensitive to climate warming and intensifying human activities26,63. Moreover, this region belongs to the Central Asian Global Biodiversity Hotspot64. Although some nature reserves and parks have been established at both national and provincial levels, including Kanas National Nature Reserve and Two-River Source Natural Reserve in China, Altai Tavan Bogd National Park in Mongolia, Golden Mountains of Altai (UkoK) in Russia, and Katon-Karagay State National Nature Park in Kazakhstan. These protected areas remain fragmented and often fail to cover transboundary habitats. Under the influence of global climate change and human interference, the pressure on ecological protection in the Altai Mountains is increasing. Effective conservation of suitable habitats for biodiversity and wildlife species also faces new challenges. Our data show that the habitats of some species such as Siberian ibex, snow leopard, gray wolf, and stoat, have been lost or fragmented. The lost habitats are mainly located in the central area of the mountain, especially around the four-country border. Furthermore, future climate change is expected to further concentrate species richness and range shifts in this transboundary high-elevation zone. These results meant that it is necessary to collaborate among the four relevant countries to protect the endangered animals as well as the entire ecosystem.

Transboundary conservation has emerged as a critical strategy in other regions facing similar challenges. Successful examples, such as conservation efforts for the Amur tiger (Panthera tigris altaica) between the Northeast Tiger and Leopard National Park in China and the Land of the Leopard National Park in Russia65, demonstrate that cross-border governance can secure biodiversity when ecological networks and protection regimes are aligned. An (2023) suggested that in the Altai Mountains, the ecological risks were primarily concentrated within a single country or between two neighboring countries66. Based on the results of our study, the junction of the four countries emerges as a priority area for the cooperative establishment of transboundary nature reserves. Duan67 also suggested establishing transboundary protected areas in the transboundary regions of the four countries, by integrating identified ecological conservation priority areas with existing protected areas, and by deepening the mechanism of multi-party transboundary conservation cooperation39. Our findings support those previous recommendations to establish large transboundary protected areas and highlight the junction of the four countries as a key area for future collaborative conservation efforts.

Furthermore, since the distribution areas of many of the species in this study span national borders, and our projections suggest animal migration routes involve border regions, it is crucial to develop strategies that facilitate migration through natural corridors to new suitable habitats and regulate human activities in these areas. Transboundary cooperation will be essential not only for conserving current biodiversity but also for enabling future range expansions. Our modeling framework provides a spatially explicit basis for identifying where transboundary alpine protection will be most effective, thereby offering concrete guidance for ongoing regional initiatives aimed at establishing a coordinated Altai conservation network.

In conclusion, our findings suggest that all species included in the study are sensitive to climate change, and as a result, their ranges are expected to change in the future. In general, the predicted shifts in range are primarily in northward and high-altitude directions as animals track the movement of their preferred climatic or non-climatic variables. The key protected regions are mainly located in the central area of the Altay Mountains. This study is the first effort to estimate the potential projections of future habitats with a quantitative assessment of range shifts for wild animals under future climate scenarios across the entire Altay Mountains. The projected patterns of change provide important insights for conservation planning for species in this transboundary region.

Methods

Study area

The Altai Mountains are located in Central Asia, spanning more than 2000 km across China, Kazakhstan, Mongolia, and Russia. The highest point in the Altai Mountains is Belukha peak with an altitude of 4506 meters. The Altai Mountains possesses representative landscapes ranging from desert steppe, montane forest, to alpine meadow and tundra, providing habitats for many endangered species such as snow leopard (Panthera uncia) and Siberian ibex (Capra sibirica). The study area is located in transboundary regions of the Altai Mountains (43.25°–52.30°N, 81.93°–100.79°E), with a total area of around 300,000 km2 (Fig. 6). With increasing awareness of ecological importance in the Altai Mountains, authorities of the four countries have established a series of protected areas, including nature parks, nature reserve, natural world heritage sites, and international important wetlands. Multilateral dialogs were initiated to enhance transboundary conservation and build transboundary protected areas66,67. There is an urgent need for collaboration among four countries in the Altai Mountains based on identifying the priorities for future biodiversity conservation.

Data collection

We selected 27 animal species (18 mammals and 9 terrestrial birds) distributed across the Altai Mountains and combined their occurrence records from multiple sources since 2000, including our own field observations, Global Biodiversity Information Facility database (GBIF, 1167 records, Supplementary Table 1) and the published papers (including some in Mongolian, Kazakh, and Russian, totaling 120 records, Supplementary Table 1) to ensure the accuracy of the data. To reduce spatial clustering and sampling bias, we performed spatial filtering in ENMTools (v1.4.4) by removing duplicate occurrence points within the same environmental grid cell before running the MaxEnt models. Based on the recorded information of the species, GBIF provides comprehensive data on the currently known distribution of the species within its native range. Field observations for these species were collected from multiple data sources associated with surveys of our team between 2014 and 2024 (e.g., Kansas National Nature Reserve and Two-river Source Nature Reserve, 844 records). We used line-transect searches, camera-trap deployments, and opportunistic sightings to record presence-only occurrences. Each record was assigned geographic coordinates using a handheld GPS or mobile phone.

We downloaded 19 bioclimatic variables (Table 3) for both current climatic conditions (averaged over 1950–2000) and future projections (2070 s, averaged over 2061–2080) from the WorldClim Dataset (v2.1, www.worldclim.org), at a spatial resolution of 30 arcseconds (~1 km). Future climate projections were derived from general circulation model (GCM) outputs provided by the WorldClim v2.1 database under Shared Socio-economic Pathways (SSPs) scenarios, which were developed as part of the Coupled Model Intercomparison Project Phase 6 (CMIP6) of IPCC (https://www.worldclim.org/data/cmip6/cmip6_clim30s.html). In this study, we selected the CMCC-ESM2 climate system model and the SSP2-4.5 scenario. The CMCC-ESM2 has been recognized as a suitable model for assessing climate change impacts at regional scale68. The SSP2-4.5 scenario represents an intermediate greenhouse gases passway, with radiative forcing projected to stabilize at 4.5 W/m2 by 2100.

To mitigate overfitting caused by multicollinearity among bioclimatic variables, the following procedures were implemented. First, the jackknife method in the MaxEnt was used to assess the initial contribution of each variable to the model. Variables that showed no contribution were removed. Second, ENMTools software was employed to analyze the pairwise correlation between factors. If two bioclimatic variables with the similar attributes had a correlation coefficient of | r | ≥ 0.8, the variable with the higher contribution rate was selected69.

We downloaded the elevation data (at 1-km resolution) from Open Topography (https://www.opentopography.org) and extracted the aspect and slope from it. The human impact index (HII) data (2015–2020) at a 1-km resolution, were also downloaded from https://wcshumanfootprint.org/data-access. The dataset was created from nine global data layers covering human population pressure (population density), human land use and infrastructure (built-up areas, nighttime lights, land use/land cover) and human access (coastlines, roads, navigable rivers). We defined the number of snow cover days as days with an SD exceeding 20mm19, with SD data sourced from the National Snow and Ice Data Centre (NSIDC, https://nsidc.org/). A small positive threshold was selected because shallow snow already affects movement and foraging for many small- to medium-sized mammals and terrestrial birds. In contrast, deeper snow (~15–20 cm) indicates subnivium conditions48 and some large ungulates respond strongly only at ~30–40 cm70 or recently fallen snow71. Using a single cutoff ensures cross-species comparability and reproducibility in this community-level analysis.

Establishing model and simulation

We selected the AUC and TSS to assess model performance. AUC is threshold-independent and its value ranges from 0 to 1. TSS takes both omission and commission errors into account by adding sensitivity and specificity and subtracting 1. TSS score varies between −1 and +1, where 1 indicates perfect model performance and values of zero or less indicate poor performance. The model evaluation criteria were: AUC > 0.9 is excellent, 0.8 < AUC ≤ 0.9 is good, and AUC ≤ 0.8 is poor72; TSS > 0.8 is excellent, 0.5 < TSS ≤ 0.8 is acceptable and TSS ≤ 0.5 is poor73.

Assessing potential habitat

The MaxEnt model (v3.4.4) was used to predict the occurrence probability (ranging from 0 to 1) of different species in each grid at a 1-km resolution. To explore trends in the habitat of each species under different climate scenarios, the continuous suitable probability maps output by the MaxEnt model need to be converted into binary (presence/absence) distribution maps. In this study, binary distribution maps for each species were classified according to the “tenth percentile training presence” threshold, which has been shown to more accurately reflect the distributional area of the species studied74. Predicted probabilities above the threshold were assigned a value of 1, indicating suitable habitat, whereas probabilities those below the threshold were assigned a value of 0, indicating unsuitable habitat. The binary distribution maps of all species were overlaid and summed on a cell-by-cell basis to generate a composite map of species richness, where each grid cell value represents the total number of species predicted to be present. We estimated the changes in the potential suitable habitat area and the distribution centroid of each species under current and future climate scenarios. The centroid of each species’ suitable habitat was calculated using the SDMtoolbox plugin in ArcGIS, which calculates the geometric center of all grid cells identified as suitable (presence = 1) for each scenario. The shift distance between current and future centroids was then measured as the Euclidean distance between the two centroid points.

Statistics and reproducibility

Model performance was assessed using AUC and TSS. The outputs of model were converted to binary presence/absence maps using the “tenth percentile training presence” threshold. Species richness, suitable area changes and centroid shifts were derived from these binary maps using a consistent 1-km grid across scenarios. A total of 2131 occurrence records across the 27 species were collected (Table 1).
