Controlling Fluorescent Readout in Paper-based Analytical Devices
Authors: Anna Luongo, Alexander Ritter von Stockert, Frank D Scherag, Thomas Brandstetter, Markus Biesalski, Jürgen Rühe
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractPaper is an ideal candidate for the development of new disposable diagnostic devices because it is a low-cost material, allows transport of the liquid on the device by capillary action, and is environmentally friendly. Today, colorimetric analysis is most often used as a detection method for rapid tests (test strips or lateral flow devices) but usually gives only qualitative results and is limited by a relatively high detection threshold. Here, we describe studies using fluorescence as a readout tool for paper-based diagnostics. We study how the optical readout is affected by light transmission, scattering, and fluorescence as a function of paper characteristics such as thickness (grammage), water content, autofluorescence, and paper type/composition. We show that paper-based fluorescence analysis allows better optical readout compared to that of nitrocellulose, which is currently the material of choice in colorimetric assays. To reduce the loss of analyte molecules (e.g., proteins) due to adsorption to the paper surface, we coat the paper fibers with a protein-repellent hydrogel. For this purpose, we use hydrophilic copolymers consisting of N,N-dimethyl acrylamide and a benzophenone-based cross-linker, which are photochemically transformed into a fiber-attached polymer hydrogel on the paper fiber surfaces in situ. We show that the combination of fluorescence detection and the use of a protein-repellent coating enables sensitive paper-based analysis. Finally, the success of the strategy is demonstrated by using a simple LFD application as an example.Keywords: POC, paper based diagnostics, scattering, μPAD, fluorescence, surface modification

Introduction

Infectious diseases (e.g., malaria) are among the leading causes for mortality in the world, accounting for about 25% of global deaths.1 Recently, point-of-care (POC) diagnostic devices have become a common approach to address this growing need,2−6 and lateral flow technology (LFT) is considered to be one of the most commercially feasible analytical tools. This technology enables rapid diagnostic tests, yielding straightforward results that can be efficiently conducted by healthcare professionals or even by patients through self-testing.

Nitrocellulose has been used for the immobilization and detection of biomolecules for more than 50 years, and nitrocellulose-based strip devices such as pregnancy tests and troponin quick tests have gained wide popularity.7−11 In 2007, Whitesides and his group popularized a method in which the substrate for a lateral flow device was simply a paper strip, avoiding the use of nitrocellulose.12−15 They fabricated paper-based bioassays, which allowed the detection of several molecules in parallel, thanks to well-defined channels confined within the paper by hydrophobic polymers. Since then, paper-based devices have attracted great interest for low-cost microfluidics and many different systems have been studied. That is because the hydrophilic nature and fibrous structure of the paper enable the diagnostic fluid to be transported by capillary action without the need for an external pump. This passive transport mechanism makes paper-based substrates perfect candidates for pump-free microfluidics.16−18 In addition, paper is made from cellulose, which is a sustainable material, as it is renewable and fully biodegradable. Another advantage of paper-based microfluidic devices is that they have a high surface-to-volume ratio, which improves contact with the fluid. In addition, the presence of many hydroxyl groups in cellulose allows simple and versatile introduction of functional groups.19−21 The sensitivity of paper-based diagnostic devices is often sufficient for applications where analyte concentrations are relatively high (i.e., the detection of pregnancy-indicating hormones). However, light scattering and unspecific adsorption of analyte molecules on the fiber surfaces remain major problems despite considerable efforts to mitigate them.22,23 Because of these problems, paper-based analyzers are not yet widely used commercially, except for qualitative testing, despite their obvious advantages.

Most of the current state-of-the-art lateral flow devices (LFDs) are still based on nitrocellulose membranes. However, producing nitrocellulose requires multiple chemical steps and stringent safety precautions.22,23 In addition, nitrocellulose has a strong autofluorescence that generally limits the readout by colorimetric detection, allowing essentially only a binary response and a “Yes or No” answer (schematically shown in Figure 1a), and quantifications cannot be performed. A “Yes or No” answer may be sufficient for some infections, but many other illnesses require a quantification of an appropriate biomarker, which is difficult to perform in a colorimetric assay.

Schematic depiction of (a) a colorimetric analysis process carried out by the human eye on a paper-based substrate giving a qualitative result (“Yes or No″-answer) and (b) quantitative determination of the analyte by fluorescent detection on a paper-based substrate.

In contrast, fluorescence readout permits for quantitative determination of the target probe (Figure 1b) and can be particularly valuable if very low concentrations of a particular analyte need to be determined.24 For this reason, fluorescence-based sensors are currently the state of the art in medical diagnostics. Since theoretically millions of photons per second can be emitted from one single dye molecule, fluorescence readout can be used even for a single-digit number of molecules per μm2.24 In direct comparison, the signals from fluorescence detection provide intensities orders of magnitude higher than those from colorimetric analyses.

While there have been some papers on fluorescence analysis with paper-based LFD systems reported in the literature,25−29 it is worth noting that the field still remains relatively unexplored, despite the obvious advantages in respect to sensitivity. In particular, the implementation of such processes and the identification of crucial parameters have not been extensively investigated. While the autofluorescence of pure cellulose is rather low, most papers contain a wide range of other molecules, e.g., primarily hemicellulose, lignin, and pectin. Among them, lignin seems to be the main candidate for causing autofluorescence, but hydroxynaphthoquinones and cinnamic acid have been also reported in this context.30,31 In addition, aggregates formed during the paper-making process cause an uneven distribution of scattering centers, resulting in relatively large standard deviations of the mean background signals. This background reduces the sensitivity and reproducibility of the analytical reading.

Another important aspect of using paper as a substrate is the deposition of the analyte solution, which is strongly influenced by the porosity of the paper. On a conventional planar surface, the solution spreads to form a roughly 2D spot, the size of which depends on the wetting properties of the substrate. In contrast, for the same volume, the same solution penetrates paper-like materials due to capillary action, resulting in a 3D distribution of the molecules of interest throughout the thickness of the substrate. While in the first case, excitation and emission occur solely on the surface, resulting in a 2D image, in the second case, volume elements (voxel) located below the surface also contribute to the signal due to the 3D distribution of the probe.

The signal readout in paper-based devices is a result of the superposition of both the fluorescence signal and light scattering. However, scattering and adsorption are two phenomena that vary greatly depending on the source of the cellulose fibers. Papers made with materials from different sources (e.g., eucalyptus vs cotton linters paper) are usually processed differently and therefore vary significantly in their composition, porosity, crystallinity, swelling properties, thickness, fiber lengths, and many other physical parameters.32

In the following, we use model papers and discuss how characteristics such as water content, thickness (or grammage), and autofluorescence of laboratory-produced paper sheets affect the detection of a strong and reproducible signal in fluorescence readout in paper-based devices. The chosen model systems are eucalyptus paper, representing a commercial material with a relatively high residual lignin content, and cotton linters as a reference material, which contains almost no lignin. In particular, we use fluorescently labeled proteins to gain insights into the variation of readout results occurring when papers with varying sheet characteristics are used. We also describe how the fluorescence signal depends on the location of the molecules within the paper, i.e., whether they are located at the surface or in lower layers. A second important question for the development of paper-based diagnostic devices is how much protein becomes lost by nonspecific adsorption. Therefore, we generate surface-attached hydrogels on the paper fiber surfaces with the goal of creating protein-repellent paper coatings. The gels are produced by a photochemical cross-linking process (C,H-insertion cross-linking, CHic), which directly leads to the formation of a coating on the fiber surfaces. Such surface-attached hydrogels strongly prevent the nonspecific adsorption of proteins.37−39 Finally, we show how a lateral flow test can be optimally designed using this specific type of paper.

Experimental Section

Materials

All chemicals and solvents (reagent grade) were obtained from Merck (Darmstadt, Germany), Carl Roth (Karlsruhe, Germany), or VWR (Darmstadt, Germany) and used as received unless otherwise noted. All water used throughout the experiments was deionized, unless stated otherwise. Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) slides were commercially purchased and cleaned for 3 min in methanol before printing. Mirror substrates are glass-based and then sputtered with aluminum, since thin aluminum film on a glass slide acts as a mirror.33 The fluorescent cyanine-5-labeled bovine serum albumin conjugate (BSA-Cy5) was obtained from Nanocs (USA). Fluorescently labeled polyclonal goat antihuman IgA antibody (Alexa Fluor 647 AffiniPure Goat Anti-Human Serum IgA, α-Chain-Specific) and Human Serum IgA were obtained by Jackson ImmunoResearch Inc., PA. BSA-biotin was obtained from Sigma-Aldrich and Streptavidin-Cy5 by VWR.

Paper-Sheet Manufacturing

The laboratory-engineered paper sheets studied in this paper were prepared from either bleached Eucalyptus Sulfate pulp (median fiber length: 0.76 mm; curl: 15.9%; fibrillation degree: 5.1%; fines content: 9.1%) or cotton linters pulp (median fiber length: 0.79 mm; curl: 12.9%; fibrillation degree: 1.8%; fines content: 19.6%), with varying fiber concentrations in order to achieve different basis weights. The grammages obtained were: 20, 30, 40, 50, 70, 80, 110, 120, and 150 g·m–2. The samples were prepared with a Rapid Köthen Sheet Former according to DIN 54358 and ISO 5269/2. No fillers or additives were applied to the paper samples. To compare with standard papers used in the literature, experiments on a standard Whatman Type 1 filter paper were carried out. To compare with nitrocellulose materials, experiments using commercial UniSart membranes were carried out (CN95 with clear backing and CN150 with white backing).

Solution Printing by MicroArray Printer

For microarray printing, a sciFLEXARRAYER S3 (Scienion, Berlin, Germany) was used to print BSA-Cy5 (1 μg/mL), Human Serum IgA (200 μg/mL), and BSA-biotin (1 μg/mL). In the former case, it was printed as droplets (1 μL), and in the latter two cases as lines (∼0.4 μL on each LFD stripe), which act as capture molecules in the lateral flow assays.

Transmittance Measurements

Transmittance was recorded as a function of the wavelength with a UV/vis Varian Cary Bio 50 spectrometer from 190 to 800 nm. A background scan was performed prior to each measurement. To perform the analysis, paper samples were applied on quartz glass (thickness of 1 mm).

Diffuse Reflection Measurements

Reflectance was collected as functions of wavelength with a Varian Cary 1E UV–vis Spectrometer equipped with a sample holder for solid samples and an integrating sphere for reflection measurements.

Fluorescence Measurements

The fluorescent analysis was carried out with a Fluorescence Microarray Reader (Sensovation AG, Radolfzell, Germany), red ex/em light: 650/670 nm.

Polymers Synthesis

The polymerization of DMAA (after removal of the stabilizer) with the cross-linker 4-methacryloyloxybenzophenone (MABP) was performed in accordance with literature procedures.34,35 Monomers were dissolved under nitrogen atmosphere in N,N-dimethylformamide (DMF) at a monomer concentration of 1 mol·L–1, and azobis(isobutyronitrile) (AIBN) was added as an initiator. Afterward, the reaction solution was degassed by freezing, pumping, and thawing (four times) and was then placed into a preheated water bath at 60 °C for 16 h. The polymer was precipitated by dropwise addition of the polymerization solution to a 10–15-fold excess of cold diethyl ether, then filtered off. The polymer was dried and taken up again in dichloromethane (this procedure was repeated three times). After drying the polymer in vacuum, it was obtained as a white powder. The MABP content of the polymer was analyzed with 1H NMR spectroscopy by the ratio of DMAA protons compared to the area of MABP protons and determined to be 1%. The Mn of the polymer was determined to be around 33,000 g/mol.

FT-1NMR: δ = 1.8–1.2 ppm (*CH2–CH), 2.94–2.90 ppm (*(CH3)2–N), 3.1 ppm (*CH–CH2), and 7.9–7.4 ppm (C–Haro) (see SI, Figure S1).

Preparation of Paper-Attached Polymer Hydrogels

The polymer coating preparation was done by dip-coating (withdraw speed = 100 mm·min–1) from H2O (30 mg·mL–1), with an immersion time of 15 s.

Lateral-Flow Assays

After drying the polymer-modified paper samples, a solution of Human IgA antibodies or BSA-biotin was printed by the microarray printer as a line (acting as capture molecules on the test line) onto the different substrates, which were later cut into strips (0.4 × 2.5 cm long). After drying, the substrates were placed on a Teflon dish and then irradiated by light using UV Stratalinker 2400 (Stratagene, USA), with an irradiation wavelength λirr = 365 nm (I = 2.1 mW·cm–2) with 1 J·cm–2, in order to cross-link and immobilize the deposited materials. 5 μL of a buffered solution (PBS was used as the buffer, 0.01 M, pH 7.4, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) with fluorescently labeled Alexa Fluor 647 Anti-Human IgA or Streptavidin-Cy5 (acting as target molecules) was pipetted onto one strip end on the substrates. Afterward, 10 μL of PBS was applied to ensure protein flow. As soon as the solution reached the other side of the strip, the strips were stirred and washed first in a Petri dish containing a PBS solution with 0.1% Tween20 (5 min) and later in a Petri dish containing a solution of only PBS (5 min). Subsequently, the fluorescence light intensities of the complete strips were measured by taking pictures with the Fluorescence Microarray Reader. The fluorescence signals were analyzed as gray scales of the fluorescence light by image software (ImageJ) and were then plotted as a function of the distance traveled by the analyte along the strip. Background signals were measured before the detection line.

Results and Discussion

Variation of the Paper Type

In order to understand how light interacts with an entangled fiber network such as that of a paper, we first studied how the background signal in fluorescence analysis is affected by the fiber type and grammage of the paper sheets. In these experiments, eucalyptus-based model papers were compared with those made from cotton linters, representing raw materials with low and high lignin contents, respectively. To this end, the fluorescent signals generated by sheets with different grammages are investigated. The grammage (i.e., the weight per area of a paper sample) depends on the thickness of the sheet and the density of the fiber mesh. For the same type of paper, the grammage is directly proportional to the paper thickness (see SI, Figure S2). The type of material and grammage both have a strong influence on the intensity of the light reflected back or backscattered from the paper surface without the presence of a fluorescent dye (Figure 2).

Background fluorescence intensities (red ex/em light: 650/670 nm) of eucalyptus and linters papers with different grammages in the (a) dry state and (b) wet state, with standard deviation (n ≥ 5).

Cotton linters, unlike eucalyptus fibers, consist mainly of cellulose and contain almost no aromatic residues from wood constituents, especially lignin, and therefore exhibit lower autofluorescence. In addition, the two types of paper have different morphologies and fiber network densities (see SI, Figure S3). Light scattering is more pronounced with increasing density, since a higher fiber density means more obstacles for photons at which they can be scattered.36 Increasing the grammage (i.e., the thickness) results in a larger number of these scattering centers. When considering dry versus wet samples, the same trend can be observed (Figure 2a and 2b). However, due to the increased transparency when water (n = 1, 3) replaces air (n = 1, 0) between the fibers (n = 1, 48), the light scattering and, thus, the background signal are much lower in the wet state than in the dry state.37

The transmission data of the eucalyptus papers in the wet and dry states are shown in Figure 3. While transmission is low in the dry state for all paper thicknesses, the very thin papers (<50 μm) showed significant transmittance in the wet state, which decreases with the paper thickness as expected. Diffuse reflectance in the dry state (Figure 3b) becomes very strong with increasing thickness, and for substrates 200 μm or thicker, it becomes more or less independent of paper thickness. Similar to the transmittance data, the diffuse reflectance in the wet state shows the same trend as in the dry state, but is shifted to lower values, due to the increased match between the refractive indices.

(a) Transmittance and (b) diffuse reflection of eucalyptus paper as a function of paper thickness in the dry and wet state (λ = 650 nm).

A second aspect concerns the distribution of the probe molecules, which differs significantly between papers and the commonly used planar sensor chip surfaces. Therefore, it is important to know to what extent fluorescent molecules located at different depths from the paper surface contribute to the overall signal. To elucidate this, in a first set of experiments, a fluorescent dye was printed on a thin, clear PMMA substrate. Then, by placing wet paper samples of varying thickness behind the substrate, we investigated the influence of additional scatterers below the dye layer on the intensity of the readout signal (Figure 4a). The net signal of each sample in this context is defined by the mean gray values of the fluorescence signal subtracted from that of the surrounding background. The net signal ratio was then calculated from net signal divided by the signal of the sample that had no wet paper behind the PMMA substrate. The measurements show that the net signal ratio increases proportionally to the thickness of the wet paper behind the clear PMMA substrate (Figure 4b). This increase in intensity is caused by the fact that light that has passed through the sample and would be otherwise lost due to transmission is backscattered and can now contribute again to an excitation, thus increasing the probability for light absorption and increasing the fluorescence. When a 300-μm-thick dry paper is placed behind the sample, the net signal intensity improves by a factor of ∼2.8 versus a factor of ∼3.2 when a mirror is placed behind it (Figure 4c). The dry paper has approximately double the diffuse reflection compared with the wet paper, as shown in Figure 3b. This situation is similar to a literature report, which shows that adding titanium dioxide (TiO2) particles to polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) substrates improves the photon collection.38

(b) Net signal ratio (net signal with paper/net signal without paper) as a function of the paper thickness (with standard deviation, n ≥ 8) placed behind the PMMA substrate. (c) Comparison between the mean gray values obtained from fluorescent solution printed on PMMA and values obtained when a dry eucalyptus paper (∼300 μm) and a mirror are placed behind the same PMMA substrate after background subtraction (excitation wavelength λex = 650 nm, emission wavelength λem = 670 nm).

Next, we placed a paper as a “hindrance” between the fluorescent molecule and the detector to study how the optical readout would change (Figure 5a). This situation reflects the condition when the light has to travel a certain distance through a paper sheet, until the light reaches the fluorescent molecule. Surprisingly, we found that wet paper sheets with thicknesses up to about 300 μm did not reduce but rather increased the net signal in comparison to the sample without paper obstacles (net signal ratio >1), as shown in Figure 5b. This increase is due to an enhanced collection and redirection of photons in front of the dye molecules, which are otherwise lost by transmission, resulting in higher or rather more efficient excitation and emission processes. The positive effect of the paper obstacles is especially surprising as long as the light transmission is lower than 10% on wet paper sheets thicker than 100 μm, as shown in Figure 3a. As is evident from the increasing function, there is practically no signal loss until reaching a peak at approximately 120 μm thickness (Figure 5b). Beyond this thickness, the net signal intensity decreases as less light becomes transmitted through the paper and fewer molecules can successfully be excited to emit fluorescence.

(a) Schematic illustration of the experimental setup: the fluorescent solution (in orange, BSA-Cy5, c = 1 μg/mL, V = 1 μL) is printed on a PMMA substrate and a wet eucalyptus paper sheet is placed above in a reflectance measurement. (b) Net signal ratio (net signal with paper/net signal without paper) as a function of the paper thickness (with standard deviation, n ≥ 8), placed above the PMMA substrate (excitation wavelength λex = 650 nm, emission wavelength λem = 670 nm).

The previous two experiments (Figure 4 and Figure 5) directly showed that the distribution of probe molecules throughout the thickness of the paper has an important influence on the net signal readout, as the exciting light has to reach the dye molecule and the emitted light needs to leave the sample. Next, we wanted to know how the combined effects of having paper layers both above and below the probe would affect the readout. As such, in a following experiment, the overall thickness of the paper layers above and below were kept constant, and thus, the probe varied (Figure 6). Surprisingly, this experiment further evidenced that molecules located relatively far below the surface contribute significantly to the overall readout. As demonstrated by the net signal ratio higher than 1, in a range of about 100 μm thickness, there was no loss of signal. Only at very high sample depths does the transmittance become so low (as shown in Figure 3a) that only very little light reaches and excites the probe molecules and then in turn can reach the detector. Although the strongest contribution to the overall fluorescence originates from molecules located at or a little below the surface, when the paper thickness is below 100 μm, all dye molecules contribute to the fluorescence since they are exposed to a larger number of scattering events and the transmittance is not yet low.

The probe depth varies, and the overall thickness is kept constant (d ∼ 290 μm). (b) Net signal ratio as a function of the probe depth (with standard deviation, n ≥ 8) within the setup mentioned in the schematic illustration. The net signal was determined by the mean gray values of the fluorescent dye printed on PMMA, subtracted by the mean gray values of the surrounding background area on the same substrate. This value was then related to the net signal obtained when the additional papers were stacked behind and/or above and plotted as a net signal ratio.

Printed Spot Sizes

In the next series of experiments, we studied how the thickness of the paper influenced the distribution of molecules in the paper substrate when the aqueous printing solution was imbibed into the paper. To this end, fluorescently labeled protein solutions (BSA-Cy5) were printed onto the substrates, and after drying, fluorescence analyses were carried out. Figure 7a shows that the printed droplets become brighter and smaller in size with increasing thickness of the paper. Additionally, the background intensity significantly increases when the paper thickness increases. The thickness dependence of the drop size can be explained as follows: when the aqueous dye solution is printed onto the paper, the dye is transported in all directions through capillary action. For now, we assume that drying processes during the printing processes described here do not play a major role in the final distribution of adsorbed dyes in the sheet. Since the paper thickness is much smaller than the extent of horizontal spreading of the liquid, the 3D spots assume an approximately cylindrical shape with a homogeneous distribution throughout the paper thickness. This homogeneous distribution is deduced from the fact that the signal intensities measured from both sides of the sample have about the same intensity, as expected for an almost symmetrical distribution (see SI, Figure S7). This inverse proportionality between spot area and paper thickness is in good agreement with the experimental data, and the calculated droplets sizes were in agreement with those measured experimentally (see SI, Figure S8).

Now, when the intensities of the spotted drops are viewed, the situation is more complex. Plots in Figure 7c and Figure 7d show that the net signal intensities (integrated over the area) increase with the paper thickness. The reason for these enhancements of the net signals is the strong scattering of light from fibers behind the fluorescent dye molecule. As a result, higher net signals can be observed, but also higher background signals. The difference in the results for the dry (Figure 7c) and the wet states (Figure 7d) can be explained by the fact that the paper in the wet state is more transparent, and molecules distributed over the whole paper thickness (here, up to 300 μm) can be used for the detection. Dry paper is much more opaque, and molecules located only in the upper 100–200 μm can be used for signal generation.

The plot in Figure 7d shows that in the wet state, at a paper thickness of roughly ∼150 μm, the highest net signal is obtained. However, with thicker papers, the intensity at the limit of detection (LoD), defined as background mean gray value plus 3 times its standard deviation,39 increases due to the stronger background. This may result in a lower sensitivity for the optical readout since it is the lowest analyte concentration that can be reliably distinguished from the background.

In order to study the combined effects of reflection and spot size on the signal readout, tests conducted with paper were compared again to those conducted on a reflective (mirror) and a transparent substrate (PMMA). The same amount of the fluorescently labeled solution (BSA-Cy5) was printed on all substrates. After the samples were dried, fluorescence measurements in reflection mode were performed (Figure 8). Printing the probe solutions on paper materials resulted in a much larger spot size due to capillary spreading. Moreover, the paper had a signal 65 times higher than that of the transparent PMMA and even approximately 12 times higher than that of the mirror (despite having the same number of printed molecules). Similar experiments were carried out by comparing our papers with commercially available filter papers, also resulting in significant stronger signals (see SI, Figure S10). This is a result of the 3D distribution of the molecules in the paper combined with the higher efficiency of light scattering. The exact details of the signal intensity depend on the ratio of the rates of drying vs spreading. Protein-Repellent Coatings on the Fiber Surfaces

As a demonstration case for a simple assay, we studied the detection of Alexa Fluor 647 labeled Anti-Human IgA. One of the main problems, when studying proteins, is the nonspecific adsorption of undesired proteins during analyte movement along the strip. In order to reduce this, paper samples were dip-coated with a hydrophilic copolymer containing benzophenone units, p(DMAA-1%-MABP), which had been incorporated into the polymers through a standard free radical copolymerization procedure, as already discussed in previous work.40 The surface modification significantly reduces the nonspecific protein adsorption and allows molecules to reach the detection line, thanks to an entropic shielding effect.41−44 p(DMAA-1%-MABP) coatings also increase the wet strength properties, an important aspect for analytical device manufacturing.41,45,46 It should be noted that in the studied wavelength range, the presence of benzophenone groups does not give rise to an additional background contribution to the signal intensity (see SI, Figure S11). By using a very thin sheet, a higher sensitivity can be achieved, due to the low LoD obtainable with weaker backgrounds. However, the thinnest paper (45 μm thick) leads to a lower signal enhancement, as shown in the plot in Figure 7d, where the highest signal was obtained for paper ∼150 μm thick. Therefore, an intermediate thickness was chosen (100 μm) to ensure a good LoD and simultaneously a better optical readout.

After the surface modification, solutions of human IgA which act as a capture molecule in the assay were printed onto the paper, and the proteins were covalently immobilized by brief UV-irradiation (1 J·cm–2). The benzophenone groups in the coating layer were used for covalent binding of the IgA molecules via C,H-insertion cross-linking. A buffered solution of fluorescently labeled Anti-Human IgA antibodies was used as the analyte target. It was pipetted onto one strip end on both uncoated and coated papers (Figure 9a). After the samples were incubated for 15 min, the fluorescence signal was read out. The uncoated samples showed a very strong nonspecific adsorption right at the spot where the analyte was deposited. In fact, no analyte reached the detection line, making a readout completely impossible. In contrast to this, papers modified with the polymer hydrogel show clear detection lines, as depicted in Figure 9b, and very strong signals could be read out.

The fluorescence signals of the coated samples were analyzed by an image software (ImageJ) to calculate the intensity of the gray value along the stripe, as shown in Figure 9c for three exemplary analyte concentrations of Alexa Fluor 647 Anti-Human IgA. Next, the integral of the peak of each curve was calculated for all the samples analyzed by using the background as baseline (Figure 9d). The polymer modification does not affect the background nor the sensitivity, as evidenced by the practically identical low values of the background intensity. When the number of analyte molecules is larger than the number of immobilized probes, not all analyte can be bound, and an increase of the background behind the detection line is visible. The success of the fluorescence readout of the assay on the modified paper is evidenced by the sharp peak in all of the experiments performed (Figure 9c) and by the integral values increasing with the analyte concentration (Figure 9d). It should further be noted that in all samples no dye was visible by eye, proving the higher sensitivity of the fluorescence readout on the paper.

In an additional experiment, commercially available nitrocellulose membranes (thickness = 240–280 μm) on clear and white polyester backings were compared to eucalyptus paper sheets of different thicknesses in a simplified fluorescence assay using biotin–streptavidin affinity tags. Although nitrocellulose is derived from cellulose through chemical treatments, its structure and properties differ from those of conventional cellulose papers. The experimental results show that higher fluorescence signals could be achieved on eucalyptus paper substrates (see SI, Figure S14). Especially in the case of 100-μm-thick eucalyptus paper sheets (50 g·m–2), the integrated fluorescence signal peaks were clearly higher than those of both tested nitrocellulose membranes. In addition, the signal enhancement increased with increasing analyte concentration (tested range: 0.3 to 10 μg·mL–1 Streptavidin-Cy5). Interestingly, the background of the nitrocellulose membrane having a white polyester backing resulted in clearly higher fluorescence signal intensities (see SI, Figure S15).

Conclusions

Paper-based substrates are, with very few exceptions, only used to a somewhat limited extent for diagnostic or complex analytical processes due to problems with the sensitivity and reproducibility of the readout. The latter is especially true when results are compared on types of paper that are not identical. Results obtained on one paper grade are difficult to transfer onto another paper grade, even if only one parameter such as paper grammage is varied. In terms of achieving proficient test sensitivity, paper-based diagnostics are mainly limited by light scattering and nonspecific adsorption, especially when fluorescence readout is performed, which is the method of choice in high sensitivity diagnostic processes. While strong light scattering is detrimental for the transmission of light through the paper, this can actually improve the net readout signal for fluorescence detection, because the amount of scattering enhances the excitation efficiency of probes in deeper layers. However, it is interesting to note that even in paper more than 100 μm thick, most of the dye molecules can contribute to the signal.

In contrast to solutions printed onto planar surfaces—as is common in microarray technology, which is the method of choice for most biomolecules—the liquid imbibes when printing on paper-based devices from aqueous solutions, and the probe molecules are distributed over the entire thickness of the paper. This essentially results in a 3D readout of the assay, and the signal intensity strongly depends on probe molecule location and distribution inside the paper sheet.

While the paper-intrinsic parameters describe the distribution of liquid and scattering, the nonspecific adsorption on the paper surfaces also plays an important role. When the papers contain protein-repellent coatings, paper-based devices can deliver excellent signals in a fluorescence-based readout.

In summary, fluorescence-based assays can produce highly sensitive and reproducible paper-based devices, thus posing an attractive alternative to colorimetric, nitrocellulose-based assays. Crucial to this is that (a) the nonspecific adsorption of the biomolecules to be detected is prevented, e.g., by coating of the fibers with a surface-attached hydrogel, and (b) paper type and paper-intrinsic parameters such as grammage are carefully chosen for the problem at hand. This could open the avenue for a much wider use of paper-based devices in analytics and pave the way for a simple and affordable diagnostic.
