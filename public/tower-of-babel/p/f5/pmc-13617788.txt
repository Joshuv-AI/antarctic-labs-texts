Self-directed learning readiness in preclinical medical education: Patterns, variation, and academic outcomes from a mixed longitudinal and cross-sectional study
Authors: Oheneba Boadum, Timothy D Wilson, Kenneth Thompson, Ellen M Robertson, Andrew Notebaert
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe transition from undergraduate education to medical school demands increasing learner autonomy and self-regulation. Guided by Zimmerman’s Self-Regulated Learning (SRL) theory and Grow’s Staged Self-Directed Learning Model, this study examined how self-directed learning readiness (SDL-R), encompassing learning motivation, planning and implementation, self-monitoring, and interpersonal communication varies across the preclinical curriculum and how these domains relate to academic performance.MethodsA mixed longitudinal and repeated cross-sectional study (2022–2024) was conducted among preclinical medical students (N = 807 responses; 434 unique students from three cohorts, Classes of 2025–2027) at an LCME-accredited US medical school. All enrolled first- and second-year students were eligible; recruitment was voluntary via web-based REDCap surveys administered once per semester. Linear mixed-effects models evaluated within-student change in total SDL-R and its four domains across semesters. Ordinary least-squares regression with cluster-robust standard errors assessed the contribution of standardized subscale scores to cumulative grade point averages. Bonferroni correction was applied within each family of comparisons, with effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals reported.ResultsSDL-R scores varied across preclinical semesters in a pattern consistent with developmental progression, with a modest decline in first-year Spring followed by recovery through year two. Planning and implementation was the strongest positive predictor of GPA in Year 1 (β = 0.142, p = .004) and in the combined model (β = 0.154, p < .001). The SDL-R domains collectively explained 22.2% of GPA variance in Year 1 (R² = 0.222) and 9.0% in Year 2 (R² = 0.090); the combined model explained 15.0% (R² = 0.150). Interpersonal communication showed a significant negative partial association with GPA, consistent with a statistical suppressor effect. Second-year students, older learners, males, and higher-achieving students showed higher SDL-R levels.ConclusionSDL-R is a dynamic, context-sensitive competency during preclinical training, with planning and implementation as its strongest academic predictor. Targeted curricular interventions that scaffold metacognitive planning, self-monitoring, and adaptive strategy use may enhance both academic performance and lifelong learning capacity.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12909-026-09867-4.Keywords: Self-directed learning, Self-regulation, Mixed-effects model, Longitudinal study, Academic performance, Preclinical medical education, Lifelong learning

Introduction

Transitioning from undergraduate studies to medical school is especially challenging during the preclinical years, as students must adapt to heavy workloads, manage time effectively, and navigate a more autonomous academic environment [1, 2]. In the United States, medical school is a four-year graduate-level program entered after completion of a bachelor’s degree. The first two years, referred to as M1 and M2, constitute the preclinical phase, in which students focus on foundational biomedical sciences through lecture, laboratory, problem-based learning, and small-group formats before advancing to clinical rotations in years three and four. This transition demands a qualitative shift in how students approach learning: from externally structured undergraduate instruction to a more self-directed, cognitively demanding environment in which learners must assume ownership of their strategies, resources, and progress. These challenges are frequently described as stressful and demanding, particularly in health-related programs [3].

Within this context, self-directed learning (SDL) has evolved from its andragogical roots to become a central pillar of medical education, emphasizing learner autonomy and lifelong learning [4–6]. Self-directed learning is defined as “a process in which students take the initiative, with or without the help of others, in diagnosing their learning needs, setting goals, identifying resources, implementing strategies, and evaluating outcomes” [4] (p. 18). Self-directed learning readiness (SDL-R) reflects a learner’s psychological preparedness to engage in this process and is associated with self-efficacy, motivation, and professional competence [7, 8].

The Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME) — the nationally recognized accrediting authority for allopathic medical education programs in the U.S. and Canada, mandates inclusion of SDL experiences in medical curricula under Standard 6.3 [5], underscoring its centrality to physician formation. To meet this expectation, many medical schools have adopted active and collaborative instructional approaches, such as problem-based learning, flipped classrooms, and longitudinal case discussions, to promote SDL [9, 10]. These methods are particularly important for preclinical students preparing for board examinations, who must independently integrate institutional and third-party resources. In this setting, institutional efforts to scaffold SDL intersect with learners’ responsibility to assume ownership of their own learning strategies. Thus, while medical school curricula provide SDL opportunities, students remain responsible for selecting and applying resources that align with their learning strategies — a shared responsibility that defines modern medical education. This autonomy can create tension between institutional expectations and individual approaches to exam preparation.

Research consistently shows that high-performing students succeed not simply through resource availability but through strategic, self-regulated study [11]. Learners with higher metacognitive awareness demonstrate greater SDL readiness [12, 13], and SDL-R tends to be higher among older students and those with greater prior academic experience [14, 15], consistent with andragogical principles linking accumulated life experience and metacognitive maturity to greater learning autonomy. Students often begin medical school uncertain of their capabilities but gain confidence and autonomy over time [16], a pattern consistent with Grow’s Staged Self-Directed Learning Model, in which learners progress from dependent to self-directed participants through iterative stages of autonomy [17, 18]. Importantly, this progression is not automatic: instructors must adjust their roles according to learner readiness, and mismatched levels of guidance can impede growth [17–19]. Subgroup analyses in this study were guided by theory and prior literature. Semester variation tests whether SDL-R is sensitive to curricular timing and progression, consistent with Grow’s staged model of autonomy development [17, 18]. Sex differences are examined because prior studies report higher self-reported SDL-R among male students, a pattern attributed to gender-related self-efficacy reporting rather than differences in capability [13]. Age and prior education are examined based on andragogical principles linking accumulated life experience and metacognitive maturity to greater self-direction [4, 6]. Class rank and GPA are included to characterize the relationship between SDL-R and academic performance. Finally, curriculum type is examined because the institution transitioned between discipline-based and integrated formats during the study period, providing a natural comparison opportunity consistent with evidence that instructional design shapes self-regulatory behaviors [20–23].

Although SDL and self-regulated learning (SRL) share conceptual overlap, they are distinct constructs. Self-directed learning emphasizes the learner’s autonomous control of the learning process, whereas SRL describes the internal cognitive and motivational mechanisms: forethought, performance, and self-reflection, that enable such control [20]. In this study, SDL-R, assessed via the Self-Directed Learning Instrument (SDLI), is the primary measured construct; SRL theory serves as the interpretive lens for understanding the processes underlying its four domains: learning motivation, planning and implementation, self-monitoring, and interpersonal communication. These frameworks are applied in a complementary, not interchangeable, manner, and their conceptual alignment is illustrated in Table 1. Integrated curricula emphasizing active learning, feedback, and reflection tend to foster stronger self-regulatory behaviors than traditional lecture-based formats [21–23], making curricular context a theoretically meaningful factor in SDL-R variation.

Despite broad recognition of SDL’s importance, its variation across the preclinical curriculum and predictive relevance for academic performance remain underexplored. Guided by Zimmerman’s SRL theory and Grow’s developmental model, this study investigates how SDL-R varies across the preclinical curriculum and how its domains associate with academic performance. By grounding the investigation in established theoretical frameworks and examining multiple learner and curricular characteristics simultaneously, the study advances prior SDL research in two important ways: it characterizes the within-student variation in SDL-R across consecutive semesters using a longitudinal design, and it situates domain-specific associations with GPA within the broader context of learner characteristics and curricular structure, a dimension that remains underexamined in preclinical medical education.

Materials and methods

Ethics approval

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of The University of Mississippi Medical Center (UMMC-IRB-1046) and conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Participation was voluntary, and informed consent was obtained electronically from all participants prior to data collection.

Study design and setting

This study employed a mixed longitudinal and repeated cross-sectional design, grounded in Zimmerman’s SRL theory and Grow’s Staged Self-Directed Learning Model. In the United States, medical school is a four-year graduate-level program entered after completion of a bachelor’s degree. The first two years (M1–M2) constitute the preclinical phase, focused on foundational biomedical sciences through lecture, laboratory, problem-based learning, and small-group learning. The final two years (M3–M4) involve clinical rotations. Most US medical schools are accredited by the Liaison Committee on Medical Education (LCME), the nationally recognized accrediting authority for allopathic medical schools in the U.S. and Canada [5]. This study focuses exclusively on the preclinical (M1–M2) phase.

The study was conducted at the University of Mississippi Medical Center School of Medicine, an LCME-accredited institution that embeds SDL throughout its curriculum via problem-based learning, independent study periods, faculty mentorship, small-group sessions, and virtual simulations. During the study period, the preclinical curriculum transitioned from a traditional, discipline-based structure to an integrated, systems-based model — providing a natural comparison opportunity for examining SDL-R differences by curricular context.

Participants and recruitment

All first- and second-year (M1–M2) medical students enrolled at UMMC during 2022–2024 were eligible to participate. Clinical students and those who chose not to participate were excluded. Recruitment occurred via email announcements distributed by an honest broker at the University’s center for informatics and analytics department at the start of each survey window. Participation was entirely voluntary and uncompensated, and students could withdraw at any time without penalty.

Three successive cohorts (Classes of 2025–2027) were surveyed once per semester (mid-semester: October and March), producing six time points (Fall 2022–Spring 2024). Of 807 total survey responses from 434 unique students, 267 (61.5%) completed surveys in two or more semesters and contributed to longitudinal mixed-effects models (195 with 2 time-points; 40 with 3; 31 with 4). The remaining 167 students (38.5%) contributed to cross-sectional descriptive analyses only. A participant flow diagram is provided as Supplementary Fig. 1. Response rates ranged from 23% to 96% per semester; the lower rate for the Class of 2025 in Fall 2022 (23.4%) reflects that this cohort entered the study mid-program during M2, having not been surveyed during M1.

Self-Directed Learning Instrument (SDLI)

All surveys were administered electronically via REDCap, a secure, validated web-based data-capture platform [24, 25]. Self-directed learning readiness (SDL-R) was assessed using the validated 20-item SDLI, rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = Strongly Disagree to 5 = Strongly Agree) [26]. The SDLI demonstrates excellent internal consistency (α ≈ 0.90–0.94) and robust validity across medical and allied health contexts [27–29]. Its four domains, conceptually aligned with SRL phases for interpretation are: Learning Motivation (LM, 6 items; forethought), Planning and Implementation (PI, 6 items; forethought–performance), Self-Monitoring (SM, 4 items; performance–reflection), and Interpersonal Communication (IC, 4 items; cross-phase social regulation). Total scores range from 20 to 100.

Reliability was assessed across all preclinical semesters (807 responses). The total scale demonstrated excellent internal consistency (α = 0.903). Three of the four subscales exceeded the commonly accepted 0.70 threshold for educational research: LM (α = 0.761), PI (α = 0.857), and SM (α = 0.836). The IC subscale (α = 0.629) fell below this threshold, consistent with its brief, heterogeneous four-item composition [30]. All findings involving IC should therefore be treated as exploratory.

Academic performance

Participants self-reported their cumulative GPA from the preceding semester (4.0 scale). GPA data were available from M1 Spring onwards. Students beginning their first semesters in medical school (M1 Fall students) reported perceived academic rank (top 25%, middle 50%, lower 25%) instead since they had no medical school GPA to disclose. Self-reported GPA has demonstrated strong concurrent validity with official transcript records (r = .82–0.90) [31]. In the current dataset, perceived rank correlated strongly with self-reported GPA among students reporting both measures (r = .79, p < .001, n = 546), supporting its validity as a proxy for incoming M1 students. Demographic variables collected included academic year, biological sex, age range, curriculum type, prior degree, and semester. All identifiers were removed by an Honest Broker during de-identification.

Data analysis

All analyses were performed using IBM® SPSS® Statistics (Version 30) with a two-tailed α = 0.05. Although some variables deviated from normality, the large sample size (N > 800) supports reliance on the Central Limit Theorem, whereby the sampling distributions of means approximate normality, justifying the use of parametric procedures [32–35]. All analyses followed STROBE recommendations for observational studies, and effect-size interpretation adhered to Cohen’s (1988) guidelines (β ≈ 0.10 small, 0.30 medium, 0.50 large) [36].

Linear mixed-effects models with random intercepts for students were used to examine within-student change in SDL-R across semesters. In primary models, semester was treated as a categorical fixed effect (four levels; reference = M1 Fall), enabling period-specific contrasts; a secondary model additionally treated semester as a continuous linear variable (coded 1–4) to characterize overall trend. Academic year, age group, biological sex, prior degree, and curriculum type were evaluated as covariates. The intra-class correlation coefficient quantified between-student variance clustering.

Cross-sectional comparisons were examined using one-way ANOVA with post hoc pairwise tests. To account for multiple comparisons, Bonferroni correction was applied within each family of related tests (the four domain-level ANOVAs plus total SDL-R constituted one family; corrected α = 0.010). For regression analyses, ordinary least-squares models with cluster-robust standard errors (cluster = participant ID) were fitted with standardized SDLI subscale scores entered simultaneously. FDR correction (Benjamini–Hochberg) was applied to control for multiple comparisons in regression analyses. Prior to modelling, inter-domain correlations and variance inflation factors (VIF) were examined to assess multicollinearity. Variance-explained statistics (R²) are reported for the four-domain models estimated on the pooled multi-cohort sample; given the modest size of individual cohorts, cohort-specific models were not used to estimate variance explained.

Female students represented a slight majority in every term (55–60%). The three cohorts contributed data across six preclinical semesters (Fall 2022–Spring 2024), with the majority enrolled in the integrated systems-based curriculum. Demographic distributions were broadly comparable across cohorts, indicating stability over the two-year observation window.

Longitudinal patterns in SDL-R across preclinical semesters

SDL-R scores varied across preclinical semesters in a pattern consistent with developmental progression (Fig. 1). Pairwise analyses of repeated participants revealed a modest decline from M1 Fall to M1 Spring (Δ ≈ −0.9%, p = .018), followed by increases through M2 Fall (+ 2.4%, p = .025) and M2 Spring (+ 1.2%, p = .012), yielding a net rise of approximately 3.6% over two years. The secondary continuous-time model showed a small, nonsignificant overall linear slope (βstd = + 0.03, 95% CI − 0.03 to + 0.10, p = .30), indicating that SDL-R gains occurred most prominently at curricular transition points rather than as a uniform linear trend.

Mean total SDL-R and domain scores across preclinical semesters (longitudinal sample, n = 267). Total SDL-R (left axis) and the four SDLI domain scores (right axis) are plotted with ± 1 SE bars. Asterisks (*) indicate significant pairwise semester-to-semester differences in total SDL-R, positioned at the midpoint of each significant transition (M1 Fall to M1 Spring: p = .018; M1 Spring to M2 Fall: p = .025; M2 Fall to M2 Spring: p = .012; Bonferroni-corrected)

At the domain level, planning and implementation increased significantly across semesters (βstd ≈ + 0.07 to + 0.09, p ≈ .01–0.04), reflecting gradual strengthening of strategic and organizational learning behaviors. Learning motivation showed a small decline (βstd ≈ − 0.08, p ≈ .03). Self-monitoring and interpersonal communication demonstrated minimal overall change. The intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC = 0.29) indicated that approximately 29% of total variance in SDL-R scores was attributable to stable between-student differences, confirming that a random-intercept model structure was appropriate for these analyses.

Cross-sectional differences in SDL-R

Within first-year cohorts, learning motivation was consistently the highest-rated domain and planning and implementation the lowest. No significant within-semester or between-cohort differences in total SDL-R were observed (F(3, 475) = 0.78, p = .51, η² = 0.019). Interpersonal communication differed significantly between cohorts after correction (F = 5.76, p < .001, η² = 0.068), with the Class of 2026 scoring higher. In second-year cohorts, no significant differences emerged between-cohorts. Cross-sectional comparisons between M1 and M2 students showed that M2 students reported significantly higher total SDL-R (M = 83.4 ± 8.6 vs. 81.1 ± 8.8; F(1, 805) = 9.66, p = .002, η² = 0.031) and scored higher in PI (F = 15.23, p < .001, η² = 0.019) and SM (F = 13.81, p < .001, η² = 0.017; Fig. 2). Learning motivation and IC did not differ significantly (both p > .40).

SDL-R differences by demographic and academic variables

Biological sex

Sex differences in SDL-R were consistent across both preclinical years. In M1 students (n = 479), male students reported higher total SDL-R than females (M = 82.42 vs. 80.16; F(1, 477) = 5.64, p = .018, η² = 0.012), with a significant advantage in PI after Bonferroni correction (F = 7.72, p = .006); LM and SM were marginal (p = .019 and p = .032 respectively, not meeting corrected α = 0.010); IC did not differ (p = .620). In M2 students (n = 328), the same directional pattern held (M = 84.73 vs. 82.23; F(1, 326) = 5.20, p = .023, η² = 0.016), with marginal advantages in PI (F = 4.56, p = .033) and SM (F = 5.87, p = .016); neither reached the corrected threshold. LM and IC did not differ significantly in either year.

When combined across all preclinical students (male n = 355, female n = 452), these patterns were confirmed with greater statistical power (M = 83.41 vs. 80.97; F(1, 805) = 11.46, p < .001, η² = 0.014). After Bonferroni correction (α = 0.010), significant advantages were observed in LM (F = 8.52, p = .004), PI (F = 13.15, p < .001), and SM (F = 10.86, p = .001); IC showed no sex difference (F = 0.03, p = .864). Effect sizes were small throughout (η² = 0.010–0.016; Fig. 3).

Age

Total SDL-R increased progressively across age groups, peaking among students aged 32–35 years before declining slightly in those older than 35 years. Mean scores were: 20–23 years (M = 80.72, n = 413), 24–27 years (M = 82.91, n = 328), 28–31 years (M = 85.93, n = 42), 32–35 years (M = 87.17, n = 18), and > 35 years (M = 82.67, n = 6). The overall effect was significant (F(4, 802) = 5.07, p < .001, η² = 0.025). Post-hoc Tukey comparisons indicated that students aged 24–27 years scored significantly higher than those aged 20–23 years (p = .029), as did students aged 28–31 years (p = .014); no other pairwise comparisons reached significance. After Bonferroni correction (α = 0.010), significant age-related differences were confirmed for LM (F = 4.17, p = .002, η² = 0.020), PI (F = 3.88, p = .004, η² = 0.019), SM (F = 4.57, p = .001, η² = 0.022), and total SDL-R; IC did not vary significantly (F = 1.20, p = .309).

Prior education

Overall SDL-R did not differ significantly by highest prior degree (F(1, 805) = 0.99, p = .32). After Bonferroni correction, no domain-level differences met the corrected threshold, including the self-monitoring comparison (p = .059), indicating that any advantage conferred by advanced prior education does not persist once multiplicity is appropriately controlled.

Class rank and GPA

Among the longitudinally tracked cohort (Class of 2026), 78.4% of students accurately predicted their class rank before examinations, while 22% misestimated (10% under-predicted, 12% over-predicted). Across all students, perceived rank categories (top 25%, middle 50%, lower 25%) showed a significant SDL-R gradient (lower 25%: M = 75.72; middle 50%: M = 81.50; top 25%: M = 86.12; F(2, 804) = 45.80, p < .001, η² = 0.102; Fig. 4). After Bonferroni correction, domain-level analyses confirmed significant effects for LM (F = 30.57, p < .001, η² = 0.071), PI (F = 64.80, p < .001, η² = 0.139), and SM (F = 31.81, p < .001, η² = 0.073); IC did not differ across rank categories (F = 0.74, p = .479).

When analyzed using self-reported GPA data (n = 546), the pattern was consistent and statistically stronger. Total SDL-R differed significantly across GPA terciles (lower: M = 79.07; middle: M = 82.26; top: M = 85.71; F(2, 543) = 20.26, p < .001, η² = 0.069). Domain analyses confirmed significant effects for LM (F = 11.63, p < .001, η² = 0.041), PI (F = 36.22, p < .001, η² = 0.118), and SM (F = 15.08, p < .001, η² = 0.053); IC showed no significant gradient (F = 0.34, p = .711). The PI gradient was particularly pronounced (lower: M = 21.58; middle: M = 23.65; top: M = 25.20), reinforcing its role as the domain most tightly coupled with academic achievement.

Class-specific analyses confirmed this performance-linked gradient within all four cohorts (all overall p ≤ .002), with planning and implementation and self-monitoring again the most discriminating domains; full cohort-level statistics are reported in Supplementary Table S3.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate a consistent, performance-linked gradient in SDL-R across all cohorts and both perceived and self-reported academic standing, most pronounced in planning and implementation and self-monitoring — the domains most directly linked to strategic execution and metacognitive control.

Curriculum type

Two successive M1 cohorts exposed to different curricular structures were compared across all preclinical semesters in which curriculum type could be determined (traditional discipline-based: n = 519; integrated systems-based: n = 288). The traditional format was associated with significantly higher total SDL-R than the integrated format (M = 82.82 ± 10.22 vs. M = 80.64 ± 10.13; F(1, 805) = 8.44, p = .004, η² = 0.010) after Bonferroni correction. Domain-level analyses revealed significant differences in IC (F = 15.57, p < .001, η² = 0.019) and SM (F = 12.95, p < .001, η² = 0.016), both favoring the traditional curriculum; PI approached but did not meet the corrected threshold (F = 5.74, p = .017); LM did not differ (p = .940).

The following analyses examine whether these domain-level differences translate into predictive associations with academic performance in multivariable models.

SDL-R and academic performance

Prior to modeling, inter-domain correlations were examined (Supplementary Table S2). Values ranged from r = .364 (PI–IC) to r = .693 (PI–SM), with no pair exceeding 0.70. VIF statistics across all three regression models ranged from 1.37 to 2.65, well below the conventional 5.0 threshold, indicating that multicollinearity did not meaningfully distort coefficient estimates.

In Year 1, GPA showed significant positive bivariate correlations with LM (r = .324), PI (r = .380), and SM (r = .258), while IC showed a near-zero bivariate correlation (r = − .081, p = .234). In Year 2, the same directional pattern persisted with weaker associations: LM (r = .138), PI (r = .280), SM (r = .219), and IC (r = .028, ns).

Within-year regression models indicated that the four SDL-R domains collectively explained 22.2% of GPA variance in Year 1 (R² = 0.222, adjusted R² = 0.207, F = 15.18, p < .001) and 9.0% in Year 2 (R² = 0.090, adjusted R² = 0.079, F = 8.03, p < .001). The combined model explained 15.0% of total GPA variance (R² = 0.150, adjusted R² = 0.144, F = 23.83, p < .001). Across all models, PI was the strongest positive predictor (Year 1: β = 0.142, 95% CI 0.045–0.238, p = .004; Year 2: β = 0.137, 95% CI 0.060–0.214, p < .001; Combined: β = 0.154, 95% CI 0.094–0.215, p < .001). Learning motivation was a significant positive predictor in Year 1 (β = 0.119, p = .006) but not in the combined model after adjustment. SM was not independently significant in any model. Interpersonal communication showed a significant negative partial association with GPA in Year 1 (β = −0.155, 95% CI − 0.229 to − 0.081, p < .001) and in the combined model (β = −0.104, 95% CI − 0.151 to − 0.058, p < .001). This pattern: near-zero bivariate correlations with GPA yet significant negative partial coefficients in adjusted models, is consistent with a classical statistical suppressor effect. Interpersonal communication correlates moderately with the other SDL-R predictors (r = .364–0.453); when this shared variance is partialled out, a conditional negative relationship with GPA emerges. Removing IC from the combined model reduces R² from 0.150 to 0.119 and attenuates LM and SM coefficients, confirming IC’s role as a suppressor variable. However, this should not be interpreted as indicating that interpersonal skills impede academic performance; it is a mathematical property of the model structure, not a substantive finding.

Discussion

This study examined SDL-R among preclinical medical students using a mixed longitudinal and repeated cross-sectional design, grounded in Zimmerman’s SRL theory and Grow’s Staged Self-Directed Learning Model. Because this was an observational study, all reported associations are correlational; causal conclusions about SDL-R’s role in academic outcomes cannot be drawn. With this caveat, the findings offer several theoretically coherent and practically relevant insights about how SDL-R varies during preclinical training and how its domains relate to academic performance.

Longitudinal trajectory and developmental patterns

SDL-R scores varied across semesters in a pattern broadly consistent with Grow’s developmental model, suggesting that readiness for self-directed learning is sensitive to curricular demands and structural transitions rather than fixed as a stable trait. The transient M1 Spring decline likely reflects academic fatigue, cognitive overload, and the waning novelty of medical school after the first semester [37]. In Zimmerman’s SRL framework, such conditions may disrupt the performance–reflection balance, reducing opportunities for strategic adjustment and undermining perceived competence. Consistent with this interpretation, the decline was most visible in total SDL-R rather than in any single domain, suggesting a broad, temporary suppression of self-regulatory engagement rather than domain-specific deterioration.

The subsequent rebound through M2 may partly coincide with dedicated USMLE Step 1 preparation periods, which provide structured autonomy, including extended self-study windows, question-bank practice, and spaced retrieval, that reinforce self-monitoring and adaptive strategy use [38–40]. From Grow’s perspective, this phase represents conditions that support later-stage self-direction, where learners function with minimal external guidance. The non-significant overall linear slope further indicates that SDL-R gains occurred non-uniformly, concentrated at curricular transition points rather than accumulating at a steady rate — an important nuance for understanding when interventions might be most effective.

Second-year students scored significantly higher than M1 students in PI and SM; the two domains most directly linked to strategic execution and metacognitive control. This is consistent with Grow’s progression toward autonomous self-direction and with Zimmerman’s view that self-regulatory behaviors are internalized through iterative practice, feedback, and reflection. Learning motivation and IC did not differ significantly between years, suggesting that motivational engagement stabilizes relatively early in training while strategic and reflective skills continue to develop. This pattern reinforces the SRL premise that motivation is necessary but insufficient: it is the forethought-to-performance translation, captured by PI, that distinguishes students who sustain academic performance over time.

Across all cohorts, learning motivation was consistently the highest-rated SDL-R domain while planning and implementation was the lowest — a pattern that persisted regardless of year, sex, or cohort. This gap between motivational readiness and strategic capability is theoretically meaningful: in SRL terms, it suggests that students broadly possess the forethought orientation toward learning but have not yet fully developed the implementation and organizational skills to act on it effectively. This consistent domain-level disparity points to a structural target for curricular intervention, independent of individual learner characteristics.

Demographic and academic variation in SDL-R

Sex differences in SDL-R, with male students reporting significantly higher LM, PI, and SM (all Bonferroni-corrected, η² = 0.010–0.016), were consistent across both preclinical years and in the combined analysis, but small in magnitude. These differences should not be interpreted as reflecting innate or fixed differences in learning capability. Rather, they likely reflect well-documented gender-related self-efficacy reporting patterns, whereby female students systematically underreport confidence in self-regulatory domains relative to comparable performance levels [41–43]. The absence of any sex difference in IC (p = .864) is consistent with prior SDL literature. Educators should be cautious about inferring ability differences from these aggregate patterns; the more productive response is targeted self-efficacy interventions that support confidence in planning and monitoring among all learners.

Cross-sectional cohort comparisons also revealed that IC was the most context-sensitive domain, showing significant differences between the Class of 2026 and 2027 that were semester-specific rather than uniform. This sensitivity likely reflects the influence of peer dynamics, group work structures, and cohort culture on collaborative learning behaviors — factors that vary meaningfully between cohorts even within the same institution and underscore that interpersonal dimensions of SDL-R are shaped more by environmental context than by stable individual characteristics.

Older students demonstrated higher SDL-R in LM, PI, and SM, consistent with andragogical principles linking accumulated life experience and metacognitive maturity to greater autonomy and self-direction [4, 6]. Post-hoc comparisons confirmed that students aged 24–27 and 28–31 years scored significantly higher than the youngest group, though the overall gradient did not extend linearly to the oldest group, possibly reflecting competing personal or professional demands among students older than 35 [44, 45]. The absence of age-related differences in IC suggests that collaborative and interpersonal tendencies remain relatively stable across age groups, independent of the broader developmental trajectory in self-regulatory skills. Prior degree differences in self-monitoring, significant in Year 1, but not surviving Bonferroni correction overall (p = .059) suggest that advanced academic training may confer a modest early advantage in reflective skills that attenuate as all students gain shared preclinical experience, consistent with curricular equalization effects.

Curriculum comparisons revealed that traditional-curriculum students reported significantly higher IC and SM after Bonferroni correction. The SM finding may reflect the sequential, discipline-specific assessment structure of the traditional format affording more frequent opportunities for formalized self-monitoring feedback. The higher IC scores may reflect informal peer networks that develop when students share a fixed course sequence. These findings are preliminary and context-specific: because curriculum type is partially confounded with academic year in this sample, observed differences may reflect year-of-study effects, cohort culture, or assessment demands rather than curricular design per se. Single-institution, naturally occurring curriculum transitions cannot be treated as controlled comparisons. Thus, multi-institutional replication is needed before any conclusions about curricular effects on SDL-R are drawn.

Collectively, these demographic and curricular patterns are consistent with Zimmerman’s triadic model of self-regulation [20], in which personal factors (e.g., age, prior education, self-efficacy), behavioral factors (e.g., planning, monitoring, strategy use), and environmental factors (e.g., curriculum design, assessment structure, peer context) interact dynamically to shape SDL-R. Curriculum functions primarily as an environmental scaffold for the social-regulatory dimension, while individual characteristics govern the cognitive-regulatory domains of motivation, planning, and monitoring — a distinction that has direct implications for how institutions target SDL-R development across diverse student populations.

SDL-R and academic performance

Among the four SDL-R domains, PI emerged as the strongest and most consistent predictor of GPA across both preclinical years and the combined model. In SRL terms, PI reflects the forethought and performance phases: goal setting, resource management, and strategic execution — the processes most directly tied to translating motivation into academic output. Notably, PI was also the lowest-rated domain across all cohorts, underscoring a well-documented pattern in medical education: students enter with strong intrinsic motivation but often lack the organizational and strategic skills to sustain academic performance [46]. Curricula that explicitly teach goal-setting, scheduling, and resource-selection strategies, rather than assuming these skills transfer from undergraduate training, may be likely to have the greatest impact on early academic performance.

The attenuation of R² from Year 1 (22.2%) to Year 2 (9.0%) is theoretically interpretable through Grow’s model: as students advance and SDL-R scores converge toward higher levels, accumulated content knowledge, refined study strategies, and test-taking familiarity assume greater explanatory weight. This does not diminish SDL-R’s practical relevance; rather, it suggests that its predictive influence is strongest during the initial transition into medical school, when students are still establishing self-regulatory habits. By Year 2, SDL-R’s role shifts from differentiating students to serving as a baseline competency that most have internalized to varying degrees.

The IC subscale warrants careful interpretation throughout. Its significant negative partial coefficient in the adjusted models does not indicate that interpersonal skills impede academic performance. This is a mathematical property of the model structure, not a substantive finding, confirmed by the near-zero bivariate correlations between IC and GPA (r = − .016 to − 0.081). Interpersonal communication’s lower subscale reliability (α = 0.629) further limits its interpretation. While IC is undoubtedly important for collaborative learning and professional formation, and likely gains prominence during clinical training, all IC findings in this study should be treated as exploratory pending replication with more reliable measurement.

Implications for medical education

These findings highlight several priorities for preclinical curriculum design. Planning and implementation was both the weakest domain overall and the strongest predictor of GPA, indicating that planning and strategic implementation skills are simultaneously underdeveloped and highly consequential. Moving beyond isolated SDL workshops toward longitudinal embedding of structured planning exercises, post-exam reflective activities, and iterative learning plans with faculty feedback, consistent with LCME Standard 6.3, is likely to yield the greatest returns. The M1 Spring decline signals a vulnerable window for which targeted mid-year support is warranted: advisory sessions, peer-learning communities, or brief SDL reinforcement modules timed to semester transitions could help sustain regulatory engagement. The consistent, performance-linked gradient in SDL-R across perceived rank and self-reported GPA, evident in all four cohorts, further underscores that early identification of students with lower planning and self-monitoring skills, followed by structured coaching, may reduce performance disparities during the preclinical years. A progressive instructional model, with closer faculty guidance in Year 1 transitioning to greater learner independence in Year 2, is consistent with Grow’s staged autonomy framework and may better align instructional support with where SDL-R is most malleable.

Limitations

This study has some limitations. Data were collected from a single LCME-accredited institution, limiting generalizability. Academic performance relied on self-reported GPA (validated at r ≈ .82–0.90 against official records [31]); however, reporting error cannot be excluded. In addition, perceived class rank, while strongly correlated with self-reported GPA in the current sample (r = .79), is a subjective proxy. The IC subscale demonstrated low internal consistency (α = 0.629, below the 0.70 threshold), which may have attenuated IC–GPA relationships, therefore, all IC findings should be treated as exploratory. Voluntary participation may have introduced selection bias, and lower response rates in some semesters may limit representativeness. Despite Bonferroni and FDR corrections, the large number of tests conducted increases Type I error risk. Curriculum type was partially confounded with academic year, limiting interpretation of curricular comparisons. Finally, the observational design precludes causal inference throughout.

Conclusion

Guided by Zimmerman’s self-regulated learning theory and Grow’s staged self-directed learning model, this study demonstrates that SDL-R is a dynamic, context-sensitive competency that varies throughout preclinical medical education in response to curricular demands, learner characteristics, and institutional structure. Students generally enter medical school with strong motivation, but academic performance was most closely associated with planning, implementation, and self-monitoring — the domains reflecting strategic execution and metacognitive control. The transient M1 Spring decline followed by recovery in M2 illustrates how curricular timing and structural supports shape SDL-R, with transitions representing the periods of greatest malleability and intervention opportunity.

Interpersonal communication, while not a direct GPA predictor in the preclinical phase, remains essential for professional formation and collaborative learning and should be cultivated alongside intrapersonal regulatory skills. Aligning curricular design and assessment practices with self-regulatory learning cycles — through longitudinal planning scaffolds, formative feedback, and autonomy-supportive structures timed to transitional periods — can help students translate motivation into effective, lasting learning behaviors.

These findings advance prior SDL research by characterizing domain-specific variation in SDL-R across semesters, cohorts, and learner characteristics within a theoretically grounded framework, and offer a foundation for future multi-institutional investigation into how preclinical curricula can best foster lifelong, adaptive learning.
