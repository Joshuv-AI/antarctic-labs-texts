A Narrative Review of Local Therapies in Hidradenitis Suppurativa: Treatment Landscape and Current Issues
Authors: Elisa Molinelli, Davide Febi, Edoardo De Simoni, Maria Luisa Dragonetti, Irene Richiardi, Matteo Candelora, Daisy Gambini, Sara Belleggia, Guendalina Lucarini, Oriana Simonetti
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is an insidious, chronic inflammatory skin disease, leading to a negative impact on a patient’s quality of life and a poor prognosis. The pathogenesis of HS is complex and multifaceted including genetic, sex hormones, smoking, obesity, and immune dysregulation as the main contributing factors to the development of the disease. HS is challenging to treat and a multimodal approach, combining topical treatment, systemic therapy, and lifestyle modifications, is a strongly recommended strategy to manage the disease. Regular skin care, topical therapy, and intralesional intervention are the mainstay of the management in HS as monotherapy in mild disease, and in combination with systemic antibiotic or immunomodulatory agents in moderate-to-severe HS. The main aim of this review is to examine local therapeutic interventions that are currently available and under investigation for the treatment of HS.

Introduction

HS is a chronic, relapsing inflammatory skin disease characterized by inflammation of the terminal hair follicle-glandular apparatus. It manifests with painful nodules, abscesses, persistently draining fistulas, and scarring [1, 2]. It commonly affects the apocrine gland-rich areas, especially the axillae, the mammary, the inguinal, the gluteal, and the anogenital regions.

HS is a challenging and progressive disease, with a complex pathogenetic mechanism and a wide range of clinical manifestations and associated conditions [3, 4]. The management is equally intricate and there is no a single valid therapy, but a combination of different strategies, which include general measures (such as weight loss, smoking cessation, and healthy lifestyle), topical medication, systemic treatments (biologics, antibiotics, retinoids, anti-inflammatory, and hormonal agents), and surgical approaches, aimed to reduce the impairment caused by the disease [5–11].

Biologic agents and small molecules have revolutionized the therapeutic armamentarium of HS, similarly to what has been observed in psoriasis [12–15]. Local therapies, however, remain a cornerstone in the management of the disease, both as a first-line treatment in mild HS and as a complementary option in moderate-to-severe cases [16].

This review aims to analyze the diverse local therapy options that represent the first-line therapy for the forms of mild disease (Hurley stage I) and may serve as complementary therapies in moderate–severe disease. Local therapies include topical treatment with antiseptics, keratolytics, and antibiotics, but also intralesional treatments, photodynamic therapy (PDT), and emerging options such as topical JAK-I [17, 18].

Methods

Bibliographic searches for qualitative review were conducted in PubMed up to 5 April 2025, with no date limits, by using the terms: (hidradenitis suppurativa OR acne inversa) AND (antiseptic agents) (hydrogen peroxide), (povidone iodine), (chlorhexidine gluconate), (pyrithione zinc), (triclosan), (bleach baths), (benzoyl peroxide), (gentian violet), (keratolytic agents), (azelaic acid), (topical retinoid), (resorcinol), (topical antibiotics), (clindamycin), (topical hormonal therapy), (finasteride), (clascoterone), (intralesional therapy), (corticosteroid), (BTX), (PDT), (topical JAK-I). Two authors (E.M. and D.F.) independently screened all titles and abstracts. Articles were limited to human data and those in English, German, and Spanish language. All types of epidemiological studies and case series regarding local treatments for HS were included and analyzed. This article is based on previously conducted studies and does not contain any new studies with human participants or animals performed by any of the authors. Reviews, guidelines, and conference abstracts were excluded. Studies and cases were excluded if clinical details were lacking. The articles considered relevant by both researchers were included in the analysis. When eligibility was in doubt, a third author (E.D.S.) was involved. Full texts of the included articles were reviewed, and reference lists were manually searched and were checked for additional sources.

Results

Bibliographic Search

A total of 259 entries were identified from the electronic database search. Once duplicates were eliminated, titles and abstracts reviewed and inclusion and exclusion criteria applied, 19 articles representing 456 patients were eligible for review.

Antiseptics

Antiseptics, also known as disinfectants, are chemical agents primarily used to reduce the risk of infection in intact skin or minor wounds. The choice of skin cleanser is often empirical, as no specific data exist for single agents. Although the evidence is limited and often discouraging, local washes with chlorhexidine, benzoyl peroxide, or zinc pyrithione are widely used in clinical practice. In addition, antiseptics are frequently combined with topical antibiotics to reduce the risk of antibiotic resistance, which is increasing in worldwide and HS populations, as well as for chronic colonization by Staphylococcus aureus and the presence of biofilm structures, which can impair wound healing and promote treatment failure [17, 19–23].

Hydrogen Peroxide

Hydrogen peroxide (HP) is a widely available biocide with nonspecific activity against viruses, bacteria, yeasts, and spores [21]. The bactericidal activity is limited, with greater effectiveness against Gram-positive organisms. Catalase-positive organisms were found to be more resistant to HP at lower concentrations [24]. Despite its antimicrobial properties, HP has been shown to be directly cytotoxic to keratinocytes, and even at very low concentrations, inhibiting keratinocyte migration and proliferation [23, 25]. Although no studies specifically focused on the antiseptic action of HP on HS lesions, several studies reported its use in other contexts. HP was added to standard skin preparation to reduce Cutibacterium acnes-positive culture rates in shoulder surgery [26], and it was used in the treatment of impetigo as an alternative to topical antibiotics [27].

Povidone-iodine

Iodophors are complexes of iodine and organic carrier compounds with a broad spectrum of activity against bacteria and fungi. They were formulated to be less irritating and allergenic, compared with pure iodine solutions, although they are less potent in their antimicrobial activity [23]. Povidone-iodine, a commonly used iodophor, has been reported in the treatment of HS, demonstrating rapid bactericidal, tuberculocidal, and virucidal effects through the release of free iodine radicals, which target free amino acids [24]. Iodophors require at least 2 min of contact to release the free iodine necessary for antibacterial activity. Iodophors are used most commonly for preoperative skin preparation [21, 28]. Povidone-iodine, a complex of bactericidal iodine and the polymer polyvinylpyrrolidone (povidone), is available in various commercial forms, including cleansers, surgical scrubs, and ointments. It is effective against methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and Enterococcus species; clinically significant resistance to povidone-iodine has not been documented [9]. The use of povidone-iodine in HS is largely limited to the prevention of surgical site infections [29].

Chlorhexidine Gluconate

Chlorhexidine is a broad-spectrum biocide belonging to the biguanide class. Its role has expanded as an effective and versatile agent for both infection control and prevention. Chlorhexidine gluconate has been shown to be active against a wide range of Gram-positive and Gram-negative bacteria, yeasts, and molds [30]. Its efficacy is highly pH-dependent, with maximum effectiveness occurring within 20 s. Literature reports the superiority of chlorhexidine to povidone-iodine and to other antiseptics in reducing colonizing flora both immediately and for several days after application [31–33]. Its use has been beneficial in reducing the incidence of bacterial resistance compared with oral antibacterial therapy [34]. In addition, chlorhexidine has proven useful in the decolonization of MRSA carriers [35, 36]. Although the risks of allergic response and anaphylaxis with use of chlorhexidine gluconate are rare, the FDA has found a total of 52 case reports between 1969 and 2015. All cases involved serious allergic responses; half of the reactions were considered life threatening and two resulted in death [37]. Some case reports also describe the possibility of type IV allergies related to the topical application use of chlorhexidine [38–41]. Although these reactions are uncommon, clinicians should be aware of the FDA-reported cases of severe anaphylaxis and take these risks into consideration when selecting chlorhexidine as an antiseptic agent.

Topical Zinc Pyrithione

Zinc pyrithione (ZPT) is a coordination complex of zinc usually found in anti-dandruff products. It has fungistatic and bacteriostatic properties, acting via the disruption of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) levels and protein synthesis. It also has anti-inflammatory activity, but the exact mechanisms is still unclear [42]. The antibacterial effect of ZPT is superior to chlorhexidine gluconate; specifically, bacterial suppression was greater at 72 h after application of ZPT compared with chlorhexidine gluconate [43]. It is particularly indicated for hair-bearing areas, such as the axillae and groin [44].

Triclosan

Triclosan is an halogenated bisphenol derivative with broad-spectrum bactericidal activity, although it is ineffective against Pseudomonas species [24, 45]. While triclosan monotherapy shows limited activity against Pseudomonas spp., it has demonstrated an adjuvant effect in disrupting Pseudomonas biofilms when used in combination with oral aminoglycosides [46]. In the context of HS, triclosan could be a valid antiseptic for the management of the disease. It is also widely used in consumer products, such as soaps, detergents, toothpastes, and cutting boards [47].

Bleach Baths (Sodium Hypochlorite Solution)

Dilute bleach baths (sodium hypochlorite) are a well-established antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory treatment for atopic dermatitis [48], and owing to their effectiveness, have also been used in the treatment of HS. However, no clinical studies have specifically evaluated the efficacy of bleach baths in HS 25. Despite sodium hypochlorite’s broad-spectrum activity against both Gram-positive and Gram-negative organisms, concerns have been raised regarding its potential cytotoxicity. A series of in vitro studies demonstrated that maximal killing of community-associated MRSA isolates required a 5-min exposure to hypochlorite at a concentration of 2.5 ml/ml [49]. Although bleach demonstrates activity against biofilms, it has been shown to have incomplete bactericidal activity even at high concentrations [50, 51].

Benzoyl Peroxide

Benzoyl peroxide (BPO), a powerful oxidizing agent, has broad-spectrum bactericidal effects. It is available in gels, creams, lotions, and washes, with concentrations ranging from 2.5% to 20% [23]. In the management of acne vulgaris, it is used predominantly in combination with antibiotics, such as clindamycin and erythromycin. The topical combinations were clinically more effective than topical antibiotics alone, preventing antibiotic resistance. These formulations are also commonly used for the topical treatment of HS lesions [52]. A randomized, controlled, assessor-blinded, intra-patient pilot trial, demonstrated similar clinical results for the topical application of clindamycin lotion and clindamycin-BPO gel in patients with mild–moderate HS [22]. The results, showing a 50% efficacy in both groups after 12 weeks, were consistent with previous studies investigating the efficacy of topical clindamycin monotherapy in patients with mild–moderate HS [9, 22, 53]. The similar outcomes observed with clindamycin-BPO gel suggest that this combination formulation is equivalent to topical clindamycin monotherapy. A shift from clindamycin monotherapy to a combination formulation in patients with mild–moderate HS may help control the emergence of bacterial resistance, as also advised in the treatment of acne vulgaris [22].

Gentian Violet

Gentian violet is a triphenylmethane dye made of hexamethylrosalin, available as an over-the-counter aqueous solution. Its popularity has grown owing to its antimicrobial properties and low cost [54]. While its antibacterial mechanism of action is not well characterized, gentian violet has demonstrated efficacy against MRSA, with complete eradication of skin colonization after 11 days of daily application of 0.1% gentian violet ointment. In addition, gentian violet has antifungal properties. A study evaluating its MIC50 and MIC90 against Candida albicans found similar results to fluconazole [54, 55]. Gentian violet has also been shown to facilitate wound healing in various dermatologic conditions and has been used prophylactically for preventing percutaneous site infections [54, 56–63]. Adverse effects of gentian violet include stinging, contact dermatitis, staining, irritation, mucous membrane lesions, epistaxis, and keratoconjunctivitis [64–67]. Serious side effects, including necrosis of the oral mucosa and skin, have been reported when applied orally and topically [68, 69]. Owing to its influence on thyroid peroxidase, gentian violet may cause hypothyroidism [70]. However, in general, it is considered a safe and well-tolerated therapeutic option for various dermatologic conditions, with most adverse effects described in case reports using different formulations and strengths [54].

Keratolytic Agents

Follicular occlusion is a crucial event in the pathogenesis of HS, caused by infundibular keratosis and hyperplasia of the follicular epithelium [71]. Local cell damage, resulting from mechanical friction and exogenous stimuli, favors the release of damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and allows microbial components to penetrate the follicles, causing follicular dilation and rupture [51]. The release of follicular content into the dermis induces a significant inflammatory response, leading to the formation of nodules, abscesses, and, subsequently, fistulas and scar tissue—key features of severe HS. Therefore, the pathogenetic role of follicular occlusion as the initial event in early lesions explains the rationale behind the use and effectiveness of keratolytic agents in the topical treatment of HS lesions.

Azelaic Acid

Azelaic acid (AzA) is a topical organic acid, traditionally used in acne vulgaris and acne rosacea. Literature data support the use of AzA as an adjunctive treatment for HS, particularly in pediatric patients, when combined with topical clindamycin [21, 34]. A 20% AzA cream has demonstrated a keratolytic effect on both normal and acne-affected skin, associated with a decrease in the synthesis of filaggrin (a protein that aggregates keratin filament). This reduction in follicular hyperkeratosis may partly explain its anti-acne action. AzA also exhibits bacteriostatic and bactericidal properties against a variety of aerobic and anaerobic microorganisms. Topical application of 20% AzA cream led to significant reductions in the density of cutaneous Micrococcaceae and intrafollicular Propionibacterium sp., as well as a decrease in the free fatty acid content of skin surface lipids. However, it did not significantly alter sebum production, sebum composition, or sebaceous gland morphology [72].

Topical Retinoids

Retinoids are a class of compounds chemically related to vitamin A. Both topical and systemic retinoids are primarily used in HS owing to their keratolytic activity and anti-inflammatory properties [10]. Although no formal evaluation of the efficacy of topical retinoids in HS has been conducted, third-generation topical retinoids (adapalene, tazarotene) have demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory activity. Despite the follicular occlusion paradigm being questioned, these retinoids are still considered effective for local managing HS lesions [21].

Resorcinol

Resorcinol is structurally and chemically related to phenol, and it is used mainly in the management of acne for the peeling effect. It has keratolytic, antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antipruritic properties, primarily by modulating prostaglandin formation [73]. The prevalent mechanism of action of resorcinol in HS seems to be related to the keratolytic effect at the follicular infundibulum [74]. Peeling effects typically begin at a concentration of 15% in most patients [75]. Although the use of resorcinol in HS has been poorly described in literature, it is usually employed for the short-term management of HS flares [73]. At the concentrations used in HS, the antiseptic properties tend to predominate over the keratolytic effects [21]. Benefits were observed in patients with Hurley stage I and II diseases, with twice-daily application for 30 days resulting in a reduction in lesion size, pain, and erythema. We recently demonstrated the efficacy of 15% resorcinol as a prolonged treatment (12 weeks), leading to a reduction in the number and size of HS lesions and significantly extending disease-free survival. The study also confirmed that 15% resorcinol is a safe treatment; irritation, desquamation, and brown discoloration were generally well-tolerated [9]. Furthermore, in a recent retrospective study involving 134 patients with mild-to-moderate HS, topical 15% resorcinol demonstrated superior clinical outcomes compared with topical 1% clindamycin over a 12-week period, with significant improvements in HS clinical response, pain, and severity scores, supporting its role as a valid alternative to topical antibiotics in order to limit antimicrobial resistance [9].

Topical Antibiotics

Clindamycin 1% topical lotion or solution is recommended in European and American guidelines as a first-line treatment for HS. With activity against anaerobic streptococcal, and staphylococcal species, clindamycin reduces cutaneous inflammation and inhibits biofilm formation in HS. It is most effective in less severe disease (Hurley stage I or mild stage II) particularly for cases with superficial pustules and without deep abscesses [17]. The European S3, European HS Foundation, and HS ALLIANCE guidelines recommend twice-daily application for up to 3 months [17]. However, prolonged treatment is discouraged owing to the risk of developing bacterial resistance [17]. This resistance is increasingly prevalent in HS, driven by the extensive use of topical and systemic antibiotics, which may reduce antibiotic efficacy and increase susceptibility to infections [19]. Therefore, as in acne vulgaris, products combining clindamycin with BPO are preferred, as studies suggest that the combination of antibiotic and antiseptic may lower the risk of developing antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains [22].

Topical Hormonal Therapy

Finasteride

Androgens are integral to skin physiology and may contribute to HS progression. However, studies on topical anti-androgen therapy for HS are limited. Recently, a case series was published describing four patients who applied commercially available topical finasteride to 2–3 HS sites, with a dosage of 50 μl of 2275 mg/ml per area. In this small cohort, clinical improvement in HS-affected areas and enhanced quality of life (QoL) were observed after 3 months of topical finasteride application. While androgens have an undisputed role in the onset of HS, current recommendations for hormonal therapies are based on limited evidence. Topical finasteride, through its inhibition of local hyperandrogenism driven by in situ androgen production, seems to be a promising local strategy. However, larger studies are needed to corroborate these findings [76].

Clascoterone

Clascoterone (cortexolone 17α-propionate) cream 1%, an anti-androgen agent approved for acne vulgaris, works by competitively inhibiting the dihydrotestosterone (DHT) binding to androgen receptors [77].

Systemic anti-androgen therapies, such as spironolactone, have shown efficacy in treating HS, and published data indicates that testosterone levels in patients with HS are not significantly different from those in non-HS, implying that androgen activity in HS may relate to the local conversion of testosterone to DHT [77].

Since clascoterone targets locally DHT, it may provide benefit in HS by mechanisms similar to those observed in acne treatment, reducing inflammation and sebum production [77].

Particularly, clascoterone could be useful in a subgroup of patients with HS with hormonal imbalance. In addition, it may serve as an alternative for male patients to avoid the systemic side effects associated with spironolactone. While further studies are needed to confirm the efficacy of clascoterone in HS, its potential as a targeted, localized treatment makes it a promising therapeutic option for future exploration [77].

Intralesional Injection Therapy

Intralesional Steroids

Intralesional triamcinolone is an effective rescue therapy to reduce the inflammation, pain, and swelling from inflammatory nodules and abscesses in HS [78], either as monotherapy or in association to systemic treatment [79]. The mechanism of actions linked to activating intralesional glucocorticoid receptors, which inhibit the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines [80]. Triamcinolone injections at dose of 5–10 mg/ml (volumes ranging from 0.2 to 2 ml) have been shown to significantly reduce lesion size, redness, edema, and suppuration in HS, with patients experiencing rapid pain relief within 1–3 days. Possible complications include skin atrophy and telangiectasias, and injections are contraindicated if bacterial infection is suspected [78]. An interventional study demonstrated clinical and symptomatic improvement in 36 out of 37 patients after 4 weeks, using ultrasound-guided injections of triamcinolone combined with lincomycin [81].

Botulinum Toxin

There are seven isoforms of BTX (BTX type A, B, C, D, E, F, and G), with BTX type A (BTX-A) and BTX type B (BTX-B) being commercially available. BTX blocks the release of acetylcholine and other neurotransmitters from presynaptic vesicles [82].

Botulinum Toxin Type A

BTX-A is an exotoxin produced by Clostridium botulinum [83]. Although the precise mechanism of action of BTX-A in HS is not fully understood, it is hypothesized that the efficacy is related to the reduction of sweating (thus controlling bacterial proliferation and inflammation) and may also affect follicular glands, preventing the rupture and diffusion of follicular material that leads to inflammation and sinus tract formation [84]. It was hypothesized that hyperhidrosis may act as a comorbidity contributing to the development of HS. BTX-A reduces sweat secretion by blocking acetylcholine release in overactive cholinergic nerve fibers [83]. The sweat reduction limits the skin microbiota’s ability to produce proinflammatory effects. In addition, BTX-A inhibits the secretory function of the entire hair follicle, including sebaceous and apocrine glands, limiting the rupture of follicle the and diffusion of follicular material into the dermis, reducing inflammation, and preventing sinus tract formation [64–85]. Currently, all relevant reports on BTX-A injection in the management of HS have shown that this method has a positive therapeutic effect and patient satisfaction. BTX could be a cost-effective treatment for HS, even at Hurley stage III. However, large-scale, prospective, controlled clinical studies are still lacking [83].

Botulinum Toxin Type B

BTX-B has a weak effect on α-motor neurons and muscles, compared with BTX-A, with a potency ratio favoring BTX-A by 1:50–100 [86]. BTX-B, which has a similar effect of BTX-A on sweat glands, is indicated making it ideal for treating larger body areas without the risk of muscle weakness. A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study evaluated the efficacy of BTX-B in patients with HS. In total, 20 patients with Hurley stage I–III HS were assigned either a placebo or BTX-B. After 3 months, all participants received BTX-B, with a follow-up at 6 months. The study demonstrated that BTX-B significantly improved QoL and self-reported symptoms. Lesion counts also decreased in the active group at both 3- and 6-month assessments, and former placebo patients showed similar improvements after receiving BTX-B. Contraindications for BTX-B include pregnancy, breastfeeding, and neuromuscular diseases. No adverse effects were reported [86].

Photodynamic Therapy

PDT has recently been explored as a treatment for patients with HS, with conflicting results. PDT consists in the application of a topical photosensitizing agent that, when irradiated with a specific light source, generates reactive oxygen species [87]. Currently, only two photosensitizers are approved for topical use: a 20% 5-aminolevulinic acid (ALA) solution used with blue light and a 10% ALA gel activated by red light. Only a few case reports and case series have reported the use of PDT in HS and no randomized controlled trials have been conducted to provide conclusive evidence. Owing to insufficient data, PDT has a low recommendation level in HS treatment guidelines. PDT options vary depending on the wavelength of light used, distinguishing mainly between blue- and red-light PDT.

Blue-Light PDT

There are only three case series examining the use of blue-light PDT for HS, involving a total of 13 patients, but the Hurley stages were not specified in any of the studies. Each study utilized 20% ALA with an irradiation period ranging from 15 min to 1.5 h. While all three studies agree on the efficacy of blue-light PDT, providing level III evidence, they were rated as poor quality with a high risk of bias [18, 88, 89].

Red-Light PDT

Red-light PDT has been proposed as a treatment option for recalcitrant HS. The treatment involves the topical application of 20% ALA to the affected area, followed by a variable period of occlusion, and subsequent irradiation with a red light (630 nm) source. Several case reports and case series have demonstrated the efficacy of red-light PDT in reducing the number of lesions and promoting wound healing [90, 91]. However, a study by Strauss et al. [30] showed no improvement in four patients treated after 8 weeks and suggested that the failure of PDT might be related to the deep lesions of HS, which may prevent the diffusion of ALA in the affected areas [92]. To address this, a case report of intralesional application of ALA showed efficacy in treating deeper lesions, suggesting that the lack of effectiveness of topical ALA may be because of the type and depth of the lesions, with deeper and more severe lesions benefiting less from the treatment owing to the limited penetration of topical ALA cream [93].

Intralesional PDT (iPDT)

The use of PDT with a 630-nm intralesional diode and 1–5% ALA was evaluated in four studies, involving a total of 110 patients [93–95]. In 53/68 (83.8%), moderate-to-complete remission was achieved. In 42 patients, a high percentage of lesion resolution or improvement was observed [93]. Two additional studies employed a 1% intralesional methylene blue solution as the photosensitizer, injected into HS lesions under ultrasound guidance in a total of 48 patients. After a 15-min incubation period, the lesions were illuminated using a 635-nm red light-emitting diode lamp [96, 97]. In the first study, five out of seven patients (71%) showed clinical improvement and sustained remission of HS in the treated area [96]. In the second study, a ≥75% reduction in the maximum lesion diameter was observed in 24 out of 41 patients (58.5%) [97].

Emerging Therapies

Topical Janus Kinase Inhibitors

Recent studies have emphasized the pivotal role of cytokines in the inflammatory processes of HS, noting an overexpression of interleukin (IL)-17A, IL-26, IFN-γ, IL-27, and IL-1β, accompanied by a downregulation of IL-22. This shift in focus has redirected research from bacterial involvement to the impact of cytokines in HS pathogenesis. Topical JAK-I have emerged as a promising therapeutic option, particularly for patients with mild-to-moderate HS. The ability of JAK-I to modulate key inflammatory pathways, including IL-6 and IL-23 signaling, offers a targeted approach to managing the localized inflammation characteristic of early-stage HS. Recent investigations into topical formulations, such as ruxolitinib cream, have provided preliminary evidence supporting their efficacy and safety in this setting. In a phase II randomized controlled trial, 69 patients with mild-to-moderate HS (Hurley stage I–II) were treated with 1.5% ruxolitinib cream applied twice daily for 16 weeks. Preliminary results, presented at the AAD 2024, demonstrated a significant reduction in abscess and inflammatory nodule count and improved Hidradenitis Suppurativa Clinical Response (HiSCR) rates compared with the vehicle group. In a pilot open-label trial (NCT04414514), ten patients with Hurley stage I-II HS were enrolled, and six completed the study. Preliminary results showed that five out of six patients achieved the HiSCR after 16 weeks of therapy. Histological analyses also revealed a reduction in epidermal and dermal inflammation, with improvements in inflammatory infiltrate composition. These findings support the rationale for further exploration of ruxolitinib cream as a topical treatment for HS [98, 99]. Patient-Reported Outcome Measures

Patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) are essential in HS, as they capture the symptomatic and psychosocial burden of the disease that may not be fully reflected by clinician-reported severity scores. Generic quality-of-life instruments, such as the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), are widely used to assess the overall impact of skin diseases. However, their ability to reflect HS-specific symptoms, such as, pain, drainage, malodor, and functional impairment is limited [100]. For this reason, the Hidradenitis Suppurativa Quality of Life (HiSQOL) questionnaire has been developed and validated as the first HS-specific PROM, providing a more accurate and disease-focused assessment of physical, emotional, and functional domains [101]. In addition to QoL tools, symptom-based measures such as the pain and itch numerical rating scales (NRS) are frequently employed to quantify the immediate symptomatic burden of HS [102–104].

PROMs are particularly relevant when evaluating local therapies. Many topical and intralesional interventions primarily reduce symptoms, especially pain, tenderness, and drainage, before significantly altering lesion counts. Incorporating PROMs alongside clinician-reported outcomes therefore allows a more comprehensive assessment of therapeutic benefit. Future studies on topical and intralesional treatments should systematically include PROMs as key secondary or co-primary endpoints, to better reflect the real-world impact of local interventions from the patient’s perspective.

Discussion

Local therapies for HS include a wide range of treatments comprise antiseptics and topical antibiotics, hormonal agents, intralesional approaches, and physical therapies. These treatments primarily target the inflammatory and infectious mechanisms involved in HS pathogenesis, and they are predominantly used as adjuncts to systemic treatments mainly antibiotics and biologics.

Although the European Guidelines for HS treatment reported that there is no scientific evidence to recommend the use, antiseptics cleansers are widely prescribed in clinical practice [16].

Antiseptics, mainly chlorhexidine, BPO, ZPT, and triclosan with antibacterial and anti-inflammatory properties, reduce bacterial colonization in HS-affected areas, which actively contribute to local inflammation, limiting HS acute flare. Chlorhexidine, which is effective against a broad spectrum of bacteria, and ZPT, which is additionally acts as an antifungal, demonstrates significant antibacterial effects [23, 44].

Moreover, as observed in the management of acne, the combination of antiseptics with topical and systemic antibiotics could mitigate the risk of antibiotic resistance, a rising problem in the management of HS. In addition, for pediatric HS, where systemic therapies might not yet be appropriate, antiseptics and topical antibiotics could represent the first-line treatment [16].

Follicular occlusion has a crucial role in HS pathobiology. Thus, keratolytic agents such as resorcinol, have been shown to effectively reduce HS lesions, preventing follicular blockage, rupture, and subsequent inflammation. Specifically, resorcinol has antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory, and antipruritic effects, and data from literature have shown that its prolonged application can lead to significant reductions in inflammatory lesion count [9, 71].

Clindamycin 1% is the only topical antibiotic specifically indicated in the management of HS and recommended across treatment guidelines. However, evidence suggests that it is not significantly more effective in reducing lesions than the nonantibiotic agent resorcinol, and it presents the added risk of antibiotic resistance. As a result, the clindamycin-BPO combination could be preferred to minimize the arising problem of antibiotic resistance. Given the lower resistance potential, antiseptics and keratolytics may be more suitable for long-term management over topical antibiotics [17, 22, 79].

Although not completely understood, the multifactorial nature of HS pathogenesis, includes potential hormonal influences, with androgens suspected of exacerbating the disease. This hypothesis supports trials investigating anti-androgenic molecules, such as finasteride and clascoterone, as topical treatments. However, existing data on these treatments are limited, and further studies are necessary before considering these agents as reliable options in HS [76, 77].

Intralesional therapies, including corticosteroids and BTX, offer alternative treatment approaches. Corticosteroids administered intralesionally have been effective in managing acute flare, reducing inflammation, pain, and size of nodules and abscesses. However, studies on corticosteroid use are limited and often involve a combination with antibiotics mainly lincomycin [105].

BTX, particularly BTX-B, is believed to reduce inflammation by decreasing sweat and sebaceous gland activity in the areas affected by HS. BTX-B, in contrast to BTX-A, has minimal effects on α-motor neurons, making it more suitable for larger areas of application without inducing muscle weakness. Both toxins have shown positive therapeutic outcomes and patient satisfaction in limited studies, but challenges in clinical practice remain owing to cost and the need for skilled administration [86].

PDT using blue or red light has also been explored in HS treatment, though with controversial results. The high cost of PDT, the need for multiple treatment sessions, and limited availability, as well as challenges with patient compliance, limit its practicality as a mainstream option. Nonetheless, PDT remains a consideration for recalcitrant HS cases, and ongoing studies may clarify its potential role and efficacy in HS management [106].

Newer topical treatments, such as JAK-I, represent an emerging frontier in HS therapy. Although still under clinical investigation, these agents offer hope as targeted treatments for the inflammatory cytokine pathways central to HS. With further study, these therapies may provide additional options for local HS management in the future [107, 108].

Conclusions

HS treatments can be broadly categorized into local and systemic therapies. Systemic therapies are generally the most effective, especially in cases of moderate-to-severe HS. In mild cases, local treatments may serve as the primary approach, though they are more commonly used as adjuncts to systemic therapies. Local treatments play a vital role in reducing inflammation and providing an antiseptic effect, which helps to limit antibiotic resistance, a growing issue that increasingly necessitates the use of advanced systemic antibiotics. Currently, the most widely used local treatments in HS management are antiseptic agents, particularly chlorhexidine gluconate, and keratolytic agents such as resorcinol. Topical antibiotics, such as clindamycin, should be limited owing to the risk of developing antibiotic-resistant bacterial strains. Persistent resistant strains in affected skin areas contribute to ongoing, hard-to-control inflammation. Furthermore, evidence suggests that topical clindamycin may not be significantly more effective than antiseptic or keratolytic treatments alone. Other local therapies, including intralesional injections (e.g., corticosteroids and BTX) and PDT, are less accessible and more costly, with limited data supporting their efficacy. Additional studies are needed to determine if these treatments could be considered viable therapeutic alternatives in patients with HS. A promising new direction involves topical JAK-I currently in clinical trials. These emerging therapies could potentially represent a significant advancement in local treatments for HS.
