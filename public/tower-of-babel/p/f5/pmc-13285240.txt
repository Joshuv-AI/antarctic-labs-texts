Agreement and 2‐year trajectories of youth‐ and parent‐reported health‐related quality of life in youth with epilepsy treated with surgery vs medical therapy
Authors: Mary Lou Smith, Klajdi Puka, Elysa Widjaja
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis cohort study examined the agreement between youth‐ and parent‐reported health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) of children treated with epilepsy surgery compared to medical therapy. A second aim was to evaluate whether changes in HRQOL over 2 years differed between the two groups, and to determine whether these trajectories differed by informant (youth or parent).MethodsA total of 255 youth–parent dyads participated, of whom 95 youth underwent epilepsy surgery and 160 continued medical management. Participants completed the KIDSCREEN‐27 at baseline, 6‐month, 1‐year, and 2‐year follow‐up. Youth–parent agreement was evaluated at the individual level as well as at the group level. Linear mixed models were used to evaluate changes in HRQOL over time.ResultsAmong 255 youth, 46% were female, and ages ranged from 8 to 18 years (mean 13 years) at baseline. Most parents were female (85%) and 40–49 years of age (58%). The surgical and medical group were largely similar across baseline characteristics. Agreement across HRQOL domains ranged from fair to good intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC .32–.66). Although average youth–parent differences were small (−1 to +3 points), Bland–Altman plots showed large discrepancies, and substantial differences between dyads were frequent. Youth reported increasing Physical Well‐being and School Environment scores in the first year after surgery and remaining stable or slightly decreasing thereafter. In contrast, parent‐reported trajectories for Physical Well‐being and School Environment did not show a significant difference. For other domains—Psychological Well‐being, Autonomy and Parent Relation, and Social Support and Peers—no differences were identified across treatment groups, and findings were consistent among youth and parent reports.SignificanceThe results emphasize the need for considering the perspective of youth as well as their parents when evaluating the outcomes of surgery or other treatments for epilepsy.

INTRODUCTION

Health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) focuses on one's perception of the impact of health on the ability to live a fulfilling life. 1 , 2 Given that epilepsy is defined as a “disease of the brain characterized by a predisposition to generate epileptic seizures, and by the cognitive, psychological, and social consequences of this condition,” 3 it is appropriate that the International League Against Epilepsy (ILAE) has identified HRQOL as an important outcome in evaluating the impact of epilepsy and of treatment efficacy. 4

Epilepsy surgery is the recommended treatment for focal drug‐resistant epilepsy (DRE) 5 and has been shown to improve seizure control in children with DRE. 6 A systematic review of HRQOL after pediatric epilepsy surgery 7 found that those who had undergone surgery had better HRQOL in comparison with their pre‐operative status and relative to non‐surgical patients with epilepsy. Improvement in HRQOL is evident within the first 6 months after epilepsy surgery, with further improvements over the 1‐year follow‐up, and remain stable at 2 years after surgery. 8 These improvements are seen in the patients who were seizure‐free after surgery, whereas those who continued to have seizures did not show substantial improvements. 9

Most studies involving children and adolescents (hereafter we use the term youth to collectively refer to children and adolescents) have been based on parents reporting on their child's HRQOL. However, HRQOL is a subjective evaluation, and in non‐surgical samples it has been shown that there are discrepancies between self‐report of youth with epilepsy and the ratings given by parents. Youth–parent agreement appears to be strongest for aspects of observable behaviors and less robust for more concealed aspects of HRQOL. 10 , 11 Longitudinal trajectories of HRQOL in non‐surgical samples have also found slight differences in trajectory profiles or great heterogeneity among participants, signaling the need to obtain the perspective of the youth and parent. 12 , 13 , 14

There have been no studies using questionnaires of HRQOL that assessed both parent and youth perceptions of change after surgery, but there is one qualitative study. Thirteen mothers and their adolescents who had undergone surgery 1 to 2.5 years earlier were interviewed separately and asked to comment on their perceptions of the adolescent's functioning and change after surgery in the domains of physical, social, affective/behavioral, cognitive/academic, and family function, areas typically included in investigations of HRQOL. 15 Adolescent and maternal perceptions were notably different. Whereas most adolescents reported only positive changes, mothers simultaneously reported negative changes in addition to positive changes following surgery. Mothers frequently described aspects of their adolescent's life that continued to be of concern, or newly emergent difficulties, whereas adolescents did not.

Given the evidence for discrepancies between youth and parents, it is important to investigate youths' HRQOL outcomes of surgery from both perspectives. The purpose of the present study was to extend the limited research comparing youth–parent views on youth's HRQOL after surgery. We employed an HRQOL questionnaire that had both youth‐ and parent‐report versions, and examined perceptions of change over time, starting with a pre‐surgical baseline and evolution over time after surgery (6, 12, and 24 months). A non‐surgical comparison group of youth with medically refractory epilepsy (on medical therapy only) and their parents were also included. The main aim of the study was to evaluate agreement between youth‐ and parent‐reported HRQOL of youth with DRE. A second aim was to evaluate whether changes in youths' HRQOL over 2 years differed between the surgical and non‐surgical comparison group, and to determine whether these trajectories differed by informant (youth or parent). HRQOL ratings were examined in conjunction with clinical and parent/family characteristics that have been found in previous studies to influence HRQOL. 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 18

METHODS

Study design and setting

Data came from the Impact of Pediatric Epilepsy Surgery on Health‐Related Quality of Life (PEPSQOL) study. 8 , 16 This multicenter, prospective cohort study followed youth with DRE treated with epilepsy surgery or medical therapy at eight centers in Canada. Participants were recruited from 2014 to 2019 from the Hospital for Sick Children in Toronto, BC Children's Hospital in Vancouver, Alberta Children's Hospital in Calgary, CHU Ste‐Justine in Montreal, The Children's Hospital in Winnipeg, Royal University Hospital in Saskatoon, McMaster Children's Hospital in Hamilton, and London Health Sciences Centre in London.

The study protocol was approved by the research ethics boards of the participating hospitals, and informed consent and assent was obtained from parents and youth. We followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting guideline. 19 Data were collected and managed using the Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap 20 ).

Participants

In the PEPSQOL study, participants were 4–18 years of age with suspected focal epilepsy, as assessed by clinical semiology and/or electroencephalography (EEG), and were being evaluated for epilepsy surgery candidacy. The present cohort is a subset of the larger PEPSQOL study sample and included youth aged 8–18 years who completed the self‐report version of the KIDSCREEN‐27, 21 which is validated for youth 8 years and older. Exclusion criteria included previous epilepsy surgery, prior or planned palliative surgery (e.g., corpus callosotomy or vagus nerve stimulator placement), neurometabolic disorders, neurodegenerative disorders, genetic epilepsy syndromes, and epileptic encephalopathies not amenable to surgery. All patients underwent detailed clinical assessment, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), video‐EEG, and neuropsychological testing, supplemented by additional neurodiagnostic tests as deemed necessary for epilepsy surgery evaluation. The decision to exclude a youth from epilepsy surgery was made as part of clinical care, for reasons such as inability to localize or lateralize a single epileptogenic focus, and anticipated neurological deficits if surgery were to proceed. The medical group consisted of youth who, after undergoing a standard evaluation for epilepsy surgery candidacy, were deemed ineligible for surgery.

Youth and their parents each completed questionnaires (described below) at the time of surgical evaluation (i.e., baseline) and on average 6, 12, and 24 months after surgical evaluation (medical patients) or after surgery (surgical patients). Only dyads with both youth‐ and parent‐rated HRQOL at any timepoint were included, since youth–parent agreement could not be evaluated without paired data.

HRQOL outcome

Youth and parents each completed the KIDSCREEN‐27 to report on youth's HRQOL across five domains. 21 These domains include: Physical Well‐being (physical activity, energy, fitness, and perceived health); Psychological Well‐being (positive emotions, life satisfaction, and absence of negative affect); Autonomy and Parent Relation (perceived autonomy, finances, and quality of support from parents); Social Support and Peers (peer interactions and social support); and School Environment (perceived cognitive capacity and school environment). Further details about the domains are presented in Table S1. Response options across the 27 items are based on five‐point Likert scales that indicate intensity or frequency. T‐scores are derived for each subscale based on international norms, where 50 reflects average HRQOL in the population (standard deviation [SD] = 10). Values above 50 indicate better‐than‐average HRQOL, whereas values below 50 indicate lower‐than‐average HRQOL. The KIDSCREEN has demonstrated robust psychometric properties. 21 , 22 , 23

Clinical, parent, and family measures

In addition to HRQOL, child clinical data were obtained from medical records, including age, sex, age at seizure onset, side and site of seizure focus, and number of anti‐seizure medications (ASMs). Parents were asked to report on their child's seizure frequency and on parent characteristics such as age, sex, employment status, household income, education, and marital status. Parents also completed three measures of family environment. The Family Adaptability, Partnership, Growth, Affective, and Resolve (Family APGAR 24 , 25 ) scale assessed satisfaction with family relationships. Family resources were examined using the family mastery and health, and extended family social support subscales from the Family Inventory of Resources for Management (FIRM 26 ), which measures the availability of family resources to assist family adaptation for stressful life events. Family demands were evaluated using the Family Inventory of Life Events and Changes (FILE 27 ), which assesses normal and nonnormal life events experienced by the family. The reliability and validity of all three measures of family functioning have been established previously. 24 , 26 , 27

Statistical analyses

Youth–parent agreement was evaluated at the individual level as well as at the group level. At the group level, intraclass correlation (ICC) for the absolute agreement was calculated to quantify the agreement between youth and parent‐proxy report. Consistent with past research, an ICC of ≤.40 was considered poor to fair agreement, .41–.60 moderate, .61–.80 good, and .81–1.00 as excellent. 28 In addition, Bland–Altman plots were used to visualize the magnitude and direction of disagreement between youth‐ and parent‐reported HRQOL across domains. The plots display the mean difference between informants and the upper and lower limits of agreement (mean difference ± 1.96 SD), which indicate the range within which most dyad differences are expected to fall. At the individual level, we calculated the proportion of dyads in which youth‐rated HRQOL was higher or lower than parents, as well as the proportion showing concordance. Disagreement was defined as a youth–parent difference greater than .5 SD of the youth‐reported score, a threshold chosen because self‐report questionnaires are regarded as the primary indicator of HRQOL. The KIDSCREEN has no established minimal clinically important difference, and prior HRQOL research consistently identifies .5 SD as a meaningful difference in HRQOL measures. 29 , 30

To evaluate changes in HRQOL over 2 years, linear mixed models were fitted for each KIDSCREEN subscale, including all participants with data in at least two time points. 31 Models included random intercepts accounting for repeated measures over time, along with fixed effects for years of follow‐up (continuous), group (surgery vs medical), and their interaction, as well as baseline variables shown previously to be associated with youth's HRQOL. The baseline variables included clinical (age at onset, number of ASMs, seizure frequency), and parent/family characteristics (parent education, parent marital status, family resources, family relationships, family demands). 13 , 14 , 16 , 17 , 18 The inclusion of random slopes and a quadratic term for years of follow‐up was based on model fit indices (Akaike Information Criterion [AIC], Bayesian Information Criterion [BIC]) and the likelihood ratio test. Study site was not included in the model as a random factor given that its variance was estimated to be zero. The linear mixed model used restricted maximum likelihood estimation (REML), a Gaussian distribution, and Satterthwaite's method to approximate the degrees of freedom. 32 The emmeans package in R was used to decompose interactions. 33 Model assumptions were checked, and no violations were identified.

As a sensitivity analysis, to evaluate the impact of seizure control on HRQOL trajectories, seizure status (seizure‐free in the previous year vs not seizure‐free) was included as a time‐varying covariate in the longitudinal models, replacing the baseline seizure frequency covariate used in the main analyses.

Sample size

A simulation‐based power analyses using the simr package 34 was conducted to estimate a sufficient sample size needed to reliably detect differences HRQOL trajectories over 2 years between surgery and medical therapy, as measured by the KIDSCREEN‐27. Simulations were run assuming mean scores of 40–50 on the KIDSCREEN‐27 at baseline, 35 a SD of 10, and a minimal clinically important difference (MCID) of 5 points (i.e., .5 × SD). In previous HRQOL research following youth with epilepsy over 2 years using the Quality of Life in Childhood Epilepsy (QOLCE) Questionnaire, the correlation of scores among participants (ICC) was .73. 36 Using these estimates, with four measurement occasions and accounting for 20% missing data, the simulations indicated that a linear mixed model with 75 participants per group would provide 90% power to detect the MCID in HRQOL between surgical and medically treated youth at α = .05.

RESULTS

Participants

Overall, 255 youth–parent dyads participated. Of the 479 eligible for the study, 61 declined to participate, 82 did not complete any survey, and 81 were removed because they lacked either youth or parent reports at all timepoints (i.e., did not have paired data at any timepoint). Children who completed the 2‐year follow‐up had baseline characteristics similar to those who did not, with the exception that a higher proportion of parents had a college or university degree among those who completed the 2‐year follow‐up (Table S2). Of the 255 participating dyads, 95 underwent epilepsy surgery and 160 continued medical management. Table 1 presents participant characteristics at baseline. Of the 255 participating youth, 46% were female and on average aged 13 years; their mean age at seizure onset was 7.2 years. Most parents were female (85%) and aged 40–49 years (58%). The surgical and medical group were largely similar across baseline characteristics, although medical patients were more likely to have a bilateral seizure focus, have multi‐lobar or unknown site of focus, and have less‐frequent seizures. Regarding follow‐up, 26% completed only one timepoint, 20% completed two, 22% completed three, and 32% completed all four timepoints. A total of 189 dyads (82 surgery, 107 medical) completed measures in at least two time points. Among participants with data in at least two timepoints, baseline characteristics were similar (Table S3).

Agreement

Agreement between youth‐ and parent‐reported HRQOL varied across domains and timepoints (Table 3). Agreement was good for Physical Well‐being (ICC .60–.66), moderate‐good for Social Support and Peers (ICC .42–.65), and moderate for Psychological Well‐being (ICC .43–.56) and School Environment (ICC .55–.59). Autonomy and Parent Relation showed fair–moderate agreement (ICC .32–.51). Across timepoints, ICCs were generally stable, although Autonomy and Parent Relation showed lower agreement at the 2‐year follow‐up (ICC = .32), and Social Support and Peers showed higher agreement at the 2‐year follow‐up (ICC = .65). Bland–Altman plots (Figure S1) show that the mean difference between informants ranged from −1 to 3 points, indicating minimal systematic bias. However, the 95% limits of agreement were wide, with upper limits ranging from 18 to 26 and lower limits ranging from −13 to −22. A difference of ~5 points is considered clinically meaningful. Similarly, substantial discrepancies were also evident at the individual level (Figure 1). Across most subscales, youth rated their HRQOL higher than parents. For Autonomy and Parent Relations, however, the proportion of youth reporting higher HRQOL was similar to the proportion of parents reporting higher HRQOL. Youth‐ and parent‐reported trajectories

Figure 2 shows youth‐ and parent‐reported HRQOL trajectories over the 2‐year follow‐up, along with estimated mean differences (95% confidence intervals [CIs]) between the surgery and medical therapy groups at each timepoint. Model coefficients are presented in Table S6. For Physical Well‐being and School Environment, youth reports showed a significant interaction and a quadratic effect, with scores similar at baseline, increasing more rapidly for surgical patients at Month 6, peaking around Year 1, and slightly decreasing or remaining stable thereafter. In contrast, parent‐reported trajectories for Physical Well‐being and School Environment did not show a significant interaction, although parents reported improved Physical Well‐being over time among youth in the surgical and comparison group. For other domains—namely Psychological Well‐being, Autonomy and Parent Relation, and Social Support and Peers—no differences were identified across treatment groups, and findings were consistent among youth and parent reports.

Youth‐ and parent‐reported health‐related quality of life (HRQOL) trajectories. The estimated mean difference (95% confidence intervals) between the two groups at each timepoint are also presented.

Sensitivity analyses incorporating seizure control as a time‐varying covariate (Table S7; Figure S3) showed that youth who were seizure‐free reported higher HRQOL in the Physical Well‐being and School Environment domains, controlling for time, group, and other covariates. In addition, in these domains the group‐by‐time interactions were largely attenuated. These findings suggest that the post‐surgical improvement observed in the main analyses was driven by seizure freedom. Another finding was that parents rated higher HRQOL for their children who were seizure‐free in the Physical Well‐being, School Environment, and Psychological Well‐being domains.

DISCUSSION

Consistent with previous findings, 16 aspects of HRQOL in these youth with DRE were lower than that experienced by same‐age peers at baseline. Youth and their parents reported lower ratings in the areas of physical well‐being, psychological well‐being, and social support and peer relations. These findings are consistent with the youths' history of a chronic disease, and with the literature demonstrating that pediatric epilepsy is accompanied by increased risk of having mental health disorders, reduced social engagement, and experiencing social stigma. 37 , 38 , 39 Scores in the School Environment domain were similar to or only slightly lower than norms, which may at face value appear to be surprising given that many youth with DRE experience cognitive impairment and academic underachievement. 40 , 41 However, the questions on the School Environment scale pertain largely to the child's view of the relationship with his/her teachers and whether the child is happy to be at school, rather than focusing on academic performance; only one question asks about cognition (paying attention in school). Parent and youth ratings of Autonomy and Relations with Parents were comparable to norms derived in typically developing youth. This result differs from previous qualitative research that revealed that youth with DRE were of the opinion that their parents are excessively worried about them and as a result they have reduced autonomy with respect to being alone at home and participating in extracurricular and community activities, and that a positive outcome after surgery was a loosening of these restrictions. 42 Research has not questioned parents on child autonomy. The discrepancy between the present finding and this past research may be due to the nature of the data collection, whereby the qualitative interview may allow for greater explanation than a rating questionnaire.

Although other studies on epilepsy have compared parent and youth report of HRQOL, the present study is the first to do so exclusively in youth with DRE; others have had heterogenous samples with respect to seizure severity and whether or not participants had active seizures. 11 , 12 , 43 , 44 Second, that is the only study to date that has examined both parent and youth report on parallel HRQOL questionnaires, comparing the two perspectives before and after surgery. Its strengths lie in its longitudinal design and inclusion of a non‐surgical comparison group. It is an important addition because parents and youth may approach epilepsy surgery with high hopes, not only for seizure control but also for improving other aspects of the functioning of the family and the youth's functioning. 45 It is important to ascertain how both perspectives match the reality of post‐operative outcomes.

The main aim of the study was to evaluate agreement between youth‐ and parent‐reported HRQOL of children with DRE. Findings for the surgical and medical patient groups were similar. In comparing youth and parent report, a striking result was that youth overall rated their HRQOL higher than did their parents. This finding replicates results from other studies on epilepsy comparing parent and youth report. 11 , 43 , 44 , 46 , 47 In addition, parents have been found to report more pervasive difficulties than their children do, affecting more aspects of HRQOL. 45 , 46 Although we did see concordance in some aspects of HRQOL, the degree of such varied across domain as has been shown in other studies. 11 , 13 , 14 , 44 Strong agreement was found for Physical Well‐being and Social Support and Peers, moderate agreement was documented for Psychological Well‐being and School Environment, and the lowest level of agreement (fair‐moderate) was seen for Autonomy and Parent Relation. The first two of these domains may be more objectively observable, leading to the higher rates of agreement. Other studies have shown that youth–parent agreement is more similar for physical domains or more external, easily visible areas of function. 11 , 48 As indicated by the Bland–Altmann plots, the differences between youth‐ and parent‐rated HRQOL were wide, that is, youth and their parents gave very different ratings of HRQOL, which exceeded what would be considered as a clinically meaningful difference. These discrepancies suggest that scores from youth and parent reports are not interchangeable, which is also supported by substantial discrepancies found at the individual level. The large individual differences perhaps reflected variability in epilepsy features, and child and family variables. 16

A second objective of this study was to examine whether changes in HRQOL over 2 years differed between the surgical and non‐surgical comparison group, and to determine whether these trajectories differed by informant. Differences between groups over time were found in some, but not all, domains, and were largely seen in youth report. In the Physical Well‐being and School Environment domains, the youth in the surgical group reported better HRQOL scores over time than did the medically treated group. The ratings of the surgical youth increased from baseline to 6‐months, peaked at 1 year, and thereafter were stable. The parental ratings differed from those of youth, in that parent‐reported trajectories for Physical Well‐being and School Environment did not show these differences between the groups over time. Nonetheless parents in both the surgical and comparison group reported improved Physical Well‐being of their children over the duration of the follow‐up. The results are consistent with those of a qualitative study in which mothers and their teenage children differed in their report of change after epilepsy surgery. 15

Differences in youth vs parent report of Physical Well‐being and School Environment over time between the surgical and medical groups could be related to several reasons. Although children's adaptation to epilepsy as they get older may have contributed to improvement in HRQOL, the effect should be similar in the surgical and medical treatment groups. There could be a differential response shift among parents and youth, resulting in underestimation of the reported improvement in Physical Well‐being and School Environment among parents. Response shift is a psychological phenomenon whereby people change their internal standards, values, or conceptualizations of the target construct such as health status or quality of life, leading to different self‐reports over time. Unmet expectations of the parents may have contributed to differential response compared to youth, and parents may focus more on youth's comorbidities when seizure control has improved. Differential response shift between parent‐ and child‐rated HRQOL has also been reported in children with newly diagnosed cancer. 49 Response shift may vary across different domains of HRQOL. A prior study has shown that parents are likely to positively recalibrate their assessments of their children's physical, emotional, and social functioning 1 year after a new diagnosis of epilepsy. 50 This response shift in the upward direction suggests that positive adaptive process takes place over time in children with newly diagnosed epilepsy. There was no response shift observed for the cognitive functioning domain.

There were changes in agreement over time, most evident at the 2‐year follow‐up, with less concordance in Autonomy and Parent Relation and higher agreement for Social Support and Peers. These changes could potentially reflect developmental changes in the youth that impact function in these domains and reinforce the importance of longitudinal follow‐up that obtains the perspective of both youth and patents. When trajectories were examined for Psychological Well‐being, Autonomy and Parent Relation, and Social Support and Peers, no differences were identified across treatment groups, and youth and parent ratings were consistent.

Seizure freedom was accompanied by better HRQOL, as has been documented in other studies based on parent report. 8 , 9 The current results extend these findings to the opinions of the youths themselves. Previous studies have utilized the QOLCE 51 , 52 to obtain parent report, and the differences in findings on which aspects of HRQOL may have improved could reflect differences in content between that questionnaire and the KIDSCREEN, or that the latter is not an epilepsy‐specific measure. Of note, however, youth who have undergone surgery report greater improvements than the medical group with respect to their physical function and school experience.

The contrast between youth and parent report reinforces the importance of obtaining the perspectives of multiple informants in determining the impact of epilepsy and the outcome of treatments on youth's HRQOL. Eliciting only one perspective may result in missing important changes or lack of changes that could influence the youth's life. Where discrepancies in report occur, they should be explored to determine why opinions differ, and to suggest ways to open communication further between youth and their parents. Understanding of these differences could potentially lead to next steps for supporting youth.

There are several limitations of this study. We used the KIDSCREEN‐27, a generic dual parent‐ and youth‐rated HRQOL questionnaire, rather than a dual parent‐ and youth‐rated epilepsy‐specific quality‐of‐life instrument, as a dual epilepsy‐specific quality‐of‐life instrument was not available at the time when the PEPSQOL study was implemented. The KIDSCREEN has been used widely across a broad range of pediatric chronic disorders, including pediatric epilepsy 53 , 54 , 55 underscoring its status as a well‐established and validated HRQOL instrument. The Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (PedsQL) Epilepsy Module, a dual parent‐ and youth‐rated epilepsy‐specific quality‐of‐life instrument was validated in 2017, 56 after our PEPSQOL study was implemented. The children with epilepsy quality of life‐25 (CHEQOL‐25) 11 was not used because it instructs parents to respond as they think their child would, rather than to express their own views of their child's HRQOL, which was the issue of interest in the present study. Neurodevelopmental disorders were not assessed and therefore the potential impact of neurodevelopmental status on HRQOL was not evaluated. Multiple tests across domains and informant types may increase Type I error; however, when differences emerged, they were consistently observed in child reports.

CONCLUSION

This study is the first to compare systematically the views of youth and parents regarding changes in youths' HRQOL after pediatric epilepsy surgery. Previous findings that ratings of HRQOL may vary by youth with epilepsy and their parents have now been extended to participants with DRE who undergo epilepsy surgery or are evaluated to determine their candidacy for surgery. The results emphasize the necessity of listening to the perspective of youth as well as their parents when evaluating the outcomes of epilepsy surgery or other treatments for epilepsy. Contrasts with previous findings indicate that the choice of HRQOL instrument can influence where change is perceived and by whom. Future research using an epilepsy‐specific dual parent‐ and youth‐rated HRQOL questionnaire, which may be more sensitive to changes in HRQOL among patients with epilepsy, could determine whether more nuanced differences exist between youth and parent perceptions of change in HRQOL following epilepsy surgery compared with medical therapy.
