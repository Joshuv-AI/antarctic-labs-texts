Two-coordinate Cu(I) sites in ZIF-derived porous carbon enable high CO working capacity for CO/N₂ separation
Authors: Congli Li, Qingmin Hu, Bin Zheng, Zhengxing Lv, Jiahui Yang, Xuling Guo, Zhitong Zhao, Lei Tang, Rufeng Tian, Qi Shi, Zhe Gao, Jinxiang Dong, Yue-Biao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractEfficient separation of carbon monoxide (CO) from nitrogen (N₂) is vital for recycling steel industry by-product gases and advancing carbon-neutral chemical production. However, current Cu(I)-based π-complexation adsorbents suffer from excessive binding strength and low working capacity. Here we report thermally-dispersed open metal sites (OMS) with two-coordinated Cu(I) units, ( ≡ C–O)CuCl, anchored onto zeolite imidazolate frameworks (ZIFs) derived oxygen-containing porous carbons (ZPC), where the Cu···CO interaction strength is precisely moderated. The optimized adsorbent, 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1, with 15 mmol g⁻¹ Cu(I) dispersed in 10–30 Å pores, delivers a high CO working capacity of 3.21 mmol g⁻¹ (0.1–1 bar, 298 K), the highest among reported recyclable Cu(I)-based adsorbents. The analyses reveal that a low-symmetric trigonal ( ≡ C–O)CuCl···CO coordination geometry weakens π-backbonding, enhances working capacity. Process simulations predict that 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 enables high productivity and low energy consumption. This work establishes OMS regulation in porous carbon as an effective strategy for efficient CO/N₂ separation.Subject terms: Metal-organic frameworks, Porous materials, Chemical engineering

Introduction

CO is an important feedstock for fuels, polymers, pharmaceuticals, and other chemicals1–4. CO-rich by-product gases from the steel industry, once used primarily for heat and power5, now represent a valuable resource for sustainable chemical production and carbon emission reduction. After pre-treatment, the by-product gases become a mixture of CO (30–60%) and N2. However, separating CO from N₂ is challenging due to their nearly identical properties6,7, including boiling points (ΔT = 4.3 K), dipole moments (Δμ = 0.11 D), polarizabilities (Δα = 0.21 Å³), and molecular diameters (Δd = 0.12 Å), which limits the effectiveness of cryogenic distillation or physisorption8. Chemisorption via metal···CO π-complexation offers a promising separation strategy9,10, yet conventional desorption remains energy-intensive, requiring very low pressures ( ~ 0.1 mbar) and high temperatures ( > 420 K). Vacuum pressure swing adsorption (VPSA) with practical desorption pressures ( ~ 0.1 bar) at 298 K generally results in limited working capacity—the difference between adsorption and desorption uptakes1,6,11,12 (Fig. 1a).

Featuring strong Cu···CO binding affinity, the classical Cu(I)/zeolite adsorbent CuCl/NaY13 exhibits a CO adsorption capacity of 2.38 mmol g⁻¹ (0.7 bar, 303 K) but a working capacity of only 0.54 mmol g⁻¹. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) with well-defined open metal sites3,14–18, such as the representative Ni-MOF-7410,19, achieves 5.70 mmol g⁻¹ CO uptake at 1.0 bar and 298 K, yet its working capacity is merely 0.70 mmol g⁻¹ (0.1–1.0 bar). Although MOFs can dynamically respond to CO and offer potential for higher working capacities, such as Fe₂Cl₂(bbta)2 reach a high working capacity of 4.07 mmol g⁻¹, but only under energy-intensive pressure–temperature swing conditions (adsorption at 200 mbar and 293 K; desorption at 1 bar and 333 K). MAF-2ME15 with quasi-open Cu(I) sites shows high O₂/H₂O tolerance but with CO working capacity only 0.97 mmol g⁻¹. MAF-2F exhibits a higher initial CO working capacity; however, its cycled sample MAF-2F-CO shows a reduced working capacity (2.70 mmol g⁻¹) after CO adsorption15. These examples highlight the need for moderate metal···CO binding, which can suppress low-pressure unusable uptake while retaining high-pressure adsorption, thereby maximizing working capacity (Fig. 1a).

Compared with the Cu(I)/zeolite13,20,21 and MOFs2,10,14,15,22,23 adsorbents, the Cu(I)/carbon24–26 adsorbent that exhibits a less steep CO adsorption curve have been observed, indicating the presence of moderate Cu···CO binding. However, a lack of fundamental understanding of the coordination environments of Cu(I) sites in Cu(I)/carbon adsorbents is a major roadblock hampering the targeted development and improvement of CO working capacity. Inspired by the design of metal-supported catalysts, we speculate that carbon support can provide a suitable pore structure and neutral functional groups to anchor Cu(I) and form new adsorption sites, which will be discussed in detail below. In addition, current carbon support lack suitable anchoring functional groups and high microporous structure to obtain high content and high-density dispersed adsorption sites, which is also beneficial to obtaining high working capacity. Porous carbon materials derived from ZIFs27–30 have been brought into stage due to the intrinsic advantages of ZIFs such as high porosity and tailorable composition diversity, which might provide infinite possibilities in producing porous carbons with desired pore structures and abundant surface groups.

In this work, we report an unsaturated two-coordinate Cu(I) unit, ( ≡ C–O)CuCl, anchored on a ZIF-derived porous carbon support, exhibiting moderate Cu···CO binding affinity to maximize CO working capacity under mild conditions (Fig. 1b). The porous carbon support was prepared via a two-step process: carbonization of the highly ordered ZIF-8 precursor at 1000 °C followed by KOH activation at 800 °C, yielding a ZIF-8-derived carbon (ZPC) with high specific surface area (3415 m2 g–1), heterogeneous micro–mesoporous structure (10–30 Å), and neutral oxygen-containing surface groups character. Thermal dispersion of Cu(I) on ZPC achieved high loading and uniform distribution (Fig. 1c). The resulting Cu(I)/ZPC exhibits a CO working capacity as high as 3.21 mmol g⁻¹ under mild conditions (0.1–1 bar, 298 K), surpassing previously reported recyclable Cu(I)-based adsorbents and most MOFs with unsaturated metal sites. Furthermore, a five-bed, seven-step VPSA cycle simulation demonstrated high CO/N₂ separation efficiency, delivering a productivity of up to 6.54 mol kg⁻¹ h⁻¹ and energy consumption as low as 1.41 MJ kg⁻¹ CO.

Results

Preparation and characterization of Cu(I) anchored porous carbon adsorbents

The porous structures of carbon support can be readily modulated by the carbonization temperatures, and followed with KOH activation for functionalization. We illustrated the fabrication of porous carbon supports with distinct pore structures by carbonizing the ZIF-8 precursor at 1000 °C and 800 °C, respectively, followed by KOH activation at 800 °C30. The resulting supports, designated as ZPC-1 and ZPC-2, exhibit distinct porous structures: ZPC-1 features a micro-mesoporous structure with a high surface area (3415 m2 g–1) and pore volume (2.01 cm3 g–1), featuring a pore size distribution predominantly within the 10–30 Å range. In comparison, ZPC-2 possesses a mesoporous structure with a larger pore volume of 3.34 cm3 g–1 and a surface area of 2710 m2 g–1, accompanied by a pore size distribution centered in the 30–60 Å range (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and Supplementary Table 1).

The ZPC-1 was chosen as the optimal support by comparison the CO uptakes with maximal Cu(I) via solid thermal dispersion method to achieves both a high content and high density of exposed Cu(I) sites. With the same Cu(I) loading (15 mmol g⁻¹), 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 exhibits a higher CO uptake of 4.63 mmol g−1 (0.82 CO molecules per Cu(I)) compare to15Cu(I)/ZPC-2 (2.82 mmol g−1, 0.50 CO molecules per Cu(I)) at 1 bar (Supplementary Fig. 5 and Supplementary Table 1). These can be attributed to the micro–mesoporous structure (10–30 Å) of ZPC-1 improves CuCl dispersion, exposes more Cu(I) sites and enhances CO adsorption while the larger mesopores (30–60 Å) in ZPC-2 lead to the aggregation of Cu(I) as evident by the XRD: no Cu(I) salts peaks were observed in 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1, whereas distinct CuCl diffraction features are observed in 15Cu(I)/ZPC-2 (Supplementary Figs. 3, 4 and Supplementary Table 1). Furthermore, adsorbents prepared by loading 15 mmol g⁻¹ of Cu(I) onto three commercial carbons with different pore structures also showed lower CO uptake (Supplementary Fig. 6). All these results support the suitable micro-mesoporous structure (10–30 Å) in ZPC-1 associates the dispersion of Cu(I) and anchoring onto the carbon support.

Evaluation of CO/N2 separation performance

Then, the Cu(I) loading from 7 to 15 mmol g⁻¹ was systematically modulated on ZPC-1 to identify the optimal dispersion capacity for achieving superior CO/N₂ separation performance. Single-component gas adsorption isotherms of CO (0-3 bar, 298 K; Fig. 2a) has shown successively increments compared with that of the pristine ZPC-1, attributing to the π-complexation interaction between Cu(I) sites and CO. The ideal adsorbed solution theory (IAST) selectivity of CO/N2 are therefore enhanced by increasing the loading of Cu(I) attributing in improvement of CO uptakes at 1 bar, for 7Cu(I)/ZPC-1, 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1, 13Cu(I)/ZPC-1, and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 as 3.50, 4.17, 4.36, and 4.63 mmol g−1, respectively, along with the decrement of N2 uptake (Figs. 2a, b and Supplementary Table 2). Further increase the loading of Cu(I) has negative effect for the performance (Supplementary Fig. 10). 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 displayed the highest IAST selectivity of 462 for CO/N2 = 50:50, and 517 for CO/N2 = 30:70 at 1.0 bar (Fig. 2b and Supplementary Fig. 11). Under typical pressure of 0.1 bar, 1 bar, and 3 bar, the optimal 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 showed uptakes of 1.42, 4.63, and 5.54 mmol g−1, corresponding to 0.25, 0.82, and 0.97 mol/mol CO/Cu, respectively (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 3), which suggested a unique opportunity to obtain high working capacity (0.1–1.0 bar).

Strikingly, the CO working capacity (298 K, 0.1–1.0 bar) of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1, as high as 3.21 mmol g−1, exceeds those of previously reported recyclable Cu(I)-based adsorbents, and even surpasses those of most MOFs containing unsaturated metal sites (Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 5)1,2,10,13,15,21,22,24–26,31–34. Specifically, the working capacities (0.1–1.0 bar) of Ni-MOF-74 and CuCl/NaY are only 0.55 and 0.52 mmol g−1, respectively (Fig. 2e and Supplementary Table 6). We note that flexible Fe2Cl2(bbta) with unsaturated metal sites has been reported to show a particularly high CO working capacity of 5.40 mmol g−1 (0.1–1.0 bar) based on its adsorption curve, but its working capacity is only 0.51 mmol g−1 based on its desorption curve (0.1–1.0 bar)2. Additionally, MAF-2F exhibits a higher initial CO working capacity of 3.30 mmol g⁻¹ (0.1–1.0 bar), however, its cycled sample MAF-2F-CO shows a reduced working capacity of 2.70 mmol g⁻¹, which can be attributed to the increase in defects and decrease in domain size after CO adsorption/desorption training15. In contrast, 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 exhibits stable recyclability (Fig. 2i, and Supplementary Figs. 15, 28).

Furthermore, the calculated isosteric heat of adsorption (Qst) of CO on 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 at 298 K is 41.6 kJ mol−1, distinctly lower than those of CuCl/NaY (53.4 kJ mol−1) and Ni-MOF-74 (50.7 kJ mol−1) (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Fig. 18). The lower Qst reflects the moderate affinity between CO and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1, resulting in both high CO working capacity and low desorption energy consumption, and its rapid desorption kinetics further enhance its potential for practical separation processes (Supplementary Fig. 20).

Dynamic breakthrough experiments35–37 were further performed to compare the dynamic working capacities of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and the representative CuCl/NaY and Ni-MOF-74. For a CO/N2 mixture (v:v = 30:70), significantly, the order of CO working capacities and regenerabilities was 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 (2.67 mmol g−1, 76.1%) > Ni-MOF-74 (1.41 mmol g−1, 43.1%) > CuCl/NaY (0.82 mmol g−1, 43.6%), which can be ascribed to the unique metal adsorption sites and corresponding moderate metal−CO affinity of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 6). In addition, significantly different breakthrough curves showed the dynamic CO working capacities of both Ni-MOF-74 and CuCl/NaY to be much lower than their dynamic CO adsorption capacities. In contrast, similar breakthrough curves showed the CO working capacity of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 was closest to its adsorption capacity. Breakthrough curves for a CO/N2 (v:v = 50:50) mixture showed the same trend (Fig. 2h and Supplementary Figs. 23–25). In the desorption stage, 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 shows the higher CO desorption amount than Ni-MOF-74 and CuCl/NaY, validating its separation efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 26). During five consecutive recycling tests, the performance of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 was well maintained, with a steady CO working capacity (Fig. 2i and Supplementary Fig. 28). Additionally, 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 was also evaluated under humid conditions and in the presence of other impurity gases (Supplementary Figs. 29, 30). Thus 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 may be regarded as an excellent candidate for real VPSA separation of CO/N2 at fixed operating temperature (298 K) under mild vacuum desorption pressure.

Structural characterization of the adsorption sites

The presence of new adsorption sites with an unsaturated Cu unit (open metal sites) within Cu(I)/ZPC-1 was confirmed by experimental characterization.

To delineate the coordination environment and electronic state of Cu sites in Cu(I)/ZPC-1, X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analysis, extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) determination, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) were conducted. As shown in the normalized Cu K-edge XANES spectra, the near-edge feature of 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 falls between those of Cu2O/CuCl and CuO/CuCl2 (Fig. 3a), implying an intermediate valence state for the Cu sites, Cuδ+ (1 <δ < 2). From detailed analysis of the XANES spectra, the average valence states of Cu sites in 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 were estimated as +1.18 and +1.34, respectively, that is, closer to Cu(I) (Supplementary Fig. 31). The Cu 2 p XPS peak (932.5 eV) combined with the Cu LMM Auger spectrum (571.3 eV) confirmed Cu(I) as the dominant site38,39 (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 32).

The precise chemical configuration and structural parameters of Cu(I) sites in Cu(I)/ZPC-1 were revealed by in-depth analysis of the EXAFS and XPS patterns. Fourier-transform (FT) EXAFS spectra for 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 featured a single distinct peak at 1.73 Å, located between the Cu–O peak (1.47 Å) of Cu2O and the Cu–Cl peak (1.87 Å) of CuCl, attributable to Cu–O/Cl coordination and possibly related to the formation of highly dispersed O–Cu–Cl units in these samples (Fig. 3d). The corresponding wavelet-transform (WT) EXAFS contour plots for 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 showed an intermediate R value (1.76 Å) between those of Cu2O (1.56 Å) and CuCl (1.89 Å) at the maximum intensity (Fig. 3e).

FT-EXAFS fitting results unequivocally confirmed that 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1 possesses a unique O–Cu–Cl configuration (denoted as OCuCl) composed of Cu–O and Cu–Cl bonds, each with a coordination number (CN) of 1.1 (Fig. 3b, Supplementary Fig. 33 and Supplementary Table 7). The CNs of the Cu–O and Cu–Cl bonds in 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 were evaluated as 1.1 and 1.4, respectively, from which it may be speculated that 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 mainly has a two-coordinated OCuCl configuration, but also a secondary tri-coordinated OCuCl2 configuration (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Fig. 34 and Supplementary Table 7). Thus, it is proposed that the main structural configuration of Cu sites in Cu(I)/ZPC-1 is OCuCl, in which Cu is coordinated by one neighboring Cl atom and one neighboring O atom. Moreover, the O 1 s XPS patterns for 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 show the appearance of a new peak at 531.8 eV in comparison with those of pure ZPC-1 (C–O/C = O, 533.5/532.3 eV)40,41, Cu2O (530.4 eV), and CuO (529.7 eV)41,42, which may be attributed to the C–O–Cu bonding arrangement at the ZPC-1 support (Fig. 3g and Supplementary Fig. 35). This implies that CuCl is bonded to surface O-containing groups on the porous carbon ZPC support through Cu–O bonds43. Based on the above results, it is proposed that a new adsorption site within an unsaturated two-coordinated ( ≡ C–O)CuCl unit is formed and docked in Cu(I)/ZPC-1.

For 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 exposed to air (denoted as 10Cu(I)/ZPC-1-invalid and 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1-invalid), XPS and XANES showed predominantly Cu(II) sites (80%), along with a small number of Cu(I) sites, demonstrating that Cu(I) sites are oxidized in air (Supplementary Fig. 38 and Supplementary Table 8). Moreover, FT-EXAFS fitting and WT-EXAFS analysis confirmed that the Cu sites involved coordination by three O atoms and one Cl atom (denoted as O3CuCl), suggesting coordinative saturation of the Cu unit and hence an inability to bond CO (Supplementary Figs. 39, 40 and Supplementary Table 7). Even after air exposure, the adsorbent’s CO adsorption performance can be effectively recovered by vacuum activation (Supplementary Fig. 14).

Theoretical calculations

We subsequently employed density functional theory (DFT) calculations and the natural bond orbital method to elucidate the coordination environment of Cu(I) sites44,45 and the corresponding Cu···CO π-complexation interactions, which are closely related to the working capacity of CO adsorption. Plausible Cu unit models of ( ≡ C–O)CuCl and ( ≡ C–O)CuCl2 for Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2Cu and ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2CuCl for CuCl/NaY were constructed based on EXAFS fitting results. For comparison, ( ≡ C–O)2CuCl were also considered.

Optimal configurations of the plausible models of Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and CuCl/NaY were calculated before and after CO adsorption. After CO adsorption, the two-coordinated Cu models were transformed from linear/right-angle to trigonal coordination geometries, and the tri-coordinated Cu models were transformed from trigonal to tetrahedral coordination geometries (Fig. 4a–e and Supplementary Figs. 41–44). While the manuscript was under review, a paper reported that CoMe-MFU-4l can capture two CO molecules per active site (adsorption capacity of 5.18 mmol g−1 at 0.01 bar and working capacity of 0.27 mmol g−1 in 0.1-1 bar). DFT calculations show that after CO adsorption, the coordination geometry of the Co sites changes from tetrahedral to distorted triangular bipyramidal46.It’s interesting that the CO adsorption models for CuCl/NaY displayed symmetric trigonal/tetrahedral coordination geometries, while in the cases, relatively low symmetric trigonal/tetrahedral coordination geometries are observed for Cu(I)/ZPC-1. Specifically, the configurations for CuCl/NaY have closed O/Cl−Cu···CO angles (138°/144° of ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2Cu···CO; 112°/113°/113° of ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2CuCl···CO), while the configurations for Cu(I)/ZPC-1 has relatively twisted O/Cl−Cu···CO angles (136°/116° of ( ≡ C–O)CuCl···CO; 115°/119°/120° of ( ≡ C–O)2CuCl···CO; 108°/113°/113° of ( ≡ C–O)CuCl2). Thus, in contrast to that of CuCl/NaY, the Cu can bind CO in a weakly linear manner for Cu(I)/ZPC-1 with low symmetric adsorption coordination geometries, which can decrease the overlap degree of the Cu and CO orbitals and corresponding contribution to the CO···Cu σ-donation and d-π∗ backdonation.

Mulliken charges, orbital electron occupancies, and adsorption energies for the plausible models of Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and CuCl/NaY were further analyzed (Fig. 4f and Supplementary Table 10). The Mulliken charges on the O (coordinated with Cu) in the different models are in the order: ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2Cu (–0.92), ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2CuCl (–0.91), ( ≡ C–O)CuCl (–0.56), ( ≡ C–O)2CuCl (–0.53), and ( ≡ C–O)CuCl2 (–0.52). It is obvious that the electronegativity of the anion O in the Cu(I)/ZPC-1 model is relatively lower than that in CuCl/NaY, which implies that less electron is transferred from Cu to ZPC-1 than from Cu to NaY. Thus, the Cu in Cu(I)/ZPC-1 has relatively higher electron occupancy (( ≡ C–O)CuCl: 0.6341) of its outer-shell orbitals than that in CuCl/NaY (( ≡ Si/Al–O)2Cu: 0.1694), which indicates a weakening of electron donation from the highest occupied molecular orbital of the CO molecule to the outer-shell orbitals of Cu and the corresponding CO···Cu σ bond. The above results will further lead to the coordination of CO to Cu in a weakly linear manner for Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and the low overlap degree between the Cu and CO orbitals. The simultaneous weakening of CO···Cu σ-donation and the d-π* back-donation interaction is discussed in detail in the context of the natural bond orbital method below. The adsorption energies results are consistent with the conclusion that Cu(I)/ZPC-1 has a relatively weaker π-complexation interaction with CO compared to CuCl/NaY.

To further analyze the strength of π-complexation, we applied the natural bond orbital method and population analysis of natural atomic orbitals47,48 to determine changes in electron occupancy of Cu orbitals upon CO adsorption in the plausible models of Cu(I)/ZPC-1 and CuCl/NaY. Here, π-complexation bonding between CO and Cu involves σ-donation, d-π* back-donation, and electron redistribution based on the changes in electron occupancy for the plausible models (Supplementary Fig. 55). Here, the increases in total electron occupancy of the 4 s and 4 p orbitals of Cu upon adsorption indicate the contribution from σ-donation (denoted as Σ(4 s + 4 p)). The increase in electron occupancy of the Cu 3 d suborbital upon adsorption indicates electron redistribution from the Cu 4 s orbital to the Cu 3 d suborbital (denoted as Σ(3 d+)). Meanwhile, the decrease in electron occupancy of the Cu 3 d suborbital upon adsorption indicates the contribution from d-π* back-donation and electron redistribution (denoted as Σ(3 d–)) (Fig. 4g). Noted that the total electron occupancies of the Cu 3 d orbitals (Σ(3 d+) and Σ(3 d–)) remain unchanged upon CO adsorption in ( ≡ C–O)2CuCl, at variance with the classical d-π* back-donation description in the literature, which have been discussed in detail in supplementary materials.

Among the two-coordinated models, ( ≡ C–O)CuCl clearly shows smaller electron occupancy changes in its Cu 4 s, 4p, and 3 d orbitals (Σ(4 s + 4 p): 0.2160, Σ(3 d–): –0.1589) compared to ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2Cu (Σ(4 s + 4 p): 0.4080, Σ(3 d–): –0.2194) upon CO adsorption, which indicates simultaneous weakening of the σ-donation bond and the d-π* back-donation bond (Fig. 4g and Supplementary Table 11). This is consistent with the conclusion that Cu(I)/ZPC-1 has a relatively weaker interaction with CO than CuCl/NaY. For the tri-coordinated models, the total electron occupancies of the 4 s and 4 p orbitals of Cu show similar increases upon CO adsorption, indicating similar contributions from σ-donation. Specifically, it is obvious that among the tri-coordinated models, ( ≡ C–O)2CuCl and ( ≡ C–O)CuCl2 show smaller electron occupancy decreases in their Cu 3 d suborbitals (Σ(3 d–): –0.1310 and –0.1528) upon CO adsorption than in the case of ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2CuCl (Σ(3 d–): –0.2912), which indicates a weakening of the d-π* back-donation bond.

VPSA process simulation

The flexible MOF can achieve high working capacity when the gate opening pressure is consistent with the operating pressure conditions of VPSA processes3,49. Herein, the high working capacity of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 under mild vacuum desorption pressure makes it an excellent candidate for VPSA separation of CO/N2 with low energy consumption. The process operation parameters and corresponding separation performance of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 were simulated and optimized through Aspen adsorption simulation of the VPSA process18,50,51. In addition, the separation performance of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 was further compared with the classic π-complexation adsorbent CuCl/NaY. A five-bed seven-step VPSA process was applied to separate a CO/N2 mixture (v:v = 30:70) at 298 K at a total adsorption pressure of 7.0 bar and a total desorption pressure in the range 0.10–0.25 bar. The seven steps comprised adsorption (AD), equalization–depressurization (ED), replacement (RP), blow-down (BD), vacuum (VU), equalization–repressurization (ER), and pressurization (PR) (Fig. 5a).

Noted that the minimum standard of CO purity ( > 98.0%) and CO recovery ( > 80.0%) have been considered in the process optimization of separation performances and operational parameters. In the optimized VPSA process of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1, the separation performances indicated that a high CO productivity of 6.54 mol kg–1 h–1 and a high CO working capacity of 3.31 mmol g–1 with 98.19% CO purity and 88.41% CO recovery could be obtained under an economic energy consumption of 1.41 MJ kg–1 CO (vacuum energy consumption of 0.17 MJ kg–1 CO) and mild operation condition (298 K and desorption pressure of 0.20 bar) (Supplementary Figs. 45, 46 and Supplementary Tables 22–25). In contrast, the separation performances of CuCl/NaY indicated that a low CO productivity of 0.90 mol kg–1 h–1 and a low CO working capacity of 0.37 mmol g–1 could be obtained based on the same standard of CO purity (98.21%) and recovery (85.59%), accompanied by a highly energy consumption of 1.95 MJ kg–1 CO (vacuum energy consumption of 0.55 MJ kg–1 CO) and relatively harsh desorption pressure of 0.11 bar. Noted that increasing the desorption pressure from 0.11 to 0.20 bar can lead to a decrease in product recovery (69.56%) of CuCl/NaY, and the corresponding energy consumption (2.12 MJ kg–1) and vacuum energy consumption (0.33 MJ kg–1) was higher than that of CuCl/NaY at a desorption pressure of 0.2 bar (Supplementary Fig. 47 and Supplementary Table 25). Thus, The CO productivity and working capacity of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 far exceeds that of conventional CuCl/NaY adsorbent when CO purity and recovery were comparable, while the energy consumption and vacuum energy consumption of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 is much lower than that of CuCl/NaY.

Specifically, CO gas–solid phase concentration profiles of the adsorption bed for 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 were examined according to the axial position (Z/H, 0 ≤ Z/H ≤ 1) at the end of each operation step under the optimized cyclic steady-state conditions (Fig. 5c, d). The variation in axial CO gas–solid phase concentration on the adsorption beds in a cycle was also examined (Supplementary Fig. 48). The results of CO solid-phase concentration changes indicate that 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 had a high adsorption capacity (5.66 mmol g–1) at the end of the RP step (3.22 bar), except at the outlet of the adsorption bed, and a high desorption capacity/working capacity (3.31 mmol g–1, desorption degree > 58.5%) at the end of the VU step (0.20 bar) (Supplementary Fig. 45). The CO working capacity directly determines the amount of CO produced per cycle in the VPSA process. Thus, the CO working capacity positively influences on key performance indicators such as productivity, recovery, and energy consumption in the VPSA process. The results of CO gas-phase concentration changes indicate that the CO concentration on the adsorption bed was higher than 99.6% at the end of the VU step.

Considering the isosteric heat of CO adsorption, a large amount of heat will be generated during the adsorption step, resulting in a corresponding increase in bed temperature. Accordingly, the Langmuir model containing temperature dependent parameters were introduced (Supplementary Figs. 50, 51 and Supplementary Tables 19, 20) and process simulation results were as follows: 15Cu(Ι)/ZPC-1 achieves a CO productivity of 2.93 mol kg⁻¹ h⁻¹ and a CO working capacity of 1.62 mmol g⁻¹ under energy consumption of 1.54 MJ kg⁻¹ CO and mild desorption pressure of 0.20 bar. In contrast, CuCl/NaY exhibits a lower CO productivity of 0.72 mol kg⁻¹ h⁻¹ and a working capacity of only 0.32 mmol g⁻¹, along with a higher energy consumption of 2.25 MJ kg⁻¹ CO and a more demanding desorption pressure of 0.10 bar (Supplementary Fig. 53 and Supplementary Table 26). Considering temperature effects on adsorption capacity, 15Cu(Ι)/ZPC-1 shows reduced CO productivity and working capacity, yet its performance remained significantly superior to that of CuCl/NaY. Some flexible MOFs possess an “intrinsic thermal management” capability that can automatically buffer system temperature, contributing to improved efficiency in the VPSA process. However, flexible MOFs suffer from the “slipping-off” phenomenon in mixed-gas separation, where the MOFs cannot adsorb target molecules when the partial pressure of the target gas drops below its gate-opening pressure. The slipping-off issue can be addressed by introducing a sequential-column system49, the unique S-shaped adsorption isotherms and hysteresis loops of flexible MOFs make the simulation of VPSA processes considerably more complex.

The VPSA process simulation results for 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 demonstrate great potential for industrial CO/N2 separation.

Discussion

In summary, we demonstrate that an unsaturated two-coordinated Cu unit ( ≡ C − O)CuCl docked within oxygen-functionalized porous carbon is a Cu(I) adsorption site that exhibits moderate Cu···CO binding providing high CO working capacity and low desorption energy consumption. The optimal 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 adsorbent has abundant and highly dispersed exposed Cu(I) sites and shows a high CO uptake (1 bar) of 4.63 mmol g−1 and a high working capacity (0.1–1 bar) of 3.21 mmol g−1 at 298 K, exceeding those of previously reported recyclable Cu(I)-based adsorbents, and even surpassing those of most MOFs containing unsaturated metal sites. Breakthrough curves of a CO/N2 mixture (v:v = 30:70) of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1 also showed a high CO working capacity (2.67 mmol g−1). State-of-the-art characterizations confirmed that Cu(I)/ZPC-1 has a mainly ( ≡ C–O)CuCl two-coordinated configuration and a secondary ( ≡ C–O)CuCl2 tri-coordinated configuration. Theoretical calculations showed that the ( ≡ C − O)CuCl in Cu(I)/ZPC-1 has weakly electronegative O-anion and high electron occupancy of Cu outer-shell orbitals, which can bind CO in a weakly linear manner with low-symmetric trigonal coordination geometries. Natural bond orbital calculations further revealed that the ( ≡ C–O)CuCl in Cu(I)/ZPC-1 undergoes smaller electron occupancy changes in their Cu 4 s and 4 p orbitals and Cu 3 d suborbitals than those of ( ≡ Si/Al–O)2Cu in commercial CuCl/NaY upon CO adsorption, which weakens σ-donation and d-π* back-donation of π-complexation. Furthermore, a five-bed seven-step VPSA cycle was simulated to evaluate the high CO/N2 separation efficiency of 15Cu(I)/ZPC-1. Overall, this work provides valuable insights for designing π-complexation adsorbents with suitable metal sites and high working capacity, which have great prospects for future industrial applications.

Methods

Preparation of ZIF-8

Zn(OAc)2·2H2O (2.64 g) and mIm (2.95 g) were dissolved in MeOH (100 mL) in a 150 mL Teflon-lined reaction vessel and heated at 100 °C for 72 h. Then, the samples were filtered and washed with MeOH.

Preparation of ZPC

Firstly, ZIF-8 (2.0 g) sample was carbonized at a heating rate of 5 °C min−1 for 3 h in a tube furnace under Ar atmosphere at 1000 and 800 °C52. The obtained materials were washed with a HCl solution (1 M) and distilled water, then dried at 120 °C for 12 h. The obtained carbonized porous carbon materials were labeled as ZPC-1000C and ZPC-800C. Secondly, ZPC-1000C and ZPC-800C (1.0 g) were mixed with KOH (4.0 g), respectively, and heated for 1 h at 800 °C under an Ar atmosphere with a heating rate of 5 °C min−1. The resulting porous carbon materials were washed with a HCl solution (2 M) and distilled water, then dried at 120 °C for 12 h. These obtained activated materials were labeled as ZPC-1 and ZPC-2 support.

Preparation of Cu(Ι)/ZPC

Cu(Ι)/ZPC-1 adsorbent were prepared through solid thermal dispersion. The CuCl2·2H2O power and ZPC-1 support were mixed and ground, and then the mixture was thermally treated at 350 °C under Ar atmosphere for 4 h. The corresponding obtained adsorbent were named nCu(Ι)/ZPC-1, where n = 7, 10, 13 and 15 represents the copper loading in millimoles per gram ZPC-1. 15Cu(Ι)/ZPC-2 was prepared using the same method, using ZPC-2 and CuCl2·2H2O as support and copper sources, with a loading amount of 15 mmol CuCl2·2H2O/g ZPC-2.

Preparation of Ni-MOF-74

Ni(OCOCH₃)₂·4H₂O (7.498 mmol, 1.866 g) and H₄DOBDC (3.666 mmol, 0.746 g) were dispersed in a solution containing THF (50 mL) and H₂O (50 mL). The mixture was dissolved uniformly, then transferred into a Teflon-lined autoclave and heated at 120 °C for 3 days. After cooling to room temperature, the resulting crystals were washed with methanol, immersed in an appropriate amount of MeOH for activation over 48 h (with the solvent replaced every 12 h), and finally filtered and dried.

Preparation of CuCl/NaY

The CuCl and NaY zeolite were mixed (mass ratio = 0.4:1) and ground thoroughly. Then, the mixture was placed in a quartz tube and calcined at 400 °C for 12 h under a 50 cm3/min Ar atmosphere.

Materials characterization

Powder X-ray diffraction patterns were collected on a Rigaku UItima IV diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). N2 adsorption and desorption isotherms were measured at 77 K using Micromeritics ASAP 2020 volumetric adsorption analyzer and the pore size distribution was analyzed by 2D-non-local density functional theory (N2 @ 77 on carbon finite slit pores, as = 4) method. The concentration of Cu in the nCu(Ι)/ZPC-1 were detected using the inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry (PerkinElmer Avio500). Cu K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy measurements were operated using an Easy XES150 system and detail X-ray absorption near-edge structure and extended X-ray absorption fine structure analysis are available in the Supplementary Information. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy and Auger electron spectroscopy were taken with a Kratos AXIS ULTRA DLD instrument with an Al Kα source and the C 1 s peak at 284.6 eV as binding energy calibration standard.

Adsorption and separation performances measurement

CO and N2 adsorption equilibrium isotherms were collected on the gravimetric gas sorption analyzer (IGA-100, Hiden) and Micromeritics ASAP 2020 analyzer. The isosteric heats of adsorption were calculated according to the virial-type equation by using data of CO and N2 isotherms measured at 273 and 288 K. Ideal adsorbed solution theory was used to predict the selectivity of CO/N2 from pure single component isotherms. The adsorption and desorption kinetics were measured by gravimetric gas sorption analyzer (IGA-100, Hiden).

The CO/N2 (v:v = 30:70 and 50:50) breakthrough experiments were measured in the home-made dynamic gas breakthrough apparatus at 298 K and different total adsorption pressures (1.0, 2.0, 4.0 and 6.0 bar). To measure the dynamic CO adsorption capacity, the Cu(Ι)/ZPC-1, Ni-MOF-74 and CuCl/NaY samples were regenerated at 453 K under evacuation (0.07 bar, the ultimate pressure of the vacuum pump). To measure the dynamic CO working capacity, after the dynamic gas adsorption capacity breakthrough experiment, the samples was desorbed at 0.2 bar and 298 K.

Adsorbent evaluation criteria: (a) CO adsorption capacity under adsorption conditions; CO working capacity is defined as the difference in adsorption capacity between adsorption (high pressure) and desorption (low pressure) conditions; regenerability is defined as the ratio of working capacity to the adsorption capacity of the targeted gas molecule. Noted that the static working capacity data in the article is obtained based on the adsorption isotherm (0.1-1 bar) for comparison with literature data (Fig. 2d). In the VPSA process simulation, the working capacity is the difference in adsorption capacity between the replacement step and the vacuum step.

Computational methods

The calculations were performed at the density functional theory (DFT) level using the Dmol3 code53,54. The exchange-correlation energy was described by the PBE functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA)55 with a spin-unrestricted shell. The Grimme scheme56 was used for the dispersion correction of long-range interactions (DFT-D). All electron relativistic effects and the double-numeric basis with polarization functions (DNP) were employed for all atoms. We took a global real space orbital cutoff of the atomic basis sets to be 4.4 Å. The convergence criteria for structural optimization and energy calculations were 1.0 × 10−6 for SCF, 1.0 × 10−5 au for energy, 2.0 × 10−3 au for maximum force, and 5.0 × 10−4 nm for maximum displacement. Natural bond orbital (NBO) analysis has been performed using Gaussian 09 program package by B3LYP functional with LANL2DZ (Los Alamos National Laboratory 2 Double-Zeta) for metal atoms and 6-31 + + G** for other atoms. Detailed structural models are available in the Supplementary Information.

VPSA process simulation

A 5-bed 7-step vacuum pressure swing adsorption (VPSA) cycle was simulated by using Aspen Adsorption to evaluate the performance of 15Cu(Ι)/ZPC-1 in separating CO/N2 (v:v = 30:70). The cyclic steady state relative tolerance is set to 1 × 10−5. This study establishes the following fundamental assumptions for the numerical model of the CO-VPSA process: (a) the behavior of gases obeys the ideal gas law; (b) radial distribution gradients of gas concentration, pressure, and temperature are negligible; (c) a one-dimensional plug-flow model is applied for axial diffusion, (d) non-isothermal and non-adiabatic processes are considered, meaning the model incorporates heat exchange with the external environment. The mass, heat and momentum transfer processes of each component in the adsorption bed were analyzed to obtain the partial differential equation that was further calculated through the finite difference method. Detailed description of mass balance, momentum balance, energy balances, adsorbent and bed characteristics, simulation of VPSA process, and performance parameters for evaluation of VPSA cycles are available in the Supplementary Information. We conducted process simulations for pressure-dependent adsorption isotherms and for adsorption isotherms that couple temperature and pressure, respectively.
