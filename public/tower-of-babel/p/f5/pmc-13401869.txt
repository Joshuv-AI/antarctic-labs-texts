Risk Factors and Nomogram for Postpartum Myofascial Pelvic Pain: A Prospective Cohort Study [Letter]
Authors: Weiwei Tong, Guan Shen
Published: 2026

Dear editor

We read with great interest the article by Cai et al entitled “Risk Factors and Nomogram for Postpartum Myofascial Pelvic Pain: A Prospective Cohort Study”.1 The study assessed four risk factors and constructed a nomogram for predicting postpartum myofascial pelvic pain (MFPP). This makes a meaningful contribution to identifying those at high risk of this postpartum complication. However, we have identified several methodological and statistical issues that may undermine the validity of this model.

Firstly, we have noted a number of issues relating to data completeness and accuracy. The validation set for this study comprised a total of 166 participants. However, the reported serum albumin results in Table 1 show that 60 participants (55.4%) had reduced levels, 74 (44.6%) had normal levels, and data for the remaining 32 participants were not reported, while the authors did not explain how any missing data were handled. Furthermore, in the section of the paper titled “Comparison of MFPP and Non-MFPP Groups in the Training Set”, it is stated that the proportion of participants in the non-MFPP group with parity ≥ 3 was 6.9%, but this differs from the figure of 24 participants (5.5%) shown in Table 2, which may be due to an error in data entry by the authors. We hope the authors will provide further clarification regarding these data in a future correspondence.

Secondly, the statistical analysis methods employed in the study carry a risk of overfitting. The authors included 13 candidate variables in the multivariable logistic regression analysis. However, only 65 participants in the training set were diagnosed with MFPP, resulting in the events per variable (EPV) of 5. According to the TRIPOD Statement and other guidelines,2,3 the internationally accepted minimum EPV threshold is 10. The EPV in the present study is considerably below this threshold, which substantially increases the risk of model overfitting. Furthermore, according to the discriminative ability criteria proposed by Swets (1988),4 the Area Under the ROC Curve (AUC) for both the training set and the validation set fall within the range of 0.5–0.7, indicating poor discriminative ability, rather than the “moderate discriminative ability” mentioned in the paper. More importantly, the 95% CI for the AUC in the validation set ranges from 0.507 to 0.804, with the lower limit approaching the threshold for no discrimination. Although the DeLong test indicates no statistically significant difference between the AUCs of the training and validation sets, this does not directly rule out the risk of no discriminative ability in the validation set. We hope that the authors will increase the sample size of the validation set in future work to derive a more precise AUC interval.

Thirdly, the article mentions that excessive mechanical load during delivery predisposes women to pelvic floor injury. Given this pathophysiological link, maternal weight status should be considered a potential key confounder in the analysis. Kuutti have demonstrated that “High body mass index (BMI) and waist circumference are well-established risk factors for pelvic floor disorders in women of all ages”.5 Maternal obesity, particularly central obesity, significantly affects pelvic floor load during delivery. A large Chinese retrospective cohort study has also reported that BMI is an independent predictor of postpartum pelvic floor muscle damage.6 We therefore suggest that maternal BMI should be incorporated into the multivariable model as a variable. Otherwise, residual confounding factors may be introduced, which could ultimately affect the model’s discriminatory power.

In conclusion, the development of a predictive model for postpartum MFPP is of significant clinical importance, and we greatly appreciate the authors’ contribution in this regard. However, the model developed in this study may lack sufficient discriminatory power. We hope the authors will consider our suggestions and develop a model with greater discriminatory power.
