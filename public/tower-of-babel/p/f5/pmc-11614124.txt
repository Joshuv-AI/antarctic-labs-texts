An analysis of three dimensional changes to comprehensively evaluate the occlusion time, disclusion time, point of first contact and force distribution at various head positions using Tekscan III: An in vivo study
Authors: G. S. Prasanthi, M. C. Suresh Sajjan, Yenumula J. B. Manikyamba, P. V. B. Chandrasekhar, Sri Lakshmi Kotemsetty
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Aim:This study aims to evaluate the changes in occlusal contacts resulting from variations in head postures (supine[S], semi-supine [SS], upright with head rest support [HR], and without head rest support [WHR]) after occlusal adjustment of conventional fixed dental prostheses using Tekscan III.Settings and Design:A cross-sectional study on 15 patients with missing single molars and undergone replacement with a fixed dental prosthesis.Materials and Methods:Fifteen patients with a three-unit tooth-supported fixed dental prosthesis replacing a missing single posterior tooth were included. Tekscan III was used to analyze occlusion time (OT), disclusion time (DT), initial tooth contact, and relative bite force in different head postures by adjusting the backrest position before and after equilibration. Occlusal adjustments were performed to achieve the balance between the right and left halves.Statistical Analysis Used:The data were statistically analyzed using ANOVA and paired t-test.Results:Posterior shift of initial tooth contact was observed from SS to supine and upright positions, whereas an anterior shift occurred from SS to WHR support posture. High-intensity forces were observed before the occlusal adjustment in various head positions, and these forces changed after adjustments in SS position. However, medium-intensity force occlusal prematurities persisted in other head postures even after corrections in SS position. There was a significant difference in OT but not in DT in all head postures after equilibration. When OT was compared from SS to remaining posture after equilibration, the major difference was found from SS to head rest (HR) and supine to HR posture which infers the need for selective grinding in these postures.Conclusion:Considering the various changes while changing the head position from the SS position to the other functional position, it can be inferred that there is a need to consider recalibration of occlusal contacts in all the functional postures.

INTRODUCTION

The primary goal of prosthodontic rehabilitation is to provide occlusal contacts compatible and in harmony with the stomatognathic system.[1] The occlusal interferences in restorations have been shown to cause hyperactivity of the masticatory muscles, leading to a state of muscle fatigue and subsequent muscle spasms. Hyperactivity of the masticatory muscles also causes occlusal overload on the restorations and subsequent consequences such as severe tooth sensitivity, fracture of restorations and abutment tooth, tooth mobility, postrestorative temporomandibular problems, widened periodontal ligament, and periodontal disease.[2] Cervical nerves C1 to C4 are primarily involved in controlling head posture, through proprioceptive inputs from the muscles and articulations of the neck.[3]

In daily clinical practice, final occlusal corrections of restoration are usually done in a semi-supine (SS) position. However, the individuals in their daily life present with different functional positions such as sleeping, sitting, and standing. The head assumes positions as upright while standing, changes from upright to slightly inclined forward while sitting referred to as active feeding position, and while sleeping, the head is parallel to the ground. The mandible is also influenced by gravity and varies in its position according to the posture.[4]

Studies have shown that a change in the body posture brings a change in the spatial relationship of the mandible to the cranium, a change in tooth contact position, and a change in masticatory muscle activity.[5] Hence, carefully finished occlusion by the clinician in a particular posture may not be comfortable in remaining head postures.

Earlier studies have demonstrated the changes in occlusal contacts, initial tooth contact, and occlusion time (OT) in various head postures of an individual.[6] However, there are no studies comparing the changes in the distribution of bite force and disclusion time (DT) in patients rehabilitated with fixed dental prosthesis before and after occlusal adjustments among various head postures. The present study is the first to analyze three-dimensional (3D) changes to comprehensively evaluate the OT, DT, point of first contact, and force distribution.

Till date, Tekscan (T-scan) is the only equipment capable to record the dynamic nature of the occlusal contact during maximal intercuspal and mandibular excursive movements and display in graphical form for the ease of interpretation.[7] Tekscan III can record a given contact sequence in 0.01 s increments as contacts proceed from the first contact through to maximal intercuspal position (MIP) and vice versa.[8]

T-scan can dynamically record and display the OT, the DT, the relative force percentage distribution, the intensity of 256 relative occlusal forces, their duration, and the timing order of all occlusal contacts.[8] It displays 2D and 3D force views and the force versus time graph for all the teeth. The occlusal contact sequence can be used as a guide for corrective occlusal adjustments.[9] By analyzing dynamic changes associated with head position, an optimum position to finish the restorative procedure can be suggested.[10]

The present in vivo study evaluates four parameters – initial tooth contact, relative bite force, OT, and DT at different head postures of individuals before and after equilibration of conventional fixed prosthesis using Tekscan III. The null hypothesis was that there would not be any change in the occlusal contacts with a change in the head positions of the patient.

SUBJECTS AND METHODS

The present study was designed as a cross-sectional study and received approval from the Institutional Review Board and Ethics Committee (IRB/VDC/MDS14 PROSTHO 1).

Sample size calculation

The sample size of 15 participants was calculated using a software program (G*Power version 3.1.9.4; Heinrich Heine University Düsseldorf). The effect size was set to be 0.2, the α error probability was 5%, power (1−β error probability) was 80%, σ (pooled standard deviation from previous studies) was 0.2, and dropout was estimated at 20%. Participants were selected from patients fulfilling the inclusion criteria who required a three-unit fixed dental prostheses at the department of prosthodontics in a time span of 1 year.

The participants were enrolled by convenience sampling. Participants (n = 15) between 20 and 50 years old with a single missing molar tooth indicated for fixed partial denture and opposed by natural teeth were included in the study. Participants with temporomandibular joint disorders (TMJ), parafunctional habits, and poor periodontal health were excluded.

The research protocol was explained to all the participants, and informed written consent was obtained. Patient postures were standardized in the following manner. Supine (S) posture was attained by reclining the backrest of the dental chair such that the head, knees, and feet are approximately at the same level. SS posture, by keeping the backrest approximately between 45° and 60° to simulate the conventional working position of the dentist. There was slight intraoperator variability in SS position as positioning of the patient and the operator since illumination and retraction for optimal visibility are the fundamental prerequisites to proper dental treatment. With headrest (HR) posture was maintained by uprighting the backrest to the maximum and the head was comfortably resting on the HR. Without HR (WHR) posture was maintained by asking the patient to sit straight and keep the head upright without head support.[1]

Study models were mounted on a semi-adjustable articulator with the help of face-bow transfer (Hanau Wide View, Whip Mix Corporation, USA) and occlusal registration. A conventional procedure was followed to fabricate a three-unit fixed dental prosthesis. During the bisque trial, the gross occlusal correction was performed using 40 µ articulating paper (BAUSH Germany) in SS position till all the teeth came into contact and the patient felt comfortable in MIP. Occlusal contacts were analyzed using the Tekscan III computerized occlusal analysis system powered by T-scan 8 software program (Tekscan, USA). The T-scan software window displays 2D force view, 3D force view, timing table, and force versus time graph in subwindows. The 2D force view displays the relative occlusal force distribution, and a pressure map of each tooth and each arch-half over time, using a color-coded scale to illustrate individual contact force intensity.[10] The 3D force view shows the same forces in 3D columns of varying heights and colors indicating the intensity of the bite force.[11] The colors ranged between the dark blue being low intensity to the dark red being high intensity. The light blue and green were considered medium intensity.[12]

The initial tooth contact, maximum tooth contact, beginning of the disclusion, and complete disclusion are indicated by the location of the A, B, C, and D lines in force versus time graph at key time moments in a recoded mandibular movement. The A–B increment denoted the OT and the C–D increment denoted the DT. The software has a provision to view initial tooth contact and MIP by command.[13] Before recording any study data, patients were trained to occlude onto the sensor in MIP, two to three times to sensitize the sensor. The sensitivity of the sensor was set to limit the saturated pink sensels to 3. The same sensitivity was kept constant per individual participant at all recordings.[14]

Initial tooth contact [Figure 1], relative bite force [Figure 2], OT, and DT were recorded in MIP, first in SS position followed by supine, HR, and WHR positions. The recorded data were saved as baseline data. As a standard protocol, keeping the patient in SS position, heavy-force contacts were identified using articulating paper in correlation with the T-scan graph. The occlusal adjustment was performed in SS position using a pear-shaped diamond point, till high-intensity zones (pink, red, yellow, and green) got converted into low-intensity contacts (blue and blue-green) and a balance between the right half and left half was obtained. Relative change in the bite force [Figure 3], OT, DT, and initial tooth contact was assessed in all the four head postures. The T-scan graphs were saved for further analysis.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistical analysis was performed using SPSS software (IBM SPSS Version 20.0. Armonk, IBM Corp., NY, USA). All the four parameters were analyzed before and after equilibration using Tekscan. Shapiro–Wilk test revealed that the study was normally distributed, and further parametric tests such as ANOVA and paired t-test were employed to compare the variance between multiple groups.

DISCUSSION

Three-unit fixed partial denture is the most common mode of replacement of a single missing tooth.[15] After cementation with a fixed prosthesis, occlusal interferences or premature contacts if any tend to cause muscle hyperactivity.[16] This in turn can lead to craniomandibular pain apart from periodontal breakdown.[17] The solution to this pathology is the complete elimination of occlusal prematurity and achieve stable occlusal contacts.[18] Therefore, patients rehabilitated with a single missing tooth were selected for the study rather than a full complement of teeth. Production of consistent occlusal contacts in a particular posture for the purpose of assessing the variations in remaining head postures was the need for the present study. In temporomandibular disorder (TMD) patients, this repeatability of occlusal contacts is unreliable due to lack of muscle engram.[13] Therefore, TMD patients were excluded from the present study.

Head postures vary according to physiologic and functional activities of human beings such as sleeping, sitting, eating, and drinking.[19] Neck muscles play a major role in positioning the head over the shoulder while walking and sitting, whereas it changes to 30° flexion during alert feeding positions.[20] Changes in the head posture result in changes in the horizontal and vertical position of the mandible due to stretching and elongation of anterior and posterior cervical muscles attached to it.[21] Similarly, changes in mandibular position can cause changes in occlusal contacts and it can also be influenced by gait and malocclusions.[22] Tooth contact variation resulting from posture may alter the guidance and could produce pathologic loading on individual tooth.[23]

Conventional ways of assessing occlusal contacts encounter their own limits in practical application.[24] Although articulating paper has been most commonly used, its marks would be affected by its width, and the salivary impregnation can diffuse the paper ink, leading to false positives. Transillumination of silicon bite registration can overcome the problems of articulating paper, but the result depends much on the orientation of the light resource and would be affected by the filler in the bite registration material.[25] T-scan was adopted in the present study to detect occlusal contacts because it could locate premature contacts and occlusal interference more clearly[26] and also relate occlusion to other elements of the articulatory system simply through OT and DT. Tekscan III is far more sensitive than Tekscan II and also recording of contact timing is 0.003 s compared to the earlier version Tekscan II which is only 0.01 s. Therefore, Tekscan III was advocated in the study.[12]

OT is directly related with patients’ occlusal contact pattern and has been considered a capable description of occlusion, whereas DT could relate tooth contacts to muscle activity.[27] Abnormalities in DT would result in a change of muscle activity, thus facilitating the occurrence of TMD.[28] Digital data recorded before and after equilibration can direct the intervention and document the improvements made to the involved structures.

During prosthetic rehabilitation, conventionally, occlusal interferences are corrected only in SS position.[29] Keeping SS position as a baseline, relative changes in occlusal contact from SS to remaining postures were compared to elucidate the need for corrections at different head positions after rehabilitation of fixed dental prosthesis. The existing maximum intercuspal position was selected as the baseline for occlusal equilibration based on the conformative approach of occlusal rehabilitation.[30]

Initial tooth contact

The initial tooth contact is the first contact in the arch as the mandible approach to come in occlusion.[31] This contact guides the mandible to travel in the direction of the other teeth that come in contact in the initial phase. Posterior shift was noticed from SS to supine and SS to HR posture. The most anterior shift in the initial tooth contact was noticed from the SS to WHR position. The WHR position is similar to the active feeding position. The reason could be attributed to the gravity and the muscular activity.[1]

The most common posture for any individual is sleeping and sitting posture. The head remains in the upright position as like in a sitting position during standing and walking.[32] Considering the similarity of the posterior shift in the initial tooth contact with the change from SS to supine and SS to HR postures, it is suggested to perform occlusal adjustment in supine posture. Keeping the patient in the supine position helps in better visibility and accessibility to the oral cavity as well as it is an ergonomically comfortable position for the operator.[29]

The posterior shift in initial tooth contact was noticed in supine and HR, whereas the anterior shift in initial tooth contact was noticed in WHR posture. This can be attributed to when the head was bent forward, the closing path approached the MIP from the anterior region, and as the head was bent backward, the closing path approached the MIP from the posterior region. The reason for this change in the direction of mandibular closing movement is gravity and masticatory muscle activity.[26] Gravity tends to pull the mandible along the vertical axis.[1] Whenever there is a change in head position, muscles have to contract to overcome the gravitational force. However, in the sleeping position, since all the masticatory and supporting muscles are minimally active, the mandible tends to change in position depending on the sleeping posture of the individual.[3]

Occlusal interference tends to stimulate the muscle contraction and does not allow the muscles to rest.[33] During sleep, there is no voluntary control of the masticatory muscles. Occlusal interference may instigate muscle contraction initiating clenching or bruxing during sleep. The involuntary contraction of masticatory muscles tends to develop higher forces (parafunctional forces) on the teeth and to the TMJ, leading to attrition of the teeth as well as TMD.[34]

Relative bite force

Before equilibration, 29% of the participants had a relatively equal distribution of forces between the right and left side, therefore, did not require any corrections. In 71% of the participants, who had an occlusal force imbalance between the right and left side, the high intensity of forces (pink) was observed in all the head positions. However, pink pillars noticed in a particular tooth in SS position varied in other head postures.

After performing occlusal adjustment in SS position, conversion of the pink pillars to light blue indicated the conclusion of the adjustments. The occurrence of green pillars (medium intensity forces) and also the variability in the position of the prematurities in remaining head postures indicated incomplete removal of occlusal interferences in supine, HR, and WHR despite appropriate occlusal corrections in SS position. Thus, there is a need to verify for any further correction required in other head positions.[35]

Occlusion time and disclusion time

All occluding surfaces of maxillary and mandibular teeth should make bilateral simultaneous contact in MIP. Ideally, the mean OT during maximum intercuspation was found to be <0.2 s to achieve bilateral occlusal contact. From the first contact, the teeth will be guided to MIP. Ideally, the DT shall be <0.4 s.[11] OT is calculated as the elapsed time between first contact to final contact in MIP. As the closing muscles get activated, the effect of gravity would not be affecting the mandibular closure; hence, the OT might have remained insignificant. DT is defined as the time from MIP to complete disclusion during mandibular movement. OT and DT are helpful in occlusal adjustment and linking the occlusion with other elements of the articulatory system. Any prematurities will increase the OT and DT. Prolonged OT indicates the presence of occlusal prematurities.[36] Hence, OT can be considered a diagnostic aid in the identification of occlusal prematurities.

In routine dental practice, it is common to perform all restorative procedures including occlusal corrections in SS posture. However, it is not a comfortable posture for the dentist. In daily life, we spend maximum time either sitting with the head upright or sleeping. Hence, doing occlusal corrections in these postures is preferable. Having the patient in supine posture is ergonomically comfortable for the dentist to keep the spine erect without bending forward, and also this posture of the patient can provide greater visibility for the dentist. In the present study, we noted re-occurrence of interference postcorrection from SS to other functional positions. Hence, supine posture is the best to perform occlusal corrections and later verifying the occlusal contacts in sitting WHR posture is crucial to assess for any remaining premature contacts. This is done to attain a harmonious relationship of fixed partial dentures as well as natural teeth with masticatory muscles and TMJ.

Clinical significance

Considering the various changes in the parametrical analysis while changing the head position from the SS position to the other functional position, it can be inferred that there is a need to consider recalibration of occlusal contacts in all the functional postures.

Instruments like T-scan can help in the identification of the specific areas of corrections and also can objectively demonstrate the achievement of desired qualities of occlusal contacts.
