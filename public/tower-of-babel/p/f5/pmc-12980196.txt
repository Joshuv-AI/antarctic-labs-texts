Analysis of Intracellular Fatty Acid Metabolism during Doxorubicin-Induced Senescence of MCF7 Cells Using Raman Imaging
Authors: Swarang Sachin Pundlik, Ashwin Venkateshvaran, Yavanica Suresh, Hitesh Mamgain, Arvind Ramanathan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Cellular senescence, a stable growth-arrested state induced by stress or chemotherapeutic agents, is accompanied by metabolic remodeling that supports the senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). Among these pathways, lipid and arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism play central roles in maintaining and propagating the senescent state. Here, we used hyperspectral confocal Raman microscopy to visualize biochemical remodeling in MCF7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells undergoing doxorubicin-induced senescence. Raman spectral analysis and principal component decomposition revealed time-dependent alterations in lipid-associated vibrational modes, particularly CH2 and CC stretching, consistent with enhanced lipid accumulation and remodeling between Days 10 and 15 after DNA damage induction. PCA of lipid-rich compartments isolated by using true component analysis also confirms progressive increases in triacylglycerol and unsaturated lipid signatures. Using deuterated arachidonic acid (AA-d 8) and COX2 inhibition, we further demonstrated real-time intracellular AA metabolism by tracking (CC)D stretching peaks (2220–2254 cm–1) in the Raman-silent window. The ratio of these deuterium bands to CH2 stretching provided a label-free quantitative metric for COX2-dependent AA turnover in senescent cells. Together, these findings establish hyperspectral Raman imaging as a powerful, nonperturbative tool to map lipid and oxylipin metabolism during cellular senescence, offering new avenues to identify metabolic vulnerabilities in senescent tumor cells.

Introduction

Cellular senescence is a stable proliferative arrest induced by diverse stresses and characterized by a senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP). The SASP includes inflammatory cytokines, proteases, and growth factors, which influence tissue remodeling, aging, and tumorigenesis. − Cancer cells, including MCF7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells, undergo senescence upon treatment with chemotherapeutic compounds like doxorubicin (Doxo). These senescent tumor cells alter the physiology of the surrounding cancerous and noncancerous cells via paracrine and juxtacrine signaling mediated by the components of the SASP, leading to secondary tumorigenesis and other pathologies. −

Metabolic reprogramming is increasingly recognized as integral to senescence phenotypes, not merely a bystander. Lipids lie at the heart of the metabolic shift. In replicative and stress-induced senescence models, lipidomic analysis showed accumulation of triacylglycerols enriched in polyunsaturated fatty acids and modulation of membrane lipid remodeling. Transcriptome and lipidome integrative analyses point to upregulated lipid uptake and storage genes in senescent cells including the fatty acid translocator CD36. In fact, forced expression of CD36 in proliferating cells induces SASP factors. These data argue for a causal axis from lipid uptake and storage to SASP execution.

Arachidonic acid (AA) metabolism is a specialized lipid axis in senescent cell biology. Wiley et al. demonstrated that senescent cells activate oxylipin biosynthesis, including prostaglandins, which reinforce cell cycle arrest and SASP expression; they identified a specific product, dihomo-15d-PGJ2, as a senolysis biomarker. Parallel work implicates COX2/PGE2 signaling in driving senescence onset and maintenance, and leukotriene outputs from lung fibroblasts in fibrotic niche remodeling. , Our previous study has shown that 15d-PGJ2, a metabolite of arachidonic acid secreted by senescent myoblasts upon DNA damage, inhibits the differentiation of skeletal myoblasts by altering intracellular HRas dynamics. Collectively, these studies place AA pathway lipids as both sensors and effectors of senescence.

To characterize the senescent state, investigators routinely use multiplexed markers. Classic hallmarks include the activity of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) at pH 6, upregulation of cyclin inhibitors p21CIP1/WAF1 and p16INK4a, and loss or reduction of Lamin B1. Persistent DNA damage foci (γH2AX) mark unresolved damage. Additional markers include chromatin reorganization (SAHF), HMGB1 relocalization, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic shifts. , Because single markers are often insufficiently specific, field consensus recommends using panels of markers.

Recent advances in vibrational microscopy now allow label-free imaging of biomolecular composition with subcellular resolution. Confocal Raman or coherent Raman modalities (such as CARS/SRS) can spatially resolve lipid droplets, membrane changes, nucleolar protein conformations, and other chemical microdomains without exogenous labels. Raman fingerprints are promising markers for studying senescence. For senescent cells, coherent Raman imaging has been used to visualize nucleolar amide-I shifts correlated with β-sheet aggregation in binucleated senescent cells. In this article, we apply confocal Raman imaging to senescent cells in conjunction with multiplexed molecular marker validation (SA-β-gal, p21 expression, nuclear enlargement, and flattening of the cell morphology). Our aims are (i) to spatially localize lipid species and AA-derived metabolites in single senescent cells; (ii) to correlate Raman spectral features with canonical senescence markers; and (iii) to propose label-free spectral metrics that reflect senescence burden or responses to senolysis. By anchoring imaging readouts to validated lipids and markers, we hope to establish a robust, nonperturbative phenotyping tool for senescent cell states that can be integrated into metabolic and pharmacologic studies.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture

MCF7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells (HTB-22) were maintained in DMEM (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco) (DMEM complete medium) at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Cells were trypsinized with 0.125% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco) and were seeded in glass-bottom dishes for experiments.

Treatments

Doxorubicin (Sigma-Aldrich) was dissolved in DMSO (10 mM). Doxo (10 mM) was diluted in DMSO to make an intermediate stock (100 μM). MCF7 cells were treated with Doxo (150 nM) by diluting Doxo in DMEM complete medium for 3 days. The medium was changed on Day 3 with fresh DMEM complete medium containing Doxo. From Day 5, the cells were incubated in DMEM complete medium without Doxo until the end of the experiment (Days 10, 15, 18, and 25). MCF7 cells were seeded in glass-bottom dishes and incubated in DMEM complete medium without Doxo for 24 h, and they were used as the proliferating control.

5,6,8,9,11,​12,14,15-d 8 arachidonic acid (AA-d 8) obtained from Cayman Chemical Company was reconstituted in DMSO (25 mM). Cay-10404, a COX2 inhibitor, was obtained from Cayman Chemical Company and dissolved in DMSO (30 mM). Senescent MCF7 cells after 15 days of treatment with Doxo were pretreated with Cay-10404 (30 μM) in DMEM complete medium for 2 h for the inhibition of COX2 enzyme. The cells were then treated with Cay-10404 (30 μM) and AA-d 8 (100 μM) in DMEM complete medium overnight. The cells were washed with 1× Dulbecco’s Phosphate Buffer Saline (DPBS) (Gibco) and treated with AA-d 8 (100 μM) in DMEM complete medium without Cay-10404 to allow the resumption of COX2-mediated metabolism of AA-d 8 in the 15 day senescent cells until the end of the experiment (0.25, 0.5, and 1 h). Senescent MCF7 cells pretreated with Cay-10404 and treated with Cay-10404 and AA-d 8 were used as control samples to measure the intracellular metabolism of AA-d 8 by COX2.

AA-d 8 (250 μM) was incubated with COX2 enzyme in reaction buffer (100 mM Tris-Cl (pH 8), 5 mM EDTA, phenol (500 μM), and hematin (100 nM)). The reaction was aliquoted in 100 μL aliquots, and the aliquots were incubated at 37 °C until the end of the experiment (0, 0.25, 0.5, and 1 h). Aliquots were harvested at the end of the experiment, flash-frozen in liquid N2, and stored at −80 °C.

Immunofluorescence

Proliferating and senescent MCF7 cells (Days 10, 15, 18, and 25) were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde in 1× DPBS at room temperature for 5 min. The cells were washed thrice with 1× DPBS. The cells were permeabilized with the permeabilization buffer (0.1% Triton X-100 in 1× DPBS) at room temperature for 15 min. The cells were then blocked with blocking buffer (2% bovine serum albumin (BSA) and 2% FBS in the permeabilization buffer) at room temperature for 90 min. The cells were incubated with α-p21 antibody (mouse monoclonal) (Santa Cruz Biotechnologies) in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. The cells were washed three times with the permeabilization buffer. The cells were incubated in Alexa Fluor 568-tagged α-mouse antibody (Cell Signaling Technology) in blocking buffer at room temperature for 90 min. The cells were incubated with DAPI (1 μg/mL) in permeabilization buffer at room temperature for 10 min. The cells were washed three times with permeabilization buffer. The cells were mounted in Prolong Gold antifade medium (Invitrogen). The cells were imaged under an FV 3000 inverted microscope (Evident) using appropriate lasers and detectors.

X-Gal Staining

Proliferating and senescent MCF7 cells (Days 10, 15, 18, and 25) were fixed with 0.25% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 5 min. The cells were washed with 1× DPBS. The cells were incubated with X-gal staining solution (X-gal (Roche) (1 mg/mL) in 40 mM citrate-phosphate buffer (pH 6), potassium ferrocyanide (5 mM), potassium ferricyanide (5 mM), sodium chloride (150 nM), and magnesium chloride (2 mM)) at 37 °C overnight. The cells were imaged under a Ti2 widefield microscope (Nikon) in bright-field mode.

Hyperspectral Raman Imaging

Label-Free

Proliferating and senescent MCF7 cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 5 min. The cells were washed three times with 1× DPBS. The cells were kept in 1× DPBS during the hyperspectral Raman imaging. Hyperspectral Raman imaging was done using an alpha 300 Ri system (WITec GmbH, Oxford Instruments) using the following parameters: objective: 40× air; laser: 532 nm; laser power: 60 mW; scan speed: 2 s/pixel; pixel size: 1 μm/pixel; detector grating: 600 mm–1.

AA-d 8 Labeled

15 day senescent MCF7 cells treated with AA-d 8 were fixed with paraformaldehyde at room temperature for 5 min. The cells were washed thrice with 1× DPBS. The cells were kept in 1× DPBS during the hyperspectral Raman imaging. Hyperspectral Raman imaging was done using an alpha 300 Ri system (WITec GmbH, Oxford Instruments) using the following parameters: objective: 40× air; laser: 532 nm; laser power: 60 mW; scan speed: 2 s/pixel; pixel size: 1 μm/pixel; detector grating: 600 mm–1.

COX2-Mediated In Vitro Metabolism of AA-d 8

Flash-frozen samples of the COX2-mediated in vitro metabolism of AA-d 8 were thawed. Lipid metabolites were isolated using the methanol–formic acid–ethyl acetate method. , The samples were resuspended in ethyl acetate and were spotted on glass coverslips and dried. Raman spectra were collected using an alpha 300 Ri system (WITec GmbH) using the following parameters: objective: 40× air; laser: 532 nm; laser power: 60 mW; scan speed: 2 s/pixel; pixel size: 1 μm/pixel; detector grating: 600 mm–1.

Image Analysis

True Component Analysis to Obtain Lipid-Rich Regions of the Hyperspectral Raman Images

Hyperspectral Raman images were processed in Project Plus 6.2 (WITec GmbH). The images were preprocessed for cosmic ray removal and background subtraction. Using the True Component Analysis Pipeline (Project Plus, WITec GmbH), the processed hyperspectral Raman images were split into 3 separate components based on spectral demixing, and lipid-rich masks were generated (Supplementary Figure S2A). The lipid-rich masks were used to generate hyperspectral Raman images from the lipid-rich regions of the cells.

Spectral Shifts in the Deuteration Peaks

Raman spectra collected from in vitro samples of COX2-mediated metabolism of AA-d 8 were exported as .mat files using Project Plus 6.2 (WITec GmbH). Spectral analysis was performed using the RamanSPy package in Python (Appendix I), where the Raman spectra in the region 2200–2300 cm–1 were cropped, and a bimodal curve fitting was done for the deuterium spectra. Modal values of the fitted curve were obtained to measure the spectral shift in the deuterium peaks after in vitro COX2-mediated metabolism of AA-d 8.

Peak Intensity Measurements

Hyperspectral Raman images obtained from proliferating/senescent MCF7 cells or the lipid-rich regions of proliferating/senescent MCF7 cells were exported as .mat files using Project Plus 6.2 (WITec GmbH). The images were processed and analyzed using the RamanSPy package in Python (Appendix II and III), where Raman spectra in the regions 1275–1775 and 2700–3000 cm–1 were cropped and concatenated. Cosmic ray removal was performed using Whitaker–Hayes despiking, and denoising was done using the Savitzky–Golay filter. Baseline correction was done using the AIRPLS method. Peak intensities for Raman shifts 1740 (ester bond stretching), 1655 (CC stretching or NCO stretching), 2850 (CH2 stretching), 2885 (CH3 stretching), and 2920 cm–1 (CH3 stretching) were measured. ,

PCA

Hyperspectral Raman images obtained from proliferating/senescent MCF7 cells or the lipid-rich regions of proliferating/senescent MCF7 cells were exported as .mat files using Project Plus 6.2 (WITec GmbH). The images were processed and analyzed using the RamanSPy package in Python (Appendix IV and V), where Raman spectra in the regions 1275–1775 and 2700–3000 cm–1 were cropped and concatenated. Cosmic ray removal was performed using Whitaker–Hayes despiking, and denoising was done using the Savitzky–Golay filter. Baseline correction was done using the AIRPLS method, and normalization was done using the vector normalization method.

Spectral data from all 5 data sets (proliferating cells, 10 day senescent cells, 15 day senescent cells, 18 day senescent cells, and 25 day senescent cells) were subjected to principal component analysis, where the Raman spectrum from each pixel was considered individually. Population pairs were plotted on the PC2 versus PC1 plot to visualize population overlaps and differences. A scatter plot projecting the loadings of the spectral shifts was plotted to visualize the contribution of different spectral shifts to the principal components. Centroids of the populations were calculated to measure the Euclidean distance between the centroid of the given population pair on the PCA plot as a measure of differences in the populations.

Intracellular Arachidonic Acid Metabolism

Hyperspectral Raman images of MCF7 cells treated with AA-d 8 were processed in Project Plus 6.2 (WITec GmbH) for cosmic ray removal and baseline correction. Spectral heatmaps of the integrated areas for the peaks corresponding to (CC)D stretching (2220 ± 17.5 and 2254 ± 17.5 cm–1) and CH2 stretching (2850 ± 15 cm–1) were obtained by creating area under the curve filters in Project Plus 6.2 (WITec GmbH). Ratiometric image analysis was done in ImageJ (NIH) by dividing the spatial heatmaps of (CC)D stretching (2220 ± 17.5 and 2254 ± 17.5 cm–1) by the CH2 stretching heatmap (2850 ± 15 cm–1) to obtain normalized heatmaps of (CC)D stretching peaks to the CH2 stretching peak. The cell-averaged intensity for the normalized heatmap of (CC)D stretching peaks was calculated.

Results and Discussion

Treatment with DNA-damaging compounds such as doxorubicin (Doxo) has been shown to induce senescence in cancerous as well as noncancerous cells, − including MCF7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells. , We induced senescence in MCF7 cells by treatment with Doxo (150 nM) for 5 days, followed by culture in DMEM complete medium. The induction of senescence was confirmed by monitoring known markers of senescence, including flattened cell morphology, increased nuclear size, increased expression of tumor suppressor protein p21, and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) activity. We observed a flattening of the cell morphology with an increase in the cell size (Supplementary Figure S1A), expression of senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA β-gal) (Supplementary Figure S1B), increased expression of tumor suppressor p21 (Supplementary Figure S1C), and an increase in the nuclear area (Supplementary Figure S1D) in MCF7 cells after treatment with Doxo, confirming the induction of senescence in MCF7 cells upon Doxo-mediated DNA damage.

Cells, upon the induction of senescence, show an altered metabolic state, acquire a secretory phenotype (senescence-associated secretory phenotype, SASP), and secrete a variety of bioactive molecules, including lipid derivatives. ,,− We wanted to capture the temporal dynamics of the altered metabolic states on a single-cell level during senescence. Raman effect-based microscopy allows for the spatial detection of biomolecular species using characteristic inelastic scattering signatures of biomolecules without extensive sample preparation and labeling. Therefore, we used hyperspectral Raman imaging to analyze intracellular biochemical composition and measure biomolecular distinction between different cell states during senescence. We induced senescence in MCF7 human breast adenocarcinoma cells by treatment with Doxo and fixed the cells with 4% paraformaldehyde on Days 10, 15, 18, and 25 after treatment with Doxo. We used MCF7 cells without treatment with Doxo as a proliferating control. We performed hyperspectral Raman imaging using high laser power (60 mW for 2 s/pixel) to achieve an optimal signal-to-noise ratio (∼50 in proliferating cells and >100 in senescent cells) in the Raman spectra. We obtained pixel-by-pixel Raman spectra of proliferating cells and cells on Days 10, 15, 18, and 25 after treatment with Doxo. Preprocessing of the hyperspectral Raman images (denoising, cosmic ray removal, baseline correction, and normalization) was done in Python using the RamanSPy package, followed by principal component analysis (PCA) to compare the hyperspectral Raman images of MCF7 cells during senescence (Appendix IV). Visualization of the PCA showed overlap between the Raman spectra of proliferating cells with those of Day 10 (Figure A), whereas the divergence became apparent by Day 15 (Figure B), which increased by Days 18 and 25 (Figure C,D). We also calculated the Euclidean distance between the populations in the PCA space and found that there is a time-dependent increase in the Euclidean distance of the population centroids from the proliferating cells (Supplementary Table 1). We also observed that there is a significant increase in the Euclidean distance between the Raman spectra of proliferating cells and 15 day senescent cells (0.1105 AU) compared to that of proliferating cells and 10 day senescent cells (0.025 AU), suggesting that there is a major change in the Raman signatures of MCF7 between Days 10 and 15 after treatment with Doxo.

Spectral loadings of the PCA demonstrated that lipid-related vibrational modes contributed to the separation of populations on the PCs (Figure E): CH2 scissoring (1445 cm–1) accounts for ∼0.0716, while CC stretching (1655 cm–1) and CH2 stretching (2850 cm–1) contributed ∼0.0223 and ∼0.065 to the PCs, respectively. Also, measuring the intensities of spectral shift peaks corresponding to biomolecular species showed a time-dependent increase in the spectral peak corresponding to CH2 stretching (2850 cm–1) (Figure F). The spectral peak intensity of CC/NCO stretching (1655 cm–1) showed a time-dependent increase until Day 18, followed by a decrease in intensity by Day 25 (Supplementary Figure S1E). The spectral peak intensity of CH3 stretching (2920 and 2885 cm–1) also showed a time-dependent increase until Day 18, followed by a decrease in intensity by Day 25 (Figure G and Supplementary Figure S1F). Finally, the spectral peak intensity of ester bond stretching (1740 cm–1) corresponding to triglycerides showed a time-dependent increase in the number of senescent MCF7 cells (Supplementary Figure S1G). This suggests that there is a distinct time-dependent alteration of metabolism of different biomolecular species during senescence.

The storage and metabolism of fatty acids in cells is compartmentalized in the cells. , We used hyperspectral Raman imaging of fixed cells, followed by true component analysis (WITec GmbH; Supplementary Figure S2A), to obtain Raman spectra from the lipid-rich regions in MCF7 cells during senescence (Figure A). We obtained hyperspectral Raman images from the lipid-rich regions of proliferating MCF7 cells and of those on Days 10, 15, 18, and 25 after treatment with Doxo. Then, we preprocessed the data in Python using RamanSPy and performed PCA on the processed data (Appendix V). We observed an overlap between the Raman spectra of the proliferating cells versus 10 day senescent cells (Figure B), and the divergence became apparent from Day 15 (Figure C), which increased on Days 18 and 25 (Figure D,E), similar to the PCA of whole cells (Figure A–D). Calculation of the Euclidean distance between the population showed that there is a time-dependent increase in the Euclidean distance of the population centroids from the proliferating cells (Supplementary Table 2). We also observed a significant increase in the Euclidean distance between the Raman spectra of proliferating cells and 15 day senescent cells (0.0905 AU) compared to that of proliferating cells and 10 day senescent cells (0.0396 AU), suggesting that there is a major change in the Raman signatures of lipid-rich regions of MCF7 cells between Days 10 and 15 after treatment with Doxo. Analysis of the spectral loadings of PCA demonstrated that lipid-related vibrational modes contributed to the separation of populations on the PCs (Figure F): CH2 scissoring (1445 cm–1) accounts for ∼0.2624, while CC stretching (1655 cm–1) and CH2 stretching (2850 cm–1) contributed ∼0.2413 and ∼1.142, respectively. Measuring the intensity of the spectral shift peaks corresponding to CH2 stretching (2850 cm–1), CH3 stretching (2920 and 2885 cm–1), CC/NCO stretching (1655 cm–1), and ester bond stretching (1740 cm–1) showed an increase in the intensity until Day 18, with Days 18 and 25 showing similar intensities (Figure G, Supplementary Figure S2B–E). These findings indicate that fatty acid-associated spectral shifts underlie the differentiation of proliferating and senescent cell states by hyperspectral Raman imaging. Together, the results establish that Raman microscopy, coupled with true component analysis and PCA, can resolve senescence-associated lipid remodeling with high specificity. This capability highlights the potential to identify senescent cell niches in tissues in a label-free manner, enabling strategies for the selective targeting of senescent cells in aging and disease.

Senescent cells show an increased accumulation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA), including arachidonic acid. Senescent cells also show increased metabolism of arachidonic acid, leading to the synthesis and release of several classes of oxylipins, including prostaglandins. , Therefore, we measured the metabolism of arachidonic acid in senescent cells using Raman microscopy. Arachidonic acid (20:4) is a polyunsaturated fatty acid that shows characteristic spectral shifts at the wavenumbers 1655 and 3005 cm–1 that correspond to CC stretching and (CC)H stretching, respectively (Supplementary Figure S3A). Arachidonic acid with deuterium atoms across all the double bonds shows the presence of a characteristic spectral shift corresponding to (CC)D stretching at ∼2240 cm–1 (Supplementary Figure S3A, Figure A) in the biologically silent region. Measuring the changes in the (CC)D stretching peaks is advantageous due to the high signal-to-noise ratio in the otherwise silent region, allowing the measurement of chemical changes only in the labeled molecule. Therefore, we used deuterated arachidonic acid (AA-d 8) to measure the intracellular metabolism of arachidonic acid in senescent cells. We observed that the Raman spectrum of AA-d 8 shows two peaks corresponding to (CC)D stretching, one at 2220 cm–1 (minor peak) and another at 2254 cm–1 (major peak), suggesting two vibrational modes of (CC)D stretching (Figure A). We hypothesized that the metabolism of arachidonic acid can be measured by changes in the vibrational modes of the (CC)D bond, which can be monitored by changes in the Raman signatures. We tested the hypothesis by the in vitro metabolism of AA-d 8 by the COX2 enzyme (Supplementary Figure S3B) and measured the changes in the spectral shifts. We did not observe any change in the wavenumber of the spectral shifts in the (CC)D peaks (Supplementary Figure S3C), suggesting no change in the vibrational modes of (CC)D stretching. We then measured any changes in the relative intensities of the (CC)D peaks (2220 and 2254 cm–1) to the CH2 stretching peaks (2850 cm–1) during the COX2-mediated metabolism of arachidonic acid. We observed a time-dependent decrease in the intensities of the (CC)D peaks relative to the CH2 stretching peaks during the COX2-mediated metabolism of AA-d 8, suggesting that the relative intensities of the (CC)D peaks can be used to measure the metabolism of arachidonic acid. So, to measure the metabolism of arachidonic acid in senescent cells, we treated ∼15 day senescent MCF7 cells with AA-d 8 (100 μM) overnight and observed complete cell death (data not shown). We hypothesized that the cell death observed after treating senescent cells with AA-d 8 overnight is due to the cytotoxic effects of arachidonic acid metabolites, a phenotype we observed in a previous study. Therefore, we pretreated senescent MCF7 cells with Cay-10404, an inhibitor of COX2, for 2 h before treating the cells with AA-d 8. We treated the pretreated senescent MCF7 cells with AA-d 8 (100 μM) in the presence of Cay-10404 overnight. We then removed the Cay-10404 from the treatment medium and incubated the cells with AA-d 8 for 0.25, 0.5, and 1 h. We then fixed the cells and performed hyperspectral Raman imaging. Spectral heatmaps of the senescent MCF7 in the regions 2220 ± 17.5 and 2254 ± 17.5 cm–1 showed intracellular accumulation of AA-d 8 in the senescent cells, predominantly in the perinuclear region (Figure C). We also observed a heterogeneous intensity distribution of AA-d 8 in the perinuclear region, suggesting heterogeneity in the intracellular accumulation of arachidonic acid. We analyzed the hyperspectral Raman images of senescent cells treated with AA-d 8 by ratiometric analysis of the spectral heatmaps of spectral shifts corresponding to (CC)D stretching to CH2 stretching (Figure D). We observed a time-dependent decrease in the major peak (2254 cm–1) intensity normalized to total lipids (2850 cm–1) after removal of the COX2 inhibitor (Figure E), similar to that in the in vitro metabolism of AA-d 8 (Figure B), suggesting metabolism of arachidonic acid in senescent MCF7 cells. We also observed a decrease in the peak intensity of the minor peak (2220 cm–1) normalized to total lipids (2850 cm–1) at 1 h after removal of the COX2 inhibitor. These observations suggest the detection of the metabolism of arachidonic acid in senescent MCF7 cells after removal of the COX2 inhibitor by Raman microscopy. This approach demonstrates that Raman spectroscopy of deuterated arachidonic acid can monitor COX2-driven metabolism both in vitro and in cells. Beyond validating COX2 inhibition, this strategy opens the possibility of screening inhibitors of other enzymes in arachidonic acid pathways. By coupling enzymatic assays with hyperspectral Raman imaging, one can spatially and temporally map lipid metabolism in senescent cells at high resolution. This capacity to resolve metabolic heterogeneity without labels establishes Raman microscopy as a powerful chemical biology tool, enabling the precise dissection of senescence-associated lipid pathways and accelerating the discovery of selective interventions against senescent cell function.
