Comparative analysis of genetic diversity and productive performance in Saudi native and carrier pigeon genotypes
Authors: Sulaiman Aljumah, Ibrahim Al-Homidan, Mohamed Elzarei, Mohanad Ibrahim, Moataz Fathi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This study aimed to characterize the genetic diversity, population structure, and productive performance of three Saudi native pigeon populations (Spotted black-beak, SBB; White black-beak, WBB; White white-beak, WWB) in comparison with the Carrier genotype. Four genotypes were reared under identical management conditions. Carcass characteristics were evaluated based on genotype and sex. Egg quality traits, including external and internal characteristics, were determined using standard physical and quality indices. The results demonstrated that genotype was the primary factor influencing carcass composition, slaughter performance, and egg quality traits, while sex had only minor effects on a limited number of traits. Significant differences were observed in breast muscle yield, wing and drumstick proportions, and internal organ percentages. Carrier pigeons exhibited higher internal organ and giblets proportions, whereas SBB and WWB showed superior breast muscle yield. Egg quality traits were also significantly affected by genotype, with the Carrier genotype outperforming native breeds in egg size, yolk color, shell thickness, and shell strength, while WWB showed the lowest values. Genetic distance analysis revealed low to moderate differentiation among populations. SBB and WBB formed a nearly indistinguishable genetic cluster (FST = 0.002) and showed close genetic affinity with the Carrier breed. In contrast, the WWB lineage was the most genetically distinct population (FST ≈ 0.10). The findings of the current study will be useful for genetic characterization and provide a foundation for developing sustainable genetic improvement and conservation breeding programs for pigeon populations.

Introduction

Pigeon is an important economic poultry species in many countries. Pigeon farming has evolved from a hobby and food supply to a popular activity among many people, particularly in urban and rural areas. Generally, three basic purposes of pigeon breeding are identified including meat production, racing and ornament (Fekete et al., 1999). Practice is not only a symbol of heritage but also an important aspect of social life, with pigeon racing and breeding being common pastimes. For racing purposes, breeders are very conscious about pigeon flight muscles, flying capability, feathers, and eyes (Kabir, 2014). Pigeon meat has been consumed all over the world due to high nutritional value and delicious taste (Lan et al., 2025). Pigeons are extensively used for scientific research, notably in genetics and physiology (Domyan and Shapiro, 2017). Furthermore, pigeons’ droppings could be used as a fertilizer to enhance desert soil fertility (Ciminari et al., 2005). Pigeon meat consumption has become popular for consumers in many countries due to delicious taste, higher protein concentration and lower cholesterol (Chang et al., 2023). In Saudi Arabia, pigeons hold significant cultural and historical value, particularly in holy places such as Mecca and the Prophet's Mosque in Medina, where they are strictly protected by law from killing.

In recent years, pigeon farming has gained even more popularity, with modern technologies and techniques being applied to improve the breeding process and bird management. With an increasing population and growing interest in high-quality meats, the demand for pigeon meat has surged, particularly in urban centers like Riyadh, Jeddah, and Mecca. Native pigeon breeds are well adapted to harsh environmental conditions, including high temperatures and limited feed resources. These breeds often exhibit strong disease resistance and the ability to survive under traditional management systems (Jiménez et al., 2024). They also possess significant genetic diversity and distinct genetic backgrounds, which are valuable for identifying and conserving animal genetic resources (Balog et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2025).Lately, microsatellite markers have been used to characterize numerous pigeon breeds and populations (Ramadan et al., 2018; Senturk et al., 2024; Sola-Ojo et al., 2025). These analyses have proven instrumental in evaluating national biodiversity and genetically characterizing indigenous breeds. However, native pigeon breeds generally show moderate body size, slower growth rates, and lower productive performance compared to specialized commercial breeds developed for meat or industrial production. On the other hand, they have lower feed requirements, natural resilience to adverse environmental conditions, and higher resistance to diseases and stress compared to conventional industrial breeds (Chang et al., 2023).

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the productive performance and genetic diversity of Saudi native pigeon varieties in comparison with the Carrier pigeon, which is renowned globally for its specialized performance, particularly in racing and homing capabilities.

Materials and methods

Study site, and ethical statement

The experiment was conducted at the Poultry Research Farm of Qassim University, Saudi Arabia. All research procedures have been approved by the Committee of Research Ethics, Deanship of Graduate Studies and Scientific Research, Qassim University, Al-Qassim, Saudi Arabia (Approval # 25-39-22 dated May 29, 2025).

Pigeon genotypes and housing

Four genotypes of pigeons were utilized in the current study, including the Carrier breed and three Saudi native pigeon populations. A total of 80 breeding pairs aged one year were used, comprising with 20 pairs assigned to each breed. Each pair (one male and one female) was housed separately in an individual cage to ensure accurate recording of egg production and to prevent interference among pairs. All birds were maintained under similar environmental and management conditions throughout the experimental period. The pigeons were kept in a well-ventilated housing system under natural photoperiod and ambient temperature. Each cage was equipped with a squab shelter nest, hanging feeder and 2 nipples. Feed and fresh drinking water were provided ad libitum. A basal diet was formulated containing 17% total protein, 2950 kcal/kg feed. Egg quality assessment

At 12 months of age, fifteen intact eggs from each genotype were collected and used to evaluate internal and external egg quality characteristics. Egg weight (g) was determined with an accuracy of 0.01 g. Egg width and egg length were measured in mm using a stainless Vernier caliper (±0.01 mm). Egg shape was calculated according to the following formula:Egg shape = [egg width / egg length] × 100

Following weighing, the breaking strength of intact eggs was determined in Kg/cm2 using an Egg Force ReaderTM (Orka Food Technology Ltd, USA). The heights of the thick albumen and egg yolk were measured by placing the egg contents on a leveled metal plate and using a tripod micrometer. The yolk was then separated, rolled on tissue paper to remove adhering albumen, and weighed. Albumen weight was calculated by subtracting yolk and shell weights from the entire egg weight. Yolk and albumen weights were expressed as a percentage of egg weight. Eggshells, including membranes were washed with gently running water to remove adhering albumen. The wet shells were air-dried for 24 h and then weighed to the nearest 0.01 g. Relative eggshell weight was calculated based on egg weight. Shell thickness was measured at three locations (two poles and the equator) using a dial gauge micrometer to the nearest 0.01 mm. Yolk color was evaluated by visually comparing the yolk with the DSM Yolk Color Fan (Hoffman-La Roche Ltd., Basel, Switzerland). Haugh units (HU) were calculated using the following equation:HU = 100 × log10 (H − 1.7W0.37 + 7.57)Where H is the albumen height (mm) and W is the egg weight (g).

The following formula was used to calculate the eggshell area (Paganelli and Olszowka, 1974):EA = 4.835 × EW0.662Where EA is eggshell area (cm2) and EW is egg weight (g).

Carcass evaluation

To evaluate the carcass characteristics, a total of 48 squabs were randomly assigned for carcass quality assessment with 12 birds (6 males and 6 females) from each genotype. Following a 8-h fasting period, squabs were weighed, euthanized via cervical dislocation, defeathered, and manually eviscerated. Dressing carcass weight was determined after removing the feet, head, alimentary canal, trachea, abdominal fat and all internal organs. The dissected carcass components, including thigh, drumstick, breast muscles (pectoralis superficialis and pectoralis profundus), both wings with skin, whole leg (drumstick, thigh with back bone part), and internal organs were weighed in g and calculated as percentage of live body weight. The bursa of Fabricius, spleen, thymus (all lobes from left side of the neck) were removed and weighed to the nearest milligram. The relative weights of lymphoid organs (organ index) were compared among different genotypes. Giblets consist of liver, heart and empty gizzard.

DNA extraction and whole-genome sequencing

Blood samples were collected from a representative number of adult pigeons representing four distinct lineages, including native Najdi sub-lineages and Carrier pigeons.

The final high-quality dataset comprised 11 individuals (SBB = 3, WBB = 3, WWB = 2, Carrier = 3), reflecting the limited availability of authenticated, pedigree-verified purebred birds of these rare native lineages, which was the primary constraint on sample size. Provenance and DNA quality were prioritized over sample count to minimize bias arising from misclassified or admixed individuals. Samples were drawn using sterile syringes and transferred into EDTA-coated tubes to prevent coagulation. Samples were stored at −20°C until further processing. Genomic DNA was extracted using a standard phenol–chloroform protocol following the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and agarose gel electrophoresis. High-quality DNA samples were used for whole-genome sequencing (WGS). Sequencing libraries were prepared using an Illumina-compatible library preparation kit. Paired-end sequencing (150 bp) was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform to generate high-coverage genomic data for each individual.

Sequence quality control and alignment

Raw sequencing reads were subjected to quality control using FastQC (v0.11.9) (Andrews, 2010) to assess read quality. Low-quality reads and adapter sequences were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v0.39) (Bolger et al., 2014) with standard parameters (e.g., SLIDINGWINDOW:4:20 MINLEN:36). Cleaned reads were then aligned to the reference pigeon genome (Columba livia) using the Burrows–Wheeler Aligner algorithm BWA-MEM (v0.7.17) (Li, 2013) with default parameters. The resulting alignment files were processed using SAMtools (v1.13) (Danecek et al., 2021) to convert, sort, and index BAM files. Duplicate reading originating from PCR amplification were marked and excluded using Picard Tools (v2.26) (Broad Institute, 2019).

Variant calling and filtering

Single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) and small insertions/deletions (InDels) were identified using a joint genotyping approach. Variant filtering was performed using BCFtools (v1.13) (Danecek et al., 2021). Strict quality filters were applied to obtain a high-confidence biallelic SNP dataset. Variants were excluded if they exhibited a missing genotype rate greater than 20% across. Joint genotyping of the 11 retained individuals yielded 20,085,882 raw SNPs. After restriction to biallelic sites and filtering on missingness (F_MISSING ≤ 0.2) and minor allele frequency (MAF ≥ 0.05), a high-confidence panel of 17,884 genome-wide SNPs was retained for all downstream analyses.

Genetic similarity and population structure analysis

Genetic similarity among pigeon genotypes was assessed based on the high-confidence genome-wide SNP dataset. Identity-by-State (IBS) pairwise distances were calculated based on allelic sharing. A breed-averaged genetic similarity matrix (Similarity Coefficient = 1 - Distance) was constructed to evaluate the core evolutionary differentiation at the population level (Euclidean and Manhattan distance averaging via Python `scipy.spatial` library). Clustering analysis was conducted using multiple complementary approaches to ensure robust inference of genetic relationships. First, dendrograms were constructed using the Unweighted Pair Group Method with Arithmetic Mean (UPGMA) based on breed similarity coefficients. In parallel, individual-level phylogenetic relationships were reconstructed using maximum-likelihood methods implemented in IQ-TREE (v2.4.0) (Minh et al., 2020), where the best-fit substitution model (GTR+F+G4+I) was automatically selected and branch support was assessed with 1000 ultrafast bootstrap (UFBoot) replicates (Hoang et al., 2018). To further explore genetic structure, network-based relationships were visualized through Minimum Spanning Trees (MST) generated using NetworkX (Hagberg et al., 2008) with Kamada–Kawai force-directed layout. Additionally, population structure and ancestry proportions were inferred using ADMIXTURE (v1.3.0) (Alexander et al., 2009), with the optimal number of genetic clusters (K) determined based on cross-validation error estimates. Genetic diversity indices, including observed heterozygosity (Ho), expected heterozygosity (He), and fixation index (FST), were calculated to quantify genetic variation within and between pigeon genotypes. Data parsing, matrix generation, and plotting (heatmaps, networks, and scaled dendrograms) were conducted using Python (pandas, numpy, seaborn, scikit-learn) and R software environments.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using JMP version 11 (SAS Institute Inc., 2013). The experimental design followed a completely randomized design (CRD), with genotype as the main factor for egg quality traits. Whereas the results of carcass evaluation were subjected to a two-way analysis of variance, including genotype and sex as fixed effects and their interaction. Results are presented as means ± the pooled standard error of the mean (SEM). Differences among groups were assessed using Duncan’s new multiple range test. Statistical significance was set as P < 0.05.

The results presented in Table 3 revealed significant differences (P < 0.05) among pigeon genotypes for all evaluated egg quality and physical characteristics. The Carrier genotype consistently showed superior values in most traits, recording the highest egg weight (19.3 g), eggshell surface area (34.3 cm²), shell weight (1.45 g) and eggshell thickness (243.5 µm) compared to the Saudi native genotypes. Among the native breeds, SBB and WBB exhibited comparable values in egg weight (16.3 and 16.4 g, respectively), while WWB showed the lowest figures in most traits, particularly egg weight (14.9 g) and eggshell surface (28.8 cm²). Regarding egg shape index, SBB recorded the highest value (0.76), significantly exceeding the other genotypes. In contrast, the Carrier and other native breeds showed similar but lower values. For internal egg quality traits, Haugh unit values differed significantly (P = 0.011), with SBB showing the highest value (89.7), while the other genotypes ranged from 85.1 to 86.7. Yolk height was significantly higher in native breeds (SBB, WBB, WWB) compared to the Carrier genotype. Yolk diameter was significantly larger (P = 0.002) in WWB (2.90 cm) and Carrier (2.94 cm) compared with SBB and WBB (almost equal). Genetic diversity and population structure

The genetic distance matrix (Table 4, Fig. 1) illustrates the magnitude of genetic differentiation among the native pigeon populations (SBB, WBB, WWB) and the Carrier population. Genetic distance values ranged from 0.118 to 0.184, reflecting a low to moderate level of genetic divergence among the investigated groups. The lowest genetic distance (0.118) was observed between SBB and WBB, indicating high genetic similarity. This close relationship likely reflects a shared genetic background, historical gene flow, or uniform selection pressures applied by breeders. Interestingly, the Carrier population exhibited low genetic distances with both SBB (0.119) and WBB (0.127). This close genetic affinity implies that Carrier pigeons may share a common ancestral lineage with these native breeds, or that historical admixture has occurred, shaping their current genetic architecture. Conversely, the WWB population demonstrated the greatest genetic divergence, showing the highest distances when compared to SBB (0.180), WBB (0.184), and the Carrier population (0.182). This pronounced differentiation suggests that WWB has experienced a distinct selection history, reproductive isolation, or variations in geographic and breeding management strategies. Analysis of heterozygosity (Table 5) showed relatively stable expected heterozygosity (He = 0.18–0.19) across all populations, indicating comparable baseline genetic variability. However, observed heterozygosity (Ho) differed among groups. In SBB, WBB, and Carrier populations, Ho was slightly lower than He, suggesting a mild heterozygote deficit potentially caused by limited inbreeding, substructure, or non-random mating practices. In contrast, the WWB population showed higher Ho values and greater variability, indicating elevated internal genetic diversity. This suggests possible historical admixture or the coexistence of multiple sub-lineages within this population. Pairwise FST values further clarified population structure (Table 6). An almost negligible differentiation was observed between SBB and WBB (FST = 0.002), confirming that they essentially form a single genetic cluster with strong gene flow or very recent divergence. The Carrier population occupied an intermediate genetic position, with low to moderate differentiation from native breeds (FST = 0.034–0.045), supporting its interpretation as an admixed or transitional lineage. Discussion

Genotype significantly influenced several carcass traits, muscle distribution, and internal organ proportions in pigeons. These findings highlight the importance of genetic background in improving carcass quality and meat yield in pigeon production systems. The absence of significant differences in body weight and eviscerated carcass percentage among genotypes suggests that growth performance among the studied pigeon breeds was relatively similar. This stability suggests that overall carcass yield relative to body weight is a conserved trait in Columba livia, consistent with other reports indicating comparable slaughter yields among traditional pigeon strains under standardized feeding and management conditions (Kokoszyński et al., 2020).Furthermore, the results of genetic similarity support the previous observation, indicating that the WWB and Carrier genotypes are genetically closer to each other compared with the other two breeds. The significant effect of genotype on wing and breast muscle yield reflects breed-related differences in carcass composition and muscle distribution. Sex-related differences observed in wing and thigh percentage, where males recorded higher values than females, could be attributed to sexual dimorphism and the influence of male hormones on muscle development. Similar findings have been reported in pigeons, where males generally exhibit larger body size and higher values in several morphological traits including wing length and body weight (Parés-Casanova and Kabir, 2020; Savaş and Erdem, 2022).Relative thigh percentage was not significantly affected by genotype. However, males exhibited higher thigh proportions than females, consistent with sexual dimorphism in muscle distribution reported in domestic pigeons (Agbolosu et al., 2021).Genotype differences were more evident in drumstick yield, with WWB showing the highest proportion and Carrier the lowest. These differences likely reflect variation in locomotor activity and muscle allocation among breeds selected for different production objectives (Stęczny et al., 2024).Significant genotype effects were also observed for all internal organ ratios. SBB pigeons had the highest relative heart weight, whereas Carrier pigeons had the lowest, suggesting differences in metabolic and cardiovascular demands (Viscor et al., 1985). In contrast, Carrier pigeons exhibited the highest relative liver percentage (2.5%), which may indicate greater metabolic activity and nutrient processing capacity (Zaefarian et al., 2019; Richardson et al., 2025). Relative gizzard weight was also higher in WWB and Carrier birds, suggesting increased digestive investment. Overall, the relative weight of giblets was also highest in the Carrier genotype. These findings may reflect variations in metabolic activity and digestive capacity among pigeon breeds, as previously reported in studies showing significant genetic effects on liver and gizzard development in Carrier and King pigeons (Stęczny et al., 2024; Kokoszyński et al., 2020).

Breast muscle yield showed pronounced genetic variation. SBB, WBB, and WWB pigeons had significantly higher breast muscle percentages (20.4–23.2%) than Carrier pigeons (15.3%), confirming that muscle distribution is strongly genotype dependent (Kokoszyński et al., 2020; Stęczny et al., 2024). Since breast muscle is the most economically valuable carcass component, the native breeds appear to possess a genetic advantage for meat production. The lower breast yield in Carrier pigeons likely reflects selection for flight performance rather than meat traits, a pattern previously reported in pigeons and other avian species (Kokoszyński et al., 2020). Sex effects were generally limited, with males showing a higher relative thigh weight and a minor sex × genotype interaction for breast muscle yield. In terms of lymphoid organs, the highest relative weight of thymus and spleen associated with Carier breed may indicate a greater development of these organs, which may reflect a stronger immune response (Ceccopieri and Madej, 2024).Overall, genotype exerted a much stronger influence than sex on slaughter and carcass characteristics in the studied pigeon breeds.

Characteristics of internal and external egg quality

The present findings revealed significant genotype-dependent variation in egg quality traits among Saudi native pigeon breeds, confirming that genetic background plays a critical role in determining egg characteristics in pigeons. Similar genotype effects have been reported in different pigeon breeds, where substantial variation in egg weight, size, and internal composition has been observed. In Nigerian local pigeons, average egg weight varied significantly across populations, with associated differences in albumen, yolk height and other internal quality traits, indicating that genetics and possibly management conditions influence both external and internal egg characteristics (Okoh et al., 2022).The superior egg weight and dimensions recorded in the Carrier breed are consistent with earlier findings in pigeons. Stęczny et al. (2024) reported that egg weight in pigeons varies significantly among breeds and is closely associated with parental body weight and genetic potential. Larger egg size in pigeons has been linked to improved squab growth and survival, as heavier eggs provide greater nutrient reserves for embryonic development(Ibrahim and Sani, 2010; Chen et al., 2016).Differences in egg shape index and component proportions across genotypes may influence shell strength and hatchability outcomes. Egg shape is often correlated with shell quality and mechanical properties, which can vary across breeds and genetic backgrounds. However, the current results confirm that genotypes are a key determinant of egg quality traits in pigeons. The superior performance of the Carrier breed in most egg characteristics suggests its potential use in breeding programs aimed at improving egg production and quality in local pigeon populations, while other genotypes may be valuable for maintaining genetic diversity and specific quality traits (Stęczny et al., 2024; Sola-Ojo et al., 2025). Haugh unit values were highest in SBB, indicating superior albumen quality and egg freshness. The Haugh unit is a widely accepted measure of internal egg quality and is influenced by both genetic and physiological factors (Sun et al., 2019; Stęczny et al., 2024). Likewise, the larger yolk diameter observed in WWB and Carrier genotypes corresponded with their higher yolk proportions, suggesting enhanced yolk deposition that may be associated with differences in lipid metabolism and follicular development (Okoh et al., 2022). Egg yolk color was more intense in the Carrier genotype, potentially reflecting greater carotenoid deposition or differences in pigment metabolism. This is consistent with recent studies indicating that egg quality traits such as albumen proportion, yolk composition, and shell characteristics are polygenic and strongly genotype-dependent (Yang et al., 2025). The higher albumen percentage observed in SBB, WBB, and Carrier genotypes suggests superior albumen deposition efficiency, an important indicator of egg freshness and protein content. In contrast, the higher yolk proportion in WWB reflects greater allocation of nutrients to lipid-rich components. Similar genetic variation in albumen and yolk proportions has been reported in pigeons and other poultry species (Sun et al., 2019).Regarding shell quality, Carrier and WBB breeds exhibited superior shell thickness and strength, indicating enhanced shell mineralization capacity. Eggshell formation depends on calcium metabolism and structural protein deposition, both of which are strongly influenced by genetic factors (Stęczny et al., 2024; Iram et al., 2025). Stronger shells are desirable because they reduce breakage and improve egg handling and marketability. In contrast, the lower egg quality measures, particularly shell properties in the WWB group likely reflect limited selection pressure for reproductive traits and possibly different genetic resource backgrounds. Overall, the Carrier genotype demonstrated superior shell quality traits, whereas SBB exhibited better internal egg quality, particularly albumen percentage and Haugh unit values. In contrast, WWB was characterized by a higher yolk proportion. These findings emphasize the importance of genotype selection according to specific production goals, whether focused on egg quality, hatchability, or nutritional value.

Genetic differentiation among native pigeon populations

The observed genetic distance values observed (0.118–0.184) indicate a low to moderate level of differentiation among Saudi native pigeon populations and the Carrier breed. This is consistent with global trends in domestic pigeons, where most genetic variation occurs within breeds rather than between them, reflecting shared ancestry and human-mediated movement (Esmailizadeh et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2021). The remarkable genomic similarity between SBB, WBB, and the Carrier population highlights persistent historical gene flow. Carrier pigeons, traditionally selected for exceptional navigational and flight-endurance characteristics, may have historically introgressed into these regional native lines to bolster physical traits, or alternatively, arose from a common regional ancestor. Similar admixture patterns are extensively documented in other regional studies, where steady gene flow maintains low background differentiation despite stark phenotypic variety (Ramadan et al., 2011; Małgorzata et al., 2018). In sharp contrast, the WWB population’s consistently higher divergence profiles (up to 0.184 substitution distance) point toward strictly localized line-breeding strategies. This pattern may be attributed to reproductive isolation, a distinct selection history, or variations in geographic and breeding management strategies. Maintaining isolated sub-populations such as WWB is critical for conserving total avian genomic variability, which simultaneously serves as a primary foundation for improving resilience against evolving environmental stressors (Balog et al., 2025).

Genetic diversity and heterozygosity analysis

The current study represents an initial genome-wide baseline. The modest number of individuals per group, particularly the WWB group, limits the precision of within-group heterozygosity estimates and the power to detect rare alleles. Consequently, the among-group differentiation reported here should be regarded as indicative, and expanding the sample size of each lineage remains a priority for future study. However, the level of genetic variability observed in the present study (0.18-0.19) indicates a low to moderate genetic diversity, which is typical for domesticated avian populations subjected to artificial selection pressures. Such levels are consistent with findings in pigeon populations, where selective breeding often maintains functional diversity while promoting specific phenotypic traits.

A consistent pattern was observed in the SBB, WBB, and Carrier populations, where observed heterozygosity was slightly lower than expected heterozygosity. This deviation suggests the presence of non-random mating or mild inbreeding, a common outcome in structured breeding programs aiming to stabilize desirable characteristics. Similar patterns have been reported by Shapiro et al. (2013), who demonstrated that strong artificial selection in domestic pigeons leaves detectable genomic signatures, often associated with reduced heterozygosity in selected loci. In contrast, the WWB population exhibited elevated observed heterozygosity, indicating a higher level of genetic mixing. Increased heterozygosity is commonly associated with admixture events or recent gene flow between genetically differentiated lineages. This may be due to the introduction of novel alleles leading to higher levels of genetic variability within populations. This observation aligns with the findings of Stringham et al. (2012), who showed that genetic diversity in pigeon populations can be significantly enhanced through the interbreeding of different fancy and utility breeds. Furthermore, recent genomic analyses based on SNP markers, Hou et al.(2025) support the notion that pigeon breeds may retain moderate diversity levels while still exhibiting clear structural differences due to breeding history and selection intensity. The extremely low genetic differentiation between SBB and WBB native breeds (FST = 0.002)indicates that these two populations are genetically almost indistinguishable. This suggests either a very recent common origin or the presence of continuous gene flow, effectively maintaining them as a single panmictic population. The intermediate differentiation for Carrier breed (FST = 0.034-0.045) reflects partial genetic separation while still retaining shared ancestry. In contrast, the WWB population demonstrated a moderate level of genetic differentiation (FST= 0.10), reflecting a more distinct genetic structure. This level of differentiation is generally interpreted as evidence of restricted gene flow, independent breeding history, or partial reproductive isolation. According to classical population genetics frameworks, FST values in this range indicate meaningful genetic structuring that may correspond to functional or adaptive divergence among populations (Wright, 1978).Comparable interpretations have been reported in population genetic studies of pigeon breeds, where moderate FST values indicate meaningful genetic structuring and differentiation among populations, often reflecting breeding history and patterns of gene flow (Bayraktar et al., 2024).

Pattern of diversity and implications for conservation

The distances observed in this targeted dataset are directly parallel with global reports. For instance, Balog et al. (2025) noted that Central European squab pigeons showed only moderate divergence despite extreme phenotypic differences in body morphology. Similarly, whole-genome analyses in Hainan, China (Xu et al., 2025), as well as microsatellite structural studies in Egypt (Ramadan et al., 2011) and Nigeria (Sola-Ojo et al., 2025) robustly confirm that human-mediated breeding and shared ancient origins severely limit extreme genetic divergence across modern Columba livia sub-groups. Molecular pathway studies utilizing SNP chips and deep resequencing confirm that while artificial selection accurately shapes performance architectures and structural body types, the foundational gene pool remains largely shared globally (Esmailizadeh et al., 2021; Hou et al., 2025). The Saudi pigeon populations faithfully follow this global pattern: exhibiting moderate, recent differentiation, with highly distinct outlier lineages such as the WWB acting as critical reservoirs for unique adaptive alleles. In consistent with the later assumption, Hou et al. (2021; 2025) stated that the local pigeons are typically shaped by natural and traditional selection under harsh environments, while Carrier pigeons have undergone artificial selection for specific traits such as navigational performance, reproduction, and body characteristics. The pronounced genetic distinctiveness of the WWB group powerfully underscores its intrinsic value for highly structured breeding networks. Utilizing genetically divergent, validated populations can dramatically enhance heterosis (hybrid vigor), directly impacting performance viability and disease resistance in regional flocks. Active conservation frameworks should specifically prioritize the WWB lineage to rigidly preserve this unique pocket of diversity. Concurrently, the observed structural admixture in the other groups (SBB, WBB) can be strategically leveraged to maintain the baseline robustness of Saudi pigeon stocks.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that pigeon genotype, rather than sex, was the main factor influencing carcass composition and egg quality traits. Native breeds exhibited superior breast meat yield, whereas Carrier pigeons showed better internal organ proportions and overall egg quality characteristics. Genetic analysis revealed low to moderate differentiation among populations, with SBB and WBB closely related to the Carrier breed, while WWB was the most genetically distinct population. These findings highlight the importance of conserving WWB as a valuable genetic resource and provide useful information for future pigeon breeding and conservation programs.
