Development of Novel mRNA Classifiers to Stratify Preoperative Thyroid Tumor Risk
Authors: Thomas F Barrett, Mohamed Jama, Mohammed Alshalalfa, Theresa Tharakan, Yangyang Hao, Joshua P Klopper, Taylor B Cavazos, Ryan S Jackson, Patrik Pipkorn, Jason T Rich, Paul A Zolkind, Douglas R Adkins, Peter Oppelt, Anthony J Apicelli, Nikhil Rammohan, Taylor C Brown, Sidharth V Puram, Sina Jasim, R Alex Harbison
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveDevelop a preoperative risk classifier for guiding the extent of surgery in differentiated thyroid carcinomas (DTCs) under 4 cm.Study DesignIn silico study, validation with retrospective cohort.SettingGenomic data repositories, academic tertiary medical center.MethodsPrognostic transcriptional groups and American Thyroid Association (ATA) risk groups in The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) were used to create novel Clinico‐TRanscriptomic (CTR) groups. Differential gene expression (DGE) was performed. Consensus clustering was used to assess the reproducibility of genomic and histologic features in: (1) Bethesda V/VI fine‐needle aspirates (FNA) from the Afirma Genomic Sequencing Classifier (GSC) database (GC cohort); (2) thyroid carcinomas annotated by ATA histopathologic risk group (surgical cohort); (3) independent surgical cohort.ResultsWithin TCGA (n = 451), CTR‐Low was enriched with BRAF V600E mutations. CTR‐Intermediate was enriched in follicular cancers, while CTR‐High had high rates of TERT promoter (TERTp) and BRAF V600E mutations. A 144 gene classifier (PreOperative INdicator of Thyroid cancer progression [POINT]) was developed based on inter‐CTR DGEs. Clustering of the GC cohort using POINT demonstrated similar genomic features with 3 groups characterized by BRAF V600E and ALK/NTRK/RET fusions, RAS mutations, or TERTp/BRAF V600E alterations. Agreement of POINT with ATA risk group in the surgical cohort (n = 129) showed fair agreement (weighted κ = 0.25), NPV 0.89 for ruling out high ATA risk, and NNT 5.3 to avoid 1 completion thyroidectomy.ConclusionThe POINT classifier is predictive of genomic alterations and ATA risk in independent data sets. This study advances precision oncology, shifting the paradigm from size‐based toward individualized, biology‐driven care.Keywords: Afirma, clustering, prognostic gene signature, thyroid cancer, thyroid nodule

Identifying patients with differentiated thyroid carcinoma (DTC) who are unlikely to experience recurrence in the preoperative setting is crucial, as this group typically demonstrates excellent survival outcomes. Besides tumor size, patient factors (eg, prior exposure to radiation), and evidence of clinical risk factors warranting postoperative radioactive iodine (eg, radiographic or clinical cervical lymphadenopathy, radiographic extrathyroidal extension), there are a lack of molecular features to confidently guide the extent of initial treatment in patients with cN0 ≤ 4 cm DTCs. Patients with ≤4 cm DTC and no evidence of regional or distant disease have the option of thyroid lobectomy. Yet, many of these patients undergo total thyroidectomy for a variety of reasons including avoiding a potential completion thyroidectomy, existing need for thyroid hormone replacement, desire for disease monitoring with thyroglobulin, and/or intraoperative findings warranting postoperative radioactive iodine. 1 In a National Cancer Database (NCDB) analysis, over 75% of patients with cN0 papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) ≤4 cm underwent total thyroidectomy with or without central neck dissection. 2 Though a marginal increase in the use of lobectomy was observed after publication of the 2015 American Thyroid Association (ATA) guidelines, several studies show relatively high rates of total thyroidectomy, even with indeterminate cytology (ITN). 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 Clinical prognostic factors such as age, tumor size, and American Thyroid Association (ATA) risk of recurrence classification are useful for postoperative risk stratification, though these are inherently not available in the preoperative setting. Molecular markers offer a complementary opportunity to further risk stratify patients preoperatively, aiding in clinical decision‐making and potentially sparing patients a total thyroidectomy when lobectomy is appropriate, or supporting surgical escalation if warranted. 6 , 7 , 8

Advances in molecular profiling have led to the refinement of the clinical risk of indeterminate nodules based on gene expression and somatic mutations. 3 , 4 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 However, molecular testing is infrequently leveraged for preoperative clinical decision‐making in patients with cytologically suspicious or positive thyroid nodules (ie, Bethesda V and VI). 10 , 13 Gene expression can be used for thyroid cancer risk stratification as observed in the 2014 Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) integrated analysis of 496 PTCs. 9 Among the key findings from this multi‐institutional study was that their patient cohort could be categorized into 5 prognostic risk groups based on bulk mRNA expression patterns, with specific clusters being associated with higher risk features (eg, cluster 5 was associated with higher stage). 9 We hypothesized that integrating molecular features with ATA clinicopathologic risk strata would yield a novel gene expression classifier refining risk estimates which could be used to aid in preoperative decision‐making.

In this study, we developed a novel mRNA gene expression classifier to guide preoperative risk stratification by incorporating tumor biology into surgical planning, enabling more accurate selection of patients for definitive total thyroidectomy versus lobectomy at the index operation. The classifier correlated with genomic alteration status in a Bethesda V/VI fine needle aspirate cohort and exhibited a high negative predictive value (NPV; 89%) for identifying pathologic low‐risk nodules in an independent surgical cohort.

Methods

Study Cohorts

This study was approved by the Washington University Institutional Review Board (IRB ID: 202308085). Clinical, genomic and transcriptomic data were extracted for 451 thyroid samples from the TCGA cohort via cBioPortal. Clinical data on disease progression, ATA risk of recurrence, disease stage, histopathology and MACIS score (metastatic, age, completeness of resection, local invasion, and size) 14 were publicly available. 15 Annotations for the 5 previously reported mRNA expression clusters were extracted for the TCGA samples from cBioPortal. Data on key genomic alterations of TERTp, BRAF V600E, and RAS were extracted in addition to whole transcriptome data from the same source. Samples with missing ATA risk groups were excluded from this analysis.

An independent cohort of 2205 Bethesda V/VI samples were extracted from the database containing data on consecutive thyroid tumors that were sent for Afirma Genomic Sequencing Classifier (GSC) and Xpression Atlas (XA) testing at the discretion of the ordering physician between May 2023‐June 2024 (the GC cohort). Genome‐wide expression data and key genomic alteration (BRAF V600E, TERTp, RAS, and ALK/NTRK/RET fusions) were collected for these samples.

An independent surgical cohort was included for further validation. Patients with Bethesda III to VI nodules who underwent surgery at Washington University between 2020 and 2024 and also had both Afirma GSC and available histopathologic reports were included. This study utilized de‐identified molecular and histopathologic data. No patient identifiers were accessed or included in the analysis.

Classifier Development in TCGA Thyroid Dataset

First, we integrated the ATA risk of recurrence group (low, intermediate, high) and 5 prognostic mRNA clusters based on progression‐free survival (PFS) in the TCGA data. We hypothesized that integrating histologic and transcriptomic data would capture disease biology that could be used to predict the final pathologic risk (ie, ATA pathology classification). TCGA mRNA clusters 2 and 4 had similar, excellent overall PFS, while clusters 1 and 3 had intermediate PFS. Therefore, TCGA mRNA clusters 2 and 4, and 1 and 3 were merged to make 2 new composite mRNA clusters. Cluster 5 had the worst PFS, so it remained an independent cluster. Next, these 3 new expression‐based prognostic groups were integrated with ATA risk of recurrence classifications (low, intermediate, or high) into 3 Clinico‐TRanscriptomic groups: (CTR‐Low (L), CTR‐Intermediate (I), and CTR‐high (H); Figure 1A). CTR‐L is defined as ATA‐low and mRNA cluster 2/4, and CTR‐H is defined as ATA‐high or mRNA cluster 5. Finally, to develop a gene classifier that would predict ATA risk of recurrence guiding individualized, biology‐driven care, we performed differential gene expression (DGE) analysis comparing expression between the CTR groups. Whole transcriptome DGE analysis was performed to identify genes upregulated in each CTR cluster. One‐way analysis of variance was used to identify differentially expressed genes with Bonferroni correction for multiple hypothesis testing. Genes with an adjusted P < .0001 and a mean log2 fold‐change >1.5, compared to the other 2 clusters, were selected as part of a final predictive 144 gene set referred to as the PreOperative INdicator of Thyroid cancer progression (POINT).

PFS for CTR clusters from (B) all patients, (C) ATA‐intermediate risk samples, and (D) BRAF V600E+ samples. PFS, progression‐free survival.

POINT classifier evaluation in Bethesda V/VI FNA samples tested with the Afirma GSC

To test the ability of the POINT classifier to distinguish genomic variants in an independent cohort, we applied the 144 gene classifier to 2205 Bethesda V/VI samples from the GC cohort. These samples were sent at the discretion of the ordering physician and clinical data, including postoperative histopathology were unknown. Clustering was performed using ConsensusClusterPlus R package to form 3 groups (POINT‐High (H), POINT‐Intermediate (I), POINT‐Low (L)) with distinct expression patterns across the 144 gene classifier. We then characterized the genomic differences between POINT‐clustered samples and compared them to the genomic alterations of TCGA CTR clusters.

Assessment of POINT‐based stratification of pathologic ATA risk class in an independent surgical cohort

Agreement between POINT and ATA was assessed with weighted kappa (quadratic), the contingency table, and percent agreement; binary metrics (sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), area under the curve (AUC)) for ATA high vs not‐high and POINT high vs not‐high were summarized with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Lesions classified as noninvasive follicular thyroid neoplasm with papillary‐like nuclear features (NIFTP) were classified as ATA low risk. Number needed to treat (NNT) to avoid one completion thyroidectomy was estimated assuming either a 30% baseline completion rate consistent with reported completion thyroidectomy rates after lobectomy, 15.7% for Bethesda III/IV nodules, 26.5% to 38% for Bethesda V/VI or lobe eligible, and 43.1% for early completion in post 2015 malignant cohorts. 16 , 17 , 18

Results

Of the 451 samples from TCGA where ATA and prognostic mRNA cluster annotation were available, 24 (5% of total) samples were annotated as ATA high‐risk. These high‐risk samples were highly associated with PFS (Supplemental Figure S1, available online). Supplemental Figure S2, available online shows the schema to integrate the mRNA‐based clusters annotated for the TCGA cohort based on PFS. Figure 1A illustrates the merging of the 3 new meta‐clusters with the ATA risk of recurrence groups creating the CTR groups. These new clusters were associated with differential disease progression and clinico‐genomic characteristics (Table 1). When characterizing the underlying molecular and clinical characteristics of these new CTR groups (Table 1), we found CTR‐L (n = 57) was enriched with BRAF V600E mutants and low‐risk DTC. In contrast, CTR‐I (n = 273) was enriched in follicular cancers, and CTR‐H (n = 121) had relatively high rates of TERTp and BRAF V600E mutants, high MACIS scores, and advanced stage disease. Genomic and Clinic‐Prognostic Features of the Integrated Clinico‐TRanscriptomic (CTR) Clusters

To test the performance of the CTR clusters in the Afirma GC cohort, we conducted DGE analysis to identify genes upregulated in each one of the CTR risk groups (Figure 2A). As a result, 123 genes were upregulated in CTR‐H group, 21 were upregulated in CTR‐L, and 8 were upregulated in CTR‐I. To concentrate the gene signature at the extremes of the prognostic groups (ie, CTR‐H and CTR‐L), we did not include the 8 DGEs in the CTR‐I group in the signature. Thus, for this analysis, we only used the genes upregulated in CTR‐H and CTR‐L forming a 144 gene classifier that we named the POINT.

The novel PreOperative INdicator of Thyroid (POINT) 144 gene classifier stratifies fine needle aspirate (FNA) samples by genomic features. (A) Discovery and evaluation of POINT. (B) Clustering of Bethesda V/VI samples in the GC cohort (n = 2205) by the POINT.

Clustering analysis of 2205 Afirma Bethesda V/VI samples based on the POINT classifier revealed 3 clusters that were molecularly analogous to the CTR clusters (Figure 2B). POINT‐Low nodules were enriched with BRAFV600E (74%) and ALK/NTRK/RET fusions (10%). POINT‐Intermediate nodules were enriched with RAS mutations (11.7%) and lacked BRAF V600E alterations. POINT‐High nodules were enriched with TERTp (15.4%) and BRAF V600E (87%) alterations (Table 2).

In the surgical cohort, there were 129 thyroid carcinoma cases with both histopathology and Afirma GC data available. RNA expression data from this group recapitulated the initial clustering analysis with POINT‐high cases enriched for ATA high/intermediate‐high and POINT‐low cases enriched for ATA low (Figure 3). POINT classification and ATA‐risk strata showed fair 3‐level agreement (weighted κ = 0.25, 95% CI 0.09‐0.39; observed agreement 44.2%). In the binary framing (POINT high vs not‐high; ATA high/intermediate‐high vs not‐high), sensitivity was 0.63 (95% CI 0.41‐0.81), specificity 0.70 (95% CI 0.60‐0.78), PPV 0.32, and NPV 0.89 (≈11% false negative rate). AUC was 0.67 (95% CI 0.56‐0.79). Under the assumed 30% baseline completion rate, the estimated strategy‐based NNT was 5.3 per lobectomy‐managed patient. In sensitivity analyses, the NNT ranged from 2.9 to 11.1 as the baseline completion rate varied from 45% to 20% (Table 3).

Discussion

In this study, we created a novel prognostic risk classifier integrating expression and pathologic risk class to improve upon the limited prognostic sensitivity and specificity of molecular variant and fusion detection that current molecular classifiers employ. Determining the optimal initial extent of surgery for DTC <4 cm remains a pivotal and often uncertain decision in contemporary practice. While molecular tests can better stratify patients’ risk profile for harboring malignancy in patients with ITN than cytology alone, there are no prospective studies utilizing molecular data to predict pathologic risk. 19 , 20 Here, we develop a novel classifier that identifies shared genomic drivers in an independent Bethesda V/VI fine‐needle aspirate cohort, while having a strong negative predictive value for classifying nodules likely to harbor ATA low‐risk disease. This study represents an important step toward precision oncology, shifting the paradigm from size‐based algorithms toward individualized, biology‐driven care.

Molecular testing is primarily used for risk stratifying ITN and may also provide valuable prognostic information, informing the appropriate extent of initial thyroid surgery in nodules with Bethesda III to VI cytology. 20 , 21 However, there is wide variability in disease presentation as a function of genomic features. 19 , 22 , 23 , 24 For example, a BRAF V600E mutated papillary thyroid cancer can present as an intrathyroidal microcarcinoma or widely metastatic stage IV cancer. RAS mutated nodules may have final histology of benign, NIFTP, low‐risk, or high‐risk malignancy which is captured in our POINT‐low and ‐intermediate groups which vary by RAS mutant status and NIFTP frequency. 19

Prior work leveraging molecular data for risk‐stratification revealed significant differences from the ATA risk strata. One study identified prognostic molecular markers in a cohort of patients undergoing thyroidectomy for thyroid carcinoma identifying 3 groups: low‐risk (RAS or RAS‐like alterations), intermediate‐risk (BRAF V600E or BRAF‐like mutations), and high‐risk (eg, TERT, TP53, and PIK3CA). 25 Using this molecular risk‐stratification, 8% of the molecular low‐risk group were ATA intermediate‐ or high‐risk on final pathology. Of the molecular intermediate‐risk group, 37% were ATA‐intermediate risk, 55% were ATA low‐risk while 8% were ATA high‐risk. Of the molecular high‐risk group, over 50% were ATA low or intermediate risk. Our classifier performed similar to this mutation‐driven classifier, although the POINT‐intermediate group in the surgical cohort contained no BRAF V600E mutations, instead enriched for RAS mutations. In contrast, our POINT‐high group in the surgical cohort was enriched in BRAF V600E mutations (53.2%) suggesting heterogeneity in the prognostic utility of genomic variants. These data, in particular, illustrate the context‐dependence of BRAF V600E as a prognostic tool in preoperative risk assessment. Our findings also underscore the variability of pathology in RAS variants ranging from NIFTP to aggressive follicular thyroid carcinomas.

The ATA risk of recurrence system serves to stratify patients based on both clinical and histopathologic features in order to guide decisions about completion thyroidectomy, adjuvant treatment, and surveillance. 26 There are some limitations to this system. Given its reliance on histopathologic features, it can only be employed postoperatively. Furthermore, in our analysis of patients in the TCGA cohort, only 5% of the patients were considered “high‐risk.” Incorporating mRNA expression data suggested that 26.7% of the cohort could be reclassified as “high‐risk,” based upon the PFS across the new risk groups. Additionally, among patients categorized as ATA‐intermediate risk, we found notable survival differences between CTR‐H and CTR‐I cohorts, further suggesting that integrating expression and histology data may improve risk‐stratification for patients with PTC.

While the presence of BRAF V600E mutations in cytologic samples is strongly predictive of thyroid carcinoma, its prognostic implications in known PTC remain controversial. BRAF V600E has demonstrated an association with increased risk of regional metastasis, extra‐thyroidal extension, and increased risk of recurrence. 24 Other work suggests that tumors lacking this mutation have higher rates of distant metastases but otherwise comparable clinical features. 22 Clinical and pathologic factors, including male gender, primary tumor size and nodal disease burden better predicted recurrence than BRAF V600E mutations in one study. 23 Surprisingly, our work revealed two clinically and molecularly distinct groups of patients with BRAF V600E‐mutated tumors, one with a low‐risk expression profile and one with a high‐risk expression profile and enrichment for TERT promotor mutations. Interestingly, BRAF mutations were not enriched in the POINT‐L or ‐I groups in the surgically‐treated cohort which likely reflects clinical and molecular heterogeneity in the three cohorts tested in this study.

The distribution of ALK, NTRK, and RET fusions across the POINT‐clustered groups raises questions regarding the biological significance of these fusions. The significance of the relatively higher frequency of fusions in the POINT‐low group in the GC cohort is unclear. One possibility is that there is underlying biological and prognostic variability in the specific fusions. For example, NCOA‐RET fusions are associated with more aggressive clinical and pathologic features (eg, solid variant thyroid carcinoma, lymph node metastasis). 27 While a deeper analysis of the genomic variants associated with the POINT clusters is beyond the scope of this study, these findings warrant further investigation at a more granular level. Future studies encompassing a comprehensive analysis of epigenetic, copy number variant, somatic nucleotide variant, gene fusion, proteomic, and tumor microenvironment features may further empower precision approaches.

There are several limitations to this work that must be acknowledged. The most significant limitation is the lack of PFS data in the validation cohorts. While the POINT classifier in the GC cohort appears to stratify by genomic features identified in the CTR groups, future work will confirm whether the gene classifier used in our study has prognostic significance when applied prospectively to preoperative FNA samples. We do not have information on the potential selection bias of samples that were sent for testing in the GC cohort, especially considering, all samples were Bethesda V or VI on FNA. Additionally, our GC cohort lacks clinical annotation such as patient risk factors, family history, or previous exposures or treatment that may be related to molecular profiles. Therefore, it cannot be generalized to some populations such as those with high prevalences of radiation‐associated thyroid cancer. This would be an exciting direction for future exploration. The analysis of the POINT classifier in the surgical cohort demonstrates agreement between POINT and the gold‐standard pathologic risk stratification in a real‐world cohort supporting its potential for preoperative risk assessment and thyroid‐sparing surgical decision‐making. However, the NPV and PPV will vary with prevalence which depends on geographic region, socioeconomic, and epidemiologic risk variables. Prospective studies will be necessary to examine if the POINT classifier can predict final thyroid histopathologic risk across diverse cohorts and inform the initial extent of surgery to avoid completion thyroidectomy when appropriate.

Conclusion

Integration of prognostic gene expression profiles with ATA risk of recurrence stratification produced novel prognostic clustering resulting in a 144 gene classifier. POINT stratified FNA samples into novel molecular risk groups and surgical samples by pathologic risk groups. These data provide a starting point for implementing molecular studies guiding precision surgical planning. Implementing the POINT signature in the management of cN0 DTCs <4 cm could initially guide selection of patients for lobectomy, potentially decreasing the need for completion thyroidectomy and improving surgical outcomes (ie, given decreased completion thyroidectomies). Prospective studies are needed to further validate the clinical utility of this gene classifier in heterogenous populations with variable thyroid cancer prevalence and clinical features.
