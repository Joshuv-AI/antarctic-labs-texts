Relationships between burnout and coping strategies among assistant medical officers in Malaysian public healthcare facilities
Authors: Nor Haniza Zakaria, Nursyahda Zakaria, Kun Yun Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAssistant Medical Officers (AMOs) in Malaysia, similar to physician assistants elsewhere, are predisposed to burnout due to stressors as healthcare frontliners. This study determined the prevalence, risk factors, and coping strategies of burnout among AMOs in Malaysia. A nationwide cross-sectional study was conducted between August and November 2019, involving 2,033 AMOs from 32 public hospitals and 28 district health offices recruited via multi-stage stratified sampling. Burnout was assessed using MBI-HSS (MP) while coping strategies were evaluated using Brief COPE. Complex sampling analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Version 26. Among 1,839 respondents, one-third (n = 584, 30.3%) experienced burnout. AMOs who were younger [AOR = 4.69 (95% CI: 1.14, 19.22)], having comorbidities [AOR = 2.46 (95% CI: 1.09, 5.51)], and with higher education [AOR = 2.65 (95% CI: 1.26, 5.55)] were associated with higher risks of burnout. Conversely, hospital AMOs had 27% lower odds of experiencing burnout than clinic AMOs [AOR = 0.73 (95% CI: 0.55, 0.98)]. Problem-focused coping strategies were protective against two burnout domains: high depersonalisation [r (1839) = 0.10, p < 0.001] and low personal accomplishment [ r (1839) = 0.30, p < 0.001]. In contrast, the use of emotion-focused and dysfunctional coping strategies was detrimental in view of their significant correlations with burnout and its domains. Regular burnout surveillance is crucial for identifying at-risk AMOs and providing timely support to mitigate burnout and promote well-being. By implementing proactive organisational initiatives, AMOs can be empowered with the knowledge of diverse coping mechanisms and application of effective coping strategies, thereby preventing burnout. Introduction

Herbert Freudenberger, an American psychologist, first conceived the concept of “burnout” in the early 1970s1. Later, Christina Maslach expanded on the principle of burnout and established the Maslach Burnout Inventory (MBI). It became one of the most widely used questionnaires in the evaluation of burnout. MBI measures three aspects of burnout, namely emotional exhaustion (EE), depersonalisation (DP), and personal achievement (PA)2. In 2019, burnout was officially defined as an occupational phenomenon caused by poorly-managed and prolonged workplace stress under the 11th version of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11)3. In response to chronic work stress, a person suffering from burnout is likely to experience EE (high levels of emotional and physical overextension), DP (a cynical, distant, and callous work attitude), and low PA (a lower sense of professional efficacy)4. Healthcare workers (HCWs) are among the occupational groups most vulnerable to burnout5 due to the professional dedication required of their work that predisposes them to exhaustion. The severity of these impacts may be linked to their coping responses to stressors6,7. Coping is defined as “thoughts and behaviours that people use to manage the internal and external demands of stressful situations”8. A recent meta-analysis found that certain coping strategies reduce burnout in nurses across EE, DP, and low PA9, with improvements in EE and DP lasting up to one year. As for the different types of coping mechanisms, emotion-focused coping (a passive approach to emotional regulation) correlated positively with EE and DP, while problem-focused coping, aiming at actively addressing issues, was more effective in enhancing PA10. Nevertheless, certain emotion-focused strategies, such as religious coping, have been found protective against burnout11. The effects of different coping mechanisms on burnout management can vary depending on the individuals and their surrounding environments.

In Malaysia, the Ministry of Health (MOH) is the primary provider of public healthcare services, operating 148 public hospitals and over 3,000 health clinics nationwide9. Assistant Medical Officers (AMOs) are one of the earliest groups of HCWs in Malaysia. There are some similarities in the job scopes with physician assistants in the United States12, feldshers in Russia13, or practicante in Spanish colonies such as Puerto Rico14. The AMO model was introduced by the British in their former colonies, such as Malaysia and Sri Lanka15. They were originally known as the “Apothecaries” in British Malaya in the 18th century, and the profession has since evolved into Hospital Assistants in 1963, Medical Assistants in 1985, and subsequently, AMOs in 200716. AMOs played an essential role in delivering health services to rural and estate communities in the past. Now, they are trained to perform basic diagnostic, curative, prescriptive, and operative procedures to assist doctors in minor ailments and emergency care in pre-hospital settings and specialty units. Similar to their counterparts in other countries, AMOs have been instrumental in task shifting/sharing with doctors as they provide vital medical and surgical services that can prevent unnecessary referrals, reduce delay in care, and ultimately decrease healthcare costs17.

According to Malaysian Health Facts, AMOs were the third largest group of HCWs in Malaysia, after doctors and nurses18. As the primary gatekeepers in MOH hospitals and clinics, AMOs are the first line of contact with the public, hence exposing them to multiple stressors that can lead to burnout. Due to the lack of research among AMOs in Malaysia and worldwide, their mental well-being remains poorly reported. Most of the burnout-related studies in Malaysia were single-centred, hospital-based, or mainly focused on medical doctors19,20 and nurses21,22. While burnout has always been a pre-existing concern among HCWs, unprecedented demands, increased exposure risk, and emotional distress during and after the COVID-19 pandemic have dramatically heightened the prevalence and intensity of burnout, with local and international studies reporting high prevalence, particularly among doctors and nurses23,24. Therefore, this study aimed to bridge the gap by assessing burnout prevalence and identifying associated risk factors among AMOs in public healthcare facilities in Malaysia, as well as evaluating coping strategies among AMOs in relation to the development of burnout. By identifying the at-risk population and modifiable stressors, the study findings can guide the implementation of targeted burnout mitigation measures to ensure that AMOs can function optimally in a healthy working environment to provide quality care to patients.

Methods

Study design

A nationwide cross-sectional study was conducted from August to November 2019 among AMOs working in hospital and primary care facilities under Malaysia’s MOH.

Participants and sample size

A nationally representative sample of AMOs was selected using multistage stratified random sampling. Those who were on prolonged leave or with underlying psychological illnesses were excluded. The sample size needed to conduct a single data analysis was calculated using a 28.0% estimated prevalence of burnout25, a design effect of 2.5, and a non-response rate of 20% was 968. Nevertheless, as there were two main categories of AMOs, the sample size was doubled to 1,936. Based on the workforce distribution data by the MOH AMO Board26, the ratio of AMOs working in hospital and primary care settings was 55% and 45% respectively. Based on the proportion, the required AMO sample size from hospitals was 1,065, while the sample size of AMOs from primary care settings was increased to meet the minimum sample size of 968, giving a total sample size of 2,033.

Following that, a two-stage stratified cluster sampling was used to select one state from each of the six zones in Malaysia (primary stratum), followed by the secondary stratum, which consisted of 32 hospitals and 28 district health offices chosen at random from the six states. The sample size of the AMOs to be recruited from each state was determined in proportion to the population size of AMOs working in each state. Lastly, the study respondents were invited at random from a list provided by the liaison officer at each facility.

Measures

A self-administered questionnaire in dual languages of English and Malay (the national language of Malaysia) was distributed. It contained three sections, namely Maslach Burnout Inventory-Human Services Survey for Medical Personnel [MBI-HSS (MP)], Brief version of the Coping Orientation to Problems Experienced Inventory (Brief-COPE), as well as baseline sociodemographic and professional characteristics.

In the first section, burnout among AMOs was assessed using MBI-HSS (MP), a validated instrument developed by Christina Maslach, a psychologist, in 1981 27. The instrument comprises 22 items designed to assess three domains of burnout: EE (9 items), DP (5 items), and PA (8 items). Responses were rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (“never”) to 6 (“every day”). The detailed description of each domain can be found in Table 1. The sum of scores for each dimension determines the presence or absence of each burnout domain, based on the following cut-off points: High EE (≥ 27 points); high DP (≥ 10 points); and low PA (≤ 31 points). Those who scored higher in EE, DP, or both EE and DP were considered to have burnout27. The Malay version of MBI-HSS (MP) reliability during development was Cronbach’s α = 0.94 (ref), and in this research, it was α = 0.79.

The second section of the questionnaire contained Brief-COPE to evaluate various coping mechanisms among AMOs. The Brief-COPE28 contains 28 items on 14 subscales (two items each). Initially, Folkman and Lazarus categorised coping mechanisms under the 14 subscales as either problem-focused (PF) or emotion-focused (EF) strategies. PF coping entails strategies aimed at directly addressing and resolving the issue, typically through the development and assessment of potential solutions. Conversely, EF coping focuses on managing the emotional response to the problem, seeking to reduce the psychological discomfort associated with it29. In 2000, Coolidge et al. further grouped the original subscales into three categories: EF coping (emotional support, positive reframing, acceptance, religion, and humour), PF coping (active coping, planning, and instrumental support), and Dysfunctional (DF) coping (venting, denial, substance use, behavioural disengagement, self-distraction, and self-blame)30. Since then, most studies have reported their findings based on these three categories31,32. The Malay version of Brief-COPE showed fairly good reliability and validity, with a total Cronbach’s alpha of 0.83 in a previous study33; while in this research, it was α = 0.839. The total scores of the items in all subscales range differently (6–24 for PF coping, 10–40 for EF, and 12–48 for DF). The mean score was calculated for each subscale and domain using averaging domain means (Equal Domain Weighting)34. The final section of the questionnaire captured baseline characteristics such as age, marital status, number of children, household income, comorbidities, and smoking status of the AMOs. Monthly household income was categorised according to the national median income level; i.e. B40 [(Bottom 40%: less than $1,031 (RM 4,360)], M40 [Middle 40%: $1031–2274 (RM 4,361-9,619)], and T20 [(Top 20%: more than $2274 (RM 9,619)] based on the 2019 Household Income and Basic Amenities Survey35. Other characteristics surveyed included education level, years of service, level of healthcare facilities, time spent on clinical activities, sleeping hours, and travelling time to the workplace. Education level refers to the highest level of training received in an institution that provides formal training to become AMOs. The minimum qualification of AMOs in Malaysia is a Diploma of Medical Assistant. Years of service is defined as the number of years an AMO has worked in MOH, while time spent on clinical activities refers to the duration of working hours dedicated to activities related to direct patient care. Sleeping hours are defined according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) and Sleep Research Society (SRS), whereby average sleep hours are deemed as more than seven hours per night36. These variables were selected because of their known associations with burnout among healthcare workers in the relevant literature37.

Data collection

The investigators visited all the selected health facilities for face-to-face data collection. Participants were briefed on the study objectives and assured that their participation was voluntary. Strict confidentiality was ensured, and no personal identifiers were included in the questionnaire. Participants provided written informed consent prior to completing the self-administered questionnaire. The completed questionnaires were collected by the investigators during the same session.

Data analysis

Data entry and data analysis were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Science (SPSS V.26). Descriptive statistics were used to calculate the overall prevalence of burnout and its domains (EE, DP, and PA), as well as the preferred coping strategies among AMOs. Logistic regression analyses were conducted to determine the association of burnout with sociodemographic and professional characteristics. Variables with a p-value of < 0.25 in the univariable analysis were included in the multivariable logistic regression. The variables included were age (years), gender, marital status, number of children, household income, smoking, comorbid, educational level, years of service, level of healthcare facilities, time spent on clinical activities, and sleeping hours. Pearson’s correlation was used to determine the correlation between coping strategies and burnout. All analyses were conducted using a complex sampling design to ensure that sample weights of the multistage stratified sampling were taken into consideration. Descriptive complex sampling analysis was used to determine the mean of coping strategies.

Ethics declarations

This study involved human participants and was approved by the Medical Research and Ethics Committee of the Ministry of Health Malaysia (NMRR ID-23-03199-3VQ). All participants provided informed consent to participate in the study before answering the questionnaire. This research was also conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the total 2,033 AMOs, 1,846 respondents consented and answered the questionnaire. After excluding incomplete responses, the final analysis included 1,839 respondents (response rate of 90.5%). The majority of the AMOs were males (88.7%), 21–30 years old (39.5%), married (79.9%), and with 1–3 children (51.6%). More than two-thirds of the respondents had only seven hours or less of sleep daily (86.7%). Nearly half of them have worked in the MOH for more than ten years (46.6%) (Table 3).

Prevalence of burnout and associated factors

Table 4 shows the prevalence of burnout among AMOs based on their baseline characteristics. Overall, approximately one-third of the AMOs experienced burnout (30.3%; 95% CI: 24.3, 37.0), with the highest prevalence reported in the domain of low PA (43.1%), followed by high EE (28.4%) and high DP (12.0%). Burnout was more common among AMOs who were younger (39.6; 95% CI: 32.5, 47.2), single (35.3; 95% CI: 27.9, 43.4), and from the B40 lower income group (36.1; 95% CI: 30.9, 41.7). Furthermore, AMOs who were smokers or had comorbidities also reported higher levels of burnout. Sleep deprivation was also associated with a higher level of burnout (32.0; 95% CI: 25.3, 39.5).

In the univariate analysis, AMOs who were younger, with fewer children, fewer years of service, and sleep deprivation were more likely to develop burnout (Table 5). Furthermore, having comorbidities led to a 2.5 times significantly higher risk of burnout (95% CI: 1.09, 5.51; p < 0.05). In addition, AMOs with higher education levels reported significantly higher odds of burnout by 2.6 times (95% CI: 1.26,5.55; p < 0.05). On the other hand, AMOs working in hospitals reported reduced risk of burnout (Table 6).

Discussion

Burnout among HCWs represents one of the most detrimental occupational health hazards as it can affect individual wellbeing and productivity, subsequently compromising healthcare delivery and patient care. This study assessed the prevalence of burnout among AMOs in Malaysia. AMOs account for one-third of the HCWs in the MOH. Our study showed that approximately one in three AMOs experienced burnout symptoms. This national prevalence was similar to a recent study among physician assistants (35.0%) in the US38 and among the HCWs (35.4%) in Taiwan39 post-COVID-19. In Malaysia, the prevalence of burnout among AMOs was also much higher than among nurses (24.4%)40 and doctors (26.5%)41 pre COVID-19 pandemic. A recent systematic review revealed that the prevalence of burnout among critical HCWs varied between 16.0% and 80.0% pre-COVID-19 pandemic to 24.3%-82.1% in the post-pandemic period39. Even though the MBI was the most common tool used for assessment, studies in the review followed different definitions of burnout, which can contribute to the wide variation in the burnout prevalence observed42.

Regarding the three domains of burnout, a notable proportion of AMOs in this study reported low PA (43.5%) and high EE (28.4%), while only a smaller proportion had high DP (12.0%). Similar findings were reported among nurses in Malaysia (low PA: 41.6%, high EE: 23.9%, high DP: 4.5%)40 and HCWs in China (low PA: 41.4%, high EE: 33.1%, high DP: 8.8%)43. Higher education levels are frequently associated with a stronger sense of PA. Literature has found that HCW from other countries with degrees or masters were more likely to have high PA39. From this, it is appropriate to infer that the higher prevalence of low PA in our findings might be related to the fact that the majority (91.5%) of the AMOs attained only a diploma qualification.

In terms of age and seniority, younger AMOs reported a higher level of burnout, similar to previous studies39,44. Burnout is particularly common among young professionals in high-risk and client-centred sectors, e.g. healthcare sector45. However, as they become more self-confident with age and seniority, the likelihood of burnout decreases with the more practical experience and adaptable methods they learn in dealing with organisational pressures44. Similar to Crowe et al. (2018) whereby paramedics who possessed bachelor’s degrees were 2.5 times more likely to report burnout46, our AMOs with higher education levels also experienced more burnout than their counterparts with lower education levels. The same scenario was observed among nurses47,48. Due to their higher education qualifications, they are more likely to be assigned more complex tasks. The expectation towards the quality of work they produced is also higher.

Another significant finding from this study was the association between sleep deprivation and burnout. AMOs who were sleep deprived (less than seven hours of sleep daily)49 recorded two times more burnout, consistent with previous studies among other HCWs50,51. Sleep is critical to ensure sufficient rest time for emotional regulation and psychological health. Regular sleep deprivation can cause negative health consequences, including obesity, diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular diseases, and depression, besides being linked with impaired performance and higher adverse events52. Hence, AMOs should receive sleep hygiene as part of burnout prevention.

When comparing the level of healthcare facilities they work in, primary care AMOs reported a higher level of burnout than hospital-based AMOs. The difference may be attributed to underlying differences in their job scopes and work settings. Primary care AMOs are tasked with delivering continuous, community-based services ranging from curative, preventive, promotive, to rehabilitative care, often to a large general population of patients over extended periods. These demands, coupled with less task specialisation and limited structural support, may heighten susceptibility to burnout. Conversely, hospital AMOs typically operate in more specialised and procedure-oriented roles, such as emergency departments or specific clinical units, where care tends to be short-term with clearer role delineation, factors known to reduce burnout risk53.

In the field of burnout research, coping mechanisms are integral in the prevention and management of stressors. Effective coping mechanisms are crucial to mitigating these effects and preventing burnout. Generally, our study found that EF coping strategies are more preferred by AMOs compared to PF and DF strategies. More importantly, AMOs with burnout were found to rely more substantially on DF and EF coping. This vital finding must be taken into consideration by stakeholders involved in the planning and implementation of burnout prevention and mental health wellness programs. Among all the coping strategies, religion (EF), positive reframing (EF), and self-distraction (DF) were the most used strategies among AMOs in this study. Similar findings were reported by studies in Indonesia54 and Pakistan55. In our study, religion was the highest-ranked coping strategy whereby AMOs relied on religious beliefs as support in navigating challenges and overcoming stress at the workplace. A systematic review also reported that recitation of religious verses from the Quran (for Muslims) and the Bible (for Christians) came across as an effective approach to mitigate stress, anxiety, and other adverse situations during the COVID-19 pandemic. Prayer may provide the necessary context, social connection, and inner control, especially in times of hardship, empowering individuals to cope with stressors more effectively56. Apart from mental wellbeing, religious belief is also beneficial for physical health57. Past research has shown that religiosity can influence an individual’s understanding and acceptance of stressful situations, subsequently lowering cortisol levels58, a biomarker of stress59.

Additionally, positive reframing emerged as the second most practised coping strategy among AMOs. It is an active cognitive method used to reduce the negative perception of stressful demands by refocusing on the positive aspects of the problem to mitigate burnout development60. Positive reframing has been integrated into various interventions such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)60. On the other hand, self-distraction was also commonly applied among AMOs in this study. Similar to a study among emergency medical services personnel in Poland61, they attempt to distract themselves and take their minds off the problem by focusing on other activities. However, this would just be a reprieve to avoid confrontation with difficult situations, hence a less-than-ideal long-term solution to prevent burnout development62.

As overall burnout is often the final stage of manifestation, it is vital to identify AMOs with high levels of each subdomain of BO, i.e., EE, DP, and low PA, so that they can be provided with the necessary support at the earliest possible stage. To effectively tackle the burnout epidemic among HCWs, such as AMOs, timely intervention should be made available by empowering them with coping mechanisms that are beneficial, while at the same time educating them about detrimental strategies. In our study, AMOs who applied PF coping strategies were protected against high DP and low PA, two of the burnout domains, whereas EF and DF coping strategies led to high overall burnout. This is concurred by Rodriguez et al. (2019), in which more frequent uses of EF coping strategies led to a higher incidence of burnout and post-traumatic stress disorder among paediatric ward HCWs64. Another study by Menaldi et al. (2023) also echoed our study findings whereby a higher sense of PA is linked to the application of PF and EF strategies31. By actively focusing on the underlying stressors and causes of burnout, actions that are taken to address distressing situations and planning for contingency can notably improve the situation63,64. Such goal setting and solutions-focused strategies are efficient means of managing stress and burnout.

Implications

Overall, there are some important implications from this study. The high burnout prevalence among AMOs in Malaysia is likely a microcosm of international trends among HCWs, in view of the evolving healthcare landscapes such as longer working hours, higher patient demands, and heavier administrative burdens. By identifying AMOs who are predisposed to burnout, early strategies can be undertaken to empower them with healthy coping mechanisms. In the early phase of stress, mechanisms that focus on emotion regulation, such as EF coping strategies, can prevent the level of discomfort from becoming too overpowering. PF coping techniques have the additional benefit of enabling appropriate actions to be taken during the later phases once the initial distress has decreased. The promotion of a healthy lifestyle, including sleep hygiene, regular exercise, and other self-care measures, can also prevent burnout. Therefore, regular burnout surveillance using simple tools to identify high-risk personnel is vital to provide support and training in terms of psychological capital and coping mechanisms. These are strategies that can be universally applied to the general population of AMOs and their equivalent counterparts in other countries.

On the contrary, despite these common challenges across healthcare systems worldwide, our Malaysian context may be mired with specific challenges such as varying levels of professional autonomy for patients, staff maldistribution, and cultural expectations revolving around patient care. While our paper focused mainly on individual-level factors, burnout among HCWs can be best tackled by striking a balance between the perception of personal vulnerability and the support in the workplace and beyond. Therefore, future research can be expanded to assess any organisational-level factors in the local setting that should be prioritised in burnout mitigation programmes, with the ultimate aim of inculcating resilience within the healthcare workforce and cultivating a supportive and respectful working environment.

Limitations

AMO is a distinctive group of HCWs. However, there is a paucity of research among them. The findings of this large sample national-level study, the first of its kind in Malaysia, serve as important baseline data for stakeholders to guide public health planning, monitoring, and evaluation of AMOs’ wellbeing. While this cross-sectional study was completed within a short period using fewer resources than a follow-up cohort study, the causal association between the exposure and outcome could not be established. Furthermore, the use of self-administered questionnaires could also lead to potential recall bias and social desirability bias.

Conclusion

One in three AMOs in Malaysia suffered from burnout, especially those who were younger or with comorbidities. Monitoring HCWs mental health is essential, as they are at the forefront of the health system. Burnout must be tackled through early interventions among high-risk groups. Self-care strategies such as resilience and healthy coping skills must be instilled to mitigate burnout among AMOs and maintain high-quality healthcare delivery.
