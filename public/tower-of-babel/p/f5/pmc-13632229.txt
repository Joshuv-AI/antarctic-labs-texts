Evidence of Anopheles stephensi involvement in the transmission of Plasmodium vivax in Djibouti City using highly sensitive sporozoite detection assay
Authors: Subhika Rao, Jeanne N Samake, Cristina Rafferty, Peter Mumba, Sheleme Chibsa, Meshesha Balkew, Bouh Abdi Khaireh, Samatar Kayad Guelleh, Mohamed Mousse Ibrahim, Abdoulilah Ahmed Abdi, Sarah Zohdy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveAnopheles stephensi is a malaria mosquito vector that has been raising international concern due to its invasive nature in Africa, including the nation of Djibouti. Since its initial detection in Djibouti in 2012, malaria morbidity and mortality have increased exponentially in the country. With the observed increases in human malaria cases and previously reported An. stephensi involvement in outbreaks, high-quality evidence of Plasmodium sporozoite-carrying An. stephensi is essential to confirm and monitor the role of the invasive vector in malaria transmission dynamics in Djibouti. This study aims to detect Plasmodium sporozoites in An. stephensi collected in Djibouti using a multiplex circumsporozoite ELISA bead assay and assess the genetic relatedness of these populations to An. stephensi across the Horn of Africa. MethodsOne hundred and ninety-six wild caught adult An. stephensi mosquitoes from Djibouti City were collected, morphologically identified, and molecularly confirmed using a portion of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 marker. All samples were then tested for infective sporozoites Plasmodium vivax 210, P. vivax 247 and P. falciparum using a multiplex circumsporozoite enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay bead assay. One hundred and fourteen COI sequences that passed further sequence analysis quality control for comparative population genetic analysis were used to characterize the genetic diversity of the sampled An. stephensi and its genetic relatedness to other An. stephensi populations across the Horn of Africa. ResultsAll 196 samples were morphologically and molecularly confirmed to be An. stephensi. Plasmodium vivax 210 sporozoites were detected in two specimens with a positivity rate of 1.0%. No Pv247 or Pf sporozoites were detected. An analysis of the COI region showed that the Pv210-infected An. stephensi belonged to the dominant invasive haplotypes circulating in the Horn of Africa.ConclusionsThe findings from this study confirm the involvement of An. stephensi in P. vivax transmission in Djibouti and the genetic relatedness of Djiboutian An. stephensi populations to other populations across the Horn of Africa. This highlights the threat of An. stephensi invasion and supports a rapid and comprehensive response, particularly through surveillance and control. Background

Malaria presents a significant burden to health systems globally, with 263 million cases and over 597,000 deaths reported in 2023, 94% of which occurs in sub-Saharan Africa [1]. Though there are malaria control programs in Djibouti, achieving control and elimination has become increasingly challenging due to emerging threats such as insecticide resistance and invasive malaria vectors like Anopheles stephensi.

Native to South Asia and parts of the Middle East, and capable of transmitting both Plasmodium falciparum and P. vivax, An. stephensi was first detected in Djibouti in 2012 [2]. In subsequent years it was detected in multiple African countries, including Ethiopia, Sudan, Somalia, Nigeria, Ghana, Kenya, Eritrea, and Niger [3].

In Djibouti, since the first detection of An. stephensi in 2012, malaria cases have increased more than 20-fold, from 1,684 cases in 2013 to 38,944 cases in 2023 [1]. The unprecedented increase includes both P. falciparum and P. vivax malaria cases [1]. This is highly concerning given that Djibouti was on the verge of reaching the pre-elimination phase of malaria before 2012 [4]. Also, unlike native African malaria vectors, An. stephensi persists year-round due to its ability to breed in artificial containers in both urban and peri-urban settings [5–7]. Indeed, An. stephensi has surpassed the native African malaria vector Anopheles arabiensis to become the primary vector in Djibouti City [7, 8]. It was found implicated in a resurgence of malaria cases in Djibouti City in 2014 [2] and 2019 [9] through positive infectivity for both P. falciparum and P. vivax based on a qualitative dipstick assay [10]. Anopheles stephensi was also suspected in an outbreak among French military personnel in Djibouti City in 2019 [7].

However, the first direct link of the invasive An. stephensi involvement in malaria transmission was reported during a malaria outbreak in an urban setting and during the dry season in the neighboring country, Ethiopia, in 2022 [11]. A multiplex bead assay of the circumsporozoite enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (csELISA) [12] was used to molecularly confirm the involvement of An. stephensi in malaria transmission in Ethiopia. This multiplex bead csELISA assay is the most sensitive and specific Plasmodium sporozoite detection assay, based on the gold-standard plate-based csELISA. The multiplex bead csELISA assay has not been utilized in An. stephensi surveillance in Djibouti. Prior Plasmodium sporozoite detections were conducted in 2014 [2] and 2019 [9] using a qualitative dipstick assay [10]. Rapid assays such as dipstick assays are beneficial for on-the-go testing in the field [13]; however, they are less sensitive and specific [14] than molecular assays such as csELISA and bead assay-based csELISA [12]. With the ongoing increase of malaria cases in Djibouti [2, 9], and the risk of malaria An. stephensi presents to an additional 125 million people in Africa [15], it is important to continue Plasmodium sporozoite testing of wild caught populations to better understand transmission dynamics and mitigate and control the spread of this invasive vector.

Thus, in this study, we aim to (1) use the csELISA molecular approach to confirm that An. stephensi is linked to the ongoing malaria outbreak in Djibouti City, and (2) determine its relatedness to other An. stephensi in the Horn of Africa. Defining the ongoing role An. stephensi plays in the high rates of malaria transmission in the country will inform targeted vector surveillance and control approaches of this invasive malaria species in Djibouti and the invaded region in Africa.

Methods

Adult Anopheles mosquitoes were collected through backpack aspiration at the Djibouti Nagad Railway Station, a train service connecting Djibouti to Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, during the dry season in February 2024 (Additional file 1: Table S1). Collections were conducted following a report of an An. stephensi-mediated malaria outbreak during a dry season in Dire Dawa, Ethiopia [11]. Other sites included routine surveillance at fixed sentinel sites in Djibouti City (Additional file 1: Table S1), the capital city of Djibouti, where over 90% of malaria cases have occurred in recent years [16]. All 196 samples collected were morphologically identified and molecularly confirmed using a portion of the cytochrome c oxidase subunit 1 (COI) marker using the Folmer COI primer set LCO1490F (5ʹ-GGTCAACAAATCATAAAGATATTGG-3ʹ) and HCO2198R (5ʹ-TAAACTTCAGGGTGACCAAAAAATCA-3ʹ) yielding a PCR product of 750 base pairs following previously described protocols [17, 18] (Additional file 1: Text S1). All 196 samples were tested for Plasmodium sporozoites [11, 12] (Additional file 1: Text S2). Briefly, mosquito heads–thoraces were separated from legs, wings, and abdomens between the second and third legs from all 196 samples, homogenized and tested for P. falciparum (Pf), P. vivax210 (Pv210), and P. vivax247 (Pv247) following the csELISA protocol [12] (Additional file 1: Text S2). All positive detections were further confirmed by a second boil of the homogenate which was then re-tested for Pf, Pv210, and Pv247 following the same csELISA protocol.

Furthermore, the COI DNA sequences were analyzed to generate population genetic statistics and conduct a phylogenetic analysis to further confirm molecular species identification and determine the level of genetic diversity of the sampled An. stephensi, and relatedness to other An. stephensi populations in the Horn of Africa (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). Briefly, COI sequences were first cleaned using CodonCode version 11.0.2 (CodonCode Corporation, Centerville, MA). Beyond the initial cleaning, 114 out of 196 COI sequences displayed high-quality alignment with previously identified An. stephensi haplotypes and were thus appropriate for further comparative analysis between the studied samples and An. stephensi populations in the Horn of Africa [5, 19–22]. The extra sequence quality control is important as high-quality DNA sequences are essential for accurate comparative population genetic analysis. Population genetics statistics were generated in DNAsp version 6 [23], including the number of polymorphic (segregating) sites (s), number of haplotypes (h), haplotype diversity (Hd), and nucleotide diversity (π). Next, the COI sequences were further aligned using CodonCode version 11.0.2 (CodonCode Corporation, Centerville, MA) with previously published An. stephensi COI sequences from the Horn of Africa retrieved from Genbank. Phylogenetic relationships were inferred using a maximum likelihood approach with RAxML GUI using the GTR model of nucleotide substitutions, gamma model for rate of heterogeneity (GTRGAMMA), and one thousand replicates in one run for bootstrap analysis [24]. Anopheles maculatus was designated as an outgroup to be consistent with previous An. stephensi phylogenetic analysis [5, 25]. The tree with the highest log likelihood was visualized and formatted using FigTree v1.44 [26]. Lastly, a Horn of Africa map showing the distribution of An. stephensi most prevalent COI haplotypes (i.e., Hap 2, and Hap 3) was generated including data from Carter et al. [19], Samake et al. [5], Hawaria et al. [21] (Ethiopia), Ali et al. [20] (Somalia), and Samake et al. [22] (Kenya).

Results and discussion

Using the NCBI BLAST tool, 196 COI sequences matched with 100% percent identity to An. stephensi. Two out of the 196 samples (1.0%) tested positive for the presence of the Pv210 circumsporozoite protein, which indicates the presence of malaria in Djiboutian An. stephensi (Table 1). Zero out of the 196 samples tested positive for the presence of the Pv247 or Pf circumsporozoite proteins. The genetic diversity statistics based on 114 out of the 196 generated COI DNA sequences revealed two segregating sites leading to two haplotypes (Table 2). Based on the phylogenetic analysis, the two haplotypes clustered with previously reported An. stephensi COI haplotypes in Djibouti, namely, haplotype 2 (n = 99) and haplotype 3 (n = 15) [27] (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). These two haplotypes were reported as the most widespread COI haplotypes of the invasive An. stephensi in Djibouti and across the Horn of Africa [19, 27] (Fig. 1).

The detection of Pv210 circumsporozoite protein using the most advanced csELISA approach in a Djiboutian An. stephensi population provides highly sensitive and specific molecular evidence for the direct link between An. stephensi and malaria transmission in Djibouti. Our finding supports the previous report of P. vivax detection in An. stephensi in Djibouti City seven years prior [9] and suggests that An. stephensi is continuing to play a role in increasingly high incidence rates of P. vivax malaria observed in Djibouti in addition to P. falciparum malaria [1, 28] (Fig. 2). Indeed, P. vivax makes up an average of 28% of malaria cases in Djibouti from 2016–2023 (Fig. 2). Though less prevalent than P. falciparum, P. vivax constitutes a significant burden on health systems [29]. Based on our findings that invasive An. stephensi is contributing to P. vivax transmission in Djibouti, it is important to optimize vector control measures to fit the Plasmodium species profile of Djibouti and prevent malaria cases overall. Though P. falciparum was not detected, which does not reflect the relative cases of P. vivax to P. falciparum in Djibouti (Fig. 2), it is likely a facet of the utilization of a relatively small (n = 196) sample size. Additionally, the fact that these Plasmodium-carrying An. stephensi were also observed to be closely related to An. stephensi populations across the Horn of Africa suggests that the invasive mosquito is possibly contributing to the transmission of malaria in other invaded areas across the region as well (Fig. 1).

Taken together, our findings fill critical gaps by (1) providing information on the detection of circumsporozoite-positive An. stephensi in Djibouti given that the last detection occurred seven years ago [9]; (2) providing the first molecularly confirmed circumsporozoite-positive An. stephensi using the innovative multiplex bead-based csELISA approach [12]; and (3) characterizing the genetic diversity of a recent An. stephensi population in Djibouti and its relatedness to other An. stephensi populations in the Horn of Africa.

With the evidence of the continued involvement of An. stephensi in malaria transmission in Djibouti, the conventional vector control measures will need adjustments to account for An. stephensi distinct behaviors. As An. stephensi have been reported to display outdoor biting behaviors, the use of indoor residual spraying (IRS) and insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) alone is insufficient to protect people from bites [30]. Additionally, Djibouti’s coastal location with frequent rainfall and flooding can contribute to an increase in larval habitats [8, 31]. Larval source management (LSM) can combat this by either killing larvae or modifying their habitats to contain the spread of populations [32]. However, LSM does not provide information on transmission indicators, as the circumsporozoite protein can only be detected in adult mosquitoes. Based on the limitations of conventional vector control strategies such as IRS, ITNs, and LSM, additional methods that track entomological indicators such as increasing adult surveillance, would be beneficial for Djibouti. Controlling adults will control all mosquito life stages and help stop further spread of this invasive vector.

Conclusions

Through this study, we molecularly confirmed that invasive An. stephensi is transmitting malaria parasites (P. vivax 210) in Djibouti. We additionally confirmed through a COI region analysis that An. stephensi populations in Djibouti are closely related to the majority of An. stephensi populations found in the Horn of Africa. This highlights the threat of An. stephensi invasion and supports a rapid and targeted response to mitigate the harm that An. stephensi cause, particularly through adult surveillance and control.
