Multicenter Expanded Access Protocol for Research Through Access to Trehalose in People With Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction/AimsExpanded access protocols (EAPs) allow individuals ineligible for clinical trials to receive investigational products. EAP data can be collected in parallel to randomized clinical trials (RCTs) and serve as a source of evidence in clinical practice. Here, we present the results of a National Institutes of Health (NIH)‐funded EAP for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS).MethodsParticipants received trehalose, a drug studied in a parallel RCT, for up to 24 weeks; clinical and biomarker data were collected throughout the study.ResultsSeventy participants were enrolled at 20 study centers across the United States. Treatment with trehalose did not affect the levels of neurofilament light chain [estimated flat slope per month was −0.005, SE = 0.0078; 95% CI (−0.021, 0.011)] or disease progression [estimated least square mean change of the ALS Functional Rating Scale—Revised total score and slow vital capacity (percent predicted) from baseline to Week 24 were −5.6 (0.67); 95% CI (−7.0, −4.3) and −4.53 (4.308); 95% CI (−13.55, 4.48)], respectively. No unexpected treatment‐related risks were identified. Serious adverse events were deemed not related to trehalose (20 occurrences in 13 [18.6%] participants with eight deaths).DiscussionThis EAP establishes a framework for implementing multi‐center EAPs that complement data collected from RCTs. Additional NIH‐funded EAPs are currently underway. Data and additional serum samples collected in this study are available to the research community for further study. Introduction

The only US Food and Drug Administration (FDA)‐approved medications for amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) are riluzole [1], edaravone [2], and tofersen [3]. Although these drugs slow disease progression, they are not curative. Thus, there is still an unmet clinical need. However, many people living with ALS are not eligible for participation in randomized clinical trials (RCTs) for a variety of reasons, primarily related to severity or length of disease.

Expanded access (EA) offers an alternative, FDA‐regulated pathway for people with a serious illness, like ALS, to access research outside the scope of clinical trials [4]. The primary aim of expanded access protocols (EAPs) is to provide investigational products (IPs) to patients ineligible for RCTs, without undermining the progress of drug development [4]. EAPs also have the potential to supplement RCTs by providing biomarker, safety, and clinical data in a broader population.

Although multicenter EAPs have been implemented for years in other medical fields, such as oncology and HIV [5, 6, 7], only a few have been reported in ALS [8, 9, 10]. In 2021, a multicenter EA program was initiated in parallel to an ongoing RCT, the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial testing multiple IPs [11, 12]. This EA program was successfully designed and launched across nine platform trial‐affiliated research sites to provide access to three drugs tested in the trial itself. While the program collected clinical and biomarker data, it primarily focused on access and safety monitoring. It also provided valuable operational experience, serving as a steppingstone for larger EA programs.

Recently, the Accelerating Access to Critical Therapies for ALS Act (ACT for ALS) enabled the launch of EAPs designed with significantly expanded research goals and geographic reach [13, 14]. These EAP studies are expected to be conducted with the rigor of a clinical trial. Data and samples collected via this mechanism are shared with the global research community through National Institutes of Health (NIH)‐funded biorepositories, thus enabling additional discovery efforts.

Trehalose (SLS‐005, Seelos Therapeutics) is an intravenously administered IP targeting autophagy [15], which is known to be dysregulated in ALS. Here we report the design, operational features, and results of an NIH‐funded, multicenter EAP, which was developed to provide access to trehalose in individuals with ALS who are ineligible for RCTs.

Methods

Overview and Site Selection

Trehalose was an IP concurrently under evaluation in an RCT‐Regimen E of the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial (NCT05136885) [16]. All sites actively participating in Regimen E were invited to indicate their interest in participating in the EAP. Sites selected for inclusion underwent an activation process, which entailed completion of local requirements, a fully executed clinical site agreement (CSA), site institutional review board (IRB) approval, study staff training, and regulatory document collection.

Organizational Framework

The operational structure of the trehalose EAP involved key stakeholders including the patient community, NIH, Seelos Therapeutics, the clinical and data coordination center, the single IRB (sIRB), vendors, and participating study sites (Figure S1). The NIH served as the financial sponsor. Seelos Therapeutics was the IND holder and regulatory sponsor. The clinical and data coordination center was located at the Neurological Clinical Research Institute (NCRI) at Massachusetts General Hospital and provided data management, project management, quality assurance, contracts and finance, information systems technology (including the electronic data capture system), vendor management, and administrative assistance for this EAP (Figure S2). This center was led by academic principal investigators. Vendor‐supported services included biostatistics, site monitoring, outcomes training, data and safety monitoring, medical monitoring, and centralized laboratory services for biomarkers and safety testing labs. Additional vendors contracted through Seelos Therapeutics were responsible for IP manufacturing, shipment, and home infusion services (Figure S3). Enrolling sites were supported by a study team, including one or more site investigators, clinical research nurses, clinical research coordinators, and outcome evaluators, grant administrators, and additional research staff (Figure S4).

Study Timeline

The protocol was registered on clinicaltrials.gov and submitted to the FDA by Seelos Therapeutics. The NCRI coordination center was responsible for securing IRB approvals. The Mass General Brigham IRB served as the sIRB of record for the study. The trehalose EAP followed a planned timeline consisting of four stages: startup, enrollment, treatment follow‐up, and closeout and reporting (Figure S2).

Patient and Public Involvement

The design of the study was informed by feedback from the Patient Advisory Committee, which consisted of approximately 15 people living with ALS and their caregivers. In addition to providing key input related to trial design, the committee continued to meet on a quarterly basis to ensure that the patient perspective was incorporated throughout the trial. A patient representative also participated in the study Steering Committee. Additionally, the study team regularly shared updates about study progress with the ALS community through public webinars.

Participant Population and Eligibility Criteria

To be considered for the trehalose EAP, participants had to be receiving routine care at an ALS center affiliated with the EAP. Participants were grouped into two cohorts: individuals who had not previously been exposed to trehalose and did not qualify for any reasonably accessible ongoing clinical trial (Cohort 1) and individuals who had completed Regimen E of the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial and were no longer eligible for enrollment in another treatment regimen (Cohort 2). The inclusion of a subgroup of individuals who had completed the trehalose trial was intended to allow continued access to the study drug while waiting for the results of the trehalose RCT to become available. All participants were required to provide written informed consent prior to screening. Participants were permitted to continue using FDA‐approved medications for ALS as part of their standard of care treatment throughout the duration of study participation. Exclusion criteria included current diagnosis of, or treatment for, diabetes mellitus and/or a screening glucose ≥ 140 mg/dL. Full eligibility and exclusion criteria can be found in the study protocol (Supporting Information).

Study Intervention and Visits

The study intervention was delivered as weekly intravenous (IV) infusions of trehalose 90.5 mg/mL, at a dose of 0.75 g/kg, which mirrored the dosage and dosing interval used in Regimen E of the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial. Each participant received up to 25 infusions over a 24‐week period. Most infusions were administered by a home infusion service, except for the first four infusions in trehalose‐naïve participants, which were administered in outpatient infusion centers at participating institutions. The full schedule of activities can be found in the study protocol (Supporting Information). The EAP was terminated when the trehalose RCT reported negative results.

Outcome Measures

The primary scientific goal was to investigate this under‐studied segment of the ALS patient population in parallel to the ongoing trehalose RCT, including the evaluation of the safety, biological activity, and clinical effects of trehalose. Safety was monitored throughout participation via clinical exams, monitoring of adverse events, and safety laboratory measurements. Additional outcome measures collected included (1) serum neurofilament light (NfL) chain levels, a measure of motor neuron integrity, (2) ALS Functional Rating Scale—Revised (ALSFRS‐R), a measure of physical functioning [17], (3) slow vital capacity (SVC), a measure of the strength of the muscles of ventilation, (4) ALS Assessment Questionnaire (ALSAQ‐40), a measure of quality of life [18], and (5) survival.

Biological Samples

Serum samples were collected to measure NfL levels in study participants. To enable further scientific exploration in alignment with ACT for ALS, additional serum samples were collected throughout the study and stored in a biorepository. These samples are now available for sharing with researchers.

Statistical Analysis

The primary analytic population, referred to as the full analysis set (FAS), included all participants enrolled in the study.

A descriptive summary of the change in log‐transformed serum NfL levels from baseline to Week 24 was performed in the FAS. A general linear mixed effects model was used to compare the rate of decrease (i.e., slope) of NfL levels over the 24‐week treatment period, including a random intercept for each participant and fixed effects for treatment group, visit, treatment by visit interaction, and bulbar onset (yes/no) as a covariate. Estimates of the slope and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) are presented.

The mean change in ALSFRS‐R total score from baseline to Week 24 was analyzed using a linear mixed‐effects model for repeated measures (MMRM) including fixed effects for treatment, visit (as a categorical variable), treatment‐by‐visit interaction, baseline value‐by‐visit interaction, bulbar onset, and baseline value as covariates, with participant as a random effect. The least square mean and standard error (SE), as well as two‐sided 95% CI, are provided.

For endpoints collected only at baseline and Week 24—SVC (percent predicted), ALSAQ‐40 total score, and ALSAQ‐40 subscales—changes from baseline to Week 24 were analyzed using an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) including main effects for treatment group, bulbar onset, and baseline value. The least square mean (SE) and two‐sided 95% CI are provided.

Overall survival (OS) was calculated in months, defined as the time from the date of first study treatment administration to the date of death due to any cause. Participants not known to have died at the time of the analysis were censored on the date they were last known to be alive. Median OS and 95% CI were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. A sensitivity analysis was performed evaluating the OS by including usage of permanent assisted ventilation (PAV) as an event outcome together with death.

As there was no placebo group in this EAP, an exploratory comparison was performed against historical controls to estimate the treatment effect on ALSFRS‐R total score and OS. Historical control data for this analysis were provided on October 28, 2024 from the Answer ALS database [19], which comprised the latest version of the data set from October 2024. The eligible historical control group was constructed by selection of those within the database who had (1) known mortality information; (2) a baseline and at least one post‐baseline ALSFRS‐R total score recorded; (3) a baseline and at least one post‐baseline vital capacity measurement; (4) met major eligibility criteria from this EAP study, including age 18 years or older and screening body weight < 144 kg; and (5) non‐missing baseline characteristics of body mass index (BMI), bulbar onset, riluzole use information, and date of ALS symptom onset and diagnosis.

To account for potential imbalances in baseline participant characteristics, the trehalose treated group from the FAS and the eligible historical control group were matched by a propensity score (PS) matching approach and PS stratification approach, respectively. This was adjusted for baseline covariates of known prognostic significance in ALS, including time since ALS symptom onset [20], pre‐baseline ALSFRS‐R slope [21] (defined as the rate of change in ALSFRS‐R total score from symptom onset to study baseline), baseline % predicted vital capacity [22], and age [20] at study baseline. Additional baseline variables included BMI, bulbar onset (yes/no), riluzole use (yes/no), time since ALS diagnosis, and baseline ALSFRS‐R total score.

PS matching was conducted in a 1:1 ratio with a caliper width of 0.1 based on the standard deviation (SD) of the logit of the PS [23]. Additional details are available in the Statistical Analysis Plan (Supporting Information).

Results

Site Selection, Regulatory Approvals

Of the 60 trial sites participating in regimen E of the HEALEY ALS Platform Trial, 48 sites expressed interest in participating in the trehalose EAP. From these, 25 geographically distributed sites were selected, 21 sites were activated, and 20 actively enrolled participants (Figure S1). The time between initial submission of the protocol to sIRB approval was 41 days, and the mean time for site approval was 9.4 days. The EAP was successfully launched and completed enrollment per planned timelines (Figure S2).

Participant Characteristics

The study enrolled 70 people living with ALS, and outcome measures were recorded over a period of 24 weeks. Notably, participants were at advanced stages of disease progression, had long disease duration, or other characteristics making them ineligible for clinical trial participation. Safety and Tolerability

A total of 51 (72.9%) participants enrolled in the study experienced treatment‐emergent adverse events (TEAEs). There were 20 serious adverse events (SAEs), with 8 resulting in death. None of the deaths were thought to be related to study drug: 7 were attributed to ALS disease progression and one was due to a motor vehicle accident. The most common TEAEs are listed in Table 2. There were no statistically significant changes in vital signs or laboratory results. Mean hemoglobin A1C was 5.13% (SD = 0.414) at baseline and 5.15% (SD = 0.343) at 24 weeks.

Neurofilament (NfL) Analysis

A total of 840 individual samples were collected from 66 unique participants. Of these, 108 samples were used for NfL testing, while 732 were donated to biorepositories for additional research. The NfL slope estimates per month were flat [−0.005, SE = 0.0078; 95% CI (−0.021, 0.011)].

Function and Survival Outcomes

The estimated least square mean change of the ALSFRS‐R total score from baseline through Week 24 was −5.6 (0.67); 95% CI (−7.0, −4.3). The estimated least square mean change in SVC (percent predicted) from baseline through Week 24 was −4.53 (4.308) [95% CI (−13.55, 4.48)].

The estimated least square mean for change of ALSAQ‐40 from baseline through Week 24 was 9.76 (1.514) [95% CI (6.73, 12.79)].

A total of 46 participants from the trehalose treated group were matched with historical controls. The comparison was limited to 46 participants out of the total 70 due to insufficient baseline data for PS matching. Comparison to historical controls showed no benefit of trehalose in ALSFRS‐R total scores or survival over the study period. The least square mean change difference (SE) of trehalose versus historical control in ALSFRS‐R from baseline at Week 24 was −1.8 (1.29) with a 95% CI of (−4.4, 0.8) and p = 0.1617. At the end of the EAP study period, the trehalose group and the historical control group had survival rates of 94.0% (77.4%, 98.5%) versus 91.0% (77.8%, 96.5%), respectively.

Discussion

The trehalose EAP is the first of a series of large‐scale NIH‐funded EAPs for ALS, representing a paradigm shift in access to research opportunities for the ALS population.

In other medical fields, including oncology and HIV [5, 6, 7], multicenter EAPs have demonstrated the feasibility of providing research access options to patients who do not qualify for RCTs. Several strengths contribute to the significance of the trehalose EAP for the field of ALS research. The trehalose EAP was launched while the corresponding clinical trial was evaluating a defined segment of the ALS population, thus enabling patients who could not access the trial to access this IP while formal efficacy testing was ongoing. The use of EAP data in conjunction with RCT data is an area of growing interest [24] and has the potential to add to the generalizability of the data [25].

Partnerships with stakeholders enabled the launch of a multisite EAP with broad geographic reach, clearly defined research goals, and a patient‐centered design. For instance, the inclusion of home infusions and remote visits was essential for ensuring participants could access the study. From an operational perspective, the coordination center and the network of sites participating in this EAP gained valuable experience with implementing EA in ALS. The coordination center developed a series of processes that can be leveraged to support the start‐up and management of future large‐scale EA programs. Additionally, the sites gained familiarity with the resources needed to support EA participants.

As noted, 80% of the sites participating in the trehalose RCT expressed interest in the trehalose EAP. A previous EAP multicenter experience in ALS revealed lower site interest (38%) [12]. This highlights that, as the EAP infrastructure becomes more established in ALS, more sites are willing and able to participate. Most clinical trials limit enrollment to the first 18–36 months after disease onset. By comparison, in this EAP, the average time since symptom onset was over 44 months, with wide variability and inclusion of some participants who had been symptomatic for several years. Functional rating scores were lower than what is typically seen in RCTs, in keeping with the goal of enrolling a more advanced population [26, 27]. This program also showed that launching an EAP while the corresponding efficacy trial is ongoing is feasible without negatively impacting RCT enrollment.

EAPs can help advance scientific knowledge. For example, bio‐samples collected during the EAP have been stored in public biorepositories and shared with researchers for additional biomarker analyses unrelated to this study, ensuring their availability for future research. Additionally, data generated throughout this study have been deposited into the Pooled Resource Open‐Access ALS Clinical Trials (PROACT) database [28]. These platforms allow researchers to further explore mechanisms of ALS, identify novel biomarkers, and refine therapeutic strategies using comprehensive datasets.

RCTs often restrict eligibility to optimize statistical power and trial efficiency. These criteria limit the evaluation of long‐term safety and efficacy across all ALS disease stages and phenotypes. EAPs address this gap, generating critical data on safety, biomarkers, and clinical outcomes complementing RCT findings.

Nevertheless, there are limitations that are inherent to EAPs. First and foremost, EAPs are not designed to test the efficacy of a specific IP. While EAP data can supplement RCT findings with safety and biomarker data from a population that was ineligible for the trial, they are unlikely to replace RCTs for assessing the efficacy of novel compounds. Exceptions may occur in ultra‐rare diseases if a curative intervention is identified in a population with a well‐defined natural history. However, this is unlikely in sporadic ALS, characterized by a heterogeneous course.

Additionally, large‐scale, research‐focused EAPs require substantial planning and coordination. In comparison to small EAPs that focus primarily on access, EAPs with robust research goals involve considerably more stakeholders and extensive infrastructure to support rigorous data collection. Therefore, the organizational complexity of this EAP is comparable to that of a RCT. This complexity incurs substantial costs to ensure high‐quality research operations (the direct costs for this EAP were $11,965,264), in alignment with published data [29, 30]. From a consenting perspective, it is important to note that the safety and efficacy of the IP are unknown at the time of enrollment. Thus, participation in an EAP should not be presented as a “treatment,” rather as an option to access a product in development and contribute to research. Another limitation of this study is the lack of a placebo group and its premature closure due to the negative results from the RCT. In the absence of a placebo group, comparisons were made with historical controls. While EAP participants represent a broader share of the ALS population compared to the RCT cohorts, including individuals with long disease duration and advanced disease, there are limitations to the generalizability of the results. For example, in this EAP, the rate of bulbar onset and positive family history for ALS were higher than the general ALS population.

In conclusion, this NIH‐funded, research‐focused, large‐scale EAP demonstrated substantial progress toward the goal of providing more inclusive research opportunities to the ALS population. We hope that the blueprint generated by this first EAP will foster further growth in the field. Additional NIH‐funded EAPs are ongoing and expected to continue to advance ALS research with increased participation from the ALS community in alignment with the goals of the ACT for ALS legislation [31]. Further, this study contributed bio‐samples and data to open repositories and knowledge‐sharing platforms, creating a valuable resource for future ALS research.
