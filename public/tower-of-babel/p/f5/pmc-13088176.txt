Asymmetric Total Synthesis and Structure Revision of (+)-Mangicol D
Authors: Yi Xie, Yutong Wang, Zhouyang Zhu, Shang Ning, Thomas J. Maimone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Isolated from the marine fungus Fusarium heterosporum, mangicols represent a new class of sesterterpenoids featuring unprecedented spirotetracyclic cores and notable bioactivities. Herein, we report the first asymmetric synthesis of a mangicol sesterterpene exploiting a domino carbopalladation-carbonylation, Conia-ene cyclization, and SmI2-mediated reductive cyclization to establish the spiro-fused tetracyclic framework in a concise manner. Our findings suggest a stereochemical revision of the polyol side chain of the initially isolated mangicols.

Marine microorganisms, particularly fungi, have long been recognized as prolific sources of structurally diverse secondary metabolites with remarkable bioactivities. In 1998, Fenical and co-workers isolated the marine fungus Fusarium sp. strain CNC-477 from a driftwood sample collected in the Bahamas Islands and identified two novel halogenated sesterterpenoids, neomangicols A (1) and B (2), from the mycelium extracts (Figure A). A separate cultivation experiment yielded neomangicol C (3) as the sole component, later suspected to be an artifact arising from the loss of HX (X = Cl, Br) from 1 or 2. Two years later, a family of biosynthetically related metabolites, mangicols A–G (4–10, Figure A), were isolated from the same fungal strain. Several members of this family displayed significant in vitro cytotoxicity against human tumor cell lines, and notably, mangicols A (4) and C (7) exhibited potent anti-inflammatory activity in a phorbol myristate acetate-induced mouse ear edema assay, with efficacy comparable to that of indomethacin.

Structural elucidation of the neomangicols revealed an unprecedented tetracyclic carbon framework that defined a new class of polycyclic sesterterpenoids. Subsequent 13C labeling experiments also supported a biosynthetic relationship between the neomangicols and the mangicols, wherein the former might be derived from the latter. Stereochemical assignment of the flexible polyol side chain of these natural products, however, proved challenging. Indeed, the stereochemistry in this region was not determined for 7–10. For the remaining mangicols (i.e., 1–6) the stereochemistry of the side chain region is based on NMR interpretation and molecular modeling of three more rigid acetonide derivatives of neomangicol C (3). Through these studies, the C17, C18, and C19 stereocenters were cautiously proposed as S-configured as shown in Figure .

These architecturally novel and biologically active sesterterpenoids have naturally stimulated considerable interest from the synthetic chemistry community. Given our past interest in the synthesis of complex sesterterpenes and future interest in exploring the biosynthetic relationships between these metabolites, we embarked on a chemical synthesis of the mangicols. , Herein, we report the first asymmetric total synthesis of mangicol D, work that has led to a structural revision of the polyol side chain of this metabolite. Our findings are also in accordance with a very recently isolated mangicol whose structure was confirmed by X-ray crystallographic analysis.

We initially targeted ester 11, which decouples the tetracyclic core from the side chain and thereby enables late-stage variation of the side chain configuration for stereochemical validation studies (Figure B). Recognizing that the A/B ring system is conserved across the mangicol family, we identified 5,6-fused bicyclic enone 12 as a key building block that could be doubly annulated with alkyne fragments containing preprogrammed oxidation states for the various mangicol C- and D-rings (Figure B). We anticipated that the enone functionality in 12 would enable C–C bond formation via both one- and two-electron processes, while the existing cis-hydrindane framework would enable stereocontrolled assembly of the spiro-fused tetracyclic core.

Our synthesis commenced from known enantioenriched diketone 14, which is prepared on a multigram scale from commercial materials in a one-pot (two-step) procedure with a single purification (Scheme ). A selective Wittig olefination converted the methyl ketone into an isopropenyl unit, and the remaining cyclohexanone was triflated (KHMDS, Comins’ reagent) to generate vinyl triflate 15, thus setting the stage for A-ring assembly. Heck cyclization established the quaternary center with excellent stereocontrol, − and trapping of the ensuing C­(sp3)–Pd­(II) intermediate with CO/MeOH afforded methyl ester 16, providing a handle for late-stage polyol side chain elaboration. Removal of the cyclic ketal with BF3·OEt2 also promoted concomitant olefin migration to yield conjugated enone 17 as the major alkene isomer in a single step.

With straightforward access to enone 17, we next proceeded to introduce the two alkynyl chains onto the six-membered ring. α-Iodination of the enone provided a handle for cross-coupling, which was accomplished via a Negishi coupling with Grignard 20 (ZnCl2, Pd­(t-Bu3P)2). Organometallic 20 was also employed in the next key C–C bond-forming step by conversion to a cuprate and diastereoselective 1,4-addition to 19 from the convex face, furnishing 21 in high yield. With a latent carbonyl and an appended alkyne in place, a Conia-ene cyclization was leveraged to establish the final quaternary center of the core. Employing Trauner’s mild conditions (t-BuOK/DMSO, room temperature), the desired C–C bond was formed in 63% yield, presumably via initial alkoxide-mediated cleavage of the silyl enol ether to generate an enolate. , Notably, both alkynyl TMS groups were also concomitantly removed under these basic conditions. The closure of the remaining five-membered ring of the terpene core was achieved through a SmI2/H2O-mediated carbonyl-alkyne reductive cyclization and delivered the tetracyclic core (see 23) in excellent yield. , While the use of an alkene (rather than alkyne) coupling partner would increase the efficiency of the synthesis, our SmI2/H2O-mediated conditions afforded cyclized material with the incorrect stereochemical configuration at C9 when this strategy was explored.

Hydrogenation of 23 with Adams’ catalyst reduced both alkenes but provided a mixture of diastereomers at the newly formed methyl stereocenters. When a bulky TMS group was placed on the tertiary alcohol, however, a completely stereoselective hydrogenation occurred, giving ester 24 after concomitant desilylation by the alcohol solvent. Burgess dehydration of the tertiary alcohol then furnished the trisubstituted alkene along with minor amounts of the tetrasubstituted isomer (3.1:1 ratio) likely due to an unusually facile E1 elimination reaction in this scaffold. Notably, both MsCl/Et3N and Martin’s sulfurane afforded the tetrasubstituted alkene isomer exclusively. Finally, a DIBAL reduction of the methyl ester generated aldehyde 25, setting the stage for introduction of the final polyol side chain.

(E)-Configured vinyl iodide 27 was selected as the terminal C4 fragment based on the proposed structure of mangicol D and a planned cis-dihydroxylation reaction (Scheme ). Lithium–halogen exchange of 27 generated the corresponding vinyl lithium, which was added to aldehyde 25 to give a 1:1 mixture of C17 diastereomeric alcohols (26 + epi-26) in 60% yield. After separation, each diastereomer was subjected to standard cis-dihydroxylation conditions (OsO4, NMO, acetone/H2O), which proceeded with low diastereoselectivity (∼1.5:1–2:1 dr) for both isomers and thus afforded access to four of the eight possible diastereomers of the side-chain region (see Figure ). A final PMB deprotection then completed assembly of the polyol motif. In accordance with Fenical’s studies during the structure elucidation of the mangicols, it was not possible to unambiguously determine the structures of these four diastereomers (either PMB-protected or as free triols) by NMR analysis. However, all four isomers of 6 showed significant discrepancies relative to the reported NMR data for mangicol D, particularly in the C16–C22 region (see SI), suggesting a stereochemical misassignment of the polyol side chain.

Determination of the true structure of 6 was aided by a recent disclosure of eight additional mangicol members derived from heterologous expression of a Fusarium oxysporum 14005 biosynthetic gene cluster. In particular, a THF-ring-containing member, mangicol M (28), was isolated under these conditions, and its structure was confirmed by single-crystal X-ray diffraction (Scheme ). Notably, mangicol M possesses 17R, 18R, 19S stereochemistry. In addition, mangicol D was also reisolated alongside 28, and its reported NMR data closely matched Fenical’s original isolate. Hypothesizing that the mangicols possess conserved stereochemistry along the polyol side chain, we therefore targeted the 17R, 18R diastereomer for synthesis.

(Z)-Configured vinyl iodide 29 was prepared, and its lithiate added to 25 to generate 30 and its C17 epimer in high yield, again as a 1:1 mixture of separable diastereomers (Scheme ). Subjecting 30 to Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation with (DHQD)2PHAL as the chiral ligand afforded 31 in 78% yield. Subsequent deprotection of the PMB group (Pd/C, H2, MeOH, Δ) furnished 6a in 95% yield. The NMR spectroscopic data of 6a were in complete accordance with natural mangicol D reported by both the Fenical and Liu and Jiang laboratories. , Despite this match, unambiguous assignment of the side chain stereochemistry was achieved by acetylation of 6a (Ac2O, DMAP, Et3N) and subsequent crystallization of the resulting triacetate derivative 32. Notably, compound 32 was the only derivative that yielded crystals suitable for X-ray diffraction among all the diastereomers prepared in this study, as well as a variety of derivatized products.

In summary, we describe the first asymmetric synthesis of mangicol D using a carbonylative Heck cyclization, Conia-ene reaction, and SmI2-mediated cyclization to efficiently forge the spirotetracyclic core. Late-stage installation of the side chain then proved instrumental in rectifying the structure of the target. The described route proceeded in 16 steps from 14 and allowed for the preparation of over 300 mg of 24, features which bode well for future syntheses of members with varying side chains. Given that the initially isolated mangicol and neomangicol structures (1–5) were all assigned by analogy, it is likely that these metabolites also possess 17R, 18R configurations along their polyol side chainsan important consideration for future synthetic and medicinal chemistry efforts toward these intriguing targets.
