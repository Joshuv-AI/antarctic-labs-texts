Application Study on the Preparation of Curcumin/PVP Coprecipitated Particles Using Coaxial Nozzle in Supercritical Antisolvent
Authors: Yechen Wang, Zirui Li, Fei Li, Wang Weiqiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The supercritical antisolvent­(SAS) technique serves as an environmentally benign processing method that effectively enhances the pharmacokinetic properties of poorly water-soluble drugs. Nevertheless, the existing supercritical fluid technology and processing methodologies remain underdeveloped, presenting significant barriers to large-scale industrial implementation. To overcome these limitations, a coaxial nozzle and its associated auxiliary apparatus were engineered. Taking curcumin as the research object, using polyvinylpyrrolidone­(PVP) as the polymer carrier, and ethanol and acetone as the organic solvents, curcumin/PVP coprecipitated particles were synthesized via the SAS process. A detailed examination was conducted to evaluate the influence of key processing parametersincluding acetone/ethanol­(Ac/EtOH) volume ratio, curcumin/PVP mass ratio, temperature, pressure, and solution concentrationon the particle size and distribution of the resulting coprecipitates. The coprecipitate was characterized by various characterization methods. The findings reveal that the SAS process effectively generated amorphous curcumin/PVP coprecipitates with a submicron-scale particle diameter of 337 ± 47 nm. These findings confirm the great potential of the SAS method in the production of polymer-drug composite systems.

Introduction

The pharmaceutical industry has long been dominated by conventional drug manufacturing technologies, such as freeze-drying, spray drying, and mechanical milling. Although these methods have had a significant impact on the development of many drug formulations, their inherent limitations, including high processing temperatures, residual organic solvents, and poor control over particle size and morphology, have substantial implications for drug efficacy and safety. These constraints have driven the exploration of novel technologies as alternatives to traditional drug manufacturing, aiming to overcome these challenges while maintaining or enhancing drug performance.

For pharmaceutical applications, supercritical fluids (SCF) technology offers an eco-friendly alternative with significant industrial potential. Among various SCF, SC–CO2 has been extensively utilized owing to its nontoxicity, low cost, and environmental benignity, effectively addressing the safety and environmental concerns associated with conventional organic solvents. SC–CO2 exhibits unique physicochemical properties that render it an ideal medium for pharmaceutical processing. The tunable solvent power of SC–CO2, achieved through precise regulation of pressure and temperature, enables accurate control over particle formation and morphology. SC–CO2-based techniques, such as rapid expansion of supercritical solutions (RESS) and supercritical antisolvent (SAS), have demonstrated significant potential in producing micro- and nanoparticles with enhanced drug delivery properties. , These technologies have been successfully applied to a large number of active pharmaceutical ingredients (APIs), including poorly water-soluble drugs, proteins, and natural compounds, resulting in markedly improved drug solubility and bioavailability.

In SC–CO2-based drug processing, nozzle design is a critical component that significantly influences the efficiency and quality of particle formation. While conventional nozzles are effective in certain applications, they often suffer from limitations such as clogging, uneven flow distribution, and insufficient control over particle size and morphology. − To address these issues, coaxial adjustable gap nozzles have been developed and integrated into SC–CO2 systems. This method is generally called solution-enhanced dispersion by SC–CO2 (SEDS), and it is an improved version of the SAS process,, , In this method, in addition to acting as an antisolvent, SC–CO2 is used as a “dispersing agent” to improve mass transfer between SC–CO2 and droplets. Therein nozzles feature concentric inner and outer channels, allowing for the simultaneous introduction of drug solutions and SC–CO2, with the gap between the channels being adjustable to fine-tune flow dynamics. The coaxial design ensures uniform mixing of SC–CO2 and the drug solution, leading to more consistent particle formation. Additionally, the phase change of CO2 at ordinary coaxial nozzles may occur due to the Joule-Thomson effect, resulting in dry ice blocking nozzles, which affects the morphology and size of particles. To address this, we designed an adjustable annular gap nozzle (Figure ). The adjustable gap enables precise control over shear forces and turbulence during mixing, effectively avoiding the Joule-Thomson effect, which is crucial for achieving the desired particle size and morphology.

Curcumin, a natural phenolic antioxidant derived from the rhizomes of Turmeric and other Zingiberaceae plants. It exhibits poor solubility in water and diethyl ether but is soluble in ethanol and acetone. This bioactive compound has garnered significant attention in pharmaceutical and food industries due to its remarkable therapeutic properties, including anti-inflammatory, antitumor, and antioxidant activities. Despite its broad pharmacological activities, the clinical application scope of curcumin is hindered by its low bioavailability and solubility. Curcumin exhibits extremely poor solubility in water and undergoes extensive first-pass metabolism coupled with rapid systemic clearance following oral administration, collectively contributing to its unfavorable pharmacokinetic profile. To circumvent these pharmacological constraints, contemporary research efforts are primarily directed toward engineering innovative drug delivery systems, encompassing polymeric nanoparticles, microsphere formulations, phospholipid-based vesicles (liposomes), and amorphous solid dispersions. These engineered delivery systems have demonstrated marked improvements in curcumin’s biopharmaceutical performance through enhanced specific surface area, optimized solubility parameters, improved molecular stability, and modulated release kinetics, thereby potentiating its therapeutic outcomes.

Micro- and nanodrug delivery systems offer many advantages over conventional formulations. Frist, these systems exhibit a higher porosity and larger specific surface area, which contribute to enhanced drug dissolution rates and bioavailability. Additionally, micro- and nanodrug formulations enable targeted drug delivery and controlled release, thereby improving therapeutic efficacy while minimizing side effects. Polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP), a hydrophilic synthetic polymer, has been widely employed in drug formulations because of its outstanding biocompatibility and film-forming capacity. PVP can serve as a drug carrier, enhancing drug solubility and stability through the formation of solid dispersions or coprecipitated particles. − It inhibits drug crystallization, maintaining the drug in an amorphous state, which significantly improves solubility and bioavailability. , Furthermore, PVP enhances the stability of drug formulations, protecting the drug from environmental factors. Combining curcumin with PVP to produce coprecipitated particles represents a promising strategy for developing curcumin formulations.

Therefore, it is imperative to conduct a more in-depth investigation into the coprecipitation of curcumin and PVP. In this work, curcumin was employed as the research object, with PVP as the carrier, ethanol and acetone as solvents, and SC–CO2 as the antisolvent. Coprecipitated curcumin/PVP particles were prepared using a coaxial annular gap nozzle. The effects of acetone/ethanol (Ac/EtOH) volume ratio, curcumin/PVP mass ratio, temperature, pressure, and solution concentration on the particle size and distribution of the coprecipitates were systematically examined. Additionally, the coprecipitates were characterized by various characterization methods.

Apparatus and Operational Procedures

Figure presents a schematic overview of the SAS apparatus configuration. Our research team designed this custom experimental setup for producing curcumin/PVP coprecipitated particles via the SAS method, which includes a coaxial adjustable annular gap nozzle and related components. The supporting SAS equipment was fabricated by Nantong Hua’an Supercritical Extraction, while the coaxial nozzle was manufactured by Luoyang Hua Cai Science and Technology.

Schematic diagram of SAS process and apparatus.

The system architecture comprises four main subsystems: the CO2 delivery module (featuring a gas cylinder, cooler, CO2 pump, heater, and buffer tank); the solution handling module (with a storage vessel and solvent peristaltic pump); the particle formation module (equipped with the coaxial nozzle, crystallizer, and separator); and supporting instrumentation (including a back pressure valve, flow meter, and thermal heating jacket).

Figure illustrates both the physical layout and schematic design of the coaxial nozzle assembly. The coaxial nozzle consists of three channels. According to the experimental requirements, the coaxial nozzle can adjust the size of the annular clearance of each channel independently.

Physical image and structural schematic of the coaxial nozzle.

At the outset of each experiment, carbon dioxide is first piped from the gas cylinder into a chiller to ensure it remains a liquid, thereby preventing pump cavitation down the line. A high-pressure plunger pump then forces the liquid CO2 into the system, where it subsequently passes through a preheater to hit the required temperature. Upon completion of heating, CO2 maintains a constant flow rate through the inner channel of a coaxial nozzle, and then into the high-pressure crystallizer, building up the required pressure. Fine-tuning the nozzle annular clearance diameter or the back pressure valve can precisely regulate the pressure of the crystallizer. Meanwhile, the electric heating jacket keeps the temperature of the crystallizer stable all the time. Moving on, the solvent is then steadily and consistently fed into the crystallizer via the coaxial nozzle’s outer channel over the span of a few minutes. This process is helpful for balancing the composition of the fluid phase. Once everything is stable, a solution containing a defined concentration of curcumin, PVP, or a mix of both, is continuously fed into the crystallizer, displacing the solvent until the reaction wraps up. Once the solution injection stops, CO2 is then flushed through the system for an additional 90 min to get rid of any last traces of solvent as completely as possible. Finally, the system is gradually brought back down to atmospheric pressure. Particle collection is carried out using a cylinder equipped with a filter membrane. Any solvent used throughout the procedure is captured using a separator.

Characterization

Scanning Electron Microscopy (SEM)

To examine the shape of drug particles produced with the supercritical antisolvent (SAS) method, we used a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Gemini SEM 300, 500, ZEISS). We started by sticking double-sided carbon tape to a copper stub and then sprinkling the sample evenly across the tape. After that, we popped the stub into a sputter coater and waited for a vacuum to form and stabilize. With the sputtering current dialed in at about 10 mA, we coated the sample with platinum for 20 min. Once sputtering was done, we let the vacuum out and moved the sample over to the SEM chamber. We took a look at everything under high vacuum conditions, using an accelerating voltage of 15 kV. We then used ImageJ to figure out the particle size and how spread out they were. For each sample, we measured around 500 particles from SEM images at different magnifications. We’re reporting the results as the mean diameter plus or minus the arithmetic standard deviation.

X-ray diffraction (XRD)

Crystalline properties were analyzed by powder X-ray diffraction (X’Pert3, Malvern Instruments Ltd.). Samples were mounted on glass substrates and scanned using Cu Kα radiation (λ=1.5406 Å, 40 kV, 40 mA) with nickel filtration. Diffraction patterns were recorded from 5° to 50°(2θ) with a step width of 0.013° and a counting time of 18.87 s per step.

Differential Scanning Calorimetry (DSC)

Thermal analysis was carried out using a DSC 214 Polyma instrument (Netzsch). Precisely weighed samples (∼5 mg) in sealed aluminum pans were subjected to a heating ramp from 40 to 250 °C at 20 °C/min under continuous nitrogen flow (50 mL/min).

Fourier Transform Infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Molecular interactions were investigated by FTIR spectroscopy (IR Tracer 100, Shimadzu). Sample preparation involved homogenizing test materials with KBr (1:100 w/w) followed by pellet formation under hydraulic pressure. Infrared spectra were collected in the 4000–400 cm–1 range at 4 cm–1 resolution.

High-Performance Liquid chromatography (HPLC)

Quantitative analysis of curcumin was performed on an Agilent 1260 Infinity II system equipped with a C18 column (30 × 2.1 mm, 2.6 μm). The mobile phase consisted of acetonitrile and 2% acetic acid (50:50 v/v) delivered isocratically at 1.0 mL/min. Column temperature was maintained at 30 °C with UV detection at 430 nm. Samples (20 μL injection volume) were filtered (0.22 μm) prior to the 15 min analysis.

Drug Dissolution In Vitro

Dissolution profiles were obtained using an RCY-1400 dissolution apparatus (Tianjin Rised Technology) with 500 mL of 0.8% SDS solution at 37 ± 0.5 °C (50 rpm paddle speed). Aliquots (2 mL) were collected at specified intervals (5–60 min), filtered (0.22 μm), and analyzed spectrophotometrically at 430 nm. The dissolution medium was immediately replenished with equal volumes to maintain sink conditions. Experimental Operating Conditions and Results

In previous work, curcumin was prepared using pure ethanol, but due to its low solubility in ethanol­(<5 mg/mL), the amount of curcumin produced per experiment was limited, resulting in low product recovery. Controlling the supersaturation of curcumin in solution is a critical factor in improving crystallization efficiency. Given that curcumin exhibits significantly higher solubility in acetone­(about 58 mg/mL) compared to ethanol, the addition of acetone to ethanol-curcumin solutions was employed to enhance curcumin solubility. This approach leverages the solubility difference between ethanol and acetone, with acetone acting as a high-solubility solvent to increase curcumin dissolution and supersaturation. The theoretical basis for this method lies in the synergistic effect of mixed solvents, which can alter solute–solvent interactions and influence curcumin solubility and crystallization behavior. , Although curcumin initially exhibits higher saturation in ethanol solutions compared to acetone-containing solutions, the presence of CO2 appears to induce a cosolvent effect with ethanol, enhancing curcumin solubility in the system, as observed in other studies. , This also explains the low product recovery when using pure ethanol solutions.

Raw curcumin (Figure a) exists in the form of highly aggregated rod-shaped crystals, with a large average particle size of approximately 14.71 μm. In contrast, curcumin treated with SAS in a 7:3 Ac-EtOH solution (Figure b, run 1) displays irregular block-like morphology, with the average particle size reduced to 3.98 μm. This size is comparable to that of curcumin treated with SAS in pure ethanol (4.13 μm) but with significantly higher experimental throughput and yield. These results demonstrate that manipulating the solvent power of organic solutions to alter solute solubility in the fluid phase has a substantial impact.

SEM characterization of: (a) raw curcumin; (b) curcumin processed by SAS­(run 1); (c) raw PVP; and (d) PVP processed by SAS­(run 2).

PVP was processed under the same conditions as curcumin (run 2). Notably, PVP exhibits much higher solubility in ethanol than in acetone (320 mg/mL>7 mg/mL), which is the opposite of curcumin. However, the use of an acetone-ethanol mixed solution achieves similar effects. Raw PVP (Figure c) consists of large spherical particles, while SAS-treated PVP (Figure d) forms irregular particles, with the average size decreasing from 34.03 μm to 17.97 μm.

Coprecipitation of Curcumin/PVP

Previous studies have shown that, when the active compound tends to assume a crystalline structure,PVP can interfere in the crystallization kinetics of some compounds by inhibiting the association of drug molecules to form crystal nuclei, , Therefore, we proceeded to prepare curcumin-PVP coprecipitates using the SAS technique and investigated the influence of five key factorsAc/EtOH volume ratio, curcumin/PVP mass ratio, pressure, temperature, and total solution concentrationon particle size and morphology. In the majority of experiments, the temperature­(328 K), pressure­(14 MPa), and CO2 flow rate­(100 g/min) were held constant to maintain a CO2 molar fraction­(x CO2) above 0.95. As reported in previous studies, such conditions correspond to the supercritical region of the CO2–solvent mixture, thereby facilitating the precipitation process. Effect of Ac/EtOH Volume Ratio

According to relevant studies, the proportion of acetone in the solvent system significantly influences the morphological characteristics of PVP particles. However, under extreme conditions (i.e., solvent systems with 0% or 100% acetone content), it is challenging to obtain a homogeneous solution that meets experimental requirements­(containing both compounds and exhibiting a transparent state). Therefore, this study selected an Ac-EtOH binary solvent system with a gradient increase in acetone volume fraction (10–90%) for coprecipitation experiments­(runs 3–7). While maintaining other experimental parameters constant­(pressure 14 MPa, temperature 328 K, total solution concentration 100 mg/mL, curcumin/PVP mass ratio 1:2), the effect of the Ac/EtOH volume ratio on particle size and distribution characteristics was systematically investigated.

The experimental findings reveal a pronounced synergistic interaction within the binary solvent system, effectively improving solution supersaturation. As shown in Figure a, when the acetone content is low, irregular block-like curcumin particles and spherical PVP particles are simultaneously observed in the system, suggesting that the coprecipitation process did not proceed smoothly, and the two solutes precipitated separately. This phenomenon is likely due to the substantial solubility difference between curcumin and PVP in the mixed solvent, leading to a large disparity in their supersaturation ratios (initial concentration/solubility) and preventing simultaneous precipitation. Therefore, determining an appropriate mixed solvent ratio is crucial. As the acetone volume fraction increases, as shown in Figure b–d, the block-like structure of curcumin gradually disappears, indicating its effective dispersion within the PVP matrix. This phenomenon can be attributed to the fact that an increase in acetone content reduces the solubility of PVP while enhancing that of curcumin. Consequently, the supersaturation ratios of both solutes become more comparable when mixed with supercritical CO2. The coprecipitate retains the morphological characteristics of the polymer, consistent with previous studies on PVP-drug systems, ,, suggesting that the precipitation process is predominantly governed by PVP. Furthermore, there is a significant decrease in the particle size of the coprecipitates, from 1537 to 397 nm, achieving a transition from micron-scale to submicron-scale. This notable change in particle size is primarily due to the influence of solvent mixture composition on the SAS process, involving mechanisms such as altered solvation capacity and the rapid pressure change upon mixing the solution with SC–CO2. Studies have shown that both ethanol and acetone exhibit similar rapid pressure transition behaviors upon mixing. When the acetone content increases to 90%, the particle size increases to 602 nm. This phenomenon can be attributed to the repulsive effect of acetone on polymer molecules, leading to a more compact structural arrangement, , Additionally, excessive acetone content promotes the aggregation of more PVP molecules, as further confirmed by the clustered spherical particles observed in Figure e.

Effect of Curcumin/PVP Mass Ratio

In the second set of coprecipitation experiments­(runs 8–12), the fixed experimental conditions are pressure at 14 MPa, temperature at 328 K, total solution concentration at 100 mg/mL, and Ac/EtOH volume ratio at 7:3. By systematically adjusting the curcumin/PVP mass ratio­(from 2:1 to 1:8), the influence of the curcumin/PVP mass ratio on particle size and distribution characteristics was systematically studied.

The experimental results reveal that when the curcumin/PVP mass ratio is 2:1, as illustrated in Figure a, the obtained particles possess a block-like, nonuniform structure with a relatively large size (1560 nm) This is likely due to the incomplete coprecipitation caused by the high curcumin content. As the curcumin/PVP mass ratio was gradually reduced to 1:6, the particle size exhibited a trend of initially decreasing and then increasing, dropping to 337 nm before gradually rising to 515 nm­(transitioning from the micron to the submicron scale). Concurrently, the particle morphology transitioned to a spherical structure, as illustrated in Figure b–d. This phenomenon aligns with previous findings on the coprecipitation of PVP with various pharmaceutical compounds, ,, , Despite a constant total solution concentration, the higher relative content of PVP likely increases the viscosity of the system, which in turn reduces the nucleation rate and facilitates the formation of smaller and more aggregated particles. − Furthermore, the increase in PVP concentration affects the interfacial tension at the particle–fluid interface, a parameter that has been demonstrated to play a critical role in controlling particle size. Notably, when the curcumin/PVP mass ratio is further reduced to 1:8, as illustrated in Figure e, the particle size exhibits a substantial increase, reaching approximately 1116 nm, likely due to excessive PVP concentration causing a significant rise in solution viscosity, leading to large-scale particle aggregation. Therefore, optimizing the curcumin/PVP mass ratio is crucial for controlling particle morphology and size distribution.

Effect of Pressure

In the third set of coprecipitation experiments (runs 13–17) of this study, the effect of pressure on the particle size and distribution characteristics of coprecipitated particles was systematically studied. The fixed experimental conditions are temperature at 328 K, total solution concentration at 100 mg/mL, Ac/EtOH volume ratio at 7:3, and curcumin/PVP mass ratio at 1:2. To explore the effect of pressure on particle characteristics, the operating pressure was incrementally adjusted from 8 to 16 MPa in the experiment, and the step pressure parameter optimization was realized.

The experimental results, as shown in Figure a–e, demonstrate that changes in operating pressure significantly affect particle morphology and size distribution. As the pressure increased from 8 to 14 MPa, the morphology of the coprecipitated particles transitioned from irregular block-like structures to regular spherical shapes, accompanied by a notable reduction in particle size. Under the condition of 14 MPa, the particles exhibited the smallest average size, measured at approximately 338 nm. However, a further increase in operating pressure to 16 MPa resulted in an unexpected enlargement of particle size. Notably, across the entire pressure range, no significant differences in particle size distribution were observed.This phenomenon can be attributed to the thermodynamic and kinetic properties of the SAS system. As the operating pressure increases, the density of the antisolvent (SC–CO2) significantly rises, promoting higher mixing intensity and mass transfer rates within the system, thereby leading to the production of finer particles. This finding is consistent with previous studies on the preparation of PVP using the supercritical antisolvent method, , However, once the pressure surpasses a critical level, the continued increase in antisolvent density leads to a reduction in the diffusion rate of the organic solvent, thereby affecting mass transfer efficiency. This phenomenon has been theoretically supported and experimentally validated in the literature, , explaining the observed increase in particle size at 16 MPa.

Effect of Temperature

In the fourth set of coprecipitation experiments (runs 18–22), the fixed experimental conditions are pressure at 14 MPa, total solution concentration at 100 mg/mL, Ac/EtOH volume ratio at 7:3, and curcumin/PVP mass ratio at 1:2. By adjusting the temperature­(from 313 to 333 K), the influence of temperature on particle size and distribution characteristics was systematically studied. The experimental findings, as illustrated in Figure a–e, reveal the complex mechanisms by which temperature affects particle properties. The study demonstrates that as the temperature increases from 313 to 328 K, the morphology of the coprecipitated particles gradually transitions from irregular block-like structures to regular spherical shapes, accompanied by a significant reduction in particle size. At 328 K, the smallest average particle size, measured at approximately 354 nm, is achieved. However, a further increase in temperature to 333 K results in a noticeable enlargement of particle size. Additionally, the particle size distribution exhibits a correlation with the changes in particle size, specifically showing a narrowing distribution as the particle size decreases.

SEM characterization and particle size distribution of curcumin/PVP coprecipitated particles at different temperatures. (a) 313 K (run 18); (b) 318 K (run 19); (c) 323 K (run 20); (d) 328 K (run 21); (e) 333 K (run 22).

This complex temperature effect can be theoretically analyzed from several perspectives. First, according to supercritical fluid thermodynamics, in the SAS process, increasing the temperature causes a reduction in the density of SC–CO2. It typically results in a lower diffusion rate, which can slow down solute homogenization and, in the absence of sufficient convective mixing, contribute to spatial variations in supersaturation and nonuniform solute distribution. Theoretically favoring the formation of irregular particle morphologies and broader size distributions. However, the experimental observations significantly deviate from this theoretical prediction, particularly the formation of more regular spherical particles at 328 K. This anomaly may be attributed to the complex influence of temperature on the particle growth process: higher temperatures may promote particles toward a chemically stable state, resulting in more uniform morphological characteristics. Second, temperature elevation has a significant impact on the kinetic properties of the system. Specifically, higher temperatures increase the kinetic energy of solute molecules, which could enhance the frequency of collisions and accelerate particle aggregation. This mechanism can explain the observed increase in particle size at 333 K. Furthermore, temperature may influence the particle formation process through the following pathways,, , (1) altering the surface tension of the organic solvent,(2) modulating the solubility of the solute in the mixed solvent system, and(3) affecting the competition between nucleation and growth rates. The results of this study corroborate previous SAS coprecipitation studies using PVP as a carrier, − further confirming the intricate effect of temperature on particle properties in the SAS process.

Effect of Solution Concentration

In the fifth set of coprecipitation experiments (runs 23–27), the fixed experimental conditions are pressure at 14 MPa, temperature at 328 K, Ac/EtOH volume ratio at 7:3, and curcumin/PVP mass ratio at 1:2. The impact of concentration on particle size and distribution was systematically examined by adjusting the total solution concentration from 25 mg/mL to 125 mg/mL. According to the data in Table , as the solution concentration increased from 25 mg/mL to 100 mg/mL, the particle size gradually decreased, with the morphology and size distribution illustrated in Figure . This phenomenon may be interpreted based on the classical nucleation–growth theory, which suggests that under low drug concentration conditions, the diffusion of SC–CO2 into the solution system results in relatively low supersaturation. Thereby leading to a slower nucleation rate, where crystal growth dominates.

SEM characterization and particle size distribution of curcumin/PVP coprecipitated particles at different solution concentrations. (a) 25 mg/mL (run 24); (b) 50 mg/mL (run 25); (c) 75 mg/mL (run 26); (d) 100 mg/mL (run 27); (e) 125 mg/mL (run 28).

However, as the mass concentration of the solution increases, the time required for the system to reach the supersaturated state is significantly shortened, which facilitates the occurrence of multiple nucleation processes, consequently in the generation of smaller particles at higher drug concentrations. An increase in particle size is evident when the solution concentration is increased to 125 mg/mL. The increase in concentration induces the transition from a single-phase to a two-phase system by altering both thermodynamic equilibrium­(solubility) and kinetic processes­(interfacial tension relaxation). At concentration increase to 125 mg/mL SAS process could have been rendered from one phase to a two-phase regime, when surface tension between solvent and antisolvent vanished more slowly than the jet breaks, and nucleation is started within the instantaneous droplets of the solvent phase. In a two-phase regime, particle size is governed by the size of these droplet, so they are usually large, several microns. Particles obtained in a one-phase regime are usually much smaller.

Notably, through multifactor comparative analysis (Figure ), it was found that among the various factors influencing particle size, the effect of solution concentration is relatively minor. SEM characterization results reveal that coprecipitated particles obtained under different concentration conditions exhibit relatively regular spherical morphologies, indicating that an appropriate concentration range does not significantly affect particle morphology. This is consistent with several published studies. ,

Characterization of XRD

Multiple analytical methods were applied to systematically examine the physical and chemical properties of the samples. In addition to using SEM to characterize the surface morphology of the samples, XRD was utilized to investigate their crystalline behavior. As shown in Figure , the XRD results reveal that untreated curcumin raw material exhibits typical crystalline structural features, with multiple distinct diffraction peaks detected. The curcumin sample treated solely by the SAS technique­(run 1) retains the characteristic peak positions of the original curcumin. However, the significant decline in diffraction peaks intensities implies that the SAS process alters the crystallinity of curcumin to some extent.

XRD patterns of raw materials and processed samples.

As a control, the XRD pattern of the unprocessed PVP exhibits features typical of an amorphous structure, with no distinct diffraction peaks observed, consistent with the structural properties of PVP as an amorphous polymer. The XRD pattern of the physical mixture (PM 1:3) shows that while all characteristic diffraction peaks of curcumin are retained, the intensities are significantly lower compared to curcumin alone, attributed to the presence of amorphous PVP. This suggests that no significant phase transition or crystalline transformation occurs during the physical mixing process. Notably, all samples prepared via coprecipitation (runs 8, 9, 10) exhibit entirely amorphous characteristics, with XRD patterns similar to that of raw PVP and no detectable characteristic diffraction peaks of curcumin. This phenomenon can be attributed to the effective encapsulation of curcumin molecules by PVP during the coprecipitation process, forming a molecular-level dispersion system that inhibits the formation of curcumin crystals. The formation of such amorphous formulations provides favorable conditions to improve the bioavailability and solubility of curcumin.

Characterization of DSC

DSC was employed to systematically characterize the crystallization behavior of the samples and the interaction between curcumin and PVP. The DSC analysis results are shown in Figure . The DSC curve of raw curcumin displays a distinct endothermic peak at 182 °C, which corresponds to its melting point (T m). This result is consistent with the melting characteristics of crystalline curcumin reported in the literature. Contrasting with curcumin, the DSC profile of raw PVP does not present a sharp melting peak; instead, it exhibits a distinct glass transition temperature (T g) at 168 °C, consistent with its amorphous structural characteristics. Notably, in the DSC curve of the PM 1:3, the melting peak of curcumin shifts slightly toward lower temperatures. This phenomenon aligns with results reported in the literature , and can be attributed to the solvation effect of PVP, where weak interactions between PVP molecules and curcumin cause slight perturbations in the crystalline structure.

DSC thermograms of raw materials and processed samples.

For the coprecipitated sample obtained from run 9, no distinct endothermic peak is detected within the characteristic melting temperature range of curcumin. This finding corroborates the XRD results, confirming that the SAS process successfully transformed curcumin into an amorphous state. Additionally, the coprecipitated sample exhibits only a single glass transition temperature, and this T g value is lower than that of raw PVP. This phenomenon supports the hypothesis that the sample forms a single-phase system. The reduction in T g can be attributed to the plasticizing effect resulting from the uniform dispersion of curcumin molecules within the PVP matrix. , Specifically, the incorporation of small drug molecules enhances the mobility of polymer chains, thereby lowering the glass transition temperature of the system.

Characterization of FTIR

FTIR spectroscopy was employed to characterize the samples and identify potential interactions between curcumin and PVP. The results of the characterization are presented in Figure . The FTIR spectrum of raw curcumin exhibits numerous characteristic absorption bands, consistent with its complex molecular structure. The prominent absorption band at 3504 cm–1 is due to the O–H stretching vibration of phenolic hydroxyl groups in curcumin molecules. The absorption peak at 1507 cm–1 arises from the CC skeletal vibration of aromatic rings. The absorption band at 1277 cm–1 is associated with the C–O stretching vibration of enol groups. The absorption peak at 1023 cm–1 corresponds to the vibration mode of C–O–C bonds. And the absorption bands at 959 cm–1 and 809 cm–1 originate from the vibration modes of trans-CH in benzoate and CH in alkenes, respectively. These characteristic peaks align well with results reported in the literature, , confirming the chemical structure of curcumin.

FTIR spectra of raw materials and processed samples.

Notably, the FTIR spectrum of the PM 1:3 essentially represents a simple superposition of the respective spectra of curcumin and PVP, with no significant changes in the positions or intensities of characteristic peaks. This indicates that no substantial intermolecular interactions occur between curcumin and PVP during the physical mixing process. However, for the curcumin/PVP samples treated by the SAS process (runs 4, 9, 12), the FTIR spectra exhibit significant changes: the characteristic O–H stretching vibration peak of curcumin (3504 cm–1) completely disappears, while the position of the CO characteristic peak of PVP shifts. This phenomenon is likely due to the establishment of hydrogen bonding interactions between the O–H functionalities in curcumin and the CO moieties in PVP. This finding is highly consistent with results reported in the literature, ,− providing critical insights into the molecular mechanism underlying the transformation of curcumin from crystalline to amorphous form. The FTIR analysis results demonstrate that the SAS process induces specific intermolecular interactions between curcumin and PVP, which may be a key factor in inhibiting the formation of a crystalline structure of curcumin.

The findings indicated that the cumulative release profiles of curcumin and PM 1:3 within 1 h were 11.9% and 14.7%, respectively. PM 1:3 showed a relatively faster drug release rate, which could be attributed to the presence of PVP reducing the surface tension of the dissolution medium, thereby enhancing the surface wettability of curcumin, indirectly leading to the improvement of curcumin solubility. The release rate of curcumin after SAS treatment can reach 40.3%, primarily attributed to the reduction in particle size and the corresponding increase in interfacial surface area. The curcumin/PVP coprecipitate prepared by SAS method showed a very high release rate, and the three groups of samples tested all reached about 100% curcumin release rate within 10 min, showing its excellent dissolution performance. Comprehensive analysis showed that the SAS process promoted the formation of coprecipitation between curcumin and PVP, and the composite particles with excellent dissolution characteristics were generated through the heterogeneous nucleation mechanism, which successfully achieved the goal of this study.

Conclusions

In this study, successful coprecipitation of curcumin with PVP was accomplished through the SAS technique employing a custom-designed coaxial adjustable annular gap nozzle system. The effects of key process parameters such as pressure, temperature, curcumin/PVP mass ratio, Ac/EtOH volume ratio, and total solute concentration on the morphological characteristics and particle size distribution of the coprecipitate were systematically investigated. The results show that optimal conditions­(14 MPa, 328 K, 100 mg/mL total concentration, 7:3 Ac/EtOH ratio, and 1:2 curcumin/PVP ratio) enabled the production of submicron-sized­(337 ± 47 nm) amorphous composite particles. Comprehensive characterization by SEM, XRD, DSC, FTIR, and HPLC confirmed the successful formation of homogeneous coprecipitates with modified physicochemical properties. These findings confirm the great potential of the SAS method in the production of polymer-drug composite systems, especially in improving the solubility and bioavailability of active drug components with poor water solubility such as curcumin, while maintaining the environmental advantages of supercritical fluid technology. Industrial implementation of SAS technologies faces major challenges from demanding equipment needs and deteriorating product consistency during scale-up, compounded by difficult solvent recovery. The developed nozzle system and process optimization strategy provide a viable pathway for overcoming current limitations in supercritical particle engineering for pharmaceutical applications.
