Insomnia and sleep duration for kidney function: Mendelian randomization study
Authors: Yang Zhang, Zhaozhong Zhong, Zuofu Tang, Ruojiao Wang, Jiaqing Wu, Ning Na, Jinhua Zhang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesExtensive researches highlight the detrimental impact of sleep disorders such as insomnia and insufficient sleep duration on kidney function. However, establishing a clear causal relationship between insomnia, sleep duration, and kidney function remains challenging. This study aims to estimate this relationship using Mendelian randomization (MR).MethodsIndependent genetic variants strongly associated with insomnia (N = 462,341) and sleep duration (N = 460,099) were selected as instrumental variables from corresponding genome-wide association studies (GWAS). Kidney function parameters, including serum creatinine, estimated glomerular filtration rate by cystatin C (eGFRcys), acute renal failure (ARF), chronic renal failure (CRF), kidney injury molecule-1, neutrophil gelatinase associated lipocalin, microalbuminuria, cystatin C, and β2 microglobulin, were derived from GWAS databases. A two-sample MR study was conducted to assess the causal relationship between sleep disorders and kidney function, and multivariable MR was used to identify potential mediators. The inverse-variance weighted was used as the primary estimate.ResultsMR analysis found robust evidence indicating that insomnia and short sleep duration were associated with an increased risk of elevated serum creatinine, regardless of adjusting for obesity. Causal links between sleep duration and eGFRcys or cystatin C were also identified. While genetically predicted insomnia and sleep duration were found to potentially impact ARF, CRF, microalbuminuria, and β2 microglobulin, the p-values in multivariable MR analysis became nonsignificant. No pleiotropy was detected.ConclusionsThis study demonstrates a causal impact of insomnia on the risk of elevated serum creatinine and a positive effect of sleep duration on serum creatinine, eGFRcys, and cystatin C. Our findings also suggest their potential indirect effects on ARF, CRF, microalbuminuria, and β2 microglobulin mediated by obesity.Keywords: Kidney function, Mendelian randomization, insomnia, sleep duration

Introduction

Kidney disease stands as one of the most important public concerns globally, contributing significantly to both morbidity and mortality [1]. The disruption of renal homeostasis and function by both acute kidney injury (AKI)/disease and chronic kidney disease (CKD) imposes a substantial burden on the global population, markedly diminishing the quality of life for affected individuals. It is reported that the incidence of impaired kidney function, marked by reduced glomerular filtration rates (eGFR) and albuminuria, reaches approximately 12.7% in Germany [2]. Notably, AKI frequently doesn’t recover and ultimately progresses to CKD [3], forming a continuum from AKI to CKD [4]. Furthermore, whether in its chronic or acute form, diminished kidney function eventually advances to renal failure, necessitating kidney replacement interventions like dialysis or kidney transplantation. Various risk factors associated with acute and chronic kidney function impairment have been identified [5]. Acute kidney disease is often associated with factors such as sepsis and nephrotoxic drugs [6–8], while the leading risk factors for CKD include systematic diseases such as diabetes [9], hypertension [10], and obesity [11,12]. In a noteworthy development, sleep-related issues have been reported in recent years as potential risk factors for kidney dysfunction, given their influence on the autonomic nervous system, vascular function, and key regulators of renal homeostasis [13]. Poor sleep quality or insomnia affects approximately half of the patients with advanced CKD [14]. Studies have reported that both extreme nighttime sleep duration and poor sleep quality can increase the risk of CKD. Conversely, optimal sleep duration and better sleep quality may be beneficial for patients with CKD [15].

Sleep is pivotal for maintaining optimal physical and mental homeostasis, acting as a foundational pillar of health. The importance of sleep is increasingly recognized in public health strategies and clinical practice [16]. However, the rapid growth, development, diversification of work, and the need to maintain high productivity in recent decades have often come at the cost of sleep, ultimately leading to a decline in the quality of life [17,18]. Unfortunately, sleep disorders can exert deleterious effects on various physiological systems, including the cardiovascular system, immunity, hormone regulation, and mental well-being, becoming a common public health problem in modern society [19]. Insomnia or sleeplessness, the most prevalent sleep disorder, is characterized by difficulty in falling or staying asleep [20]. Recent data indicate up to 50% of primary care patients are affected by insomnia, with 37.5% patients experiencing persistence insomnia over a 5-year follow-up period [21,22]. Moreover, studies have linked chronic insomnia to a rapid loss of kidney function, elevating the risk of both the development and progression of CKD [23]. Sleep deficiency, marked by inadequate sleep duration, is another widespread issue in modern society [24]. Approximately 35% of American adults sleep less than 7 h per night, and 25%–30% report 6 or fewer hours per 24 h [24,25]. A cross-sectional study involving 0.5 million Chinese adults found that 23% of these people get <6 h of sleep [26]. Notably, short sleep duration has been associated with impaired kidney function, as reported in a prospective cohort study showing a higher odds ratio for impaired renal function among those with less sleep time [27]. However, insomnia and sleep duration are considered as common lifestyle factors whose impact on kidney function may be underestimated. Particularly, with insomnia and sleep deprivation becoming increasingly prevalent, understanding their relationship with kidney health is of paramount importance as it could be one of the potential interventions to improve kidney health. The causal relationship between insomnia, sleep duration, and kidney function, as well as the potential positive effects of sleep disorder treatment on deteriorating kidney function, still remain largely unknown.

Mendelian randomization (MR), an innovative analysis method utilizing genetic variants as instrumental variables, offers advantages in mitigating potential limitations such as confounding and various biases [28]. MR provides robust evidence for causal inferences between exposures and outcomes. In this study, two-sample MR (TSMR) was employed to analyze data from genome-wide association studies (GWAS), aiming to estimate the causal effects of insomnia and sleep duration on kidney function.

Materials and methods

The study’s analytical process overview is depicted in Figure 1. As all the data of exposure and outcome were sourced from large and well-established consortia, there is no need for ethics approval.

Overview of the study’s analytical process.

Kidney function is always measured as the clearance of small molecules such as creatinine and urea [29]. Serum creatinine, the clinical surrogate for eGFR, is the key criterion to AKI, defined as a 50% increase in serum creatinine over a week or >26 μmol/L increase per 48 h [30]. Additionally, kidney injury molecule-1 (KIM-1) [31] and neutrophil gelatinase associated lipocalin (NGAL) [32] have been considered as accurate candidate markers to predict acute kidney dysfunction. Microalbuminuria is associated with an abnormal glomerular capillary permeability to protein, reflecting the kidney dysfunction and damage [33]. Cystatin C and β2-microglobulin are also widely accepted as markers for changes in kidney function. Given these factors, serum creatinine, eGFR by cystatin C (eGFRcys), acute renal failure (ARF), chronic renal failure (CRF), KIM-1, NGAL, microalbuminuria, cystatin C, and β2 microglobulin were selected as outcomes representing kidney function. The data for insomnia, sleep duration, and outcomes were collected online, with detailed characteristics provided in Table 1.

GWAS data for exposures

Summary statistics data of insomnia and sleep duration were obtained from UK Biobank. Genetic variants related to insomnia were retrieved from 9,851,867 single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) of 462,341 European samples in UK Biobank. The data of sleep duration was obtained from 460,099 European individuals including both genders, of which the mean sleep time is about 7 h, and short sleep time refers to <7 h. Weak instrument bias is of practical importance for the design and analysis of MR studies, which increase as the expected F-statistic decreases [34]. The criteria for genetic instruments that are strongly associated with the exposure were consequently set as GWAS-correlated p-value <5 × 10−8 which is considered as genome-wide significant threshold, F statistic >10 for excluding weak SNPs, and linkage disequilibrium (LD) r2<0.01 with 10,000 kb clumping window to retain independent SNPs. In addition, we searched the PhenoScanner database for genotype–phenotype associations of the SNPs to confirm that the SNPs themselves were unlikely to directly influence the outcome [35].

GWAS data for outcomes

GWAS summary data for serum creatinine was extracted from 118,460 samples in CKDGen consortium. Data for ARF and CRF were acquired from the IEU Open GWAS with 482,266 European individuals (7,695 cases and 474,571 controls) and 482,858 samples (8,287 cases and 474,571 controls), respectively. KIM-1 data included 21,758 European samples. Genetic instruments of NGAL came from 3,301 individuals. The samples of eGFRcys data were 460,826 participants and cystatin C data were from UKB database 389,834 individuals. Microalbuminuria comprised 54,116 samples from the CKDGen consortium.

GWAS data for risk factors

It has been widely accepted that diabetes, hypertension, and obesity are the most common risk factors of chronic kidney function impairment [5]. GWAS summary data for diabetes were collected from 484,598 samples (24,659 cases and 24,659 controls). Summary statistics for hypertension were obtained from 129,909 cases and 354,689 control samples. Genetic instruments for obesity were derived from 463,010 European individuals via IEU Open GWAS.

MR analysis

The inverse-variance weighted (IVW) is the primary method in TSMR with its advantage in minimizing variance [36]. Therefore, after harmonization analysis of the GWASs between exposures and outcomes or risk factors, we applied the IVW as the primary MR approach to estimate their causal relationship. The associations between exposures and the most common risk factors were analyzed to test the assumption of ‘no association between instrumental variable and confounder’. The assumption of ‘no horizontal pleiotropy’ is the fundamental rule of MR analysis. These SNPs with pleiotropic can bring biased results and loss of statistical power in IVW analysis, so the MR Pleiotropy RESidual Sum and Outlier (MR PRESSO) was performed to select optimal SNPs via removing the underlying outliers [37]. Besides, considering the character of more robust estimates in a broader set of scenarios despite less efficient, MR-Egger [38] and Weighted Median [39] were used as complement methods.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis is a vital part of TSMR in identifying the potential pleiotropy and heterogeneity. Cochran’s Q test [40], MR-Egger regression test, and MR PRESSO were applied for detecting the heterogeneity or pleiotropy. If the heterogeneity was present, the IVW with multiplicative random effects model was used for MR estimates [41]. In addition, leave-one-out analysis was performed to assess single SNP sensitivity to MR results.

Multivariable MR analysis

To understand whether insomnia and sleep duration exerts indirect effects via confounders or risk factors, the causal association between exposures and risk factors, including diabetes, hypertension, and obesity, was analyzed. If exposures had the relationship with risk factors, the multivariable MR analysis, a recent extension of MR, was conducted to identify the effect of each factor on outcomes [42]. This method can make clear whether insomnia and sleep duration have a true causality relationship with outcomes.

Statistics

All analyses utilized the TwoSampleMR package (version 0.5.6) [43] and MR PRESSO package (version 1.0) [37] in R project (version 3.6.1).

Results

MR analysis between insomnia and kidney function

A total of 42 SNPs genetically related to insomnia were identified (Supplementary Figure 1A and Table 1). Following harmonization and MR PRESSO analysis of causal relationship between insomnia and serum creatinine, three SNPs, being palindromic with intermediate allele frequencies, and two outlier SNPs were removed. MR analysis with IVW indicated a potential association between insomnia and serum creatinine with statistical significance (OR = 1.04, 95%CI: 1.00 − 1.08, p = 0.030, FDR p = 0.016), suggesting impact of insomnia on the risk of increasing serum creatinine. MR-Egger and Weight median also showed a significant causal relationship. MR analysis of the final selected 30 SNPs revealed evidence of a causal association between insomnia and eGFRcys (OR = 0.98, 95%CI: 0.95 − 0.99, p = 0.047, FDR p = 0.017) using IVW method. MR evidence with IVW suggested a causal effect of insomnia on ARF (OR = 1.89, 95%CI: 1.14 − 3.13, p = 0.014, FDR p = 0.016) and CRF (OR = 1.63, 95%CI: 1.03 − 2.59, p = 0.038, FDR p = 0.017) with final selected 39 SNPs. In terms of the biomarkers of kidney dysfunction, only the risk of microalbuminuria (OR = 2.85, 95%CI: 1.19 − 6.80, p = 0.018, FDR p = 0.016) and cystatin C (OR = 1.14, 95%CI: 1.00 − 1.29, p = 0.042, FDR p = 0.017) were found to increase with insomnia (Figure 2A). Scatter plots illustrating the MR analysis, depicting the causal association trends between genetically predicted levels of insomnia and kidney function, are presented in Figure 3. No evidence was found for the relationship between insomnia and KIM-1 (IVW: p = 0.605, FDR p = 0.139), NGAL (IVW: p = 0.406, FDR p = 0.097), or β2 microglobulin (IVW: p = 0.083, FDR p = 0.088) with all three MR methods (Supplementary Table 3 and Figure 2).

MR analysis between sleep duration and kidney function

There were 71 SNPs identified to genetically predict sleep duration (Supplementary Figure 1B and Table 2). MR results of selected 52 SNPs, after harmonization and MR PRESSO analysis, revealed a protect role of sleep duration against the risk of serum creatinine increase in IVW algorithm (OR = 0.96, 95%CI: 0.93 − 0.98, p = 0.002, FDR p = 0.003). MR analysis suggested the positive effect of sleep duration on eGFRcys (OR = 1.02, 95%CI: 1.00 − 1.03, p = 0.011, FDR p = 0.015). IVW analysis indicated significant causal effects of sleep duration on ARF (OR = 0.58, 95%CI: 0.38 − 0.88, p = 0.011, FDR p = 0.007) and CRF (OR = 0.66, 95%CI: 0.46 − 0.94, p = 0.023, FDR p = 0.010). In addition, sleep duration displayed an effective association with microalbuminuria (OR = 0.52, 95%CI: 0.27 − 0.96, p = 0.036, FDR p = 0.013), cystatin C (OR = 0.88, 95%CI: 0.81 − 0.96, p = 0.002, FDR p = 0.003), and β2 microglobulin (OR = 0.22, 95%CI: 0.05 − 0.97, p = 0.045, FDR p = 0.014) based on IVW results (Figure 2B). Scatter plots for MR analysis are displayed in Figure 4, illustrating the causal association trends between genetically predicted levels of sleep duration with kidney function. However, similar to insomnia’s results, there is no evidence for significant effects of sleep duration on KIM-1 (IVW: p = 0.583, FDR p = 0.127) and NGAL (IVW: p = 0.226, FDR p = 0.054) (Supplementary Table 4 and Figure 3).

Sensitivity analysis

In this study, the Cochran’s Q test, MR-Egger regression test, leave-one-out plot, and funnel plot were performed. MR-Egger regression test results showed no horizontal pleiotropy (Supplementary Table 5). Cochran’s Q test indicated the heterogeneity between insomnia and NGAL (Q = 56.25, p = 0.028), cystatin C (Q = 23.01, p = 0.010) (Supplementary Table 6). Therefore, IVW with multiplicative random effects model was applied to NGAL and cystatin C, and IVW with fixed effects model was used in other outcomes. Heterogeneity was also found between sleep duration and serum creatinine (Q = 72.34, p = 0.026), cystatin C (Q = 134.84, p = 0.006). The consistency of the results is shown in Figures 5 and 6 and Supplementary Figure 4, 5 via leave-one-out analyses, which can identify robustness of the results. Analysis of risk factors and multivariable MR analysis

The TSMR analysis between exposures (insomnia and sleep duration) and risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, and obesity) revealed no direct effects of exposures on diabetes and hypertension (Table 3). However, relationships were found between exposures and obesity (insomnia: OR = 1.01, 95%CI: 1.00 − 1.02, p = 0.011, FDR p = 0.016; sleep duration: OR = 0.99, 95%CI: 0.98 − 1.00, p = 0.049, FDR p = 0.015), suggesting obesity as a potential confounder of insomnia and sleep duration. Therefore, we applied multivariable MR analysis to adjust for obesity. As a result, the evidence of causal association between insomnia and eGFRcys, ARF, CRF, microalbuminuria as well as cystatin C became nonsignificant. Similarly, there were no causal effects of sleep duration on ARF, CRF, microalbuminuria, cystatin C, and β2 microglobulin. Interestingly, even after adjusting for obesity, the causal relationship between insomnia (p = 0.035), sleep duration (p = 0.043), and serum creatinine remained robust. Furthermore, consistent evidence supported a causal association between sleep duration and both eGFRcys (p = 0.025) and cystatin C (p = 0.039) (Table 4).

Discussion

Our study employed TSMR to investigate the causal links between insomnia, sleep duration, and a range of kidney function markers, including serum creatinine, eGFRcys, ARF, CRF, KIM-1, NGAL, microalbuminuria, cystatin C, and β2 microglobulin. Our findings indicate that insomnia and short sleep duration increase the risk of elevated serum creatinine. Furthermore, our study provides robust evidence that adequate or longer sleep duration has a protective effect on both eGFRcys and cystatin C. Intriguingly, a potential association of these sleep disturbances with ARF, CRF, microalbuminuria, and β2 microglobulin emerged. However, its significant results disappeared when adjusting for obesity, suggesting the potential mediating role of obesity in linking insomnia or sleep duration to ARF, CRF, microalbuminuria, and β2 microglobulin.

Numerous research attests to the detrimental impact of sleep disorders on kidney function [44]. Studies reported that insomnia tends to be chronic and can increase the risk of medical [45] and psychiatric [46] morbidity and mortality [47], including kidney function impairment. A cross-sectional study of Han-Chinese women in peri–post menopause emphasizes the adverse effects of sleep disturbance on kidney function, highlighting the importance of sleep to postmenopausal people [48]. Similarly, Manabu Kadoya et al. reported that the risk for renal outcome in diabetic patients, eGFR decline to <60 mL/min/1.73 m2 for more than 3 months, can be enhanced by poor sleep quality [49], which can be induced by insomnia. A Japanese prospective cohort study conducted by Sachiko Sasaki et al. highlighted the correlation between awakening at night, a manifestation of insomnia, and an elevated risk of CKD [50]. Investigation on American veterans, Jun Ling Lu et al. claimed the association of chronic insomnia with higher risk of insufficiency and rapid loss of kidney function [23]. Our study aligns with these findings, substantiating the detrimental impact of insomnia on declining kidney function. Regarding sleep duration, another vital item for sleep quality, our results underscore a causal relationship between shorter sleep durations and an elevated risk of serum creatinine. The kidney, influenced by circadian rhythms and its 24-h cycle, exhibits sensitivity to sleep duration variations. Notably, short sleep durations (<6 h) are associated with the risk of renal hyperfiltration, which is independent of diabetes, hypertension, and obesity [51]. A large sample study also displayed the association of short duration with rapid decline in kidney function, finding that the odds ratios of people with ≤5 h sleep per night were 1.91, participants with 6 h sleep were 1.30 when compared to people sleeping 7–8 h one night [27], which is recommended as the optimal sleep time for lowest all-cause mortality and health [52–54]. Singapore Chinese health study also found that the risk of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) in people with ≤5 h sleep per day was increased compared to those with 7 h sleep duration per day [55]. Noteworthy, our MR results suggested potential benefits of longer sleep duration on kidney function, but caution is advised. McMullan et al. claimed no significant increased risk of kidney function loss for ≥9 h sleep per night [27]. Conflicting findings proposed that overlong sleep per day may increase the risk of kidney function impairment [51,56], ESRD [54], and all-cause mortality in U or J shaped pattern [52,57]. In addition, our TSMR revealed the causal effects of both insomnia and sleep duration on microalbuminuria and cystatin C, rather than on classical renal tubular injury biomarkers KIM-1 and NAGL, suggesting a potential predilection of the two sleep disorders for glomerular injury. This is in line with a population-based cohort study, which identifies short sleep duration as independently a risk factor for the progression of proteinuria [58].

Established risk factors such as diabetes, hypertension, and obesity play pivotal roles in kidney dysfunction. Diabetes contributes to small blood vessel damage and nephropathy independently of hyperglycemia [59]. The role of hypertension in accelerating irreversible kidney dysfunction, development of CKD, and eventual ESRD is widely accepted [60]. The pathologic kidney damage induced by hypertension involves arteriosclerosis, tubular atrophy, global glomerulosclerosis, interstitial fibrosis, and nephrosclerosis [61]. Obesity, recognized as a global health issue, is identified as a driver of kidney diseases, with proposed mechanisms including oxidative stress, inflammation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and lipotoxicity [62]. Chronic metabolic abnormalities in obese individuals lead to gradual nephron loss and kidney function reduction [63]. Therefore, we performed MR analysis to make sure whether sleep disorders were related with these factors. The MR analysis did not reveal significant associations of sleep disorders with diabetes and hypertension. However, a statistically significant relationship was found with obesity, suggesting a potential mediating role of obesity between sleep disorders and kidney outcomes. Over the past decade, the link between sleep problems and weight gain/obesity has gained significant attention. A systematic review found that short sleep duration (<5 or 6 h per day) could increase the incidence of obesity by 38% [54]. This association arises from various mechanisms, including uncontrolled energy intake and imbalances in appetite hormones. A meta-analysis revealed that sleep insufficient exerts significant influence on brain activity in response to food stimuli, leading to an average increase intake of 252.8 more kcal/day [64], particularly favoring high-carbohydrate foods, fats, sugar-sweetened beverages, and alcohol [65–67]. Furthermore, sleep deprivation can increase ghrelin levels and decrease leptin levels, resulting in enhanced appetite and an increased risk of obesity [68]. To further explore potential mediating factors, a multivariable MR analysis was conducted, adjusting for obesity. Interestingly, this analysis weakened the causal association between sleep disorders and ARF, CRF, and microalbuminuria. However, a consistently robust causal link persisted between insomnia or sleep duration and serum creatinine, as well as between sleep duration and both eGFRcys and cystatin C.

Numerous scholars have made contributions to investigating the effects of sleep disorders on kidney function, but the precise mechanisms remain elusive. During normal sleep, autonomic nervous system exhibits enhanced vagal and reduced sympathetic activity. Plasma renin and aldosterone follow a 24-h cycle, with increased secretion during sleep [69]. Based on the previous studies, it is proposed that sleep disorders stimulate the sympathetic nervous system [13], leading to higher nocturnal blood pressure [70]. The other statement is that sleep deprivation is implicated in dysregulation of the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system [13]. Sleep disorders represent a significant public health concern worldwide, contributing to a range of physical health issues. Studies have demonstrated that sleep disturbances, particularly short sleep time (<6 h), can lead to a decline in kidney glomerular function. This decline is estimated at an annualized rate of 1.2 mL/min/1.73 m2/year in eGFR for individuals sleeping 5 h or less per night and 0.9 mL/min/1.73 m2/year for those sleeping 6 h per night. Sleep questions are modifiable risk factors for kidney function. Therefore, improving sleep quality and increasing sleep duration are crucial for maintaining kidney health. The American National Sleep Foundation recommends 7–9 h of sleep for adults [71].

Several studies have employed MR to investigate the relationship between sleep deprivation and kidney diseases. Sehoon Park et al., utilizing data from the UK Biobank prospective cohort, conducted a clinical analysis focusing on sleep duration and CKD stages 3–5 [72]. Their study, leveraging the advantages of clinical data, revealed the adverse effect of short sleep duration (<6 h per 24 h) on kidney function, further supported by MR verification. Mohsen Mazidi et al. reported the potential harmful effects of longer sleep duration on eGFR and CKD, employing an LD threshold of r2 < 0.8, a distinct approach from other studies [73]. While Kaixin Li et al. performed MR studies between six sleep traits and ESRD (eGFR ≤ 15 mL/min per 1.73 m2), no strong evidence of causal association was found [74]. However, our current study differs significantly in several key aspects. Firstly, previous studies focused on a single kidney disease, whereas our study examined the two most common and impactful types of sleep disorders, insomnia and short sleep duration, and included a broader range of outcome phenotypes encompassing both acute and chronic kidney dysfunction, aiming to elucidate their effects on kidney dysfunction development. Secondly, despite utilizing data from similar European populations, the databases and SNP selection criteria varied across studies, making it possible to conduct stratification analyses in different research contexts. Thirdly, heterogeneity in regional populations and genetic testing practices can introduce variations between studies.

It is noteworthy that this study only selected the three most common factors as confounders among numerous risk factors for kidney function. Further research is needed to determine whether other factors might also act as confounders in this relationship. Besides, the present study’s inclusion of only the European population and its emphasis on establishing causal relationships, rather than mechanisms, limit its generalizability to other ethnic groups and a comprehensive understanding of pathophysiology. While population stratification inevitably leads to a loss of statistical power, it remains a limitation of this study that we were unable to stratify analyses to examine the associations in different participant subgroups. Therefore, further investigations are warranted to explore these intricate mechanisms and provide evidence of stratification.

Conclusion and future direction

In conclusion, our TSMR analysis reveals a causal association between insomnia or short sleep duration and an increased risk of elevated serum creatinine. Additionally, our findings suggest that short sleep duration negatively impacts eGFRcys and cystatin C. However, genetic evidence for causal effects on ARF, CRF, microalbuminuria, and β2 microglobulin is less robust when considering obesity, suggesting potential indirect effects mediated by obesity.

Given the prevalence of both kidney disease and sleep disorders as significant public health issues, our study provides compelling evidence to prioritize approaches for maintaining kidney function. This underscores the importance of prioritizing daily sleep habits and ensuring adequate sleep duration, acknowledging their profound impact on kidney health. It is reasonable to consider implementing routine screening and planning interventions for sleep disorders as part of comprehensive kidney management. Further research is imperative to delve into the intricate mechanisms underlying these associations, non-European populations and stratification analyses. These efforts would aim to: (1) identify specific pathology pathways and potential therapeutic targets between sleep disorders and kidney function and (2) determine the causal relationship in stratified subgroups, such as those with different stages of kidney function, and varying durations or severities of sleep disorders.
