Evaluation of the predictive accuracy of scoring systems for retrograde intrarenal surgery in a pediatric population

Background

Pediatric stone disease is endemic in many developing countries, and both its incidence and the need for surgical intervention are steadily increasing [1]. Advances in technology and the miniaturization of endoscopic equipment have significantly expanded the role of minimally invasive techniques for managing kidney stones in children. Among these, retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) has become an important alternative, especially as instrumentation has become smaller and more adaptable to the pediatric anatomy. Traditionally, the percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) approach has been considered superior in achieving higher stone-free rates (SFR) for large and lower pole stones [2, 3]. However, current literature indicates that flexible ureteroscopy provides comparable outcomes for stones up to 20 mm, with the advantage of lower morbidity in children [4–6].

Despite these advances, predicting surgical success in pediatric RIRS remains challenging. Stone size, anatomical features such as infundibulopelvic angle (IPA) and renal infundibulopelvic length (RIL), as well as the number and location of stones, all play critical roles in determining postoperative outcomes. For this reason, several scoring systems have been developed to help clinicians evaluate the likelihood of success and optimize patient selection. Among them, the Resorlu–Unsal Stone Score (RUSS) and the retrograde intrarenal surgery (R.I.R.S.) nomogram are the most widely studied [7, 8]. While both systems have been validated primarily in adult populations, their comparative accuracy in children is not well established — a clinically important gap, as pediatric anatomy and stone characteristics differ substantially from those of adults. Direct comparison of these two systems in a dedicated pediatric cohort may therefore inform more evidence-based preoperative counselling and patient selection. We hypothesized that the R.I.R.S. nomogram and RUSS would each demonstrate acceptable predictive accuracy for stone-free status after pediatric RIRS, and that their discriminative performances, as measured by AUC, could be formally compared. Therefore, the aim of this study was to evaluate and compare the predictive accuracy of the R.I.R.S. nomogram and RUSS in a single-center pediatric cohort undergoing RIRS.

Methods

Study design

In this single-center retrospective study, children aged 15 years and younger who underwent RIRS in our center between January 2016 and January 2024 were investigated. A total of 142 patients were initially screened; 51 were excluded (26 due to inaccessible computed tomography (CT) images, 17 due to prior minimally invasive treatment within six months, and 8 due to ureteral stenosis precluding stone access). A total of 91 patients were included in the final analysis. A descriptive statistical study was performed, with patients divided into two groups: those who were stone-free and those who were not stone-free after a single session of RIRS. CT measurements and records of predictive parameters were made by two experienced urologists (MC and AI). IPA was defined as the angle between the longitudinal axis of the renal infundibulum and the axis of the ureteropelvic junction, measured on coronal CT reconstructions. RIL was defined as the distance from the tip of the lower-pole infundibulum to the renal pelvis, measured on the same coronal plane. Interobserver agreement for IPA and RIL measurements between the two urologists was assessed using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), which demonstrated excellent reliability (ICC > 0.85 for both parameters). Then, based on these parameters, R.I.R.S. nomogram and the RUSS were calculated. All measurements and scores were reviewed by MSS, who has more than 15 years of experience in pediatric urology.

A written informed consent was obtained from all patients’ parents prior to undergoing RIRS. The study was approved by the local ethics committee (Approval No: 2025/0169) and conducted in accordance with the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki, as revised in 2013. All patients’ parents were provided with comprehensive information regarding the potential complications and advantages associated with RIRS. Being a retrospective study, no randomization was applied; however, all data were reviewed independently by two urologists (MC and AI) to reduce observer bias.

Data collection

A comprehensive data set encompassing patient demographics, preoperative stone dimensions, stone burden, and the extent of hydronephrosis, laterality, stone density, and anatomical measurements such as RIL and IPA were recorded. Although the Society of Fetal Urology (SFU) grading system was originally validated for ultrasonographic assessment, it was applied to CT imaging findings in this study to grade the severity of hydronephrosis, as CT was the primary preoperative imaging modality. The duration of hospital stay, operation time, postoperative complications, and the SFR four weeks after the initial operation were documented. The SFR was determined through the use of either a kidney–ureter–bladder plain X-ray or non-contrast CT, with a residual fragment of less than 2 mm in size being considered stone-free. This threshold was adopted in accordance with contemporary urolithiasis literature, as fragments ≤ 2 mm are generally considered clinically insignificant and expected to pass spontaneously [7, 8]. It should be noted that the use of two different postoperative imaging modalities introduces a degree of heterogeneity; plain X-ray is less sensitive than non-contrast CT for detecting small residual fragments, which may result in an underestimation of residual fragment rates in patients evaluated by KUB.

Operative details

Following the acquisition of sterile urine culture samples, patients were prepared for RIRS. Surgical procedures were performed by three endourologists, each with more than 5 years of experience. Using a 4.5 Fr – 6.5 Fr semirigid urethroscope, two 0.035 inch diameter hydrophilic guidewires were inserted into the ureter and diagnostic ureteroscopy was performed. Under fluoroscopic guidance a 7.5 Fr flexible ureteroscope (Flex-X2; Karl Storz GmbH, Tuttlingen, Germany) was then delivered to kidney through the guidewire. Access to the kidney was achieved by advancing the flexible ureteroscope under endoscopic visualization. Once the stone was reached, lithotripsy was performed using a 272-micron Holmium-YAG laser probe (Quanta system, Litho, Milan, Italy). At the end of the procedure, the JJ stent appropriate for the patient’s age and size was pushed over the guide and placed. Intraoperative stone clearance was assessed by systematic endoscopic inspection of all accessible calyces at the conclusion of lithotripsy, supplemented by fluoroscopic evaluation.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences version 25 for Windows (SPSS Inc., IBM, NY, USA). Normality tests were conducted using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Descriptive statistical methods, including mean, standard deviation, frequency, ratio, and percentage, were used to evaluate the study data. The two independent groups were compared using Mann Whitney U test and chi-square test. To identify independent predictors of residual fragments, univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression analyses were performed, and results were reported as odds ratios (OR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI). Variables with p < 0.05 in univariate analysis were entered into the multivariate model. ROC curve analysis was performed to evaluate the predictive accuracy of scoring systems, and AUC values with 95% CI were calculated. Formal statistical comparison of the two AUC values was performed using DeLong’s method. Given the retrospective nature of this study, a formal a priori power analysis was not conducted; however, the sample size of 91 patients with 38 events is consistent with the commonly used rule of at least 10 events per predictor variable for logistic regression. A statistically significant p-value was considered to be < 0.05.

Results

This study evaluated the predictive accuracy of scoring systems for RIRS in pediatric patients. A total of 91 patients were analyzed, with 53 achieving stone-free status and 38 having residual fragments. The demographic characteristics, including gender, age, family history, and surgical history, were not significantly different between the two groups (p > 0.05). However, preoperative hydronephrosis (p = 0.008), largest stone diameter (p < 0.001), sum of stone diameters (p < 0.001), and IPA (p < 0.001) showed significant differences (Table 1).

Regarding scoring systems, the R.I.R.S. and RUSS scores demonstrated variable sensitivity and specificity at different cut-off values. The R.I.R.S. score at a cut-off of ≤ 6 provided a sensitivity of 94.3% and a specificity of 73.7%, while a cut-off of ≤ 7 yielded a sensitivity of 100% but reduced specificity (52.6%). The RUSS score at a cut-off of ≤ 1 had a sensitivity of 90.6% and specificity of 68.4%, while a cut-off of ≤ 3 reached 100% sensitivity but very low specificity (18.4%) (Supplementary Table). Discussion

This study investigated the predictive value of anatomical and stone-related parameters as well as scoring systems (R.I.R.S. nomogram and RUSS) in pediatric patients undergoing RIRS. Our findings highlight that larger stone size (≥ 15 mm), lower IPA (≤ 30°), increased RIL (≥ 25 mm), and presence of multiple stones are significant predictors of residual fragments after RIRS. These results align with prior studies which emphasized that stone size and complexity are major factors influencing the success of pediatric RIRS surgeries [9–11]. However, the significance of IPA and RIL measurements in predicting residual fragments was not consistent with the findings of Alma et al. [10]. Nevertheless, our findings are consistent with several studies that found that anatomical variables such as IPA and RIL significantly affect postoperative outcomes [12, 13].

We demonstrated that the R.I.R.S. nomogram achieved a numerically higher AUC than the RUSS score (0.911 vs. 0.876); however, formal comparison using DeLong’s test revealed no statistically significant difference between the two AUC values (p = 0.337). Both systems therefore demonstrated comparably high predictive performance in our pediatric cohort. Prior studies have reported similar or superior performance of the R.I.R.S. nomogram compared to other models, underscoring its potential utility in pediatric populations [9, 10]; however, no definitive conclusions regarding the superiority of one scoring system over the other can be drawn from our data alone.

Preoperative anatomical challenges in children, such as narrow ureteral diameters and unfavorable calyceal anatomy, have been reported to influence RIRS success [14]. Despite these limitations, RIRS remains a preferred option due to its minimal invasiveness and acceptable complication profile. Alzahrani et al. recently reported that both RIRS and PCNL have comparable SFR in pediatric patients, but RIRS is associated with fewer major complications [4]. Our results, indicating an SFR of 29% in patients with stones > 15 mm and 79% in those ≤ 10 mm, support the notion that stone size remains a key determinant of outcome.

As demonstrated by Castellani et al., while preoperative stenting may facilitate ureteral access in children, it does not significantly improve SFR but may reduce the risk of needing secondary interventions [15]. In our patient group, routine preoperative stenting was not performed, and a ureteral access sheath was not used to avoid ureteral injury [16, 17]. This may be one factor that explains the slightly higher rate of residual fragments compared to similar studies.

Interestingly, we found that R.I.R.S. scoring at a threshold of ≤ 6 yielded high sensitivity and specificity for predicting success. While this offers clinical utility, balancing sensitivity with specificity in selecting cut-off values for practical decision-making is important [18]. Furthermore, it should be acknowledged that our study lacked a standardized intraoperative verification protocol for complete stone clearance. More rigorous approaches have been described in the literature; for instance, Asali et al. proposed a triple-test combining endoscopic inspection of all calyces, fluoroscopy, and retrograde pyelography for intraoperative confirmation of stone-free status [19]. The absence of such a protocol in our study may have influenced residual fragment rates, particularly in patients with complex calyceal anatomy. Clinical decision-making must be individualized for each patient. A less invasive approach does not always guarantee maximum benefit for the patient. Therefore, if there are no contraindications, mini-PNL should be considered the more advantageous method for achieving stone-free surgery in larger stones [5, 12, 20].

Our study is not without limitations. It was retrospective and single-center in design, which may limit generalizability. Small sample size limits the power of the study. Moreover, outcomes could be influenced by the surgical expertise available. The use of two different postoperative imaging modalities (KUB and non-contrast CT) introduces heterogeneity in outcome assessment, as plain X-ray may underdetect small residual fragments. The wide age range of the pediatric cohort (up to 15 years) encompasses substantial anatomical variability; subgroup analyses by age category were not performed and would be warranted in future studies with larger samples. Additionally, the multivariate model included several predictors relative to the number of events (38 non-stone-free cases), raising the possibility of overfitting and contributing to the wide confidence intervals observed for some variables. Formal AUC comparison by DeLong’s test showed no statistically significant difference between the R.I.R.S. nomogram and RUSS (p = 0.337), indicating that neither scoring system can be declared statistically superior to the other based on the current dataset. As a retrospective study, a formal a priori power analysis was not performed, which should be considered when interpreting our results. Nonetheless, the inclusion of a well-defined pediatric cohort and standardized evaluation protocols strengthens the validity of our findings.

Conclusion

In summary, this study confirms the significant influence of stone size, anatomical parameters, and scoring systems on RIRS outcomes in pediatric patients. Both the R.I.R.S. nomogram and RUSS demonstrated high discriminative accuracy in our cohort; however, formal statistical comparison using DeLong’s test revealed no significant difference between their AUC values (0.911 vs. 0.876; p = 0.337). Accordingly, neither system can be declared statistically superior, and both may serve as useful adjuncts in preoperative assessment. Clinical decisions should nonetheless remain individualized, and these scoring tools should be interpreted alongside patient-specific anatomical and clinical factors. Further prospective, multicenter trials with larger samples are warranted to validate these findings and to develop scoring models specifically tailored to the pediatric anatomy.
