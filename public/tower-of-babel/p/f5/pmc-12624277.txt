First‐Trimester Serum n‐3/n‐6 Fatty Acids Predict Large‐for‐Gestational‐Age Risk: A Nested Case‐Control Study
Authors: Xiaxia Cai, Haiyan Zhu, Ning Liang, Yandi Zhu, Qinyu Dang, Min Gao, Huanling Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPolyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) are critical for fetal development during pregnancy. This study evaluates the association between maternal serum n‐3 and n‐6 PUFAs in early pregnancy and the risk of large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) infants.A nested case‐control study was conducted with 45 LGA‐appropriate for gestational age (AGA) matched pairs from a cohort of 2839 pregnant women. Maternal serum samples collected during early pregnancy were analyzed for lipid profiles using gas chromatography–mass spectrometry (GC–MS). Associations between fatty acid levels and LGA risk were assessed using Spearman correlation, chi‐square tests, and logistic regression.The results indicated that higher arachidonic acid (ARA, p = 0.024) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA, p = 0.039) levels were positively correlated with LGA risk. Linoleic acid (LA, p = 0.009), alpha‐linolenic acid (ALA, p = 0.040), and the n‐6/n‐3 PUFA ratio (p = 0.034) were negatively correlated with LGA. Adjusted odds ratios indicated that higher quartiles of ARA (OR = 2.293, p = 0.078) and DHA (OR = 2.593, p = 0.038) increased LGA risk, while higher n‐6 PUFA levels reduced the risk (OR = 0.340, p = 0.019).In conclusion, early pregnancy serum n‐3 and n‐6 PUFA levels are associated with LGA risk. Elevated ARA and DHA levels may increase LGA risk, while higher n‐6 PUFA levels may be protective. These findings suggest that PUFA levels could serve as early indicators for targeted nutritional interventions.Keywords: arachidonic acid, docosahexaenoic acid, early pregnancy nutrition, fatty acids, large‐for‐gestational‐age, n‐6/n‐3 ratio

Introduction

Pregnancy is a critical period for fetal development, during which maternal nutrition plays a key role in influencing health outcomes (Moore 2016). With rapid economic growth, overnutrition during pregnancy has become increasingly prominent. Overnutrition during pregnancy has been associated with adverse outcomes, including large‐for‐gestational‐age (LGA) births (Catalano and Shankar 2017; Sun et al. 2020). LGA is associated with several neonatal complications, including asphyxia, shoulder dystocia, brachial plexus injury, mechanical ventilation, and neonatal death (Dwi Putra et al. 2020; Chauhan et al. 2017). For mothers, LGA can result in complications such as premature rupture of membranes, uterine rupture, birth canal lacerations, postpartum hemorrhage, and even life‐threatening conditions (Rossi et al. 2013). Furthermore, LGA infants are linked to higher risks of long‐term health issues such as obesity and cardiometabolic risk (Viswanathan et al. 2022).

Several studies have suggested that energy‐providing substances, such as lipids, are strongly associated with fetal birth weight and LGA. Research has confirmed that elevated triglyceride (TG) levels during pregnancy are linked to an increased risk of impaired glucose tolerance, gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), macrosomia, and LGA (Lewis, Childs, et al. 2018; Saini and Keum 2018; Videla et al. 2022). During pregnancy, maternal TGs are broken down into free fatty acids by placental lipase, which are then taken up by the fetus (Lewis, Childs, et al. 2018).

Among these fatty acids, alpha‐linolenic acid (ALA, C18:3 n‐3) and linoleic acid (LA, C18:2 n‐6) are essential polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) that cannot be synthesized by humans and must be obtained from dietary sources such as flaxseeds (ALA) and vegetable oils (LA) (Saini and Keum 2018). Through sequential reactions catalyzed by Δ‐6 desaturase, elongase, and Δ‐5 desaturase, ALA is metabolized to eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), while LA is converted to arachidonic acid (ARA) (Videla et al. 2022). The efficiency of these conversions is influenced by dietary factors (e.g., zinc, vitamin B6 intake) and genetic variations in FADS genes (Valenzuela et al. 2024).

These essential fatty acids and their longer‐chain metabolites (particularly ARA and DHA) play critical biological roles, including maintaining cell membrane structure and function, regulating cell growth, facilitating intracellular signaling, influencing gene expression, and producing bioactive lipid mediators. These processes are essential throughout pregnancy (Duttaroy and Basak 2020; Khaire et al. 2020). A meta‐analysis demonstrated that oral omega‐3 supplementation may elevate birth weight (Abdelrahman et al. 2023). However, most studies to date have focused on maternal fatty acid levels during mid‐to‐late pregnancy (Abdelrahman et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Meher et al. 2016; Hoge et al. 2020; Cinelli et al. 2018). However, research on maternal serum EFA levels during early pregnancy is relatively limited, particularly regarding the roles of n‐3 and n‐6 fatty acids and their metabolites (such as ARA and DHA) in the first trimester.

Therefore, this study aims to address this gap by examining the levels of maternal EFAs (primarily LA and ALA) and their metabolites (mainly ARA and DHA) during the first trimester, and exploring their relationship with LGA risk. We hypothesize that first‐trimester ALA/LA levels and their metabolites (ARA/DHA) exhibit distinct predictive patterns for LGA.

Materials and Methods

Study Population and Design

The recruitment of participants for this cohort took place between January 2016 and June 2017 at the Obstetrics Department of Fuxing Hospital, affiliated with Capital Medical University, and the Maternity and Child Health Hospital of Fengtai District (Beijing, PR China). Women in early pregnancy (6–12 weeks) were enrolled. A total of 2839 pregnant women were included in this prospective cohort.

Inclusion criteria: Pregnant women aged 20 to 40 years in their first trimester (6 to 12 weeks), expecting a single birth, willing to participate in the study, undergoing regular prenatal examinations at the research sites, and delivering in the above hospital.

Exclusion criteria: Pregnant women with in vitro fertilization pregnancies, chronic hypertension, infectious diseases, cardiovascular diseases, malignant diseases (such as tumors), or previously delivered newborns with Apgar scores of less than 7, or those unable to complete follow‐up were excluded.

Within the cohort described above, a nested case‐control study was conducted. Pregnant women who delivered LGA newborns were defined as the case group (LGA group). Women who delivered appropriate‐for‐gestational‐age (AGA) newborns were selected as the control group (AGA group) and were matched 1:1 with the LGA group based on age and pre‐pregnancy BMI.

Anthropometrics and Clinical Evaluation

General maternal conditions during pregnancy, as well as obstetric and neonatal information, were measured by professional clinicians and nurses. The following data were collected during both the 6–12 weeks and 36–38 weeks of pregnancy. Pre‐pregnancy BMI was calculated by measuring the height and weight of pregnant women at their first prenatal examination (6–12 gestational weeks). The first‐trimester medical record was serving as secondary validation, which was referenced only when the discrepancy between clinically measured weight and self‐reported pre‐pregnancy weight exceeded ±2 kg. Weight during pregnancy was also monitored. Gestational weight gain (GWG) was defined as the difference between the weight measured during the final prenatal examination before delivery and the weight measured during the initial prenatal examination. GWG was classified according to the 2009 Institute of Medicine guidelines (Tennekoon 2022).

The delivery of pregnant women was recorded by doctors or midwives, including details such as gestational age, mode of delivery, labor progression, blood loss, and the presence of intrauterine infection. Neonatal outcomes, including gender, birth weight (BW), length, chest circumference, Apgar score, and the presence or absence of intrauterine distress, were also documented. LGA was defined as a birth weight above the 90th percentile for gestational age, while AGA was defined as a birth weight between the 10th and 90th percentiles for gestational age, based on international standards (Tao et al. 2024).

Blood Sample Collection

During early pregnancy (6–12 weeks), 4 mL of venous blood was collected from pregnant women in a fasting state (fasting for ≥ 8 h) during routine physical examinations. The blood samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, and TG, total cholesterol (TC), and high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) were analyzed in the plasma using an Olympus AU480 automatic biochemical analyzer (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). The serum was stored at −80°C until fatty acid extraction. After birth, 4 mL of venous cord blood was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The serum was then stored at −80°C until fatty acid extraction.

Fatty Acid Analysis

Serum fatty acids were measured by gas chromatography‐mass spectrometry (GC‐MS). The sample preparation, gas chromatographic and mass spectrometry conditions were showed in the supplemental information.

Statistical Analysis

Data were collated and analyzed using SPSS Statistics for Mac (Version 26.0, IBM Corp, NY, USA). Descriptive statistical analysis was performed to assess the general condition of the pregnant women and the birth outcomes of the newborns. Quantitative data that followed a normal distribution were expressed as mean and standard deviation (SD), while data that did not conform to a normal distribution were expressed as median (P25, P75), and non‐parametric tests were used for group comparisons. Categorical data were presented as n (%). Fatty acid profiles in the maternal and cord blood were compared between the AGA and LGA groups using paired t‐tests and independent samples t‐tests, respectively.

Spearman correlation analysis was conducted, and Spearman's correlation coefficient was calculated to evaluate the relationship between fatty acids and LGA. Pearson correlation analysis was also performed, and Pearson's correlation coefficient was calculated to assess the correlation between fetal and maternal fatty acids. Results were considered significant if the p‐value was less than 0.05. χ 2 tests and Cochran‐Mantel‐Haenszel (CMH) tests were performed to investigate the association between fatty acids and LGA risk, with significance defined as p < 0.05. The results were expressed as odds ratios (OR) or adjusted odds ratios (AOR) with 95% confidence intervals (95% CI). Finally, the ROC curve and area under the curve (AUC) were used to evaluate the ability of various variables, particularly fatty acids, to detect neonatal LGA.

Ethics Statement

The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Capital Medical University (2012SY29). This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (1975), as revised in 1983, as well as the guidelines of the center's institutional review board. All participants were informed of the study details, and each provided written informed consent.

Results

Maternal and Infant Characteristics

In our study, 45 pairs of pregnant women and their infants were included. Women in the LGA group had significantly higher gestational weight gain (GWG, p = 0.001) and placental weight (p = 0.003) compared to the AGA group. No significant differences were found in age, weight, pre‐BMI, or the proportion of multiparous women and cesarean sections between the LGA and AGA groups.

No significant difference was observed in the gender composition of the neonates between the LGA and AGA groups. The birth weight of the LGA newborns was significantly higher than that of the AGA newborns (p < 0.001) (Table 1).

Fatty acids were divided into quartiles. The χ 2 test and the Cochran‐Mantel‐Haenszel (CMH) test, adjusted for age, pre‐BMI, GWG, and parity, were used to analyze the relationship between serum fatty acids in early pregnancy and the risk of LGA.

Results from the unadjusted Chi‐square test analyzing the associations between serum fatty acids in early pregnancy and LGA risk are summarized in Supporting Information S1: Table S1. ARA (OR = 2.522, 95% CI: 1.069–5.95, p = 0.033), DHA (OR = 2.571, 95% CI: 1.079–6.13, p = 0.031), and n‐6 PUFA (OR = 0.396, 95% CI: 0.168–0.935, p = 0.033) were associated with the risk of LGA. For ARA and DHA, the higher quartile had a higher risk of LGA compared to the lower quartile. For n‐6 PUFA, the higher quartile had a lower risk of LGA compared to the lower quartile.

Results from the CMH test analyzing the associations between serum fatty acids in early pregnancy and LGA risk are summarized in Table 5. After adjusting for parity (AOR = 2.509, 95% CI: 1.066–5.907, p = 0.035), ARA levels remained associated with the risk of LGA. However, after adjusting for GWG, ARA levels were no longer associated with LGA risk. After adjusting for parity (AOR = 3.034, 95% CI: 1.276–7.211, p = 0.012) and GWG (AOR = 2.593, 95% CI: 1.053–6.385, p = 0.038), DHA levels remained associated with the risk of LGA. Similarly, after adjusting for parity (AOR = 0.378, 95% CI: 0.152–0.937, p = 0.036) and GWG (AOR = 0.340, 95% CI: 0.138–0.840, p = 0.019), n‐6 PUFA levels remained inversely associated with the risk of LGA.

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated that maternal serum n‐3 and n‐6 PUFA levels in early pregnancy are significantly associated with LGA risk. Elevated ARA and DHA levels may promote fetal growth but also increase the risk of LGA, suggesting a dose‐dependent effect. Conversely, LA and ALA levels in early pregnancy were negatively correlated with the risk of LGA. Interestingly, higher n‐6 PUFA levels and a greater n‐6/n‐3 ratio appeared protective, possibly through mechanisms such as regulating placental development and nutrient transport (Fujii et al. 2023). However, this requires further investigation.

Numerous studies have explored the relationship between PUFAs and birth weight (Abdelrahman et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Meher et al. 2016; Hoge et al. 2020; Cinelli et al. 2018). For example, prolonged supplementation of n‐3 fatty acids, particularly DHA, has been shown to increase birth weight significantly, especially when provided from the second trimester onward (Abdelrahman et al. 2023). A randomized controlled trial demonstrated that DHA supplementation enhanced maternal red blood cell phospholipid DHA levels at delivery and lowered the risk of low birth weight (Wang et al. 2023). Studies have found that maternal erythrocyte DHA levels during the second (Meher et al. 2016; Hoge et al. 2020) and third trimesters (Cinelli et al. 2018) were also positively associated with birth weight.

While extensive research has established the impact of mid‐to‐late pregnancy PUFA status on birth weight (Abdelrahman et al. 2023; Wang et al. 2023; Meher et al. 2016; Hoge et al. 2020; Cinelli et al. 2018), our study revealed that maternal serum DHA levels in early pregnancy were significantly higher in the LGA group compared to the AGA group. These results affirm DHA's role in promoting fetal growth but highlight a unique association with LGA risk, an aspect often overlooked in prior studies. The magnitude of this effect may be modulated by maternal nutritional status, as fish oil supplementation has been shown to significantly increase DHA levels in both maternal erythrocytes during pregnancy and breast milk during lactation (Stoutjesdijk et al. 2018; Argaw et al. 2021). Notably, a Brazilian study reported that higher PUFA intake (n‐3 and n‐6) reduced LGA incidence when assessed through dietary surveys during mid‐to‐late pregnancy (de Lima et al. 2021). Nevertheless, it is worth noting that higher DHA levels were associated with an increased risk of LGA in our study, suggesting a potential “dose‐dependent” effect of DHA on fetal growth. This underscores the need for personalized approaches that consider baseline nutritional status and ongoing dietary patterns to optimize fetal outcomes without compromising metabolic health.

The role of n‐6 PUFAs, particularly ARA and LA, appears more complex. In line with our results, maternal serum ARA levels were significantly higher in the LGA group, corroborating previous findings by Cinelli et al (Cinelli et al. 2018)., where ARA levels in the third trimester positively correlated with birth weight. Conversely, Meher et al. (2016) reported that elevated concentrations of maternal plasma and erythrocyte ARA were associated with lower birth weight. These contradictory findings may arise from differences in sample timing (early vs. late pregnancy) or assessment methods (serum vs. erythrocytes).

Our study also identified an inverse relationship between maternal LA levels and LGA risk, as well as a protective effect of higher n‐6 PUFA levels and a greater n‐6/n‐3 ratio. Similar findings were reported in a South Korean cohort, where increased maternal n‐6 PUFA intake was negatively associated with birth weight (Lee et al. 2018). Considering that LA are converted into DHA and ARA (Videla et al. 2022), they may preferentially pass through the placenta (Lewis, Wadsack, et al. 2018), potentially influencing fetal growth. However, the conversion of ALA to DHA is extremely limited in humans (Greupner et al. 2018). This implies that circulating ARA levels reflect both dietary intake and endogenous synthesis, whereas DHA levels predominantly reflect direct dietary sources. These metabolic considerations are particularly important for clinical translation: Optimizing LA/ALA levels primarily requires dietary modifications (e.g., vegetable oil selection). Modulating ARA/DHA may involve either dietary interventions (e.g., fish oil) or metabolic approaches (e.g., zinc/vitamin B6 to support desaturase activity) (Videla et al. 2022). Moreover, we need to control the ratio of LA to ARA intake.

Interestingly, maternal n‐6 PUFA levels in the second trimester were positively correlated with birth weight, but this relationship reversed in the third trimester (Meher et al. 2016). This temporal shift may explain some inconsistencies in existing studies and highlights the importance of assessing PUFA levels early in pregnancy. Our study uniquely addresses this gap by focusing on early pregnancy serum PUFAs and their relationship with LGA risk.

The n‐6/n‐3 PUFA ratio is widely regarded as a critical factor reflecting the balance of these two fatty acid families. Previous studies in South India (Mani et al. 2016) and Korea (Lee et al. 2018) demonstrated that lower n‐6/n‐3 ratios are associated with higher birth weight and reduced SGA risk. Conversely, our findings suggest that a higher n‐6/n‐3 ratio may reduce LGA risk. This discrepancy underscores the complexity of PUFA interactions and their varying effects across populations and gestational periods. Notably, a study in Belgium (Hoge et al. 2020) failed to establish significant associations between the n‐6/n‐3 ratio in the second trimester and birth weight after adjustment for confounders. These inconsistencies could stem from differences in dietary habits, genetic factors, or ethnic variations among study populations. Further research is needed to validate these findings across diverse cohorts and clarify the mechanisms by which the n‐6/n‐3 ratio modulates fetal growth. Importantly, our study is among the first to highlight the protective role of the n‐6/n‐3 PUFA ratio during the first trimester, emphasizing the potential for early nutritional interventions to optimize pregnancy outcomes.

Limitations

We acknowledge the limitations in our study. First, the study was conducted at only two hospitals in the Beijing area, which may limit the generalizability of the findings due to the regional nature of the sample. Second, while serum PUFA levels provide reliable biomarkers of circulating availability, the lack of dietary data precludes differentiation between nutritional intake and endogenous metabolic contributions. This limits our ability to determine whether the observed associations reflect dietary patterns or intrinsic metabolic variations. Future research should incorporate detailed dietary assessments and longitudinal analyses to better elucidate the relationship between maternal PUFA intake, serum levels, and fetal outcomes. Third, balanced intake of n‐3 and n‐6 fatty acids, especially DHA and EPA, during pregnancy is crucial for fetal neurodevelopment (Reis et al. 2024). However, neurodevelopment outcomes in infancy were not assessed due to challenges in longitudinal follow‐up data collection in the present study. These important aspects will be prioritized in our planned prospective study with detailed nutritional records and developmental follow‐up.

Despite these limitations, our study provides novel insights into the associations between early pregnancy n‐3 and n‐6 PUFA levels and LGA risk, underscoring the importance of early nutritional monitoring and intervention.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our results suggest that the levels of EFAs and their metabolites, n‐3 and n‐6 PUFAs, in the first trimester are significantly associated with the risk of LGA. These findings provide preliminary evidence that first‐trimester serum fatty acid levels may serve as complementary biomarkers for LGA risk prediction. The regulatory role of the maternal n‐6/n‐3 PUFA ratio in fetal growth offers a new perspective for nutritional interventions during pregnancy. Future research should further explore the specific mechanisms of different fatty acids, particularly their role in the placental development mechanisms related to LGA.
