Psychometric properties of shortened versions of the Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire in Brazilian women
Authors: Andreia Dutra Gonçalves, Vitória Bagolin, Pedro Henrique Berbert de Carvalho, Maria Fernanda Laus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThe Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (EDE-Q) is a widely used 28-item instrument for screening eating disorders, of which 22 are attitudinal items used to compute subscale scores and to assess factorial structure, while six open-ended items assess behavioral frequency and are excluded from dimensional analyses. Shorter versions, such as the EDE-Q7, EDE-Q8, and EDE-Q18, have been proposed to enhance accessibility and psychometric properties. This study aimed to evaluate different factorial structures of the EDE-Q and identify the model that best fits a sample of Brazilian adult women.MethodsA total of 712 women completed the EDE-Q and measures of body satisfaction and sociocultural pressures. The factorial structure was analyzed through confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), and convergent validity and internal consistency were also assessed.ResultsThe multifactorial structure of the original EDE-Q and the 18-item model did not fit the sample well. Although the 7-item model showed acceptable fit, the 8-item version demonstrated the best fit. The EDE-Q8 factors showed a negative association with appearance evaluation and body area satisfaction, and a positive association with internalization of the thin and muscular body ideal and body mass index. Internal consistency was adequate.ConclusionThe 8-item short version of the EDE-Q is a reliable and practical psychometric tool for assessing disordered eating behaviors in Brazilian adult women. Future studies should explore the psychometric performance of the EDE-Q8 across different age ranges, gender groups, and clinical populations to strengthen evidence for its cross-cultural validity and generalizability.Level of Evidence: Level III, well-designed cohort or case–control analytic studies.Keywords: Brazil, Disordered eating, EDE-Q, Factor analysis, Assessment, Psychometrics

Introduction

Eating disorders (EDs) are psychiatric conditions with multifactorial origins, marked by significant changes in eating behavior that can lead to weight loss or gain. The most common types are anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa, and binge eating disorder. These behaviors often include food restriction or excessive intake, fasting, exclusion of certain food groups, and compensatory actions such as excessive exercise and self-induced vomiting [1]. In most cases, there is an overvaluation of body shape and/or weight, leading to intense body dissatisfaction [2, 3], along with emotional distress and physical, psychological, and social impairments [4–6].

Despite their increasing prevalence and severity, EDs are associated with some of the highest mortality rates among psychiatric conditions, resulting from both medical complications and suicide [6–9]. Nevertheless, most cases remain undiagnosed and untreated, especially in low- and middle-income countries. In Brazil, the scarcity of psychometric studies examining disordered eating in female populations further underscores the importance of validating culturally adapted instruments. Therefore, investigating the factor structure and validity of the EDE-Q in Brazilian women is an essential step toward improving screening and diagnostic practices in this high-risk group. The full version of the Eating Disorder Examination-Questionnaire (EDE-Q 6.0) [10], with 28 items, is one of the most widely used self-report instruments for identifying EDs, and its use is recommended by the National Institute for Mental Health in England [11]. It is derived from the Eating Disorder Examination (EDE) [12], considered one of the gold standards in ED assessment, but is shorter and self-administered.

The EDE-Q 6.0 comprises 28 items in total, of which 22 are attitudinal items, organized into four subscales (restraint, eating concern, weight concern, and shape concern), while the remaining six open-ended items assess the frequency of eating disorder behaviors (e.g., binge eating and compensatory behaviors). Applying all items is necessary to establish a diagnosis of an eating disorder. However, only the attitudinal items are used for dimensional analyses and global score, which are calculated by averaging the subscales [10]. Thus, only the 22 items can be used to assess the instrument's structure and symptom severity. While some studies support the original four-factor structure [13–15], some have failed to replicate it [16]. The length of the EDE-Q 6.0 can be a limitation in studies with large samples or time constraints.

Given the inconsistent findings regarding the factorial structure of the EDE-Q 6.0 and the practical limitations of its full version, recent studies have proposed shortened models that retain the instrument’s conceptual foundations while improving psychometric performance and accessibility, such as the 7-item [17], 8-item [18], and 18-item [19] forms. Machado et al. [20] found that these shortened versions retain strong psychometric properties, including high internal consistency and strong correlations with the full version. These versions are particularly useful for screening and research purposes, enabling the assessment of disordered eating attitudes and ED symptoms in non-clinical and epidemiological contexts.

The EDE-Q7, consisting of three factors, dietary restraint, shape and weight overvaluation, and body dissatisfaction, is widely recognized for its strong psychometric performance across diverse populations [14, 21–34] and is the most used short version. Comparative studies have shown that it outperforms other shortened forms of the EDE-Q [20, 21, 33]. The EDE-Q8 retains the original four-factor model but excludes behavioral items and has also been adapted for children [35]. The EDE-Q18 features three factors, restriction, shape and weight concerns, and preoccupation and eating concern, but remains less widely used and studied [19, 20].

Body image disturbance is a diagnostic criterion for anorexia and bulimia nervosa, according to the DSM-5-TR [1] and ICD-11 criteria [36]. The internalization of socially desirable body ideals is a key risk factor for eating disorders, contributing to disordered eating behaviors [37]. Studies indicate that high scores on measures of body ideal influence and dissatisfaction strongly correlate with EDE-Q scores and predict significant eating pathology [38, 39]. Body dissatisfaction is directly linked to behaviors aimed at altering body composition and is influenced by sociocultural and psychological factors, such as social comparison and internalization of ideals [37]. Thus, the EDE-Q is valuable for assessing dysfunctional eating attitudes and behaviors, which can be examined in relation to psychosocial variables through concurrent measures.

In Latin America, EDs represent a growing public health issue, with prevalence rates similar to or exceeding those of developed countries. Estimates suggest a prevalence of up to 6%, especially among adolescent and young adult females [40]. However, the lack of validated screening tools for the Brazilian population [41], specifically the EDE-Q's validity in Brazil, particularly among women [42, 43], limits the capacity to screen for disordered eating risk in diverse populations, and robust studies are necessary to validate these tools in clinical and non-clinical groups [16]. Considering that women are the group most affected by body dissatisfaction and sociocultural appearance pressures, and that adult women face specific vulnerabilities associated with life stage, social roles, and body changes, validating a brief EDE-Q version for this population represents a crucial step toward improving early identification and advancing cross-cultural research in this field.

As part of a broader research project on muscularity, body image, and eating behavior among Brazilian women, this study aimed to evaluate the previously proposed factor structures of the EDE-Q and identify the best-fitting model in this sample. We targeted adult women aged 18–50 years to encompass a period of life significantly impacted by ED symptoms and dissatisfaction with body image. This broad age range was selected because disordered eating behaviors and negative body image remain highly prevalent and clinically relevant across adulthood, not only in young women but also among middle-aged adults [44]. Although the incidence of clinical ED diagnoses such as binge-eating disorder (BED; point prevalence ≈1.4%) and bulimia nervosa (BN; ≈0.7%) is relatively low in the general adult population, disordered eating behaviors and body dissatisfaction are remarkably widespread. For instance, up to 66% of Brazilian adult women report being dissatisfied with their bodies [45, 46]. These patterns demonstrate that risk factors such as the drive for thinness and body overvaluation persist beyond early adulthood, contributing to significant psychiatric and medical comorbidities and functional impairment throughout the adult female life cycle. Therefore, including women aged 18–50 years allows for the validation of an instrument with broader public health applicability, capturing the continuity of risk across adulthood.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the internalization of appearance ideals is positively associated with EDs psychopathology. Specifically, internalization of thinness shows medium to large positive correlations with EDE-Q scores among women, reflecting the central role of the drive for thinness in disordered eating [47, 48]. Similarly, muscularity internalization has been consistently associated with higher EDE-Q scores in men and, to a lesser extent, in women, although cultural variations have been observed across different samples [49–51]. EDE-Q scores also tend to increase with higher body mass index (BMI), as elevated BMI is associated with greater body dissatisfaction and disordered eating severity [52–55]. In contrast, appearance evaluation and body area satisfaction are negatively correlated with EDE-Q subscales, indicating that greater eating pathology corresponds to lower satisfaction with one’s body and appearance. Finally, EDE-Q scores are typically inversely associated with age, consistent with evidence that ED symptoms peak in adolescence and early adulthood [53, 56, 57].

Based on these findings, the following hypotheses were proposed: (1) the original four-factor model of the EDE-Q (dietary restraint, eating concern, weight concern, and shape concern) would exhibit poorer fit compared to shorter versions; (2) the best-fitting EDE-Q versions would be positively associated with thinness/low body fat internalization, muscular internalization, and BMI, and negatively associated with appearance evaluation, body area satisfaction, and age; and (3) the best-fitting EDE-Q versions would demonstrate satisfactory internal consistency, with McDonald's omega (ω) greater than 0.70.

Method

Participants

The sample size requirements were based on guidelines recommending a 20:1 participant-to-item ratio for factor analysis [58]. The EDE-Q 6.0 includes 22 attitudinal items, resulting in a minimum sample size of 440 participants for CFA [10]. Inclusion criteria comprised being a cisgender woman aged 18–50 and providing informed consent. A priori exclusions were applied to individuals who did not meet the inclusion criteria prior to analysis. Among those who consented and started the questionnaire, post-consent eliminations were conducted based on predefined conditions (e.g., incomplete responses to key measures, pregnancy or childbirth within the past year, health conditions directly affecting eating behavior or physical appearance, or current or past bariatric surgery). A total of 1,834 women initiated the survey. Of these, 1,122 were later excluded due to incomplete data or ineligibility identified after consent (e.g., pregnancy, health conditions, bariatric surgery). The final analytical sample consisted of 712 participants. This high rate of elimination likely reflects the open online recruitment strategy, which, while increasing reach, may have introduced some degree of selection bias.

Measures

Sample characterization. Participants reported their age, race/ethnicity, gender identity, sexual orientation, marital status, educational level, and socioeconomic status. Socioeconomic status was assessed using the multiple-choice question: “What is your socioeconomic level (considering the current minimum wage of BRL 1,412.00)?” with the following response options: “Class A (monthly family gross income above 20 times the minimum wage)”, “Class B (between 10 and 20 times the minimum wage)”, “Class C (between 4 and 10 times the minimum wage)”, “Class D (between 2 and 4 times the minimum wage)”, and “Class E (up to 2 times the minimum wage)”. Educational level was assessed by asking: “What is your highest completed level of education?” with response options: “incomplete elementary school”, “complete elementary school”, “complete high school”, “complete higher education”, and “postgraduate degree”. They also self-reported weight and height, which were used to calculate BMI.

Eating Disorder Examination Questionnaire (EDE-Q 6.0) [10] (Brazilian Portuguese translation 43]). The EDE-Q 6.0 assesses core ED psychopathology over the past 28 days. It comprises a total of 28 items, with 22 attitudinal items forming four subscales (restraint, eating concern, weight concern, and shape concern), along with six open-ended items that assess the frequency of eating disorder behaviors (e.g., binge eating and compensatory behaviors) (refer to Table 1 for a detailed description of the factors). The EDE-Q 6.0 uses a 7-point Likert scale (0–6), except for behavioral frequency items, which are scored by episode count over the past 4 weeks. Subscale scores are the mean of completed items, and the global score is the average of the four subscale means. Higher scores indicate greater ED severity. Importantly, although all 28 items from the original version of the instrument were administered, only the 22 attitudinal items were used for the analysis of the factor structure of both the original version and its shortened versions (i.e., the 7-item, 8-item, and 18-item versions). The factorial structures of each model were maintained in accordance with the subscales and item composition suggested initially by their respective authors.

Multidimensional Body-Self Relations Questionnaire-Appearance Scales (MBSRQ-AS) [59, 60] (Brazilian version [61]). The Brazilian version of the MBSRQ-AS has 28 items and five subscales; this study used two: appearance evaluation (five items) and body area satisfaction (nine items). Items are rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1–5). Subscale scores are the mean of item responses. Higher scores indicate greater satisfaction and a more positive attitude toward appearance. Internal consistency for the MBSRQ-AS subscales in the present study was as follows: appearance evaluation ω = 0.92 (95% CI 0.91–0.93) and body area satisfaction ω = 0.86 (95% CI 0.85–0.87).

Sociocultural Attitudes Towards Appearance Questionnaire-4-Revised-Female (SATAQ-4R-Female) [62] (Brazilian version [63]). This scale measures sociocultural pressures and internalization of body ideals in women. The Brazilian version includes 31 items across seven subscales; two were used in this study: thin/low body fat internalization (four items) and muscular internalization (five items). Rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1 to 5), item 10 is reverse-scored. Subscale scores are determined by averaging item responses. Higher scores reflect stronger internalization and perceived sociocultural pressure. Internal consistency for the SATAQ-4R-Female subscales in the present study was as follows: thin/low body fat internalization ω = 0.89 (95% CI 0.87–0.90) and muscular internalization ω = 0.93 (95% CI 0.92–0.94).

Procedures

After obtaining ethics approval from the Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 6.907.185), participants were recruited between July and September 2024 through digital channels managed by the research team. Recruitment materials, which included institutional identification, were distributed via institutional emails, WhatsApp®, and social media posts on Instagram® and Facebook®. To reach a wider audience, a paid advertisement campaign (Meta Ads®) targeted women aged 18–50 throughout Brazil, segmented by interests commonly associated with the target population (e.g., beauty, cooking, physical activity, health, fitness, self-care, and cultural activities). The study was also promoted on the University of São Paulo’s website and featured in a regional television report. Participants accessed the survey through a link that directed them to the REDCap platform, where they provided digital informed consent before completing the anonymous questionnaires. IP addresses were tracked to prevent duplicate entries, and no monetary incentives were provided.

Data analysis

Descriptive analyses (means, standard deviations, and frequencies) were used to describe the sample. To assess the factor structure of the EDE-Q versions, Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA), convergent validity, and internal consistency were conducted using R (version 4.4.1) and RStudio (version 2024.09.0).

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA). The final sample (n = 712) was analyzed using the Unbiased Least Squares with Missing Values (ULSMVS) method, as it is suitable for categorical data. Model fit was assessed using fit indices, including the Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA), Standardized Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR), and Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI). Acceptable fit was defined as CFI and TLI ≈ 0.95, and RMSEA and SRMR < 0.08 [64, 65]. Factor loadings (λ) were considered adequate if greater than 0.50 [65]. Table 1 presents the four factorial models tested, including the original EDE-Q version, and outlines the structure of each model.

Convergent validity. Convergent validity was assessed using Spearman’s correlation test between the total and subscale scores of the EDE-Q and the subscales of Appearance Evaluation, Body Areas Satisfaction, Internalization (Thin/Low Body Fat and Muscularity), BMI, and age. Correlations between 0.10 and 0.29 were considered small, between 0.30 and 0.49 medium, and above 0.50 large [66].

Internal consistency. The internal consistency of the instruments used in the study was evaluated in the current sample. McDonald’s omega (ω) was calculated for the subscales of the MBSRQ-AS, SATAQ-4R-Female, and the total scores of the EDE-Q7 and EDE-Q8, with values above 0.70 considered adequate [67]. Since the subscales of the EDE-Q7 and EDE-Q8 contain only two items, which makes the calculation of ω statistically inappropriate and unstable, item–total corrected correlations (item–rest correlations) were computed to evaluate item reliability and internal homogeneity.

Additionally, item–total corrected correlations (item–rest correlations) were examined to assess item reliability and internal homogeneity. For the EDE-Q7, the item–total correlations for each subscale were as follows: Restraint (item 1 = 0.7–95% CI 0.66–0.74; item 2 = 0.69–95% CI 0.65–0.73; item 3 = 0.66–95% CI 0.62–0.7); Shape and weigh overvaluation (0.9; 95% CI 0.87–0.91); Body dissatisfaction (0.84; 95% CI 0.82–0.86). For the EDE-Q8, the item–total correlations for each subscale were as follows: Restraint (0.65; 95% CI 0.6–0.69); Eating concern (0.45; 95% CI 0.39–0.51); Shape concern (0.75; 95% CI 0.72–0.78); Weight concern (0.74; 95% CI 0.71–0.77).

Discussion

The present study aimed to evaluate the factorial structures of different versions of the EDE-Q, namely: the original EDE-Q 6.0 [10], the EDE-Q7 [17], the EDE-Q8 [18], and the EDE-Q18 [19], and to identify the best-fitting model in a sample of Brazilian adult women. Our hypothesis was confirmed that the original 28-item version of the EDE-Q would show a poorer fit than shorter versions. The 4-factor model of the original EDE-Q 6.0 could not be estimated in the current sample, reinforcing the limitations of this model in specific contexts, such as the complexity of its proposed factorial structure and the inadequate specification of the correlation matrix among questionnaire items, which aligns with previous findings [13, 16]. Similarly, the 18-item version did not demonstrate a good structural fit. This aligns with the EDE-Q 6.0 results but contrasts with those of Carey and colleagues [19], who, using data from university students and community members, reported adequate fit indices for the EDE-Q 6.0. It is worth noting that the 18-item EDE-Q factor structure lacks studies that support its factorial solution [20].

In contrast, the reduced 7- and 8-item versions presented better structural fit indices, with the 8-item version being the most robust in the current sample of Brazilian adult women. Our results differ from those of Machado and collaborators [20], who conducted CFA using data from both clinical (i.e., ED) and non-clinical samples of adolescents and women. The EDE-Q7 received the strongest support for its factorial structure, while its reliability and validity findings were similar across different versions. In our sample, we observed that item 22 (“Has your weight influenced how you evaluate/judge yourself as a person?”) in the 7-item version had a standardized factor loading greater than 1 (1.004). This result aligns with Grilo and colleagues [17], who also reported high factor loadings for items related to the overvaluation of weight (1.000) and body shape (1.054). In the 8-item version (EDE-Q8), however, item 22 is not included, suggesting that the reorganization of items may have favored the stability of this version. Furthermore, the 8-item version showed good model fit and demonstrated adequate internal consistency and convergent validity, which aligns with previous studies [18, 35].

The superior performance of the reduced versions supports the findings of Machado and contributors [20], who suggest that shorter versions of the EDE-Q may be effectively used for both clinical and non-clinical settings, such as ED screening or treatment outcome evaluation. Their greater parsimony and specificity can explain this in assessing the constructs of interest, reducing redundancy and minimizing potential cultural influences that may affect longer versions [69]. This result is consistent with our findings, indicating a better fit for the reduced versions of the EDE-Q.

Conflicting findings regarding the EDE-Q’s factorial structure across studies may stem from its original structure being theoretically based but lacking empirical support [70]. Additionally, methodological differences and sample characteristics, such as gender, age, and the distinction between clinical and community contexts, can also influence the outcomes.

Importantly, compared with the EDE-Q7, the EDE-Q8 maintains the distinction between shape concern and weight concern as separate yet correlated constructs, which enhances its theoretical coherence with the original model proposed by Fairburn and Beglin [10]. Although psychometrically sound, the EDE-Q7 combines these domains into broader attitudinal factors (shape and weight overvaluation and body dissatisfaction), which also differ in item composition (e.g., the inclusion of items 22 and 23 in Grilo et al. [17], but not in Kliem et al. [18]), resulting in a slightly more parsimonious but less content-comprehensive structure. Consequently, the factorial model of the 8-item version offers a concise yet conceptually consistent measure of the core cognitive–attitudinal components of eating disorder symptomatology. Overall, the EDE-Q8 provides a balanced representation of the multidimensional nature of eating disorder psychopathology.

Confirming our second hypothesis, the reduced versions of the EDE-Q showed consistent associations with constructs related to body image and eating behavior. Specifically, the EDE-Q7 and EDE-Q8 showed significant positive correlations with internalization of thinness/low body fat and significant negative correlations with the appearance evaluation and body area satisfaction subscales. This suggests that higher EDE-Q scores are associated with lower overall satisfaction with appearance and body areas. These findings are consistent with previous research linking disordered eating behaviors to negative body image [48, 54].

Regarding muscularity internalization, significant positive small correlations were found between dietary restraint, overvaluation of shape and weight subscales, but not with the body dissatisfaction subscale. For the EDE-Q8, significant positive small correlations were observed with the restraint and eating concern subscales but not with shape and weight concern subscale. These correlations suggest that muscularity internalization may impact eating behavior, notably through dietary restraint and concern [51]. However, it also indicates that the internalization of thinness and muscularity ideals follows different patterns in their association with body image variables. Individuals striving for a more muscular body may not necessarily perceive themselves as dissatisfied [50] but instead are motivated to achieve an ideal physique.

Wagner and partners [71], in a study of 300 female undergraduate students, found that while participants who endorsed only the thin ideal reported greater body dissatisfaction and avoidance, those who internalized the muscular ideal did not exhibit the same negative relationship. Thus, while the thin ideal appears more directly linked to negative body perception, the pursuit of muscularity may influence eating behavior without necessarily affecting body dissatisfaction.

The positive association between BMI and the EDE-Q was also expected; a study has shown that as BMI increases, particularly among individuals with overweight or obesity, concerns with appearance, body area dissatisfaction, and disordered eating behaviors tend to intensify [52]. The negative association between age and the overvaluation of shape and weight subscale of the EDE-Q7, although small, is consistent with research showing that EDs tend to emerge mainly during adolescence and early adulthood [56, 57, 72].

The internal consistency of the reduced versions, evaluated using the McDonald’s omega coefficient (ω = 0.94), was excellent, exceeding the recommended threshold (≥ 0.70) and confirming our third hypothesis. This result reinforces the reliability of the EDE-Q8 and its suitability for both research and clinical settings in Brazil. Thus, reducing the number of items did not compromise the instrument’s precision, indicating that more compact models can provide reliable results and greater practicality.

In conclusion, the results of this study indicate that the 8-item version of the EDE-Q is the most suitable for assessing disordered eating behaviors in Brazilian adult women. The high internal consistency and convergent validity demonstrate that this reduced version is a reliable and practical psychometric tool, providing a faster means of tracking eating disorder symptoms. These findings underscore the importance of adapting instruments to specific cultural and sample contexts, contributing to the advancement of assessment and diagnostic practices in mental and behavioral health.

Strengths and limits

This study stands out due to its sample size and, to our knowledge, is the first to validate the EDE-Q in a sample of Brazilian women. However, some limitations should be acknowledged. A substantial number of participants were eliminated due to incomplete or inconsistent responses, which may have resulted in a partial loss of information and limited the representativeness of the final sample. Additionally, while online data collection allowed for a broad geographic reach, it introduces potential bias because the lack of a strict identity verification process prevents full certainty about who completed the questionnaires. Participants were recruited through non-probabilistic sampling methods. Therefore, the results cannot be generalized to the broader population. Furthermore, volunteer bias should be recognized, as it might impact the external validity of the findings. Future research could overcome these limitations by using mixed recruitment methods and controlled administration processes.

Also, although convergent validity was examined, the discriminant validity of the EDE-Q8 has yet to be investigated, and future studies could assess its ability to distinguish between individuals with and without diagnosed EDs. Furthermore, additional research is needed to strengthen evidence on the applicability of the EDE-Q across different cultural and population contexts. Measurement invariance between clinical and non-clinical samples, as well as validation in male samples, would also be necessary steps toward expanding the use and understanding of the EDE-Q. Therefore, further studies using samples from eating disorder patients are essential to enhance the instrument's reliability, and complementary methodologies will provide deeper insight into the instrument’s effectiveness in screening and monitoring eating disorders.

What is already known on this subject?

The EDE-Q is a widely used measure of symptoms of eating disorders (EDs). However, some studies have failed to replicate its four-factor structure, and the instrument's length can be a barrier. Shorter versions of the EDE-Q, such as the 7-item, 8-item, and 18-item versions, have been proposed.

What your study adds?

The original version of the EDE-Q has been translated into Portuguese; however, neither this version nor the shorter versions have been validated for Brazilian women. This study validates a shortened version of the EDE-Q, providing a more accessible, quick, and culturally adapted tool for assessing eating disorder symptomatology. The findings may improve the evaluation and understanding of eating disorder features among Brazilian women, contributing to clinical practice and research within specific cultural contexts. Practically, the EDE-Q8 assesses the psychological and evaluative aspects of disordered eating, especially restraint, eating concern, and overvaluation of shape and weight, rather than focusing on the frequency of behavioral symptoms. Although this characteristic limits coverage of behavioral aspects and restricts its direct use for diagnostic purposes, it makes the 8-item version particularly suitable for large-scale surveys, screening, and monitoring symptom changes in both clinical and non-clinical settings.
