Process-integrated engineered resting cells for biocatalytic production of rare natural sugars from sole methanol molecules
Authors: Yujie Wang, Guangyu Liu, Feng Gao, Hongwei Zhang, Aobo Chen, Jiangyuan Pan, Wei Xiong, Meng Jiang, Nana Wang, Xiaofeng Cui, Tingting Kong, Chao Gao, Yujie Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The biocatalytic conversion of green methanol, a promising feedstock, into high value-added products is seen as an attractive way to establish sustainable biomanufacturing. Although natural and engineered microorganisms can utilize methanol, efficiently converting C1 carbon into valuable chemicals in growing cells remains challenging because non-native pathways often suffer from low carbon utilization and limited catalytic stability. Here, we report the integration of an engineered enzyme cascade into resting cells and its coupling with a screened alcohol oxidase enzyme to develop an efficient and stable enzyme-resting cell cascade system for methanol conversion, achieving customizable production of L-erythrulose (C4 sugar) or L-sorbose (C6 sugar). This system shows a stimulated carbon atom economy for L-erythrulose production from methanol and significantly improved stability compared to free enzyme catalysis. By integrating enzyme cascades with durable resting cells, this strategy provides a versatile platform for converting methanol and other C1 substrates into value-added products.

Introduction

Methanol is a basic chemical feedstock that can be produced easily from various carbon sources, such as coal, natural gas, coke oven gas, carbon dioxide and biomass1–3. Due to its liquid state at room temperature and atmospheric pressure, methanol is more convenient to store, transport and use than natural gas4. Commercial green methanol production has begun, with an estimated annual output of about eight million metric tons by 20275. Therefore, using green methanol as a C1 feedstock to produce valuable multi-carbon products is a promising way to achieve a circular carbon economy6,7. Unlike chemical catalytic conversion, biological catalytic conversion of methanol using isolated enzymes or whole cells is an exciting way to produce valuable, complex, long-chain carbon compounds efficiently and selectively under mild conditions8,9. Currently, biomanufacturing predominantly relies on sugars derived from edible crops as feedstocks8, raising ethical concerns about using food for chemical production. Therefore, it is imperative that renewable methanol is used as a low-cost alternative feedstock to facilitate the sustainable development of biomanufacturing.

In recent years, genetically engineered methylotrophic microorganisms have been developed to convert methanol into valuable products. This has been achieved by introducing existing methanol metabolism pathways or new synthetic metabolic pathways into model microorganisms such as Escherichia coli, Saccharomyces cerevisiae and Corynebacterium glutamicum10–15. Two pivotal steps are instrumental in facilitating the metabolism of methanol in the realm of synthetic methylotrophs. Firstly, methanol is oxidized to formaldehyde and, secondly, formaldehyde is assimilated into biomass. Formaldehyde functions as a key intermediate metabolite in this process, entering the central carbon metabolism through different assimilation pathways (e.g., ribulose monophosphate pathway, xylulose-5-phosphate pathway and serine pathway) to support the growth, division and fermentation of methylotrophs16,17. These strategies have achieved notable progress, and both fermentation-based processes and in vitro enzymatic cascades have demonstrated practical potential for methanol bioconversion. Nevertheless, challenges remain in optimizing methanol utilization efficiency in synthetic methylotrophs. For example, the establishment of efficient methanol assimilation often requires coordinated expression of multiple heterologous genes and careful pathway balancing11,12,18,19. In addition, methanol oxidation generates formaldehyde, a reactive intermediate that necessitates tight regulation to maintain cellular homeostasis20,21. Furthermore, carbon flux distribution toward target products can be suboptimal, leading to relatively low yields for certain value-added compounds (typically yielding less than 0.1 g gmethanol-1, Supplementary Table 1)22,23.

In this context, resting cells (i.e., non-growing but metabolically active cells) provide a complementary whole-cell catalytic platform for methanol bioconversion. Rather than replacing established systems, resting cells offer distinct operational advantages that can be beneficial in specific scenarios. This is because they possess several competitive advantages: (i) they enable the conversion of certain cytotoxic substrates or intermediate metabolites that are inaccessible to growing cells, as the growth-arrested state of resting cells makes them more stable in complex environments24; (ii) they offer high product yields by separating the cell growth and production phases, which reduces unnecessary carbon shunting to intermediate metabolites25; (iii) they improve the economics of biomanufacturing, as resting cells can be recycled and reused26; and (iv) they simplify the preparation process of biocatalysts (i.e., proteins expression and purification)24,27–29. However, to fully exploit the potential of resting cells for methanol bioconversion and achieve efficient carbon chain extension from the key intermediate formaldehyde, novel assimilation pathways beyond natural routes are highly desirable.

Unlike natural formaldehyde metabolism pathways, artificial formolase (Fls) has been reported to catalyze the carboligation of three molecules of formaldehyde to form a three-carbon dihydroxyacetone (DHA), providing a key carboligation step for the non-natural formaldehyde assimilation route toward artificial C1 fixation30. Beyond that, D-fructose-6-phosphate aldolase (Fsa), a stereoselective aldolase from E. coli, has been demonstrated to catalyze stereocontrolled cross-aldol additions of aldehydes and hydroxylated ketones31. The A129S mutant Fsa (FsaA129S), an enzyme in which alanine-129 is replaced by serine, significantly enhances the conversion of formaldehyde and DHA31–33. Therefore, an artificial formaldehyde metabolism pathway can be designed to customize sugar production by coupling formolase with stereoselective aldolase in a rational manner.

Here, we present a design for an engineered resting cell system that can be integrated into processes for the biocatalytic production of rare natural sugars from methanol, using an artificially designed formaldehyde metabolism pathway (Fig. 1). This approach uses inexpensive industrial methanol as a feedstock to produce L-erythrulose (Supplementary Table 2), a rare and valuable natural ketose with significant industrial applications as a food additive, cosmetic ingredient, pharmaceutical precursor and raw material for other rare sugars. In this approach, methanol is first oxidized by alcohol oxidase (Aox) from Pichia pastoris in the presence of oxygen to form abundant formaldehyde in vitro. The hydrogen peroxide generated during methanol oxidation is decomposed into water and oxygen by catalase (Cat) from Bacillus methanolicus to eliminate the detrimental effects of excessive hydrogen peroxide on the biocatalytic system. The integration of exogenous Fls and FsaA129S within engineered resting cells via gene editing and overexpression enables efficient and sustainable formaldehyde conversion to L-erythrulose in vivo. Compared to conventional microbial fermentation from glucose-derived erythritol, our system offers a methanol-derived route. The biological catalytic conversion of methanol to formaldehyde using isolated enzymes renders it particularly biocompatible as a feeding stock in biocatalytic systems. Furthermore, the engineered resting E. coli cell platform, which is designed for relatively concise genetic modification, shows promise for industrial-scale applications. Additionally, the modularity of the enzyme cascade pathway in these resting cells demonstrates the ability to convert methanol into another C6 sugar (L-sorbose), providing a simple yet versatile biocatalytic platform for the conversion of methanol into various products.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the conversion of methanol to L-erythrulose catalyzed by an enzyme-resting cell cascade system.The enzyme catalyzes the conversion of methanol to formaldehyde and the resting cell catalyzes the conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose.

Results

Designing an artificial pathway for methanol conversion

Taking inspiration from methylotrophs, we constructed an artificial methanol conversion pathway using four enzymes (i.e., Aox, Cat, Fls and FsaA129S, Fig. 2a, b). The oxidation of methanol to formaldehyde is a critical step in the bio-conversion of methanol13,34,35. To optimize the conversion of methanol to formaldehyde, we selected four different enzymes capable of this conversion: alcohol dehydrogenase (YqhD) from E. coli MG1655; NAD-dependent alcohol dehydrogenase (Mdh) from Cupriavidus necator N-1; alcohol dehydrogenase 1 (Adh1) from S. cerevisiae; and alcohol oxidase (Aox) from P. pastoris (Supplementary Fig. 1a and Supplementary Table 3). Among, the oxygen-dependent alcohol oxidase is thermodynamically more favorable for the methanol-to-formaldehyde conversion than the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD)-dependent methanol dehydrogenase (Supplementary Fig. 2 and Supplementary Table 4)36. Of these enzymes, Aox exhibits the most impressive catalytic activity for methanol oxidation, achieving a formaldehyde production rate of 4.2 ± 0.22 mM h−1 (Supplementary Figs. 1b and 3, and Supplementary Tables 5 and 6). However, the methanol oxidation process catalyzed by Aox is accompanied by the generation of hydrogen peroxide, which can cause various oxidative stress reactions with biological macromolecules such as proteins, lipids and nucleic acids37. In view of this fact, Cat from B. methanolicus, which is widely used to eliminate hydrogen peroxide generated during methanol oxidation12,38,39, was introduced into our system. This significantly improved formaldehyde production. After 1 h of reaction, the formaldehyde production activity catalyzed by the Aox and Cat enzyme cascade was increased by 94.44% compared with that of Aox alone, with its yield reaching 3.15 ± 0.19 mM (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, the effect of the different components (methanol, Aox and Cat) on the reaction was evaluated to optimize the conversion of methanol to formaldehyde (Supplementary Fig. 4). Following the synthetic roadmap, we then attempted to construct an enzyme cascade pathway for the conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose by employing Fls and FsaA129S. In this pathway, Fls catalyzes the conversion of three formaldehyde molecules to dihydroxyacetone, while FsaA129S catalyzes the conversion of one dihydroxyacetone and one formaldehyde molecule to L-erythrulose. Product yields of dihydroxyacetone and L-erythrulose show a positive correlation with substrate concentration (Supplementary Fig. 5). FsaA129S significantly enhances the condensation of formaldehyde and DHA, resulting in a 3.3-fold increase in the catalytic rate of L-erythrulose production compared to wild-type Fsa (Supplementary Fig. 6). We further verified the conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose catalyzed by Fls and FsaA129S. As expected, conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose increased with prolonged reaction time. After 1 h, 7.78 ± 0.34 mM of L-erythrulose was obtained from 50 mM of formaldehyde, representing a 63% conversion of formaldehyde (Fig. 2d). The Fls-FsaA129S enzyme cascade exhibits tolerance to high doses of formaldehyde, even up to 100 mM, while maintaining a high conversion rate of 80% (Supplementary Fig. 7).

To achieve the conversion of methanol to L-erythrulose, we brought together Aox, Cat, Fls and FsaA129S and verified their collaborative activity in vitro. As expected, methanol was successfully converted to formaldehyde and then to L-erythrulose. Over the course of the reaction, 500 mM of methanol was initially oxidized by Aox and Cat to produce formaldehyde, reaching a concentration of 56.47 ± 1.11 mM after 12 h. Subsequently, the formaldehyde was converted into L-erythrulose, catalyzed by Fls and FsaA129S. After 12 h, significant amounts of L-erythrulose were detected, with a concentration of 6.31 ± 0.03 mM (Fig. 2e). However, with increasing methanol concentration, the yield of L-erythrulose increased gradually. In contrast, methanol conversion exhibited an initial increase followed by a decrease (Fig. 2f). As mentioned above, the catalytic activity of Aox increases with methanol concentration (Supplementary Fig. 4b). As elevated methanol concentrations can significantly alter the secondary structures of enzymes, thereby affecting the efficiency of biocatalytic processes40, we conclude that a high methanol concentration (>100 mM) primarily has a detrimental effect on the catalytic activity of Fls and FsaA129S. Examining the cascade activity of Fls and FsaA129S at different methanol concentrations reveals a significant decrease in the formaldehyde-to-L-erythrulose conversion catalyzed by Fls and FsaA129S as the methanol concentration increases (Supplementary Fig. 8, and Supplementary Note 1). To understand the deactivation mechanism at high methanol concentrations, we used circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy to analyze the secondary structure of the protein, including helix, beta, turn and random41. Following incubation with 500 mM methanol, significant alterations were observed in the secondary structures of Fls and FsaA129S. A marked increase in the random structure was observed in both proteins (Fig. 2g, and Supplementary Fig. 9). This finding suggests that methanol is toxic to Fls and FsaA129S as a substrate in the enzyme cascade system, posing a significant challenge to the sustainable biocatalytic conversion of methanol to L-erythrulose in vitro using an artificial enzyme cascade system.

Utilizing resting cells for methanol conversion

Compared to enzymatic catalysis, whole-cell catalysis is a more convenient and stable approach for the biotransformation of carbonaceous products27. Furthermore, resting cells do not require the protein purification and immobilization procedures required for immobilized enzymes, thus significantly streamlining the workflow42. Given that whole-cell catalysis provides a stable catalytic environment for biological enzymes, we incorporated an artificial enzyme cascade pathway into E. coli resting cells via gene engineering and verified their tolerance to methanol.

To introduce the constructed enzymatic cascade pathway into E. coli resting cells, we validated the catalytic activities of the enzymes involved (i.e., Aox with Cat, Fls, and FsaA129S) within the intracellular environment (Fig. 3a). It should be noted that overexpressing aox in E. coli BL21(DE3) is challenging, as it is typically localized and functionally active in yeast peroxisomes. Given these considerations, Western blot analysis was employed to validate the intracellular expression of Aox. The results revealed trace-level expression of Aox in E. coli, which provided an opportunity for the conversion of methanol to formaldehyde in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 10, and Supplementary Note 2). In addition, marginal formaldehyde production catalyzed by intracellular Aox was detected in the cell supernatant in the presence of 500 mM methanol and Cat, at a concentration of 44.3 ± 1.38 μM over 6 h (Fig. 3b). What’s more, no significant formaldehyde production was detected when Aox was expressed alone in cells compared to the control group, indicating that Cat plays a critical role in Aox-mediated oxidation of methanol to formaldehyde. Interestingly, despite Fls exhibiting poor cellular kinetics (Supplementary Table 6), detectable levels of DHA were consistently observed in the supernatant of resting cells overexpressing Fls. The productivity reached 37.65 ± 1.79 μM h-1 under 50 mM formaldehyde (Fig. 3c, and Supplementary Fig. 11). Furthermore, FsaA129S demonstrated a prime activity for the condensation of formaldehyde and DHA, achieving 18.35 ± 0.04 mM by 20 mM formaldehyde and DHA for 30 min (Fig. 3d, and Supplementary Fig. 11). As expected, resting cells of E. coli BL21(DE3) overexpressing Fls or FsaA129S exhibited enhanced tolerance to methanol compared to free enzymes. This was particularly evident in resting cells of E. coli overexpressing Fls (Fig. 3e, and Supplementary Fig. 12). To elucidate the effects of methanol on resting cells, RNA sequencing and untargeted metabolomics were performed on E. coli BL21(DE3) (Supplementary Fig. 13). Methanol exposure significantly upregulated oxidative stress-related genes (e.g., grcA, clpA, and uspF), indicating activation of stress-response mechanisms to maintain intracellular homeostasis (Fig. 3f). Gene ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) further revealed that methanol markedly perturbs pathways associated with amino acid metabolism, oxidative stress response, and transporters (Figs. 3g, h, and Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15). At the metabolic level, NAD abundance was significantly increased and positively correlated with phosphatidylethanolamine metabolism, suggesting enhanced membrane stability (Fig. 3i). Collectively, these results demonstrate that methanol induces coordinated oxidative stress and metabolic adaptation in resting cells, which contributes to maintaining cellular integrity and catalytic activity under methanol conditions.

Given that E. coli BL21(DE3) resting cells exhibit enhanced tolerance to methanol, the next step is to utilize the artificial methanol metabolism pathway in these cells to convert methanol into L-erythrulose. Firstly, the frmA gene of encoding S-(hydroxymethyl)glutathione dehydrogenase43 which involves the dissimilation of intracellular formaldehyde to carbon dioxide, was deleted in E. coli BL21(DE3) using λ-Red homologous recombination technology to enhance the intracellular conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose (Supplementary Fig. 16). The above results have demonstrated that overexpression of Aox and Cat in E. coli could catalyze the sluggish generation of formaldehyde from methanol (7.38 ± 0.23 μM h-1). Therefore, the artificial methanol metabolism pathway (i.e., Aox, Cat, Fls and FsaA129S) was overexpressed in E. coli ΔfrmA to produce L-erythrulose from methanol (Supplementary Fig. 17a, and Supplementary Note 3). Unfortunately, this yielded only 9.30 ± 0.58 μM of L-erythrulose after 2 h, with methanol conversion remaining at just 0.007% (Supplementary Fig. 17b, c). This unexpectedly low conversion efficiency suggests that integrating the artificial pathway into resting cells is insufficient to enable effective methanol utilization. This limitation may arise from multiple factors, including inefficient methanol oxidation, inadequate channeling of the key intermediate formaldehyde, or incompatibility between upstream and downstream enzymatic steps.

Catalyzing formaldehyde-to-L-erythrulose conversion with resting cells

These observations prompted us to systematically dissect the methanol-to-product conversion process. To this end, we first decoupled the pathway by directly using formaldehyde as the substrate, thereby decoupling the downstream carbon chain extension module to evaluate its intrinsic catalytic performance. Accordingly, the overexpressed plasmid (pET28a-T7-fls-fsaA129S) was constructed to overexpress Fls and FsaA129S in the E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔfrmA for the formaldehyde-to-L-erythrulose conversion (Fig. 4a, and Supplementary Fig. 18a). As a result, 12.16 ± 0.31 mM L-erythrulose were produced from 100 mM formaldehyde by resting cells of E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔfrmA overexpressing Fls and FsaA129S (denote as E. coli ΔfrmA FA) after 2 h, exhibiting a formaldehyde-to-L-erythrulose conversion of 48% (Fig. 4b, and Supplementary Fig. 18b, c). Following treatment with 250 mM methanol, E. coli ΔfrmA FA exhibited a 1.5-fold increase in tolerance to methanol compared to free enzymes, retaining 96% of its activity in the conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose (Fig. 4c). In order to explore its potential applications, the cycling stability of the formaldehyde-to-L-erythrulose conversion catalyzed by E. coli ΔfrmA FA was examined. It was found that resting cells significantly improved the cycling stability of the formaldehyde-to-L-erythrulose conversion (Fig. 4d). The catalytic activity of E. coli ΔfrmA FA was found to remain at 83% after three cycles, which increased the catalytic activity for L-erythrulose production by a factor of ten compared to the free enzyme system. Furthermore, the catalytic activity of E. coli ΔfrmA FA remained at approximately 50% after five cycles, whereas the free enzyme system showed complete inactivation. The aforementioned observations collectively suggest that the rate-limiting step in the in vivo conversion of methanol to L-erythrulose is mediated by the formaldehyde oxidation process catalyzed by Aox and Cat. This limitation may be associated with the distinct environmental conditions between the E. coli cytoplasm and yeast peroxisomes, which likely result in reduced methanol oxidation activity of Aox in E. coli. However, the above comparison shows that using resting cells as catalysts for methanol conversion has several advantages over using free enzymes, including satisfactory methanol tolerance and cycling stability. Furthermore, preparing purified free enzymes requires complex processes, whereas resting cells that overexpress target proteins significantly reduce the time and cost of protein purification, providing a suitable platform for methanol conversion.

Catalyzing methanol-to-sugar conversion with an enzyme-resting cell cascade system

To convert methanol to L-erythrulose, we created an enzyme-recycling cell cascade system. This system integrates methanol-to-formaldehyde conversion, catalyzed by enzymes (Aox and Cat), and formaldehyde-to-L-erythrulose conversion, catalyzed by E. coli ΔfrmA FA (Fig. 5a). In this system, a high concentration of formaldehyde was first produced in vitro from methanol catalyzed by Aox and Cat, and the reaction solution was then used for the conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose catalyzed by E. coli ΔfrmA FA. As a result, a significant amount of L-erythrulose was identified in the supernatant as the reaction progressed (Fig. 5b, and Supplementary Fig. 20). Furthermore, 13C-labeled methanol was used as the sole carbon source to trace the origin of L-erythrulose. The results showed that 84.03% of erythrulose was fully labeled, with 91.32% of its carbon atoms derived from 13C-methanol. The remaining carbon likely originated from the endogenous folate-mediated one-carbon cycle44 (Fig. 5c, Supplementary Fig. 21, and Supplementary Note 4). To improve the conversion efficiency of the enzyme-resting cell cascade system, the reaction parameters (i.e., reaction time, cell density, substrate concentration and temperature) were systematically optimized (Supplementary Fig. 22). The maximum yield of L-erythrulose from methanol, when catalyzed by the enzyme-resting cell cascade system, is 350 mg gmethanol-1, with a maximum methanol conversion rate of 37% (Supplementary Fig. 23). To further identify factors limiting conversion efficiency, 13C-tracer analysis was performed during the conversion of 13C-labelled methanol to L-erythrulose in E. coli ΔfrmA FA. The results showed that methanol-derived formaldehyde was partially assimilated into central metabolism via intermediates such as glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate, entering pathways including the EMP pathway and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (Fig. 5d, and Supplementary Figs. 24 and 25). However, while labeling was observed in key metabolites such as Asp (~45%), its downstream derivatives (e.g., isoleucine and lysine) showed minimal labeling, indicating limited propagation of carbon flux into biosynthetic pathways. This pattern is consistent with the reduced metabolic activity of resting cells.

Furthermore, resting cells benefit from the growth-arrested state and dominant expression of catalytic proteins (e.g., Fls and FsaA129S), which gives them significant advantages over growing cells in large-scale systems27. This system shows no significant decrease in L-erythrulose production with the expansion of reaction volume, paving the way for future industrial scale-up (Supplementary Fig. 26). Furthermore, E. coli ΔfrmA FA exhibited substrate versatility in converting 37% (w/v) industrial-grade formaldehyde feedstock into L-erythrulose (Supplementary Fig. 27, Supplementary Table 7, and Supplementary Note 5). Beyond that, given the substrate diversity and stereoselectivity of FsaA129S, our enzyme-resting cell cascade system has the potential to produce other sugars. For example, based on our previous work33, we have introduced the same core enzyme cascade consisting of Fls and Fsa into our resting-cell system (named as E. coli ΔfrmA FF) to achieve de novo synthesis of L-sorbose from methanol (Supplementary Fig. 28, and Supplementary Note 6). At an optimal modular ratio of 2:1 between E. coli ΔfrmA FF and E. coli ΔfrmA FA, 5.37 ± 0.12 mM of L-sorbose can be produced after 24 h of reaction (Fig. 5e, Supplementary Figs. 28 and 29, and Supplementary Note 7). In principle, the enzyme-resting cells systems can be programmed to produce different products such as glycolate45, glycol46, L-threitol47, lactic acid48, and hexoses (e.g., glucose, allulose and mannose)49, by paving different metabolic pathways (Supplementary Fig. 30, and Supplementary Note 8), providing a promising platform for converting methanol or other C1 molecules into customizable multi-carbon products.

Discussion

In summary, using bioinformatic analysis and genetic engineering, we have demonstrated an intriguing hybrid enzyme-resting cell cascade system that couples an enzyme module (Aox and Cat) with resting cells of E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔfrmA overexpressing Fls and FsaA129S. The system provides a customizable, sustainable, scalable and carbon-neutral approach to the conversion of methanol to rare natural sugars (e.g., L-erythrulose and L-sorbose). At room temperature and atmospheric pressure with methanol as the sole carbon source, this biocatalytic system achieves the conversion of methanol to rare ketose (L-erythrulose) with a high yield of 350 mg gmethanol-1, which is a high level of methanol conversion compared to the state-of-the-art works. Impressively, this system for methanol conversion exhibits enhanced methanol tolerance and cycling stability compared to free enzymes in vivo, thereby achieving a balance between activity and stability. The modular design principles underlying this system allow generalizable adaptation to diverse metabolic pathways, extending its potential to produce not only sugars but also other multi-carbon products, such as glycolate, glycol, L-threitol, lactic acid and hexoses (e.g., glucose, allulose and mannose). Moreover, the self-replicating capacity of the engineered strains enables low-cost, scalable production of biocatalysts, facilitating translation toward industrial methanol bioconversion. This work provides a versatile platform for advancing C1-based biomanufacturing.

However, the present study remains at the preliminary laboratory exploration stage. Further extensive and systematic engineering is essential to advance this resting-cell cascade system toward practical commercial production, including improving the stability and catalytic efficiency of key enzymes, engineering the host strain for enhanced robustness and adaptability to industrial conditions, and optimizing large-scale bioprocess parameters. What’s more, the current system is limited to methanol as the sole C1 feedstock, and future work will focus on expanding substrate scope and enhancing platform flexibility for broader chemical production. Additionally, it is important to acknowledge a limitation of our current resting cell biocatalytic system: the need for an extra carbon source, typically glucose, during the initial phase of cell growth for biomass production, and then again when preparing the resting cells. This external carbon input is inefficient in terms of the overall carbon utilization of the process, as it reduces the contribution of methanol as the sole target feedstock for the desired product. Addressing this challenge is a key focus for future optimization. Promising strategies include exploring the use of low-value or waste-derived carbon sources, such as acetate, formate or glycolate, for cell growth50–52. Furthermore, advances in metabolic engineering and synthetic biology hold significant potential for developing E. coli strains that can achieve sufficient product yields using methanol as the sole or main external carbon source, with no or minimal additional organic co-substrates. These strains could potentially leverage C1 compounds themselves during the growth phase in an integrated future process. Furthermore, from an industrial perspective, through rational design of the flow system, the two-step conversion reactions can be integrated into a combined flow platform. Progress in this direction would substantially enhance the carbon efficiency and sustainability of our methanol-to-sugar conversion platform.

Strains and plasmids

All strains and plasmids used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 1. All amino acid sequences of enzymes used in this study are listed in Supplementary Data 2. E. coli DH5α was used as the host to construct all the vectors in this study. E. coli BL21(DE3) was used for overexpression of yqhD, mdh, cat, fls, fsa and fsaA129S. E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔfrmA was constructed using λ-Red homologous recombination and serves as a factory to produce L-erythrulose. The pKD4, pKD46 and pCP20 were used for λ-Red homologous recombination. The cat was cloned into pET26b using MscI and XhoI. Gene sequencing and primers synthesis services are provided by General biol (Anhui, China, Supplementary Table 8).

Expression and purification of YqhD, Mdh, Cat, Fls, Fsa and FsaA129S

Based on the codon preference of E. coli BL21(DE3), the target gene was codon optimized to facilitate the expression of the exogenous gene. The protein expression plasmids were transformed into competent E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (Supplementary Data 1). The expression and purified these proteins were performed as previously described33. In brief, the recombinant Fsa and FsaA129S were expressed using 0.1 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 20 h, and the recombinant Cat and Fls were expressed using 0.5 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 20 h. The overexpressing Fls and FsaA129S were induced using 1 mM IPTG at 16 °C for 20 h. To obtain the target protein, Ni-NTA resins binding the target proteins were washed with wash buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 2 mM imidazole), and then eluted by elution buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 50 mM imidazole). The purified proteins were concentrated by ultrafiltration and dispersed at stock buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl). The purified proteins were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis. The concentrations of proteins were quantified using Bradford Protein Assay Kit, with bovine serum albumin as a standard protein (Supplementary Fig. 31).

Analysis of formaldehyde, dihydroxyacetone and L-erythrulose

Formaldehyde was quantified by acetyl acetone method53. The assay solution contained 0.02 M acetylacetone, 0.05 M acetic acid and 2 M ammonium acetate. 1 mL sample and 100 μL of the acetylacetone assay solution were used to react at 60 °C for 15 min. The OD414 was detected when the reaction solution reached room temperature. The standard curve of formaldehyde dissolved in phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.2) was used for standard calibration (Supplementary Fig. 32).

Dihydroxyacetone, L-erythrulose, L-glyceraldehyde and L-sorbose were measured using an analytical high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) system (Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity II) on a Bio-Rad Aminex HPX-87H instrument with a 300 × 7.8 mm column. Samples (20 μL) were injected and eluted under the following conditions: 5 mM H2SO4 at a flow rate of 0.5 ml min−1 under 50 °C, 40 min run time per sample, detection using both refractive index (RI) and ultraviolet (UV) at 192 nm detectors. The amount of product was quantified from the peak areas. The standard curve of dihydroxyacetone, L-erythrulose, L-glyceraldehyde and L-sorbose dissolved in 50 mM TEA buffer (pH 7.2) was used for standard calibration (Supplementary Fig. 33).

Quantification and activity assay of purified enzymes

The activity of Aox for methanol to formaldehyde was measured in phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.2). The reaction mixture (20 mM methanol, 5 mM MgCl2 and 1U Aox) was incubated at 30 °C and subsequently boiled to deactivate the enzyme. Different concentrations of methanol (0.5, 1, 50, 500 mM), Aox (0.1, 0.5, 1, 5, 10 μ mL-1) and Cat (0.01, 0.1, 0.25, 0.5, 1 mg mL-1) were reacted in phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.2) at 30 °C for 1 h. Formaldehyde was quantified by the acetylacetone method as mentioned in the previous “Analysis of formaldehyde, dihydroxyacetone and L-erythrulose” section.

The activity of Fls for formaldehyde-to-dihydroxyacetone conversion was measured in 50 mM TEA buffer (pH 7.2). The reaction mixture (25 mM formaldehyde, 5 mM MgCl2 and 0.5 mg mL-1 Fls) was incubated at 30 °C and subsequently boiled to deactivate the enzyme. Dihydroxyacetone was quantified by HPLC analysis.

The activity of Fsa and FsaA129S for formaldehyde and dihydroxyacetone to L-erythrulose was measured in 50 mM TEA buffer (pH 7.2). The reaction mixture (25 mM formaldehyde, 25 mM dihydroxyacetone, 5 mM MgCl2 and 0.1 mg mL-1 Fsa or FsaA129S) was incubated at 30 °C and subsequently boiled to deactivate the enzyme. L-erythrulose was quantified by HPLC analysis.

The activity of Fls and FsaA129S for formaldehyde to L-erythrulose was measured in 50 mM TEA buffer (pH 7.2). The reaction mixture (50 mM formaldehyde, 5 mM MgCl2, 0.5 mg mL-1 Fls and 0.1 mg mL-1 FsaA129S) was incubated at 30 °C and subsequently boiled to deactivate the enzyme. L-erythrulose was quantified by HPLC analysis.

The activity of Aox, Cat, Fls and FsaA129S for methanol to L-erythrulose was measured in phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.2). The reaction mixture (500 mM methanol, 5 mM MgCl2, 10 μ mL-1 Aox, 1 mg mL-1 Cat) was incubated at 30 °C for 12 h, and then 1 mg mL-1 Fls and 0.5 mg ml-1 FsaA129S were added to catalyze the conversion of formaldehyde to L-erythrulose at 30 °C. Formaldehyde was quantified by acetyl acetone method and L-erythrulose was quantified by HPLC analysis.

Western blot analysis

The Aox and Cat were overexpressed in BL21(DE3). Cells were collected by centrifugation (2500 × g, 10 min at 4 °C) and lysed in RIPA buffer for 15 min at ice bath. Then the lysis solution was further centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C to obtain supernatant. After quantifying protein concentration, SDS-PAGE loading buffer was added and denatured at 95 °C for 5 min, and then incubated on ice for 10 min. Samples were electrophoresed on SDS-PAGE gels with acrylamide concentrations optimized for target protein molecular weights, employing an initial voltage of 80 V through the stacking gel followed by 120 V in the separating gel until bromophenol blue tracking dye reached the gel periphery. Subsequently, proteins were transferred to methanol-preactivated PVDF membranes via wet transfer methodology (300 mA constant current, 3 h). Membranes were blocked with 5% (w/v) skim milk in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.1% Tween-20 (TBST) for 1 h at ambient temperature. Primary antibody incubations were performed with His-Tag monoclonal antibody (1:2000) and GAPDH monoclonal antibody (1:20,000) in blocking buffer at 4 °C overnight. Following three 10-min TBST washes, membranes were probed with species-specific HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (1:5000 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Post-secondary antibody incubation, membranes underwent three rapid TBST rinses (≤30 s each) prior to chemiluminescent detection. Enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) reagent was prepared by mixing commercial ECL Solution A and B at a 1:1 volumetric ratio. After uniform application of ECL substrate, membranes were incubated for precisely 60 s before implementing optimized radiographic exposure protocols based on real-time luminescence intensity monitoring.

RNA sequencing

To verify the effect of methanol to genes expression of E. coli BL21(DE3) resting cells, resting cells were treated with 500 mM methanol at 30 °C for 2 h. The bacterial pellets (~107 cells) were taken, and total RNA was isolated using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen Life Technologies). Quality and integrity were determined using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific) and a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). Zymo-Seq RiboFree Total RNA Library Kit was used to remove rRNA from total RNA. Random oligonucleotides and SuperScript III were used to synthesize the first strand cDNA. Second strand cDNA synthesis was subsequently performed using DNA Polymerase I and RNase H. Remaining overhangs were converted into blunt ends via exonuclease/polymerase activities and the enzymes were removed. After adenylation of the 3′ ends of the DNA fragments, Illumina PE adapter oligonucleotides were ligated to prepare for hybridization. To select cDNA fragments of the preferred 400–500 bp in length, the library fragments were purified using the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, CA, USA). Products were purified (AMPure XP system) and quantified using the Agilent high sensitivity DNA assay on a Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent). The sequencing library was then sequenced on NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina) by Shanghai Personal Biotechnology Cp., Ltd. R packages such as DESeq, Pheatmap and ClusterProfiler were used to analyze the sequencing data.

Untargeted metabolomics

To verify the effect of methanol to cellular metabolism of E. coli BL21(DE3) resting cells, resting cells were treated with 500 mM methanol at 30 °C for 2 h. The bacterial pellets (~107 cells) were taken and mixed with 1 mL of extraction solution [MeOH: ACN: H2O, 2: 2: 1 (v/v)] that contained deuterated internal standards, and the mixed solution was vortexed for 30 s. Homogenization beads were added and homogenized for 4 min, then transferred to an ice-water bath to sonicate for 5 min. The samples were then allowed to thaw at room temperature and vortexed for 30 s. This freeze–thaw cycle was repeated three times. Then the samples were sonicated for 10 min in 4 °C water bath and incubated for 1 h at −40 °C to precipitate proteins. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh glass vial for analysis. For polar metabolite analysis, a Vanquish ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography (UHPLC) system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used. Chromatographic separation of target compounds was performed on a Waters ACQUITY UPLC BEH Amide column (2.1 mm × 50 mm, 1.7 μm). The mobile phase consisted of 25 mmol L-1 ammonium acetate and 25 mmol L-1 ammonium hydroxide in water as solvent A, and acetonitrile as solvent B. The autosampler was maintained at 4 °C, and the injection volume was 2 μL. Mass spectrometry data were acquired using an Orbitrap Exploris 120 mass spectrometer operating under the control of Xcalibur software (version 4.4, Thermo Fisher Scientific) in both MS and MS/MS modes. The detailed instrument parameters were as follows: sheath gas flow rate, 50 Arb; auxiliary gas flow rate, 15 Arb; capillary temperature, 320 °C; full MS resolution, 60,000; MS/MS resolution, 15,000; stepped normalized collision energy (SNCE), 20/30/40; and spray voltage, 3.8 kV in positive ion mode and −3.4 kV in negative ion mode. Raw mass spectrometry data files were converted to the mzXML format using the MSConvert tool in the ProteoWizard package (v3.0.8789). Peak detection, peak filtering, and retention time alignment were performed using the XCMS package in R, generating a quantitative metabolite feature table. To minimize batch effects and instrumental drift, signal intensities were corrected using a quality control (QC)-based locally weighted scatterplot smoothing (LOESS) normalization approach. Metabolite features with a relative standard deviation (RSD) greater than 30% in QC samples were excluded from further analysis. Metabolite annotation was performed by matching accurate mass and MS/MS spectra against public databases, including HMDB, MassBank, LipidMaps, mzCloud, and KEGG, together with an in-house spectral library. The metabolomics data have been deposited to MetaboLights54 repository with the study identifier MTBLS14812.

Construction of frmA gene-knockout E. coli BL21(DE3)

E. coli BL21(DE3) ΔfrmA was constructed using λ-Red homologous recombination. The frmA knockout primers are shown in Supplementary Table 8. The pKD46 plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21(DE3), and then 1 mM of L-arabinose was used to induce recombinant enzyme expression to obtain competent cells. The donor DNA for frmA knockout was amplified by using pKD4 as a template, and then the donor DNA was transformed into the competent cells that express λ-Red recombinase, followed by overnight incubation at 30 °C. After dilution and spreading on kanamycin-resistant plates, single colonies were allowed to grow. PCR screening was performed to identify mutant strains with successful homologous recombination. The pCP20 plasmid was transformed into the successfully recombined electrocompetent cells, and positive colonies were selected using ampicillin. Finally, a positive colony was incubated at 42 °C to eliminate the pCP20 plasmid.

Activity assay of enzymes-resting cell cascade system

13C-labelled metabolites tracer analysis

The analysis of 13C-labelled amino acid and metabolites in the central metabolic pathway (TCA and EMP) was conducted using the E. coli BL21(DE3) resting cells overexpressing Fls and FsaA129S following the methanol-to-formaldehyde reaction at 30 °C for 4 h. These cells were then resuspended in 0.6 mL cold (4 °C) 50% aqueous methanol containing 100 µM norvaline as an internal standard and inserted in dry ice for 30 min. After that, the samples were thawed on ice. Subsequently, 0.4 mL of chloroform was added and vortexed for 30 s before centrifugation for 10 min at 14,000 rpm (4 °C). The supernatant was then transferred to new 1.5 mL tubes for evaporation, followed by storage at 80 °C prior to analysis. Metabolites were derivatized for GC/MS analysis in accordance with the following procedure: Firstly, 70 μL of pyridine was added to the dried pellet and incubated for 20 min at 80 °C. Following cooling, 30 µL of N-tert-butyldimethylsilyl-N-methyltrifluoroacetamide (Sigma) was added, and the samples were re-incubated for 60 min at 80 °C, followed by the centrifugation for 10 min at 14,000 rpm (4 °C). The supernatant was transferred to an autosampler vial for GC/MS analysis. A Shimadzu QP 2010 Ultra GC-MS was programmed with an injection temperature of 250 °C and injected with 1 µL of sample. The temperature of the GC oven commenced at 110 °C for 4 min, subsequently rising to 230 °C at a rate of 3 °C/min and to 280 °C at a rate of 20 °C/min, with a final hold at this temperature for 2 min. The flow rate of the helium carrier gas in the GC was 50 cm/s. The GC column used was a 20 m × 0.25 mm × 0.25 mm Rxi-5ms. The temperature of the GC-MS interface was set to 300 °C, while the electron impact ion source temperature was adjusted to 200 °C, with an ionization voltage of 70 V. The mass spectrometer was set to perform a scan within the m/z range of 50–800, utilizing a 1 kV detector.

Analysis of 13C-labelled L-erythrulose

To verify that the L-erythrulose produced by the enzyme-resting cell cascade system was entirely derived from methanol, 13C-labelled methanol was added to the enzyme-resting cell cascade as a carbon source in phosphate buffer solution (pH 7.2). The experimental group consisted of reaction mixtures using 13C-labelled methanol as the sole carbon source, whereas the control group used 12C-labelled methanol under identical reaction conditions. The analysis was performed once for each group (n = 1) to determine the isotopic labeling pattern. 13C-labelled L-erythrulose was analyzed using a TripleTOF 4600 hybrid quadrupole time-of-flight mass spectrometer (SCIEX, USA) equipped with a dual electrospray ionization/atmospheric pressure chemical ionization (ESI/APCI) source. Reaction solutions were collected after reaction 24 h and immediately centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to remove cells. The resulting supernatants were mixed thoroughly with pre-cooled (−20 °C) extraction solvent (methanol/acetonitrile/water, 2:2:1, v/v/v). After centrifugation, the clarified supernatants were collected, evaporated to dryness under a gentle stream of nitrogen, and reconstituted in methanol solvent for LC-MS analysis. Prior to injection, all samples were passed through 0.22 μm membrane filters. Samples were separated on a HILIC-Amide analytical column using an isocratic mobile phase consisting of acetonitrile and water (60:40, v/v). Mass spectrometric data were acquired in electrospray ionization (ESI) mode over a quadrupole mass range of m/z 20–1250. External mass calibration was performed before each analytical batch to ensure mass accuracy. The data was analyzed by PeakView software (version 2.2). HRMS calcd for C4H7O4 [M-H]-:119.0339.
