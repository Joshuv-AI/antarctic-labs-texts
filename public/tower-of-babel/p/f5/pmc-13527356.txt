Diagnostic performance of Node-RADS in assessing regional lymph node metastasis and prognosis in resected gastric cancer
Authors: Wang Li, Honglang Li, Wenbin Shu, Bin Lai, Lin Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic value of the preoperative CT-based Node Reporting and Data System (Node-RADS) score for lymph node metastasis (LNM) and its prognostic significance for disease-free survival (DFS) in NAC-naive, radically resected non-metastatic gastric cancer (GC, pTNM stage I–III), and to construct nomogram models for predicting LNM and DFS.MethodsThis retrospective study included NAC-naive GC patients who underwent radical gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy between January 2019 and December 2022. Node-RADS scores were independently assessed by two radiologists. Logistic and Cox regression analyses were performed to identify independent predictors of LNM and DFS. Nomogram models were developed and evaluated using area under the curve (AUC), concordance index (C-index), calibration curves, and decision curve analysis.ResultsA total of 150 patients were included (median age 61 years). Pathological LNM was present in 72.7% of patients. Node-RADS scores were significantly associated with tumor size, CEA level, pTNM stage, N stage, and recurrence (all P < 0.05). Inter- and intra-observer agreement were good (κ = 0.746 and 0.798). Tumor size, CA19-9, and Node-RADS score were independent predictors of LNM. The LNM nomogram achieved an AUC of 0.88. During follow-up, 34.7% of patients developed recurrence, and Node-RADS score was significantly associated with DFS.ConclusionPreoperative CT-based Node-RADS scoring shows good diagnostic and prognostic value in gastric cancer and may assist in preoperative risk stratification.

Introduction

Gastric cancer (GC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, ranking fifth in incidence and fourth in mortality. 1 As a vital pathological characteristic of gastric cancer, regional lymph node metastasis (LNM) fundamentally dictates staging criteria, clinical management schemes, and individual survival outcomes.2,3 Radical gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy is the standard curative treatment for resectable GC, 4 and accurate preoperative assessment of regional LNM is essential to guide surgical planning, determine the need for neoadjuvant/adjuvant therapy, and stratify patient risk.

Computed tomography (CT) is the most commonly used imaging modality for preoperative staging of GC, owing to its high spatial resolution, wide availability, and ability to evaluate both the primary tumor and regional/distant metastases. 5 Conventional CT assessment of LNM relies primarily on LN size (short-axis diameter ≥10 mm as a threshold for malignancy), yet this criterion suffers from limited diagnostic accuracy. 6 Reactive hyperplastic LNs can present as enlarged nodules, while small micrometastatic LNs may remain undetected by size alone, leading to misdiagnosis of LNM status. 7 Additionally, morphological features such as heterogeneous texture, irregular borders, and spherical shape are known to correlate with metastatic LNs, but their clinical application lacks standardized assessment criteria. 8

The Node Reporting and Data System (Node-RADS), introduced in 2021, is a standardized scoring system for LN assessment in malignancies using CT/MRI, integrating both size and morphological features into a 5-tier scale (1–5) that quantifies the likelihood of malignancy from very low to very high. 9 Node-RADS has demonstrated superior diagnostic performance for LNM in various solid tumors, including bladder, lung, colorectal, and renal cancer.10–13 Recent studies have explored the value of Node-RADS in GC, showing that post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC) CT-based Node-RADS can accurately predict residual LNM and survival outcomes in locally advanced GC (LAGC). 14 However, the diagnostic and prognostic value of preoperative Node-RADS in unselected patients with resected GC remains underinvestigated. 15

This study aimed to fill this gap by evaluating the diagnostic performance of preoperative CT-based Node-RADS for regional LNM in a cohort of 150 treatment-naïve, radically resected non-metastatic gastric cancer patients covering pTNM stage I–III (excluding neoadjuvant chemotherapy, stage IV disease, and incomplete follow-up data), with pathological results as the gold standard. We further investigated the association between Node-RADS scores and survival outcomes (disease-free survival [DFS]) and developed a Node-RADS-based prognostic nomogram to facilitate preoperative risk stratification. We hypothesized that Node-RADS would outperform conventional size-based LN criteria for LNM diagnosis and serve as an independent prognostic indicator in this specific cohort.

Materials and methods

Study design and participants

This retrospective study was reviewed by the Institutional Ethics Committee of The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University on January 18, 2024. The committee waived the requirement for written informed consent owing to the retrospective nature of the investigation, in accordance with the revised 2024 Declaration of Helsinki.

We screened consecutive patients with pathologically confirmed primary GC who underwent radical gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy at The Second Affiliated Hospital of Nanchang University between January 2019 and December 2022. The inclusion criteria were: (1) pathologically confirmed primary GC ranging from pTNM stage I to III treated with radical gastrectomy; (2) radical gastrectomy with D2 lymphadenectomy performed in accordance with the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) and Chinese Society of Clinical Oncology (CSCO) GC guidelines4,16; (3) no prior anticancer treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, immunotherapy) before surgery; (4) preoperative contrast-enhanced abdominal CT performed within 2 weeks before surgery, with high-quality images suitable for LN assessment; (5) complete clinical, imaging, pathological, and follow-up data; (6) no concurrent other malignancies or distant metastasis (M0) at diagnosis, according to the 8th edition American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) TNM staging system. 17

Exclusion criteria were: (1) received neoadjuvant chemotherapy or other preoperative anticancer treatment; (2) diagnosed with stage IV GC initially; (3) accompanied with other malignant tumors; (4) absence or poor quality of abdominal CT images (motion artifact, inadequate contrast enhancement) precluding LN assessment; (5) follow-up duration <1 year or no defined follow-up data; (6) incomplete clinical, imaging, or pathological information.

A total of 236 patients were initially screened, and 86 were excluded based on the above criteria (Figure 1). The final study cohort included 150 patients (119 males, 31 females) with a median age of 61 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 54–67.75 years).Figure 1.Study flowchart of patient selection.

Study flowchart of patient selection.

The present study aimed to validate the full-range diagnostic and prognostic performance of preoperative CT Node-RADS across all surgical candidates with resectable GC, rather than only high-risk LAGC. Stage I early GC mostly presents Node-RADS 1–2 benign LN manifestations with extremely low LNM risk, serving as an essential low-risk reference group to establish the baseline threshold of the scoring system. Excluding stage I patients would eliminate the complete spectrum of benign LN imaging features and narrow the applicable scope of our predictive nomograms. The integrated nomograms built on this full-stage cohort can be applied to all treatment-naive GC patients before surgery for individualized risk stratification in routine clinical practice.

Clinical and pathological data collection

Clinical data collected included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), tumor location, preoperative serum carcinoembryonic antigen (CEA) and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 (CA19-9) levels, number of harvested LNs, and length of postoperative hospital stay. Pathological data included tumor size, pTNM stage, T stage, N stage, lymphovascular invasion, neural invasion, Lauren classification, tumor differentiation, and pathological types, which were confirmed by two senior gastrointestinal pathologists blinded to CT Node-RADS results according to the 8th edition AJCC TNM staging system and standard pathological diagnostic criteria. 17 Two separate tumor size metrics were recorded: (1) maximum primary tumor diameter measured on preoperative contrast-enhanced CT (used as a predictive variable in the LNM nomogram); (2) pathological tumor size measured on formalin-fixed surgical specimens by senior pathologists, used only for clinicopathological correlation analyses. All resected LNs were dissected and mapped station-by-station following the Japanese gastric cancer LN classification scheme. Each isolated LN was serially sliced into 2 mm-thick sections for routine hematoxylin-eosin (HE) staining. For nodes with suspicious tiny lesions but negative initial HE staining, additional serial sections and cytokeratin immunohistochemistry were performed to identify micrometastases (tumor deposits ≤ 2 mm) and isolated tumor cells, which were fully recorded when determining final pN status. Follow-up data were collected through outpatient review and telephone follow-up, with the primary endpoint of DFS, defined as the time from radical gastrectomy to the first occurrence of tumor recurrence or death from any cause; patients without recurrence at the last follow-up were censored.

All clinical records, preoperative CT images, and pathological specimens involved in this study were fully de-identified prior to analysis. All personal identifiers including medical record numbers, patient names, contact information, and unique ward labels were permanently removed to prevent any possibility of individual patient recognition.

Preoperative CT examination and Node-RADS scoring

All patients underwent preoperative contrast-enhanced abdominal CT examination within 2 weeks before surgery using a 64-slice spiral CT scanner (Siemens Somatom Definition AS). The scanning parameters were as follows: tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 200–300 mAs, layer thickness 5 mm, pitch 1.2. Contrast agent (iopromide, 300 mgI/mL) was injected intravenously at a rate of 2.5–3.0 mL/s, with a total dose of 1.5 mL/kg. Arterial phase (25–30 s after contrast injection), venous phase (60–70 s), and delayed phase (120 s) scans were performed sequentially. Images were reconstructed with a layer thickness of 1 mm and sent to the picture archiving and communication system (PACS) for storage and analysis.

Two radiologists with more than 5 years of experience in abdominal imaging independently assessed LN status on preoperative contrast-enhanced CT images and assigned Node-RADS scores following standardized criteria. 9 The two readers were fully blinded to postoperative pathological staging, carcinoembryonic antigen levels and survival outcomes; only anatomical tumor location visualized on CT scans was available during grading. Detailed definitions for each Node-RADS tier were as follows: Score 1 (normal LN): LN size ≤5 mm, round or oval, uniform density, no enhancement, clear boundary; Score 2 (benign-looking LN): LN size 6–10 mm, oval, uniform density, mild homogeneous enhancement, clear boundary; Score 3 (indeterminate LN): LN size 6–15 mm, irregular shape, slightly uneven density, moderate enhancement, or LN size >10 mm but with benign morphological features; Score 4 (suspicious malignant LN): LN size >15 mm, irregular shape, uneven density, obvious enhancement, or adjacent fat space invasion; Score 5 (highly suspicious malignant LN): Multiple LNs with score 4 features, LN fusion, or distant nodal metastasis.

For each patient, the final Node-RADS grade was determined by the highest single LN score observed on CT rather than a summed value across all detected nodes. This maximum-node grading rule was consistently implemented across all cases to standardize evaluation and facilitate reproducibility in subsequent external validation studies.

If there was a discrepancy between the two radiologists’ scores, a consensus was reached through joint consultation. Outcome measures and follow-up

The primary outcome measures were: (1) the diagnostic performance of Node-RADS score for LNM (pN+ status); (2) the prognostic value of Node-RADS score for DFS; (3) the predictive performance of the constructed nomogram models for LNM and DFS. Follow-up was conducted every 3 months within the first year after surgery, every 6 months in the 2nd–5th years, and once a year after 5 years. Follow-up examinations included physical examination, serum tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9), abdominal ultrasound or CT, and gastroscopy as appropriate. The follow-up deadline was December 2025.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software and R 4.5.1 software. Measurement data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR). Normally distributed continuous variables between two independent groups were compared using independent unpaired t-test; non-normal data were analyzed via Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test as appropriate. Count data were expressed as n (%), and compared using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Inter- and intra-observer agreement for Node-RADS scoring was evaluated using weighted kappa coefficient (kappa < 0.40: poor agreement; 0.40–0.59: moderate agreement; 0.60–0.79: substantial agreement; ≥0.80: almost perfect agreement). 18

Univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed using preoperatively available variables: CT-measured tumor size, preoperative serum CA19-9, and CT-based Node-RADS score, to identify independent preoperative predictors of pathological LNM (pN+ status), and a nomogram model for LNM prediction was constructed. Univariate and multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were used to identify independent prognostic factors for DFS, and a nomogram prognostic model for DFS was established. Internal validation of the DFS prognostic nomogram was implemented via 1000 bootstrap resampling to quantify optimism bias induced by limited sample size. Discriminative performance was evaluated by the C-index, and the bootstrap-corrected C-index was calculated to eliminate overfitting bias. The performance of the LNM nomogram was evaluated by receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (AUC), calibration curves, and DCA. The performance of the DFS nomogram was evaluated by C-index, time-dependent ROC curves (1-, 3-, 5-year AUC), and calibration curves. Kaplan–Meier method was used to plot DFS curves, and log-rank test was used to compare DFS differences among different Node-RADS subgroups. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The reporting of this retrospective observational cohort study conforms to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines. 19

The clinicopathological characteristics of the entire cohort and the comparison between pN- and pN+ groups are summarized in Table 1. There were no statistically significant differences in age, gender, BMI, number of harvested LNs, length of postoperative hospital stay, T stage, differentiation, tumor location, and pathological types between the pN- and pN+ groups (P > 0.05). In contrast, there were significant differences in tumor size, CEA level, CA19-9 level, pTNM stage, lymphovascular invasion, neural invasion, Lauren classification, recurrence, and Node-RADS between the two groups (P < 0.05). For Node-RADS score, the proportion of low scores (1-2 scores) was higher in the pN- group (34.15% for 1 score, 29.27% for 2 score) than in the pN+ group (1.83% for 1 score, 9.17% for 2 score), while the proportion of high scores (4-5 scores) was higher in the pN+ group (35.78% for 4 score, 17.43% for 5 score) than in the pN- group (9.76% for 4 score, 2.44% for 5 score), showing a significant difference between the two groups (χ2=50.61, P < 0.001).

Node-RADS score distribution and association with clinicopathological features

Node-RADS scores at the patient level were distributed as follows: 1 (10.67%, 16/150), 2 (14.67%, 22/150), 3 (32.67%, 49/150), 4 (28.67%, 43/150), and 5 (13.33%, 20/150), with score 3 being the most common. Significant differences were detected in tumor size, CEA level, pTNM stage, N stage, and recurrence with increasing Node-RADS scores (all P < 0.05). These included gradually elevated median tumor size and CEA levels, higher proportions of advanced pTNM stage III and N3 stage, and a markedly rising recurrence rate (from 12.50% to 70.00%, P < 0.001). In contrast, age, gender, BMI, number of harvested LNs, hospital stay, CA19-9, neural invasion, Lauren classification, and differentiation showed no significant differences (all P > 0.05). Inter- and intra-observer agreement

Inter-observer weighted kappa for Node-RADS scoring was 0.746 (95% CI: 0.612–0.880), indicating substantial agreement. Intra-observer weighted kappa was 0.798 (95% CI: 0.683–0.913), indicating substantial to almost perfect agreement.

Diagnostic performance of LNM-predicting nomogram

Clinical variables and Node-RADS scores were used to construct the LNM-predicting nomogram model to predict pN+. Univariable and multivariable logistic regression analyses demonstrated that the tumor size, CA19-9, and Node-RADS score had independent predictive value for pN+ (all P < 0.05, Table 3). The LNM-predicting nomogram model is shown in Figure 3. The three independent preoperative predictors integrated into the LNM nomogram were CT-measured primary tumor size, preoperative serum CA19-9 concentration, and CT Node-RADS score, all assessable before radical gastrectomy without postoperative pathological data. The LNM-predicting nomogram provides a quantitative, visual tool for personalized risk stratification in clinical practice. The LNM-predicting nomogram shows the highest area under the curve (AUC = 0.88; 95%CI: 0.82–0.95), followed by Node-RADS (AUC = 0.82; 95%CI: 0.75–0.90), tumor size (AUC = 0.79; 95%CI: 0.70–0.88), and CA19-9 (AUC = 0.64; 95%CI: 0.55–0.73) (Figure 4(A); Table 4). To quantify the incremental predictive gain from Node-RADS, we compared the full model with a baseline model containing only conventional predictors (age, CA19-9, tumor size). The baseline model reached an AUC of 0.828, and adding Node-RADS elevated the AUC to 0.883 with a ΔAUC of 0.055. The continuous NRI was 0.687 and IDI was 0.121, both revealing prominent improvements in risk discrimination and patient reclassification, which verified the independent incremental diagnostic value of Node-RADS beyond routine clinical indicators. The calibration curves demonstrated the LNM-predicting nomogram’s predicted probabilities of LNM are well calibrated with observed probabilities (Figure 4(B)); quantitatively, the overall Brier score of the nomogram was 0.115, far lower than the null benchmark model (0.199), reflecting small overall prediction error. Bootstrap internal validation with 100 resamples further verified reliable calibration: the bias-corrected median calibration index was 0.0089, extremely close to the ideal value of 0 and suggestive of negligible systematic risk overestimation or underestimation, while the median optimism reached only −0.0123, demonstrating minimal overfitting and stable internal predictive performance. The DCA curves indicated that the LNM-predicting nomogram offers a higher net benefit across a wide range of threshold probabilities, indicating its potential clinical utility for personalized risk stratification (Figure 4(C)). Subgroup stratified analysis was further performed to exclude the confounding influence of stage I patients. When only stage II–III LAGC patients (n=142) were analyzed, the AUC of the LNM nomogram remained 0.86 (95%CI: 0.80–0.93), while Node-RADS alone achieved an AUC of 0.80 (95%CI: 0.73–0.88). Survival outcomes and prognostic value of nomogram prognostic model

During follow-up, 52 patients (34.7%) experienced recurrence, and the median DFS was 44 months (IQR: 18.0–67.0). DFS was analyzed according to the Node-RADS stratification system using Kaplan–Meier methods (Figure 5). Patients were stratified into five distinct subgroups (Node-RADS 1 to 5). A highly significant difference in DFS was observed across these subgroups (log-rank P < 0.001). Specifically, patients with a Node-RADS score of 1 exhibited the most favorable DFS, with a continuous, high probability of remaining recurrence-free throughout the follow-up period. In contrast, patients with higher Node-RADS scores (4 and 5) demonstrated progressively worse DFS, characterized by steeper declines in recurrence-free probability.Figure 5.Kaplan-Meier curves for DFS stratified by the Node-RADS score (1-5).The shaded areas represent the 95% confidence intervals for each stratum. Clinical variables and Node-RADS scores were used to construct the prognostic nomogram model to predict DFS. The nomogram prognostic model is shown in Figure 6(A). The C-index of the nomogram prognostic model was 0.764 (95%CI: 0.707–0.825). After 1000 bootstrap resampling internal validation, the bias-corrected C-index slightly decreased to 0.755, which indicated only mild overfitting and acceptable discriminative capacity of the model. The AUC values of 1-, 3-, 5-year time-dependent ROC of the nomogram prognostic model were 0.862 (95%CI: 0.795–0.929), 0.862 (95%CI: 0.787–0.936) and 0.795 (95%CI: 0.704–0.886) (Figure 6(B)). The C-index of the DFS nomogram in stage II–III subgroup was 0.748, close to the overall C-index of 0.764 in all stage I–III patients. Discussion

In the present study, we comprehensively explored the clinicopathological correlations, diagnostic efficacy for LNM, and prognostic significance of the Node-RADS scoring system based on preoperative CT in 150 patients diagnosed with GC. Furthermore, we developed and validated two integrated nomogram models—one for predicting LNM (pN+ status) and another for estimating DFS—with the ultimate goal of providing reliable, reproducible, and clinically actionable tools for preoperative risk stratification and individualized clinical decision-making in this patient population. This cohort enrolled all resectable non-metastatic gastric cancer (stage I–III) without NAC, which differs from Sun et al.’s cohort limited to post-NAC stage II–III LAGC. 14 Unlike Sun et al. who emphasized overall survival (OS), we prioritized recurrence-related prognostic value, addressing a critical clinical need for early recurrence prediction that is often overlooked in existing research. Notably, Zheng et al. developed a transformer-based deep learning network (DLN) for LNM prediction in post-NAC LAGC, 20 but their model relies on complex deep learning algorithms that require specialized technical support, whereas our Node-RADS-based nomograms are simpler, more reproducible, and easier to popularize in clinical settings with limited technical resources.

Consistent with previous literature, this cohort featured a high proportion of pN+ cases (72.7%), neural invasion (81.33%), and advanced pTNM stage III (52.67%), reflecting the aggressive nature of GC worldwide (5-year survival rate < 40%).21–23 Comparative analysis identified tumor size, CEA, CA19-9, pTNM stage, lymphovascular invasion, neural invasion, Lauren classification, recurrence status, and Node-RADS score as significant differences between pN- and pN+ groups (all P < 0.05), with no differences in age, gender, BMI, and other indicators (all P > 0.05). This contrasts with Sun et al.’s post-NAC cohort, where LNM incidence was lower (67.8%) due to NAC’s tumor-reducing effect, and where no association between tumor markers (CEA, CA19-9) and LNM was observed—an inconsistency attributed to NAC altering tumor marker expression, 14 which was further supported by Jiang et al. 24 Our finding that low Node-RADS scores (1–2) were more prevalent in pN- groups and high scores (4–5) in pN+ groups (χ2=50.61, P < 0.001) aligns with Sun et al.’s observation of high scores in ypN+ groups, 14 but extends this correlation to preoperative settings. Rawicz-Pruszyński et al. reported the association between elevated Node-RADS grades and LNM. 25 Building on their findings, this cohort further extended this relationship to preoperative recurrence risk stratification, which may support individualized preoperative clinical decision-making. These findings underscore that metastatic LNs are closely correlated with tumor burden, invasive biological behaviors, and early recurrence, highlighting the crucial clinical need for accurate preoperative assessment of LNM in patients with GC.

Strong correlations were identified between Node-RADS scores and tumor biological aggressiveness, with score 3 being the most prevalent (32.67%) in our cohort. Sun et al. reported a similar distribution (scores 3 and 5 most common) in post-NAC patients, 14 confirming Node-RADS cross-treatment-stage relevance, but this cohort further demonstrated that rising Node-RADS scores correlate with increased tumor size, CEA levels, advanced staging, and recurrence rate (0.00% in score 1 to 70.00% in score 5). This trend aligns with Röcken et al., who linked tumor size and markers to aggressiveness, 26 but we uniquely integrated these correlations with Node-RADS to develop practical predictive tools. Sun et al. focused on post-NAC pathological indicators (ypN stage, TRG, LNR), 14 while Zheng et al.’s DLN model targeted intratumoral heterogeneity 20 ; existing Node-RADS studies such as those by Sun et al. and Zheng et al. predominantly evaluate patients following neoadjuvant therapy. The preoperative design of this research compensates for this understudied area of baseline risk stratification. Consistent predictive performance across preoperative and post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy cohorts verifies that Node-RADS serves as a robust imaging stratification system. The present analysis further validates its diagnostic value among patients who receive no neoadjuvant treatment.

Reproducibility is critical for clinical translation. Node-RADS delivers standardized grading frameworks that boost inter- and intra-observer agreement over conventional non-uniform LN evaluation methods. 9 In this research, inter-observer weighted kappa was 0.746 at the patient level, and intra-observer kappa was 0.798—both indicating substantial agreement. This is consistent with previous studies in GC 14 and other malignancies,10,12 confirming that Node-RADS is a reproducible tool for LN assessment in clinical practice. This study uniquely verified the interobserver reproducibility of Node-RADS in preoperative untreated patients. By contrast, Sun et al.’s analysis targeted post-neoadjuvant cohorts, 14 and Zhong et al. combined multiple gastrointestinal malignancies without stratifying by treatment history. 27 This targeted validation ensures our results are more relevant to preoperative clinical practice, minimizing inter-observer variability in preoperative radiological interpretation and facilitating standardized assessment across institutions.

LNM is a major prognostic factor in GC.2,3 Conventional CT assessment of LNM in GC relies on LN short-axis diameter (≥10 mm), yet this criterion has limited accuracy due to overlapping features between metastatic and reactive LNs.6,7 In this research, the size-based criterion had a low sensitivity (0.59) for LNM diagnosis, consistent with previous reports,14,19 meaning nearly half of metastatic LNs were missed by size alone. To enhance preoperative LNM prediction, we constructed a LNM-predicting nomogram integrating tumor size, CA19-9, and Node-RADS score (all independent predictive factors, P < 0.05). Distinct from Sun et al.’s model (only Node-RADS 14 ) and Zheng et al.’s complex DLN model, 20 our integration of clinical indicators and Node-RADS is advantageous: Sun et al.’s post-NAC cohort had altered indicator utility, 14 while our preoperative cohort retained their value. Though our Node-RADS OR values (3–5: 7.03, 22.53, 34.32) were lower than Sun et al.’s, 14 additional indicators improved comprehensiveness, and our LNM-predicting nomogram is more accessible than Zheng et al.’s DLN model. 20 Our LNM-predicting nomogram achieved an AUC of 0.88, slightly higher than Sun et al.’s (0.870 14 ) and superior to Zheng et al.’s DLN model (0.748–0.804 20 ). At optimal cutoff (0.735), it showed high accuracy (0.83), sensitivity (0.82), and specificity (0.85)—better than Sun et al.’s (78.9% 14 ), reducing unnecessary LN dissection. Calibration and DCA curves confirmed reliability, and its preoperative focus fills a gap by optimizing early surgical planning, unlike Sun et al.’s post-NAC focus. 14

Previous studies have shown that GC recurrence mainly occurs within the first few years after radical resection, with long-term survival closely related to preoperative risk status. 28 Preoperative imaging and serological indicators reflect tumor aggressiveness and systemic burden, and are linked to long-term outcomes. 29 Consistent with these observations, our Kaplan–Meier analysis confirmed significant DFS differences among Node-RADS subgroups (log-rank P < 0.001), with score 1 associated with the best DFS and scores 4–5 with the poorest. Consistent with the results reported by Sun et al., who demonstrated that elevated Node-RADS scores were associated with worse overall survival, 14 this research further analyzed the correlation between preoperative Node-RADS grades and disease recurrence using survival methods. Unlike Sun et al., who reported 5-year OS rates of 31.8% (high scores) vs. 76.3% (low scores), 14 we focused on recurrence—a more clinically actionable precursor to poor OS. Our observation that recurrence rates increased with Node-RADS scores (12.50% to 70.00%) extends Sun et al.’s work on post-NAC tumor residue, 14 validating Node-RADS as a non-invasive prognostic tool for NAC-naive patients. To translate these findings into clinical practice, we established a DFS nomogram incorporating preoperative CT-based Node-RADS, age, and CA19-9 to provide a convenient preoperative risk stratification tool. This nomogram achieved a favorable C-index of 0.764 (comparable to GC pathological prognostic models14,22), with 1-, 3-, and 5-year time-dependent AUCs of 0.862, 0.862, and 0.795, respectively, indicating reliable short- and long-term recurrence prediction. Calibration curves confirmed good agreement between predicted and actual DFS probabilities. Importantly, the nomogram effectively stratifies preoperative recurrence risk: high-risk patients (especially those with high Node-RADS scores) may be considered for more intensive adjuvant therapy or surveillance, while low-risk patients avoid overtreatment, optimizing individualized management and improving long-term prognosis.

The median number of harvested LNs in our overall cohort was 14, slightly below the widely accepted threshold of 16 nodes for complete D2 lymphadenectomy, which may carry a risk of stage migration and occult understaging in patients with limited nodal dissection.30,31 To test the robustness of our results against this potential bias, we conducted a prespecified sensitivity analysis stratified by LN yield (cutoff=16 nodes). Although Node-RADS distribution differed slightly between subgroups, the LNM positive rate showed no significant intergroup difference. Critically, the LNM nomogram and DFS prognostic nomogram maintained comparable discrimination ability in both low and adequate lymph harvest subgroups, with only minor fluctuations in AUC and C-index values. These stratified analyses demonstrate that the predictive utility of preoperative Node-RADS is stable and not substantially confounded by incomplete LN resection, supporting the reliability of our preoperative risk stratification models in routine clinical practice.

Node-RADS exhibits excellent clinical accessibility for routine preoperative LN assessment, as it relies only on standardized visual radiological evaluation without additional data processing or dedicated computational equipment. However, conventional visual scoring systems and artificial intelligence imaging models are not mutually exclusive competitive tools. Emerging hybrid strategies integrating structured radiological scoring criteria with radiomics features or transformer-based deep learning pipelines can synergize their respective strengths 32 : Node-RADS provides intuitive, reproducible visual grading, while AI-based quantitative imaging analysis extracts high-dimensional invisible imaging information to refine risk prediction. Combined multimodal approaches may achieve superior predictive performance in future clinical practice, and further prospective studies are needed to validate such complementary hybrid workflows for gastric cancer nodal risk stratification.

Histological subtype constitutes a core dimension of biological heterogeneity that reshapes lymphatic metastatic patterns and modifies the diagnostic and prognostic performance of Node-RADS.21,29 While Lauren classification was incorporated into our baseline clinical data, we further contextualize subtype-specific tumor behavior using recent European cohort studies focusing on nodal biology and surgical staging.33–35 First, the proportion of signet-ring cell components drives divergent long-term prognosis in poorly cohesive gastric cancer; pure and partial signet-ring cell lesions display distinct metastatic risks and survival trajectories, rather than representing a uniform high-risk group. 33 Second, diffuse and mixed-type tumors demonstrate globally elevated LNM incidence across all nodal stations, with frequent submucosal infiltrative growth and widespread micrometastasis within radiologically normal-sized LNs. 34 These occult micrometastases foci cannot be captured by morphological CT features underpinning Node-RADS grading, which readily generates false-negative low scores for diffuse and signet-ring dominant tumors.21,29 Third, nodal burden evaluation gains layered clinical meaning in the neoadjuvant setting, where histologic subtype interacts with nodal response to chemotherapy and shapes postoperative recurrence risk stratification. 35 Clinicians must therefore interpret Node-RADS results with histotype-specific caution. Low visual scores cannot reliably exclude hidden nodal dissemination in diffuse or signet-ring cell predominant tumors, so combined serum biomarkers and multimodal predictive models are required to compensate for the limited morphological sensitivity of visual scoring alone. Future stratified analyses separated by Lauren subtype and signet-ring cell percentage are necessary to establish histology-adapted Node-RADS cut-off values and refine individualized preoperative nodal risk evaluation.

From a translational clinical perspective, Node-RADS stratification can directly guide personalized perioperative and postoperative management decisions. The integrated nomograms combining preoperative CT Node-RADS, tumor markers and clinical parameters could serve as an exploratory auxiliary tool for preoperative LNM and recurrence risk stratification. The risk stratification results derived from this model may provide preliminary directional reference for individualized perioperative management; however, formal treatment adjustment strategies based on this scoring system remain hypothetical and require further validation in large prospective multicenter cohorts with independent external datasets and complete adjuvant chemotherapy records before clinical implementation. Importantly, the nomogram effectively stratifies preoperative recurrence risk: high-risk patients (especially those with high Node-RADS scores) may be considered for more intensive adjuvant therapy or surveillance, while low-risk patients avoid overtreatment, optimizing individualized management and improving long-term prognosis.

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, which may introduce selection bias and limit the generalizability of the results. We only performed bootstrap internal validation with 100 replicates (showing minimal overfitting and stable internal performance), while no independent external dataset with standardized Node-RADS labeling was available for cross-center verification. Future multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate our findings and the generalizability of this nomogram. Second, all patients received standardized D2 lymphadenectomy, yet the median number of harvested LNs was only 14. Suboptimal nodal retrieval may result from variations in surgical radicality, specimen processing, or pathological LN dissection. Insufficient nodal evaluation can cause underestimation of metastatic disease and stage migration, potentially biasing the measured diagnostic performance of Node-RADS, as pathological nodal status acted as the gold standard in our analysis. Third, complete standardized records of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy (regimen, initiation timing, completion rate) were unavailable for all patients, so we could not adjust for this powerful prognostic confounder in survival analyses. This may introduce residual bias to our secondary DFS endpoint. In addition, incomplete records of postoperative adjuvant chemotherapy regimens and cycles limited our ability to reliably validate the causal association between nomogram-derived risk stratification and optimal adjuvant treatment strategies, so we cannot draw definitive clinical treatment recommendations based on current data. Fourth, we only evaluated CT-based Node-RADS; future studies could compare Node-RADS performance between CT and MRI, which has better soft tissue contrast for LN assessment. 36 Fifth, we did not assess the value of Node-RADS in predicting cancer-specific survival, another critical clinical endpoint. Sixth, we did not include radiomic features or machine learning algorithms, which may further improve the diagnostic and prognostic performance of Node-RADS. 37

Conclusion

Preoperative CT Node-RADS scoring enables reliable preoperative prediction of lymph node metastasis and postoperative disease-free survival in stage I–III neoadjuvant chemotherapy-naive gastric cancer patients. The two integrated nomograms built on CT-measured tumor size, CA19-9 and Node-RADS achieve favorable discrimination and calibration. This internally validated single-center model can only act as an exploratory auxiliary tool for preoperative risk assessment. Definite clinical treatment strategies cannot be derived from current data, and further large prospective multicenter external validation studies are required to confirm its clinical value before routine application.
