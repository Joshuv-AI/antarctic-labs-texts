Mixed-methods study to test and assess uptake for gender-specific WASH indicators in Bangladesh: study protocol
Authors: Bethany A Caruso, Madeleine Patrick, Sonia Shahid, Ashraful Islam, Ramkrishna Paul, Abul K Shoab, Mahbub-Ul Alam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionRegular collection and reporting of gender data is critical for informing water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH)-related policies, programmes and investments that benefit all. Priority gender-specific indicators for WASH monitoring were identified and published in March 2024. However, the indicators need testing and there is a lack of understanding about what may enable or prevent governments from adopting them. This study aims to test and report against the priority gender indicators in Bangladesh and identify barriers and enablers to national-level indicator adoption.Analysis and methodsFour primary activities were identified to achieve the study aims: stakeholder meetings, cognitive interviews, survey data collection and key-informant interviews (KIIs). First, stakeholder meetings engage those involved in work related to gender, WASH and/or monitoring to discuss and solicit feedback on the proposed data collection plan, including if any additional Bangladesh-specific indicators should be tested. Second, 48 cognitive interviews (24 men and 24 women) assess face validity of the survey questions that generate indicator data. Findings are then used to strengthen survey question wording. Third, survey data collection with 600 participants (300 men, 300 women) generates data to test indicator performance. Finally, KIIs with 20–30 individuals engaged in WASH, gender and/or monitoring seek to gain impressions of the indicator data produced and to identify barriers and facilitators of indicator adoption. Qualitative data will be analysed thematically. Quantitative analyses will follow the approach used by the Bangladesh National Hygiene Survey.Ethics and disseminationInstitutional Review Boards at the University of Dhaka Institute of Health Economics and Emory University reviewed and approved the study. Indicator data will be shared with stakeholders during KIIs and more broadly through a learning workshop and via policy briefs. We aim to present at conferences and publish results in peer-reviewed journals. Data will be publicly available on publication of key findings.

Introduction

In 2015, the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) were established to enable sustainable economic, social and environmental development. Among the goals, SDG 6 seeks to ‘Ensure availability and sustainable management of water and sanitation for all’ by 2030.1 To measure progress against SDG 6, eight targets were set, with Targets 6.1 and 6.2 focused on achieving equitable access to drinking water, sanitation and hygiene (WASH) for all. A decade has passed since the SDGs were established and the most recent monitoring data show that the world is not on track to reach SDG Targets 6.1 and 6.2 and that access to WASH remains out of reach for billions, particularly those residing in low-resource settings.2

While SDG 5 focuses on achieving gender equality and the empowerment of all women and girls, the preamble to the SDG Agenda specifically notes that gender equality and empowerment are priorities across all 17 SDG goals and related targets.1 Yet not all SDGs integrate gender, a social and cultural construct distinguishing differences in the attributes of men and women and boys and girls.3 UN Women has identified several SDGs, including SDG 6, as being ‘gender blind’ because they lack gender-specific indicators that disaggregate data by sex and/or specifically indicate gender equality to be an underlying objective.4 For Targets 6.1 and 6.2 in particular, household-level data are leveraged to report against indicators, limiting assessment of individual, gender-specific and intrahousehold experiences and inequalities related to WASH, and constraining understanding of how WASH may hinder or enable gender equality.

Data that capture gender differences are critical for informing WASH-related policies, programmes and investments that benefit all.5 However, the lack of gender-specific indicators and regular data collection and reporting have kept gendered impacts and needs related to WASH invisible, prevented assessment of change over time, and stifled targeted action.69 This lack of gender-specific monitoring data cannot be attributed to a lack of understanding of how WASH experiences and needs are gendered. On the contrary, research has established that women and girls shoulder the burden of WASH-related work globally. For example, they are the primary water collectors when water is not readily and consistently available in the home,2 10 11 making them responsible for supporting the drinking, hygiene and cooking needs for all household members. These WASH-related activities can take substantial amounts of time, which is rarely calculated, reported or compensated.12 13 Further, those shouldering the burden of these activities may face risk of injury, impair their own health, expend considerable energy and miss out on economic, sleep, school and leisure opportunities.71417

Despite being vital to improving the WASH conditions of their households and enabling WASH-related behaviours for their families, women’s needs and life outcomes are rarely the primary focus of WASH programmes, policies, interventions or investments.13 17 It is plausible that women’s (and girls’) experiences and roles in WASH provision—as providers and rarely as beneficiaries—have not been monitored because they are not expected to change. Or it may be that women’s and girls’ experiences and needs have not been prioritised as worthy of understanding or trying to change.

There is a need to make the work, needs and roles of women—and all genders—visible to ensure that WASH services are serving all and can be, as envisioned, on the pathway to gender equality.18 In recognition and response to this need, the Integrated Monitoring Initiative for SDG 6 embarked on a ‘gender contextualisation’ of all SDG 6 global indicators to determine how to integrate gender into monitoring.19 Further, the WHO/UNICEF Joint Monitoring Programme for Water Supply, Sanitation and Hygiene (JMP) (the global custodians of data on drinking WASH) committed to advancing monitoring of gender for SDG6 WASH targets (6.1, 6.2). The WHO/UNICEF JMP partnered with Emory University to identify priority gender-specific WASH indicators to complement the existing indicators for these SDG targets.

A set of priority gender-specific indicators for WASH monitoring was identified and published in March 2024.20 However, despite publication of these priority indicators and the need for gender-specific WASH monitoring data, research has yet to generate the data needed to report against all of the proposed indicators or to test how the indicators and their survey measures perform. Research is also needed to understand the processes of indicator adoption for regular reporting by governments, including what may enable or prevent uptake. To fill these needs and research gaps, this protocol outlines the methods for a study that aims to (a) test and report against the priority gender indicators in Bangladesh and (b) identify barriers and enablers to national-level indicator adoption.

Methods and analysis

This research represents ‘phase 3’ of work Emory is carrying out in partnership with the JMP team to advance gender monitoring related to the SDG 6 WASH targets (see figure 1).

Briefly, phase 1 (2020–2021) involved carrying out a review of opportunities for gender-specific WASH monitoring. A final report collated activity learning, identifying myriad ways gender could be measured and monitored in WASH and presenting a glossary of relevant terms (table 1 provides definitions of select terms which were used in this research). Phase 2 (2022–2023) identified priority gender-specific WASH indicators. Inputs from invited experts in WASH, gender and monitoring informed a preliminary list of potential indicators and measures and comments from the public were used to revise and finalise the list.

Phase 3—the current phase—seeks to test and report against the priority gender-specific indicators and assess barriers and facilitators to government-level adoption. This phase is comprised of four primary activities: stakeholder meetings, cognitive interviews (CIs), small-scale data collection and key-informant interviews (KIIs) (figure 1). This protocol describes each of these activities in turn.

Setting

All research for phase 3 will be carried out in Bangladesh where there is extensive architecture for data collection, strong commitment to monitoring the SDGs (particularly goal 6) and numerous stakeholders working in WASH, gender and monitoring. The Bangladesh government, WaterAid Bangladesh and the International Centre for Diarrhoeal Disease Research, Bangladesh (icddr,b) led two rounds of the National Hygiene Survey (2014, 2018),23 24 which generated nationally representative WASH data to aid evidence-based decision making and intervention targeting. These surveys were undertaken because the Bangladesh Demographic and Health Surveys and the Multiple Indicator Cluster (MICS) Surveys were deemed insufficient at providing comprehensive enough data to effectively inform policy and practice.23

Partners

The Emory team leads the study, including conceptualization, design, funding acquisition, and supervision. Athena Infonomics is the primary research partner, leading all data collection activities. They maintain strong connections with key WASH stakeholders across the country and are a member of the Technical Advisory Group for Strengthening Public Data Systems for Sanitation, a multistakeholder platform anchored by the Department of Public Health Engineering under the Ministry of Local Government, Rural Development and Co-operatives. The icddr,b team provides inputs related to sampling and analysis, drawing on their experience from the National Hygiene Survey and other nationally representative data collection efforts.

Activity 1: stakeholder meetings

The primary purpose of stakeholder meetings was to solicit feedback about the proposed data collection areas and data to be collected. As it is critical that data collected reflect the varied WASH conditions found throughout the country, stakeholder review and feedback on proposed study areas was necessary. Stakeholder meetings were carried out in person from 2 February 2025 to 6 February 2025 with seven participants representing government, research, practice and multilateral organisations. Based on feedback received, we increased the number of study areas from three to six to best capture the diverse populations, climate vulnerabilities, ethnic minorities and income disparities in Bangladesh. Additionally, it is critical that data collected be useful to stakeholders in Bangladesh. We expect these indicators will yield valuable data to decision-makers in Bangladesh and beyond given the extensive process for identification. That said, we acknowledge that the priority indicators are meant to be applicable across country contexts and may not be inclusive of country-specific conditions, experiences or challenges. Thus, to ensure that data yield insights that reflect the interests of country actors, capture country-specific gender-related priorities, and aid decision-making, stakeholders also were invited to suggest additional indicators and/or measures to add to the survey.

Activity 2: CIs

The purpose of the CIs was to assess face validity of the measures (survey questions) used to generate data for the indicators. Measures for the indicators were proposed by experts engaged in phase 2 (‘prioritisation’ phase: figure 1). However, testing was needed prior to data collection to determine if the wording was well-understood and appropriate for assessing what the indicators seek to assess (a concept known as ‘face validity’).25

CIs, which have been used to validate other WASH26 27 and hygiene measures,28 29 involve reading potential survey questions (the measure) to participants and having them provide a response (eg, always, often, sometimes, never). Participants then explain the question in their own words and justify their response. This method allows researchers to verify if participants understand questions and provide responses that align with their explanations. If there is discordance or a lack of understanding, interviewers can explain what questions intend to ask and discuss with the participant alternative ways questions or responses could have been phrased to improve understanding.

We completed 48 CIs in March 2025, 8 in each of the 6 study areas. Within each study area, four were completed in a rural village (two with women, two with men) and four were completed in an urban mahalla (two with women, two with men), ensuring diverse experiences and perspectives. As no sample size criteria exist for CIs, we based our sample size on guidance for in-depth interviews, with 48 being sufficient for saturation.30 The CI tool included questions to assess understanding of the indicator measures; demographic and other data on WASH household conditions were also collected. Data collection was carried out in Bengali by female facilitators who had a multiday training that covered the study objectives, CI methodology, a review of the tool, ethics and field logistics.

Eligible individuals were age 18 or older, resided in the area visited, and consented to participate. Facilitators recruited participants via snowball sampling; a key informant (eg, local leader) helped to identify the first participant, who then helped identify the next, and so on.31 CI facilitators took extensive notes and all CIs were digitally recorded and transcribed into English.

Analysis of CIs began immediately after data were collected. CI facilitators debriefed with members of the Athena and/or Emory teams after each data collection day to discuss key findings.32 CI facilitators identified key issues related to survey question wording and provided recommendations for adjustment, which allowed for iterative adaptation of the CI instrument. Notes for each participant and about each question were kept on an excel sheet to prepare a data display, allowing review by question once all CIs were completed. These notes were then used to make adjustments to the survey tool. In-depth thematic analysis33 on CI transcripts is planned and findings will be incorporated into a paper explaining and presenting the validation findings.

The survey included measures to assess the priority gender-specific indicators (see table 3) for priority gender-specific indictors to be tested) and ‘additional’ indicators identified by the stakeholders and study team, with refinements made to wording and translation based on the CIs. It also included select demographic and WASH information used in the National Hygiene Survey, DHS and MICS to enable subgroup analyses.

We aimed to collect data from 600 individuals (300 women and 300 men; 100 individuals per study area). This number was determined by available resources. We acknowledge that the sample size is small, not powered for inference testing, and may limit precision of estimates, but it remains adequate for indicator performance assessment and pilot analyses, the stated goals of this work.

The survey sampling strategies were informed by those outlined in the Bangladesh National Hygiene Survey 2018. Specifically, a multistage, cluster-stratified random sampling method was used to randomly select 4 clusters for data collection in each study area, 2 urban (mahallas) and 2 rural (villages) (24 total clusters). We used the Bangladesh Population and Housing Census 201134 as the primary sampling frame for selecting clusters (data for the most recent census was not possible to obtain due to cost). For each study area, clusters were stratified by whether urban or rural, producing 2 lists per study area: 1 for urban mahallas and another for rural villages (12 total lists; 2 per study area). Each list functioned as an independent sampling frame for cluster selection. We randomly generated lists of 10 clusters per sampling frame to review for eligibility. A cluster was eligible for inclusion if considered safe to visit, accessible without any undue logistical challenges, and not proximate to an already selected cluster. The first two clusters on each list that met eligibility criteria were selected for inclusion.

Within each cluster, enumerators randomly selected households for inclusion. They started from the centre of the village or mahalla (or a point close to the bus/rickshaw stands) and found the nearest household. They then screened that household for an eligible participant (over age 18, residing at the household, and speaks Bangla). If a household member met the criteria, they were recruited and consented; if not, the enumerator proceeded to the nearest household to screen for eligibility. After completing a survey, the enumerator skipped a predetermined number of households to identify the next household to approach. That predetermined number was specific to each community and was determined by dividing the cluster population size by 25 (the number of surveys per cluster). As census data are old, we gathered cluster population sizes by contacting the Deputy Commissioner of the district in which the mahalla or village is located. If the enumerator previously sought a woman, they would next seek a man to participate, and vice versa. Enumerators alternated who they were seeking and checked in with the field supervisor regularly to ensure that the target numbers of women and men per cluster area were obtained across the study team. Enumerators collected data until they completed 25 surveys per cluster (~50% men, ~50% women).

Data collection was carried out from May to July 2025 in Bengali by female enumerators who underwent a 5-day training, which included 3 days of classroom-based training and 2 days of piloting (April 2025). Classroom training covered the study objectives, the survey tool, ethics, use of tablets and field logistics. The team practiced all aspects of data collection (eg, identifying eligible participants, consenting, using the tablets, administering the survey) during the two pilot days, also enabling team members to identify any errors in the tablet programming or with the survey.

Post data collection, Athena Infonomics will follow a pre-established data cleaning protocol to prepare survey data for analysis. Personally identifiable information will be removed to ensure ethical compliance. Outliers and inconsistencies will be identified through range checks and logical validations across variables and duplicate entries will be resolved using unique identifiers and metadata comparisons. The cleaned dataset will be archived with detailed documentation to ensure transparency and reproducibility.

We will follow the National Hygiene Survey analysis strategy.23 We will generate descriptive statistics and calculate indicator data using weighted proportions so findings can be representative, whether of broader cluster population or by relevant subgroups. Mimicking reporting strategies used in Bangladesh and by the JMP for reporting on progress for SDG Targets 6.1 and 6.2,2 23 data will be presented in tables, disaggregated by rural/urban residence and study region, at a minimum. Departing from previous reporting, we will disaggregate participant and indicator data by gender.

Activity 4: KIIs

The purpose of the KIIs is to understand barriers and facilitators to uptake of the priority gender-specific WASH indicators. Identifying priority indicators does not assure they will be taken up for regular data collection and reporting. It is necessary, therefore, to learn what stakeholders in Bangladesh think about the indicators, including their reflections on the indicator data produced as part of activity 3, and what they believe may facilitate or hinder indicator adoption and regular data collection and reporting by government bodies going forward. While learning is context specific and may not be generalisable, we expect findings will inform research on drivers of indicator uptake in other settings.

Our approach for engaging stakeholders to understand indicator uptake is adapted from the ‘Exemplars in Global Health Research Approach Framework’.35 Countries like Bangladesh, that have distinguished themselves as ‘exemplars’—because they have exhibited exceptional history, experiences, systems or outcomes—can provide lessons for replication and acceleration elsewhere.35 While Bangladesh has yet to formally adopt any of the indicators, the country already has distinguished itself as an exemplar in WASH monitoring and in integrating gender into WASH monitoring. Specifically, Bangladesh has demonstrated commitment to improving systems for SDG monitoring and has invested in multiple rounds of national-level WASH monitoring and reporting.23 Additionally, Bangladesh has already collected and reported on menstrual health data at the national level, signalling that data on sex-specific and gender-specific issues have not only been carried out but are valued.23

Through approximately 20–30 KIIs, we will seek to understand perceived facilitators or barriers to indicator adoption in Bangladesh. We will also seek perceptions of the data collected in Bangladesh, and which indicators may be of greatest interest for uptake. KIIs are suitable for this work as they intentionally seek people who have specific knowledge about and experience with institutions, systems and policy that can inform their responses.36

To identify KII participants, we will create a stakeholder map of individuals from institutions that have a role in WASH and/or monitoring in Bangladesh. The list, which will include individuals from non-governmental organizations (NGOs), government bodies, research organisations, donors and bilateral organisations, will serve as our sampling frame and be iteratively adapted throughout the project period. Athena Infonomics will create the first draft of the stakeholder map. We will seek recommendations for others to engage from the KII participants themselves (snowball technique).31 Eligible participants work or have worked on monitoring related to gender and/or WASH or engage work that has a specific interest in monitoring data related to gender and/or WASH.

KIIs are expected to begin in May 2026, and run through June 2026. As the country has been undergoing political change after the Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina was ousted in August 2024,37 we intentionally have waited until after the elections in February 2026. KIIs will be led by Athena Infonomics, which is well-positioned given their experiences and relationships in Bangladesh. Recruitment will happen in waves. Team members from Athena Infonomics will call up to five individuals at a time from the stakeholder list to explain the study and seek participation. After calling (and in cases where contact via phone is not successful), Athena will email information about the study purpose. We will intentionally seek both men and women and different stakeholder types to ensure a diversity of perspectives is included. At each wave, only five individuals will be contacted to facilitate scheduling and allow the sample to adapt based on who has already participated and what perspectives remain needed.

KIIs will take place at a location of the participant’s choosing. Participants will be provided an informed consent document to read or be read by Athena. The interview will begin once the participant provides oral consent. Interviews will be recorded for verbatim transcription and detailed notes will be taken. All identifying information will be removed from transcripts and notes.

KII transcripts will be analysed thematically by team members at both Emory and Athena33 and will be used to create a case study38 about how indicators may be adopted. Specifically, themes will be identified deductively and inductively.39 Example a priori deductive codes include ‘utility of government indicator adoption’, ‘barrier to adoption’ and ‘facilitator to adoption’ Inductive codes will be identified from the data throughout the analytic process, including during debriefings held with the Emory and Athena teams after interviews are conducted,32 first reads of transcripts and during coding. We will use MAXQDA (VERBI) to code all transcripts and notes and Excel (Microsoft) to aggregate coded text into summative tables that will inform thematic memos. Thematic memos will summarise all barrier and facilitator types described. Following ‘Exemplars in Global Health Research Approach Framework’ guidance, input from country stakeholders will be sought during analysis for validation. From the coded data, summative tables and memos produced, we will summarise key learning on the barriers and facilitators to indicator adoption in Bangladesh, which can serve as a case study both for future advocacy work in Bangladesh as well as for others investigating indicator uptake in other settings.

Patient and public involvement

This study involves no patients. Public involvement occurs at multiple phases. For activity 1, the purpose of engaging with stakeholders was explicitly to gain insights and ideas about the study areas and the data to be collected. Feedback was incorporated to strengthen the research and ensure that data relevance and utility. For activity 2, the CIs were conducted to elicit the emic perspectives of men and women who are like those who would participate in the survey. Their insights strengthened the tool by clarifying the wording of questions. Activity 4 is centred on gaining feedback from members of the public engaged in WASH and/or gender monitoring to validate the findings and assess how the indicators, data, and tools may be used. After analyses are complete, stakeholders from activities 1 and 4 will be invited to participate in learning workshops where we will share learning about the indicators and identify actions for advancing indicator adoption.

Limitations

The total sample size was limited by our budget. Resulting estimates may not be precise or enable inference, but data are adequate for testing and initial analyses, and will inform data collection at a larger scale.

We aspired to select clusters from each of the 12 district-specific sampling frames using probability proportional to size methodology, which gives larger clusters (those with a greater population or a higher number of households) a higher probability of selection than smaller clusters. This approach ensures households within the study area have an equal probability of being selected, regardless of cluster size. However, the accessible census data were outdated and we were not confident that the method would result in the intended outcome. While the approach we are pursuing instead (a multistage, cluster-stratified random sampling method) can skew representativeness, we will apply poststratification weights and carry out sensitivity analyses as mitigation.
