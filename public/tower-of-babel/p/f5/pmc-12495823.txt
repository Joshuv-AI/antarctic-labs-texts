Niosome-loaded silibinin and methotrexate for synergistic breast cancer combination chemotherapy: in silico and in vitro study
Authors: Aysan Salemi, Masoumeh Kaveh Zenjanab, Mohammad Amin Adili Aghdam, Niloofar Sahragardan, Rana Jahanban Esfahlan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundBreast cancer, a leading cause of cancer-related deaths in women, faces significant treatment challenges due to drug resistance. Methotrexate (MTX), an effective chemotherapy medication for various malignancies, often encounters resistance in breast cancer, reducing its efficacy. This resistance underscores the urgent need for novel therapeutic strategies. Nano-drug delivery systems (NDDSs), such as niosomes, offer a promising solution. These systems can encapsulate both hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs, enabling reduced dosages and enhanced delivery. By overcoming drug resistance, NDDSs pave the way for more effective combination chemotherapy in breast cancer treatment.MethodsIn this study, two pharmacological agents, (i) methotrexate (MTX) as a hydrophilic drug and (ii) silibinin (SiL) as a hydrophobic drug, were simultaneously loaded into the hydrophilic and lipophilic part of niosome, respectively. Niosomes were synthesized by the thin film layer hydration method and characterized by zeta sizer, FTIR, and TEM. Also, MTT assay, DAPI, dead/alive and F-actin/DAPI staining, and spheroid cell culture were used to analyze nanoparticle biocompatibility, cell viability, apoptosis, cell adhesion density, and anti-tumor response, respectively, in 2D and 3D cultured MDA-MB-231 mammospheres. Additionally, an in-silico network analysis was conducted to investigate the interaction of MTX and SiL with human proteins, especially those that contribute to breast cancer pathways.ResultsIdeal niosomes with spherical morphology, ~ 87 nm size and ~-15 mV zeta potential, and high biocompatibility were successfully synthesized. The combination of MTX and SiL exhibited significant synergistic effects, as evidenced by the Fa value of 0.5 for NiO@MTXSiL at a concentration of 3.84 µg/mL. This value is markedly lower compared to those observed for MTXSiL (11.78 µg/mL), SiL (26.24 µg/mL), and MTX (18.48 µg/mL). Importantly, in the TNBC microtumor model, lower doses of NiO@MTXSiL achieved an almost complete anti-tumor drug response, leaving only ~ 6% residual tumor cells. Moreover, our computational analysis identified seven human proteins (i.e. BRCA1, CCND1, CDK4, CDK6, CDKN1A, Rb1, and Tp53) as breast cancer key players in the MTX and SiL interaction network with human proteins. Of these, Tp53 emerges as the most crucial protein, serving as a hub-bottleneck node, a common direct neighbor of MTX and SiL, and a key player in four breast cancer subtypes.ConclusionThe designed nano-niosome, NiO@MTXSiL, is safe, stable, and has an optimal size and surface charge. It offers high drug loading capacity for co-delivering hydrophobic and hydrophilic chemotherapeutics with different anti-cancer mechanisms, improving anti-tumor response and overcoming MDR. It shows higher cytotoxicity against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells compared to free drugs, making it a promising candidate to combat MTX resistance in breast cancer.Graphical abstract Keywords: Breast cancer, Combination therapy, Dual drug delivery, Drug resistance, Methotrexate, Niosome, Silibinin

Introduction

Breast cancer remains a major health concern globally, with 2.3 million new cases in 2020, accounting for 11.7% of all new cancer diagnoses [1]. The progression and recurrence of breast cancer are often linked to cancer stem cells (CSCs), a small crucial subpopulation of cancer cells with capabilities for self-renewal, unlimited proliferation, and multidirectional differentiation, compared with other common stem cells [2]. These CSCs are key players in tumor initiation, development, invasion, metastasis, drug resistance, and recurrence, making them key targets for innovative cancer therapies. Advances in targeting CSCs include strategies focused on their niche, signaling pathways, and mitochondria, supported by rapid developments in nano-drug delivery systems (NDDSs) [3].

Mammospheres, spherical clusters of cells enriched for stem-like cells, including cancer stem cells (CSCs), are derived from primary breast tumors or cell lines and are thought to originate from CSC subpopulations [4]. Unlike traditional two-dimensional (2D) methods, culturing these mammospheres in three-dimensional (3D) methods better maintains their malignant tumor phenotype and CSC original characteristics, such as self-renewal and chemoresistance [5]. This 3D culture system allows for enhanced cell and cell-matrix interactions, closely mimicking the tumor environment in vivo, and is crucial for exploring many aspects of cancer biology and testing anti-tumor drugs [6, 7].

The challenges of treating breast cancer include drug resistance, inadequate drug selectivity, and severe adverse effects associated with conventional chemotherapy [8]. Despite the wide array of therapeutic options, there exist persistent hurdles in novel therapeutic approaches [9, 10]. In this context, NDDSs have emerged as a promising avenue for enhancing breast cancer treatment efficacy, particularly through targeted drug delivery [11–13]. Among NDDSs, niosomes, bilayer-structured vesicles made from non-ionic surfactants, have been identified as superior carriers for a variety of pharmacological compounds (e.g., antigens, therapeutic drugs, genes, and peptides) [14]. Niosomes protect drugs from degradation, enhance permeation through cellular membranes, and improve biocompatibility. They also offer advantages in drug dose reduction, co-loading hydrophobic and hydrophilic drugs, and cost-effective production without requiring specialized storage conditions [15].

There is a growing focus on combination therapy by repurposing established drugs with known safety profiles for breast cancer treatment [16]. For example, Celecoxib, a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory, and Metformin, a medication for type 2 diabetes, have shown promise in preclinical studies due to their effects on breast cancer cell growth and metabolism [17]. Combining such repurposed drugs with NDDSs could potentially revolutionize breast cancer therapy by enabling more effective and combinative targeted therapy approaches. For this aim, this study investigates the potential of NDDSs by combining Methotrexate (MTX), a well-established drug, and Silibinin (SiL), a promising natural lignan, as a novel combinative therapeutic approach for breast cancer. MTX, a folate analog, inhibits DNA synthesis and replication, and is used in treating various cancers, including breast cancer [18]. However, achieving therapeutic effectiveness often requires high doses that can lead to toxicity and drug resistance [19, 20]. Natural compounds like SiL, extracted from Silybum marianum, offer effective strategies for combating cancer while potentially reducing toxicity and side effects compared to conventional treatments [21]. SiL has broad applications, including liver protection, diabetes management, and cancer treatment SiL [22]. It reduces stemness properties and tumor growth, decreases the viability and growth rate of mammospheres, and interacts with genes contributing to metastasis [23]. Both MTX and Sil have demonstrated promising anti-cancer effects in preclinical studies, and have safety profiles in clinical settings [21, 24, 25]. MTX resistance is a common feature for solid tumors [26], and we previously showed that NiO@MTXSiL improved MTX efficacy against CT26 colon cancer cells [27]. The current work represents both the initial evaluation of combining MTX and SiL in breast cancer and the first instance of utilizing their niosomal formulation (NiO@MTXSiL) for breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-231 mammospheres. By integrating the mechanisms of action of MTX and SiL, our goal is to develop a novel treatment modality that enhances the therapeutic alternatives for breast cancer patients. Additionally, using the 3D spheroid model allows for a more effective evaluation of NiO@MTXSiL’s efficacy compared to traditional 2D experiments, providing insights into the in vivo behavior of NiO@MTXSiL while an in silico study revealed possible hub gene(s) responsible for the synergistic action of two drugs.

Materials and methods

Materials

The MDA-MB-231 cell line was obtained from the Pasteur Institute of Iran. Methotrexate (MTX) was procured from NanoAlvand Co. (Tehran, Iran). The silibinin (SiL), Span 60, Cholesterol, Tween 80, 3-(4,5-dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)−2,5-diphenyl-2 H-tetrazolium bromide (MTT), 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI), and Trypsin-EDTA were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Additional materials included the Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit (MabTag, Germany), RPMI1640 and FBS (AnnaCell, ScienCell, USA), and Penicillin-Streptomycin solution (P/S, ScienCell, USA). All other analytical-grade reagents and solvents utilized in this study were supplied by Merck.

Nanoparticle Preparation and characterization

Preparation of niosomes

Niosomes and their components were synthesized using the thin film hydration (TFH) technique [27, 28]. Briefly, Span 60 (10 mg), Tween 80 (18 mg), cholesterol (7 mg), and SiL (2 mg) were dissolved in a mixture of methanol (5 mL) and chloroform (5 mL). The solution was subjected to rotary evaporation for 1 h at 60 °C and 120 rpm under vacuum to remove the solvents. The resulting proniosome (lipid film) was hydrated with 10 mL of PBS containing 2 mg of MTX. The NiO@MTXSiL niosomes were then sonicated using a probe sonicator (Fisher Scientific Co., USA) at an amplitude of 25% and power of 200 W to form a niosome suspension [29].

Determination of size and zeta potential

Dynamic light scattering (DLS) with a Zetasizer Nano ZS instrument (Malvern, UK) was used to measure the size and zeta potential of the niosomes [30].

Nanoparticle morphology

The morphology of niosome was determined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Additionally, atomic force microscopy (AFM) was employed to analyze the size and surface morphology of the niosomes [31].

FT-IR spectroscopy

The physicochemical interactions between the blank niosomes, drug-loaded niosomes, and the drugs were analyzed using Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR; Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer) spectroscopy in the IR range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹ [27].

Encapsulation efficiency

The efficacy of entrapment of MTX and SiL in niosomes was investigated by UV–Vis spectroscopy [32, 33]. In brief, NiO@MTXSiL was added to the dialysis membrane bag (molecular weight cut-off 12 000 Da) and immersed in 70 mL PBS with stirring for 30 min on ice. Then the unloaded MTX and SiL were measured by UV–Vis spectroscopy (Cecil BioAquarius CE 7250, England) at 310 nm and 290 nm, respectively. In vitro drug release studies

To determine the release profiles of MTX and SiL from NiO@MTXSiL nanoparticles, we placed the NiO@MTXSiL NPs into two dialysis membrane bags and immersed them in 100 mL of PBS with pH values of 7.4 and 5.8, respectively. These setups were maintained under continuous shaking conditions (90 rpm) at 37 °C. To sustain the sink condition, we periodically removed 2 mL of the release medium at designated time intervals (15 min, 30 min, 1 h, 2 h, 3 h, 4 h, 5 h, 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h) and replaced it with 2 mL of fresh buffer after each withdrawal. The amounts of MTX and SiL released were quantified by measuring absorbance at 310 nm and 290 nm, respectively [27].

Drug combination index (CI)

The synergistic effects of combined forms of free MTX and SiL, as well as their nanoformulations (NiO@MTX and NiO@SiL), were evaluated using CompuSyn software 1.0.

Drug release kinetics

Drug kinetic behavior of NiO@SiL and NiO@MTX normal and acidic pH were determined according to our previous work [27] using the following mathematical models for drug kinetics including Zero-Order, First-Order, Peppas Korsmeyer Higuchi, and Hixson-Crowell.

Cell culture and cellular toxicity

The human MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cell line was cultured in RPMI1640 medium supplemented with 100 µg/mL streptomycin, 100 U/mL penicillin, and 10% FBS. A total of 5000 cells were seeded in each well of 96-well plates. After 24 h, the cells were treated with specific doses from the experimental groups and incubated for 48 h. To assess the toxicity of niosome-containing drugs, the cell culture medium was replaced with fresh medium, followed by the addition of MTT dye (0.5 mg/mL) to each well. The cells were incubated for 4 h at 37 °C. Subsequently, DMSO was added as a solubilizer, and cell viability was measured using an ELISA reader (Stat Fax 4200, RayBiotech INC, USA) at 570 nm [35].

Spheroid culture and treatment with nio@mtxsil

Briefly, a 1% agarose solution was prepared by dissolving 20 mg of agarose powder in 20 mL sterile distilled water in a centrifuge tube and heated to 90 °C for 1 min. This solution was poured into a 96-well plate, 100 µL each well, and was allowed to solidify at room temperature under sterile conditions. After that, 2 × 104 MDA-MB-231 cells were seeded on agarose beds. Spheroids were checked every day by a light microscope (Olymus, Japan) and spheroids with a size of ~ 300 μm were used for further studies. The potential anti-tumor effects of NiO@MTXSiL on the size and morphology of MDA-MB-231 spheroids investigated at 24, 48, and 72 h post-treatment [34].

Assessment of apoptosis induction via live/dead cell staining

To determine the proportions of live (pre-apoptotic) and dead (apoptotic) cells in a 3D cell culture, we used Annexin V-FITC/PI staining [34]. To this, 24 h after treatment with Nio@MTXSiL, the cells were washed twice with PBS and stained with 5 µL of Annexin V-FITC and 2 µL of PI dye for 10 min. The results were analyzed using a Citation V cell imaging system, with filters of 515–545 nm for FITC and 535–617 nm for PI.

Cell adhesion density via dapi/collagen staining

Double staining of the extracellular matrix with F-actin and DAPI staining was applied to assess cell adhesion density [27]. For this, 5,000 cells per well were seeded and treated with Nio@MTXSiL for 24 h. The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and incubated with 0.1% Triton X-100 for 10 min to increase permeability. After washing with PBS, the cells were stained with phalloidin for 45 min and DAPI dye for 5 min. Cellular imaging was performed using a Cytation™ 5 cell imaging instrument (BioTek, Winooski, USA).

Interaction of MTX and SiL with homo sapiens proteins

To explore the interactions of MTX and SiL with human proteins, we utilized the STITCH database (Search Tool for Interactions of Chemicals, version 5; http://stitch.embl.de/). STITCH is a comprehensive database that provides information on known and predicted interactions between chemicals and proteins. The database integrates evidence from various sources, including experiments, databases, and literature, to establish links between chemicals and their interacting proteins [36]. Additionally, the UniProt database (the world’s leading high-quality, comprehensive, and freely accessible resource for protein research [37]; https://www.uniprot.org/), was utilized for gene ontology analysis. The MTX and SiL protein-protein interaction (MTX|SiL-PPI) network was visualized using Cytoscape v3.10.2 software [38], and network centralities (degree, betweenness, and bottleneck) were calculated using its CytoHubba [39] and CytoNCA [40] plugins. Based on the sum of the calculated centralities, proteins were ranked, and the top 20% of nodes in the high-confidence network were identified as potential targets for breast cancer therapy research, such as drug development. Finally, the obtained interactions were analyzed using the KEGG database (a comprehensive database for bioinformatics research in various omics fields, systems biology, and drug development [41]; https://www.genome.jp/kegg/pathway.htmL) to find breast cancer-related pathways.

Statistical analysis

For the statistical analysis, we used GraphPad Prism 9.0 software. One-way or two-way ANOVA tests were conducted to assess mean differences among experimental groups, followed by a post hoc Tukey test to identify specific group differences. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Niosome characterization

The successful synthesis of nanosized niosomes was accomplished using a preparation method, outlined in the workflow shown in Fig. 1A. DLS results indicated that the niosomes had a size of 87.54 ± 20.55 nm, a polydispersity index (PDI) of 0.582, and a zeta potential of −15 ± 8 mV (Fig. 1B-C). TEM revealed that the niosomes have a spherical morphology with a size of 60 nm (Fig. 1D). Also, AFM measurements indicated that the size of the niosomes is approximately 60 nm (Fig. 1E). The encapsulation efficiency was determined to be 88.5 ± 3% and 81 ± 15% for SiL and MTX, respectively. The encapsulation efficiency was determined to be 88.5 ± 3% for SiL and 81 ± 15% for MTX. Figure 1F shows the FT-IR spectra of niosomes and NiO@MTXSiL. The FT-IR spectrum of blank NiO exhibited characteristic peaks at 2923 and 2853 cm⁻¹, corresponding to the C-H symmetric stretch. Stretching vibrations of C = O, C-N, and C–O–C, as well as O-H groups, were evident at 1737, 1634, 1109, and 3436 cm⁻¹, respectively. The absence of additional peaks in NiO and NiO@MTXSiL indicated the physical loading of drugs into the niosomes.

Furthermore, a drug release study demonstrated an increased release of both drugs in PBS at a pH of 5.8 compared to a pH of 7.4. Specifically, the release rates were 88.62% vs. 65.62% for MTX (a hydrophilic drug) and 86.64% vs. 65.84% for SiL (a hydrophobic drug) over 72 h (Fig. 1G-H).

For MTX, both the Higuchi and Korsmeyer-Peppas models showed comparable good fits (Higuchi: R²=0.874 and 0.869; Korsmeyer-Peppas: R²=0.878 and 0.862 at pH 7.4 and 5.8 respectively). The strong correlation with the Higuchi model suggests that the primary mechanism of drug release follows diffusion-based kinetics, where the release rate is proportional to the square root of time. The first-order model also showed a good correlation (R² >0.8 for all conditions), indicating concentration-dependent release behavior. The zero-order model showed the lowest R² values across all conditions (ranging from 0.756 to 0.860), suggesting that the release rate is not constant over time. Interestingly, for both drugs, the release kinetics showed slightly better correlation coefficients at pH 7.4 compared to pH 5.8 across most models, indicating more predictable release behavior at physiological pH.

Drug combination index

To study the mode of combination effects, we evaluated CI and (Fa, effect). A CI value of > 0.1 means a very strong synergism effect; CI = 0.3–0.8 indicates a strong to slight synergism effect, CI: 0.9–1.1 corresponds to an additive effect and CI > 1 refers to antagonism [42]. Further, the results of combination therapy determined CI values of 0.54 µg/mL and 0.431 µg/mL, for free drugs and their niosomal formulation, respectively. This indicates a good synergistic effect which was higher in niosomal formulations compared to a combination of free drugs. Also, for the free drug combination and their corresponding niosomal formulation, Fa = 0.5 (equal to the IC50 in the MTT assay) was determined to be 11.78 µg/mL and 3.84 µg/mL, respectively. Also, the drug reduction index (DRI) was 3.13 µg/mL and 4.45 µg/mL for MTX and SiL drugs and 3.63 µg/mL and 6.38 µg/mL for NiO@MTX and NiO@SiL respectively (Table 2).

Cell viability assay

Cell toxicity was evaluated using the MTT assay in MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. The cells were treated with different doses (0, 0.5, 2, 5, and 10 µg/mL) of free drugs and niosomal formulations over 48 h. Results showed that in all studied concentrations, the cell viability in groups treated with blank niosomes was > 85%, indicating the safety and biocompatibility of prepared niosomes. Meanwhile, NiO@MTXSiL resulted in more toxicity compared to MTXSiL in all doses in a dose- and time-dependent manner. At the highest dose of 10 µg/mL, NiO@MTXSiL reduced cell viability to 21.4% compared to MTXSiL with 32.86%, MTX 49.88%, SiL 58.2%, NiO@MTX 39.7%, NiO@SiL 45.3%, respectively. Thus, combination therapy of MTX and SiL by niosomal formulation favored enhanced drug toxicity compared to free drugs (Fig. 2).

Anti-tumor response of nio@mtxsil in TNBC microtumors

We further evaluated the effect of NiO@MTXSiL5 on the size and morphology of MDA-MB-231 spheroids for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h. The diameter of spheroids in the untreated control group was unchanged (~ 300–400 μm), and spheroids showed an intact circular morphology with soft edges, while the group treated with NiO@MTXSiL5 showed almost complete elimination of TNBC microtumors by 72 h, resulting in significant size reductions of 104.51 ± 13.25 μm, 67.01 ± 7.9 μm, and 6.07 ± 1.5 μm for 24 h, 48 h, and 72 h, respectively (Fig. 3).

Live/dead cell staining

We also examined the effect of NiO@MTXSiL on apoptosis induction by assessing live/dead cells using Annexin V-FITC/PI staining in MDA-MB-231 tumor spheroids. The results showed that in the untreated group, almost all cells were alive. In contrast, groups treated with different doses of NiO@MTXSiL exhibited dose-dependent toxicity. That is, there was a partial induction of apoptosis (~ 50%) in NiO@MTXSiL2 while in NiO@MTXSiL5 and NiO@MTXSiL10, a complete anti-tumor response and almost complete induction of apoptosis was evident (~ 100% dead cells) (Fig. 4).

Cell adhesion density

We also assessed the effect of NiO@MTXSiL on cellular adhesion density by F-actin/DAPI staining. Phalloidin is a fluorescent derivative compound with high affinity to cytoskeleton protein F-actin, while DAPI stains cells (nucleus and cytoplasm). Compared to the untreated control, NiO@MTXSiL5 resulted in a significant reduction in MDA-MB-231 cell adhesion density by 42.528% after 24 h, while in the group treated with blank niosome (20 µg/mL) there were no significant changes in cell adhesion density compared to the untreated group (p < 0.05) (Fig. 5).

Discussion

The objective of this study was to investigate the potential of NiO@MTXSiL (niosome-loaded methotrexate and silibinin) in targeting triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) mammospheres, as a rich source of CSCs. It is important to note that the characterization of CSCs cannot solely rely on cell surface markers, as suggested by previous studies [43, 44]. Therefore, the use of mammospheres, which better replicate the tumor hypoxic microenvironment, cell-cell, and cell-substrate interactions, was considered more appropriate for this study [23]. Also, in breast cancer, resistance to MTX is reported due to decreased expression of MTX transport proteins and reduced folate carriers which undermine the anti-tumor efficacy of this common chemotherapeutic [26]. To this end, 2D and 3D cultures using mammospheres derived from MDA-MB-231 cells were treated with NiO@MTXSiL, and various analyses were conducted to evaluate (i) drug loading efficacy, (ii) in vitro drug release, (iii) cellular toxicity, (iv) drug combination index, (v) induction of apoptosis, (vi) cell adhesion density, (vii) anti-tumor response of spheroids, and (viii) network-based analysis of MTXSiL interaction with cancer-related proteins.

In our study on NNDSs, we selected niosomes due to their unique properties. Niosomes can simultaneously carry both hydrophilic and hydrophobic drugs, exhibit lower cytotoxicity compared to other nanoparticles, are easy to synthesize, and are cost-effective [45]. Niosomes can be prepared using several techniques, including TFH, ether injection, and reverse phase evaporation processes. In this study, niosomes containing SiL as a hydrophobic drug in the lipophilic domain and MTX as a hydrophilic drug in the aqueous core were synthesized using the TFH method [46]. DLS analysis showed a size of 87.5 nm and a zeta potential of −15 mV for the prepared niosome, while TEM analysis revealed spherical shapes and a smaller size of about 60 nm. This discrepancy arises because DLS measures the hydrated size, which is typically larger. TEM and AFM analyses confirmed the small, uniform, spherical shape of the niosomes, beneficial for effective drug delivery due to enhanced permeability and retention in tumor tissues. The zeta potential, indicating physical stability, was within the acceptable range, ensuring stability in biological conditions [10]. The FT-IR technique was used to determine the physicochemical interactions between the blank niosome, NiO@MTXSiL, and free drugs. The data obtained with FT-IR show that the drugs loaded into the niosome do not exhibit physicochemical interactions [47]. The loading efficiency of SiL and MTX in the niosomes, a critical physicochemical parameter of NDDSs, was determined to be greater than 80%. The TFH method for synthesizing niosomes results in a higher drug-loading capacity [48]. This increase in drug loading is also influenced by the specific components used in the synthesis. Span 60, which is utilized in the preparation of niosomes, significantly enhances drug entrapment. Span 60 has a longer alkyl chain (C18) and the highest transition temperature (Tc = 53 °C) among Spans, allowing it to form more stable membrane bilayers. This stability leads to greater entrapment efficiency compared to other Spans, such as Span 20 [49, 50]. Drug loading efficiency is improved by the addition of cholesterol, which facilitates the formation of vesicles from surfactants and enhances their stability [51]. The release behavior of the proposed systems was also studied at two pH values (5.8 and 7.4). The release of SiL and MTX was monitored using UV–Vis absorption, revealing a two-stage release behavior. Initially, the release is relatively rapid, followed by a plateau, indicating equilibrium, at two different pH values. Niosomes are so sensitive to acidic pH because amphiphilic surfactants are sensitive to acidic pH and can enhance drug release from niosome-based carriers [52–54]. The reason for this is the typical behavior of niosomes in an acidic state, which leads to swelling and decomposing of the niosome in this state [55]. Tumor cells have high acidity that is attributed to hypoxia, or lack of oxygen due to insufficient blood supply [56]. These data indicate that the pH-sensitive niosomal NPs are promising NDDSs to combat cancer.

Multidrug resistance remains a significant and persistent challenge in cancer therapies [2]. NPs offer a promising solution by penetrating cells through receptor-mediated endocytosis, thereby increasing intracellular drug concentration. Notably, while membrane efflux transporters can pump free drugs out of the cell, P-glycoprotein does not recognize drugs encapsulated within NPs [57]. In this study, the combined delivery of SiL and MTX in a niosome enhanced their therapeutic efficacy against MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells. This combination therapy demonstrated a synergistic effect, leveraging the distinct mechanisms of SiL and MTX to combat breast cancer more effectively [58]. SiL exerts its anti-cancer effects through multiple pathways, including the induction of apoptosis, inhibition of cell proliferation, reduction of angiogenesis, modulation of inflammation, and alteration of cancer cell metabolism [59]. Additionally, SiL has been shown to reduce tumor volume in 3D cultures, inhibit sphere formation, and decrease colony formation [23]. MTX, a first-line treatment for breast cancer, functions by inhibiting dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR). This inhibition leads to the accumulation of polyglutamated folate, which further inhibits glycinamide ribonucleotide formyltransferase and thymidylate synthase. Consequently, the synthesis of purine nucleotides is impaired, disrupting DNA synthesis and thereby reducing cell proliferation [60].

The results of this study demonstrate the potential of NiO@MTXSiL as an effective combination therapy for TNBC. The MTT assay showed that NiO@MTXSiL exhibited superior toxicity against MDA-MB-231 cells compared to free drugs and individual niosomal formulations at all tested concentrations. This enhanced toxicity suggests a synergistic effect between MTX and SiL when delivered via niosomes. The CI analysis further substantiated the strong synergism between the two drugs, with niosomal formulations showing a lower CI value (0.431 µg/mL) than free drugs (0.54 µg/mL). Additionally, the drug reduction index (DRI) values for NiO@MTX and NiO@SiL were higher than their free drug counterparts, indicating the potential for dose reduction while maintaining therapeutic efficacy. The anti-tumor response of NiO@MS5 was further evaluated in TNBC microtumors. Over a 72-h period, a significant reduction in spheroid size was observed, with the average diameter decreasing from about 104 μm at 24 h to about 6 μm at 72 h. The live/dead staining of tumor spheroids treated with NiO@MS revealed a dose-dependent induction of apoptosis. At higher doses (NiO@MS5 and Nio@MS10), almost complete cell death was observed, highlighting the potent anti-tumor effect of the niosomal formulation. Furthermore, F-actin/DAPI staining demonstrated a significant reduction in cell adhesion density (42.528%) in MDA-MB-231 cells treated with NiO@MS5 compared to the untreated control, suggesting an inhibitory effect on cell attachment and potential anti-metastatic properties. This marked reduction in tumor size, accompanied by morphological changes, underscores the effectiveness of the niosomal formulation in targeting and eliminating TNBC microtumors.

Our results using large-sized MDA-MB-231 mammospheres as reliable 3D cancer biomimetics indicated NiO@MTXSiL can effectively deliver drugs in deep hypoxic tumor cores where CSCs are residing and are inaccessible to the chemotherapeutics as well as many NDDSs [2, 61]. It is worth noting that according to our previous works, NiO@MTXSiL possessed maximum tumor penetration depth and was capable of almost complete residual tumor cell elimination down to 6%, compared to other cationic NPs such as hypoxia-responsive dendrimer [35] and chitosan NPs [34] which leave ~ 10% live cells post-therapy, in TNBC tumor spheroids. This finding can be explained by the physiochemical properties of niosome nanocarrier as well as the combined work of two different drugs upon resistant and highly CSC-enriched tumors such as CT26 colon [27] and MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells.

Network analysis and graph theory have emerged as powerful tools in breast cancer drug development. By modeling complex biological systems as networks, researchers can gain insights into the intricate relationships between genes, proteins, and signaling pathways involved in breast cancer [62]. By representing proteins as nodes and their interactions as edges, graph theory algorithms enable the identification of critical nodes and hubs within these networks, which can serve as potential drug targets. This approach facilitates the discovery of novel therapeutic strategies that can effectively disrupt the key molecular mechanisms driving breast cancer growth and progression [63]. Moreover, network-based methods can help predict drug responses, optimize combination therapies, and identify biomarkers for personalized treatment. The integration of network analysis and graph theory in breast cancer research holds immense promise for the development of more targeted, efficient, and patient-specific therapies [64]. According to network biology, the topological structure of a network can be directly associated with biological functions in breast cancer. In this way, topological parameters in combination with biological knowledge can shed light on key mechanisms and determinants of a breast cancer network. Nodes with a high degree of connectivity, known as hub proteins, and nodes with a high betweenness centrality, called bottlenecks, are often essential proteins encoded by essential genes [65]. Knockout or mutation of these genes can affect many phenotypic traits or result in cancer cell death because these genes can be analogized to a heavily used intersection in foremost biological highways or bridges in breast cancer [66, 67]. The centrality-lethality rule suggests that deleting hub nodes is more likely to be lethal to a breast cancer cell than deleting non-hub nodes, highlighting the importance of network centralities in determining network function [68]. In conclusion, network analysis and graph theory provide a comprehensive framework for understanding the complex biological systems involved in cancer. By focusing on hubs, bottlenecks, and their associated pathways, researchers can identify key proteins that serve as potential drug targets, leading to the development of more targeted therapies for breast cancer patients [69–71].

For this purpose, the MTX|SiL-PPI network was constructed to identify hub-bottleneck nodes in breast cancer pathways. Among the direct and indirect neighbors of MTX and SiL, three proteins, ABCG2, PCNA, Tp53, are common. Also, Tp53[26: Number of degree], BCL2[24], PCNA[22], CCND1[21], CDK2[21], CDK4[21], CDKN1A[21] serve as hub nodes of MTX|SiL-PPI network, of which PCNA[4: Number of bottleneck], CDK2[4], Tp53[3], BCL2[3] are bottleneck nodes. Consequently, these four proteins, PCNA, CDK2, Tp53, BCL2, as hub-bottleneck nodes, can be considered key proteins in this network and are highly potential targets for drug development. Additionally, gene ontology analysis revealed that most of the proteins in the MTX|SiL-PPI network are intracellular and specifically located in the nucleus. This finding is consistent with available wet-lab results. For example, MTX inhibits dihydrofolate reductase (DHFR), an enzyme essential for nucleotide synthesis in the nucleus [72]. Similarly, SiL has been shown to interact with nuclear proteins such as cyclins, cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs), and the tumor suppressor protein Tp53, all of which play crucial roles in cell cycle regulation and apoptosis [73]. Pathway enrichment analysis identified a set of key proteins that play crucial roles in breast cancer, including BRCA1, CCND1, CDK4, CDK6, CDKN1A, Rb1, and Tp53. Interestingly, all of these proteins, except for BRCA1, were found to have significant involvement across the four major subtypes of breast cancer (Luminal A, Luminal B, HER2-positive, and Basal-like/Triple-negative breast cancer). This finding highlights the importance of these proteins in the development and progression of various breast cancer subtypes, suggesting their potential as therapeutic targets or biomarkers for a wide range of breast cancer patients to overcome chemotherapy resistance.

Our computational analysis reveals that PCNA, CDK2, Tp53, BCL2 are hub-bottleneck nodes and direct neighbors of MTX and SiL. Furthermore, BRCA1, CCND1, CDK4, CDK6, CDKN1A, Rb1, and Tp53 are key players of MTX|SiL-PPI network in breast cancer. Among these, Tp53 emerges as the most crucial protein in our network, as it is a hub-bottleneck node, a common direct neighbor of MTX and SiL, and a key player in four breast cancer subtypes. The Tp53 (cellular tumor antigen p53), a crucial tumor suppressor protein, plays a central role in maintaining genomic stability and preventing cancer development. According to previous studies, Tp53 is found in increased amounts in a wide variety of transformed cells and is frequently mutated or inactivated in about 60% of cancers. In breast cancer, targeting Tp53 has emerged as a promising strategy for drug development, as mutations or alterations in the Tp53 gene are frequently observed in various breast cancer subtypes. Researchers are developing innovative therapeutic strategies to combat breast cancer by restoring wild-type Tp53 function [74], disrupting Tp53-MDM2 interaction [75], exploiting Tp53-mediated synthetic lethal interactions [76], and utilizing immunotherapy targeting p53 [77]. Thus, targeting Tp53 in breast cancer drug development holds great promise, as our understanding of Tp53 biology continues to grow, it is expected that more targeted and effective therapies will emerge, ultimately improving the outcomes for breast cancer patients.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides compelling evidence for the potential of NiO@MTXSiL as an effective combination therapy for breast cancer. The niosomal formulation demonstrated enhanced synergism, reduced toxicity, and potent anti-tumor responses, suggesting its potential to lead to more effective treatment strategies. The capacity of niosomes to simultaneously encapsulate both SiL (a hydrophobic drug) and MTX (a hydrophilic drug) has proven to be beneficial in combating cancer and addressing relapse issues. The results indicate that NiO@MTXSiL has higher cytotoxicity on MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells compared to free drugs, making it a promising candidate for breast cancer treatment. Furthermore, the integration of computational and experimental approaches revealed that the dual delivery of MTX and SiL can target Tp53, a key protein in the breast cancer network. The concordance between computational results and validated experimental findings underscores the robustness and reliability of the in silico approach, reinforcing the validity of the insights gained through our analysis. Nonetheless, these findings lay the groundwork for the development of novel nanomedicine-based approaches for the treatment of breast cancer and other aggressive cancers; however, further in vivo studies and clinical trials are necessary to fully evaluate the safety and efficacy of this combination therapy before its translation into clinical practice.
