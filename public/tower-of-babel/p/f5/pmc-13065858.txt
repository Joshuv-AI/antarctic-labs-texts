Maternal Isoimmunization‐Associated Fetal Anemia After First‐Trimester Abortion: A Case Report and Literature Review
Authors: Fatemeh Golshahi, Behrokh sahebdel, Fatemeh Rahimi‐Sharbaf, Mahboobeh Shirazi, Nafiseh Saedi, Khadijeh Adabi, Arash Jafarieh, Maedeh Mollaalipour Amiri, Zahra Hamidi Madani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIsoimmunization and fetal anemia pose considerable challenges in obstetrics, especially when there is a documented history of RhD incompatibility or other antibodies that may threaten fetal well‐being. These conditions frequently result in serious complications, including hydrops fetalis, fetal anemia, and, in certain situations, fetal loss. Effective management of such pregnancies needs careful monitoring and prompt interventions, such as intrauterine transfusion (IUT), along with thorough post‐delivery follow‐up to safeguard both maternal and neonatal health. Due to ongoing uncertainties concerning the administration of Rh immunoglobulin (RhIg) in RhD‐negative mothers during the first trimester of pregnancy, specifically before 12 weeks of gestation, this rare case series examines two pregnancies complicated by fetal anemia and isoimmunization, emphasizing the clinical trajectory, management approaches, and outcomes for the neonates involved. This presentation discusses two cases of Rh isoimmunized pregnant women with significant histories of early pregnancy loss. The first patient, a 27‐year‐old G3Ab2 woman, had experienced two previous miscarriages—one at 9 weeks induced pharmacologically and another spontaneous abortion at 8 weeks without curettage. She underwent her first intrauterine transfusion (IUT) at 21 weeks and 2 days with an Anti‐D titer of 1:512 and a fetal hemoglobin level of 9.2 mg/dL. A total of two IUTs were performed, and she delivered a healthy neonate at term with normal Apgar and growth. The second patient, a 32‐year‐old G2Ab1 woman, had one prior medically induced abortion at 7 weeks using misoprostol, with no surgical intervention. She received her first IUT at 24 weeks and 5 days, with a lower anti‐D titer of 1:164 and a fetal hemoglobin level of 5.1 mg/dL. Despite requiring four transfusions, she delivered at 37 + 1 weeks a healthy neonate with normal development. In the first case, the newborn presented with a bilirubin concentration 7.2 and exhibited positive results for anti‐D and anti‐C antibodies, both at elevated titers. In the subsequent case, the infant had a birth weight of 3 kg, normal Apgar scores, and a hemoglobin level of 10.1. Upon discharge, the infant was reported to be in stable condition and demonstrated normal growth and development at 37 months of age. The management of abortions occurring before 12 weeks of gestation in women with a negative RhD and fetuses with a positive RhD is crucial for the prevention of isoimmunization. Therefore, it seems reasonable to administer RhIg in abortions under 12 weeks to prevent future consequences.

Introduction

Rh immunoglobulin (RhIg) is a limited human blood derivative employed to avert sensitization to the Rh antigen in individuals who are Rh‐negative and are exposed to substantial amounts of Rh‐positive blood. Before the advent of RhIg, approximately 9%–10% of Rh‐negative patients experienced sensitization with every full‐term pregnancy [1]. Fetal RhD‐positive red blood cells have the potential to sensitize an RhD‐negative woman in the event of fetomaternal hemorrhage [2]. As gestation advances, both the likelihood and severity of fetomaternal hemorrhage tend to rise. While the precise gestational age at which immunization may begin is not definitively established, it is widely accepted that the risk remains minimal before 8 weeks of gestation [3].

As of early 2019, the Society of Family Planning [4], World Health Organization [5], the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists [6], and the Society of Obstetricians and Gynecologists of Canada [7] advocate for the administration of anti‐D immunoglobulin to all RhD‐negative women following an induced abortion, irrespective of gestational age or the method employed. Nevertheless, the evidence supporting a definitive advantage of this intervention during early pregnancy, the specific gestational age at which sensitization may occur, and the distinctions among aspiration, sharp curettage, and medication abortion remain inadequately defined [8]. Limited research indicates that the administration of RhIg in the first trimester may lower the risk of RhD alloimmunization associated with both spontaneous and induced abortions, although precise estimates of this effect have yet to be established [9]. The current guidelines are founded on a limited dataset that fails to adequately establish the safety of omitting RhIg in cases of first‐trimester abortions or pregnancy losses [9].

Due to ongoing uncertainties concerning the administration of RhIg in RhD‐negative mothers during the first trimester of pregnancy, specifically before 12 weeks of gestation, this rare case series highlights two pregnancies complicated by fetal anemia and maternal isoimmunization, where both mothers had histories of early miscarriage without RhIg administration. It explores the clinical progression, management strategies, and outcomes, emphasizing the critical role of early detection and intervention in similar scenarios.

Case History/Examination

Below, we describe 2 cases. The two patients were from an urban setting in Iran and had access to tertiary care (national referral center). Both had secondary education and a middle socioeconomic status. There were no apparent barriers to care except the uncertainty of clinical guidelines. In these two cases, the patients had no previous full‐term deliveries, blood transfusions, uterine trauma, or other known sensitizing events (e.g., ectopic pregnancies, invasive procedures). Case 1 had two abortions without curettage, and Case 2 had one abortion without instrumentation. In both cases, isoimmunization was likely due to fetomaternal bleeding from previous first‐trimester abortions. Case 1 could be due to the induced abortion at 9 weeks of gestation or the spontaneous abortion at 8 weeks of gestation, but in Case 2, the misoprostol abortion at 7 weeks of gestation is likely, though it is difficult to attribute the cases definitively without data regarding antibody titers. The fetal surveillance involved serial ultrasounds performed at 1–2 week intervals after the intrauterine transfusion (IUT). The middle cerebral artery peak systolic velocity (MCA‐PSV) was performed for recurrent anemia, the amniotic fluid index for hydrops, and biometric measurements for growth restriction. Non‐stress tests and biophysical profiles were started at 28 weeks of gestation. The subsequent tests showed no growth restriction or hydrops following the initial IUT procedures. Delivery was planned at ~37 weeks to minimize risks of further IUTs (e.g., procedural complications like infection or preterm labor). A 27‐year‐old woman, gravida 3, abortion 2 (G3Ab2), with a history of two early pregnancy losses—one medically induced abortion at 9 weeks of gestation and one spontaneous abortion at 8 weeks of gestation without curettage—presented with RhD‐negative blood type and a positive antibody screen. She had never received Rh immunoglobulin (RhIg) during any of her previous pregnancies. Additional testing revealed positive results for anti‐D and anti‐C antibodies, with anti‐C at a titer of 1:1024 and anti‐D at 1:512. At 21 weeks and 2 days of gestation, she was referred to a national referral center for intrauterine transfusion (IUT) in Iran due to fetal anemia. The initial fetal hemoglobin level was measured at 9.2 mg/dL. Then a second IUT was performed successfully, during which the fetal hemoglobin was found to be 6.9 mg/dL, and the platelet count was 143,000. A total of 6 IUTs were performed. At 37 weeks and 5 days, the patient delivered a healthy neonate with a normal Apgar score, normal growth and development, indirect bilirubin level of 7.2, and direct bilirubin level of 0.3.A 32‐year‐old woman, gravida 2, abortion 1 (G2Ab1), with a history of one misoprostol‐induced abortion at 7 weeks of gestation without uterine instrumentation or surgical intervention, was referred at 24 weeks and 5 days of gestation to a prenatal clinic due to fetal ascites. She had no history of blood transfusion or uterine surgery. Ultrasound evaluation confirmed severe fetal anemia. Maternal antibody screening revealed an anti‐D titer of 1:164, indicative of isoimmunization, likely due to the prior early abortion. Cordocentesis revealed a fetal hemoglobin level of 5.1 mg/dL, bilirubin of 6.5, and a reticulocyte count of 30%. The patient subsequently underwent four IUTs: the second at 26 weeks and 4 days, with a post‐transfusion fetal hemoglobin level rising from 9.7 to 16.8 mg/dL. The pregnancy progressed uneventfully thereafter. At 37 weeks and 1 day, she delivered a 3‐kg neonate with normal Apgar scores, pH, and a hemoglobin level of 10.1. The newborn was admitted to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) for 16 days and required one postnatal blood transfusion. The infant was discharged in stable condition and, as of February 13, 2025, at 37 months of age, demonstrates normal growth and development. Conclusion

This case series highlights the potential risk of fetal anemia following early abortion in RhD‐negative women who do not receive Rh immunoglobulin (RhIg). Although intrauterine transfusion (IUT) can be an effective treatment with favorable neonatal outcomes, it remains a complex and resource‐intensive intervention. Given the low cost and well‐established safety profile of RhIg, these findings suggest that RhIg administration should be considered as a preventive strategy in early abortion to reduce the risk of alloimmunization and the subsequent need for invasive fetal therapies. However, due to the limited number of cases, definitive conclusions cannot be drawn. Larger, well‐designed studies—ideally randomized controlled trials—are needed to clarify which patients may benefit most from RhIg prophylaxis before 12 weeks of gestation and to inform cost‐effective, evidence‐based clinical guidelines better.

Discussion

In the presented rare case series, it was shown that both mothers had a history of first‐trimester abortion without RhIg administration and presented with isoimmunization and fetal anemia in the current pregnancy. Therefore, the fetuses were born in stable conditions after performing IUT. It seems that such outcomes can be prevented by RhIg administration during abortion in RhD‐negative mothers at any stage of pregnancy.

Fetal anemia represents a significant challenge within the field of perinatal science, and inadequate management of this condition can result in severe complications, including fetal hydrops and potential mortality. This approach is a lifesaving method for fetal suffering from anemia followed by RhD sensitization [10]. In the hands of skilled practitioners, IUT appears to be a relatively secure intervention. Nonetheless, complications can still arise, including in fetal cases. Previous research indicates that the incidence of pregnancy loss following IUT is approximately 1.2%, with instances of fetal demise primarily attributed to hydrops before the procedure [11]. However, since prevention is always better than cure, in such cases it seems that adopting preventive approaches may be more appropriate.

The study examined the rate of RhD isoimmunization in RhD‐negative women requesting abortion at < 12 weeks of gestation. The researchers searched medical databases and selected and analyzed 105 articles that specifically addressed this topic. The results of the study suggest that the rate of isoimmunization in this group is very low, and there is limited evidence of an increased risk of RhD sensitization after first‐trimester abortion. These findings help challenge the need for routine anti‐D administration in this setting and may influence future changes in clinical protocols [12]. A research investigation involving 52 women who experienced induced abortions focused on the extraction of fetal cells from placental tissue to analyze the fetal blood group & Rh. The findings of this study indicated that for RhD‐negative mothers at specific gestational ages, careful fetal RhD testing is essential before administering RhIg, thereby preventing unnecessary immunoglobulin injections. This approach emphasizes the importance of precise testing protocols to optimize maternal care and minimize the risk of excessive treatment in RhD‐negative pregnancies [13]. Early fetal assessment can present challenges in certain situations, making it more reasonable to consider the administration of RhIg. A dose of 50 mg within 72 h following either a spontaneous or induced abortion is generally sufficient to address the potential volume of fetomaternal hemorrhage during the first trimester. However, in some clinical environments, obtaining this lower dosage may be problematic. Consequently, guidelines suggest administering a 300 mg dose of RhIg when the lower dosage is not accessible [14, 15]. Also, cost analyses indicate that for pregnancies with a sensitization rate below 3%, it is feasible to screen RhD blood type and the administration of RhIg before 12 weeks of gestation. By eliminating this low‐value care, healthcare payers in the United States could potentially realize savings of up to $5.5 million per 100,000 pregnancies, while also preserving RhIg for administration at later stages of pregnancy [16]. However, even though several clinical guidelines, including those from NICE (2021) [19], RCOG [20], and ASRM [18], recommend the administration of RhIg following any abortion as a preventive measure against alloimmunization. Several systematic reviews have shown that the risk of alloimmunization before 12 weeks of gestation is low, with an incidence of less than 3% [12, 21]. In fact, several countries, such as the Netherlands and Denmark, have adopted a more targeted approach by choosing to avoid the administration of RhIg following very early abortions that occur before 7–10 weeks of gestation. In this context, the two cases that are the subject of this study illustrate the fact that although the risk of alloimmunization is low, such events are possible even without the administration of RhIg. However, the fact that the two cases were successfully managed with intrauterine transfusion (IUT) illustrates the fact that such fetuses can indeed receive effective treatment, although such treatment is often complex, invasive, and resource‐intensive.

From the point of view of health economics, the results of the cost‐effectiveness analyses suggest that if the rate of sensitization is less than 3%, the exclusion of RhIg prophylaxis before the 12th week of pregnancy could result in considerable cost savings as well as the preservation of RhIg for later gestational ages [22]. However, the results of the analyses may vary according to the healthcare system. For example, in resource‐scarce settings such as Iran, where access to specialized fetal therapies is limited and the costs of intrauterine transfusions, including the costs of repeated procedures as well as neonatal intensive care, are considerable, the more liberal use of RhIg could be considered a cost‐effective approach. Nevertheless, the more liberal use of RhIg prophylaxis must be balanced against the risk of unnecessary RhIg therapy. New approaches, including non‐invasive fetal RhD genotyping, have been proposed as a means of optimizing the use of RhIg [23].

This case series is limited by the very small sample size, comprising only two cases, which precludes any statistical inference and raises the possibility that the observed findings may reflect coincidental associations rather than a definitive causal relationship. Consequently, the results should be interpreted with caution. Larger prospective cohort studies and randomized controlled trials are required to more accurately estimate the true risk of Rh alloimmunization following early abortion in RhD‐negative women. In recognition of these limitations, we have deliberately adopted cautious, non‐dogmatic language in interpreting our findings.
