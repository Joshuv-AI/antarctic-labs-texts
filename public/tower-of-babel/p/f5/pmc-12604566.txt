Peripartum Management and Labor Stage Duration in Hypertensive Disorders in Pregnancy: A Retrospective Study in a Single Center
Authors: Hao Zhu, Bian Wang, Yi Yu, Rong Hu, Weirong Gu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTHypertensive disorders in pregnancy (HDP) are a major cause of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality. The impact of HDP on labor stage duration and maternal and neonatal outcomes in nulliparous women remains unclear. To assess labor stage duration and maternal and neonatal outcomes in nulliparous women with HDP. A retrospective cohort of 31 472 singleton pregnancies from January 2018 to December 2023 at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University was analyzed using 1:4 propensity score matching (PSM) and inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW). Labor stage durations and maternal and neonatal outcomes were analyzed between women with and without HDP. HDP pregnancies had shorter first and total labor stages but longer second and third stages. The HDP group also had higher oxytocin (OCT) use, reduced fetal distress, intrapartum fever, and increased postpartum hemorrhage. Subgroup analysis by labor onset showed that for spontaneous onset, the HDP group had a significantly shorter first stage and total labor duration, with a significantly longer second and third stage duration. In subgroup analysis by HDP type, among pregnancies with spontaneous onset, those with superimposed preeclampsia on chronic hypertension had the shortest labor duration, followed by gestational hypertension and preeclampsia groups, with chronic hypertension alone showing the longest. Similarly, in the induced labor subgroup, gestational hypertension had the shortest duration, followed by superimposed preeclampsia and preeclampsia, with chronic hypertension again exhibiting the longest. The study indicates HDP affects labor duration, with implications for obstetric policies and childbirth safety.

Introduction

Hypertensive disorders of pregnancy (HDP) comprise a group of complications characterized by elevated blood pressure, including gestational hypertension, preeclampsia/eclampsia, chronic hypertension, and chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia [1]. HDPs are leading causes of maternal and perinatal mortality and morbidity worldwide [2]. Early prevention, timely diagnosis, prompt intervention, and immediate treatment are critical clinical strategies for managing these disorders during pregnancy.

HDP should not be considered an absolute indication for cesarean section [3]. Vaginal delivery may reduce the risk of postpartum hemorrhage and support postpartum recovery. The decision to attempt vaginal delivery should be based on a comprehensive assessment of the maternal condition, fetal well‐being, and the professional judgment of the obstetrician [4]. In cases where a trial of labor is deemed appropriate, continuous and vigilant monitoring of both maternal and fetal status is essential to ensure timely intervention if necessary.

Furthermore, understanding labor progression characteristics in pregnancies complicated by HDP is crucial for effective obstetric management. This knowledge enhances pregnant women's confidence in attempting vaginal delivery. Although some suggest that women with HDP experience a shorter labor duration [5, 6, 7], this remains controversial. Previous studies have not demonstrated clinically significant labor differences in nulliparas with preeclampsia compared to controls [8]. Recent trials indicate that women with chronic HDP may experience longer labor, whereas those with relatively acute forms of the disease may labor for shorter durations [5]. The specific labor characteristics associated with different HDP types remain unclear.

This study aimed to evaluate labor stage duration in pregnancies with HDP, assess perinatal management, and observe maternal and neonatal outcomes. These findings may provide valuable insights for clinical practice, supporting patient autonomy and informed decision‐making. Additionally, they may contribute to reducing cesarean section rates, aligning with evidence‐based obstetric practices that prioritize the safety and well‐being of both mother and child.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This retrospective cohort study included all mothers who delivered vaginally at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Hospital of Fudan University in Shanghai, China, from January 2018 to December 2023. Exclusions were made for patients with twin pregnancies, breech presentations, deliveries before 34 weeks of gestation, stillbirths, congenital fetal anomalies, or incomplete medical records. Maternal demographics included age, gestational age, gravidity, and parity, along with comorbidities such as HDP (including gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, chronic hypertension, chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia, HELLP [hemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, and low platelets] syndrome, and eclampsia), pregnancy with diabetes mellitus (including gestational and pregestational diabetes), thyroid dysfunction, uterine fibroids, history of uterine surgery (including hysteromyomectomy and mediastinal surgery), TOLAC (trial of labor after cesarean section), FGR (fetal growth restriction), placenta previa, ICP (intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy), cervical issues (including cervical incompetence and prior cervical surgery), and IVF‐ET (in vitro fertilization and embryo transfer).

Labor Management

A comprehensive prenatal assessment was performed for each participant, encompassing a review of medical history, prenatal care documentation, physical examination, and an evaluation of maternal and fetal health status. Women were informed about the childbirth process and options for labor analgesia. In this study, the onset of labor was defined by regular uterine contractions occurring at 5–6 min intervals, lasting 30 s or more, accompanied by cervical effacement, cervical dilatation, and fetal descent [9]. If a woman required pregnancy termination but could not go into labor naturally, induction was initiated. Induction methods included Cook balloons or misoprostol for cervical ripening and low‐dose oxytocin (OCT) infusion for labor induction. In cases of premature membrane rupture, if labor did not commence within 2–12 h post‐rupture, low‐dose OCT was administered for induction.

Epidural analgesia was provided based on individual needs during labor. Labor progress was monitored following routine obstetric procedures, with vaginal examinations every 4 h during the latent phase and every 2 h during the active phase. If labor progression was inadequate, artificial rupture of membranes (AROM) and/or OCT infusion was utilized to expedite the process. For women receiving epidural analgesia, the first hour of the second stage could involve delayed pushing, depending on circumstances. After fetal delivery, active management of the third stage was conducted to prevent postpartum hemorrhage. During the first 2 h postpartum, vital signs, uterine contractions, and vaginal bleeding were monitored every 15–30 min. Continued monitoring of vital signs, vaginal bleeding, and uterine contractions was performed throughout the first 24 h postpartum to promptly address any complications.

Since 2017, our institution has implemented safety‐based checklists for perinatal health management in HDP. In this study, we followed standard management protocols for labor stages as outlined by these checklists [10]. Pregnancy termination was considered for women with HDP under the following conditions [11]: (1) for gestational hypertension and preeclampsia without severe clinical features, delivery was anticipated at 37 weeks of gestation; (2) for preeclampsia with severe clinical features after 34 weeks accompanied by serious complications threatening maternal and fetal health, pregnancy termination was recommended; (3) chronic hypertension in a stable condition anticipated delivery at 38 weeks; and (4) for HELLP syndrome, eclampsia, or other severe conditions that posed immediate risks to mother and child, delivery was induced as soon as the maternal condition was stable.

During labor, we closely monitored the vital signs and discomfort complaints of HDP patients and recorded their fluid intake and output. Blood pressure was maintained below 160/110 mmHg, with antihypertensive treatment administered as needed. For HDP patients in active labor, MgSO4 (magnesium sulfate) was given prophylactically. Throughout labor, uterine contractions and fetal heart rate changes were closely monitored, and ergot alkaloid medications were avoided.

According to the ACOG Guidelines 2019 [1], HDP patients were treated with antihypertensive therapy, MgSO4 prophylaxis, or corticosteroids for fetal lung maturity as necessary. Antihypertensive Therapy : In our department and based on the literature [1, 12, 13, 14], oral antihypertensive agents such as labetalol and nifedipine were administered to patients with blood pressure in the range of 150–160/100–110 mmHg. For patients with persistent blood pressure ≥ 160 mmHg systolic or 110 mmHg diastolic, intravenous nicardipine was used. Nifedipine was used as a single dose of 10 mg and was not used more than twice. An infusion of nicardipine to achieve the desired blood pressure level was only used for pregnant women if BP exceeded 160/110 mmHg. MgSO4 Prophylaxis : Patients with preeclampsia with severe features, chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia with severe features, or eclampsia were treated with MgSO4. For preeclampsia without severe features, MgSO4 treatment was determined at the physician's discretion. The dosage involved an intravenous loading dose of 4–6 g over 20–30 min, followed by a maintenance dose of 1–2 g per h. For vaginal deliveries, the infusion continued for 24 h postdelivery. For cesarean deliveries (performed before labor onset), the infusion ideally began before surgery, continued during surgery, and extended for 24 h post‐surgery. Corticosteroids for Fetal Lung Maturity : For women at risk of delivery within the next 7 days at 34+0 weeks or less of gestation, corticosteroids were administered to promote fetal lung maturity. In our department, 5 mg dexamethasone was injected intramuscularly every 12 h over 2 days for these patients.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was labor stage duration, comparing times between HDP and non‐HDP groups. Additionally, labor stage durations were stratified by HDP group and induction methods, with effects of various HDP types subsequently analyzed within each labor subgroup.

Secondary outcomes, including obstetric interventions, maternal, and neonatal outcomes, were compared between groups. Obstetric interventions involved AROM and OCT administration to expedite labor. Maternal and neonatal outcomes included delivery mode, postpartum hemorrhage, intrapartum fever, chorioamnionitis, fetal distress, postpartum fever, and other severe maternal complications such as amniotic fluid embolism, pulmonary embolism, VTE, pulmonary edema, and heart failure. Neonatal outcomes included birth weight, Apgar scores at 1 and 5 min postdelivery, and short‐term adverse outcomes such as asphyxia, acidosis, wet lung, infection or sepsis, uncontrolled hemorrhagic shock, respiratory distress syndrome (RDS), and neonatal mortality.

Statistical Analysis

Data were analyzed using SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) and R software version 4.2.3. Baseline characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics and compared between groups with t‐tests and χ 2 tests. All statistical comparisons were two‐sided, with a p value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. To directly compare outcomes between HDP and non‐HDP groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was used to control for potential confounders. In the PSM process, HDP patients were matched to non‐HDP patients with replacement using a 1:4 ratio, nearest‐neighbor matching, and a caliper width of 0.1.

Three criteria guided variable selection for PSM: (1) baseline differences between groups with a p value less than 0.05; (2) variables considered relevant based on previous studies and clinical considerations; and (3) missing data of less than 10% [18]. The propensity score was calculated using a logistic regression model with predictors including maternal age (continuous), gestational age at delivery (continuous), gravidity (continuous), parity (continuous), gestational diabetes mellitus (gestational vs. pregestational vs. none), thyroid dysfunction (yes vs. no), uterine fibroids (yes vs. no), history of uterine surgery (hysteromyomectomy vs. mediastinal surgery vs. none), TOLAC (yes vs. no), FGR (yes vs. no), placenta previa (yes vs. no), ICP (yes vs. no), cervical issues (yes vs. no), IVF‐ET (yes vs. no), PROM (yes vs. no), and intraspinal labor analgesia (yes vs. no). Standardized mean difference (SMD) assessed the balance of baseline covariates between groups in the matched sample, with an SMD greater than 0.1 indicating a significant imbalance.

To assess the robustness of findings, a sensitivity analysis was performed using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), employing the same covariates as PSM. In IPTW, weights were calculated as the inverse of the propensity scores [19], and the distribution of covariates after weighting was evaluated. As with PSM, an absolute SMD of less than 0.1 was deemed acceptable. Another sensitivity analysis was performed by adding the factors that were used for PSM (age, gestational weeks, number of pregnancies, gestational diabetes, FGR, and cervical issues) as covariates. The sensitivity analysis results were expressed as adjusted mean differences (aMDs), adjusted odds ratios (aORs), and their 95% confidence intervals (95% CI).

Results

Patient Characteristics

A total of 31 971 deliveries were identified, excluding 100 twin births and 42 breech deliveries. Among the remaining 31 829 singleton vaginal deliveries with cephalic presentation, 175 deliveries occurred before 34 + 0 weeks of gestation, 94 cases were stillbirths or had congenital fetal anomalies, and 88 cases had incomplete data. Ultimately, 31 472 deliveries were included in the study (Figure 1). Among these, 2449 women (7.78%) were diagnosed with HDP, including 943 cases of gestational hypertension, 1170 cases of preeclampsia, 209 cases of chronic hypertension, 123 cases of chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia, 3 cases of HELLP syndrome, and 1 case of eclampsia, while 29 023 women did not have HDP.

Labor Duration

In our study, significant differences in labor stage durations were observed between the two groups after PSM and IPTW adjustments (Table 2; sensitivity analyses in Table S1). The first stage of labor was significantly shorter in the HDP group compared to the non‐HDP group (482.46 ± 359.21 min vs. 531.84 ± 351.75 min, p < 0.001). Although the second and third stages were longer in the HDP group (62.28 ± 64.07 min vs. 54.68 ± 52.82 min, 6.63 ± 5.30 min vs. 6.22 ± 4.57 min, respectively, p < 0.001), the total labor duration was shorter in the HDP group than in the non‐HDP group (550.75 ± 361.65 min vs. 592.70 ± 354.72 min, p < 0.001). The mode of labor onset differed significantly between groups, with the non‐HDP group showing a higher rate of spontaneous onset, while the HDP group had a higher rate of labor induction (39.9% vs. 12.1% after PSM, 32.5% vs. 12.4% after IPTW, p < 0.001).

Obstetric Interventions

Table 2 also shows that the HDP group had a higher rate of OCT treatment to strengthen uterine contractions and accelerate labor progression (63.5% vs. 29.6% after PSM, 59.4% vs. 33.7% after IPTW, respectively, p < 0.001). There was no difference in AROM intervention between the groups. Delivery mode did not significantly differ between the two groups. Similar results were observed in the sensitivity analyses after adjustment for confounders (Table S1).

Maternal and Neonatal Outcomes

Women in the HDP group had higher rates of postpartum hemorrhage (4.9% vs. 1.9% after PSM, 4.6% vs. 2.0% after IPTW, p < 0.001) and severe postpartum hemorrhage (1.1% vs. 0.4% after PSM, 1.3% vs. 0.5% after IPTW, p < 0.001). The rate of intrapartum fever was lower in the HDP group (12.3% vs. 13.9%, p = 0.013 after PSM; 12.4% vs. 14.4%, p = 0.023 after IPTW). No significant differences were observed between the groups in other maternal adverse outcomes, including chorioamnionitis, postpartum fever, and other severe complications (Table 2).

The HDP group exhibited a slight decrease in fetal distress incidence (12.3% vs. 13.9%, p = 0.042 after PSM, p = 0.032 after IPTW). There were no differences between groups in neonatal birth weight, 5‐minute Apgar score < 7, neonatal birth depression, wet lung, sepsis, or neonatal death rates. However, discrepancies were found between PSM and IPTW analyses in the 1‐minute Apgar score < 7 rate (p = 0.02 after PSM, p = 0.083 after IPTW), neonatal RDS (p = 0.862 after PSM, p < 0.001 after IPTW), and idiopathic infection rates (p = 0.03 after PSM, p = 0.345 after IPTW) (Table 2).

Discussion

Our study detailed the clinical characteristics of labor in nulliparous women with HDP at over 34 weeks’ gestation: (1) the first and total labor stages were shorter in HDP cases compared to non‐HDP, while the second and third stages were longer in HDP cases; (2) all labor stages were shorter in HDP cases with spontaneous onset, with no significant differences observed in induced labor cases; (3) within the HDP group, those with chronic hypertension complicated by preeclampsia had the shortest labor, while those with isolated chronic hypertension had the longest; (4) women with intrapartum HDP were more likely to require OCT to accelerate labor, with a reduced incidence of fetal distress and intrapartum fever but an increased rate of postpartum hemorrhage; and (5) maternal and neonatal outcomes were favorable under standard labor management in HDP pregnancies.

Pregnancy and labor are inherently linked to inflammatory processes [20, 21, 22]. This inflammatory activation is a critical part of parturition, facilitating the physiological changes required for delivery. Preeclampsia is considered a state of excessive systemic inflammation, with its development believed to follow a two‐stage process [23, 24]. Initially, impaired placentation in early pregnancy leads to placental hypoperfusion, ischemia/hypoxia, and insufficiency. Given that both preeclampsia and labor involve inflammation, it is plausible that preeclampsia could intensify the inflammatory response associated with labor, potentially reducing labor duration. In our study, HDP altered the duration of the first labor stage in women with spontaneous labor onset, likely reflecting the impact of an inflammatory state during pregnancy. Previous studies have reported that women with chronic hypertension or superimposed preeclampsia required more time to progress through the first labor stage, while those with gestational hypertension or preeclampsia progressed more rapidly [5]. Our findings, however, indicate that the first‐stage labor duration was shortest in chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia cases, while it was slightly longer in preeclampsia cases than in gestational hypertension. The longer second and third stages in HDP pregnancies suggest differing mechanisms for labor initiation signals and the labor process [25, 26], warranting further investigation into specific factors influencing labor. Additionally, exploring whether disease chronicity affects the uteroplacental unit's response to labor signals may yield valuable insights.

Standardized labor management is essential for reducing cesarean section rates. During vaginal delivery, all pregnant women were monitored according to clinical diagnostic and treatment protocols, including tracking maternal vital signs, contraction intensity and duration, and fetal heart status to assess labor progress. In clinical practice, heightened attention is typically given to severe cases. HDP poses potential risks to maternal and fetal prognosis, increasing morbidity and mortality risks for both mother and child. Consequently, these women often require more intensive monitoring and proactive measures during delivery. Our study observed that HDP pregnancies received more interventions to accelerate labor and exhibited reduced fetal distress and intrapartum fever incidence, possibly due to active maternal‐fetal management. Additionally, no evidence suggested that MgSO4 prolonged labor duration, though it might be associated with maternal postpartum hemorrhage [27]. The overall rate of postpartum hemorrhage remained low and manageable, attributed to a heightened focus on maternal conditions. Notably, with standardized labor management, neonatal outcomes showed no significant differences between groups. Compared with the normal pregnancy group, HDP pregnancies managed with standardized labor protocols did not exhibit adverse maternal or neonatal outcomes.

HDP affect up to 8% of pregnancies [28]. Our institution, with an average annual childbirth volume of about 10 000, reports an HDP prevalence of 7%–8%. National clinical guidelines were rigorously followed after implementing safety‐based checklists for HDP management in 2017. This initiative improved the quality of clinical strategies and treatments, leading to better maternal and neonatal outcomes. Previous research at our institution showed a reduction in cesarean delivery rates, with no maternal or fetal mortalities reported [10]. In this study, no increase in neonatal adverse outcomes was observed, although some inconsistencies between PSM and IPTW findings suggest areas for further exploration. Among participants, there were three cases of HELLP syndrome and one case of eclampsia, all of which had favorable prognoses due to timely detection and treatment during labor. Managing labor in HDP pregnancies requires close observation, adherence to clinical guidelines, and preparation for emergency interventions. Regular emergency drills are recommended to ensure staff readiness for multidisciplinary team responses [14]. Enhancing clinical guidance on labor management is crucial for improving perinatal care, fostering confidence in vaginal delivery among women with HDP, and reducing cesarean section rates in this high‐risk group.

Strengths

A key strength of this study is its pioneering examination of labor stages in late‐preterm and term HDP cases using contemporary safety‐based checklists. Over 6 years, we collected clinical data on labor characteristics and durations, assessed maternal and neonatal outcomes, and compared labor progression in HDP‐complicated pregnancies to non‐HDP cases. This analysis contributes to improved clinical understanding of labor management, potentially lowering elective cesarean section rates and enhancing maternal and child healthcare quality. Additionally, the study utilized PSM and IPTW, effectively matching baseline characteristics between the two groups in a retrospective design, thus approximating the rigor of a randomized controlled trial. Sensitivity analyses that used the PSM matching factors for adjusting the results in all patients yielded similar results to those after PSM, supporting the validity of the results in the study population.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. Firstly, despite a 6‐year data collection period, it remains a retrospective study conducted at a single center. Secondly, the available data were limited to what was recorded in the medical charts, and the exact details regarding the administration of the Ca antagonists and Mg preparations could not be obtained. Still, such patients normally belonged to the HDP group since patients in the non‐HDP group usually do not receive such treatments. The finding of shorter first and total stages of labor in the HDP group, despite exposure to drugs that inhibit uterine contractions, suggests that the pathophysiology of HDP may exert a stronger prolabor effect that overrides the medications’ effect. Thirdly, labor onset was based on clinical examination by obstetricians or midwives, which may have lacked precision, especially in multiparous patients with rapid labor progression. Lastly, future prospective studies on labor management in HDP pregnancies are needed to provide further insights for clinical practice.

Conclusion

HDP were associated with a shorter first stage of labor duration in women with spontaneous labor onset and varied by HDP type. Women with chronic conditions, such as chronic hypertension, tended to have prolonged labor, while those with more acute or severe features, including chronic hypertension with superimposed preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, and emergency complications, experienced more rapid labor progression. Differences between the mechanisms of the first, second, and third labor stages suggest that future research should focus on labor initiation signals and the labor process. Standardized labor management positively impacts maternal and neonatal outcomes. A comprehensive understanding of labor characteristics, combined with optimized management strategies, can advance obstetric policies and enhance childbirth safety.
