Necroptosis in alveolar epithelium orchestrates lung ischemia-reperfusion injury: a multi-omics study
Authors: Fuxiang Liang, Tong Wang, Zhiling Lou, Jinsheng Li, Huan Yu, Zhijie Xu, Yifan Yu, Xiaolong Hu, Xiaofang Xu, Yongchao Wang, Yaling Chen, Weiping Zhang, Yunbi Lu, Ming Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundLung ischemia-reperfusion injury (LIRI) is a leading cause of early morbidity and mortality following lung transplantation and other cardiopulmonary procedures. It is characterized by acute sterile inflammation driven by regulated cell death (RCD). While various RCD modalities, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, have been implicated in lung injury, their relative contributions and distinct activation patterns in LIRI remain poorly defined.MethodsWe employed an integrated multi-omics approach combining transcriptomics and proteomics with histological and functional validations in a murine hilar clamping model of LIRI. Key findings were further corroborated using single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data from human lung transplant recipients. The functional role of necroptosis was validated using pharmacological inhibitors (Nec-1, GSK’872) and Mlkl-deficient (Mlkl−/−) mice.ResultsLIRI triggered acute, time-dependent lung injury peaking within 24 h of reperfusion. Although transcriptomic profiling suggested broad activation of multiple RCD pathways, proteomic and biochemical analyses revealed a distinct landscape in our experimental setting: markers of apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis were either downregulated or showed no significant positive correlation with injury severity and inflammatory peaks. In contrast, the necroptotic pathway emerged as a highly activated modality. Specifically, necroptosis, marked by phosphorylated RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL, was localized primarily in alveolar epithelial cells, correlated strongly with cytokine release and histological lung injury, and preceded the inflammatory response. Pharmacological inhibition or genetic ablation of necroptosis significantly attenuated tissue damage and inflammation. This pronounced necroptotic signature appeared distinct from the broad multi-pathway activation observed in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced lung injury. Translational analysis of human scRNA-seq data further confirmed the selective upregulation of necroptosis signatures in alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells following lung transplantation.ConclusionOur multi-omics analysis identifies necroptosis, particularly in alveolar epithelial cells, as a critical driver of sterile inflammation and tissue injury in the early phase of LIRI. Targeting alveolar epithelial necroptosis may represent a precise and promising therapeutic strategy for lung transplantation and ischemia-reperfusion-associated pulmonary disorders.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12931-026-03794-0.

Introduction

Lung ischemia-reperfusion injury (LIRI) is a critical pathological process encountered in various clinical contexts, including lung transplantation, cardiopulmonary bypass, and pulmonary embolism. It is characterized by acute pulmonary edema, disruption of the alveolar-capillary barrier, and an intense sterile inflammatory response, often culminating in primary graft dysfunction or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [1, 2]. Despite its clinical significance, the molecular mechanisms linking ischemic insult to widespread inflammation and tissue destruction remain incompletely understood, limiting the development of effective targeted therapies [2].

Recent advances have highlighted the pivotal role of regulated cell death (RCD) in ischemia-reperfusion (IR) injury across multiple organ systems [3, 4]. RCD comprises a spectrum of genetically programmed death pathways, such as apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, each governed by distinct molecular machinery and associated with unique immunological consequences [5–10]. Importantly, the activation profile of specific RCD modes appears to be organ-specific: ferroptosis plays a prominent role in renal IR injury [11], while myocardial IR involves multiple overlapping death programs [12]. However, the relative contribution of these RCD modalities in LIRI, and their specific impact on inflammation and tissue injury, remains to be systematically characterized.

Historically, apoptosis was considered the central mechanism of LIRI-related cell death [13–16]. However, the expanding landscape of RCD has introduced new candidates, particularly necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, that have been sporadically implicated in experimental models [17–19]. This has created a fragmented understanding of LIRI pathogenesis, with isolated studies reporting partial benefits from targeting individual RCD pathways [20–22]. Yet, a comprehensive, comparative analysis of these death modes, and their temporal and spatial dynamics, remains lacking.

Clarifying the hierarchical RCD landscape in LIRI is of both mechanistic and therapeutic importance. Given the immunogenic nature of certain RCD modalities, particularly necroptosis and pyroptosis [9, 10], identifying key driving pathways may enable the development of targeted interventions to limit inflammation, preserve epithelial integrity, and improve LIRI outcomes.

In this study, we employed an integrated multi-omics strategy, combining bulk transcriptomics, quantitative proteomics, histological and functional validation, and single-cell RNA sequencing, to systematically dissect the cell death landscape in LIRI. We demonstrate that necroptosis, predominantly occurring in alveolar epithelial cells, is a critical driver of inflammation and tissue injury following pulmonary IR. These findings provide mechanistic insight into the immunopathogenesis of LIRI and suggest that selective inhibition of necroptosis may offer a promising therapeutic approach in lung transplantation and related IR-associated pulmonary disorders.

Methods

Experimental design and animal models

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–12 weeks old, specific pathogen-free) were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Zhejiang Academy of Medical Sciences (Hangzhou, China). All animals were housed under standard conditions with ad libitum access to food and water. Mice were randomly assigned to sham, ischemia, and ischemia-reperfusion (IR) groups. In the ischemia group, the left pulmonary hilum was occluded for 1 h, and the lung tissues were collected immediately. In the IR group, mice underwent 1 h of ischemia followed by reperfusion for 2 h, 6 h, 1 day, 3 days, or 7 days. Each group included 12 animals: six were used for histological and biochemical analyses, and six for bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis.

Mlkl knockout (Mlkl−/−) mice were kindly provided by Prof. Hai Song (Zhejiang University) [23, 24]. The knockout efficiency was validated by RT-qPCR and Western blotting. Wild-type (WT) and Mlkl−/− mice were subjected to LIRI (1 h ischemia followed by 1 day reperfusion), and lung tissues were collected for histological and biochemical analyses. Six animals were used per group.

Lung ischemia-reperfusion model

Mice were anesthetized with an intraperitoneal injection of sodium pentobarbital (80 mg/kg). After oral endotracheal intubation with a 20 G catheter, mechanical ventilation was performed using a small animal ventilator (RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China) with 10 mL/kg tidal volume, 130–140 breaths/min respiratory rate, I:E ratio 1:1, and 2 cm H2O positive end-expiratory pressure. A left thoracotomy was performed at the third intercostal space to expose the left pulmonary hilum, which was occluded using a non-crushing microvascular clamp. Tidal volume was reduced to 6 mL/kg during ischemia. After 1 h, the clamp was removed to allow reperfusion, and ventilation parameters were restored. The thorax was closed with 5 − 0 polypropylene sutures, and mice were weaned from the ventilator. Body temperature was maintained using a heating pad.

Blood gas analysis

At indicated time points after reperfusion, mice were re-anesthetized, intubated, and ventilated with room air for 5 min. A median sternotomy was performed, and 0.5 mL of arterial blood was collected from the left ventricle using a heparinized syringe. Arterial blood gases (PaO2 and PaCO2) were measured using a blood gas analyzer (ABL80, Radiometer, Copenhagen, Denmark).

Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) analysis

BALF was collected from the left lung after ligating the right hilum via instillation and retrieval of sterile saline. Protein concentration in BALF was determined using a BCA Protein Assay Kit (Cat. No. P0009, Beyotime Bio, China). Levels of myeloperoxidase (MPO), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturers’ instructions (see Table S1, in Additional file 1).

Pathological evaluation

Upper left lung lobes were fixed in 10% formalin for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 4 μm thickness. Hematoxylin and eosin (HE) staining was performed for histopathological assessment. Lung injury scores were evaluated independently by two blinded investigators based on established criteria [25]. Epithelial thickness [26] and perivascular edema [27] were quantified by measuring epithelial layer width and the ratio of perivascular cuff area to vessel area, respectively, using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) and immunofluorescence (IF) staining

Paraffin sections (4 μm) were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with 3% H2O2 to block endogenous peroxidase. Antigen retrieval was performed using sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0) in a microwave. After cooling, sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Table S1), followed by incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. DAB was used for chromogenic detection, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Sections were dehydrated, mounted, and imaged under a microscope (Olympus BX51, Japan). Positive staining was quantified using ImageJ (NIH, USA).

For IF staining, paraffin sections were processed similarly, and triple immunofluorescence labeling was performed using a TSA-based kit (Recordbio, Shanghai, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Co-localization of cell death markers and lung epithelial or endothelial markers was visualized under a fluorescence microscope (Olympus BX51, Japan).

Western blotting

Total protein was extracted from lung tissues and quantified using a BCA assay (Cat. No. P0009, Beyotime Bio, China). Equal amounts of protein (50–70 µg) were separated on 10-12.5% SDS-PAGE gels, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes (Pall Corporation, USA), and blocked with protein-free blocking buffer (Cat. No. PS108, Epizyme Inc., China). Membranes were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies (Table S1), followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Protein bands were visualized using a ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad, Canada) and quantified with Image Lab software. GAPDH served as a loading control.

Quantitative real-time PCR (RT-qPCR) analysis

Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA quality was assessed by NanoDrop, and cDNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit (Takara, Japan). RT-qPCR was performed using SYBR Green Master Mix (Applied Biosystems, USA) on a QuantStudio 6 Flex system (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Relative expression of Tnf, Il6, and Mlkl were normalized to β-actin using the 2−ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences:

Tnf: Forward (5′ to 3′): CCTGTAGCCCACGTCGTAG; Reverse (5′ to 3′): GGGAGTAGACAAGGTACAACCC.

Il6: Forward (5′ to 3′): TAGTCCTTCCTACCCCAATTTCC; Reverse (5′ to 3′): TTGGTCCTTAGCCACTCCTTC.

Mlkl: Forward (5′ to 3′): AACCTGCCCGATGACATTACT; Reverse (5′ to 3′): CTTCTTGCTGAACTTTTCTATCTGC.

β-actin: Forward (5′ to 3′): GGCTGTATTCCCCTCCATCG; Reverse (5′ to 3′): CCAGTTGGTAACAATGCCATGT.

RNA-seq-based transcriptomic profiling and bioinformatics analysis

Total RNA was extracted from lung tissues of sham and LIRI mice (1 h ischemia followed by 1 day reperfusion; n = 3 animals per group) using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA purity and integrity were assessed with a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and Agilent Bioanalyzer 2100. Only samples with RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0 and concentration > 50 ng/µL were used for downstream processing.

Poly(A)+ mRNA was enriched and used to construct strand-specific libraries. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (LC-Bio, Hangzhou, China) to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw data were processed using fastp for quality control. Clean reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38) using HISAT2, followed by transcript assembly and quantification with StringTie.

Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified using edgeR with thresholds of fold change ≥ 2 and adjusted p-value < 0.05 [28]. Functional enrichment analysis, including Gene Ontology (GO), Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG), and gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA), was conducted using the clusterProfiler package [29]. Data visualization was performed using pheatmap and ggplot2.

The RNA-seq dataset has been deposited in the Genome Sequence Archive under accession number CRA034810.

TMT-based quantitative proteomics and bioinformatics analysis

Lung tissue samples from three mice in the sham group and three in the LIRI group (1 h ischemia followed by 1 day reperfusion) were subjected to TMT-based quantitative proteomic analysis (Westlake Omics, Hangzhou, China).

Lung tissue proteins were homogenized, quantified, and digested with trypsin. Peptides were labeled with TMTpro 16plex reagents (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), fractionated using a DIONEX UltiMate 3000 RSLCnano system equipped with an XBridge Peptide BEH C18 column (Waters, USA), and separated into 30 fractions. LC-MS/MS was performed, and raw spectra were analyzed with Proteome Discoverer (v3.0, Thermo Fisher Scientific) against the UniProt Mouse database (16,985 reviewed entries).

Trypsin/P was used as the digestion enzyme with up to two missed cleavages. The precursor and fragment mass tolerances were set to 10 ppm and 0.02 Da, respectively. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was set as a static modification, while methionine oxidation and N-terminal acetylation were considered dynamic modifications. Protein and peptide identifications were filtered at a false discovery rate (FDR) < 1%.

Differentially expressed proteins (DEPs) were defined as those with fold change ≥ 1.2 and p-value ≤ 0.05. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using clusterProfiler [29], and the GSEA was performed on normalized protein expression data to identify enriched proteomic signatures. All proteomics data were deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the iProX partner repository under accession number PXD044899.

Single-cell RNA-seq analysis of human lung transplant samples

Publicly available single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data were obtained from the GEO database (accession: GSE220797) [30]. Raw count matrices were processed using the Seurat package (v5.3.0) in R [31]. After quality control, gene expression data were normalized and scaled. Highly variable genes were used for principal component analysis (PCA), followed by dimensionality reduction with uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) and unsupervised clustering using the Louvain algorithm.

Cell types were annotated based on canonical marker genes identified using the FindAllMarkers function, referencing both the original publication and the PanglaoDB database [32].

Single-cell necroptosis activity was quantified using the UCell package, based on a curated gene set derived from GeneCards by selecting the top-ranked necroptosis-related genes [33]. Redundant or nonspecific genes were manually removed prior to scoring. Enrichment scores were visualized using violin and radar plots via ggplot2. For pathway-level analysis within specific cell types, GSEA was performed using the clusterProfiler package [29]. Results were visualized with GseaVis.

Treatment of necroptosis inhibitors

Mice were randomly divided into four groups: sham, LIRI, LIRI + Nec-1 (Cat. No. 586587, J&K scientific, China) and LIRI + GSK’872 (Cat. No. 5303890001, Millipore Corp, USA) [34–36]. Each group contained 12 animals, six for pathological and biochemical evaluation and the others for BALF analysis. Animals were intraperitoneally injected with Nec-1 (1 mg/kg in 1%DMSO), GSK’872 (0.75 mg/kg in 1%DMSO) or DMSO (1%, LIRI group) 1 h before occlusion of the left lung hilum. Drug doses were chosen according to the published literature [34–36]. One day after the operation, mice were sacrificed. The BALF was used for ELISA assay and lung tissues were subjected to histological and biochemical analyses.

Lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury and the treatment of apoptosis activator

Mice were allocated into five groups: control, lung ischemia-reperfusion injury (LIRI), LPS-induced acute lung injury (LPS, Cat. No. L8880, Solarbio), apoptosis activator Apoptozole (Cat. No. 1054543-47-3, MedChemExpress), and Apoptozole + LIRI. Each group contained three animals. Mice in control, Apoptozole, and LPS groups were administered sterile saline (50 µL per mouse), Apoptozole (4 mg/Kg, 50 µL in sterile saline per mouse), and LPS (5 mg/Kg, 50 µL in sterile saline per mouse) via intratracheal injection, respectively. The animals were then sacrificed for lung tissue sampling 1 day later. Mice in the LIRI and Apoptozole + LIRI groups were pretreated with sterile saline and Apoptozole via intratracheal injection 6 h before the left lung hilum clamping, respectively. Lung tissues were sampled after 1 day of reperfusion.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9 (GraphPad Software, CA, USA). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was assessed using one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test or unpaired Student’s t-test. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated for association analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Ischemia-reperfusion induces acute and time-dependent lung injury and inflammation

To investigate the dynamic changes of lung injury following ischemia-reperfusion, mice were subjected to 1 h of left lung ischemia followed by reperfusion for indicated time (0 h, 2 h, 6 h, 1 day, 3 days, and 7 days). Gross examination revealed progressive lung discoloration and consolidation after reperfusion, which gradually resolved over time, indicating a temporal injury and repair process (Fig. 1A). Histological analysis showed mild alveolar collapse and edema after ischemia alone, while reperfusion exacerbated lung injury, characterized by parenchymal edema and inflammatory cell infiltration, peaking at reperfusion for 6 h to 1 day and partially resolving by day 7 (Fig. 1A). Quantitative assessments of lung injury score, perivascular edema, and epithelial thickness confirmed this pattern (Fig. 1B-D).

Neutrophil infiltration, evaluated by MPO immunohistochemistry, was minimal after ischemia alone but significantly increased during early reperfusion (2 h to 1 day), both in lung sections and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) (Fig. 1A, E, G). In contrast, macrophage recruitment, assessed by F4/80 staining, was delayed, becoming prominent at reperfusion for 1 day and declining thereafter (Fig. 1A, F). Concurrently, IL-6 levels in BALF were markedly elevated during early reperfusion, suggesting a robust pro-inflammatory response (Fig. 1H).

Arterial blood gas analysis demonstrated a transient drop in PaO2 and a rise in PaCO2 between 2 h and 1 day post-reperfusion, indicative of impaired pulmonary function (Fig. 1I-J). Consistent with this, phosphorylated IκBα levels were elevated in lung tissue at early reperfusion stages, reflecting NF-κB pathway activation (Fig. 1K-L).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that ischemia-reperfusion induces acute, time-dependent lung injury and inflammation, with a peak response occurring within the first 24 h of reperfusion.

Transcriptomic profiling reveals activation of multiple regulated cell death pathways in LIRI

To elucidate the transcriptional responses underlying lung ischemia-reperfusion injury (LIRI), we performed bulk RNA sequencing of lung tissues collected 1 day after reperfusion. Principal component analysis demonstrated a clear separation between LIRI and sham groups, indicating substantial transcriptomic differences (Fig. S1A, see Additional file 1). A total of 1,458 differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified between groups (Fig. 2A, Fig. S1B).

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of DEGs revealed significant involvement of inflammation-related processes and regulated cell death pathways, including NF-κB signaling, TNF production, apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis (Fig. 2B-C, Fig. S1C). To further validate these findings, gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) was performed, which confirmed transcriptional upregulation of multiple cell death programs, including intrinsic apoptosis, pyroptosis, ferroptosis, and TNF production signaling (Fig. 2D-G).

To visualize representative gene-level changes, we generated heatmaps of hallmark genes within each cell death pathway. Compared with sham controls, LIRI lungs showed increased expression of pro-apoptotic genes (e.g., Casp3, Bax, Apaf1), pyroptosis-related genes (e.g., Il1b, Nlrp3, Pycard), ferroptosis mediators (e.g., Hmox1, Fth1, Slc7a11), and necroptosis effectors (e.g., Ripk3, Mlkl, Tnf) (Fig. 2H-K).

Collectively, these transcriptomic data indicate that LIRI induces a robust inflammatory response accompanied by coordinated activation of multiple regulated cell death pathways.

Proteomic profiling reveals significant enrichment of necroptosis pathway in LIRI

To determine whether these transcriptional changes translate to the protein level, we conducted TMT-based quantitative proteomic analysis. A total of 7,494 proteins were identified, among which 2,062 were differentially expressed between LIRI and sham lungs (Fig. 3A, Fig. S2A-B). Principal component analysis confirmed distinct proteomic profiles between groups.

GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses of DEPs revealed different modes of cell death, including apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis, were involved in ischemia-reperfusion injury (Fig. 3B-C). In addition, pathways related to leukocyte chemotaxis, NF-κB signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, and tissue regeneration were enriched in LIRI lungs (Fig. 3C, Fig. S2C).

GSEA results further showed that ischemia-reperfusion significantly upregulated TNF production, inflammatory responses, iron transport, and downregulated pyroptosis, but no difference was found in lipid oxidation and apoptosis between the two groups (Fig. 3D-G, Fig. S2C). These findings suggest a complex but selective regulation of cell death programs at the protein level. According to the expression levels of cell death-related proteins, most apoptosis- and pyroptosis-related proteins were downregulated after ischemia-reperfusion, while some key proteins of necroptosis were upregulated (Fig. 3H-K).

These findings suggest that although multiple death pathways are transcriptionally activated, the necroptosis pathway is preferentially executed at the protein level during the early phase of LIRI.

Necroptosis is the primary RCD pathway activated in the early phase of lung ischemia-reperfusion injury

To validate the multi-omics findings and determine the dominant form of cell death in LIRI, we systematically assessed key molecular markers of apoptosis, necroptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis using immunoblotting and immunofluorescence.

Expression of canonical apoptosis-related proteins revealed a suppressed apoptotic response, especially in the early stage of reperfusion. Bax, a pro-apoptotic Bcl-2 family member, exhibited a downward trend following ischemia-reperfusion, while anti-apoptotic proteins Bcl-2 and Bcl-xL were upregulated (Fig. 4A-B). Notably, the Bcl-2/Bax ratio, reflecting cellular resistance to apoptosis, was significantly increased during the early stage of reperfusion (Fig. 4B). Additionally, cleaved caspase-3 and cleaved caspase-8 levels, executors of apoptotic cell death, remained unchanged, further indicating limited apoptotic activity. Immunofluorescence further revealed that the number of cleaved caspase-3-positive cells did not differ significantly from sham controls (Fig. 4C).

In contrast, activation of the necroptotic pathway was prominent and time-dependent. Phosphorylated RIPK1 (p-RIPK1), RIPK3 (p-RIPK3), and MLKL (p-MLKL), central mediators of necroptosis, were markedly increased within the first 6 h after reperfusion and declined gradually thereafter (Fig. 4A-B). Consistently, levels of HMGB1 in BALF, a marker of necrotic cell death [37, 38], mirrored the expression of p-MLKL (Fig. 4D). Immunofluorescence further revealed that p-MLKL was predominantly localized to alveolar and bronchial epithelial cells, identifying these cells as the primary targets of necroptosis (Fig. 4E).

To evaluate pyroptosis, we examined inflammasome-related proteins. Although NLRP3 was moderately upregulated, the levels of mature IL-1β, cleaved caspase-1, and N-GSDMD were significantly decreased following reperfusion, suggesting that pyroptotic signaling was not effectively engaged (Fig. S3A-B). Immunofluorescence staining also revealed no significant increase in GSDMD expression in lung tissues (Fig. S3C-E), further supporting the absence of pyroptosis activation in the lung.

We next assessed ferroptosis-associated pathways. Ischemia-reperfusion led to increased expression of ferroportin (SLC40A1) and ferritin (FTH) in the lung, suggesting an adaptive response to iron handling. However, ACSL4, an enzyme that promotes lipid peroxidation [7, 39], was significantly downregulated, and GPX4, a key antioxidant enzyme that suppresses ferroptosis [40], showed a tendency toward upregulation (Fig. S4A-B). Although FTH and GPX4 were predominantly expressed in epithelial cells, their expression patterns suggest that, despite evident iron overload and oxidative stress, the conditions were insufficient to trigger extensive lipid peroxidation or ferroptotic cell death (Fig. S4C-F).

Together, these data identify necroptosis as a critical driver of regulated cell death in our LIRI model, exhibiting a distinct activation pattern compared to apoptosis, pyroptosis, or ferroptosis.

Necroptosis correlates with inflammation and contributes to lung injury in LIRI

To investigate the relationship between regulated cell death pathways and inflammation in LIRI, we performed correlation analyses using representative molecular markers: p-MLKL for necroptosis, cleaved caspase-3 for apoptosis, N-GSDMD for pyroptosis, and ACSL4 for ferroptosis. Inflammatory responses were evaluated by neutrophil infiltration (MPO⁺ cells), NF-κB activation (p-IκBα), and NLRP3 expression. Pearson’s correlation analysis revealed that necroptosis, as indicated by p-MLKL levels, positively correlated with neutrophil infiltration (R = 0.46, P = 0.002), NF-κB pathway activation (R = 0.52, P < 0.001), NLRP3 expression (R = 0.40, P = 0.009), and histological lung injury scores (R = 0.45, P = 0.003) (Fig. 5A). In contrast, apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis markers showed either weak or negative associations with these inflammatory parameters (Fig. S5A-D), suggesting a limited contribution of non-necroptotic cell death to LIRI-associated inflammation.

Time-course analysis further supported a temporal link between necroptosis and inflammation. Among all cell death types, only necroptosis showed a marked and early increase, peaking at 2 h after reperfusion (Fig. 5B). This rise preceded or coincided with increases in neutrophil infiltration, NLRP3 expression, NF-κB activation, and lung injury severity, which peaked between 6 h and 1 day post-reperfusion (Fig. 5C). These findings indicate that necroptosis may act upstream of inflammatory amplification in LIRI.

To functionally evaluate the role of necroptosis, we pharmacologically inhibited RIPK1 and RIPK3 using Nec-1 and GSK’872, respectively. Histological analysis revealed that both inhibitors significantly alleviated lung tissue injury and reduced neutrophil infiltration compared to the untreated LIRI group (Fig. 5D-E). These protective effects were further supported by decreased levels of MPO and HMGB1 in BALF (Fig. 5F), and reduced expression of inflammatory cytokines Il6 and Tnf in lung tissue (Fig. 5G). Mechanistically, necroptosis inhibition suppressed the upregulation of p-MLKL and attenuated NF-κB pathway activation (Fig. 5H-I).

Collectively, these findings indicate that necroptosis not only precedes but also contributes to inflammation and tissue damage in LIRI.

Histological analysis revealed that, following LIRI, Mlkl−/− mice exhibited significantly less lung tissue damage and neutrophil infiltration than did LIRI-subjected wild-type (WT) controls, as demonstrated by HE staining and MPO immunohistochemistry (Fig. 6B-C). Consistent with these findings, mRNA levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines Il6 and Tnf were markedly lower in Mlkl−/− mice after LIRI (Fig. 6D). Together, these results indicate that MLKL deficiency attenuates the inflammatory response to LIRI.

Notably, this protective effect was accompanied by a marked attenuation of LIRI-induced activation of the upstream necroptotic mediators, RIPK1 and RIPK3. In parallel, the LIRI-driven upregulation of key inflammatory markers, such as p-IκBα and HMGB1, was also suppressed in the lungs of Mlkl−/− mice (Fig. 6E-F).

However, MLKL deficiency had no significant effect on the protein levels of key markers for pyroptosis (N-GSDMD) or ferroptosis (ACSL4, GPX4) after LIRI induction. Conversely, cleaved caspase-3 levels were increased in Mlkl−/− lungs, implying a compensatory activation of apoptosis in the absence of necroptosis (Fig. 6E-F).

Altogether, our results demonstrate that genetic deletion of MLKL protects against LIRI by inhibiting necroptosis and its downstream inflammatory cascades.

LIRI exhibits a distinct cell death profile characterized by necroptosis compared to LPS-induced lung injury

To determine whether necroptosis is a defining feature of the early phase of LIRI, we compared its cell death signaling profile with that of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced acute lung injury (ALI). Histological analysis showed that LPS (5 mg/kg) caused more prominent lung damage and neutrophil infiltration than did the LIRI protocol (ischemia 1 h / reperfusion 1 day) (Fig. S6A-C). Western blotting revealed that LPS markedly upregulated apoptosis markers (cleaved caspase-3, Bax), pyroptosis markers (NLRP3, N-GSDMD), and the ferroptosis marker (ACSL4), yet failed to induce the necroptosis marker (p-MLKL) (Fig. S6D-E). Conversely, LIRI selectively engaged the necroptotic pathway without significantly triggering other cell death pathways (Fig. S6D-E).

To test whether increasing apoptosis could influence necroptosis, we treated mice with Apoptozole, a small-molecule apoptosis activator. While Apoptozole increased the levels of the apoptotic markers cleaved caspase-3 and Bax, it failed to exacerbate lung injury or neutrophil infiltration. Notably, it did not increase p-MLKL levels under basal or ischemia-reperfusion conditions (Fig. S6A-E). These data indicate that inducing apoptosis is insufficient to alter the dominant necroptotic pathway driving LIRI.

Together, these findings suggest that necroptosis is a distinguishing feature of the early phase of LIRI, contrasting with the broad-spectrum cell death activation observed in LPS-induced injury.

Discussion

In this study, we systematically profiled the landscape of regulated cell death (RCD) in lung ischemia-reperfusion injury (LIRI) using a multi-dimensional approach encompassing bulk transcriptomics, proteomics, histopathology, functional validation, and single-cell transcriptomics. Among multiple RCD modalities, we identified necroptosis, particularly in alveolar epithelial cells, as a critical and pathogenic mechanism that initiates pulmonary inflammation and tissue damage. Both pharmacological inhibition and genetic ablation of necroptosis significantly reduced lung injury, neutrophil infiltration, and pro-inflammatory cytokine release, highlighting necroptosis as a key therapeutic target in LIRI.

While apoptosis has long been considered a central mechanism in IR-induced tissue injury [13–16], our findings suggest that it plays a minimal role during the early phase of LIRI. Although apoptotic markers were detected in epithelial cells, their levels were reduced at the whole-lung level during early reperfusion, possibly due to high basal turnover of alveolar epithelial cells or delayed apoptosis in infiltrating immune populations [22, 41–43]. Moreover, apoptosis did not correlate with inflammation or tissue injury severity, and pharmacological activation of apoptosis failed to exacerbate lung damage. These observations collectively indicate that apoptosis is not a primary driver of LIRI pathogenesis.

In contrast, necroptosis, characterized by phosphorylation of RIPK1, RIPK3, and MLKL [44], was robustly activated and strongly associated with tissue injury and inflammatory activation. Inhibition of necroptosis, either pharmacologically or genetically, significantly reduced DAMP release (HMGB1), neutrophil infiltration, and activation of downstream inflammatory pathways such as NF-κB. These findings suggest that necroptosis acts as a major amplifier of sterile inflammation [17, 22, 45]. Interestingly, genetic ablation of necroptosis resulted in a compensatory increase in cleaved caspase-3, yet paradoxically conferred protection rather than exacerbation of injury. This highlights the distinct immunological consequences of different RCD programs: necroptosis causes plasma membrane rupture and DAMP release, fueling a cytokine storm, whereas apoptosis maintains membrane integrity and is largely immunologically silent [7]. Therefore, reprogramming cell death from necroptosis to apoptosis may represent a novel strategy to reduce immunogenicity and preserve lung function.

The pathogenesis of LIRI is complex, involving multiple regulated cell death (RCD) pathways that may function in a context-dependent manner. Recent studies have demonstrated the involvement of ferroptosis and pyroptosis in LIRI. For instance, ferroptosis has been identified as a critical driver of oxidative injury during the early reperfusion phase (e.g., 2 h) [46, 47], while pyroptosis has been shown to mediate inflammatory responses, particularly in pulmonary endothelial cells or within specific experimental models (e.g., rat models) [19, 48, 49]. These findings provide valuable insights into the vascular and acute oxidative components of the injury. Complementing these studies, our multi-omics analysis and functional validation reveal that necroptosis is a prominent RCD modality specifically in the alveolar epithelium (AT2 cells). This distinction is crucial because AT2 cells fulfill a different physiological role than endothelial cells. Thus, we propose that LIRI acts as a spatially and temporally heterogeneous process, wherein distinct RCD modalities, including ferroptosis, pyroptosis, and necroptosis, may dominate within specific cell types or injury phases, underscoring the potential necessity of multi-target therapeutic strategies.

Our study expands the traditional view of LIRI pathogenesis. Beyond the well-established role of endothelial dysfunction [1, 2, 50], our integrated single-cell transcriptomic and spatial analysis highlights that alveolar epithelial cells (AECs) serve as critical, early drivers of injury. This specific vulnerability of AECs is likely attributable to their unique anatomical position, which subjects them to direct and rapid fluctuations in oxygen tension during ischemia and reperfusion. Consequently, AECs act as the initial sentinels of oxidative stress. Our data demonstrate that rather than being passive victims, these epithelial cells function as active immune sensors, initiating the sterile inflammatory cascade via the RIPK1/RIPK3/MLKL axis.

Importantly, our comparative analysis with LPS-induced acute lung injury revealed fundamental differences in RCD profiles. While LIRI is characterized by robust necroptosis activation, LPS-induced injury engages a broader spectrum of death pathways, including apoptosis, pyroptosis, and ferroptosis [51–53]. This suggests that necroptosis is not a generic response to lung injury but a context-specific outcome of ischemia-reperfusion stress, likely driven by ATP depletion, mitochondrial dysfunction, and metabolic collapse [54, 55].

We also evaluated the potential translational relevance of our findings. Analysis of publicly available single-cell RNA-seq data from human lung transplant recipients indicated a selective upregulation of necroptosis-related genes in alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells [30]. These transcriptomic signatures align with our murine findings, suggesting a conserved vulnerability of the alveolar epithelium to reperfusion stress across species. Notably, in our murine model, necroptosis peaked within 2–6 h of reperfusion, indicating a narrow but actionable therapeutic window. This suggests that interventions targeting necroptosis, administered at reperfusion or incorporated into preservation protocols, could substantially reduce lung injury in transplant settings.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, while our murine model utilized warm ischemia, the human validation data (Fig. 7) were derived from lung transplantation involving cold ischemia and preservation injury. Although necroptotic signatures were consistent across settings, the distinct metabolic stressors of cold storage may differentially influence cell death pathways [56]. Given the clinical heterogeneity inherent in human transplantation (e.g., varying cold ischemia times, donor demographics), future studies utilizing large, independent clinical cohorts with rigorous protein-level validation are necessary to definitively establish the prevalence and clinical correlation of necroptosis in human LIRI. Second, while our study highlights AT2 cells as the primary drivers of necroptosis in LIRI, the involvement of immune cells warrants further attention. Notably, while macrophage necroptosis has been reported in other lung pathologies, infiltrating neutrophils are classically associated with NETosis rather than necroptosis [57–62]. The crosstalk between epithelial necroptosis and these distinct immune cell death modalities represents a critical avenue for future investigation. Third, the upstream triggers of necroptosis in alveolar epithelial cells remain undefined. Potential factors such as oxidative stress, calcium overload, and mechanical stretch warrant further investigation [63–65]. Finally, although we observed crosstalk between RCD pathways, including compensatory apoptosis and inflammasome activation, a comprehensive temporal and spatial mapping of these interactions was beyond the scope of this study [66].

Conclusions

In summary, our study identifies necroptosis as a prominent driver of regulated cell death in LIRI, occurring primarily in alveolar epithelial cells and playing a central role in driving sterile inflammation and tissue injury. Targeting necroptotic signaling offers a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating LIRI in clinical settings, particularly in lung transplantation.
