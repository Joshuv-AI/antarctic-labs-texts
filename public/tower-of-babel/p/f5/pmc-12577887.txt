A non-surgical approach to colonic polyposis syndrome: a case of early detection and endoscopic intervention to prevent malignancy
Authors: Muhammad Husnain Ahmad, Masab Ali, Ali Gohar, Asad Ullah Mahmood, Ghias Un Nabi Tayyab, Abdul Rehman, Bilal Ahmed, Ch Hummd Abbas
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and Importance:Colonic polyposis syndrome, characterized by multiple colorectal adenomatous polyps, presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. This case emphasizes the importance of early detection, histopathological evaluation, and endoscopic management in colonic polyposis in a young patient.Case Presentation:We report the case of a 45-year-old male who presented with chronic diarrhea, generalized weakness, and severe anemia. Initial investigations revealed a hemoglobin level of 4.2 g/dL and a positive stool occult blood test, suggestive of gastrointestinal blood loss. Upper gastrointestinal endoscopy shows no active bleeding, while colonoscopy identified multiple pedunculated polyps extending from the distal sigmoid colon to the right colon. Histopathology confirmed tubulovillous adenomas with focal high-grade dysplasia, but no invasive carcinoma. Management involved serial surveillance colonoscopies with polypectomies initially at 2-month intervals, resulting in the resolution of anemia and symptomatic improvement, with hemoglobin increasing to 11.0 g/dL. Surveillance colonoscopy with polypectomy relieved symptoms, resulting in the avoidance of major destructive surgery. Genetic testing could not be performed due to financial constraints.Clinical Discussion:The patient showed marked symptomatic and hematological improvement with endoscopic management alone, avoiding colectomy. This highlights the importance of early detection and regular surveillance in resource-limited settings.Conclusion:This case highlights the need for accessible screening and ongoing endoscopic evaluation to prevent adenomatous polyps from progressing to colorectal cancer.

Introduction

A polyp is characterized as any mass protruding into the lumen of a hollow viscus. Based on their macroscopic appearance, colorectal polyps can be characterized as sessile (flat, emerging directly from the mucosal layer) or pedunculated (extending through a fibro-vascular stalk)[1]. Notably, the frequency of having an adenoma increases with age, affecting 12% of women and 24% of men under 50, and 27% of women and 40% of men over 80 years of age[2,3]. Adenomatous polyps are further classified as tubular, villous, or tubulo-villous subtypes by their histological appearance. Tubulo-villous adenomas have elements of both of the cellular patterns that were previously mentioned. Approximately 10–25% of polyps are tubulo-villous, exhibiting an intermediate risk of carcinoma (22%)[4,5]. A positive fecal occult blood test (FOBT) due to bleeding from a polyp correlates with the polyp size and its proximity to the rectum. Despite its poor sensitivity, the FOBT is a part of the screening algorithm for early detection of colon cancer[1,6]. Colonoscopy is regarded as the “gold standard” method of observing intra-luminal colonic lesions. However, its sensitivity is not 100% as several studies have demonstrated a variable “missed” polyp rate[7,8]. Our case report features that surveillance colonoscopy with polypectomy can provide favorable outcomes rather than colectomy. This case report was reported in line with the SCARE guidelines[9].

Case presentation

A 45-year-old male with no known comorbidities presented to the outpatient department with complaints of chronic loose stools for the past 3 months. He also reported poor appetite, generalized body weakness, and exertional shortness of breath. There was no history of fever, weight loss, abdominal pain, or overt gastrointestinal bleeding. On examination, he appeared pale, but no lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly was noted. Cardiovascular and respiratory examinations were unremarkable.

Laboratory investigations (see Table 1) revealed a hemoglobin level of 4.2 g/dL, consistent with severe anemia, with a mean corpuscular volume of 80 fL, indicating normocytic anemia. The stool occult blood test was positive, indicating the likelihood of gastrointestinal blood loss. To identify the source of bleeding, an upper gastrointestinal endoscopy was performed, which revealed mild non-erosive reflux esophagitis and mild duodenitis without any active bleeding. A flexible colonoscopy was subsequently performed, which demonstrated multiple pedunculated polyps (see Fig. 1) of varying sizes extending from the distal sigmoid colon to the right colon, with the rectal mucosa appearing normal and showing an intact vascular pattern. Histopathological examination of the polyps revealed mixed tubulovillous adenomas with scattered foci of high-grade dysplasia. No evidence of invasive carcinoma was detected. Based on these findings, a provisional diagnosis of colonic polyposis syndrome was made. The patient was placed on a regimen of surveillance colonoscopies with polypectomies every 2 months.

After three cycles of colonoscopies and polypectomy, his hemoglobin level improved to 11.0 g/dL, and his symptoms of chronic diarrhea and weakness resolved. Family history was negative for gastrointestinal malignancies or polyposis syndromes. However, genetic testing to rule out familial adenomatous polyposis could not be performed due to financial constraints.

Discussion

Adenomatous or neoplastic colorectal polyps are benign growths that develop from the mucus-producing epithelial cells lining the colon[1]. Adenomatous polyps are histologically categorized into three subtypes: tubular adenomas, villous adenomas, and tubulovillous adenomas. Tubulo-villous adenomas have constituents of both tubular adenomas and villous adenomas[1]. A hallmark of all adenomas is the presence of dysplasia, which involves changes in glandular structure and damage to intercellular components. Adenomas can be found anywhere throughout the colon, with large adenomas found in a distribution similar to that of carcinoma, with left colonic predominance[3].

Screening programs for colonic adenoma detection, as in our case, frequently employ FOBTs, which offer higher sensitivity for detecting larger polyps and carcinomas, though with limitations in identifying smaller polyps. Fecal immunochemical testing (FIT) is a more sensitive screening method compared to FOBT, which is a limitation in our case report, highlighting the fact that both FOBT and fecal immunochemical test should be used for the detection of polyps so that polyps of almost any size can be identified[1,5]. The gold standard for the detection of colonic lesions is colonoscopy, done in our case, signifying the fact that provisional diagnosis of colonic polyposis can be made with colonoscopy. The quality of screening colonoscopy is directly linked to the successful identification and removal of adenomas, which in turn decreases the risk of developing colorectal cancer, highlighting the need to do colonoscopy at regular intervals[2]. Colonoscopy, when compared with the fecal immunochemical test, has an increased likelihood of identifying adenomas, as reported by Quintero et al[7].

A key feature of this case was the resolution of severe anemia through a non-surgical approach involving polypectomy and serial colonoscopies. This underscores the pivotal role of endoscopic management in addressing both anemia and the potential malignant transformation of adenomatous polyps. Cohort studies have demonstrated that polypectomy reduces the risk of colorectal cancer by approximately 80%, reinforcing its importance in preventing malignancy in patients with adenomas[6,7].

Endoscopic surveillance not only obviated the need for colectomy—a procedure associated with significant risks such as autonomic nerve injury, sexual dysfunction, and infertility—but also minimized complications commonly associated with polypectomy, such as bleeding, perforation, and sepsis. Remarkably, our patient experienced no such complications, further emphasizing the safety and efficacy of this approach[1,3]. These findings align with studies emphasizing the benefits of endoscopic surveillance in managing colonic polyposis syndromes[6,8].

Another significant aspect of our case is the importance of regular colonoscopy in detecting and managing colonic polyps, especially in settings where genetic testing is inaccessible due to affordability constraints. While genetic analysis can provide valuable insights and guide long-term management, our case illustrates that vigilant clinical follow-up and timely interventions can yield favorable outcomes even in the absence of advanced molecular diagnostics. Studies from tertiary care centers in South India have similarly emphasized the efficacy of systematic clinical management in resource-constrained settings[6].

Serial polypectomy followed by surveillance colonoscopy, after a 3-year interval for adenomas with high-grade dysplasia and a 1-year interval for cases with more than 10 completely removed adenomas[10], not only improves patient outcomes but also highlights the effectiveness of a non-surgical approach, as demonstrated in our case. Patients with colonic polyps remain at elevated risk for advanced neoplasia due to factors such as missed lesions, incomplete removal, or an inherent predisposition to neoplasia[11].

Surveillance colonoscopy is therefore warranted to identify missed or newly developed lesions. While no randomized trials have directly confirmed its impact on Colorectal Carcinoma (CRC) incidence or mortality, retrospective studies indicate a 3–4-fold increased risk of CRC in patients who do not undergo surveillance—particularly those with advanced adenomas. In contrast, individuals with non-advanced adenomas have a risk similar to that of the general population[11].

Colonic polyposis syndrome poses unique challenges, particularly in the absence of a family history and limited access to genetic testing. Our case illustrates the critical role of early initial detection and endoscopic management in preventing the progression of adenomatous polyps to colorectal cancer. These findings reinforce the need for regular colonoscopic evaluation in patients with colonic polyposis and highlight the importance of initiating surveillance colonoscopy at the age of 40 in first-degree relatives of affected patients[5,8]. FIT is more sensitive than FOBT; however, its unavailability in our case is a limitation.

Conclusions

Colonic polyposis syndrome poses unique challenges in diagnosis and management, particularly in the absence of a family history and limited access to genetic testing. This case underscores the importance of early detection and endoscopic management of adenomatous polyps in a young patient to prevent progression to colorectal cancer. Clinical status of the patient improved through serial polypectomy and surveillance colonoscopy highlights the effectiveness of a non-surgical approach. This case reinforces the need for ongoing vigilance and regular colonoscopic evaluation in patients with colonic polyposis, ensuring timely intervention and reducing the risk of malignancy and the need for surveillance colonoscopy at the age of 40 years in the first-degree relatives of the patient.
