The dynamic and diverse experience of adults with brachial plexus birth injury: a collective case study
Authors: Jenny M. Dorich, Jordyn Whiting, Vicki L. Plano Clark, Richard F. Ittenbach, Roger Cornwall
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPurposeTo explore brachial plexus birth injury (BPBI)-affected adults’ health-related quality of life (HRQoL) experiences informed by the theoretical framework of the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health.MethodsThis study applied a qualitative collective case study design. Twelve participants who participated in a prior survey study were recruited using maximum variation sampling. One-on-one semi-structured interviews focused on gaining in-depth understanding of participants’ experiences with HRQoL. Interviews were recorded verbatim and thematically analysed.ResultsWe identified two themes. First, the theme biopsychosocial dimensions of the experience comprised the ways BPBI affects physical and emotional health and how BPBI is intertwined with affected individuals’ identities, activities/participation, and social environments. Second, the theme lifelong and variable experience encompassed how BPBI is a chronic condition that changes over time and varies among affected individuals. The cross-case analysis derived a conceptual model of BPBI HRQoL to describe the BPBI experience through the lifespan. In this model, multiple dimensions of BPBI HRQoL expand through the lifespan while BPBI-specific health resources’ availability contract.ConclusionsBy providing insight into the many ways that BPBI affects and is affected by an individual’s functioning and personal, social, and healthcare environmental factors, these findings underscore lifelong individualized care for BPBI-affected persons is needed.

Introduction

Brachial plexus birth injury (BPBI) is a leading cause of childhood paralysis, occurring in 1–3 of 1000 live births (Foad et al., 2008). BPBI results from birth trauma and is characterized by upper limb paralysis. Full recovery can occur within the first several months of life; however, at least 30% of the children experience incomplete recovery (Foad et al., 2009; Pondaag et al., 2004). A variety of musculoskeletal complications, such as persistent weakness, joint impairments, and altered limb growth, are characteristic upper limb sequelae associated with incomplete recovery (Kozin, 2011). During childhood, therapeutic and surgical interventions are commonly applied to minimize a child’s musculoskeletal impairment, yet full resolution of sequelae is rare (Hale et al., 2010).

Despite awareness of long-term BPBI-related musculoskeletal impairments, knowledge of BPBI’s effects on individuals’ long-term health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is not fully understood. Early small regional studies examining the HRQoL outcomes of BPBI-affected adults revealed disparate HRQoL outcomes based upon PROMs chosen by clinicians (Butler et al., 2017; de Heer et al., 2015; Kirkos et al., 2005; Patridge & Susan, 2004; Ploetze et al., 2020; van der Holst et al., 2018) or non-standardized survey questions (Ploetze et al., 2020). In our prior BPBI Survey Study (Dorich et al., 2023) we found diversity in HRQoL among a large sample of BPBI-affected adults who we recruited by posting a survey link on two international BPBI support groups’ social media sites. Most participants reported that BPBI affects functional hand and arm use, activity participation, life roles, quality of life, and overall health. Open-ended responses provided broad insights into the responses, such as the types of health concerns attributed to BPBI and the life roles impacted. Although prior research has consistently reported a diversity of experiences in the studied samples, research describing the complexity of BPBI- affected adults’ individual experiences is lacking. To fully understand long-term HRQoL outcomes for this population, exploration and description of individual experiences is needed. Therefore, in the current qualitative study, we apply a collective case study approach with a diverse sample of affected adults recruited from the BPBI Survey Study to elucidate the complexity of BPBI HRQoL through the lifespan to inform affected individuals, their families, and medical professionals.

Theoretical underpinnings

The World Health Organization (WHO, 2002) established the International Classification of Functioning, Disability, and Health (ICF) as a universal classification structure for understanding the human experience of health as a continuum of functioning and disability. The ICF considers a biopsychosocial model of health including both medical and social components of an individual’s functioning (Cieza et al., 2008; WHO, 2002). As depicted in Figure 1, the bodily components (e.g., Body Functions and Body Structures) and the health-related domains of functioning (e.g., Activities and Participation) and the interaction between them influence an individual’s experience of functioning and disability. The ICF model also encompasses an individual’s unique context, including their personal attributes (e.g., Personal Factors) and the environments in which they participate (e.g., Environmental Factors). By extension, an individual’s context is expected to impact one’s functioning/disability continuum and vice versa. Similarly, a reciprocal relationship exists between an individual’s health condition and the components of their functioning/disability continuum. Taken together, the interactions of the health condition, functioning/disability continuum, and the contextual factors create an individual’s HRQoL experience. It has been proposed that while one’s activities, participation, and social environments are often not considered to be part of an individual’s general level of health, many consider them to be so closely aligned with one’s health that failing to include them in a comprehensive view of determinants could result in an incomplete picture of key components (Cieza et al., 2008). Thus, we extend Cieza et al. (2008)’s definition of HRQoL as “the individual’s perceptions of how life is going in health and health-related domains” (p. e48) to include the individual’s context. We assume that an individual’s personal experience of HRQoL results from the dynamic interplay of one’s health, their functioning/disability continuum, and any related contextual factors. We applied this perspective of an individual’s experience of BPBI in the context of the ICF as we described and compared the performance of BPBI-affected adults to generate knowledge of BPBI HRQoL.

Our approach is also grounded in pragmatic assumptions. According to Morgan (2013), individuals have unique experiences that evolve from their past experiences and the beliefs that arise from those experiences. Meaning is interlaced with the human experience and dependent upon the context of the experience (Dillon et al., 2000). Therefore, our collective case study approach focused on describing and interpreting the complexity of BPBI-affected individuals’ experiences to derive a comprehensive multidimensional perspective of health-related phenomena across the lifespan.

Methods

Study design

All study procedures were reviewed and approved by the institutional review board of a large, Midwestern, tertiary-care and paediatric medical centre. The current study is part of a larger programme of inquiry globally examining HRoL outcomes in BPBI-affected adults. Participants in the current qualitative study were recruited from participation in a previous survey study focused on describing broad trends of HRQoL in BPBI-affected adults. The current study was conducted to obtain greater depth about affected persons’ HRQoL experiences. Thus, in the current study, we considered each participating BPBI-affected adult as a case and performed a collective case study analysis of their unique experiences (Stake, 2013). Collective case studies include multiple cases and are generally recognized as an effective approach for examining complex social phenomena (Creswell & Poth, 2018; Stake, 1995) and providing comprehensive multidimensional perspectives of health-related phenomena (Crowe et al., 2011).

Research Team

Our multidisciplinary research team has varied experience with BPBI and the methodology used here. Three authors have direct experience with BPBI. JD is an occupational therapist who provides care to BPBI-affected children and a clinical researcher in rehabilitation sciences. JW has BPBI and is a nurse, a board member of the United Brachial Plexus Network, and the co-founder of the Adults with BPBI Facebook group. RC is a paediatric BPBI surgeon, a founder of Plexus Nexus (i.e., an international consortium of brachial plexus experts), and the director of a research laboratory investigating BPBI-related joint contractures. Our other research team members were introduced to BPBI through this research. They contribute expertise in qualitative and mixed methods research VPC and in biostatistical methods RI to the entire research project. Additionally, JD and RC have experience with the ICF framework in prior work (Dorich et al., 2023). Throughout the entire research project, we met routinely as subgroups and as a full team to share updates and discuss study methods procedures, data analyses, and interpretation.

Participants

Participants in the current qualitative study were recruited from a sample of 183 participants in the prior BPBI Survey Study, 80% (n = 144) of whom consented to participate in future individual qualitative interviews. Individuals were eligible to participate in our programme of inquiry if they met the following inclusion criteria: fluency in English, age of 21 years old or older at the time of the study, and diagnosis of a brachial plexus injury that occurred during birth. Participants completed an electronic consent prior to interviews. At the start of each interview, participants were informed of their right to discontinue study participation at any time.

Given both the large number of potential participants and our belief that a sample of diverse participants would be most informative to our study purpose, we applied a maximum variation sampling plan (Suri, 2011) to identify participants with heterogeneous experiences as the cases for this collective case study (Crowe et al., 2011; Stake, 2013). We considered seven parameters to achieve our maximum variation study sample. Four parameters were specific to participant demographics including age, biological sex, affected upper extremity, and history of childhood surgery. We grouped the participants into generational age groups (i.e., 21–39, 40–59, 60+), with proportions in each age group representative of the sample from which they were recruited (i.e., declining numbers in advancing age groups). We planned to achieve a balanced distribution between male and female participants since the incidence of BPBI is relatively equal among the sexes (Foad et al., 2008). We desired to have at least one participant with bilateral BPBI and a slightly greater percentage of right affected as compared to left affected individuals to coincide with the prevalence of affected extremity in the broader BPBI population (Suri, 2011). A history of surgery on the affected extremity in childhood was a proxy for injury severity. Given that only participants younger than 60 years old had a history of childhood surgery, we sought to have participants with and without a history of childhood surgery in the two younger age brackets.

Wanting diversity in participants’ life experiences along with demographic diversity, we analysed responses to the open-ended questions in the previously conducted BPBI Survey Study in terms of participants’ experiences across three parameters: magnitude of experience, variation in life experience, and attribution of medical conditions to BPBI. Magnitude of experience refers to the impact of BPBI on everyday experiences, ranging from no impact to impacting multiple facets of one’s life. Variation in life experience reflects the continuum of participant experiences, ranging from the perspective that BPBI fosters unique opportunities (e.g., Paralympic athletics) to BPBI, causing negative experiences (e.g., limited career options). Attribution of medical conditions to BPBI refers to the extent to which individuals attribute health conditions to BPBI, ranging from associating no health concerns to multiple sequelae.

Our desired sample size was 12 participants to achieve a diverse sample with a minimum of two participants per each of the demographic parameters. This sample size aligns well with the collective case study approach in terms of achieving an in-depth examination of the complexity of participant experience (Mills et al., 2010). We recruited participants via email using an iterative process to ensure we met the sampling parameters. Out of the initial 12 selected participants, 9 agreed to participate. Additional three participants were selected according to the maximum variation sampling plan and were subsequently recruited to reach a sample size of 12.

Data collection

Once the sample of participants was obtained, we conducted one-on-one semi-structured interviews with individuals over a Zoom® videoconference link. Interviews took place from May 2021 to October 2021 and were 54–110 minutes in duration. AA conducted all interviews, taking field notes and performing reflexivity before and after all interviews. The participants and interviewer were unknown to each other prior to the interviews. For example, when a participant reported “overcompensating” with the unaffected arm, the interviewer asked the participant describe the experience of “overcompensating.” Interviews occurred iteratively with data analysis, and subsequent interview prompts were updated as informed by the ongoing data analysis throughout the data collection period (Creswell & Poth, 2018).Table I.Interview guide prompts.Primary interview questions• What is it like to have a BPBI?• What should others know about the experience?• How has the experience of having a BPBI been through the lifespan?• How have medical care experiences been?• What advice is recommended to medical professionals and BPBI-affected children and their families?

Data analysis

Our data analysis employed an interpretivist approach (Denzin & Lincoln, 2018) informed by the theoretical assumptions of the ICF. We applied Braun and Clarke’s (2006) six phases of thematic analysis to our data. Furthermore, throughout all stages of data analysis, we used a combined inductive and deductive approach (Fereday & Muir-Cochrane, 2006). We first applied an inductive approach to coding and theming and then referenced the ICF theoretical framework employing a deductive approach to refine coding and themes. The primary data sources for each case were the interview transcript and researcher fieldnotes. In addition, the analysts reviewed the participants' responses to the previous BPBI Survey (Dorich et al., 2023) for additional context.

AA and BB independently read transcripts entirely to elucidate the findings, with both researchers making independent case reflection memos on their initial impressions of each case (Houghton et al., 2013). Then, AA and BB read transcripts line-by-line and generated code labels based on the meaning of the information from their individual perspective, using reflective memos to track code definitions and meaning (Felner & Henderson, 2022). After independent coding, AA and BB discussed how each of the researcher’s perspectives from their different experiences with BPBI may impact their data interpretation and brought consensus to coding (Creswell & Poth, 2018). Then, AA and BB collaboratively developed individual case summaries for each participant (Stake, 2013), which described the participants’ experiences in childhood, adolescence, and adulthood. For the first four interviews, analysis was performed on hard copies of interview transcripts. The codebook of the qualitative codes only required minor revisions after these four interviews were analysed. Thus, all subsequent coding was performed in MAXQDA 2020 (VERBI Software, 2019) using the previously outlined approach.

Once coding was completed, AA and BB performed cross-case comparisons to identify similarities and differences in experiences across individuals (Stake, 2013). Researchers [AA and BB] used an iterative process of categorization and thematic development that included reviewing the codes in MAXDQA, grouping the codes into categories using post-it notes, discussing each of the researchers’ perspectives, and describing the connections and larger thematic patterns among the codes in analytical memos (Miles et al., 2020) (Table II). Methodological rigor

A variety of procedures were applied to ensure methodological rigour. Our entire interdisciplinary study team met regularly throughout the research process to discuss and inform study methods and analysis. Additionally, AA and BB utilized memoing and reflexive journaling throughout data collection and analysis (Shenton, 2004). During interviews, quiet concern and probes facilitated participants’ detailed accounts of their experiences (DeJonckheere & Vaughn, 2019). Study results include detailed descriptions and direct participant quotes providing a rich and transparent illustration of the study findings (Amin et al., 2020; Shenton, 2004).

We performed member checking by providing all interview participants with the opportunity to review their case summaries, drafts of the written results, and the BPBI HRQoL conceptual models (Creswell & Miller, 2000). These documents were sent via email to all study participants with guiding questions to facilitate participants’ evaluation of the accuracy and completeness of study findings. Five participants returned responses, with all participants indicating a favourable review. One participant included comments and a sketch of recommended enhancements to the conceptual models of BPBI HRQoL, which informed model revisions.

All participants resided in North America. Participants’ marital status varied, including unmarried, engaged, married, and widowed. Some participants reported having children; others were not parents. Participants were in varied life roles ranging from being a full-time college student, employed, to retiring. The spectrum of reported occupations included careers in office settings, manual labour, physical fitness/athletics, healthcare, and education. Participants’ leisure interests spanned engagement in arts activities, team and individual sports, physical fitness/exercise, arts/crafts, and volunteer work. Of these activities, some were only childhood activities, some lifelong interests, and others were new skills acquired in adulthood.

From these 12 diverse cases, our thematic and cross-case analyses revealed how BPBI-affected adults experience a continuum of health and disability within the biopsychosocial model of health. We first present the two overarching themes that describe the experience of living with BPBI: Biopsychosocial dimensions of the experience and Lifelong and variable experience. We then present an emergent conceptual model that describes BPBI HRQoL across the lifespan. We use participant quotes to illustrate an individual’s experiences. Pseudonyms are used to maintain anonymity.

Biopsychosocial dimensions of the experience

The first theme emphasizes that the experience of BPBI-affected individuals is multidimensional. BPBI affects one’s physical and emotional health along with one’s personal identity. Individual’s activity participation and environments are additional dimensions that are impacted by having BPBI and that can impact one’s experience living with the condition.

BPBI affects physical and emotional health

Participants described how BPBI affects their health, including the affected arm, other body areas, and emotional and mental health as key dimensions of the BPBI experience. Universally, participants described how BPBI affects their arm function and development. Bill described functional deficits in his affected extremity: “I can’t raise my arms above my head … I don’t have … a lot of manual dexterity.” Kiesha noted how bimanual tasks are affected: “When I’m trying to reach something in my cupboards … where it should take both my hands to … I have to do it with one arm. So that’s a struggle.” Reflecting upon physical development, Lynne explained, “… my nerves didn’t develop the same, and my muscles didn’t develop the same. And [BPBI] left some very underdeveloped things in my body.” Evelyn described, “there’s a vast difference between a birth injury and [being] traumatically injured because our entire body changes. One side of our body changes. My foot is smaller. My hand is smaller.”

Participants described experiencing pain and attributed health concerns to BPBI in anatomical regions beyond the affected arm, with times of pain onset ranging from childhood to adulthood. Huey shared, “I’ve just noticed in the last maybe year or two years, … if I sleep on this side too much, [my affected arm] starts to hurt here.” Additionally, overuse problems in the unaffected upper extremity were a concern. Ann describes the effects on her unaffected arm: “The grip is not as good. And I have carpal tunnel … It’s a weak arm now.” Kiesha described how BPBI affects her body beyond her affected arm: “So it’s not just your shoulders and your arm. It’s your whole body. Your right side of your body, for the rest of your life, is literally compensating for whatever the left side of your body is not doing.”

Participants described various ways they felt having BPBI had impacted them emotionally and with respect to mental health. Some participants explained how being frequently reminded of their injury was an emotional burden. Huey described, “The mental impact is just as big, if not bigger than the actual physical.” Participants described experiencing emotional challenges at different points across the lifespan, including childhood trauma they associated with having BPBI that continued into adulthood. Charlie described how a leadership coach at a work training helped him identify that his anger about having BPBI was affecting work relationships. He stated, “you’ve had to deal with [having BPBI] your entire life, every day of your life, and you’re angry about it.” Participants described being concerned with their appearance, especially during adolescence. Gemma described, “It used to always bother me when I looked in the mirror and like, ok, my … right arm is shorter. It would bother me that clothes wouldn’t fit right.” Participants described various ways they attempted to hide their BPBI, including adapting clothing and strategically posing in photos, primarily in adolescence. Frankie shared that BPBI “caused a lot of trauma” and further described how as a child he, “… didn’t want to live with it.” He shared that as a young child he held knives to his throat but felt the physical limitations in his right arm prevented him from being able to take his life. Kiesha expressed the value of therapy to cope with the emotional burden of BPBI as a child: My mother … told the doctors, “look, she’s not dealing with this well.” That’s when they gave me a referral to see a social worker. It was a good experience because I was able to talk about it … As a child, I never talked about my arm. I never came home and told my mother that I get teased in gym, and I got bullied … I didn’t tell anybody … I just kept it to myself.

BPBI is encompassed within affected individuals’ personal identities

Participants’ descriptions of having BPBI as foundational to their personal identities also emerged as a dimension of the BPBI experience. Delilah described her experience as, “… Both positive and negative… When I describe it to people, I would say I wouldn’t give [BPBI] up… I wouldn’t know who I am without it.” Julia shared: “I can’t imagine having never had this … I just don’t know what I’m missing. I cannot even fathom the idea of putting both hands up in the air, and that’s, I don’t think, anything to pity… I just don’t know any different.”

Participants also described personal factors they attribute to having BPBI including empathy, determination and persistence, self-confidence, problem-solving, the ability to self-advocate, and curiosity about BPBI and the human body. Ivan expressed, “[BPBI has] made me at least be a creative thinker, thinking outside the box about how to address certain problems.” Gemma shared, “I don’t know if I would be the carefree fearless open person that I am, and, you know, a good work ethic, determined, all of that kind of stuff without [BPBI].”

BPBI impacts activity participation and activity participation can impact living with the condition

A third biopsychosocial dimension is that participants described various ways their activity participation has been affected by BPBI and how activity participation has impacted the experience of living with BPBI. Experiences among participants universally included limitations or insecurities related to BPBI, which impacted activity participation in various ways. Participants described the need to adapt or have assistance to participate. Bill described how adaptations are commonplace for affected individuals: “I’ve had to modify everything. Any time I exercise, I have to modify that. Even when cooking I have to modify because my arms are weak.” Participants described activities they avoided or in which they were physically unable to participate. Ann shared, “I would love to have done things like go out for cheerleaders or do anything that normal teenagers do, twirl a baton or do certain dances.” Charlie recounted he did not attend pool parties: “Sometimes it was maybe not how I would react but how other people react to seeing it…. And I would rather just not have that experience, so I just would avoid it.” Holding participants back from participating in certain activities because of their condition by family members was an experience among some participants. Gemma shared, “I wasn’t allowed to do PE or anything like that because … my parents were so afraid I was going to get hurt.”

Conversely, participants also shared that they participated in specific activities or had unique opportunities because of having BPBI. Huey recounted that his parents enrolled him in swimming lessons and karate to help his arm and described how this led his family to become more involved in sports: “We would not have done any of this stuff if it wasn’t for my arm. And I think that was like awesome because it exposed them to stuff that they would never have done on their own.” Participants expressed that BPBI fostered their involvement in BPBI-specific volunteer work or para-athletics. Participants described ways BPBI has impacted their career choices such as being drawn to specific occupations (i.e., working in healthcare), or having to change careers or retire early due to BPBI-related health concerns.

Additionally, participants shared how involvement in certain activities has impacted their experience living with BPBI. Participants described using physical activities, including yoga, Thai chi, and stretching, to improve their mobility or control pain. Bill said, “I exercise every day. And that … helps keep down the pain level.” Others described how participation in activities negatively affected their physical health. Julia shared: I ran cross-country in high school and college, and after one semester of college, I had to quit because the high mileage combined with my arm was throwing everything off … I was having to get cortisone shots in my knee … And I was having to go to the physical therapist just to get my hips aligned just because I was not swinging right.

Delilah described how impactful teaching yoga at a camp for BPBI-affected children was for her: “It was the most moving thing for me, as an affected person, to teach yoga to kids, kids and parents, and teens who were feeling like terrible about their bodies and who had never tried it before.”

BPBI influences affect individuals’ social environments, and the experience of having BPBI is impacted by affected individuals’ social environments

Similar to activity participation, participants described a reciprocal relationship in which BPBI impacts their social environment (e.g., individuals in their lives) and vice versa. For example, participants expressed concerns about BPBI affecting their family members negatively. Frankie described concern his BPBI would negatively impact his relationship with his school-aged daughters: Like [at a] daddy daughter dance, am I going to be able to … stretch down and, you know, dance with my daughter? and the other [BPBI] one is just like there, and then people are staring? … I would hate for them to be like, “oh, Dad, you’re embarrassing me” … that would kill me.

Like [at a] daddy daughter dance, am I going to be able to … stretch down and, you know, dance with my daughter? and the other [BPBI] one is just like there, and then people are staring? … I would hate for them to be like, “oh, Dad, you’re embarrassing me” … that would kill me.

Participants worried they would experience worsening physical challenges with age and thus become a burden to their family. Some participants described receiving more attention or better opportunities than their siblings. One participant shared that she was afforded experiences, such as attending a private college, which her siblings did not have because she had financial trust from a malpractice settlement.

Participants explained that parents’ reactions to a child’s BPBI affects the child and believed that it is important for parents to know that. Delilah shared that she was affected by her mother’s response to her injury, stating, “It was really hard for my mom. She felt really guilty… It continues to be a source of tension. I got care, but I was always very embarrassed and ashamed about that.” Family members’ refusal to acknowledge their BPBI was also an experience among some participants. Bill recounted: “My mother never talked about my condition, never … I was hurt by it.” Conversely, family involvement and support helped make the experience easier for some. Evelyn described her siblings as being supportive: “I didn’t know it was part of my physical therapy. I thought my brothers were playing with me. I had a brother that would take me to the park, and they had these small parallel bars. And he taught me to try to hold onto them. He would try to teach me grip.”

Participants strongly recommended that parents not only avoid limiting their children’s activities but also encourage their affected children to do things they are capable of. Ann shared: “I’m hoping … … that the parents don’t, say, ‘You can’t do that thing. You just can’t do that because you have this wrong with you.’ I think that’s totally wrong. You got to say, ‘Yeah, we’ll figure out a way. You can do this.’”

Participants also described both positive and negative interactions with various people outside their family, including friends, other BPBI-affected persons, and classmates. Huey described how his high-school friends supported him in soccer: “They knew like if the ball went out, they would run and go take the throw-in for me.” Bill described how meeting someone on an online support group was helpful: “I got to talk to somebody who used to do a lot of running outside. And … when I was running [my hand] would get numb … She told me to just put my thumb into like a brace … or hang onto a shirt … I had never thought of that.” Conversely, Kiesha shared, “In gym [class], … I literally got bullied and made fun of, and so I hated it…. I used to like, kind of have … panic attacks. Stomach hurting when it was time for me to go to gym.”

Lifelong and variable experience

The second overarching theme captures how BPBI-affected adults described a wide variety of experiences related to living with BPBI, including differing examples from throughout their lives. Participants described BPBI as a chronic condition, shared how their own experiences have changed over time, and expressed that experiences differ between individuals.

BPBI is a chronic condition

Participants described BPBI as lifelong. Evelyn referred to BPBI as a “cradle-to-grave injury.” Huey reflected on the chronicity of BPBI, stating, “… the one thing that I do really hate about it is it’s just never going to go away. … it’s just something I’m going to have to forever be like working at.” Participants described the need for lifelong healthcare due to BPBI’s chronicity.

Experiences change over time

Participants described how the experience of having BPBI has changed throughout their lives. For example, some described new physical concerns they have encountered with ageing, while others described having emotional challenges as a child or teenager that improved in adulthood.

Participants noted physical changes they had noticed over time, such as worsening pain, muscle atrophy, and decreasing mobility. Bill expressed that his independence has decreased from the physical effects he associates with ageing with BPBI: “As I’ve gotten older, … I began to accept that I just can’t do certain things. And I have to be willing to, you know, say I need help.” Some expressed they had been unaware they would experience BPBI-attributed health concerns with ageing. Gemma articulated this phenomenon: “Now I have all these back and neck problems related to [BPBI] that, obviously, as a child, we had no idea [were] going to happen.” Conversely, some participants described improvements in range of motion and strength as they became more involved in physical therapy as adults. Ivan shared, “I decided to really dig in with my PT and see what we can do … I was able to improve like overhead to like 125 degrees from 76 degrees in a few weeks.”

Some emotional challenges described by participants were experienced primarily during childhood and adolescence. Huey described how having BPBI had gone from a mostly negative experience to a more positive one, stating, “It’s brutal having it as a kid. And it’s brutal having it as a teen. But I think as an adult … … if you can internalize it in a positive way, it’s like a blessing in its own way.” Similarly, some participants stated their ability to cope with BPBI has improved with age. Charlie stated, “You’re obviously much more … emotionally ready to deal with the injury [as an adult]. But as a child, you’re not nearly as equipped, and other children can be cruel. So, I think how it’s impact on me has changed over the years.”

Experiences vary among affected individuals

Participants articulated variability in experience of having BPBI. Evelyn stated, “Not every injury is the same. Our injuries are like thumbprints.”

Participants noted how they felt their physical limitations and sequelae might compare to others’ when describing the severity of their own injury. For example, Charlie recognized that while others were affected by BPBI-related pain, he was not, stating, “I’ve come to learn is a lot of people have a lot of pain. I don’t have any pain at all.” Participants also described differences in how individuals cope with their condition. Evelyn expressed this perspective, saying, “Some people will be positive. Some people are going to be very sad about it.”

Participants’ knowledge about their condition varied widely. Some participants described having an early understanding of their condition, including its name, while others only learned about having BPBI as an adult. Ann shared that she learned the name of her condition while watching a television show: “There was a little boy on there. And he … couldn’t put his backpack and coat on like that because he has the brachial plexus injury. And I said, huh, because that little boy was just like me.”

Conceptual model of BPBI HRQoL through the lifespan

Our themes and subthemes capture the broad perspective of BPBI HRQoL through the lifespan as experienced by our participants and informed by the ICF (WHO, 2002). Our first theme, Biopsychosocial dimensions of the experience, illustrates the health and disability continuum experienced among affected individuals within the four ICF domains (Figure 1). The second theme, Lifelong and variable experience, depicts the diversity of BPBI-related experiences through the lifespan and among individuals.

Together with the BPBI Functioning/Disability Continuum, the Healthcare Environmental Factors and Social Environmental Factors comprise the biopsychosocial dimensions of living with BPBI-HRQoL as depicted by the horizontal continuum towards the bottom of Figure 2. As BPBI-affected individual age and assume more life roles their Social Environmental Factors expand. Similarly, participants described that the impact of BPBI on the bodily components and activities and participation expands with age. Participants described increased body concerns with ageing and participation in a greater number of activities. Participants expressed concern that the availability of these BPBI-specific medical services became scarce in adulthood and described having to piece together various healthcare resources to manage their BPBI-related concerns as depicted by the small, scattered circles on the right hand side of Figure 2.

Then, with ageing, the BPBI-affected person assumes more activities, such as self-care skills, academic pursuits, and leisure activities. Furthermore, this more detailed model depicts that each person describes general health concerns (as shown by the cone with a dashed outline in Figure 3) which seem independent of BPBI in childhood. As individuals age, a BPBI-affected person’s general health concerns become more intertwined with BPBI-attributed impact on health. Similarly, health literacy skills are personal factors that emerge through the lifespan and are applied to enhance HRQoL at the interface with Healthcare Environmental Factors.

Discussion

This collective case study reveals the complexity of BPBI HRQoL among a heterogeneous sample of adults with the condition. BPBI-affected adults describe BPBI as a chronic medical condition having a wide range of attributed health concerns that evolve through the lifespan. The functioning and disability continuum is multidimensional for BPBI-affected individuals with an expansion of impacts experienced by ageing as individuals engage in increased activities and life roles. Throughout the lifespan, personal and environmental factors interweave to impact the HRQoL of BPBI. While participants described common experiences, variability in individual experiences was discovered.

Our results demonstrate a wide range of health impacts beyond the physical effects of BPBI, suggesting the need for holistic care for BPBI-affected persons. This finding underscores the importance of using a biopsychosocial model of health, such as those embodied by the ICF, in both clinical care and research for conditions such as BPBI. Indeed, for other chronic conditions in adulthood, the use of the ICF as a conceptual framework has enabled the identification of similar broad biopsychosocial impacts (Alford et al., 2015). Of particular importance in the current study is the impact of psychosocial health on BPBI, with similar findings previously reported among children (Mentrikoski et al., 2015). Psychosocial functioning has been found to be associated with coping strategies (ICF personal factors) and peer support (ICF environmental factors). This finding is not surprising, as chronic physical illness in children and adolescents is known to impact more than just physical function, including academic and social function (Pinquart & Teubert, 2012). How a child’s psychosocial factors translate to psychosocial functioning in adulthood remains unknown, although personal factors such as self-determination in adolescence may play a role (Bergman et al., 2018). Further research is warranted regarding the interplay between the different domains of the biopsychosocial model in both children and adults with BPBI to identify opportunities to improve long-term HRQoL.

Using a holistic view of BPBI across the lifespan, the current study’s findings also reveal that BPBI HRQoL can be impacted by a number of environmental factors, including social supports, healthcare resources, and activity participation. Importantly, participants articulated how their condition approached in childhood by their social supports, such as their parents, affects their experience living with BPBI through the lifespan. Therefore, beginning in childhood we must consider the families of affected individuals, rather than focus solely on the affected child in the delivery of care for BPBI. Previous studies have identified an impact of BPBI on families of affected individuals, including correlations between severity of injury and family impact (DeMatteo et al., 2014), between perceived child functioning and parental worrying (van der Holst et al., 2016), and between medical malpractice litigation (against the delivering obstetricians) and parents’ reports of child’s function (Eismann et al., 2014). Thus, not only is BPBI a stressor to the families of affected individuals but also stressors within the family can play pivotal roles in the perceived outcomes of BPBI. Our study underscores the importance of this dynamic by identifying how the families’ approaches to BPBI in childhood impact the affected individuals’ lifelong experience with the condition. Indeed, support for the families of affected children will not only benefit the families but may also impact the long-term HRQoL of the affected individuals themselves. Future investigation is warranted regarding this interaction, as family attitudes may represent a novel and important modifiable determinant of long-term HRQoL for individuals affected by BPBI.

The current study also identified variable experiences among BPBI-affected individuals. This finding, similar to our prior study (Dorich et al., 2023), helps to reconcile seemingly conflicting findings of earlier studies regarding pain (de Heer et al., 2015; Patridge & Susan, 2004), upper extremity function (de Heer et al., 2015; Kirkos et al., 2005; Ploetze et al., 2020), physical function (Butler et al., 2017; Ploetze et al., 2020), psychosocial function (Butler et al., 2017; Patridge & Susan, 2004; Ploetze et al., 2020) and quality of life (Butler et al., 2017; van der Holst et al., 2018; Yau et al., 2018). These earlier studies examining health outcomes among BPBI-affected adults all applied narrowly focused measurement tools, ranging from solely relying on clinical measurements (Kirkos et al., 2005), using a symptoms checklist (Patridge & Susan, 2004), or applying a variety of researcher-chosen patient reported outcome measures (Butler et al., 2017; Ploetze et al., 2020; van der Holst et al., 2018; Yau et al., 2018). Thus, each of these inquiries elucidated a piece of the puzzle, yet their findings could be seen as conflicting when the diversity of experiences among individuals is not recognized. In contrast, the current study used in-depth individualized semi-structured interviews to elucidate the complexity of experience among a diverse sample of BPBI-affected adults. The saliency of the rich diversity of experiences ascertained by our study was confirmed by one participant who during member checking noted specific differences between others’ experiences and his own.

In identifying this diversity of experience, our study findings illuminate the need for individualized care due to the variable nature of BPBI HRQoL. Individualized care is a pillar of musculoskeletal and rehabilitative care. Although prior research has not focussed on adult outcomes of BPBI, individualized care has been cited as central to improving outcomes of other childhood-onset neuromuscular problems such as cerebral palsy (Arnevik Austrheim et al., 2023), other chronic adulthood musculoskeletal problems such as osteoarthritis (Marriott & Birmingham, 2023), and even adulthood outcomes of other childhood-onset neurologic problems such as autism (Mazurek et al., 2023). Even more broadly, in prior qualitative research across diverse patient and clinician populations, personalization of care was identified as a key component of overall patient- and family-centred care (Hsu et al., 2019). Thus, it is critical to recognize the diversity of experiences among adults affected by BPBI if we are to improve patient-centred care for this population before, during, and after the transition to adulthood. Furthermore, as healthcare moves towards standardized patient assessment protocols for conditions such as BPBI (Brown et al., 2024; Pondaag & Malessy, 2018), we must ensure that these assessments are sensitive to the diversity of patient experiences.

Finally, our study identified variations in BPBI impact over time and an expansion of the impact with age, coupled with a perceived decrease in BPBI specific medical care available for affected adults. While an in-depth analysis of these findings is beyond the scope of this paper, further investigation of this perception is warranted as an opportunity exists to improve care for BPBI-affected adults. Armed with greater knowledge of the breadth, depth, and complexities of BPBI HRQoL among affected adults, not only that clinicians are poised to provide better long-term prognostic information to affected individuals and families in childhood, but also that we are better informed to guide appropriate long-term care.

Study limitations and future work

This study is not without limitations. Participants were recruited from social media support groups, potentially creating a selection bias. However, this strategy allows us to reach a larger and more diverse sample than prior studies recruiting from local clinical populations. Similarly, despite using maximum variation sampling, inclusion of additional participants may have elucidated additional experiences. Nonetheless, by identifying variability in experiences as a theme, our study underscores the need to tailor care to each affected individual, including those with experiences not reflected in our sample. Finally, because of the diversity of age in this cross-sectional study, generational differences and historical changes in medical care may account for some differences among individuals’ experiences, limiting generalizability to future individuals. However, the presence of themes common to these generations and ages highlights the importance of certain concepts, such as the impact of childhood family interactions on the adult experience with BPBI, and the scarcity of BPBI-specific care in adulthood, which must be further explored in the future research. Additionally, an investigation of how best to operationalize individualized healthcare services for BPBI-affected adults is warranted.

Conclusion

Through a comparison of individual experiences, this collective case analysis enriches the perspective of BPBI HRQoL. The HRQoL impacts among BPBI-affected adults are multifactorial, spanning across all aspects of a biopsychosocial model of health throughout the lifespan and expanding with age. Individual experiences are variable among affected individuals as well as throughout their lifespans. Lifelong individualized care is paramount for this population.
